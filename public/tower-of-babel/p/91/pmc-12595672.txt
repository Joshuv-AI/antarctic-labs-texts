Two-stage replantation of a severed digit with blood supply from the first dorsal metatarsal artery
Authors: Guo-hua Ni, Xing Jia, Wei-yang Gao, Jian-jun Hong, Yong-gang Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study is to examine the feasibility and clinical outcomes of a two-stage digit reconstruction technique involving initial ectopic implantation followed by secondary replantation in cases where direct in situ replantation of severed digits is not possible. The technique employes the first dorsal metatarsal artery as the source of vascular supply.MethodsA retrospective review was performed on 13 patients (8 males, 5 females; age range: 7–61 years; mean age: 38 ± 14.4 years) who sustained crush-avulsion injuries of the fingers and underwent treatment between December 2009 and December 2023. The intact amputated digits were temporarily transplanted to the dorsum of the foot, where blood supply was maintained via the first dorsal metatarsal artery. After a period of 1 to 4 months, during which graft survival was ensured, secondary replantation was conducted for reconstruction of the affected digits. Postoperative monitoring included assessments of vascular perfusion and evaluation of sensory and motor functions of the reconstructed digits. Functional recovery was assessed based on the criteria set forth by the Chinese Society of Hand Surgery.ResultsAll 13 patients met the criteria for temporary ectopic implantation. The temporarily transplanted digits included 7 thumbs, 2 index fingers, 3 middle fingers, and 1 ring finger, all of which survived the initial implantation. During the second-stage reconstruction, a total of 13 fingers were successfully replanted, including 10 thumbs, 1 index finger, 1 middle finger, and 1 ring finger. Follow-up durations ranged from 1 to 12 years (mean: 4.2 years). Functional outcomes were categorized as excellent in 2 cases, good in 8 cases, and fair in 3 cases; no poor outcomes were reported.ConclusionThe dorsum of the foot provides a viable and effective site for one-stage ectopic replantation of severed digits when in situ replantation is not feasible. Utilization of the first dorsal metatarsal artery as a vascular source for digit reconstruction demonstrates both procedural safety and favorable clinical outcomes.Keywords: Amputation, Ectopic implantation, Finger, First dorsal metatarsal artery, Reconstruction, Replantation

Introduction

With advances in microsurgical techniques, the survival rate of digital replantation has markedly improved [1, 2]. Nevertheless, severe crush and blast injuries to the hand often result in multiple digit amputations, frequently accompanied by composite tissue defects involving skin, nerves, blood vessels, and metacarpals of the palm. In cases where the amputated digit remains intact, conventional replantation techniques may still be unfeasible due to extensive palmar damage [3, 4]. Although simultaneous transplantation of tissues such as vascular or free flaps has been reported as a successful approach for digit replantation, the procedure remains complex and carries a substantial risk of vascular complications [5]. Consequently, the survival rate following replantation may be compromised, and functional outcomes may be suboptimal. In the event of replantation failure, the intact severed digit cannot be utilized, and further injury may occur [6]. Since Godina’s first successful use of the thoracodorsal artery as a vascular conduit for replantation in 1986, there has been an increasing number of reports describing secondary replantation of digits utilizing ectopic vascular sources [4, 7].

Various anatomical sites have been described as vascular conduits in the literature, including the chest wall, lateral thigh, dorsum of the foot, lower leg, forearm, dorsum of the hand, abdomen, and groin [7–13]. The corresponding feeding arteries at these sites comprise the thoracodorsal artery, descending branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery, dorsalis pedis artery, posterior tibial artery, wrist branch of the ulnar artery, radial artery, ventrolateral artery, and superficial epigastric artery respectively. These ectopic implantation sites present certain limitations, such as difficulties with dressing, restrictions in clothing, and mobility challenges. Thigh-based flaps may exhibit increased swelling when transferred to the hand, which can impede function of the unaffected hand. Additionally, the exposure of implanted digits increases the risk of accidental trauma.

The scarcity of superficial veins in thigh skin suitable for anastomosis with the dorsal digital vein complicates venous connection during subsequent replantation and reconstruction. When the epicutaneous branch of the ulnar or radial artery serves as the feeding artery for ectopic implantation, harvesting the implanted digit necessitates sacrifice of the ulnar or radial artery, resulting in greater trauma to the hand. Moreover, skin grafting is often required at the wrist following harvest, adversely affecting the appearance of the unaffected hand. Currently, we have searched through a large amount of data and found that only the dorsal artery of the foot is suitable for supplying blood to the severed finger.

The aim of this study is to identify a more suitable ectopic implantation site and corresponding feeding artery for severed digits. A retrospective analysis was conducted on clinical data from patients who underwent two-stage replantation of severed digits with vascularization provided by the first dorsal metatarsal artery. Between December 2009 and December 2023, patients who underwent secondary digit reconstruction following ectopic banking of amputated digits vascularized by the first dorsal metatarsal artery were included. The analysis focused on three principal objectives: (1) technical feasibility of arterial anastomosis between the first dorsal metatarsal artery and the digital proper arteries, (2) postoperative viability of the banked digits, and (3) functional recovery following second-stage replantation. This investigation evaluated the use of the first dorsal metatarsal artery as a feeding artery for amputated digits and assessed clinical outcomes of secondary replantation aimed at establishing a novel, stable, and reliable feeding artery and an optimal ectopic implantation site for severed digits.

Materials and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) heavily contaminated hand wounds with multiple digit amputations, including the thumb, accompanied by severe palmar injuries; (2) infeasibility of direct replantation due to grade III (according to six-degree scale of finger defects [14]) or higher digit defects or distal defects involving multiple metacarpophalangeal joints, making reconstruction through techniques such as ectopic replantation or digit transposition impractical; (3) presence of at least one intact phalanx in the severed finger, with no indication for in situ replantation due to severe palmar damage, thus meeting the criteria for ectopic implantation; and (4) absence of foot trauma or deformity, along with a strong dorsalis pedis pulse and satisfactory filling and reflux of the superficial dorsalis pedis vein.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) classification of the first dorsal metatarsal artery as Gilbert Type III; (2) inability to tolerate prolonged or multiple surgical procedures; and (3) history of mental illness or noncompliance with medical care.

General information

This retrospective study included patients who underwent two-stage replantation of severed digits using the first dorsal metatarsal artery as the vascular supply at a medical institution, between December 2009 and December 2023. The cohort consisted of 13 patients (8 males and 5 females), aged 7 to 61 years (mean age: 38 ± 14.4 years). The interval from injury to surgery ranged from 1 to 5 h (mean: 2.5 ± 1.4 h). Causes of injury included cannon blast trauma (n = 3), mixer-related strangulation injuries (n = 5), blunt force from a heavy object (n = 1), and traffic-related trauma (n = 1), blender injury by meat grinder (n = 3). Surgical methods

Preoperative preparation

Radiographic imaging of the affected hand was performed to evaluate the extent of injury. Based on the severity of the digit defect, an amputated digit of appropriate length, unsuitable for direct replantation, was selected for ectopic implantation. Doppler ultrasonography was utilized to assess the anatomical branching pattern of the first dorsal metatarsal artery in the donor foot. The course of the dorsal foot veins was mapped, and vascular anatomy was accurately marked.

Surgical procedures

One-stage surgical intervention was conducted concurrently for the upper and lower limb groups. In the upper limb group, debridement of the hand and severed digits was performed. Digital replantation was carried out for amputated digits meeting established replantation criteria. Residual hand wounds were managed using flap transplantation, skin grafting, or vacuum sealing drainage (VSD) to ensure adequate wound coverage. Blood vessels, nerves, and tendons required for subsequent digit reconstruction were identified and marked. Tissue defects necessitated the first-stage transfer of nerves, blood vessels, and tendons from other digits for use in the second-stage surgery. Following completion of digit debridement, ectopic implantation was undertaken.

For the lower limb group, ectopic implantation of the amputated digit was performed on the dorsum of the foot. The length of the ectopically implanted digit was determined based on the defect size of the digit. The bone ends and flexor/extensor tendon stumps of the digit were trimmed, and the dorsal digital veins, proper digital arteries, and proper digital nerves were dissected. An incision was made along the surface projection of the first dorsal metatarsal artery between the first and second metatarsals. When a unilateral skin defect was present in the digit, a tongue-shaped flap was designed to address the deficit. Dissection of the dorsal foot superficial veins was performed in alignment with the dorsal digital vein distribution to facilitate venous anastomosis. The deep peroneal nerve was dissected in preparation for future anastomosis with the proper digital nerve during the reconstructive phase.

During the first surgery, when performing the hand debridement, it is necessary to examine whether the fingers that will be reconstructed in the future (such as the thumb) have any defects in the intrinsic nerve or the superficial palmar nerve, any defects in the intrinsic artery and the superficial palmar artery, or any defects in the flexor tendons and extensor tendons. If there are nerve defects, when the severed finger is grafted onto the dorsum of the foot, the peroneal deep nerve should be dissected and cut at the distal end, and the proximal end should be anastomosed with the intrinsic nerve of the grafted finger. When reconstructing the finger, the peroneal deep nerve that will be anastomosed with the intrinsic nerve should be retracted proximally to a sufficient length to repair the nerve defect of the hand reconstruction finger. If the assessment of the future reconstructed finger in the hand indicates that there will be flexor or extensor tendon defects, when grafting the severed finger, in the same incision, the extensor tendon of the second toe should be dissected and cut at the distal end, and the proximal end should be sutured to the flexor or extensor tendon of the grafted finger. To avoid affecting the extension function of the second toe, the distal end of the extensor tendon should be sutured together with extensor digitorum brevis of the toe. The course and diameter of the first dorsal metatarsal artery were examined, and transection was performed at a site matching the digital artery’s diameter and required length to enable arterial anastomosis with one proper digital artery. The digit was then secured to the metatarsal using Kirschner wires, and the skin incision was closed.

Two-stage ectopic implantation procedures were subsequently conducted to reconstruct the affected digits. These surgeries were also carried out simultaneously for both the upper and lower limb groups.

In the upper limb group, the recipient area was explored by incising the skin at the defect site. The proper digital nerve, arteriovenous vessels, and flexor and extensor tendons were exposed. In the absence of an anastomotic digital artery or superficial dorsal vein, the radial artery or its terminal branch was employed as the arterial inflow, and the cephalic vein as the venous outflow.

In the lower limb group, the ectopically implanted digit was harvested. When a scar or a small estimated skin defect was observed at the hand defect site, a partial flap from the dorsum of the foot was included during digit harvest. Dissection began contralateral to the arterial anastomosis and proceeded toward the vascular anastomosis site, with proximal dissection extending along the first dorsal metatarsal artery. In cases involving arterial defects of the hand, the dorsalis pedis artery was included in the harvested graft. For concurrent tendon and nerve defects, the peroneal deep nerve or the extensor tendon should be retracted proximally to the required length for repairing the nerve or tendon defect of the hand, and then cut. Once the composite graft—comprising the digit, vascular pedicle, tendons, and nerves—was prepared, it was transferred to the defect site. Skeletal fixation was accomplished using Kirschner wires. Vascular anastomosis was performed between the first dorsal metatarsal artery or dorsalis pedis artery and either the digital or radial arteries; between the superficial veins of the dorsal foot and either the dorsal hand veins or cephalic vein. Additionally, tendon sutures were completed between the flexor and extensor tendons of the digit and their corresponding proximal hand tendons, while the proper digital nerve and deep peroneal nerve were anastomosed with the proximal hand nerves. Skin grafting was employed to address the skin defect at the donor site on the dorsum of the foot.

Postoperative management

Following the first stage of surgery, postoperative care adhered to standard nursing protocols for severed finger replantation. Clinical monitoring involved regular assessment of the skin color, capillary refill, pulp tension, and overall vascular status of both the ectopically implanted digit on the foot and the replanted finger. As clinically indicated, anti-vasospastic agents, anticoagulants, anti-infective medications, and analgesics were administered.

The patients were advised to maintain bed rest while performing active bending and extension of the lower limb joints to promote circulation. Muscle massage of the lower limbs was encouraged to prevent deep vein thrombosis and to minimize the risk of accidental trauma to the ectopically implanted digit, including compression by bedding. Wound exudate from the hand was monitored, along with any signs of erythema or edema at the incision site.

For patients who underwent flap coverage, vascular perfusion of the flap was regularly evaluated to ensure adequate healing. In the presence of a VSD system, it was essential to verify the patency of the drainage tube and to monitor the appearance and quantity of the bloody drainage fluid.

After secondary replantation of the ectopically implanted digit for finger reconstruction, care continued under standard postoperative protocols for digital reconstruction. Radiographic imaging was obtained one week postoperatively to evaluate fracture alignment, with follow-up radiography at six weeks to assess fracture healing. Upon confirmation of osseous union, Kirschner wires were removed, and the patient was instructed to perform active and passive interphalangeal joint exercises with appropriate protective measures. Post-discharge follow-ups occurred biweekly, during which time rehabilitation guidance was adjusted as needed.

Rehabilitation exercises included the following: (1) Once survival and positional stability of the ectopically implanted digit were confirmed, the Kirschner wire used for fixation was removed at three weeks postoperatively, after which passive mobilization of the interphalangeal joint was initiated at a frequency of three sessions per day, each lasting ten minutes, to prevent joint stiffness; (2) For digit reconstruction, early passive mobilization at non-fracture sites was permitted once viability had been established. Kirschner wires impeding joint motion were removed promptly following initial fracture healing to allow for both passive and active joint movement; (3) Upon removal of internal fixation, active functional rehabilitation exercises of the joints were initiated, with physiotherapy employed to promote recovery and restore optimal hand function.

Outcome measures and clinical efficacy evaluation

Following the second stage of surgery, the reconstructed digits were regularly evaluated for vascular perfusion, fracture healing, sensory recovery, and motor function. Final follow-up assessments were conducted using the trial criteria for digit functional reconstruction established by the Chinese Society of Hand Surgery (Table 2) [15]. These criteria evaluated affected digit mobility, strength of the reconstructed digit, sensory function, and overall hand utility. The total evaluation score was 15 points, with the following classifications: 13 to 15 points indicated excellent function, 9 to 12 points indicated good function, 5 to 8 points indicated fair function, and 4 points or below indicated poor function.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0. Measurement data that conformed to a normal distribution were reported as means ± standard deviation ± s).

Results

A total of 13 patients met the criteria for ectopic implantation of severed digits, comprising 8 males and 5 females, aged 7–61 years (mean age: 38 ± 12.4 years). The distribution of digit amputations included 2 cases involving the thumb, index, middle, ring, and little fingers; 4 cases involving the thumb, index, middle, and ring fingers; 3 cases involving the index, middle, ring, and little fingers; 2 cases involving the thumb, middle, ring, and little fingers; and 2 cases involving the thumb, index, and middle fingers.

A one-stage procedure was conducted for all amputated digits considered suitable for replantation, with 15 of 16 digits surviving postoperatively. Among these, 13 digits met the indications for ectopic implantation. The ectopically implanted digits consisted of 7 thumbs, 2 index fingers, 3 middle fingers, and 1 ring finger. All 13 digits survived following ectopic implantation onto the dorsum of the foot, utilizing the first dorsal metatarsal artery as the feeding vessel. The duration of replantation and ectopic implantation procedures (the time for the first surgery) ranged from 4.2 to 7.9 h (mean: 6.2 h). Intraoperative blood loss ranged from 250 to 750 mL (mean: 520 mL). One case of venous crisis was identified in an ectopically implanted digit on the first postoperative day. Vascular exploration indicated venous anastomotic thrombosis resulting from obstruction of venous return due to angulation between the ectopically implanted digit and the dorsum of the foot. The complication was resolved through re-anastomosis after correcting the orientation of the implanted finger. In the remaining cases, the color of the ectopically implanted digits was consistently pink, with capillary refill times ranging from 1 to 3 s and moderate pulp tension. All ectopically implanted digits survived. No infections were observed at the foot incision sites; however, two cases of infection occurred at the hand incision sites, both of which responded effectively to appropriate antibiotic therapy.

After a period of 1–4 months post–one-stage surgery when the hand wound was stable, such as controlled infection, survived skin flap, completely survived other replanted fingers, etc., as well as the complete survived amputated finger in the foot, and patient’s stable condition, two-stage ectopic implantation procedures were performed for affected digit reconstruction. These included 10 reconstructed thumbs, along with 1 case each of reconstructed index, middle, and ring fingers. The duration of these surgeries ranged from 3.0 to 5.5 h (mean: 4.1 h). Intraoperative blood loss ranged from 110 to 310 mL (mean: 220 mL). Postoperative observations revealed pink digit color, capillary refill times of 1 to 3 s, and moderate pulp tension. No vascular complications occurred, and all incisions healed by primary intention. All 13 transferred digits survived after reintegration into the hand.

Follow-up durations ranged from 1 to 12 years (mean: 4.2 years) following the two-stage procedure. The foot donor sites healed by primary intention. The appearance of the reconstructed digits was deemed satisfactory, with no evidence of pulp atrophy. Sensory recovery—including tactile, pain, and temperature sensation—and motor function were satisfactory. The reconstructed digits were capable of cooperating with other fingers to perform basic grasping, holding, and pinching functions. At the final follow-up, functional evaluation outcomes indicated excellent function in 2 cases, good in 8 cases, fair in 3 cases, and there were no cases with poor outcomes.

Discussion

Digit amputations accompanied by palmar destruction injuries pose substantial challenges due to extensive defects involving hand skin, blood vessels, nerves, tendons, and the presence of open comminuted metacarpal fractures with severe contamination. Even when the severed digit remains intact, the probability of successful replantation and survival is considerably low, often resulting in impaired hand function and a high rate of disability [16]. To address this, temporary ectopic implantation of the severed digit can promote its survival. Subsequent replantation and reconstruction of the digit following successful ectopic implantation significantly improves hand function and reduces disability [17]. In all 13 cases reviewed, the first dorsal metatarsal artery was employed as the feeding artery for the ectopically implanted finger.

Anatomical basis of the first dorsal metatarsal artery as the feeding artery for ectopically implanted digits

Gilbert classified these vessels into three types [18]. Type I vessels are relatively superficial and easily dissected, Type II vessels are located more deeply but can be mobilized effectively as feeding arteries, while Type III vessels are slender and unsuitable for use as feeding arteries. In the present cases, Types I and II accounted for 88% of the vessels encountered. Further anatomical studies have classified the first dorsal metatarsal artery into five types [19]. Among these, Types I (supramuscular), II (intramuscular), and III (submuscular) are considered suitable feeding arteries for free tissue transfer. The average lengths are 39.9 ± 6.3 mm (Type I), 28.8 ± 5.3 mm (Type II), and 26.7 ± 3.3 mm (Type III), with a diameter at the origin of approximately 1.5 ± 0.3 mm for all three types. These dimensions align well with the size of the proper digital artery of the finger, confirming their appropriateness as feeding arteries for ectopic implantation. The arterial course is also classified into three types, with Gilbert Type III posing particular challenges for free vessel dissection, rendering it unsuitable as a feeding artery [20]. Consequently, preoperative Doppler ultrasound evaluation is recommended to verify the presence of vessels, determine the direction of the main trunk, and locate the vascular pedicle.

Advantages and disadvantages of the first dorsal metatarsal artery as a feeding artery for ectopically implanted digits

The first dorsal metatarsal artery presents several advantages when used as a feeding artery for ectopically implanted digits: (1) it is a well-recognized arterial vessel among hand surgeons and microsurgeons, facilitating familiarity with the donor site anatomy; (2) the skin texture and color at the ectopic implantation site closely resemble those of the finger, allowing for effective skin graft integration during hand defect repair in replantation and reconstruction; (3) its vascular caliber closely matches that of the proper digital artery; (4) the surgeon’s positioning during the procedure tends to be more ergonomically comfortable; and (5) the donor site typically experiences minimal postoperative adverse effects.

However, several disadvantages are associated with its use: (1) the first dorsal metatarsal artery exhibits anatomical variation, and not all types are suitable for use as feeding arteries for ectopic implantation; (2) utilizing this artery precludes the possibility of employing the second toe—vascularized by this artery—for subsequent digit reconstruction.

The difficulties and complications of supplying the severed finger through other arteries

The position of the plantar artery is superficial and can be easily felt on the body surface, making it easy to locate. However, if it is used as the blood supply artery for the fingers, its diameter is approximately twice that of the intrinsic artery of the fingers. Due to the significant difference in diameter, anastomosis is not easy, and the risk of thrombosis leading to necrosis of the grafted fingers increases. The diameter of the lateral plantar artery is not much different from that of the intrinsic artery of the fingers, but its anatomical location is deeper and is less commonly used in clinical practice. The surgeon of this study did not select the plantar artery and the lateral plantar artery for this area of anatomy. Moreover, we searched relevant articles about replantation of digit, the sites of tissue grafting reported in the literature include the chest wall, the outer side of the thigh, the back of the foot, the lower leg, the forearm, the back of the hand, the abdomen, and the inguinal region. The supplying arteries are the thoracoepigastric artery, the descending branch of the lateral femoral artery, the foot artery, the posterior tibial artery, the dorsal palmar artery of the wrist, the radial artery, the lateral abdominal artery, and the superficial abdominal artery. These grafting sites all have some drawbacks, such as affecting the wearing of clothes and pants, and causing inconvenience in movement. The grafting of the finger on the thigh results in a more bulky appearance after the skin flap is moved to the hand, the movement of the healthy hand is affected, and the grafting finger is prone to accidental collisions. In addition, the skin on the thigh lacks superficial veins that can be anastomosed with the dorsal veins of the finger, making it difficult to perform the later reimplantation and reconstruction of the thumb with anastomosed superficial veins. The dorsal palmar artery of the wrist or the radial artery serves as the supplying artery for the grafting finger. During the removal of the grafting finger, the dorsal artery of the wrist or the radial artery is sacrificed, resulting in larger hand trauma. Moreover, after the removal of the grafting finger, the wrist generally requires skin grafting, affecting the appearance of the healthy hand. In this study, the supplying artery for the grafting finger is the first metatarsal dorsal artery, which has a similar diameter to the intrinsic artery of the finger, abundant foot veins, and a diameter similar to the dorsal veins of the finger, making anastomosis easier. After the cutting of this artery and vein, there is no impact on the blood circulation of the foot, and the following repair of nerve defects in the finger, repair of finger tendon defects with the tendon of the extensor digitorum longus, repair of skin defects in the foot with skin from the foot, and similar skin texture to the hand can be achieved using the peroneal deep nerve at the foot incision to repair nerve defects, the tendon of the extensor digitorum longus to repair finger tendon defects, and the skin at the foot to repair skin defects in the hand.

Precautions for the procedure

(1) Surgical indications must be strictly adhered to. The authors identified the following criteria for ectopic digit implantation: (a) the patient’s physical condition permits surgery; (b) there is a strong desire to preserve the digit(s); (c) severe hand injury involves multiple severed digits that are intact but unsuitable for direct replantation; and (d) defects exist in the digits after debridement and suturing. Given the prolonged treatment cycle and multiple surgeries, the patient’s psychological and financial capacity must be considered; (2) Preoperative vascular Doppler ultrasonography is recommended to evaluate the anatomical variations of the first dorsal metatarsal artery; (3) Anastomotic arteries and veins should avoid acute or right-angled configurations. Careful adjustment of the angle between the foot and the ectopically implanted digit is essential, and fixation with a Kirschner wire is advised; (4) In cases of proximal skin defects on the ectopically implanted digit, although these defects can be addressed by shortening the phalanx, new challenges may arise in repairing the metacarpal defect after digit transfer. The first dorsal metatarsal artery flap can be used to repair the proximal metacarpal skin defect in one stage, while the proximal phalanx can be used to address the metacarpal defect upon repositioning to the hand. (5) Toe transplantation for digit reconstruction requires cautious planning. Excessive reconstruction in a single procedure may compromise overall effectiveness. The number of replanted and ectopically implanted digits should be limited. (6) Attention should be paid to the repair of nerves and tendons. Although the establishment of blood supply plays a crucial role in the survival of foster fingers and reconstructed fingers, the reconstruction of sensory and motor functions of reconstructed fingers is also of vital importance. Only with good sensory and motor functions can reconstructed fingers have more significant clinical value. Feng [21] The first dorsal metacarpal artery flap with dorsal metacarpal digital nerve was used to repair the thumb tip skin defect. After more than 17 months of observation, it was found that the anastomosis between the dorsal branch of the proper digital nerve and the stump of the digital nerve in the recipient area of the finger was anastomosed with or without the dorsal branch of the proper digital nerve. There was no significant difference in the reconstruction of the sensation of the flap. However, for the reconstruction of the thumb and finger through the replanting of the finger, since the nerve has been completely severed and some still have defects, the repair of the finger nerve is crucial for the reconstruction of sensation, and some may still require nerve transplantation.

In this study, although severed digits met the criteria for ectopic implantation, only single-digit ectopic implantation was performed, followed by secondary replantation for key digit defects. For single digit amputations, even with severe proximal palm injury, ectopic implantation is generally not recommended. Instead, in situ replantation should be prioritized using techniques such as vascular transplantation or flow-through flap transplantation [22].

In summary, the dorsum of the foot provides a suitable site for ectopic implantation of a finger, with the first dorsal metatarsal artery functioning as a reliable and effective vascular supply for the ectopically implanted finger. In patients with polydactyly who have intact fingers but are not candidates for single-stage replantation, the first dorsal metatarsal artery can be utilized to vascularize the ectopically implanted digit. Furthermore, two-stage digit reconstruction is both safe and feasible, yielding satisfactory clinical outcomes. However, due to the anatomical variability of the first dorsal metatarsal artery, not all types are appropriate as feeding arteries for ectopic implantation, representing a limitation of this technique. Consequently, preoperative assessment with vascular Doppler ultrasonography is essential. Finger grafting and replantation for thumb and finger reconstruction involve the repair and reconstruction of blood vessels, nerves, tendons and bones, and each aspect is very important.For this study, how the fingers survive is particularly important. Although the repair of nerves, tendons and bones is also a key point in the surgery, it has not been summarized and discussed in detail. We will continue to study it in the future.
