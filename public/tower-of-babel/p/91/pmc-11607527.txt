A Helpful Bug in the System: Gut Microbes and Their Positive Impact on Portal Pressure Modulation
Authors: Moira B Hilscher
Published: 2024

Portal hypertension (PHTN) is a major driver of morbidity, mortality, and liver transplantation in patients with cirrhosis. The pathophysiology of PHTN is complex and multifactorial, with several contributing pathologic factors, including endothelial dysfunction and architectural distortion of the liver caused by fibrosis. Hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) are the primary drivers of liver fibrosis. HSCs also contribute to the pathophysiology of PHTN through regulation of intrahepatic vascular resistance. Because of the paucity of therapeutic options available for PHTN, studies that target the more reversible and actionable modulators of portal pressure, such as contraction of HSCs, are merited.

Liver disease impacts the gut microbiota through several mechanisms, including changes to the enterohepatic circulation with impaired secretion of primary bile acids into the gut lumen,1 leading to decreased production of secondary bile acids by gut bacteria; decreased gut motility2; increased bacterial translocation secondary to physical changes imparted by elevated portal pressure3; and impairment of the gut-associated immune system.4 Pathogen-associated molecular patterns derived from gut microbiota in turn can exacerbate the systemic inflammation associated with cirrhosis. In a previous issue of Cellular and Molecular Gastroenterology and Hepatology, Li et al5 identify a mechanism through which gut-derived molecules in turn impact liver disease by modulating HSC contraction and intrahepatic vascular resistance.

Impaired diversity of the microbiome has been identified in cirrhosis, with further reductions noted at the onset of decompensating events.6 Microbial metabolites impact the pathogenesis of liver disease, with prior studies identifying short-chain fatty acids as important regulators of epithelial function,7 lipid and glucose metabolism,8 and inflammation.9 In this study, Li et al5 study urolithin A (UroA), a by-product of the gut microbiota, and find that it modulates intrahepatic vascular resistance by regulating the metabolism, activation, and contraction of HSCs. They identify lower levels of UroA in the feces and plasma of patients with clinically significant PHTN, and they additionally identify a negative correlation between UroA levels with portal pressure and complications of PHTN, including ascites and the Child-Pugh score. Administration of UroA to mice with liver fibrosis caused by carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) and bile duct ligation decreased fibrosis and complications of PHTN, suggesting a potential therapeutic impact on the regulation of portal pressure. The authors then administer UroA at more advanced stages of fibrosis in these models and find that it ameliorates PHTN, suggesting a potential impact on the reversible modulation of portal pressure even at advanced stages of fibrosis. Transcriptomic analysis of liver tissue from UroA-treated mice reveals that UroA impacts pathways that regulate HSC phenotype, including vascular smooth muscle contraction and fibroblast proliferation. They use in vitro studies to confirm that treatment of HSCs with UroA decreases HSC activation, contraction, and proliferation by regulating glutamine metabolism. Administration of the UroA-producing bacterial strain Lactobacillus marinus alleviates liver fibrosis and PHTN in murine models of fibrosis, presumably through production of UroA in its metabolism.

These studies on the gut microbiome highlight potential considerations in clinical care that may decrease the rate of decompensation and acute-on-chronic liver failure triggered by gut dysbiosis. This includes antibiotic stewardship, and more discerning use of proton pump inhibitors. Prior data indicate that up to 25% of patients with cirrhosis are maintained on long-term antibiotics as primary or secondary prophylaxis of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis.10 Although infection in cirrhosis is associated with decompensation and acute-on-chronic liver failure, indiscriminate use of antibiotics may exacerbate gut dysbiosis and disrupt symbiotic intestinal bacterial populations.6 Rifaximin is an antibacterial drug in the rifamycin class that is poorly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract. It is commonly used in the treatment and prevention of hepatic encephalopathy. Although rifaximin has broad antimicrobial effects against gram-positive and gram-negative aerobes and anaerobes, it may promote establishment of beneficial bacterial taxa, including Lactobacillus.11,12 It has been demonstrated to decrease circulating levels of gut-derived endotoxins, such as lipopolysaccharide,13 and may also reduce systemic inflammation.14 Li et al5 identify Lactobacillus as potentially beneficial genera in modulating portal pressure. Collectively, these studies nominate potentially impactful microbial and metabolic manipulations, and raise clinical considerations to modulate the actionable regulators of portal pressure.
