Introduction

The recent decision of the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to halt new clinical trials involving the transfer of US patient cells for processing in designated countries, prominently including China, frames vital biomedical collaboration through an adversarial geopolitical lens. While data security and patient consent are paramount, this sweeping measure risks stifling innovation in critical fields like genetic engineering and cell therapy, disproportionately impacting patients with rare and complex diseases. The FDA's new policy overlooks China's established regulatory frameworks for biomedical ethics and data security, disregards the tangible benefits of past and present Sino‐US scientific collaboration, and ultimately undermines the global fight against shared health challenges of the 21st century. Prioritizing open, transparent, and equitable international scientific cooperation, underpinned by robust mutual standards, is not merely preferable—it is essential for human health.

On June 18, 2025, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) announced an immediate halt on new clinical trials exporting live cells from US citizens to China and other designated “hostile countries” for genetic engineering or processing before reinfusion [1]. Citing concerns over inadequate patient consent regarding international transfer and potential misuse of sensitive genetic data, this action leverages recent US data security rules and executive orders aimed at countering perceived foreign threats. While protecting patient rights and data is nonnegotiable, the FDA's approach, painting collaborative biomedical research with broad geopolitical suspicion, represents an alarming step towards scientific decoupling with profound negative consequences for global health progress.

The Imperative of Global Collaboration in Biomedicine

Modern biomedical breakthroughs, particularly in complex fields like gene editing (e.g., CRISPR/Cas9 and base editing [2]), cell therapies (e.g., CAR‐T [3] and stem cells), and new alternative methodologies (e.g., organoids and organs‐on‐chips [4, 5, 6]), are inherently global endeavors. Rare diseases, aggressive cancers, and novel infectious threats do not respect national borders. Tackling them requires pooling diverse patient populations, specialized expertise, advanced technological capabilities, and significant resources that no single nation possesses in abundance.

China has emerged as a major contributor in these fields during the past decade [7, 8]. Its significant investments in biotechnology infrastructure, large and genetically diverse patient populations, and growing cadre of highly skilled researchers offer invaluable assets. Numerous peer‐reviewed studies published in leading journals, including The Lancet, The New England Journal of Medicine (NEJM), Nature, and Science, attest to the quality and impact of research emerging from Chinese universities or institutions [7, 8, 9]. Collaborative projects between US and Chinese scientists have accelerated drug discovery, advanced fundamental understanding of disease mechanisms, and developed promising therapeutic approaches. Halting the flow of biological samples and data essential for such trials doesn't just slow down US research; it impedes global progress against intractable diseases.

China's Commitment to Bioethics and Data Security: A Reality Check

The FDA action implicitly questions the ethical and security standards of biomedical research in China. This characterization overlooks the following significant developments. (1) Evolving regulatory framework: China has established comprehensive regulations governing human genetic resources (HGR) and clinical trials, while the institutionalization and capacity‐building of scientific research ethics have been ongoing for several decades. The Biosecurity Law and subsequent updates to the Administrative Regulations on HGR provide a legal structure for oversight, including strict provisions for informed consent, data privacy, and data protection [10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. While implementation is an ongoing process, the framework exists and is actively enforced. (2) Adherence to international standards: In China, leading research institutions and hospitals rigorously implement international ethical guidelines (e.g., Declaration of Helsinki, CIOMS). Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) or Research Ethics Committees are competent. Participation in global clinical trials necessitates compliance with international Good Clinical Practice (GCP) standards. (3) Data security measures: Concerns about data security are legitimate globally. However, Chinese labs involved in high‐level international collaborations routinely employ advanced data encryption, secure storage solutions, and access controls comparable to those in Western institutions. The assertion that sensitive data is inherently less secure or more susceptible to “misuse by foreign governments” within a collaborative research context requires concrete evidence, not broad geopolitical labeling.

While the FDA's policy is framed as a safeguard against foreign threats, its categorical prohibition contrasts sharply with China's Personal Information Protection Law (PIPL) and Biosecurity Law, which establish conditional pathways for international data exchange. The PIPL (effective 2021) explicitly protects all natural persons' data processed within China (domestic citizens or foreigners), which is broadly consistent with relevant EU regulations, mandating lawful, transparent, and necessary processing with explicit consent (Articles 13–14, Chapter II) [15]. Crucially, cross‐border transfers require: (1) passing a security assessment by the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC) for “important” data; (2) certification by accredited institutions; or (3) a binding contract with overseas recipients enforcing PIPL standards (Articles 38–40, Chapter III) [15]. The fundamental legislative purpose of China's PIPL is not only to safeguard personal data but also to promote its responsible use, seeking an optimal balance between protection and utilization. Though stringent, this framework permits transfers subject to procedural safeguards, unlike the FDA's geopolitical veto. The Biosecurity Law (2024) and HGR Regulations further regulate genetic data via multi‐tiered reviews, requiring approvals for foreign collaborations but not banning them outright [10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Thus, while US policymakers might cite China's HGR restrictions as a reciprocal justification, the distinction lies in their intents and mechanisms. China's laws codify risk‐managed access through defined procedures (e.g., security reviews, contracts), whereas the FDA policy imposes a blanket ban without comparable avenues for compliance or appeal. This divergence underscores a substantive imbalance: China's framework, though burdensome, enables collaboration under mutual oversight; the FDA's approach erects an absolute barrier predicated on nationality rather than project‐specific risk.

The primary issue highlighted by the FDA—inadequate patient consent regarding international transfer under specific US waiver clauses—points to a failure of US regulatory oversight and corporate transparency under the previous framework, not an inherent flaw in Chinese research ethics. Addressing this requires fixing the US consent process and ensuring partner compliance, not severing ties with an entire nation's scientific community.

The Cost of Scientific Fragmentation

The FDA's halt, framed as protective, carries substantial negative repercussions. Firstly, it will delay cures for patients. Slowing down or halting promising clinical trials directly impacts patients, particularly those with rare diseases or advanced cancers, where few options exist. The specialized expertise or unique patient cohorts available in China can be crucial for advancing these specific therapies. Denying access delays potential life‐saving treatments for Americans and global citizens alike. Besides, it will stifle innovation. Scientific progress thrives on the cross‐pollination of ideas, data, and techniques. Isolating US research from significant hubs of innovation like China impedes the rapid iteration and validation necessary in fast‐moving fields like genetic engineering. It risks creating parallel, less efficient scientific ecosystems. It will also hinder the economic growth of small and medium‐sized US biotech firms, which often pioneer in novel therapies, frequently rely on international partners for cost‐effective, specialized processing or access to specific patient groups. The biotechnology sector thrives on global partnerships. However, this FDA action creates significant financial and operational burdens, potentially driving innovation offshore or halting promising projects entirely. More importantly, it will lead to the erosion of trust. Labeling collaborative partners as “hostile” based on nationality, rather than specific, proven violations, damages the foundation of mutual respect essential for scientific cooperation. It fosters suspicion and undermines the goodwill built over decades of productive exchange. Last but not least, it will result in a global leadership vacuum to some degree. Retreating from international collaboration cedes leadership in setting global ethical and technical standards for emerging biotechnologies.

The Path Forward: Cooperation, Not Containment

Protecting patient data and ensuring ethical rigor are universal obligations. However, achieving these goals requires cooperation, not isolation. Mutually agreed, verifiable safeguards are paramount. The US, China, and other leading scientific nations must intensify diplomatic efforts to establish clear, reciprocal frameworks for cross‐border biomedical research. This includes: (1) Harmonized consent standards: Developing internationally recognized templates ensuring explicit, comprehensive patient consent covering all aspects of data and sample transfer, storage, and use. (2) Robust data security protocols: Agreeing on minimum technical standards for data encryption, anonymization/pseudonymization, access control, and secure transfer, potentially verified through independent audits. (3) Transparent governance: Creating clear mechanisms for oversight, incident reporting, and accountability for all entities involved in international trials. Additionally, more strengthened regulatory dialogues are needed. Direct, sustained communication between the FDA, NIH, China's National Medical Products Administration (NMPA), Ministry of Science and Technology (MOST), and National Health Commission (NHC) is crucial. Joint workshops, regulator exchanges, and shared inspection protocols can build trust and address specific concerns constructively. Meanwhile, we should focus on specific risks rather than national origin. Security and bioethical reviews should be based on specific, credible risks associated with particular entities or technologies, not blanket nationality‐based prohibitions. This allows for targeted mitigation while preserving broad collaboration. What's more, championing open science responsibly is vital for global health [16]. Encouraging pre‐competitive research, data sharing within agreed secure frameworks, and international consortia for tackling major health challenges (e.g., antimicrobial resistance and pandemic preparedness) can demonstrate the tangible benefits of cooperation while managing risks.

Conclusion

The FDA's decision to halt clinical trials involving cell exports to China reflects a concerning trend of allowing geopolitical tensions to dictate scientific engagement. While vigilance regarding data security and patient consent is imperative, the response must be precise, cooperative, and grounded in evidence, not broad‐brush stigmatization. China has built substantial biomedical research capacity and established regulatory frameworks deserving of engagement, not dismissal.

The challenges facing human health—from genetic disorders to future pandemics—are global. Overcoming them demands the pooled intellect, resources, and diverse perspectives of the international scientific community. Retreating behind walls of suspicion serves no nation's long‐term health interests. Instead, the United States, China, and all scientific powers must urgently recommit to building the transparent, equitable, and robust collaborative frameworks necessary to harness biotechnology's full potential for humanity. The cost of fragmentation is measured not just in delayed therapeutics and economic inefficiency, but in lives not saved. We should embrace biotechnology collaboration. The health of the world depends on it.
