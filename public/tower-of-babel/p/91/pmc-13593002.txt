Gender and racial differences in the relationship between mean arterial pressure, pulse pressure, and cognitive function in older adults: NHANES 2011 to 2014
Authors: Tianqi Wang, Weiwei Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The impacts of mean arterial pressure (MAP) and pulse pressure (PP) on cognitive decline have not been fully characterized, and little is known about the gender and racial differences in this relationship. This study aimed to look into the above-mentioned relationship. Information was obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2011 to 2014. The Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease Word Learning Test (CERAD W-L), the Animal Fluency Test, and the Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST) were used to assess cognitive functioning in participants aged 60 years and older. Weighted linear regression analyses were employed to detect the relationship between MAP, PP (continuous or quartiles), and 3 cognitive test scores. Additionally, forest maps and smooth curve fitting were utilized to demonstrate subgroup analyses stratified by gender and race. There were 2612 participants in this study. After fully adjusting for covariates, MAP was negatively correlated with cognitive functioning estimated by CERAD W-L (β: −0.026, 95% confidence interval [CI]: −0.045 to −0.008), and PP was negatively associated with cognitive test scores through DSST (β: −0.039, 95% CI: −0.065 to −0.013). This tendency remained statistically significant across different MAP quartile groups and PP quartile groups (P for trend < .01). In the CERAD W-L test, subgroup analyses stratified by race as well as sex revealed that the negative relationship between MAP, PP, and cognitive scores remained significant in females (MAP: β: −0.042, 95% CI: −0.066 to −0.017; PP: β: −0.018, 95% CI: −0.033 to −0.002) and non-Hispanic White (NHW; MAP: β: −0.031, 95% CI: −0.052 to −0.010; PP: β: −0.016, 95% CI: −0.029 to −0.002). Additionally, a negative relationship between PP and cognitive scores was also found in DSST in females (β: −0.044, 95% CI: −0.077 to −0.011) and NHW (β: −0.041, 95% CI: −0.070 to −0.012). Our study suggested that elevated levels of MAP and PP were negatively correlated with cognitive functioning, particularly in females and NHWs. Therefore, the management of MAP and PP might be helpful for the prevention of poor cognitive performance in the elders. However, the cross-sectional nature of this study limits causal inference.

1. Introduction

Cognitive impairment, which develops gradually from varying degrees of visuospatial, language, and memory decline, is an important feature of Alzheimer disease, the primary cause of dementia, which has caused great suffering and a serious burden on older adults and their families.[1,2] Since cognitive decline often progresses to dementia, for which there are no specific drugs to treat, early detection of risk factors for cognitive decline is crucial.[3]

Existing studies have concluded that high blood pressure (BP) is correlated with executive dysfunction, slowed thought processing, and memory deficits. It accelerates the rate of cognitive decline and increases the risk of cognitive disease.[4–6] Mean arterial pressure (MAP) and pulse pressure (PP) are important cardiovascular indicators, but their roles in cognitive decline, particularly in older adults, remain inadequately explored.[7] MAP is the average arterial BP at each instant of a cardiac cycle and receives major influence from cardiac ejection and peripheral vascular resistance.[8] PP provides an easily accessible monitor for understanding cardiac function and its interaction with the vascular system, and the main determinants of PP are stroke volume and aortic impedance.[9,10] While some studies suggest that PP may play a more substantial role in cognitive function than MAP, others have found MAP to be a more reliable predictor of cognitive performance, creating a gap in the literature that warrants further exploration.[11–13]

To the best of our knowledge, the effects of MAP and PP on cognitive decline have not been fully characterized, particularly in relation to racial and gender differences. Previous studies have shown mixed findings, and the differences between these 2 BP indices in predicting cognitive outcomes remain unclear. Hence, the purpose of this research was to estimate the mentioned correlations using a representative sample from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2011 to 2014.

2. Methods

2.1. Study population

Data for this study came from NHANES, a survey combining medical examinations and interviews that has been conducted biennially since 1999 in America. The information and data in NHANES are freely available to the public.[14]

A total of 19,931 individuals who signed a written informed consent form were initially enrolled, among whom those under the age of 60 did not meet the inclusion criteria (n = 16,299). Then, the elders who did not accomplish any cognitive functioning measurement (n = 698) and had missing data for MAP and PP (n = 322) were excluded. Finally, 2612 participants were included (Fig. 1).

Selection of study population. AFT = the Animal Fluency Test, CERAD W-L = the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease Word Learning Test, DSST = the Digit Symbol Substitution Test, MAP = mean arterial pressure, PP = pulse pressure.

2.2. MAP and PP

NHANES consists of 3 consecutive BP measurements and 1 additional measurement if necessary. MAP and PP were calculated from brachial BP as 1/3 systolic blood pressure (SBP) + 2/3 diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and SBP − DBP, respectively.[15]

2.3. Cognitive performance

Cognitive function was examined by the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease Word Learning Test (CERAD W-L), the Animal Fluency Test (AFT), and the Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST). Three consecutive learning trials and a delayed recall test together comprised the CERAD W-L test.[16] Ten points were the maximum score for each experiment. AFT examines verbal fluency. People who participated in AFT were required to name animals within 60 seconds.[17] A point was awarded for each successfully named animal. We required participants to repeat the corresponding symbols into the boxes adjacent to the numbers within 2 minutes for a total of 133 points in the DSST test, which reflects working memory capacity and executive function.[18] Higher scores represented better cognitive abilities.

2.4. Covariables

Covariates brought into this study included race (Other Hispanic/non-Hispanic White [NHW]/Mexican American/non-Hispanic Black/Other race), sex, age, body mass index (BMI), poverty income ratio (PIR), smoking status (never/ever/now), alcohol consumption (no/yes), marital status, educational levels, hypertension, high cholesterol level, asthma, diabetes, coronary heart disease, heart attack, angina/angina pectoris, congestive heart failure, and stroke. All the above information was collected by trained health technicians. Several variables, such as medication use, depression, physical activity, and dietary factors, were not included in this study. On the one hand, covariates were selected based on prior research examining the association between BP and cognitive function, focusing on the most widely recognized confounders in the literature.[19–23] On the other hand, we optimized the number of covariates in our adjustment models to mitigate multicollinearity. We acknowledge that residual confounding may exist, and these variables should be considered in future studies. This limitation has also been illustrated in the discussion.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Categorical variables in the weighted baseline characteristic table were represented as counts (%), while continuous data were described as mean ± standard deviation. T tests and chi-square tests were employed to discern intergroup disparities. We employed weighted linear regression analyses to detect the association by calculating the β and 95% confidence interval (CI) between MAP, PP (continuous or quartiles), and 3 cognitive test scores. To assess the clinical significance of the changes in cognitive scores, we reviewed the previous research literature and referenced established thresholds for cognitive impairment. Most studies showed[24–26] that there was a linear relationship between the 1-point decrease in CERAD W-L or DSST and cognitive impairment. Therefore, even a 1-point decrease in CERAD W-L or DSST is also commonly considered indicative of clinically meaningful cognitive decline. Three different regression models were constructed. The crude model did not adjust for any covariates. Race, gender, and age were adjusted in model 1. Race, gender, age, BMI, PIR, smoking status, alcohol consumption, education levels, marital status, hypertension, high cholesterol level, asthma, diabetes, coronary heart disease, heart attack, angina/angina pectoris, congestive heart failure, as well as stroke were adjusted in model 2. Multiple imputation was applied to handle missing data, using chained equations under the assumption of missing at random. Twenty imputations (m = 20) were performed to ensure stability of estimates, including all analytic variables (MAP, PP, cognitive test scores, and all covariates) in the imputation model. The percentage of missingness in key variables was below 5%. The imputed datasets were combined according to Rubin’s rules to obtain pooled regression coefficients and standard errors. In addition, forest maps and smooth curve fitting were utilized to demonstrate subgroup analyses stratified by gender and race. R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, http://www.R-project.org) as well as EmpowerStats (X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, http://www.empowerstats.com) were utilized for analyzing data. P < .05 indicated that the data were statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Characteristics of study population

A total of 2612 older adults were included in this research, among whom 1309 were males and 1303 were females. Compared with male participants, females had significantly lower levels of education, PIR, smoking, alcohol consumption, diabetes, angina/angina pectoris, coronary heart disease, heart attack, as well as AFT scores, and had higher levels of hypertension, asthma, PP, CERAD W-L, and DSST scores. Additionally, differences in marital status and BMI were notable (Table 1).

3.2. Association between MAP, PP and 3 cognitive test scores

After adjusting for all covariates, MAP was found to be negatively correlated with cognitive test scores through CERAD W-L (β: −0.026, 95% CI: −0.045 to −0.008, P = .00511). While a 1-point decrease in CERAD W-L or DSST scores may reflect early signs of functional cognitive impairment, the clinical significance of these findings should be further validated through longitudinal studies. When MAP (continuous) was converted into a categorical variable, cognitive test scores dropped by 78.4% in the fourth quartile (Q4; β: −0.784, 95% CI: −1.422 to −0.145, P = .01625) compared with participants in the first quartile (Q1). This tendency remained statistically significant across different MAP quartile groups (P for trend = .002; Table 2).

3.3. Subgroup analyses

In the CERAD W-L test, after fully adjusting for covariates, subgroup analyses stratified by gender and race showed a negative relationship between MAP and cognitive test scores in females (β: −0.042, 95% CI: −0.066 to −0.017, P = .0008) and NHW (β: −0.031, 95% CI: −0.052 to −0.010, P = .0036), while no significant associations were found in males or all other races except NHW (Fig. 2).

After fully adjusting for covariates, subgroup analyses stratified by gender and race indicated a negative relationship between PP and cognitive test scores in females (β: −0.018, 95% CI: −0.033 to −0.002, P = .0227) and NHW (β: −0.016, 95% CI: −0.029 to −0.002, P = .0209) in the CERAD W-L test. Furthermore, a negative relationship between PP and cognitive scores was also found in the DSST test in females (β: −0.044, 95% CI: −0.077 to −0.011, P = .0086) and NHW (β: −0.041, 95% CI: −0.070 to −0.012, P = .0059), but not in males and all other races except NHW (Fig. 3). It is important to note that the sample sizes in non-White racial categories were relatively small, which may lead to unstable estimates. Therefore, the subgroup findings should be regarded as exploratory rather than confirmatory, and future studies with larger, more diverse populations are needed to validate these results.

4. Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the correlation between MAP, PP, and cognitive functioning in a nationally representative sample of American elderly. After adjusting for all confounding factors, we observed that MAP was negatively related to cognitive function through CERAD W-L and PP was negatively correlated with cognitive function through DSST, particularly in females and NHW. However, as this was a cross-sectional study, the temporal sequence between BP parameters and cognitive function cannot be determined, and causality cannot be inferred. While the statistical association is significant, it is important to emphasize that causality cannot be established in a cross-sectional design. It is also possible that early cognitive decline might lead to dysregulation of BP rather than elevated MAP or PP, causing cognitive impairment. This potential for reverse causation should be noted when interpreting our findings.

Hypertension is a major contributor to cognitive dysfunction.[27,28] The relationship between hypertension and cognitive dysfunction is most commonly discussed in terms of SBP and DBP, but MAP and PP have not been as thoroughly studied in relation to cognitive impairment. However, MAP has gained attention as a potentially more accurate reflection of overall vascular health and vital organ perfusion.[29] Some studies suggest that PP may play a more substantial role in cognitive function than MAP, whereas others propose that MAP is a more reliable predictor of cognitive performance.[11–13] Our study contributes to this debate by confirming a significant negative association between both MAP and PP with cognitive function. However, opposite or null results have also been reported in other studies.[30–32] These discrepancies may be due to variations in design, population, covariates, or cognitive assessment methods.[33,34] Future longitudinal and mechanistic studies are warranted to clarify the temporal and causal direction of these associations. These discrepancies highlight the need for further research to clarify these relationships, particularly in terms of causality and underlying mechanisms. In this study, we observed a stronger negative association between MAP, PP, and cognitive dysfunction in women compared with men. Educational attainment and biological factors, such as sex hormones and sex chromosomes, may help explain these gender differences. Research has shown that women tend to have a higher risk for cognitive decline, especially in the context of hypertension,[35–37] but other studies have reported conflicting results regarding gender differences in the effects of hypertension on cognition.[38–40] Therefore, further studies are needed to explore the role of gender in the relationship between BP and cognitive function.

There are several reliable mechanisms for the relationship between hypertension and cognitive dysfunction in elders. Hypertension contributes to cerebral vasculature changes, including small artery disease, atherosclerosis, and blood flow disorders,[41,42] which can lead to cerebral atrophy, white matter lesions, and reduced perfusion.[43–45] It may also accelerate the deposition of β-amyloid and tau phosphorylation, key markers of Alzheimer disease, and increase homocysteine levels, contributing to cognitive decline.[46,47]

Due to relatively small sample sizes in non-White populations, the subgroup analyses may have led to unstable estimates. Hence, the results from these subgroups should be considered exploratory rather than confirmatory. These results highlight the need for future studies with larger, more diverse populations to validate the current findings and further explore the role of race and gender in the relationship between BP and cognitive function.

This study used a large, nationally representative sample of American older adults, and the data were rigorously collected, reliable, and highly generalizable to the U.S. elderly population. The use of validated cognitive assessments (CERAD W-L, AFT, and DSST) and comprehensive adjustment for multiple demographic and clinical covariates further strengthens the robustness of our findings. Moreover, multiple imputation was performed to minimize bias from missing data and enhance the stability of the estimates.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. As a cross-sectional study, our research cannot establish causality, and reverse causation cannot be ruled out. Although multiple imputation was applied, potential bias from missing data or model assumptions remains. Additionally, this study did not include certain potentially relevant covariates, such as medication use (e.g., antihypertensives, statins, psychoactive medications), depression, physical activity, and dietary factors. While these factors are certainly important in understanding cognitive health, the decision to omit them was primarily based on the focus of this study on the direct relationship between BP and cognitive function. Many of these variables, while recognized as contributing to cognitive decline, are complex and may interact with BP in ways that are not straightforward. Given the early-stage nature of this study, we aimed to simplify the analysis and concentrate on the most direct variables identified in previous literature as influencing cognitive function. Additionally, controlling for a broader range of factors could introduce collinearity or confounding, which might obscure the primary relationship we are investigating. However, we acknowledge that the omission of these variables is a limitation, and future studies could consider including them to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how lifestyle factors and medication use may further modulate the relationship between BP and cognitive function. Although our study provides valuable insights into the negative relationship between MAP, PP, and cognitive function, particularly in women and NHW participants, further research is needed to confirm these associations and investigate the underlying mechanisms. The discrepancies observed in different racial and gender groups suggest that there may be important factors influencing this relationship that require deeper investigation. Finally, the effect sizes observed were modest, indicating that, although statistically significant, their clinical relevance may be limited.

5. Conclusion

Elevated MAP and PP were negatively associated with cognitive functioning, especially in females and NHW. Therefore, timely cognitive assessment and intervention are needed for older adults with elevated MAP and PP. However, the cross-sectional nature of this study limits causal inference.
