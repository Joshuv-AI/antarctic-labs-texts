Efficacy of artesunate-amodiaquine for the treatment of uncomplicated Plasmodium falciparum malaria among children aged 2–11 years in the Asante Akim North Municipality, Ghana: a single-arm open trial
Authors: Henry Hanson, Kwadwo Sarfo Marfo, James Osei-Mensa, Anna Jaeger, Nimako Sarpong, Diana Nunana Tsali, Alfred Martey, Appiah Kubi Gyimah, Esther Placca, Morrah Oppong, Geoffrey Foli, Nana Safi Almoustapha, John Amuasi, Jürgen May, Oumou Maïga Ascofaré
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe burden of malaria in Ghana is significantly high. The emergence of resistance to artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT), the primary treatment for malaria, necessitates constant surveillance. In this study the efficacy of artesunate-amodiaquine (AS-AQ), a first-line treatment for uncomplicated malaria in Ghana since 2005, and the prevalence of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in resistance markers associated with this antimalarial drug were assessed.MethodsThis prospective single-arm trial was conducted between July 2018 and June 2019 at two health facilities. A total of 173 children aged 2–11 years who presented with fever or a history of fever and tested positive by rapid diagnostic test (RDT) were recruited for the study. The participants were prescribed AS-AQ based on their body weight. The study followed the 2009 World Health Organization (WHO) protocol for monitoring antimalarial drug efficacy, with adequate clinical and parasitological response (ACPR) assessed on day 28. A total of 155 samples were successfully analysed for mutations in the pfk13, pfcrt, and pfmdr1 genes.ResultsA total of 164 participants completed the three-day treatment regimen while adhering to the study protocol. During directly observed treatment, 8.1% of participants experienced vomiting after at least one dose. By day 3, all treated participants achieved total parasite clearance. Thirteen (7.9%) participants were either lost to follow-up, non-compliant or withdrew voluntarily. The ACPR on day 28 was 92.07% and 100% by the modified intention to treat and Kaplan–Meier methods, respectively. No treatment, clinical or parasitological failure was observed during the 28-day follow-up period. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) analysis did not detect any pfk13 gene mutations linked to artemisinin resistance. The pfmdr1 NFD haplotype (86 + 184 + 1246) was identified in 27.1% of isolates, while the pfcrt K76T mutation was found in 1.9% of the isolates.ConclusionsThe study confirmed that artesunate (AS) combined with amodiaquine (AQ) remains an effective treatment for uncomplicated Plasmodium falciparum malaria in children aged 2–11 years in Ghana. Additionally, a high prevalence of a haplotype associated with reinfection following artemether-lumefantrine treatment, one of Ghana’s first-line antimalarial therapies, was observed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05490-8.Keywords: Malaria, Plasmodium falciparum, Artemisinin-based combination therapy, Artesunate-Amodiaquine, Therapeutic efficacy study, Children, Drug resistance markers, Ghana

Background

Malaria continues to have a considerable influence on human health, despite breakthroughs in antimalarial development, diagnostics, and vector control methods [1, 2]. Since the 2000s, Artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) has been recommended as the first-line treatment for uncomplicated Plasmodium falciparum malaria in sub-Saharan Africa, where the disease is endemic [3, 4]. The widespread deployment of ACT has reduced the burden of malaria-related mortality, particularly in Africa [5].

In Ghana, artesunate combined with amodiaquine (AS-AQ) has been implemented since 2005 as one of the first-line treatments for uncomplicated malaria [3], in addition to artemether-lumefantrine (AL). Other studies have reported on the efficacy of ACT in Ghana and Africa for various age groups [6–9]. Ghana remains a high-burden, high-impact country with an estimated 8 million malaria cases and 15,000 deaths in 2021, which, although representing a decline in malaria-related deaths from approximately 25,000 in 2020, is still high [2].

Poor compliance with the treatment schedule by caregivers and suboptimal medical literacy in ensuring dose adequacy in case AS-AQ is vomited by the child could reduce the efficacy of ACT and promote the emergence of drug resistance [10–12]. Many studies have shown that a significant number of infected individuals do not adhere to their medication as directed, although good adherence reduces the severity of the disease and overall deaths [10–12]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends continuous monitoring of the efficacy of antimalarials to detect the emergence of drug tolerance or resistance to antimalarials [13].

The decreased susceptibility of P. falciparum parasites to artemisinin was first noted along the Cambodian-Thai border [14, 15]. Prolonged parasite clearance time (PCT, which corresponds to a parasite clearance half-life > 5 h) was found to be strongly associated with single point mutations in the P. falciparum kelch 13 gene (pfk13) in that region. These slow-clearance parasites associated with pfk13 mutations have now been detected throughout Southeast Asia from southern Vietnam to central Myanmar [16, 17].

Treatment of uncomplicated malaria is one of the essential interventions in the Global Technical Strategy for Malaria (2016–2030), as it delays the progression to severe illness and mortality [18]. The emergence of parasites resistant to the primary pharmaceutical-based compounds for ACT now threatens this recent progress in reducing the malaria burden worldwide, particularly in Ghana, where between 2005 and 2015, malaria incidence and deaths fell by nearly 50% and 65%, respectively [18].

The C580Y and R561H mutations in the propeller domain of pfk13 linked to resistance to artemisinin in Southeast Asia have been reported in studies across Africa and isolates from Chinese migrants travelling from Ghana [19–22]. Uwimana et al. [22] in 2021 reported on the emergence of the R561H resistant marker in samples collected in 2018 that was associated with delayed parasite clearance, while in 2024, Awor et al. [23] reported the occurrence of the C469Y resistance marker, which was associated with in vitro resistance to ACT.

Plasmodium falciparum resistance to amodiaquine is associated with the K76T mutation in the P. falciparum chloroquine resistance transporter (pfcrt) gene and the YYY (86Y + Y184 + 1246Y) P. falciparum multidrug resistance-1 (pfmdr1) haplotype in the African population [24, 25]. Lumefantrine, when combined with artemether, serves as a primary treatment for malaria in Ghana. The NFD haplotype (N86 + 184F + D1246) has been associated with decreased sensitivity to lumefantrine [26, 27].

Although several studies have been conducted on ACT, especially AS-AQ and AL, and these studies have reported high cure rates, the emergence of artemisinin resistance, as already described, calls for constant surveillance [6, 7, 28–31]. This study assessed the efficacy of AS-AQ administered via directly observed therapy (DOT) for the treatment of uncomplicated P. falciparum malaria in children aged 2–11 years as most prescribed antimalarials in children at the time of conducting the reported study. It also examined the occurrence of P. falciparum resistance markers in the Asante Akim Municipality, a holoendemic area of Ghana.

Methods

Study design

The study was a prospective single-arm open label trial to evaluate the clinical, parasitological, and haematological outcomes of DOT for uncomplicated P. falciparum malaria with AS-AQ in children aged 2–11 years in the Asante Akim North Municipality, Ghana.

Study settings

The study was conducted between July 2018 and June 2019 at the pediatric outpatient departments of two healthcare facilities in the Asante Akim North Municipality, Ghana. The municipality is located within Ghana’s moist semi-deciduous forest zone. It has tropical weather, with two wet seasons dominating the woodland regions [32]. Malaria is considered holoendemic with perennial transmission in the Municipality. The Juansa Health Centre and Agogo Presbyterian Hospital were the healthcare facilities (Fig. 1). The Juansa Health Centre, located between Konongo-Odumase and Agogo, serves approximately 15,000 residents (Fig. 2). It has a 12-bed capacity and is run by physician assistants, nurses, and dispensary technicians to provide routine check-ups, preventive care, and treatment for common illnesses. The Agogo Presbyterian Hospital provides general, specialized, and referral services to residents in Agogo-Hwidiem township, surrounding communities, and other residents of the Ashanti Region, as well as the neighboring countries of Togo, Côte d’Ivoire, and Burkina Faso for ophthalmological care. It serves a population of approximately 81,186 with a 250-bed capacity [33]. These facilities were selected because they provide a comprehensive range of primary care services and prioritize routine check-ups, which are essential for the early detection of potential health issues. Furthermore, these healthcare centres are equipped to diagnose and treat common illnesses, ensuring that patients receive timely and appropriate care for acute conditions.

Study population

The initial age target at the start of the study was children from 2 to 5 years old; however, the age group had to be increased through an amendment due to a low recruitment rate despite recruiting in two facilities. Hence, the study screened children aged 2–11 years with uncomplicated malaria confirmed by malaria rapid diagnostic test (RDT), and presented with fever or a history of fever indicative of malaria during the past 24 h.

Patients with anaemia (defined as a haemoglobin level below 8 g/dL), severe malnutrition as defined by WHO standard growth charts, known hypersensitivity and contraindications to any AS-AQ components, intake of any antimalarial drug three days prior or residing outside the study towns were excluded. Informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians, and informed assent was obtained from participating children aged 7 years and above, prior to the start of any study procedure.

Sample size

A convenience sampling method was employed with the recommendation by the WHO of a minimum sample size of 73 for a medicine with an expected failure rate of 5%, a confidence level of 95% and a precision level of 5% and an additional 20% to account for withdrawals, lost-to-follow-ups and exclusions after recruitment.

Patient enrollment, treatment, and follow-up

Potential participants were medically screened for eligibility. After obtaining capillary blood samples, an RDT (CareStat™) for malaria was performed on the samples. Thick and thin blood films were prepared and microscopically examined for malaria parasites. Body weight and height were recorded, and nutritional status was assessed using the MUAC scale. On day 0, after screening, 2 ml of blood was collected from patients who tested positive by RDT, into an EDTA tube for full blood count analysis and further molecular investigation of drug resistance markers.

Weight-based guidelines and a 3-day regimen of standard dose (25 mg/67.5 mg for 4.5–8 kg; 50 mg/135 mg for 9–17 kg; 100 mg/270 mg for 18–36 kg) [18] were followed to administer AS-AQ (Winthrop®) to participants under DOT.

Full doses were re-administered if the patient spat out or vomited the medications within 30 min. A participant was withdrawn from the study if the re-administered medication was vomited. All excluded participants were given alternative treatment and care.

Clinical and laboratory assessments were performed on days 0, 1, 2, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28. Primary caregivers were strongly encouraged to visit the study facilities whenever the child experienced a febrile illness or adverse effects. Active follow-up visits were conducted by study team members at participants' homes on days 1, 2, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 after starting antimalarial drug treatment.

At each visit, thick and thin blood films were prepared and stained with 10% Giemsa working solution and read independently by two experienced microscopists to monitor the parasite clearance rates. Hemoglobin (Hb) concentrations were also measured, and blood samples (50 μl) were spotted on filter paper (DBS; Perkin Elmer).

The treatment outcome was classified according to the WHO recommendation [32] as adequate Clinical and Parasitological Response (ACPR), early treatment failure (ETF), late clinical failure (LTF), and or late parasitological failure (LPF). An adverse event was defined as any undesirable medical occurrence in a patient during the study, regardless of its relation to treatment.

Baseline dried blood spots (DBS) were shipped to MalariaGEN for whole genome sequencing (WGS). The sequence reads were analyzed using a locally developed Nextflow workflow to detect single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the pfk13, pfcrt and pfmdr1 genes. Demultiplexed fastq files were cleaned using the fastp [33] program to trim reads of length less than 100 base pairs and remove bases with quality scores less than 15 and aligned with Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (bwa mem) [34] to the PF3D7 reference genome. Variant sites were called using bcftools [35], and annotation was performed using snpEff [36].

Data management and statistical analysis

Data were recorded in the Case Report Forms of the respective participants and doubly entered into and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at the Bernhard-Nocht Institute of Tropical Medicine [37, 38]. Data were analyzed using R software [39]. The primary treatment outcome, ACPR at day 28 was analysed using Kaplan–Meier (KM) and modified intention-to-treat (mITT) methods (includes all participants who received all doses of study treatment, had confirmed infection by microscopy, and completed at least one follow-up). The efficacy analysis included only participants who satisfactorily completed the three-day treatment regimen of AS-AQ by DOT. Secondary treatment outcomes investigated were patterns of fever and parasitaemia over the first four days and the median differences between pre-treatment and post-treatment (days 7, 14 and 28) Hb concentrations. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to compare medians, and the Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used to compare proportions. The binomial exact test was used to compute percentage confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Of all the children screened for malaria infection within one year at the two recruitment sites, 198 were enrolled. At recruitment, 25 (12.6%) participants were determined to be negative for malaria parasite infection by microscopy (subsequently confirmed by PCR), although they were diagnosed as positive by RDT. These were subsequently excluded from the analysis.

Of the 173 participants who were positive by microscopy at day 0, the number of males among the study participants was 102 (59.0%). At recruitment, the median age was 5.4 years [IQR: 3.9–7.6] with a median temperature and Hb concentration of 37.8 °C [IQR: 36.9–38.9] and 10.5 mg/dL [IQR: 9.6–11.6] respectively. The baseline temperature and parasite density were slightly higher in participants under 5 years compared to the older age group, with a median of 38.2 °C versus 37.6 °C (p = 0.025) and a geometric mean of 113,695 parasites per µL versus 39,415 parasites per µL, respectively (p = 0.003) (Table 1).

Overall, 14 participants vomited within 30 min of AS-AQ administration: six on day 0, four on day 1, and four on day 2. Of these 14 participants, three were further excluded from the efficacy analysis because they vomited repeat doses within 30 min of direct observation. The proportion of participants who vomited per age category was not significantly different (p = 0.3) (Supplementary Table 1, Additional file 1). Forty-five participants reported health complaints during the drug administration period. The most frequent symptoms were associated with the gastrointestinal tract system (n = 25; 55.6%), followed by headaches (n = 11; 24.4%) (Supplementary Table 2, Additional file 1). Twenty-six participants had health complaints over the follow-up period, with the majority being fever (n = 7; 26.9%) and gastrointestinal tract-related complaints (n = 6; 23.1%) (Supplementary Table 2, Additional file 1). The proportion of health complaints among the participants over the study period per age category was not significantly different (p > 0.9) (Supplementary Table 2, Additional file 1). A drop in the median Hb concentration from 10.55 g/dL on day 0 to 9.85 g/dL on day 7 (p = 9.39e-07 was observed). At day 14, most participants had recovered baseline Hb concentration (10.40 g/dL; p = 0.146), and a continuous increase was recorded at day 28 (11.35 g/dL; p = 1.50e-06) (Fig. 3).

No parasitaemia was recorded after the completion of treatment on day 2. However, less than 3% of participants still experienced fever (Fig. 4). Participants who had achieved ACPR on day 28 were 151 (92.07%; 95% CI 87–96%) based on the mITT population. The cure rate on day 28, as estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, was 100%. Thirteen (7.9%) participants were either lost to follow-up, non-compliant or withdrew voluntarily. Only one mutation, A578S, was identified in the BTB/POZ or propeller domain of the pfk13 gene, occurring in three (1.9%) isolates. Other mutations observed outside BTB/POZ and the propeller domain are shown in Supplementary Table 4, Additional file 1.

The other mutations were found in one sample each (Supplementary Table 5, Additional file 1). Forty-two harboured the haplotype pfmdr1 NFD (N86 + 184F + D1246), 112 isolates had the wild-type NYD, two had the YFD haplotype, and 1 had the YYY haplotype (Table 3).

Discussion

This study assessed the efficacy of AS-AQ by DOT in children aged 2–11 years and the presence of resistance markers for ACT. Efficacy studies are recommended by the WHO to be conducted in children under five. However, malaria burden has shifted towards older children according to the 2019 Ghana Malaria Indicator Survey [40], with a decrease from 21% in 2016 to 14% in 2019. A study by Dosoo et al. [41] confirms a higher incidence among older children (5–10 years) compared to their younger counterparts.

Overall, AS-AQ showed high efficacy in clearing malaria parasites, similar to other studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa [6, 29, 31]. No treatment, clinical or parasitological failures were reported within the study. This is comparable with other ACT efficacy studies conducted in the country [6, 7, 42] that reported cure proportions above the > 90% WHO threshold. The study findings demonstrate an ACPR of 92.07% by the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) analysis and 100% by the Kaplan–Meier method on day 28. The difference in the reported percentages can be attributed to how both methods operate. While both include data from all participants, the Kaplan–Meier method accounts for each participant only up to their last day of observation, provided they were not classified as treatment failures. In contrast, the mITT approach includes all participants in the denominator if they received all doses of the study treatment, had a confirmed infection by microscopy, and completed at least one follow-up visit.

Notably, no instance of clinical or parasitological failure was reported. The slight variation in the overall percentage is attributed to participant withdrawals and lost to follow-ups rather than treatment ineffectiveness. The mITT and KM methods both exhibited high treatment efficacy, which was consistent with WHO guidelines [32], despite the difference in cure rates. The general consistency between the two approaches lends support to the overall reliability of the treatment outcome.

The WHO recommends restricting antimalarial treatment to patients with positive malaria test results [43]. This is to help prevent the emergence of resistance due to the indiscriminate use of antimalarial medications [13, 44]. Consequently, the 12.6% false-positive rate observed in our study when comparing microscopy results to RDT is concerning. A similar rate of false positives has been reported previously [45]. In high transmission areas, reliance on HRP2-based RDTs for fever management may contribute to diagnostic inaccuracies and inappropriate treatment. One possible explanation for this finding is the persistence of pfHRP-2 antigens in the bloodstream, even after successful treatment, which has been documented in earlier research [46–52]. The CareStat™ RDT kits used in this study have a reported sensitivity of 98% (95% CI: 95.26–100) and specificity of 97.5% (95% CI: 95.34–99.66). Nevertheless, the false-positive rate observed aligns with the WHO’s recommended threshold of up to 10% for RDT procurement [51]. While the observed inaccuracy raises concerns, alternative explanations should be considered, such as partial antimalarial treatment before hospital presentation or improper storage conditions of RDT kits between manufacturing and use. Further targeted studies are needed to explore and address these potential contributing factors.

Fever is one of the most common symptoms of malaria and is a significant inclusion criterion in malaria treatment efficacy trials. Fever usually begins when the malaria parasite invades a red blood cell, causing the cell to rupture [10]. All participants recruited had an axillary temperature of ≥ 37.5 °C or a history of fever within the previous 24 h, and the fever resolved quickly after the first dosage of AS-AQ. This finding is consistent with several other studies that indicate the ability of ACT to lower fever quickly [53]. Adverse effects were reported, with only 14 participants receiving a re-administration after vomiting within thirty minutes, of which four vomited the repeated dose. This indicates a general tolerance to AS-AQ, as described by [29], confirming its suitability.

WGS analysis of the pfk13 gene revealed no mutations associated with artemisinin resistance or delayed parasite clearance. This finding aligns with the observed absence of delayed clearance or early recrudescence in the study population. Previous reports of the pfk13 C580Y mutation—linked to artemisinin resistance—identified in Ghana or in isolates believed to have been imported from China [19, 20] were not confirmed in our study. In contrast, the pfk13 A578S mutation was detected at low prevalence, consistent with earlier reports from Ghana [54] and across sub-Saharan Africa [55]. Although large surveys across Asia and Africa have not established an association between the A578S mutation and delayed parasite clearance or treatment failure with artemisinin-based therapies [56, 57], structural studies have suggested that the alanine-to-serine substitution at codon 578 could cause minimal backbone fluctuations and may impose a fitness cost on the parasite. These findings warrant further investigation into the potential clinical relevance of the A578S variant.

The pfcrt 76 T allele is selected in reinfection following treatment with amodiaquine, while the pfmdr1 86Y allele is selected in recrudescence following amodiaquine treatment [58, 59]. However, none of the participants harboring isolates with these alleles at recruitment had any reinfection or recrudescence within the 28 days of follow-up. The low prevalence of the 76 T allele may suggest that parasites are losing their resistance to chloroquine. Njiro et al. noted a significant shift in pfcrt 76 T alleles following the withdrawal of chloroquine, shedding light on the complex dynamics of resistance evolution. This suggests a high decline in Africa due to the withdrawal of chloroquine and reduced administration of amodiaquine in Africa. Continuous and robust surveillance is needed to confirm the absence of this mutation in African regions and the possible fitness cost to the parasite [60]. The pfmdr1 gene is associated with resistance to artemisinin partner drugs such as amodiaquine and lumefantrine. In our study, the NFD haplotype was the most prevalent, detected in 42.8% of samples at baseline. This haplotype has been previously shown to be selected following reinfection after treatment with artemether-lumefantrine [24, 27, 58]. The rising prevalence of the NFD haplotype in Ghana is therefore concerning, particularly given the widespread use of ACT with lumefantrine for the treatment of uncomplicated malaria [59, 60]. In contrast, the YYY haplotype, typically selected following AS-AQ treatment, was detected only once. This is not unexpected, as although multi-first-line therapy (MFT) is recommended in Ghana, AL remains the most used treatment due to its lower incidence of side effects [1]. However, the widespread occurrence of the NFD haplotype raises concerns about the potential erosion of lumefantrine’s efficacy as a partner drug in ACT regimens, which will be translated by an increasing case of reinfection after AL treatment.

Conclusions

This study has shown that AS-AQ remains highly efficacious in clearing malaria parasites in children 2–11 years of age in the study area in Ghana. Mutations associated with the pfk13 gene were not detected. The high prevalence of NFD haplotype (pfmdr1) associated with the selection of reinfection after AL treatment should be closely monitored, and local health authorities should reinforce the guidelines of MFT. Continuous surveillance of antimalarials on a larger scale is needed for early detection and to limit the spread of resistance.
