Identification of RUNX1 as a Biomarker for Right Ventricular Remodeling in Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension Using WGCNA
Authors: Wenjie Chen, Min Peng, Yusi Chen, Jun Luo, Jingyuan Chen, Haihua Qiu, Jiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRight ventricle (RV) dysfunction is central to pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) yet its molecular mechanisms remain unclear. This study aimed to identify biomarkers and therapeutic targets for RV remodeling in PAH. The dataset GSE240941 have 39 RV tissue samples, including normal RV, compensated RV (comRV), and decompensated RV (decomRV). Differential analysis revealed no significant gene differences between comRV and normal RV tissues but identified 2384 different expression genes between decomRV and normal RV tissues. Weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (WGCNA) highlighted the Coral2 module as strongly associated with decomRV remodeling (cor = 0.51, p = 0.01). Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analyses of Coral2 module identified potential mechanisms and 78 hub genes (|MM | > 0.80 and |GS | > 0.50). Among these hub genes, CDK2, ITGB3, and RUNX1 emerged as key candidates through further filtering and CytoHubba analysis. While all three genes showed elevated expression in the training set, only RUNX1 was significantly upregulated in the validation dataset (GSE120852). RT‐qPCR and western blot from RV tissue of monocrotaline‐induced pulmonary hypertension rats showed that RUNX1 was significantly elevated in RV tissue. Immunofluorescence indicated that RUNX1 was primarily localized in the nucleus. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis demonstrated the ability of CDK2, ITGB3, and RUNX1 to differentiate decomRV remodeling in training dataset and validating dataset. Based on the results of WGCNA and preliminary validation from animal experiments, RUNX1 may be a potential biomarker for RV failure in the context of PAH.Keywords: biomarkers, pulmonary arterial hypertension, right ventricular dysfunction, right ventricular remodeling, WGCNA

Introduction

Right ventricular (RV) dysfunction can result from a variety of underlying clinical etiologies [1]. Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is characterized by increased pulmonary vascular pressures, ultimately leading to a significant rise in RV afterload and RV failure [2]. Due to the unclear molecular mechanisms underlying RV remodeling, therapeutic strategies remain limited, contributing to the poor prognosis observed in PAH patients [3, 4]. Therefore, early identification of RV remodeling in PAH and exploration of its underlying mechanisms are of significant clinical importance.

In recent years, bioinformatics methods have become widely used and have proven to be reliable tools for identifying potential treatment targets [5]. Weighted Gene Co‐expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) is a systems biology approach used to analyze high‐throughput gene expression data. By calculating the co‐expression correlation between genes, WGCNA can identify gene modules and key driver genes (hub genes) associated with specific phenotypes. The strength of WGCNA lies in its ability to not only identify genes significantly linked to phenotypes but also to group functionally related genes into “modules,” which often share common biological functions. This makes WGCNA widely applied in biomedical research, including studies on cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and rare diseases [6, 7, 8].

In this study, we constructed a WGCNA using the GEO dataset to identify key gene modules associated with RV remodeling. We then explored the potential mechanisms underlying RV remodeling through Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses. The RUNX1 was found to be present in key modules related to RV remodeling and further evaluated using an independent dataset and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Subsequently, RUNX1 was preliminarily validated as a biomarker for right ventricular failure in the RV tissue of monocrotaline‐induced PH (MCT‐PH) rats through quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR) and western blot. Our findings may offer a potential therapeutic target for right heart failure in PAH.

Methods

Data Selection

High‐throughput sequencing datasets GSE240941 and GSE120852 were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). GSE240941 contains 40 RV tissue from normal controls (n = 13), compensated RV (comRV) remodeling patients (n = 14; 1 sample missing expression profiles, GSM7712117), and decompensated RV (decomRV) remodeling patients (n = 13). In brief, participants with RV dilation, preserved cardiac idex (> 2.2 L/min/m2)), or normal (≥ 17 mm) tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion measured by echocardiography were considered as compensated hypertrophy, whereas PAH participants who received a lung transplantation or who died with RV failure were considered as those with decompensated RV. GSE120852 includes left ventricular (LV) (n = 5) and RV tissues (n = 5) from normal controls, LV (n = 5) and RV tissues (n = 5) from LV heart failure patients, and LV (n = 5) and RV tissues (n = 5) from biventricular heart failure patients. For our study, we selected the RV expression matrices from normal controls (n = 5) and biventricular heart failure patients (n = 5) in GSE120852 as the validation dataset to further explore candidate marker genes associated with RV remodeling. The group, tissue type, and the platforms used for bioinformatics analysis are provided in Tables S1‐S2. The workflow for bioinformatics analysis in our study is illustrated in Figure 1.

Data Preprocessing and Screening of DEGs

Data analysis was performed using R software (version 4.4.1). Clinical information for the samples was extracted using the pData function, and RV tissues were divided into three comparison groups: comRV remodeling versus normal controls (group 1), decomRV remodeling versus normal controls (group 2) and decomRV remodeling versus comRV remodeling (group 3). The group information was shown in Table S3.

Next, we checked the consistency of the expression matrices and applied the limma package to correct for potential batch effects. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the limma package. DEGs were selected based on the following criteria: |log2FoldChange | > 1 and an adjusted p‐value < 0.05.

GO and KEGG Functional Enrichment Analysis

The ‘clusterProfiler’ package was used to perform functional enrichment analysis. An adjusted p value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

WGCNA Analysis

The median absolute deviation (MAD) of each gene's expression level was calculated to assess its variability [9]. The variability threshold was set at 0.01, selecting genes in the top 75% of MAD values to enhance the robustness of the data and the validity of the analysis. Before performing WGCNA, we used hierarchical clustering to detect and exclude outlier samples. First, we calculated the distance matrix between samples using the hclust package and performed clustering analysis using the average method. Then, based on the visualization of the dendrogram, we selected a threshold (cutHeight = 85) for cutting to identify outlier samples. The co‐expression network was constructed in R software using the ‘WGCNA’ package. An appropriate soft threshold was chosen to ensure that the constructed network fits a scale‐free topology (R2 > 0.85). Hierarchical clustering and the dynamic tree cut function were used to detect gene modules, with a minimum module size set to 50. This approach allowed for the identification of gene modules that were highly correlated with the phenotype of interest, facilitating the discovery of key gene modules involved in right ventricular remodeling.

Identification of Candidate Biomarker Genes

Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated for module characteristic genes (MEs) and disease traits for each module to identify the key modules most closely related to RV remodeling. The module membership (MM) represents the correlation between MEs and gene expression profiles within each module. Gene significance (GS) was calculated for genes within the module, representing the correlation between individual genes and disease traits. Genes with high MM and GS within key modules are highly correlated with the disease traits.

After selecting the key modules, genes with |MM | > 0.8 and |GS | > 0.50 were defined as hub genes in the key modules. To further refine the identification of hub genes, the ‘Cytoscape’ plugin, ‘CytoHubba’, was used to calculate two different metrics—eccentricity and bottleneck—helping to identify the top 30 hub genes. Candidate marker genes were selected based on the overlap of genes identified using the three methods described above.

Validation of Candidate Marker Genes and ROC Curve Analysis

The expression of candidate marker genes was assessed in both the training and validation datasets. ROC curve analysis was conducted using the “pROC” package.

Right Ventricular Tissue and RT‐qPCR

RV tissues were derived from MCT‐PH rats and control rats previously established by our team. The MCT‐PH model was established by a single intraperitoneal injection of monocrotaline at a dose of 60 mg/kg, while the control group received an equal volume of PBS. RV tissue was collected 4 weeks after the first injection of MCT. At this time, MCT‐PH rats usually show significant RV remodeling [10, 11].

We purchased an RNA Animal Tissue Extraction Kit from Beyotime (R0026, shanghai, China) to extract total RNA from RV tissues, and then cDNA was synthesized according to the procedures described previously via ABScript Neo RT master mix (RK20433, ABclonal Technology Co., China). The cDNA was then amplified by BrightCycle Universal SYBR Green qPCR MIX with UDG (RK21219, ABclonal Technology Co., China) in Real‐Time PCR System (Bio‐RAD co., American). We normalized the RT‐qPCR results against β‐actin and reported them as 2–ΔΔCt. The primer sequences utilized were listed in Table S4.

Western Blot

Total protein was extracted from rat right ventricular tissue. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Samples (10 μg of protein per well) were separated by 10% SDS‐PAGE and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The membrane was blocked for 1 h in Tris‐buffered saline with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (TBS‐T), followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies against RUNX1 (A2055, Abclonal, 1:1000, China) and GAPDH (1:10000, Cat. 10494‐1‐AP, Proteintech, China). The membrane was then incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody for 1 h. Western blot results were quantified using densitometry.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence staining, sections were prepared from formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded samples. Deparaffinization and rehydration were performed by treating the sections with xylene, followed by washes with 100%, 85%, and 75% ethanol for 5 min each. The sections were then rinsed in water for 5 min. To retrieve antigens, the sections were placed in boiling sodium citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) for 2 min. After natural cooling, the sections were washed three times with PBS, each for 5 min. The sections were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide solution at room temperature, protected from light, for 25 min. After incubation, the sections were incubated with the primary antibody (RUNX1, A2055, Abclonal, 1:100, China) at 4°C overnight. Following incubation, the sections were washed three times with PBS, each for 5 min. Then, the sections were incubated with a fluorescence‐labeled secondary antibody (SeraCare, 5220‐0336, 1:400, USA) at room temperature, protected from light, for 1 h. Afterward, the sections were washed three times with PBS, each for 5 min, and mounted with DAPI.

Statistical Analysis

An unpaired t‐test was performed for the expression of hub gene between the two groups using GraphPadPrism (version 9.0). A p‐value < 0.05 is significant.

Results

Data Preprocessing and DEGs Screening

The dataset GSE240941, consisting of 39 samples, was divided into three comparison groups: Group 1, Group 2, and Group3. The results of the differential expression analysis showed no significant DEGs in Group 1, 2384 DEGs were identified in Group 2, while 123 genes in Group3 (Table S5). As no differentially expressed genes were detected in group 1 and only a few were identified in group 3, it indicates that comRV and decomRV may exhibit substantial similarity. Therefore, the objective of this study was to distinguish normal RV from remodeled RV, and thus Group 2 was used as the training dataset. The heatmap and volcano plots of Group 2 are shown in Figure 2.

GO and KEGG Analysis of DEGs

The most significant biological process (BP) terms were enriched in the adenylate cyclase‐modulating G protein‐coupled receptor signaling pathway, sodium ion transmembrane transport, and potassium ion transport. The cellular component (CC) terms were mainly associated with the external side of the plasma membrane, monoatomic ion channel complex, and transmembrane transporter complex. The most enriched molecular function (MF) terms were associated with metal ion transmembrane transporter activity, monoatomic ion channel activity, and gated channel activity (Figure 3A‐C). In the KEGG analysis, the DEGs were enriched in Neuroactive ligand‐receptor interaction, cytokine‐cytokine receptor interaction, and motor proteins (Figure 3D).

Construction of WGCNA and Identification of Key Modules

14,562 genes were identified and used as input for the WGCNA network construction. Sample clustering identified 3 outlier samples, and finally retained 23 samples (Figure 4A). The optimal soft threshold (β = 14), determined by the “sft$powerEstimate” function, constructed a scale‐free network (Figure 4B). BThirteen gene modules were identified, and the hierarchical clustering of genes along with the corresponding module colors are depicted in the dendrogram in Figure 4C.

WGCNA network construction of genes with the top 75% MAD in dataset GSE240941. Construction of WGCNA network. A: Detection and removal of outliers. B: Suitable soft threshold powers selection, the red line indicating 0.85. C: The cluster dendrogram of genes in the WGCNA network. MAD, median absolute deviation; WGCNA, Weighted gene co‐expression network analysis.

Our study focused exclusively on the relationship between decomRV remodeling and the gene modules. Pearson correlation analysis revealed that the Coral 2 module showed the most significant correlation with decomRV remodeling (cor = 0.51, p = 0.01) (Figure 5A). The correlation results of GS and module connectivity indicated that the Coral 2 module is a key module related to decomRV remodeling (Figure 5B).

Identification of the key module related to decomRV in the dataset GSE240941. A: Correlations between the gene module and decomRV. B: Scatterplot of connectivity versus gene significance for decomRV of coral2 modul. C: Scatterplot of gene significance for decomRV versus module membership in the coral2 module. D: Scatterplot of intramodular connectivity versus module membership in the coral2 module; decomRV, decompensated right ventricular tissue.

MM and GS for each module were calculated to verify the correlation between decomRV remodeling and the Coral 2 module. The Coral 2 module showed a strong correlation with decomRV remodeling (cor = 0.46, p < 2.9e^−69) (Figure 5C). The correlation between MM and intramodular connectivity also suggested a high correlation between the Coral 2 module and decomRV remodeling (cor = 0.75, p < 2e‐16) (Figure 5D). These results indicate that genes within the Coral 2 module are strongly associated with decomRV remodeling.

GO and KEGG Analysis of Key Modules

The Coral 2 module serves as the key module for functional enrichment analysis. GO enrichment analysis revealed that BP terms are predominantly enriched in leukocyte chemotaxis, cell chemotaxis, and leukocyte migration. CC terms were primarily enriched in the collagen‐containing extracellular matrix, endocytic vesicle lumen, and the external side of the plasma membrane. MF terms were mainly enriched in cytokine receptor activity, integrin binding, and protease binding (Figure 6A‐C). Additionally, KEGG pathway analysis indicated significant enrichment in the TNF signaling pathway, viral protein interaction with cytokines, and cytokine‐receptor interactions (Figure 6D).

Identification and Validation of Hub Genes

Based on the threshold criteria (|MM | > 0.8 and |GS | > 0.5), 78 hub genes were identified. The top 500 genes from the key module were imported into Cytoscape. CytoHubba, a Cytoscape plugin, was employed to identify the top 30 hub genes using the ‘Bottleneck’ and ‘eCCentricity’ algorithms for biological network analysis. Overlapping candidate marker genes selected by these three methods were used to identify potential marker genes. Three overlapping candidate marker genes, CDK2, ITGB3, and RUNX1, may play significant roles in decompensated right ventricular remodeling (Figure 7A). Although CDK2 and ITGB3 were significantly elevated in decompensated RV (decomRV) of the training dataset (GSE240941), no significant difference was observed in the validation dataset (GSE120852) (Figure S1A‐D). In contrast, RUNX1 was consistently elevated in both the training and validation datasets (Figure 7B and C). Therefore, RUNX1 was further validated as a potential biomarker for RV failure.

We successfully established the MCT‐PH animal model to simulate RV failure, characterized by significantly increased right ventricular systolic pressure (p = 0.02) and right ventricular hypertrophy index (RV/(LV + S), p = 0.0001) in the MCT‐PH group compared to the control group (Figure 7D‐F). Additionally, immunofluorescence showed that RUNX1 was almost undetectable in the RV tissue of the control group, but its expression was significantly increased in the RV tissue of MCT‐PH (Figure 7G). Further RT‐qPCR (Figure 7H) and Western blot (Figure 7I) consistently showed an increase in RUNX1 in the RV tissue of MCT‐PH (p = 0.02 and p = 0.04, respectively).

ROC Curve Analysis of Hub Genes

ROC curve analysis was performed to validate the potential marker genes and assess their diagnostic ability of decomRV remodeling by calculating the area under the curve (AUC). In the training dataset, CDK2 (AUC = 0.811), ITGB3 (AUC = 0.811), and RUNX1 (AUC = 0.751) demonstrated good predictive performance (Figure 8A). The validation results from the GSE120852 dataset further confirmed these findings, with CDK2 (AUC = 0.760), ITGB3 (AUC = 0.800), and RUNX1 (AUC = 0.920) also showing strong predictive ability (Figure 8B).

Discussion

RV function is crucial for the prognosis of patients with various types of PAH [12, 13]. In the early stages of PAH, comRV hypertrophy occurs, and patients typically show no symptoms or only exercise‐induced dyspnea. As the disease progresses, the compensated right ventricle transforms into a decompensated right ventricle. The differential analysis of the GSE240941 dataset reflects these clinical characteristics. Compared to the normal controls, no significant DEGs were detected in comRV patients, while 2384 DEGs were identified in the decomRV remodeling patients.

Early identification of decomRV remodeling and the discovery of potential therapeutic targets are crucial. Currently, the treatment of PAH lacks effective drugs to reverse RV remodeling [14]. Through WGCNA, we identified key gene modules and hub genes associated with decomRV remodeling. Three candidate marker genes—CDK2, ITGB3, and RUNX1—were identified. In the GSE240941 dataset, CDK2, ITGB3, and RUNX1 showed significant upregulation in decomRV remodeling. Due to differences in sample selection and microarray platforms, gene expression levels can vary between datasets. Therefore, not all candidate marker genes can be verified in independent datasets. In the GSE120852 dataset, CDK2 and ITGB3 did not show significant differences, while RUNX1 was significantly upregulated. Its expression was further verified by qRT‐PCR and western blot in MCT‐induced rat PH.

Although not validated in the GSE120852 dataset, CDK2 and ITGB3 are also involved in cardiac remodeling in cardiovascular disease. The CDK2 (cyclin‐dependent kinase 2) gene encodes a catalytic subunit of the cyclin‐dependent kinase complex. It is crucial for DNA replication and for cells entering the S phase, and it also participates in cell cycle‐independent functions, such as DNA damage repair [15]. It is well known that CDK2 plays an important role in cellular senescence and cancer therapy [16]. In heart failure, studies have found a link between CDK2 and impaired autophagic flux as well as AMP‐activated protein kinases, and inhibition of CDK2 reduced cardiac remodeling in mice with heart failure [17, 18]. In addition, Xia et al. found that CDK2 is involved in doxorubicin‐induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis and cardiomyopathy [19].

The process of RV remodeling is accompanied by myocardial hypertrophy, myocardial fibrosis, and extracellular matrix deposition [20]. Integrins are cell surface glycoproteins composed of α and β subunits, and are involved in cell migration, differentiation, and signal transduction [21]. ITGB3 (integrin beta‐3) is a member of the integrin family and plays a key role in the cardiac stress response [22]. Research has shown that in a myocardial hypertrophy model, ubiquitination is enhanced in cardiomyocytes [23], and ITGB3, by mediating the transient ubiquitination of target proteins, facilitates the transduction of survival signals [24]. Cardiac fibroblasts from ITGB3 (‐/‐) mice showed impaired migration and proliferation capabilities. In vitro experiments suggested that ITGB3 mediates the non‐receptor tyrosine kinase Pyk2 signaling pathway to participate in cardiac fibrosis [25]. In the acute myocardial infarction (AMI) model, ITGB3 may play a crucial role in cardiac regeneration. Inhibiting ITGB3 reduced attachment, retention, and therapeutic benefits of human cardiospheres in mice with AMI [26].

RUNX1 (Runt‐related transcription factor 1), also known as acute myeloid leukemia 1 protein (AML1), plays a critical role in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival across multiple systems. Although RUNX1 is expressed at relatively low levels in the normal adult heart, its expression increases under stress conditions and is closely associated with adverse cardiac remodeling [27, 28, 29, 30]. Runx1‐deficient mice were protected against adverse cardiac remodeling after MI, which might be explained by the effect of RUNX1 on calcium homeostasis in cardiomyocytes [28]. In a zebrafish heart regeneration study, RUNX1 was found to inhibit the proliferation and survival of cardiomyocytes, and the absence of RUNX1 alleviated this damage [31]. More recently, Qi et al. found that inhibition of RUNX1 facilitates heart failure progression by regulating TGF‐β‐induced cardiac remodeling in the transverse aortic constriction mouse model [32]. We preliminarily validated the increased expression of RUNX1 in the right ventricular tissue of MCT‐PH (n = 3 vs 3) through RT‐qPCR and Western blot. Based on the above evidence and our results, RUNX1 may be a potential biomarker for RV failure in the context of PAH. Interestingly, RUNX1 is also involved in PAH and may be a novel target for the disease. Euy‐Myoung Jeong et al. treated rats with specific RUNX1 inhibitors before, during, and after the development of SuHx‐induced PH and found that RUNX1 inhibition could prevent the onset, progression, and reversal of established SuHx‐PH [33]. Additionally, RUNX1 knockdown in a mouse model of PH improved hemodynamics and pulmonary vascular remodeling, while inhibiting the proliferation, invasion, and migration of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells [34].

In this study, several limitations should be noted. Firstly, our validation dataset comes from patients with biventricular heart failure, which makes it difficult to accurately simulate RV failure caused by PAH. Due to the difficulty in obtaining samples from RV failure patients, we validated the expression level of RUNX1 by establishing an MCT‐PH rat model to simulate RV failure. Additionally, the sample size of the validation dataset is relatively small, which may lead to overfitting and instability in model evaluation during statistical analysis. Therefore, further validation with more samples is needed to confirm RUNX1 as a specific biomarker for RV failure under the context of PAH. Finally, we did not explore the dynamic changes and potential mechanisms of RUNX1 during the development of RV failure. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses indicate that inflammatory factors and extracellular matrix may be potential mechanisms by which RUNX1 promotes the development of RV failure.

Conclusion

Based on transcriptomic data, the increase in RUNX1 in the rRV tissue of human PAH and biventricular heart failure is consistent with the changes observed in our MCT‐PH rat model. Enrichment analysis of the key modules in RV failure focused on the functional annotation of immune cells and cytokines. These results suggest that RUNX1 may be involved in RV failure in the context of PAH through inflammation and could serve as a potential biomarker for this condition.
