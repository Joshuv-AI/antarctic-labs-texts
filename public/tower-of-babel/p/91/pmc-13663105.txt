Development of an RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a fluorescent detection method for the detection of bovine viral diarrhea virus 1
Authors: Youli Yu, Wei He, YunNan Fang, Jiandong Wang, Zhengqing Yu, Yanan Guo, Yuxi Zhao, Haihui Gao, Pan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBovine viral diarrhea virus (BVDV) is a viral pathogen that may cause reproductive disorders, health, and performance. BVDV serotypes have undergone dynamic changes due to sustained genetic diversity, particularly in BVDV type 1 (BVDV1), which has compromised the specificity and sensitivity of diagnostic assays. Furthermore, BVDV1 is prevalent worldwide, causing significant losses to the livestock industry. Therefore, in order to further enhance the control and elimination of BVDV, it is necessary to detect and prevent the spread of BVDV. Therefore, establishing a faster and more accurate on-site detection method is particularly important.MethodsTraditional PCR and ELISA methods are limited by cost, sensitivity, and equipment dependence. In this study, we designed primers based on the conserved 5’Untranslated Region (5’UTR) of BVDV1 and developed a method that combines one-step reverse transcription recombinase assisted amplification (RT-RAA) and CRISPR/Cas12a based fluorescent substrates in a mixture to quickly screen and accurately distinguish BVDV1 for the diagnosis and differentiation of common bovine viruses such as Bovine Coronavirus (BCoV) and Bovine Herpesvirus type 1 (BoHV) et al. The established RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a scheme can achieve the entire inspection process in a portable metal bath at 40℃. We further optimized the reaction conditions of RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a to detect its sensitivity, specificity, and repeatability. The established RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a and conventional PCR were used to detect 300 clinical samples of cattle, verifying the actual detection ability.ResultsThis method did not show any cross-reactivity with the other nine common bovine viruses, demonstrating excellent specificity. The minimum template concentration required to trigger significant trans cleavage activity without RT-RAA amplification is 10 copies/µL of BVDV template. However, combined with RT‑RAA amplification, the detection limit was significantly decreased to one copy of the BVDV template per reaction. In simulated sample testing, this method achieved 100% accuracy, and the entire detection process can be completed within 30 min. In clinical sample testing, RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a showed a higher sensitivity rate for BVDV1, as the positive detection rate was 1.85 times higher than that of conventional PCR.ConclusionIn summary, the BVDV-1 RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection method established in this study offers several advantages, including fast operation, high sensitivity, strong targeting, and good repeatability. Moreover, the same test tube reaction system makes it widely applicable in resource-limited environments, making it of great value in large-scale screening during BVDV outbreaks. This method not only expands the diagnostic toolbox of BVDV but also provides an up-and-coming solution for controlling the spread of BVDV in cattle herds.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05719-x.

Introduction

Bovine viral diarrhea virus (BVDV) is a non-enveloped, positive-sense, single-stranded RNA virus composed of multiple structural and non-structural proteins encoded by its genome. BVDV is a member of the genus Pestivirus of the family Flaviviridae, alongside other significant pathogens such as the hepatitis C virus and Zika virus. BVDV have been classified into three viral species: Pestivirus bovis (BVDV1), Pestivirus tauri (BVDV2) and Pestivirus brazilense (HoBi-like pestivirus, BVDV3) [1–3]. This virus is highly contagious and primarily affects cattle of all ages, and also poses a significant threat to other species of the Artiodactyl genus, including sheep, pigs, camels, and goats [4, 5], resulting in adverse effects on reproduction, animal health and performance, especially BVDV1 [6]. Typical manifestations of BVDV infection include acute diarrhea, mucosal diseases, respiratory and reproductive system diseases, immune suppression, and persistent infection [7]. BVDV infection has significant economic impacts on the livestock industry worldwide. The virus is transmitted through direct contact, contaminated feed and water, or via biological vectors such as ticks. Its ability to persist in the environment and infect naive populations underscores its potential for rapid dissemination [8–13]. Since its initial identification, BVDV outbreaks have been reported globally, including in North America, Europe, Asia, and South America, highlighting its widespread distribution and adaptability [14]. The key steps for controlling and eradicating BVDV include culling of PI animals, prophylactic vaccination, and avoiding mixed farming practices. Therefore, early diagnosis of BVDV is crucial, with the main goal of identifying and removing BVDV PI cattle from the herd. Effective diagnosis can support BVDV eradication projects and economic growth in the livestock industry.

Diagnosis of BVDV infection remains challenging due to the overlapping clinical signs with other viral and bacterial diseases. Traditional diagnostic methods, such as serological assays (e.g., Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)), virus isolation, and PCR-based techniques, have been used to detect BVDV. Virus isolation (VI) is the most reliable method for detecting BVDV and is considered the “gold standard” for BVDV diagnosis [15]. Although the VI detection method is easy to operate, its application has obvious limitations: it can only recognize and cytopathogenic (cp.) type BVDV and cannot detect non-cytopathogenic (ncp) type; the process is time-consuming and labor-intensive, making it difficult to handle large quantities of samples; and the interpretation of results is easily affected by factors such as cell sensitivity, sample preservation, and transportation [16]. While PCR offers high sensitivity, it requires precise handling to avoid cross-contamination, and serological assays may fail to detect acute infections due to the delayed antibody response in infected animals. Based on the p80 and gp53 genes encoding NS3 and E2 proteins, and the 5’UTR, respectively, built several Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) methods to identify BVDV from other bovine viruses [17–19]. Conventional PCR has insufficient sensitivity, making it difficult to detect samples with low latent viral load, and is susceptible to false negatives caused by interference from sample inhibitors. Real-time PCR is also a highly specific and sensitive method for BVDV detection [20–22]. Although PCR and qPCR have high sensitivity, they rely on expensive instruments and professional operators, making it difficult to popularize in grassroots farms. ELISA is an important tool for serological testing, used to distinguish various antigen and antibody targets [23]. At present, ELISA methods for detecting BVDV include blocking ELISA (bELISA) or competitive ELISA using NS3 protein as the recognition antigen (cELISA). ELISA is limited by the delayed antibody response in infected animals, making it unsuitable for detecting early acute infections [24, 25]. PCR and qPCR, while highly sensitive and specific, require expensive thermal cyclers, reliable electricity, and trained personnel, which restricts their use in resource-limited settings such as farms and field stations [26–28].

The CRISPR/Cas system, renowned for its sequence-specific recognition and cleavage capabilities, has emerged as a powerful tool for pathogen detection. The application of CRISPR/Cas technology to detect BVDV represents a promising avenue for overcoming the limitations of conventional diagnostic methods. Hwang et al. [29] designed specific crRNA and optimized LwCas13a protein expression by accurately targeting the 5’UTR region of BVDV-1b, and achieved visual detection by combining it with lateral flow chromatography strips; A similar system has also been established [30], achieving a detection sensitivity of 103 pM with fluorescently labeled probes. This method has outstanding advantages such as speed, sensitivity, specificity, high throughput, result visualization, and ease of on-site detection. But in the absence of nucleic acid amplification, the detection sensitivity of CRISPR-Cas varies between 0.01 and 1 nM and thus is not suitable for clinical diagnosis of pathogens [31], and PCR is commonly used for nucleic acid amplification, but it often comes with relatively high costs, longer durations, and the need for specialized equipment. In recent years, isothermal amplification technology has gradually become an important method for molecular diagnosis due to its ability to complete nucleic acid amplification at a constant temperature. Common isothermal amplification systems include loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA), and recombinase-assisted amplification (RAA) [32, 33]. These methods have the advantages of fast reaction, easy operation, and low dependence on equipment, and are suitable for laboratory conditions or rapid on-site detection needs.

This study combined RAA and CRISPR-Cas12a to develop a rapid and highly sensitive BVDV detection system. So we designed crRNA and sequence specific primers for the 5’UTR conserved region to achieve detection. The entire reaction can be completed within 30 min in a single tube at 40℃, and the sensitivity of virus RNA detection is about 10 copies/µL. Therefore, the platform has the potential for rapid, accurate, and on-site diagnosis of BVDV, and is cost-effective.

Design RAA primer and CRISPR-Cas12a crRNAs targeting BVDV

Select the highly conserved region 5’UTR sequence of BVDV1 as the detection target. Download 1135 BVDV1 (species ID: 11099) reference sequences from the NCBI Virus website in China, and exclude all sequences except for the 5’UTR sequence and complete genome sequence through bioinformatics methods, leaving 847 sequences; 847 sequences containing 5’UTR were aligned through the MAFFT online website (Fig. 1A), and highly conserved regions were selected as detection sites for RAA primer and crRNA design. Connect the 5’UTR sequence of the BVDV1 reference sequence to the PUC57 vector to synthesize a plasmid. The plasmid map is attached. As shown in Table S1, a total of 5 RAA primer sequences were obtained, resulting in 5 well-conserved primer combinations (F1R1, F1R2, F2R1, F2R2, F3R1) and 5 crRNA sequences. The target sites are shown in Fig. 1B.

Test RAA primer and CRISPR-Cas12a crRNAs targeting BVDV

We first used BVDV1 cDNA as a template for RT-PCR amplification, and the target band with a size of 209 bp was obtained (Figure S1A). Connect the target fragment to the PUC57 vector to construct a recombinant plasmid. After sequencing verification, it was named BVDV1-pUC57 plasmid standard (Figure S1B), with an initial concentration of 1.56 × 1010 copies/µL.

Using the BVDV1-pUC57 plasmid as a template, PCR amplification was performed using 6 pairs of RAA primers designed by cross-pairing (Table S1). The band size, fluorescence intensity, non-specific amplification and the formation of primer dimer of gel electrophoresis products were analyzed to screen the best primer pair. The results showed that the F1/R1 primer had the best amplification efficiency (Fig. 2), and it was selected for subsequent experiments.

Construction of the CRISPR/Cas12a detecting method

Next, we used the RAA primer F1/R1 to construct the CRISPR/Cas12a detecting system with the five designed crRNAs. This method utilizes the collateral cleavage of quenched fluorescent DNA probes, and when Cas12a/crRNA specifically recognizes its DNA target and is activated for DNA cleavage. We established the single-tube format detection method and measured the fluorescence signal for 30 min. As shown in Fig. 3, crRNA3 had the highest fluorescence value, indicating the best detection result.

Specificity and sensitivity test of RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a

To evaluate the specificity and sensitivity of the assay, we systematically tested 7 bovine pathogens, including BVDV-1, Bovine herpes virus 1 (BoHV1)、Bovine coronavirus (BCoV)、Bovine Enterovirus (BEV)、Bovine respiratory syncytial virus (BRSV)、Bovine Rotavirus (BRV)、Bovine parainfluenza virus type 3 (BPIV-3). As shown in Fig. 4 (representative experiment), only BVDV-1 elicited a pronounced fluorescence signal. Across three independent replicates (Supplementary Table S2), the mean fluorescence intensity of BVDV-1 was significantly higher than that of all other viral targets (p < 0.001, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test), and no significant difference was observed between any non-target virus and the negative control (p > 0.05). Similarly, for sensitivity testing (Fig. 5, representative experiment), the limit of detection (LOD) was determined to be 1 copy per reaction. Based on three independent replicates, the fluorescence signal at 1 copy/µL consistently exceeded the threshold (mean negative control + 3 × SD), with a coefficient of variation below 15% (Supplementary Table S2). These results demonstrate that the established RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a method has excellent specificity and high sensitivity for BVDV-1 detection.

Clinical samples detecting with RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a and RT-PCR

The established BVDV-1 RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay and a conventional RT-PCR method were applied in parallel to test 300 bovine clinical serum samples. As shown in Table 1, the RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay detected 144 positive samples, with a positivity rate of 48% (144/300). In contrast, the conventional RT-PCR method detected 78 positive samples, corresponding to a positivity rate of 26% (78/300). The detection rate of the RT-RAA-CRISPR/ Cas12a assay was 1.85 times higher (p = 0.0146) than that of the conventional RT-PCR. Furthermore, all samples identified as positive by RT-PCR were also positive by the RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a method, indicating that the RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay possesses higher sensitivity than the conventional RT-PCR.

Discussion

Bovine viral diarrhea virus type 1 (BVDV-1) is a pathogen of major economic importance to the global livestock industry. Its rapid transmission within farms complicates effective disease control and prevention [14, 16]. Consequently, early and accurate detection is critical for containing outbreaks. While reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) for viral RNA is a widely adopted, sensitive, and specific nucleic acid detection method [21], conventional techniques often rely on precise thermal cycling equipment and may lack the sensitivity required for samples with low viral loads, leading to potential false negatives [34–37]. Furthermore, the dependency of these methods on cold-chain transport, skilled personnel, and stable power supply limits their utility in field settings such as farms and border ports [38, 39]

The emergence of CRISPR/Cas12a-based diagnostics offers a promising new strategy for molecular detection, particularly for RNA viruses. To address the need for rapid, on-site testing, we developed a one-tube RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay. This system enables the detection of BVDV-1 RNA at a constant temperature of 40 °C within 30 min and can specifically distinguish BVDV-1 from other common bovine viruses. Although previous studies have employed CRISPR/Cas13a for BVDV detection [29, 30] and an RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay with a reported limit of detection (LOD) of 3 copies/µL has been described, our work establishes a novel system with enhanced performance.

In this study, we explored a nucleic acid detection platform integrating recombinase-aided amplification (RAA) with CRISPR/Cas12a. The assay procedure is straightforward: RNA extracted from clinical samples is reverse transcribed to cDNA before being added to the reaction tube for RAA amplification and fluorescence detection. This method achieved an ultra-low LOD of 1 copy/µL. When evaluating 300 clinical bovine serum samples, it demonstrated a 1.85-fold higher detection rate (48% positivity vs. 26%) compared to conventional RT-PCR, confirming its superior sensitivity for identifying infected animals. The assay also exhibited high specificity, showing no cross-reactivity with other common bovine pathogens like BoHV-1 and BRSV, which minimizes the risk of false-positive results.

It should be noted that the specificity panel of the present study did not include BVDV‑2 or BVDV‑3 (HoBi-like pestivirus) due to the unavailability of clinical samples or reference strains at our institution. Given the high sequence conservation of the 5’UTR region among pestiviruses, cross‑reactivity with BVDV‑2 is theoretically possible but requires experimental confirmation. In contrast, BVDV‑3 shows greater divergence in the 5’UTR, making cross‑reactivity less likely. Future studies will include BVDV‑2 and BVDV‑3 strains to fully validate the specificity of this RT‑RAA‑CRISPR/Cas12a assay.

Despite its advantages in sensitivity, speed (results within one hour), and specificity, current limitations must be acknowledged. While robust under controlled laboratory conditions, the practical deployment of this assay in resource-limited settings, such as local laboratories or farms, may be constrained by factors including cost, equipment needs, and operational complexity. Future research will focus on cost reduction, development of portable devices, and simplification of the workflow to facilitate broader application at the point of need. Further validation will also extend to detecting other BVDV subtypes.

Conclusion

We have successfully developed an ultra-sensitive, rapid, and highly specific BVDV-1 detection method. This method combines the CRISPR/Cas12a system, DNA pre-amplification, and two different sensor systems. The established method showed good performance when applied to clinical BVDV disease detection. It can flexibly adjust experimental settings in remote testing locations or well-equipped laboratories, which fully demonstrates the enormous potential of CRISPR/Cas12a in rapid on-site detection and has important hope in controlling the spread of BVDV.

Methods

Extraction of viral genomic DNA/RNA

Genomic DNA/RNA was extracted from nucleic acid samples and clinical serum samples of BVDV-1, BPIV3, BCoV, BEV, BRSV, and BoHV-1 using a commercial viral genomic DNA/RNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted nucleic acids were stored at -80 °C for subsequent use.

All extracted viral RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The reverse transcription reaction was performed under the following conditions: 50 °C for 15 min for reverse transcription, followed by 85 °C for 5 s to inactivate the reverse transcriptase. The resulting cDNA was stored at -20 °C for subsequent RAA amplification and CRISPR/Cas12a detection.

Construction of standard plasmids

Using BVDV-1 total RNA as a template, it was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit, and the target sequence was amplified by RT-PCR. The amplified product was subjected to 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the target fragment was purified using the DNA recovery kit. The purified product was TA cloned with the PUC57 vector to construct a recombinant plasmid (Fig.S1). After DH5a peptide transformation, LB culture, and plasmid extraction, the recombinant plasmid concentration was determined by UV spectrophotometry and validated by sequencing by General Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Finally, plasmids with OD260/OD280 ratios in the range of 1.8–1.9 and validated by sequencing were selected as quantitative standards. The primer sequence for amplifying the target 5’UTR gene is as follows: the forward primer is 5’ - CTTGGTCTGCGAAACTGAAC − 3’, the reverse primer is 5’ - GCGGTACAGTCAATGTCCTG − 3’.

Design of crRNA, forward/reverse primer, direct repeat sequence

Download 1135 reference sequences of BVDV1 (species ID: 11099) from the NCBI Virus website in China. All sequences except for the 5’UTR sequence and the complete genome sequence were excluded, leaving 878 sequences; 878 sequences containing 5’UTR were aligned through the MAFFT online website, and highly conserved regions were selected as detection sites for RAA primer and crRNA design(https://publish.illinois.edu/msaevaluation/mafft-software/). Compare and select highly conserved regions as detection sites for the RAA primer and crRNA design༈see in Supplementary material 2༉. The sequence of the relative crRNA and RAA is listed in Table S1.

RAA reaction system

The RAA amplification system is 20 µL, including 11.5 µL of buffer, 1 µL (10 µmol/L) of upstream and downstream primers, 4.5 µL of DEPC water, 1 µL of cDNA template, and 1 µL of RAA reaction activator. The reaction procedure is 40℃ for 30 min. The amplified product was analyzed by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Six pairs of specific primer pairs (F1/R1, F1/R2, F2/R1, F2/R2, F3/R1, F4/R1) were designed through cross combination targeting the BVDV-1 target. The amplification efficiency was evaluated using the BVDV-1-PUC57 recombinant plasmid as a template, and the primer pair with the most significant amplification efficiency was selected for subsequent experiments.

Establishment of RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection system

The target dsDNA consisted of linearized double-stranded DNA containing both the PAM site and the crRNA-complementary region. The CRISPR/Cas12a reaction was performed in a total volume of 30 µL, comprising 1 µL of Lb Cas12a, 1.2 µL of DNA reporter, 3 µL of Cleavage buffer, 1 µL of crRNA primer, 1 µL of target dsDNA, and DEPC-treated H₂O to a final volume of 30 µL. The reaction mixture was incubated at a constant temperature of 40 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, fluorescence signals were collected using a constant-temperature fluorescence detection device, and results were visualized and documented with a blue-light transilluminator.

Sensitivity and specificity detection of RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a

Dilute plasmid standards in a continuous 10-fold gradient to a concentration range of 1 × 100 copies/µL ~ 1 × 103 copies/µL, using this as a template and DEPC H2O as a negative control. Use primers RT-RAA-F1/R1 for conventional RT-PCR (following the manufacturer’s instructions of the ChamQ Blue Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix kit) and RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a reactions to evaluate the detection sensitivity of different methods.

To evaluate the specificity of the RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay, viral DNA/RNA genomes from BVDV-1, BoHV-1, BCoV, BEV, BRSV, BRV, and BPIV-3 were used as templates, with DEPC H₂O serving as the negative control.

Repetitiveness detection of RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a

Using a recombinant plasmid with a concentration of 1 × 100 copies/µL as a template and DEPC H2O as a negative control, repeat the test three times to evaluate the repeatability of the method.

Testing of clinical samples

A total of 300 bovine serum samples were collected from three large-scale dairy farms (designated Farm A, B, and C) in Yinchuan City, Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, China, between March 2024 and September 2024. Based on clinical examination performed by licensed veterinarians at the time of sampling, cattle were classified into three clinical categories: (i) clinically healthy (no signs of diarrhea, respiratory distress, or reproductive disorders, n = 112); (ii) mild clinical symptoms (intermittent diarrhea, mild fever [39.5–40.5℃], or reduced milk yield, n = 128); and (iii) severe clinical symptoms (persistent diarrhea, mucosal lesions, respiratory distress, or reproductive failure, n = 60). The age distribution was as follows: calves (0–6 months, n = 82), heifers (6–18 months, n = 91), adult cows (18 months-5 years, n = 84), and older cows (> 5 years, n = 43). None of the animals had been vaccinated against BVDV in the 12 months prior to sampling. Serum samples were obtained via jugular venipuncture into vacuum tubes without anticoagulant, allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min, centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min, and stored at -80 °C until RNA extraction. Viral RNA was extracted using a commercial viral genomic RNA extraction kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. All samples were tested in parallel by the established RT-RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay and conventional RT-PCR.

Conventional RT-PCR was performed as a comparator method using the same primer pair (RT-RAA-F1/R1, Table S1) targeting the 5’UTR region of BVDV-1. The reaction was carried out using the ChamQ Blue Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: reverse transcription at 50 °C for 15 min; initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The amplified products were analyzed by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. This RT-PCR method was adapted from previously published studies targeting the BVDV 5’UTR region and has been routinely used in our laboratory for BVDV detection.
