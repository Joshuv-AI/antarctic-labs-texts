White matter functional connectome topology and its clinical correlations in adolescent major depressive disorder
Authors: Kangjia Chen, Xuemei Li, Hongyi Chen, Yaohui He, Yang Huang, Wenjing Wang, Shenglan Ran, Zhiqiang Huang, Kangyu Qiu, Xin Shu, Xiaocong Jiang, Xianghua Long, Wei Li, Wei Liao, Xinyu Zhou, Jiao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescence is a critical period for brain network remodeling and the onset of major depressive disorder (MDD); however, white matter (WM) functional topology in adolescent MDD remains underexplored. Given that WM functional signals reflect meaningful neural activity and are disrupted in psychiatric disorders, this study aimed to characterize WM functional connectome alterations in adolescents with MDD and examine their clinical associations.MethodsResting-state fMRI data were obtained from a cohort of adolescents with MDD (n = 320) and healthy controls (HCs, n = 144), as well as from an independent replication cohort. Following the construction of thresholded WM functional networks, graph-theoretical analyses were used to calculate global topological properties. Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) was used to examine associations between topology and clinical symptoms, while exploratory classification assessed their discriminative information and generalizability. Furthermore, subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the effects of a history of suicide attempt, non-suicidal self-injury, childhood trauma, and sex.ResultsCompared with HCs, adolescent MDD exhibited significant reductions in the clustering coefficient, characteristic path length, and local efficiency. CCA identified distinct covariation patterns: reduced global integration was linked to severe suicidal ideation and depressed mood, while impaired local segregation was associated with vegetative symptoms such as weight loss and insomnia. Subgroup analyses revealed significant sexual dimorphism, with male patients demonstrating more severe topological impairments than females. A similar pattern was observed in the independent replication cohort. The classification analysis achieved above-chance accuracy (69.6 and 60% in the two cohorts).ConclusionsOur results reveal a topologically shifted WM functional connectome structure in adolescent MDD, providing new clues to aid in understanding the pathophysiology of its pathophysiology.Keywords: adolescent major depressive disorder, white matter functional connectome, graph theory, small-world topology, sex differences

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) has emerged as one of the leading causes of disability and global disease burden in adolescents (Thapar et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2024), with its prevalence exhibiting a significant upward trend in recent years (Mojtabai et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2024). As a critical window for neurodevelopment and psychosocial transition, adolescence represents a peak period for the onset of MDD, characterized by marked sex differences (Salk et al., 2017), high recurrence rates, and persistence into adulthood (Dunn and Goodyer, 2006). Depressive episodes during this developmental stage not only severely impair social functioning and academic performance but also constitute a primary cause of suicide among adolescents, posing a substantial challenge to public health systems (Abraham and Sher, 2019; Thapar et al., 2022). Consequently, elucidating the pathological features of adolescent MDD is crucial for facilitating early identification and effective intervention, particularly given the increasing recognition of biologically annotated heterogeneity in depression (Li et al., 2026).

Human brain connectomics aims to map the brain’s connections at multiple scales, from macroscopic (brain regions) to microscopic (neurons), and to reveal their intrinsic organizational principles underlying these connections (Liang et al., 2010). Among numerous network properties, small-worldness is a fundamental concept. It refers to the brain’s ability to maintain high local clustering while enabling efficient information transfer via short global paths, reflecting a balance between global and local efficiency (Achard et al., 2006; Bassett and Bullmore, 2017; Liao et al., 2017; Suo et al., 2018). An in-depth analysis of the topological properties of brain networks provides a new perspective for understanding normal brain function and the pathophysiological mechanisms of neuropsychiatric disorders.

Historically regarded as merely structural, white matter (WM) is increasingly recognized as exhibiting genuine functional neural activity, evidenced by detectable hemodynamic responses coupled with metabolic demands and intrinsic topological organizations that predict cognitive traits like fluid intelligence (Li et al., 2020b, 2023a, 2025a; Fan et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Ji et al., 2025). In the context of MDD connectomics, extensive evidence has characterized topological abnormalities in gray matter (GM) networks, such as widespread reductions in global efficiency and disruptions in sensorimotor and default mode networks (Gong and He, 2015; Yang et al., 2021; Yun and Kim, 2021; Sun et al., 2024). However, investigations into the WM functional connectome remain relatively scarce. Recent studies in adult MDD patients have begun to reveal significant reductions in WM small-worldness and clustering coefficients, indicating impaired information integration and segregation (Li et al., 2020b). Moreover, transdiagnostic evidence suggests that WM dysfunction in psychiatric disorders is associated with neurotransmitter and genetic profiles, indicating that WM functional abnormalities may reflect biologically meaningful disease-related mechanisms (Li et al., 2021; Ji et al., 2023). However, despite adolescence being a critical window for neurodevelopment and the peak period for MDD onset, to our knowledge, no study has yet characterized the topological alterations of the WM functional network in this specific population. This gap highlights an urgent need to investigate adolescent-specific WM functional abnormalities to better understand early disease mechanisms.

This study aims to systematically investigate topological alterations in WM functional connectomes in adolescent MDD and to examine their clinical associations. Based on existing findings, we hypothesize that adolescents with MDD will exhibit WM functional network topological abnormalities similar to those observed in adult patients, particularly disruptions in small-world topology. Specifically, this study will first construct WM functional connectivity networks using graph theory-based methods. Second, it will quantitatively compare the topological differences in the network between the adolescent MDD group and healthy controls (HCs). Third, it will explore the association between these topological properties and individual symptom severity in adolescent MDD. Finally, we examined whether the discovery findings showed similar patterns in an independent replication cohort (Fig. 1).

Study design. After registering structural images and preprocessed functional images, the Pearson correlation coefficient between each pair of nodes was calculated using the MWMA-200 atlas to construct the functional connectivity matrix. The AUC values of topological properties were evaluated under the sparsity of 0.1:0.01:0.3. Subsequently, the relationship between topological properties and clinical scales was evaluated, and an exploratory classification analysis was performed using topological properties.

Methods

Participants

Discovery cohort

For the discovery cohort, MDD patients aged 10–18 years were recruited from the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. HCs, also aged 10–18 years, were recruited via local media advertisements. Adolescent MDD diagnoses were confirmed using the Structural Clinical Interview for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) by experienced psychiatric physicians. The severity of depressive symptoms was assessed using the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17). Anxiety symptom severity was evaluated using the 14-item Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA-14).

Inclusion criteria for MDD patients were (1) a confirmed diagnosis of MDD according to DSM-5 criteria, with a total HAMD-17 score greater than 7; (2) first-episode depression; and HCs had a total HAMD-17 score of 7 or less. Exclusion criteria for all participants included: (1) a history of severe medical, neurological, or psychiatric illness; (2) substance use disorder, head trauma, or loss of consciousness; (3) any contraindications to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanning; and (4) left-handedness. The Ethics Committee of First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University approved this study (approval number: 2020-864). All participants and one of their parents or guardians received information about the study procedures and provided written informed consent.

Replication cohort

The replication cohort was obtained from the publicly available dataset on OpenNeuro (https://openneuro.org/datasets/ds004627/versions/1.0.0) (Nielson et al., 2023), which is part of the National Institute of Mental Health Characterization and Treatment of Adolescent Depression (NIMH CAT-D) study. Participants with MDD were scanned every 4 months, and HCs were scanned yearly. For this study, only the initial scan data were utilized.

Data acquisition

Discovery cohort

MRI scans were acquired on a Siemens Magnetom Skyra 3T scanner with a 32-channel head coil. During scanning, each participant was instructed to lie still, relax, close their eyes, remain awake, and avoid performing any specific cognitive tasks. High-resolution T1-weighted (T1w) structural images were obtained using a magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2000 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.56 ms, inversion time (TI) = 900 ms, flip angle = 9°, matrix = 256 × 256, field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm2, slice thickness = 1 mm, number of slices = 192, and voxel size = 1.0 mm × 1.0 mm × 1.0 mm. Rs-fMRI data were collected using a gradient-recalled echo planar imaging sequence with the following parameters: TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 90°, FOV = 220 × 220 mm2, number of slices = 36, and slice thickness = 3.0 mm. A total of 240 EPI volumes were acquired. The reconstructed voxel size was 3.4 mm × 3.4 mm × 3.0 mm, with a slice gap of 3.0 mm.

Replication cohort

MRI scans were acquired on a GE DISCOVERY_MR750 3T scanner with a 32-channel head coil. High-resolution T1w structural images were obtained using an MPRAGE sequence with the following parameters: TR = 7.66 ms, TE = 3.48 ms, TI = 900 ms, flip angle = 7°, matrix = 256 × 256, FOV = 256 × 256 mm2, slice thickness = 1 mm, number of slices = 192, and voxel size = 1.0 mm × 1.0 mm × 1.0 mm. Rs-fMRI data were collected using a gradient-recalled echo planar imaging sequence with the following parameters: TR = 2500 ms, TE = 15 ms, flip angle = 80°, FOV = 64 × 64 mm2, number of slices = 32, and slice thickness = 3.0 mm. A total of 196 EPI volumes were acquired.

Data preprocessing

All images were preprocessed using WMFPrep (https://github.com/weiliao81/WMFPrep) (Ji et al., 2017; Li et al., 2019, 2020a, b) and DPABI (http://rfmri.net/dpabi) (Yan et al., 2016). Preprocessing steps for rs-fMRI images included removing the first 10 time points, slice timing correction, and motion realignment. To further reduce motion-related artifacts, framewise scrubbing was performed during preprocessing by removing volumes with framewise displacement (FD) > 0.5 mm, together with one preceding and two subsequent neighboring time points. T1w structural images underwent brain extraction, registration, and segmentation. A WM mask was then applied to the denoised functional images, followed by normalization, linear detrending, and band-pass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz). This frequency range was selected because it has been widely used in previous WM functional connectivity studies and allows comparability with prior work (Li et al., 2020a, b). Subjects with mean FD greater than 3 mm (or rotational displacement > 3°) were excluded. The final discovery cohort included 320 MDD patients and 144 HCs, who were included in subsequent analyses.

Construction of WM functional connectomes

We defined nodes (N = 200) using the MWMA-200 Atlas (Zhou et al., 2026). For each subject, we constructed a correlation matrix by calculating Pearson correlation coefficients for each pair of nodes. Subsequently, we applied Fisher’s r-to-Z transformation to the correlation matrices. Finally, we assessed topological properties based on the thresholded WM functional connectome.

Topological properties of WM functional connectomes

We used GRETNA software to calculate global topological properties of the WM functional connectome for each sparsity threshold (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/gretna/) (Wang et al., 2015). For network construction, only positive functional connections were retained by setting the connectivity matrix sign to “Positive.” Thus, negative correlation coefficients were excluded from the thresholded networks and were not included in the calculation of graph-theoretical metrics. Small-worldness supports both integrated and segregated information processing, global efficiency focuses on the network’s information-propagation capacity, and local efficiency concerns the information-processing capacity of specific regions. Therefore, we calculated seven global topological properties: clustering coefficient (Cp), normalized clustering coefficient (γ), characteristic path length (Lp), normalized characteristic path length (λ), small-worldness (σ), local efficiency (Elocal), and global efficiency (Eglobal) (Bullmore and Sporns, 2009; Rubinov and Sporns, 2010; Gong and He, 2015; Suo et al., 2018). Subsequently, we computed the area under the curve (AUC) for each topological property across a sparsity range of 0.1–0.3 (with an interval of 0.01). This sparsity range was selected based on previous WM functional connectome studies (Li et al., 2020b). The lower bound of 0.10 was used to maintain sufficient network connectedness. The upper bound of 0.30 was used to avoid overly dense networks and reduce the inclusion of weak or potentially spurious connections. The AUC considers performance across the entire sparsity range rather than a single threshold, making it highly sensitive to detecting abnormalities in brain topological properties across various diseases (Zhang et al., 2011).

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Demographic and clinical characteristics were assessed between adolescent MDD patients and HCs. Differences in age and education were analyzed using either a non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test (for non-Gaussian distributed data) or two-sample t-tests (for Gaussian distributed data). Sex comparisons were performed using the χ2 test.

Global topological property comparisons

Differences in the AUC values of global topological properties between groups were examined using either a non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test (for non-Gaussian distributed values) or two-sample t-tests (for Gaussian distributed values). Age, sex, education, and mean FD were included as covariates. The significance threshold was set at P < 0.05, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied for multiple comparisons.

Relationships between small-world topology and depression severity

To explore the relationship between alterations in global topological structure and depression severity, we employed canonical correlation analysis (CCA). The CCA is a valuable tool for uncovering complex relationships among numerous variables within large datasets. A key advantage of CCA is its ability to simultaneously evaluate two distinct sets of variables without assuming any specific form of prioritization or directionality. This was done to identify the primary dimensions of shared variance that describe the global topological structure and clinical manifestations. Clinical symptoms were quantified by a weighted combination of scores from the HAMD-17 (Drysdale et al., 2017; Xia et al., 2018). The contributions of clinical manifestations and global topology were then measured by calculating Pearson correlation coefficients between the weighted combination of clinical symptoms and the actual individual HAMD-17 scores, and between the weighted combination of global topology and the actual AUC values for each topological property. The significance threshold was set at P < 0.05, with FDR correction applied for multiple comparisons.

Exploratory classification analysis

To explore whether WM functional topological metrics can discriminate adolescent MDD from HCs, we performed exploratory support vector machine (SVM) classification analyses using LIBSVM toolbox v3.22 (https://www.csie.ntu.edu.tw/∼cjlin/libsvm/). The four global topological metrics that showed significant group differences in the discovery cohort (Cp, λ, Lp, and Elocal) were used as classification features. Classification performance was first evaluated in the discovery cohort using five-fold cross-validation. To determine whether the observed classification accuracy exceeded the empirical chance level, permutation testing was performed by randomly shuffling group labels and repeating the full cross-validation procedure. To further assess external generalizability, the classifier was trained on the discovery cohort using the same four features and directly applied to the independent replication cohort. Classification performance was assessed using accuracy.

Subgroup analysis

To further investigate how different characteristics influence the manifestation of MDD, we conducted a subgroup analysis of adolescent MDD. The subgrouping criteria were as follows: (i) suicide attempt history: participants were grouped based on whether they had a history of suicide attempts. This subgroup was aimed at identifying patients with higher suicide risk, as suicide attempts are frequently associated with severe depressive symptoms and elevated risk factors (Li et al., 2024b). (ii) Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) behavior: participants were also categorized by the presence or absence of NSSI behaviors. NSSI is often a coping mechanism for emotional distress in MDD patients, and understanding its occurrence helps identify patients’ emotion regulation strategies and their potential psychological needs (Huang et al., 2021). (iii) Childhood trauma: we grouped participants based on whether they had experienced childhood trauma. Childhood trauma is considered a significant risk factor for MDD and can profoundly impact an individual’s psychological development and emotion regulation (Li et al., 2023b). (iv) Sex: multiple studies have indicated significant sex differences in the presentation of MDD, with males and females exhibiting distinct symptomatology and responses to treatment (Mansouri et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2024; Tian et al., 2024). For each subgroup, we analyzed seven topological properties using either two-sample t-tests or Mann–Whitney U test, depending on the distribution of the data.

Network analysis on replication cohort

To assess the stability of our results, we conducted a replication analysis using an independent, publicly available dataset. We compared the topological structure of functional connectivity between adolescent MDD patients and HCs using either a Mann–Whitney U test or two-sample t-tests. Since the dataset did not provide participants’ years of education, we only included sex, age, and mean FD as covariates. The significance threshold was set at P < 0.05, with FDR correction applied. To further evaluate the consistency of group differences across datasets, the Cohen’s d effect sizes were calculated for each global topological metric in both the discovery and replication cohorts, with negative values indicating lower values in MDD patients. The direction and magnitude of effect sizes were then compared across datasets.

Sensitivity analyses

Medication-related analysis

To examine medication status effects on WM functional network topology, we divided MDD patients into medicated and unmedicated groups. Using two-sample t-tests, we compared the topological properties between medicated and unmedicated patients, controlling for age, sex, and mean FD.

Sex-matched analysis

To assess whether sex imbalance influenced the main findings, we performed a sex-matched subgroup analysis in the discovery cohort. After matching the sex distribution between MDD patients and HCs, we repeated the group comparisons of the seven global topological metrics while controlling for age and mean FD. FDR correction was applied across the seven metrics.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

In the final analysis, the discovery cohort included 320 MDD patients and 144 HCs. There were no significant differences between MDD patients and HCs in terms of age (P = 0.2906) or years of education (P = 0.7903). However, a significant difference was observed in sex distribution (P = 0.0009) (Table 1). The replication cohort included 78 MDD patients and 52 HCs. There was a significant difference in age between MDD patients and HCs (P = 0.0210), whereas no significant difference was observed in sex distribution (P = 0.0601) (Table S1).

Alterations of small-world topology

This study investigated data-specific global topological structures within a sparsity range of 0.1–0.3 (step size 0.01) while regressing out sex, age, and mean FD as covariates. Compared to HCs, adolescent MDD patients exhibited significant reductions in Cp (t = −3.13, P = 0.0131, FDR corrected), λ (t = −2.53, P = 0.0203, FDR corrected), Lp (t = −2.61, P = 0.0203, FDR corrected), and Elocal (t = −2.79, P = 0.0194, FDR corrected) (Fig. 2). Despite preserved small-worldness, the simultaneous reductions in clustering coefficient and path length suggest a randomization of the network topology in adolescent MDD patients, reflecting impaired local segregation.

The relationship between small-world topology and depressive severity

The CCA identified two significant modes of covariation between global topological metrics and HAMD-17 scores. The first canonical variate pair yielded a significant association (r = 0.44, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3a). Symptom variables showed positive loadings for somatic symptoms gastro-intestinal (r = 0.38), early hours of the morning (r = 0.37), and anxiety somatic (r = 0.36); whereas suicide (r = −0.25) and depressed mood (r = −0.24) exhibited negative loadings. Among global topological indicators, Lp (r = 0.50) and Cp (r = 0.45) demonstrated positive loadings (Fig. 3b and c). Given the reduced topological properties generally observed in MDD, this pattern indicates that lower Lp and Cp values are specifically associated with more severe suicidal ideation and depressive mood. Conversely, relatively preserved topology correlates with a symptom cluster dominated by somatic anxiety and insomnia. The second canonical variate pair also showed a significant association (r = 0.35, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3d). This mode revealed a distinct clinical dimension characterized by positive loadings for insight (r = 0.31) and strong negative loadings for loss of weight (r = −0.53), middle of the night (r = −0.38), and anxiety psychic (r = −0.35). Topologically, all global metrics demonstrated strong positive loadings, particularly Cp (r = 0.86) and Elocal (r = 0.81) (Fig. 3e and f). This inverse relationship suggests that severe network disruptions (decreased Cp and Elocal) are associated with pronounced vegetative symptoms, such as weight loss and sleep maintenance difficulties. Exploratory classification analysis

Using the four topological metrics that showed significant group differences in the discovery cohort, the SVM classifier achieved an accuracy of 69.61% in the discovery cohort based on five-fold cross-validation. The classification accuracy was significantly higher than the empirical chance level (permutation P = 0.0099). To further assess external generalizability, the classifier trained on the discovery cohort using the same four metrics was directly applied to the independent replication cohort, yielding an external validation accuracy of 60.00%. These results suggest that WM functional topological metrics showed exploratory discriminative information in the discovery cohort, but the classifier did not demonstrate strong external generalizability.

Subgroup analysis

To explore potential heterogeneity within the adolescent MDD group, subgroup analyses were based on history of suicide attempts, NSSI, childhood trauma, and sex. No significant differences in global topological metrics were observed regarding suicide attempts, NSSI, or childhood trauma (all P > 0.05). However, a significant sex difference was identified (Table S2). Specifically, male adolescent MDD exhibited significantly reduced global network properties compared to female patients, including Cp (t = 3.47, P = 0.0015), λ (t = 3.47, P = 0.0015), Lp (t = 3.25, P = 0.0022), Eglobal (t = 2.65, P = 0.0117), and Elocal (t = 3.44, P = 0.0015) (Fig. 4). These results suggest that sex may be an important biological factor in the global network topology of adolescent MDD, with male patients exhibiting more severe topological disruptions than females.

Topological properties in replication cohort

To examine whether the discovery findings showed similar patterns in an independent cohort, we performed the same topological analysis in the replication cohort. In this cohort, none of the group differences survived FDR correction for multiple comparisons. Specifically, adolescent MDD patients in the replication cohort exhibited nominally significant reductions in λ (t = −2.14, P = 0.0342), Lp (t = −2.35, P = 0.0203), and Elocal (t = −2.01, P = 0.0467). Additionally, Cp showed a trend toward reduction (t = −1.74, P = 0.0850) (Fig. S1). To further evaluate the consistency of group differences across datasets, we compared Cohen’s d effect sizes between the discovery and replication cohorts. Cp, λ, Lp, Eglobal, and Elocal showed directionally consistent negative effect sizes across both datasets, indicating lower values in adolescent MDD patients than in HCs. The effect sizes were generally small to moderate. In contrast, γ and σ showed negligible effects in the replication cohort (Table S4). These effect-size comparisons provide additional preliminary support for the discovery findings, but the replication results should still be interpreted cautiously given the lack of FDR correction.

Sensitivity analysis

After regressing out age, sex, and mean FD, no significant differences were observed between medicated and unmedicated MDD patients in any of the seven global topological metrics (all P > 0.05; Table S3). After matching the sex distribution between MDD patients and HCs, the main pattern of group differences remained largely consistent with the primary analysis. Significant differences were observed in Cp, λ, Lp, Eglobal, and Elocal after FDR correction, whereas γ and σ remained non-significant (Fig. 5). These results suggest that the main WM functional topological alterations were unlikely to be solely driven by sex imbalance or medication status.

Discussion

In this study, we leveraged a large-sample dataset to delineate the topological disruptions of the WM functional connectome in adolescent MDD. Our results reveal several key insights. First, compared to HCs, adolescent MDD exhibited a randomized network architecture, characterized by simultaneous reductions in Cp, Lp, and Elocal. This altered network structure showed exploratory discriminative information in the discovery cohort, but the classifier did not demonstrate robust reproducibility or external generalizability. Second, using CCA, we identified two distinct multivariate patterns linking topological deficits to specific symptom dimensions: one pattern associated with reduced global integration with core depressive symptoms (suicide/mood), and another linked impaired local segregation to vegetative symptoms (weight loss/insomnia). Third, subgroup analyses revealed sexually dimorphic topological disruptions, with male patients exhibiting more severe network alterations than females. In contrast, clinical history (e.g. suicide attempts, childhood trauma) did not significantly impact global topology. Finally, an independent replication cohort showed similar but uncorrected trends across several topological metrics.

Network randomization and impaired segregation in adolescent MDD

The human brain typically exhibits a “small-world” architecture, balancing local information processing (segregation) with efficient global information transfer (integration) (Achard et al., 2006; Bassett and Bullmore, 2017; Liao et al., 2017; Suo et al., 2018). Our results indicate that while adolescents with MDD retain fundamental small-world properties, their WM functional networks display a significant shift toward randomization. This shift is characterized by reduced Cp and Elocal, accompanied by decreased Lp and λ. This specific configuration—low clustering coupled with short path lengths—suggests a deviation from the optimized small-world organization toward a random network, reflecting impaired local segregation and a disorganized network topology (Suo et al., 2018; Li et al., 2020b; He et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2024). This finding aligns with, yet extends, previous observations in adult MDD. For instance, Li et al. reported a similar trend toward randomization (decreased Cp) in unmedicated adult MDD patients, but found no significant alteration in Lp (Li et al., 2020b). In contrast, our adolescent MDD exhibited a simultaneous reduction in Lp. This discrepancy may imply that the adolescent brain, in a critical developmental window of synaptic pruning and myelination (Luna and Sweeney, 2004; Blakemore, 2012), exhibits more pronounced network reorganization or unstable plasticity when exposed to MDD pathology than adults do.

The shortened Lp observed here likely represents “pathological over-integration” or “noisy synchronization” rather than enhanced efficiency, suggesting that the network establishes widespread but non-specific connections at the expense of local functional specialization. Furthermore, contrasting our findings with structural network studies provides a complementary perspective. Wu et al. reported decreased Cp but increased Lp (indicating reduced Eglobal) in the structural networks of first-episode adolescent MDD (Wu et al., 2024). The divergence between our findings (decreased Lp) and their structural findings (increased Lp) strongly suggests a structural–functional decoupling in MDD pathology. It is plausible that structural impairments (e.g. damaged WM integrity acting as physical barriers) weaken the physical constraints on neural signaling. This loss of structural constraint may paradoxically lead to the unconstrained, random propagation of functional signals, manifesting as a randomized network with pathologically shortened functional paths.

Associations between topology and clinical symptoms

To explore the clinical significance of topological disruptions in the WM functional connectome, we employed CCA to uncover complex covariation patterns between global network properties and multidimensional clinical symptoms. The first canonical mode revealed a critical pathological pattern: lower Lp and Cp were strongly associated with severe suicidal ideation and depressed mood. Cp and Elocal reflect the brain’s capacity for segregating information, specifically the ability of local regions to perform specialized processing. Their impairment (i.e. randomization) implies a compromised ability to integrate distinct cognitive and emotional information, potentially leading to cognitive rigidity and profound hopelessness characteristic of suicidal ideation (Chu et al., 2023). Conversely, individuals with a relatively preserved topological structure (higher Lp and Cp) exhibited a symptom profile predominantly characterized by somatic anxiety and insomnia. This finding supports the heterogeneity hypothesis of MDD (Cipriani et al., 2018; Zhuo et al., 2019; Li et al., 2024a, 2025b), suggesting that patients with predominant somatic symptoms may possess less severe underlying damage to the WM functional network, or involve distinct neural circuits related to emotional regulation. The second canonical mode linked reduced Cp and Elocal primarily to vegetative symptoms (e.g. weight loss and middle insomnia) and deficits in insight. This suggests that severe impairments in local network segregation may specifically disrupt brain networks responsible for homeostatic regulation and self-referential processing, resulting in autonomic dysregulation.

Recent network-based evidence further suggests that distinct antidepressant therapies may act on a common depression-related brain network (Ji et al., 2026). Together with transdiagnostic findings linking WM dysfunction to neurotransmitter and genetic profiles (Ji et al., 2023), these studies support a network-level framework for understanding depression. Our findings are broadly consistent with this framework by linking WM functional topological alterations to symptom dimensions in adolescent MDD. However, whether these WM functional topological changes directly reflect a common mechanism of depression or remission requires further investigation in longitudinal and treatment-response studies.

Sex-specific vulnerability of the WM functional connectome

A pivotal finding from our subgroup analysis is that male patients with MDD exhibited significantly more severe impairments in global network properties—including Cp, λ, Lp, Eglobal, and Elocal—compared to their female counterparts. This observation aligns with the growing body of literature suggesting that MDD has a sex-specific neurobiological basis (Mansouri et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2024; Tian et al., 2024). Despite the higher epidemiological prevalence of MDD in females, our results suggest that males may experience more profound disruptions in network topology (Gaspar et al., 2022; Kenney et al., 2025). This discrepancy may be attributable to sexual dimorphism in adolescent brain development (Matte Bon et al., 2025); specifically, the male brain undergoes more pronounced network reorganization during this critical developmental window, potentially rendering it more vulnerable to depression-related pathological changes. Intriguingly, clinical characteristics such as a history of suicide attempts or NSSI did not significantly affect network topology, further emphasizing sex as a core biological factor in distinguishing neurobiological subtypes of adolescent MDD. These findings highlight the importance of considering sex-specific factors in future studies of adolescent MDD neurobiology.

Limitations and future directions

The current study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing a causal relationship between topological abnormalities and the onset of MDD. Longitudinal studies should track high-risk adolescent MDD to investigate causality and explore whether these abnormalities predict disease progression or treatment response. Second, some patients had already received antidepressant treatment, which could potentially confound WM functional networks. Although our medication-related analysis revealed no significant differences in global topological metrics between medicated and unmedicated patients, medication effects cannot be fully excluded because detailed information regarding medication type, dosage, and treatment duration was not uniformly available. Third, we applied a conventional 0.01–0.10 Hz band-pass filter for WM functional connectivity analysis. This frequency range has been commonly used in previous WM functional connectome studies (Li et al., 2020a, b). However, WM BOLD signals have a lower signal-to-noise ratio than GM BOLD signals and may exhibit distinct frequency characteristics. Future studies should systematically examine frequency-dependent WM functional connectivity and network topology across different frequency bands. Fourth, differences in scanning parameters, sample sizes, and clinical heterogeneity between the discovery and replication cohorts could have contributed to the reduced effect sizes observed in the replication cohort. Finally, the present study focused on static WM functional connectivity. Future studies could explore dynamic WM functional networks to capture time-varying network states in adolescent MDD.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study characterizes a shift toward a randomized network architecture in the WM functional connectome of adolescents with first-episode MDD. We provide novel evidence that these topological alterations were differentially associated with clinical symptom dimensions, with disrupted global integration linked to core depressive symptoms. Furthermore, our findings highlight a distinct male vulnerability to these network alterations. Together, these findings reveal the abnormal topological properties of WM functional connectome in adolescent MDD, and provide clues to the WM dysfunction patterns associated with multiple clinical symptoms.
