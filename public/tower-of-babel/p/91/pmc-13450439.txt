Quantitative MRI T1 relaxation time as a biomarker of complete response in rectal cancer six weeks after neoadjuvant therapy: a prospective study
Authors: Mathias Byriel, Torben F. Hansen, L. Henrik Jensen, B. Rahr Hans, Signe Timm, Jan Lindebjerg, Søren R. Rafaelsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•44 rectal cancer patients prospectively included for analysis of T1 relaxation time.•T1 relaxation time distinguished complete from incomplete response with AUC of 0.94.•T1 relaxation time is a promising tool for identification of complete response.

Introduction

Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy followed by surgery is the standard treatment for locally advanced rectal cancer. Organ-sparing treatment has emerged as a promising alternative to major surgery. A substantial proportion of patients achieve complete response (CR) after therapy [1], in which case the decision must be made to either proceed with surgery or follow the patient up closely in a watch-and-wait (WW) regimen to spare the rectum [2], [3]. The safety of organ-sparing treatment relies on accurate assessment of treatment response, and this is still a challenge [2], [4], particularly in case of pronounced fibrosis [5]. Diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) has been recommended for standard diagnostic procedure for restaging, but the method is not without difficulties [6]. There is still a need for a more accurate method to determine response to neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) [7].

Emerging magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques based on T1 relaxation time (T1RT) mapping have shown promise for assessing the treatment induced tissue changes. T1RT reflects the rate at which excited protons realign with the external magnetic field after a radiofrequency pulse and is influenced by tissue composition. MRI exploits tissue-specific differences in relaxation times to generate contrast, allowing differentiation between viable tumour tissue and fibrosis, which is a key challenge in response assessment after NAT [8].

T1RT has proven relevant as a diagnostic tool within several other organs [9]. The method remains insufficiently investigated in rectal cancer; few studies reported promising results when looking for a reliable tool to add to rectal cancer diagnostics [10], [11].

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the ability of T1RT to identify CR using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Furthermore, we sought to explore potential correlations between T1RT and both tumour volume and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a prospective, single centre study at University Hospital of Southern Denmark, Vejle, Denmark (Beriderbakken 4, 7100, Vejle, Denmark). All participants underwent standardised MRI scans on 1.5 T MRI-units (Koninklijke Philips N.V., Amsterdam, the Netherlands). T1 maps were processed in IntelliSpace software (Koninklijke Philips N.V., Amsterdam, The Netherlands).

The gold standard for CR was defined as pathological complete response (pCR) histopathological confirmation on the surgical specimen, while or clinical complete response (cCR) was radiological evidence on three-month follow-up MRI with DWI combined with endoscopy.

The trial is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov under ID NCT05876026.

Participants

All participants followed the national fast-track treatment pathway guidelines for rectal cancer.

The inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, histologically confirmed rectal adenocarcinoma, planned treatment with NAT as first-line therapy, and subsequent management with either surgical resection in accordance with national guidelines or a WW strategy for organ preservation [12].

Exclusion criteria were contraindications for MRI, or previous radiotherapy to the pelvis for other reasons.

Two gastrointestinal radiologists (SR, MB) drew the regions of interest by contouring the tumours upon the T1 maps. The exams were performed during the routine MRI scans for each participant. The first T1RT was measured on the primary staging MRI. The final and second measurement was done on the restaging MRI scan six weeks after conclusion of NAT.

Treatment

Treatment was recommended by a multidisciplinary team conference in accordance with national guidelines [13].

Radiotherapy as monotherapy consisted of short-course radiotherapy (SCRT), delivering 25 Gy in 5 fractions.

Long-course chemoradiotherapy consisted of 50.4 Gy in 28 fractions combined with capecitabine chemotherapy. Some patients were randomised to receive 62 Gy as part of a separate study conducted outside our department. In these patients, 62 Gy was delivered in 28 fractions as a radiotherapy boost, while the standard treatment field, including the mesorectum, received 50.4 Gy.

Total neoadjuvant therapy followed the RAPIDO and PRODIGE-23 regimens. The RAPIDO regimen combines SCRT with six cycles of CAPOX chemotherapy. The PRODIGE-23 regimen combines long-course chemoradiotherapy with capecitabine and six cycles of FOLFIRINOX.

Chemotherapy as monotherapy was selected for certain frail patients.

Immunotherapy consisted of nivolumab or ipilimumab and was selected for patients with deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) genes [14].

Endpoint

The gold standard for CR was pCR, confirmed by histopathological examination of the resected specimen, which was available for surgically treated patients. Patients in the WW regimen could achieve cCR, which was defined as absence of residual tumour on restaging at the three-month follow-up MRI scan (including resolution of DWI signal) combined with endoscopy.

MRI T1RT measured six weeks after concluded NAT was compared with the gold standard. The response assessment was based on all three modalities: endoscopy, T2W-MRI, and DWI. If any discrepancies, the overall response assessment relied more on endoscopy than on MRI. A near-CR evaluation/term was used in patients with good responses and a high likelihood of evolving into a cCR response with further observation [7]. The histopathological method was the postsurgical pathological tumour-node-stage version 8 [15].

MRI and measurement of T1 relaxation time

Experienced gastrointestinal radiologists drew the regions of interest by contouring the rectal tumours upon the T1 maps with a free hand technique (Fig. 1). T2 weighted MRI sequences were evaluated as deemed necessary to contour tumours. Areas of artefacts were excluded. In cases where the tumour spanned multiple slices, regions of interest were marked on each slice with visible tumour. The T1RT was measured blinded to the histopathological properties and choice of treatment. Both radiologists had participated in an MRI rectal cancer workshop. The senior radiologist had 20 years of MRI experience of staging rectal cancer.

The parametric T1RT maps were generated at the MRI unit using the vendor's software. Transfer to IntelliSpace (Koninklijke Philips N.V., Amsterdam, the Netherlands) was done on the MRI scanner console. We used a modified look-locker inversion recovery (MOLLI) sequence and fixed the heartrate to 60 beats per minute. The scheme was a 5(3)3-rhythm. The matrix size (field of view, anterior-posterior x Right-Left x Foot-Head) was 300 × 157.5 × 62. The slice thickness was 10 mm. Scan duration was 55 s. The reconstructed voxel size was 0.94 × 0.94 mm, while the acquired was 1.45 × 1.75 mm. Parameters for the T1RT and all other sequences for rectal cancer MRI are recently published in detail [16].

Volume and diffusion restriction

Tumour volume was measured by drawing with free hand technique on the T2 weighted images axial to the tumour axis. This was done in RIPA SYD (Koninklijke Philips N.V., Amsterdam, the Netherlands). The software accumulated and calculated the volume in cm3.

Diffusion restriction properties were evaluated by the ADC. This was measured in RIPA SYD when drawing a region of interest on the ADC sequence. The value for magnetic gradient pulses (b-values) was 200–400–600-800-1000 s/mm2.

Sample size

The null hypothesis posits that T1RT had no diagnostic value in determining treatment induced fibrosis and CR in rectal cancer following NAT. Based on this hypothesis, a sample size calculation was performed to reach a statistical power of 80% with an α = 0.05. Assuming a rate of CR of 10% and 20% of missing data/loss to follow-up, the target sample size was 76 patients. The calculation was based on numbers from Lian et al. [10].

Statistics

Descriptive statistics will be reported as counts (n) for categorical variables and as mean with standard deviations for continuous variables.

The MRI T1RT was analysed using paired t-test to compare the relaxation time before and after NAT. We performed a variance comparison test to verify the two groups had equal statistical variance at baseline. Data were visualised using box plots.

Diagnostic performance of pre- and posttreatment T1RT when detecting response to NAT was analysed with ROC. The gold standard was used to define treatment response. To establish a cut-off for T1RT indicating CR, Youden's Index was applied [17]. The robustness of this threshold was subsequently evaluated through a five-fold cross-validation, to reduce the risk of overestimating performance by avoiding validation on the same dataset from which the method was developed.

Pearson's correlation coefficients were used to estimate the correlation between T1RT and tumour volume and ADC, respectively.

Statistical analysis was performed in Stata (version 18.0, StataCorp, Texas, USA). A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics

The study posed no additional risk, as T1RT mapping was obtained using non-invasive MRI sequences acquired during the clinically indicated MRI. This project was approved by The Regional Committees on Health Research Ethics for Southern Denmark (S-20230004) and conducted in accordance with national regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki [18]. Written informed consent was waived by the committee. No data has been shared. Participant data was pseudonymised.

T1 Relaxation time

In our population sample, we found T1RT to be 1399 ms (95% CI 1356–1442) at baseline, and 1027 ms (95% CI 895–1160) six weeks after conclusion of NAT, p < 0.01.

Before treatment, the CR-group had a T1RT of 1370 ms (95% CI 1294–1446) whereas the iCR group had a T1RT of 1419 ms (95% CI 1365–1473), p = 0.22. T1RT decreased after concluding NAT, the CR-group decreased significantly to 632 ms (95% CI 542–723) and the iCR-group did not but ended at 1301 ms (95% CI 1165–1437). This difference at six weeks between the two groups was statistically significant, p < 0.01.

The groups had equal statistical variance of T1RT at baseline. We found that there was a higher statistical variance in the iCR-group at six weeks follow-up compared with the CR-group. The iCR-group had a statistical variance of 134 ms that increased to 337 ms, whereas the statistical variance in the CR-group went from 153 ms to 183 ms (Fig. 3). In the iCR-group, we observed two outliers with a low T1RT, these instances were due to incorrect angulation, which lead to suboptimal T1 maps.Fig. 3Box plots of the T1 relaxation time at baseline and at six weeks follow-up in the complete and incomplete response groups. When comparing T1RT found in the CR-group and the one in the iCR-group, we found the optimised cut off value for determining CR was 1088 milliseconds. At this threshold value, the sensitivity was 88.5% (95% CI 69.8–97.6) and the specificity was 100% (95% CI 81.5–100) while the AUC was 0.94 (95% CI: 0.82–1.00). This results in a positive predictive value for CR of 100% (95% CI 85.2–100) and a negative predictive value of 85.7% (95% CI 63.7–97.0).

To further test the robustness of the model, we performed a five-fold cross validation with bootstrap sampling. The five-fold cross validation was chosen to achieve the optimal sized samples within our population size. This resulted in a mean AUC of 0.91 (95% CI 0.78–1.00).

Tumour categories

T1RT was not statistically different across the different tumour categories, neither at baseline, p = 0.06, or six weeks follow-up after completion of NAT, p = 0.06 (Fig. 4 A). Tumours in categories cT1–2 had a mean T1RT of 1373 ms (95% CI 1325–1420). We suspected that the association between T1RT and CR could be confounded by tumour category. However, a logistic regression model of T1RT at follow-up adjusted for baseline tumour categories showed a significant increase in risk of iCR with increasing T1RT. For every 100 ms increase in T1RT at six weeks follow-up, odds ratio increased by 2.16 (95% CI 1.23–3.78), p < 0.01.

Node stage

Tumour T1RT was not statistically different across the cN-stages, neither at baseline, p = 0.83, nor at six weeks follow-up, p = 0.29 (Fig. 4B). Patients with cN0 disease had a mean T1RT at 1372 ms (95% CI 1296–1449), cN1 disease 1414 ms (95% CI 1368–1460) and cN2 a mean of 1400 ms (95% CI 1326–1473).

Extramural venous invasion status

When comparing positive and negative extramural venous invasion on MRI (mrEMVI) at baseline, the T1RT had equal statistical variance. Further, T1RT was similar independently of mrEMVI status at baseline. Patients with mrEMVI- disease had a mean T1RT of 1418 ms (95% CI 1359–1477) and for patients with mrEMVI+ disease the mean was 1384 ms (95% CI 1318–1449), p = 0.34. At six weeks follow-up comparing T1RT in relation with baseline mrEMVI status, the T1RT was significantly lower in the group with mrEMVI- disease with a mean of 796 ms (95% CI 597–996) and for the mrEMVI+ group 1220 ms (95% CI 1074–1366), p < 0.01 (Fig. 4C).

Treatment regimen

Treatment regimens were different in the CR and iCR-groups (Table 1). At baseline, all treatment regimen-groups were similar in statistical variance of T1RT. At six weeks follow-up, T1RT had more statistical variance in all groups (Fig. 4D). Comparing treatments across response types, the treatment regimens are also similar regarding T1RT (Fig. 4EF), although statistical variance was larger in the chemoradiotherapy-group. All treatment strategies revealed equal T1RTs at follow-up.

Mesorectal fascia involvement.

Involvement of the mesorectal fascia (MRF) was found to have no impact on T1RT in tumours at baseline. MRF- disease had a mean T1RT of 1393 ms (95% CI 1353–1433) and MRF+ disease a mean of 1404 ms (95% CI 1333–1474), p = 0.81. At follow up, we found the mean of tumours with initial MRF- disease had a mean of 860 ms (95% CI 654–1065) and MRF+ disease had a mean of 1144 ms (95% CI 976–1313), p = 0.03 (Fig. 4G).

Sigmoid take-off

Comparing T1RT based on tumour location in relation to the sigmoid take-off (STO) we saw a mean for tumours being completely rectal of STO at 1411 ms (95%CI 1362–1460) and partly oral of STO at 1345 ms (95% CI 1244–1447), p = 0.24.

At follow-up the mean T1RT for tumours initially below STO was 985 ms (95% CI 832–1139). For tumours initially partly oral of STO had a mean of 1217 ms (95% CI 970–1464), p = 0.18 (Fig. 4H).

Tumour volume

The tumour volume did not correlate with the T1RT. Neither at baseline, −0.071, nor at six weeks follow-up, 0.392. The change in tumour volume did not correlate with T1RT at follow-up either, 0.244 (Fig. 5CD).

We saw no correlation with tumour volume and the ADC value at baseline, −0.143 nor at six weeks follow-up, −0.178. Neither did we see a correlation between the change in T1RT and tumour volume, 0.24.

Discussion

In this prospective study, quantitative MRI T1RT demonstrated high accuracy for identifying CR following NAT in patients with rectal cancer. Tumours classified as cT1–2 at baseline exhibited a significant reduction in T1RT six weeks after completion of NAT. We found no correlation between T1RT and tumour volume, or between T1RT and ADC, indicating that T1RT provides tissue information that is complementary to, and independent of, conventional morphological and diffusion-based MRI parameters.

The diagnostic performance of T1RT observed in our study hold promise for avoiding unnecessary surgery. A larger fraction of patients is likely to be selected for NAT, as patients are younger and tumours more advanced [13], [19]. The improvement of treatment effectiveness will ultimately result in more patients achieving CR and only solidifies the necessity for more reliable tools available to select patients for WW with more confidence [20].

We observed an excellent interobserver agreement with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.93 and 0.91 during two readings of 41 T1 maps. We saw an excellent intraobserver agreement for one observer with an ICC of 0.94 and for the second observer 0.90 [21].

Our study found T1RTs in rectal adenocarcinomas that were comparable to those literature reported previously [10], [11], [20]. However, we found a lower T1RT than Lian et al. This may be due to Lian et al. combining ypT0 with ypT1 tumours, which is much different from our analysis. We achieved a higher rate of CR, at 40%, compared to Lian et al. at 30% [10]. This might be due to a higher radiation dose used in our population compared with standard recommendations. Lian et al.'s study did not focus on radiation dose.

No previous studies have investigated the correlation between T1RT and either the ADC or the tumour volume.

Tumour volume decreased universally after NAT and T1RT did not, as the iCR group was unchanged. T1RT varied more between CR and iCR. We did observe a slightly increasing linear fit with a weak correlation between T1RT and tumour volume at six weeks follow-up. This is most likely due to T1RT reflecting the tissue structure rather than volume. Small tumours are more difficult to detect on the T2 weighted images and are at greater risk of shine-through artefacts on DWI [6]. Therefore, a T1RT that differs greatly between fibrosis in CR to active tumour tissue in the iCR may be advantageous. This finding was interesting, as tumour volume and change in tumour volume is linked to the rate of CR [22], [23].

The ADC did not correlate with T1RT. Lian et al. did not investigate a possible correlation between T1RT and the ADC and found no difference in ADC between the good and poor-response groups. Low post NAT ADC values have been associated with poor prognosis [24]. We did not investigate a possible link between the ADC prior to and the T1RT after NAT. The ADC prior to NAT has been associated with pathological CR to total neoadjuvant treatment [25]. However, Schurink et al. concluded that clinical evaluation of DWI should be limited to visual assessment and not ADC quantification in current practice [26].

When comparing tumours according to their location relative to the STO, we observed a significant difference in T1RT at both baseline and follow-up for tumours located below the STO. This subgroup comprised 36 patients. The observed difference may reflect the general reduction in T1RT across all tumours rather than an effect of tumour location, although the converse cannot be excluded. Further investigation in a larger sample is required to clarify this finding.

For tumours with MRF involvement, the 95% confidence intervals for T1RT overlapped at both baseline and follow-up MRI. However, at follow-up, the p-value indicated statistical significance. A larger study population may provide greater statistical power to determine whether this association is robust.

Our study has some limitations. The study was a single centre study, due to challenges with obtaining licenses from vendors, and a multicentre study seemed beyond our reach. We did not reach the desired population size as set by the sample size calculation. We tried to compensate for the smaller sample size by cross validation with bootstrapping to test the robustness of the model. Previous findings demonstrated that mucinous tumours have higher T1RT compared to the non-mucinous parts of the adenocarcinomas [27]. Unfortunately, we did not have a large enough population sample to do any meaningful statistics on this. Recently, a study reported low variation of T1RTs across multiple scanners, softwares, and vendors in both phantoms and in vivo [28].

Six weeks follow-up from completion of NAT was standard within the department as this follows the recommendations by the European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) [29]. Continued measures of T1RT in participants could be of interest, as it is known that treatment response evolves well beyond six weeks after completion. Especially, as total neoadjuvant therapy may have an optimal evaluation window beyond 20 weeks after completion.

Future research could aim towards a multi-centre study and for a larger population to confirm our results. Measuring T1RT in rectal cancer has been shown to be very reproducible [10], [11]. Additional investigation is warranted to determine whether mrEMVI status can serve as a predictor of CR to NAT. In our cohort, baseline T1RTs differed according to mrEMVI status. Patients with mrEMVI- disease exhibited mean T1RTs and 95% CI below the threshold associated with CR at six weeks after completion of NAT. These findings suggest that mrEMVI status may have an important role in the early identification of patients likely to achieve CR. Further, artificial intelligence might be a well-suited addition to this tool, as T1 maps provide objective, numerical values and could provide clear results. Micro-enema may enhance the results from DWI [30], [31].

In conclusion, quantitative MRI T1RT demonstrates high diagnostic accuracy for identification of CR six weeks after completion of NAT in patients with rectal cancer. Importantly, T1RT showed no correlation with tumour volume or the ADC, indicating that it captures treatment induced tissue characteristics that are not reflected by conventional metrics. These findings support the role of T1RT as a complementary imaging biomarker for response assessment and suggest potential identification of candidates for organ-preserving approaches.
