Experience Using Efgartigimod to Treat Juvenile Myasthenia Gravis in China: A Multicenter Retrospective Study
Authors: Jing Lin, Guoyan Qi, Susan Luo, Qilong Jiang, Bitao Bu, Min Liu, Yuzhen Wei, Meigui Han, Junhong Yang, Zhilan Zhao, Lili Zhang, Yifan Zhang, Haoyou Xu, Xingjie Hao, Zhijun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsCurrent therapeutic management of juvenile myasthenia gravis (JMG) predominantly relies on conventional immunosuppressive therapies and expert consensus extrapolated from adult data, creating a critical gap in high‐quality, pediatric‐specific clinical evidence. The aim of this study was to assess the efficacy and safety of efgartigimod in the pediatric population.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective study enrolled JMG patients (under 18 years), from 12 Chinese centers, who received at least one dose of efgartigimod. Efgartigimod was administered as weekly intravenous infusions. Study visits included W0 (baseline: pretreatment) and W1–W4 (weekly posttreatment follow‐ups). Efficacy was assessed via MG‐related Activities of Daily Living (MG‐ADL) score, Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis (QMG) score, and the MG Composite (MGC) scale.ResultsSeventeen JMG patients (3 males, 14 females) with a median disease duration of 23 months were included. At W0, 88.2% of patients were classified as Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America (MGFA) class II–V and were anti‐acetylcholine receptor (anti‐AchR) antibody positive. After efgartigimod treatment, clinically meaningful improvement (CMI) was observed in 70.6% (W1), 73% (W2), and 91.7% (W4); 66.7% achieved minimal symptom expression by W4. MG‐ADL and QMG scores decreased by 30% and 30.1% (W1) and by 87.6% and 71.8% (W4) compared to W0. No treatment‐related adverse events occurred.DiscussionOur study suggests efgartigimod is an effective therapeutic option for JMG, with favorable efficacy and safety profiles. Further studies are necessary to validate the efficacy and safety of efgartigimod in the pediatric population.

Introduction

Juvenile myasthenia gravis (JMG), characterized by onset before 18 years of age, shares pathophysiological and symptomatic features with adult‐onset MG [1, 2], and accounts for up to 45% of MG cases in Southern China [3]. Due to its low incidence, prospective randomized trials are challenging to conduct, and treatment strategies are largely extrapolated from adult guidelines. However, some JMG patients respond inadequately to conventional immunosuppressants, particularly corticosteroids, or develop substantial adverse effects such as growth retardation, necessitating faster, more effective, and safer therapeutic alternatives [2, 4].

Recent advancements in adult generalized MG (gMG) management have focused on innovative biological agents, particularly neonatal fragment crystallizable receptor (FcRn) blockers [5]. Efgartigimod, a humanized IgG1 Fc fragment that accelerates pathogenic IgG degradation by blocking FcRn, has demonstrated rapid efficacy and safety in adult gMG, leading to the US Food and Drug Administration approval in December 2021 for anti‐acetylcholine receptor (AChR) antibody‐positive adult gMG patients [6, 7]. Given the shared pathophysiology between JMG and adult MG, efgartigimod presents a potential therapeutic alternative for pediatric patients who require faster and safer therapeutic outcomes. In this multicenter retrospective study, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of efgartigimod in Chinese JMG patients.

Methods

Study Design

This multicenter retrospective case series was conducted at 12 centers in China between September 2023 and May 2024. All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki (2000). Informed consent was obtained from all legal guardians before treatment for the off‐label use of efgartigimod in JMG. Patients diagnosed with JMG were administered efgartigimod at a dose of 10 mg/kg once weekly. Each treatment cycle comprised four weekly doses of efgartigimod. Additionally, all patients continued to receive standardized immunosuppressive therapies, including steroids and other immunosuppressants.

The demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with JMG were collected, including age at onset, weight, height, initial symptoms, disease duration, clinical presentation, Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America (MGFA) classification [8], comorbidities, ongoing immunosuppressive therapies, and timing of efgartigimod administration. Additionally, serum IgG, IgM, IgA, total cholesterol, and albumin levels were measured before and after efgartigimod treatment. The treatment efficacy was evaluated at each visit using the MG‐related Activities of Daily Living (MG‐ADL) and Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis (QMG) scores [8, 9].

Clinically meaningful improvement (CMI) was defined as a reduction of at least 2 points in the MG‐ADL score compared to the baseline [10]. Minimal symptom expression (MSE) was defined as a score of 0 or 1 on the MG‐ADL scale [11]. The primary efficacy endpoint was the proportion of patients with JMG achieving CMI at W4. The secondary efficacy endpoints were the mean changes in the MG‐ADL and QMG scores at W1 and W4 compared with the baseline. W0 (baseline) refers to the visit before efgartigimod administration, while W1 to W4 denote weekly follow‐up visits after the first injection at W0. All adverse events, including injection‐related and hypersensitivity reactions, as well as infectious and noninfectious reactions, were systematically documented.

Participants

The definitive diagnosis of all participants was established according to the following criteria: (1) Typical clinical presentation with fluctuating muscle weakness and fatigability, plus at least one of the following; (2) positive neostigmine test; (3) abnormal neuromuscular transmission confirmed by repetitive nerve stimulation (RNS); and (4) the presence or absence of AChR antibodies. Participants younger than 18 years with JMG who received at least one dose of intravenous efgartigimod were included. Patients with refractory JMG (R‐JMG) were defined as those who had received ≥ 2 immunotherapies at adequate doses and durations with suboptimal clinical responses [12].

Appropriate testing was performed to exclude the following conditions: (1) congenital myasthenic syndromes, (2) transient neonatal myasthenia, (3) Kearns–Sayre syndrome (KSS), and (4) atypical Grave's disease.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses and graphing were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.2.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data or as median and interquartile range (IQR) for non‐normally distributed data. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess data normality. A one‐way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to normally distributed datasets for longitudinal analyses across multiple time points. At the same time, the Friedman test was used for nonparametric variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of JMG Patients

This study included 17 JMG patients with a notable female predominance (82.4%) and a median duration of 23 months. The cohort represented a population with a substantial disease burden: At baseline, most patients presented with moderate‐to‐severe disease (MGFA classes II–V) and were AChR antibody positive (Table 1). A high proportion of ocular‐onset MG patients progressed to gMG. The primary indications for efgartigimod administration were acute exacerbation (16/17) or insufficient clinical response (1/17) despite previous treatment with pyridostigmine and various immunomodulatory agents. Patients received between one and four doses of efgartigimod based on clinical response and economic considerations, as detailed in Tables 1 and S1.

Therapeutic Responses to Efgartigimod in JMG Patients

Efgartigimod demonstrated rapid and potent therapeutic effects in this JMG cohort. Following the initial dose, 70.6% of patients (12/17) achieved CMI at W1, with Patient #6 achieving MSE (Figure 1A). Patient #2 experienced myasthenic crisis 1 day following the first injection due to coronavirus infection, and recovered quickly with steroid pulse therapy (500 mg/day for 4 days) while continuing efgartigimod treatment. This early response translated into sustained and deeper clinical benefits: Among the 12 evaluable patients at W4, 91.7% (11/12) achieved CMI, and 66.7% (8/12) met rigorous MSE criteria. Additionally, Patients #11 and #12 maintained sustained remission with MSE status after 2 cycles of efgartigimod therapy. Six patients did not complete one cycle of treatment due to symptom relief and economic considerations. All six patients demonstrated clinical improvement after one or two treatments, leading to discontinuation of efgartigimod therapy. Among these six patients, four (66.7%) achieved CMI at W1, while two patients (#7, #13) showed no change (Table S1; Figures S1 and S2).

This study included 11 R‐JMG patients, all of whom exhibited clinical deterioration prior to efgartigimod initiation (Figure S3). Following treatment, all R‐JMG patients achieved significant MG‐ADL improvement by W4, with over half attaining MSE status (Figure S4A,B).

Efgartigimod provided rapid stabilization for patients in myasthenic crisis (MGFA class V) and demonstrated sustained efficacy in seronegative individuals. These findings suggest that efgartigimod serves as a robust intervention for complex JMG cases that are traditionally difficult to manage (Figure S4).

Safety Profiles of Efgartigimod in JMG Patients

None of the patients in this cohort reported medication‐related allergic reactions, infections, or serious adverse events during the treatment or follow‐up periods. No deaths attributable to adverse events were observed. At W4, following the completion of the initial treatment cycle, serum IgG levels were significantly reduced compared to the baseline (6.57:9.786, p = 0.0203) (Figure S5A). However, no significant differences were observed in IgM, IgA, total cholesterol, or albumin levels before or after treatment (Figure S5B–E). Patient #2 developed COVID‐19 during efgartigimod therapy, triggering a myasthenic crisis; however, the patient rapidly recovered. Patient #1, with a history of HCV infection, exhibited no evidence of liver dysfunction or acute viral reactivation posttreatment.

Discussion

In this study, efgartigimod demonstrated a rapid clinical response during the 4‐week observation period in patients with JMG. A high proportion of patients achieved CMI as early as the first week, with the majority attaining MSE by W4. Consistent efficacy was confirmed across both MG‐ADL and QMG scores. These findings are consistent with data from Phase 2/3 clinical trials in adult patients with MG [13, 14]. Furthermore, we observed that efgartigimod rapidly improved strength across all muscle groups, including ocular, bulbar, limb, and respiratory functions.

The management of JMG has long relied on extrapolation from adult treatment guidelines. While glucocorticoids (GC) remain effective, chronic administration carries significant risks including growth retardation. Conventional immunosuppressants such as azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, and tacrolimus offer alternatives, but require extended periods to achieve therapeutic efficacy and are frequently used off‐label [15, 16]. In contrast, efgartigimod provides rapid clinical response without the steroid‐associated systemic metabolic burden. The therapeutic landscape for pediatric gMG has recently expanded, with the US Food and Drug Administration approving eculizumab for patients aged 6 years and older [17]. A Phase III trial of eculizumab (NCT03759366) in refractory pediatric gMG showed significant improvement in QMG scores from baseline to Week 26 [18]. Our study contributes to this evolving evidence base by demonstrating the efficacy of efgartigimod in JMG, suggesting it as a promising treatment option. This is further supported by ongoing investigations, including a clinical trial evaluating the pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and safety of efgartigimod in children with gMG (NCT04833894) [19], and the ADAPT Jr SC study examining efgartigimod PH20 SC in children aged 2 to less than 18 years (NCT06392386) [20].

A particularly notable finding is the robust efficacy of efgartigimod in R‐JMG. Our cohort, characterized by inadequate response to conventional therapies, showed substantial clinical efficacy shortly after initiating efgartigimod, consistent with outcomes observed in the adult REGAIN trial [21, 22]. These findings align with the demonstration that ≥ 50% of refractory MG patients achieved clinical benefit, with 80% able to reduce or discontinue concurrent medications [23]. Additionally, a recent case report highlighted the efficacy of efgartigimod in a seronegative refractory patient [24]. This rapid and robust response rate underlines the potential of efgartigimod as a transformative therapy for R‐JMG.

Beyond chronic management, efgartigimod demonstrates utility in acute disease exacerbations. Notably, several patients in our cohort with disease exacerbation achieved CMI within one to two doses of efgartigimod, indicating rapid therapeutic onset. Traditional salvage therapies such as intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and plasma exchange (PE) also decrease circulating pathogenic IgG levels [25]. Although IVIG may be more feasible than PE for pediatric patients with difficult venous access [2], its availability is often limited. Efgartigimod offers a valuable alternative for acute exacerbations or crises, providing targeted FcRn blockade that induces rapid pathogenic IgG reduction with greater convenience and biological specificity than PE [26]. While initiating this high‐cost therapy in acute settings requires careful consideration, the ability to achieve early disease stabilization may prevent prolonged hospitalizations or myasthenic crises, as exemplified by the rapid recovery of our patient with COVID‐19‐induced crisis. From a clinician's perspective, this risk‐benefit profile supports considering efgartigimod when rapid antibody clearance is required. However, the long‐term sustainability and cost‐effectiveness remain to be further evaluated.

This study had several limitations. First, the analysis was based on a small sample size which may have been affected by different disease states and was conducted across multiple centers using a retrospective design. Second, the multicenter retrospective approach complicates the standardization of clinical assessments and serological examinations. Third, because efgartigimod is used off‐label in children, it is often selected when traditional immunosuppressants are ineffective, resulting in a predominance of refractory cases. Fourth, all the pediatric participants were adolescents; therefore, the findings cannot be generalized to infants and toddlers. Fifth, the research and follow‐up periods were relatively short, limiting the availability of long‐term efficacy and safety data. Additionally, data on the reduction of GC or immunosuppressive agents were not collected, which may contribute to alleviating the side effects associated with long‐term GC use. Finally, most patients did not undergo repeat AChR‐Ab testing at W4, which prevented further assessment of efgartigimod's effectiveness in clearing pathogenic antibodies.

In conclusion, efgartigimod has a favorable safety profile and potential clinical efficacy in patients with JMG, including those with refractory disease. These preliminary findings support further investigation of efgartigimod as a therapeutic option; however, its effectiveness and safety require definitive validation in larger, well‐controlled studies.
