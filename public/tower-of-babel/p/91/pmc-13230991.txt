Association between hospital frailty risk score and in-hospital mortality in critically ill patients with sepsis: results from MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Yali Xu, Xiya Wang, Andong Li, Shubin Guo, Xue Mei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the relationship between frailty assessed by the HFRS and in-hospital mortality in ICU patients with sepsis.MethodA retrospective analysis of septic ICU patients from the MIMIC-IV database assessed frailty through the Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS). Patients were categorized into non-frail (HFRS < 5, n = 4,882), pre-frail (5 ≤ HFRS < 15, n = 3,134), and frail (HFRS ≥ 15, n = 2,575) groups. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Logistic regression combined with restricted cubic splines (RCS) was employed to evaluate the association between HFRS (categorical and continuous) and mortality. Inverse probability weighting (IPW) validated the results, and subgroup analyses explored frailty-mortality correlations in different patient groups.ResultsA total of 10,591 patients were included, with 4,737 (44.7%) males and median age of 68.9[57.6, 79.6] years. Altogether, 3,024 (28.6%) experienced mortality during hospitalization. Elevated frailty levels were associated with increased in-hospital mortality, consistent across both continuous and categorical HFRS analyses. A linear association between HFRS and mortality risk was indicated by results from RCS. After controlling for potential confounders, both pre-frail and frail statuses were significantly correlated with higher in-hospital mortality risk (pre-frailty, RR = 1.15, 95% CI: [1.06, 1.26], P = 0.002; frailty, RR = 1.29, 95% CI: [1.17, 1.42], P < 0.001). Furthermore, frailty was significantly positively correlated with longer hospital and ICU stays. These findings were confirmed by IPW.ConclusionElevated frailty assessed via HFRS was associated with an increased risk of in-hospital mortality and prolonged hospital and ICU stays in sepsis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47654-9.

Introduction

Sepsis is defined as a dysregulated host response to infection that leads to organ dysfunction1. It is one of the leading causes of death globally, accounting for 19% to 33% of total deaths and being one of the most common factors for ICU admission in critically ill patients2, with an in-hospital mortality rate as high as 40%3,4. To improve clinical outcomes in sepsis, extensive research, awareness campaigns, and improvement initiatives for sepsis identification and treatment have been published and implemented1,2. Some sepsis-related deaths can be prevented through more aggressive inpatient treatment and supportive care5, making it essential to identify new risk factors for effective intervention.

Frailty is a complex, clinically identifiable syndrome characterized by a decline in multiple physiological systems and an increased susceptibility to stressors, which results in an elevated risk of adverse outcomes6. Due to the absence of a unified assessment standard for frailty, the global prevalence of frailty remains unclear. However, it is certain that frailty is highly prevalent in middle-aged and elderly populations: approximately 7%–30% of community-dwelling older adults7,8, 40%–50% of hospitalized middle-aged and elderly individuals6,44, and 40% of infected ICU patients are classified as frail9,10.As a common geriatric syndrome, frailty is typically linked to immune dysfunction and chronic low-grade inflammation8, characteristics that resemble the immune imbalances observed in sepsis. Frail patients are more likely to have poor responses to both internal and external stressors, which contributes to an augmented risk of various adverse outcomes, including functional decline, falls, disability, and death. In the long term, even after discharge, decreased physiological function, impaired quality of life, and increased susceptibility to infections can be caused by frailty, which leads to repeated hospitalizations11. Frailty can impair immune function, chronic inflammation, and immune tolerance, leading sepsis to display a slower and less effective immune response when facing infections, thereby resulting in more severe clinical consequences12–15. Increasing evidence suggests that frailty is a key factor affecting the prognosis of sepsis, and the coexistence of frailty and sepsis may contribute to increased mortality in infected patients10.

A globally standardized assessment tool for frailty remains yet to be established. Although a variety of assessment instruments are available for the clinical evaluation of frailty16, the application of these methods is limited by several drawbacks. Frailty identification generally requires face-to-face assessments conducted by qualified clinicians; some assessments also involve the measurement of grip strength and walking speed using specialized equipment, which are susceptible to subjectivity and delays in assessment17,18. For clinicians, instrument-based and bedside frailty assessment metrics are essential; however, there is also a need for a simple, time-efficient frailty assessment tool that facilitates decision-making regarding intervention implementation and nursing resource allocation6, thereby enabling frailty screening for the majority of hospitalized middle-aged and elderly patients. The Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS) stratifies patients into different frailty categories based on the number of comorbidities extracted from electronic health record (EHR) data. Its primary strength lies in avoiding extra data collection, and it has been validated to exhibit universal applicability in medical settings for predicting adverse clinical outcomes19–22. HFRS has been proven effective in predicting clinical outcomes across multiple patient populations, including those with heart failure20,21, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease23, malignant tumors24 and pancreatitis25. However, research on the application of HFRS in ICU sepsis patients remains relatively limited. The objective of this study was to explore the association between frailty status assessed by HFRS and the risk of in-hospital mortality among ICU sepsis.

Methods

Data extraction

In this study, all data were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database (v3.1; Certification ID: 66,067,288) via SQL with Navicat Premium (v16.3.7). The database is an open-access relational database managed by the Laboratory for Computational Physiology at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. It includes data from ICU patients at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, spanning 2008 to 2022. The dataset covers hospitalized patients with a total of 546,028 admission records, among which 94,458 were admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU), with diagnoses coded according to the ICD-9 and ICD-10 classification systems.

Study population

The inclusion criteria were patients diagnosed with sepsis who were admitted to the ICU for treatment. Both ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnostic codes were employed to minimize omissions in early coding information, with the ICD codes (International Classification of Diseases) for identifying sepsis provided in the supplementary materials. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) ICU admission time less than 1 day; 2) previous ICU admission; 3) age under 18 years. The included and excluded patients are depicted in the technical flowchart. The following data were collected for the study population: basic characteristics (including age, sex, Body Mass Index), vital signs within 24 h of the first ICU admission, laboratory items from the first day of ICU admission, disease severity scores (GCS, LODS, SAPS II), comorbidities, interventions administered in the ICU (including CRRT, MV, and vasopressors), as well as the length of hospital and ICU stays, and survival status at discharge.

Frailty assessment based on ICD-10 Codes AndICD-9 Codes

Frailty in sepsis were evaluated by the Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS), a validated tool specifically designed to quantify frailty based on a patient’s medical history40. The HFRS is a weighted scoring system that employs ICD-10 codes to assess frailty in hospitalized individuals. It calculates an initial frailty score for each one by summing the weights of one or more of the 109 ICD-10 diagnostic codes recorded at first hospital admission. These codes encompass not only traditional chronic diseases but also conditions such as limitations in mobility, cognitive impairments, and emotional disorders. The inclusion of these diverse factors enables the HFRS to capture frailty-related elements beyond conventional comorbidities, thereby enhancing its utility as a valuable tool for identifying high-risk frailty. All diagnostic codes recorded during hospitalization were extracted and matched against HFRS criteria. Missing scoring items corresponding to diagnostic codes were not assigned any values. A total of 3,334 patients (31.4%) with an HFRS of 0 were categorized as non-frail and enrolled in subsequent analyses. To mitigate the risk of missing early ICD-9 coding information, the 109 ICD-10 codes were mapped to their corresponding ICD-9 codes, and the HFRS score was calculated using both ICD-10 and ICD-9 codes26. Detailed mapping information is provided in the supplementary materials. Frailty was categorized according to the HFRS as follows: non-frail (HFRS < 5), pre-frail (5 ≤ HFRS < 15), and frail (HFRS ≥ 15).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was defined as in-hospital mortality occurring during the index sepsis hospitalization. The secondary outcomes included total hospital length of stay and ICU length of stay during this admission. For the primary outcome adjudication, patients were considered to have died during the current admission only when both a death record existed and the death time matched the discharge time.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R software (Version 4.5.1). During the data preprocessing phase, variables with more than 20% of missing values were excluded from the analysis. The variables ultimately excluded from the analysis were as follows: neutrophils (23.38%), lymphocytes (23.3%), body mass index 39.63%), albumin (34.56%), and lactate dehydrogenase (44.93%). Missing values for other variables were imputed using the Predictive Mean Matchin method, as implemented in the MICE package(version 3.18.0). Five imputed datasets were generated, with the imputation selected based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Frailty assessed by HFRS were utilized to present the baseline characteristics of sepsis. The normality of continuous variables was determined using the Anderson–Darling test. Skewed distributions were summarized as the median and interquartile range (IQR, Q1–Q3), and the Kruskal–Wallis H test was applied to compare characteristics across different frailty stages. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and proportions, with intergroup comparisons performed using the chi-square test. The P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The relationship between frailty and in-hospital mortality was evaluated using both univariate and multivariate logistic regression, with non-frail as the reference category, to compare the effects of pre-frail and frailty on mortality risk. Additionally, the effect of a 1-point continuous HFRS increment on outcomes was evaluate. Three logistic regression models were constructed: Model 1 was a univariate model with frailty assessed by the HFRS; Model 2, based on Model 1, was adjusted for age, gender, disease severity scores, and comorbidities; Building upon Model 2, Model 3 incorporated laboratory items and further adopted bidirectional stepwise regression for variable selection. To prevent redundancy and over-adjustment, cerebrovascular and renal disease were excluded because scores for these conditions are built into the HFRS. Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) analysis was performed for all variables included in the model to avoid multicollinearity, and the collinearity diagnostic report is provided in the supplementary materials.

Inverse probability weighting (IPW) was employed to construct a pseudo-randomized comparison between sepsis with different frailty, adjusting for all variables except the outcome variables. Covariate balance after adjustment was evaluated by analyzing the standardized mean difference (SMD), with values less than 0.1 indicating balance for variables. IPW allocates weights according to each individual’s exposure probability, thereby adjusting to control for selection bias and confounding factors and reducing systematic discrepancies. The results of all logistic regression were presented as adjusted relative risk (Adjusted RR) with 95%CI. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) regression was utilized to evaluate the potential nonlinear relationship between the HFRS and in-hospital mortality risk. Exploratory subgroup analyses were performed to assess differences in in-hospital mortality risk associated with frailty across distinct characteristics of sepsis.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 10,591 sepsis patients from the MIMIC-IV 3.1 database were included in the Investigation. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. According to the HFRS, 4,882 patients (46.1%) were classified as non-frail, 3,134 patients (29.6%) as pre-frail, and 2,575 patients (24.3%) as frail. The median age was 68.9 years, and 4,737 (44.7%) were male. Patients with higher frailty levels were generally older and had a higher proportion of females. Significant differences in vital signs, including heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, SpO2, and glucose, were observed among different frailty groups. Specifically, high-frailty patients presented lower heart rates and higher blood pressure and glucose levels. Laboratory items revealed significant differences in white blood cell, platelets, hematocrit, hemoglobin, bicarbonate, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, calcium, sodium, potassium, INR, APTT, ALT, ALP, and bilirubin among different frailty. Comorbidities including coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, chronic pulmonary diseases, and diabetes were found to increase in prevalence with higher frailty severity. Frailty and outcome events

The outcomes of sepsis with different degrees of frailty are summarized in Table 1. In-hospital mortality occurred in 3,024 sepsis patients, corresponding to a cumulative mortality rate of 28.6% in the cohort. Mortality rates significantly increased with the level of frailty (non-frail group: 25.6%, pre-frail group: 29.8%, frail group: 32.6%). Hospital and ICU length of stay also rose significantly as frailty severity increased (length of hospital stay: non-frail 9.65,17, pre-frail 116,20, frail 17 [9, 30.6]; length of ICU stay: non-frail 3.2 [1.9, 7.1], pre-frail 3.4 [1.9, 7.1]], frail 5.1 [2.5, 11.3]). These findings reveal a significant positive correlation between frailty and in-hospital mortality, as well as both hospital and ICU length of stay in sepsis patients.

Findings from the logistic regression conducted to investigate the association between frailty and in-hospital mortality risk in sepsis are presented in Table 2. Model 1 shows that both pre-frailty and frailty are significantly associated with an increased risk of in-hospital mortality (pre-frail Adjusted RR = 1.16 [95% CI: 1.08, 1.25], P < 0.001; frailty Adjusted RR = 1.27 [95% CI: 1.19, 1.37], P < 0.001). Model 2, which was based on Model 1, adjusted for age, sex, GCS, LODS, SAPS II score, as well as hypertension, coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease, diabetes, and rheumatic diseases. Subgroup analysis

Exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted for hypothesis generation to investigate the differential association of frailty with in-hospital mortality risk across sepsis patients with heterogeneous characteristics. Forest plots are provided in the Supplementary Material (Figure S1). The findings suggested potential heterogeneity in the association between frailty and the administration of vasopressors, MV, and CRRT. Nevertheless, no robust evidence of interaction was established, and the interaction tests were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. Thus, all subgroup results should be interpreted cautiously.

Discussion

The association between frailty assessed by HFRS and the outcome of sepsis in the ICU was investigated. The findings indicated that frailty was descriptively associated with increased in-hospital mortality, as well as prolonged hospital and ICU stays. These associations persisted after IPW adjustment for measured confounders.

Frailty is prevalent among critically ill adults and is strongly correlated with elevated mortality, increased healthcare utilization, and post-discharge disability, which may further enhance risk stratification in ICU patients27–29. Sepsis, a systemic inflammatory response syndrome instigated by infection, induces immune dysregulation and widespread tissue damage, culminating in multi-organ dysfunction2. Sepsis-related mortality is not solely attributable to the infection itself but often represents the “final blow” for frail individuals10. Aging individuals undergo a decline in physiological reserves due to various factors, and their immune and anti-inflammatory responses are impaired, which exacerbates the vulnerability of frail patients. This renders them more susceptible to the deleterious effects of inappropriate inflammatory responses, which ultimately become the “last straw” leading to death30.

The HFRS offers supplementary information beyond age and conventional risk factors, such as whether a patient has mobility limitations, emotional disturbances, or cognitive impairments. Previous research has demonstrated that frailty is significantly associated with a variety of adverse outcomes, including disability, infection, and mortality31–33. More than half of sepsis patients exhibit frailty34,35, and frail sepsis patients face twice the in-hospital risk compared to non-frail patients34, with elevated risks of mortality and adverse events36. Frailty also influences the one-year prognosis of discharged patients37, underscoring the necessity for early frailty screening and intervention. Frailty is a dynamic process, not irreversible30, and can be mitigated through moderate exercise, nutritional support, and other interventions, potentially enhancing outcomes for sepsis patients38,39.

Previous studies have found that frailty, assessed using clinical frailty scales, is an independent predictor of mortality in sepsis patients35. The presence of frailty at admission was associated with an increased in-hospital mortality rate (adjusted odds ratio (OR) and 95% CI: 2.00 [1.39–2.89], p < 0.001). In this study, frailty in sepsis patients was assessed by the HFRS, yielding similar conclusions. Even after adjusting for confounding variables and performing IPW analysis, the effect of the HFRS on mortality risk in sepsis has remained consistent overall, with this effect exhibiting favorable stability and uniformity. In-hospital mortality risk continues to rise progressively as frailty severity increases. These findings demonstrate that HFRS, as a frailty assessment tool, can provide prognostic information for sepsis, facilitate the early identification of high-risk patient groups, and thus support patient care and early intervention implementation.

HFRS can be seamlessly integrated into electronic health record systems, and some hospitals have already successfully implemented it to assess frailty levels. Gilbert and his colleagues conducted external validation, confirming that HFRS’s predictive ability for adverse outcomes is comparable to the Fried and Rockwood scales40. HFRS captures frailty-related information beyond what is covered by age or the Charlson Comorbidity Index. In patients with congestive heart failure, Kwok et al. found that HFRS was a risk factor for increased in-hospital mortality21. Moreover, HFRS-assessed frailty is an independent risk factor for increased stroke risk41, delirium in patients42, and acute exacerbations in COPD23, all of which are associated with longer hospital stays. The principal advantage of HFRS stems from its reliance on automated scoring from patient diagnostic codes and medical records, reducing the potential for observer-related errors compared to face-to-face assessments or physical tests, which feature facilitates rapid utilization in emergency and critical care settings, enabling quick assessment and decision-making20,43.

According to our findings, differences in hypertension prevalence across frailty groups were also identified, with higher prevalence observed in the non-frail group than in the pre-frail and frail groups. This finding potentially linked to reduced physiological reserves, weight loss, and decreased activity levels in frail individuals. The subgroup analysis further yielded significant insights. In exploratory, hypothesis-generating subgroup analyses, the association between frailty and in-hospital mortality appeared to differ across strata defined by vasopressor use, CRRT, and MV. Frailty was associated with higher in-hospital mortality in patients not receiving MV or CRRT, whereas no clear or consistent association was observed in those undergoing these interventions or with septic shock. As the interaction tests were not adjusted for multiple comparisons, these findings may primarily reflect differences in disease severity and treatment indications rather than true effect modification by frailty. Given these limitations, the subgroup results should be interpreted with caution, and further studies with prespecified hypotheses and appropriate multiplicity control are warranted for validation. Furthermore, considering the potential reversibility of frailty, more proactive care and nutritional support may be considered for pre-frail patients undergoing CRRT or MV, potentially mitigating the impact of pre-frailty on mortality risk.

Our research has several strengths. First, to our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the relationship between HFRS and in-hospital mortality in ICU sepsis. Second, the data were obtained from the real-world MIMIC-IV database, which offers a large sample size and robust support. However, our study has several limitations. Considering the retrospective, observational design, selection bias and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Although multivariable adjustment and IPW were applied, key unmeasured factors in MIMIC-IV, such as premorbid functional status, goals of care, and treatment limitations, were unavailable. In addition, missing or incomplete diagnostic information may lead to underestimation of the HFRS, and because HFRS is derived from ICD-coded diagnoses, it may incompletely capture frailty-related domains and may partially reflect comorbidity burden and illness severity rather than baseline frailty per se. Lastly, causal inference is constrained, and prospective multi-cohort studies are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusion

It is demonstrated by this investigation that frailty assessed by the HFRS is associated with an increased risk of in-hospital mortality, prolonged hospital length of stay, and extended ICU length of stay among ICU sepsis patients, with these associations exhibiting variability across different subgroups.
