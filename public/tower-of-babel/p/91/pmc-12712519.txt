Takedown and Repair of Rotator Cuff PASTA Tear With Augmentation Using Long Head of Biceps Tendon
Authors: Ayyappan V. Nair, B. Sai Theja, Sreejith Thampy J, Kiran V, Aravind Rajan, Prince Shanavas Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Partial rotator cuff tears are a prevalent cause of shoulder pain and dysfunction; these are classified into bursal, articular, or within the tendon. Delamination negatively affects healing and clinical and radiologic outcomes, as these are frequently lined with synovial cells. Treatment remains controversial. Recent studies support patch augmentation to enhance healing and outcomes. While bursal-side augmentation has been reported, no study addresses intra-articular augmentation of PASTA (partial articular supraspinatus tendon avulsion) tears. Here we describe the intra-articular augmentation of a PASTA tear of a retracted deep laminar part with the long head of the biceps tendon.

Partial rotator cuff tendon tears are a prevalent cause of shoulder pain and dysfunction, first documented by Codman in 1934.1 These can manifest on the bursal surface, articular surface, or within the tendon, with articular-sided tears being 2 to 3 times more frequent. Tears involving ≥50% of tendon thickness are more likely to progress to full-thickness tears. Tears ≥50% thick progressed in 55% of cases, compared to 14% for <50% tears.2 Most authors recommend repairing a tear with ≥50% thickness, irrespective of the surface involved.2 Treatment remains controversial. Surgical options for delaminated tears encompass “layer-to-layer” repair, excision of the laminated segment of the tear, or a combination of these.3

Recent studies support patch augmentation to enhance healing and outcomes. Many varieties of patches are available, divided into xenografts, allografts, autografts, and synthetic grafts. Synthetic patches do not possess the mechanical properties of native tissue and create more stress shielding, which negatively impacts healing. Xenografts and allografts are culturally unacceptable in some countries.4 The augmentation of the long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) serves as a local autologous source of collagen graft.5 This technique describes a step-by-step arthroscopic approach for integrating the LHBT into the partial articular-sided supraspinatus tendon tear intra-articularly (Video 1).

Surgical Technique

Position

Surgery is typically conducted under general anesthesia with an interscalene block. A standard operating room table is arranged, and the patient is placed in a lateral decubitus position, with the arm positioned at 30° of abduction and 20° of flexion, with traction applied. The bone landmarks are delineated after draping and sterile preparation.

Portal Placement, Diagnostic Arthroscopy, and Acromioplasty

The posterior portal is the primary viewing portal, while the anterior working portal is utilized to assess the glenohumeral joint. Biceps tendonitis and a tear in the articular surface are detected (Fig 1, Fig 2). The same posterior portal is then used to guide the arthroscope into the subacromial region. An 18-gauge spinal needle is employed to accurately locate the lateral portal, strategically positioned at the midpoint of the exposed footprint and 3 cm lateral to the outer edge of the acromion. The bursa is then meticulously cleared using a motorized arthroscopic shaver and radio frequency probe. Acromioplasty is completed.Fig 1Patient in the left lateral decubitus position. Biceps Tenodesis

The biceps tendon in the groove is identified through the lateral portal. Using the anteroinferior portal, the tendon sheath over the biceps tendon is incised, and suprapectoral tenodesis is done with 1 double-loaded all-suture anchor (Sironix). Tenotomy is performed 1 cm proximal to the location of the tenodesis.

Rotator Cuff Footprint Preparation

The partial tear is located, and a takedown is done. The shaver (Arthrex) is now used to prepare the footprint. The bone is meticulously abraded to preserve osseous integrity and lower the possibility of suture anchor withdrawal. A spinal needle is employed to accurately determine the placement for an anchor portal, which is generally positioned just off the lateral edge of the acromion. A triple-loaded suture anchor (Sironix) is then inserted at a 45° angle to the tuberosity plane after a socket has been made using an awl and just lateral to the articular margin. The microfracture awl creates several microfractures across the footprint to promote a healing response. Starting from the anterior aspect, sutures are retrieved individually and passed using a suture-passing device (Scorpion; Arthrex) across the entire tear width. After each pass, the sutures are retrieved and brought out through the anterior portal, with each pass spaced approximately 3 mm apart. The risk of suture cut-through is reduced by placing the suture-passing device as medially as feasible, close to the musculotendinous junction, where tissue quality is usually better. The tenotomized proximal biceps tendon is moved into the deep retracted laminar part of the torn articular side of the rotator cuff defect. The tenotomized proximal biceps tendon is integrated into the deep retracted laminar defect of the torn cuff tendon by utilizing a FiberTape (Arthrex) from a triple-loaded suture anchor. Lateral Row Repair

We enter the subacromial space through the posterior portal. We verify that all 6 components are correctly situated within the subacromial space. We retract all threads to assess the coverage of the footprint. To enhance the coverage of the cuff, an additional free FiberWire (Arthrex) is introduced through the anterior cuff, followed by lateral row fixation using 2 knotless suture anchors (SwiveLock, Arthrex; HEALICOIL, Smith & Nephew). This lateral row repair, in conjunction with the takedown repair and biceps augmentation technique, effectively mitigates the risk of buckling within the bursal layer of the cuff (Fig 5). Postoperative Management

A sling is used for immobilization for 6 weeks. Active assisted range-of-motion exercises for the elbow are initiated in a supine position immediately following the surgery and continued for 6 weeks. Passive range-of-motion exercises for the shoulder commence at 6 weeks postsurgery, with the introduction of active and strengthening exercises permitted at 10 weeks.

Discussion

Resnick6 refers to a specific category of horizontal intrasubstance tears, which involve the retraction of certain intratendinous fibers, as “cuff delamination.” Nakajima et al.7 and Sonnabend et al.8 noted greater retraction on the articular side than the bursal side due to differential stress responses—articular fibers are more prone to tensile loading, leading to intratendinous lamination. Laminated tears are frequently lined with synovial cells, which may inhibit the adherence and healing of opposing tissue layers.8 Dynamic functional studies have shown that PASTA (partial articular supraspinatus tendon avulsion) tears can induce biomechanical dysfunction similar to full-thickness tears.9 Delamination negatively affects healing and clinical and radiologic outcomes. It is often difficult to repair because it cannot reach the retracted end of the deep laminar with a Scorpion. Intra-articular visualization is crucial to ensure suture passage through the retracted layer and prevent repair failure or tear progression and the need for a second surgery.

The ultimate goal of any surgical intervention is biological healing. Although surgical repair can relieve pain and restore function, failure rates remain high within the first year, prompting interest in exploring augmentation methods and incorporating patches.10 The perfect patch would be one that provides desirable biomechanical reinforcement, promotes healing and cellular infiltration without an adverse tissue reaction, and is easy to deliver surgically and stabilize arthroscopically.4 Mihata et al.11 used an autologous fascia lata graft for superior capsular reconstruction for an irreparable retracted rotator cuff (RC) tear.

Past studies focused on bursal-side patching for PASTA tears, which fills the gap over the bursal side and increases the thickness of the cuff. However, patch integration chances are lower, which leads to less stress sharing and more load sharing, which leads to failure of repair. So, to prevent these, we can consider patch augmentation intra-articularly. The LHBT is an alternative to fascia lata and dermal allografts in superior capsular reconstruction, avoiding donor site morbidity and high costs. Biceps-augmented RC repair is used for retracted, degenerate tears with sufficient excursion to the footprint without excessive tension.12 This tenodesis “repair and patch” method is particularly applied in cases where the tendon-capsule tissue is compromised.10 The LHBT is an excellent graft due to its high collagen content, nonimmunogenic properties, mechanical strength, local availability, and minimal morbidity following harvesting. A biceps tenodesis or tenotomy is typically performed in many cases of PASTA repairs. In retracted deep laminar PASTA lesions, the arthroscopic technique of takedown repair, combined with the tenotomization of the pathological biceps tendon, may be an effective treatment option. This approach can cover the defect intra-articularly, maintain the integrity of the RC footprint, enhance tendon healing, and possibly mitigate tendon buckling, residual pain, and retear of the repair associated with traditional tendon repair and patch augmentation methods.
