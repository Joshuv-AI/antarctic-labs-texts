Beauvericin Induces Mitochondrial Apoptosis and Attenuates EMT-Associated Phenotypes and Angiogenic Signaling in Colorectal Cancer Cells In Vitro
Authors: Mikyoung Kwon, Hye Jin Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, largely due to metastasis and therapeutic resistance. Beauvericin, a cyclohexadepsipeptide mycotoxin produced by Beauveria and Cordyceps species, has demonstrated anticancer activity in multiple malignancies; however, its mechanistic effects in CRC have not been fully defined. In this study, we investigated the cellular and molecular effects of beauvericin in HCT116 and SW480 CRC cells using in vitro models. Beauvericin reduced cell viability and clonogenic growth, induced G0/G1 cell-cycle arrest, and activated mitochondria-dependent apoptosis through modulation of Bcl-2 family proteins and caspase activation. At sub-cytotoxic concentrations, beauvericin significantly suppressed migratory and invasive phenotypes and attenuated epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT)–associated features, accompanied by reduced activation of integrin α6/FAK/Src/ERK1/2 signaling without altering total protein expression. Conditioned medium from beauvericin-treated CRC cells markedly inhibited endothelial proliferation, invasion, and tube formation, coinciding with reduced VEGF secretion. Network-based target prediction and immunoblot validation further demonstrated that beauvericin treatment was associated with decreased phosphorylation of JAK2, STAT1, and STAT3. Collectively, these findings indicate that beauvericin modulates multiple malignant phenotypes of CRC cells in vitro by inducing mitochondrial apoptosis and attenuating EMT-associated signaling, VEGF-dependent endothelial activation, and JAK/STAT pathway activity. These results provide mechanistic insight into the anti-tumor potential of beauvericin and support further preclinical evaluation.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. In 2022, it accounted for over 1.9 million new cases and nearly 900,000 deaths, with an estimated global 5-year prevalence exceeding 5 million cases. The development of CRC is closely associated with lifestyle factors such as smoking, high-fat or low-fiber diet, excessive alcohol consumption, sedentary behavior, and obesity [1, 2]. Clinically, CRC is categorized as non-metastatic (stages I–III) or metastatic (stage IV), and the prognosis declines sharply once distant metastases occur [3]. While surgical resection remains the mainstay for early-stage disease, metastatic CRC is typically managed with 5-fluorouracil (5-FU)-based chemotherapy combined with targeted or immunotherapeutic agents, including bevacizumab and anti-PD-1 antibodies [3, 4]. However, the emergence of drug resistance, high recurrence rates, and limited curative options result in unsatisfactory long-term survival [5]. Therefore, the discovery of novel compounds capable of suppressing both proliferation and metastasis is essential for improving CRC treatment outcomes.

Resistance to apoptosis represents a fundamental hallmark of cancer and a major obstacle to effective therapy [6]. Apoptosis is a tightly regulated cell death program that maintains tissue homeostasis and can be triggered through the extrinsic (death receptor) or intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway [7]. The intrinsic pathway, which integrates stress signals via mitochondrial regulation, is a principal target of most anticancer drugs [6, 8]. The Bcl-2 family proteins are central regulators of this pathway, balancing pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic members to determine cell fate [9]. Overexpression of anti-apoptotic proteins such as Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL inhibits mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, thereby blocking cytochrome c release and caspase activation [9, 10]. Consequently, restoring mitochondrial apoptosis by modulating these proteins is an effective therapeutic approach for cancer treatment, including CRC.

Metastasis is responsible for the majority of CRC-related deaths, as it enables tumor spread to distant organs, most frequently the liver and lungs [11]. The epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) plays a pivotal role in this process by allowing epithelial cells to lose polarity and adhesion while acquiring mesenchymal properties that enhance motility and invasiveness [12]. Focal adhesion kinase (FAK), a non-receptor tyrosine kinase activated by integrin binding to the extracellular matrix (ECM), regulates multiple cellular events—including migration, proliferation, and survival—through downstream signaling cascades such as Src and ERK1/2 [13-15]. Activation of these pathways induces EMT-associated transcription factors such as SLUG, TWIST, and ZEB family proteins, resulting in E-cadherin repression and upregulation of mesenchymal markers including N-cadherin and vimentin [15, 16]. Therefore, targeting the integrin/FAK/Src axis offers a promising strategy for the prevention of CRC metastasis.

Beauvericin is a cyclohexadepsipeptide mycotoxin produced by entomopathogenic fungi such as Beauveria bassiana and Cordyceps bassiana [17, 18]. Although originally identified as a natural contaminant of cereals, beauvericin has attracted attention for its broad biological activities, including antimicrobial, ionophoric, anti-inflammatory, and antitumor effects [18]. Increasing evidence indicates that beauvericin exerts potent anticancer activity in several tumor types. It induces apoptosis in non-small-cell lung cancer through modulation of Bcl-2 family proteins and caspase activation [19], reverses EMT via Notch signaling inhibition in triple-negative breast cancer [20], and suppresses glioma growth by blocking the Src/STAT3 pathway [21]. Our previous study demonstrated that beauvericin inhibits metastasis and triggers both intrinsic and extrinsic apoptosis in melanoma cells [22]. However, a systematic analysis of its cellular effects and signaling mechanisms in colorectal cancer has not been fully explored.

In the present study, we performed a comprehensive in vitro investigation of beauvericin in CRC cells, focusing on its effects on mitochondrial apoptosis, EMT-associated phenotypes, angiogenic signaling, and JAK/STAT pathway activity. By integrating functional assays with signaling and network-based analyses, we aimed to define the molecular context in which beauvericin modulates malignant characteristics of CRC cells.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Beauvericin was purchased from MedChemExpress (USA). ECM gel, gelatin, and 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester (TMRE) was from Invitrogen (USA), and MitoSOX Red from Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was supplied by R&D Systems (USA). Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) was obtained from Cytiva (USA), and Endothelial Cell Growth Medium-2 (EGM-2) with antibiotics from Lonza (USA). Primary and secondary antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (USA). The human VEGF ELISA kit was from Koma Biotech (Republic of Korea), and the ECL detection kit from DoGEN Bio (Republic of Korea).

Cell Culture

HCT116 and SW480 colorectal cancer (CRC) cells, the human colon–derived fibroblast cell line CCD-18Co, and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were maintained in DMEM or EGM-2 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic solution, as appropriate. All cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Finland).

Cell Viability and Tumor Cell–Induced HUVEC Proliferation Assays

HCT116 and SW480 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and treated with increasing concentrations of beauvericin for 24 h. Cell viability was determined using the CellTiter-Glo ATP bioluminescence assay (Promega, USA), and IC50 values were calculated via nonlinear regression using GraphPad Prism 6.0 (GraphPad Software, USA).

For the tumor cell–induced proliferation assay, HCT116 and SW480 cells were treated with beauvericin for 24 h, followed by incubation in serum-free medium for another 24 h to collect conditioned medium (CM). Serum-starved HUVECs were then stimulated with CM for 72 h, and proliferation was quantified using the same bioluminescence assay [23].

Clonogenic Assay

HCT116 and SW480 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and treated with beauvericin for 14 days. Colonies were fixed with 3.7% formaldehyde, stained with 0.5% crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich), and counted. Colony numbers were expressed as a percentage relative to the untreated control [22].

Flow Cytometric Analysis of Cell Cycle and Apoptosis

HCT116 and SW480 cells were exposed to beauvericin for 24 h and analyzed using the Muse Cell Cycle Reagent or the Muse Annexin V & Dead Cell Reagent (Luminex, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The distribution of cell cycle phases and apoptotic populations was determined using the Guava Muse Cell Analyzer and MuseSoft v1.8.0.3 software.

Fluorescence-Based Assays

To examine nuclear morphology, mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mROS), and mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), HCT116 and SW480 cells were treated with beauvericin for 24 h and stained with 20 μg/ml DAPI, 1 μM MitoSOX Red, or 100 nM TMRE, respectively. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Korea Lab Tech, Republic of Korea), and fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Wound Healing Assay

Ibidi Culture-Inserts (Ibidi GmbH, Germany) were placed in 24-well plates and seeded with HCT116 and SW480 cells. After 24 h, inserts were removed to generate a cell-free gap, and cells were treated with beauvericin. Images were acquired at 0 h and after 30 h (HCT116) or 48 h (SW480) using an optical microscope (Olympus, Japan). Wound closure was quantified using ImageJ software and expressed as the percentage of closure normalized to the initial wound width.

Transwell Migration Assay

Cell migration was evaluated using Transwell inserts (8-μm pore size; SPL Life Sciences, Republic of Korea) without ECM coating to specifically assess chemotactic migration. Serum-starved HCT116 and SW480 cells were seeded into the upper chambers (6 × 104 cells/insert) in serum-free DMEM. The lower chambers were filled with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS as a chemoattractant, and beauvericin was added to the lower chambers. After 6 h of incubation, migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with methanol, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and counted under a light microscope. Migration was expressed as a percentage relative to control. To minimize potential confounding effects of cell proliferation, migration was assessed under serum-free conditions in the upper chamber and within a short incubation period (≤6 h), which is insufficient to allow meaningful cell cycle progression.

Cell Invasion and Tumor Cell–Induced Invasion Assays

For invasion analysis, Transwell inserts were precoated with gelatin (1 mg/ml, upper surface) and ECM gel (3 mg/ml, lower surface). HCT116 and SW480 cells were seeded in the upper chamber, and beauvericin was added to the lower chamber. After 18 h, invading cells were fixed with methanol, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and counted under a microscope. Invasion was expressed as a percentage relative to control [22].

For the tumor cell–induced invasion assay, HCT116 and SW480 cells were pretreated with beauvericin for 24 h. Serum-free conditioned medium (CM) from these cells was used to stimulate serum-starved HUVECs seeded in the upper chamber. After 18 h, invaded HUVECs were fixed, stained, and quantified as above [23].

Tumor Cell-Induced HUVEC Tube Formation Assay

For the tube formation assay, ECM gel (10 mg/ml) was coated onto μ-Slide 15 Well 3D plates (Ibidi GmbH), and serum-starved HUVECs were seeded onto the gel at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well in conditioned medium (CM) collected from CRC cells treated with beauvericin. Tube-like structures were imaged after 4 h of incubation, and the total number of tubes formed was quantified under a light microscope. Tube formation was expressed as a percentage relative to the control group.

Immunoblot Analysis

Proteins were extracted from cell lysates, separated by SDS–PAGE, and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking in TBST containing 5% skim milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (1:2000–1:10,000), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:3000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL detection and quantified with ImageJ by normalizing to β-actin.

Measurement of VEGF by ELISA

HCT116 and SW480 cells were treated with beauvericin for 24 h, followed by incubation in serum-free medium for another 24 h. VEGF levels in culture supernatants were quantified using a human VEGF ELISA kit (Koma Biotech), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using TMB substrate [23].

Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) Network Pharmacology

CRC-associated genes were retrieved from the NCBI Gene database (Homo sapiens; accessed June 20, 2025). Beauvericin potential targets were predicted using PharmMapper based on its structure obtained from PubChem. Overlapping genes between CRC-associated genes and beauvericin-predicted targets were analyzed using STRING v11.5 (Homo sapiens; accessed June 20, 2025) with a minimum interaction score of 0.7 (high confidence). Hub genes were ranked by node degree.

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments (n = 3) unless otherwise stated. Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test (SPSS v9.0). Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Beauvericin Reduces Viability and Clonogenic Growth of CRC Cells

HCT116 and SW480 cells were treated with beauvericin (0–20 μM) for 24 h. Beauvericin significantly decreased cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner, with IC50 values of 10.22 μM and 10.52 μM, respectively (Fig. 1A). Consistently, long-term clonogenic assays revealed a marked dose-dependent reduction in colony formation (Fig. 1B). These findings indicate that beauvericin effectively suppresses the viability and proliferative capacity of CRC cells.

To evaluate potential tumor selectivity and to support the rationale for the concentration range used in subsequent experiments, cell viability was additionally assessed in the non-tumorigenic human colon–derived fibroblast cell line CCD-18Co (Fig. S1). Beauvericin reduced CCD-18Co cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner; however, within the 5–10 μM concentration range, CCD-18Co cells were less sensitive than colorectal cancer cell lines. Importantly, the concentrations used for migration, invasion, and angiogenesis assays (1–2 μM) exerted minimal effects on cell viability in both normal and cancer cells, indicating a functional in vitro therapeutic window for phenotypic modulation independent of overt cytotoxicity.

Beauvericin Induces G0/G1 Cell Cycle Arrest and Apoptosis

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the observed cytotoxicity, HCT116 and SW480 cells were treated with beauvericin (5 and 10 μM) for 24 h and analyzed for cell cycle progression. As shown in Fig. 2A, beauvericin caused an accumulation of cells in the G0/G1 phase in a concentration-dependent manner. In parallel, flow cytometric analysis demonstrated a significant increase in the apoptotic cell population following 24 h of treatment with beauvericin (5–20 μM) (Fig. 2B). Together, these results suggest that beauvericin inhibits CRC cell growth by inducing G0/G1 arrest and promoting apoptosis.

Beauvericin Triggers Mitochondria-Dependent Apoptosis

To further characterize beauvericin-induced apoptosis, nuclear morphology was assessed by DAPI staining after treatment with 5 or 10 μM beauvericin for 24 h. Pronounced nuclear condensation and fragmentation were observed in both cell lines, confirming apoptotic cell death (Fig. 3A). Mitochondrial superoxide production, measured using the MitoSOX Red probe, increased significantly in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3B). In contrast, TMRE staining revealed a marked reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), indicating mitochondrial depolarization (Fig. 3C). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that beauvericin induces apoptosis through mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in CRC cells.

Beauvericin Activates the Intrinsic Apoptotic Pathway

To elucidate the molecular basis of apoptosis induction, the expression of key regulators of the intrinsic pathway was examined by immunoblotting. Beauvericin treatment downregulated the anti-apoptotic proteins Bcl-xL and Bcl-2, while upregulating the pro-apoptotic proteins Bad and Bax (Fig. 4). Furthermore, survivin, an inhibitor of caspase activation, was decreased, whereas cleaved caspase-9 and cleaved caspase-3 were markedly elevated. These results confirm that beauvericin activates the intrinsic apoptotic pathway by shifting the Bcl-2 family balance toward a pro-apoptotic state and promoting caspase activation.

Beauvericin Suppresses Migration and Invasion of CRC Cells

To determine whether beauvericin affects migratory and invasive phenotypes, wound-healing, Transwell migration (uncoated), and ECM-coated Transwell invasion assays were performed. At sub-cytotoxic concentrations (1–2 μM), beauvericin significantly reduced wound closure in HCT116 and SW480 cells (Fig. 5A). Consistently, beauvericin decreased chemotactic migration across uncoated Transwell membranes within 6 h (Fig. 5B), supporting an anti-migratory effect under short-term conditions. Moreover, beauvericin suppressed invasion through ECM-coated Transwell inserts after 18 h (Fig. 5C). These results indicate that beauvericin attenuates migratory and invasive phenotypes of CRC cells in vitro.

Beauvericin Modulates Integrin/FAK/Src/ERK1/2 Signaling and Attenuates EMT-Associated Marker Expression

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-metastatic effects of beauvericin, the expression of integrin-associated signaling mediators and EMT-related markers was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 6, beauvericin treatment reduced the expression of integrin α6 and markedly suppressed the phosphorylation of FAK and Src, key regulators of focal adhesion signaling, without significantly affecting the expression levels of total FAK and total Src. Similarly, phosphorylation of ERK1/2, a downstream effector of this pathway, was decreased, while total ERK1/2 expression remained largely unchanged. Consistent with these signaling changes, beauvericin downregulated EMT-associated transcription factors, including SLUG and TWIST1/2, and additionally reduced the expression of ZEB1 and ZEB2. Concomitantly, beauvericin restored E-cadherin expression and reduced N-cadherin and vimentin levels in a dose-dependent manner. Morphological analysis further revealed that beauvericin-treated CRC cells exhibited a shift toward a more epithelial-like phenotype, characterized by enhanced cell–cell adhesion and reduced spindle-like morphology (Fig. 6). Collectively, these findings indicate that beauvericin attenuates EMT-associated phenotypes in CRC cells and reduces metastatic potential through suppression of integrin α6/FAK/Src/ERK1/2 signaling without altering the expression of total signaling proteins.

Beauvericin Inhibits CRC Cell–Induced HUVEC Proliferation, Invasion, and Tube Formation via VEGF Suppression

To assess the impact of beauvericin on tumor-induced angiogenesis, conditioned medium (CM) from CRC cells treated with beauvericin at sub-cytotoxic concentrations (1–2 μM) was applied to HUVEC cultures. CM from untreated HCT116 and SW480 cells markedly enhanced HUVEC proliferation, whereas CM from beauvericin-treated cells significantly attenuated this effect (Fig. 7A). Similarly, beauvericin-derived CM significantly suppressed HUVEC invasion (Fig. 7B). In addition, CM from beauvericin-treated CRC cells dose-dependently impaired HUVEC tube formation, indicating reduced endothelial activation (Fig. 7C). Finally, ELISA analysis revealed a marked reduction in VEGF levels in CM from beauvericin-treated CRC cells (Fig. 7D). Collectively, these results indicate that beauvericin suppresses VEGF-dependent endothelial activation in vitro by downregulating tumor-derived VEGF, thereby limiting endothelial proliferation, invasion, and tube-forming capacity.

Beauvericin Is Associated with Reduced JAK/STAT Pathway Activation in CRC Cells

To identify potential molecular targets, CRC-related genes were integrated with beauvericin-predicted protein targets to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network (Fig. 8A). Network topology analysis revealed JAK2, STAT1, and STAT3 as central nodes (Fig. 8B). Immunoblot validation confirmed that beauvericin markedly reduced the phosphorylation of JAK2, STAT1, and STAT3 without significantly affecting their total protein levels (Fig. 8C). These findings suggest that beauvericin treatment is associated with reduced activation of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway, which may contribute to the suppression of CRC cell proliferation and metastatic phenotypes under the experimental conditions tested.

Discussion

In this study, we provide a systematic in vitro characterization of beauvericin in colorectal cancer cells, demonstrating concentration-dependent induction of mitochondrial apoptosis and sub-cytotoxic attenuation of migratory/invasive phenotypes and tumor-driven endothelial activation. Our findings integrate functional assays with signaling analyses to define pathway activity patterns associated with these phenotypes (Fig. 9).

Beauvericin markedly reduced CRC cell viability and clonogenic potential and induced G0/G1 cell cycle arrest, consistent with cytostatic effects observed in other cancer models [19–21]. Mechanistically, beauvericin activated the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway, as evidenced by nuclear condensation, increased mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mROS), and loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP). These effects were accompanied by a shift in the Bcl-2 protein balance toward a pro-apoptotic state, characterized by downregulation of Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL and upregulation of Bax and Bad, leading to the activation of cleaved caspase-9 and caspase-3. Together, these data demonstrate that beauvericin induces intrinsic apoptosis through mitochondrial dysfunction and caspase activation.

At sub-cytotoxic concentrations, beauvericin significantly suppressed migratory and invasive behaviors without substantially affecting cell viability, indicating a dissociation between cytotoxic and anti-metastatic effects. Mechanistically, these phenotypes were associated with reduced activation of integrin-linked focal adhesion signaling [24]. Beauvericin decreased integrin α6 expression and reduced phosphorylation of FAK, Src, and ERK1/2, while total FAK/Src/ERK1/2 levels were largely unchanged, indicating modulation at the level of pathway activation rather than global protein suppression. In parallel, beauvericin attenuated EMT-associated marker programs, including downregulation of ZEB1/2, SLUG, and TWIST1/2, restoration of E-cadherin, and reduction of N-cadherin and vimentin. Morphological analysis further supported a partial shift toward epithelial-like features. Collectively, these findings support attenuation of EMT-associated phenotypes rather than complete EMT reversal, consistent with the dynamic and context-dependent nature of EMT programs.

Angiogenesis is another essential process driving tumor progression and dissemination. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) promotes endothelial proliferation, survival, and migration, primarily through VEGFR-2 activation [25]. Elevated VEGF expression in tumor cells fosters a pro-angiogenic microenvironment that enhances EMT and metastatic dissemination [26, 27]. We also demonstrate that beauvericin suppresses tumor-driven endothelial activation in vitro. Conditioned medium from beauvericin-treated CRC cells inhibited endothelial proliferation, invasion, and tube formation, coinciding with reduced VEGF secretion. These results support the interpretation that beauvericin indirectly limits endothelial activation through downregulation of tumor-derived VEGF-dependent signaling. Given known bidirectional coupling between angiogenic signaling and EMT-associated programs, concurrent attenuation of these processes may contribute to the overall anti-migratory phenotype observed.

Network-based analysis identified the JAK2/STAT1/3 axis as a central signaling node associated with beauvericin treatment. Consistent with this prediction, beauvericin reduced phosphorylation of JAK2, STAT1, and STAT3 without affecting total protein levels. Because phosphorylation of JAK/STAT components can be influenced by multiple upstream factors, including cytokine signaling and cellular stress, our data support an association with reduced pathway activation rather than direct enzymatic inhibition. Nonetheless, given the established role of JAK/STAT signaling in CRC survival, invasion, and angiogenesis [28–30], attenuation of this pathway likely contributes to the observed phenotypic effects.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. All findings are based on in vitro models, and the ionophoric properties and known systemic toxicity of beauvericin necessitate careful evaluation in in vivo systems. Future studies using xenograft or orthotopic CRC models, as well as combination strategies with standard chemotherapeutics (e.g., 5-FU and oxaliplatin) or targeted agents (e.g., anti-VEGF or JAK/STAT inhibitors), will be essential to define therapeutic feasibility and safety.

In conclusion, our study provides a systematic in vitro characterization of beauvericin’s effects in colorectal cancer cells, demonstrating coordinated induction of mitochondrial apoptosis and attenuation of EMT-associated signaling, VEGF-dependent endothelial activation, and JAK/STAT pathway activity. These findings offer mechanistic insight into beauvericin’s anti-tumor actions and provide a foundation for future preclinical investigation.
