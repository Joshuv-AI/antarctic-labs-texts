Widespread pylephlebitis and infective thromboembolism due to Streptococcus anginosus-related appendiceal rupture: A case report
Authors: Jessica Hinh, Cindy Ho, Joshua Haron Abasszade, Keat Yee Low, Sasha Hall, Jacqueline Fraser, Michael Patrick Swan, Michael Braude, Sally Jane Bell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStreptococcus anginosus group is increasingly being recognised as a cause of invasive infections with a propensity to cause pyogenic abscesses. The most common sites of infection described include intra-abdominal, skin and soft tissues and bloodstream. Prompt identification of this bacteria as a cause of pyogenic disease is needed to appropriately commence antibiotic therapy, usually with beta-lactams. We describe an atypical case of Streptococcus anginosus infection with pylephlebitis, widespread infective thromboembolism and presumed mycotic pseudoaneurysm related to an appendiceal rupture. In this case, a blood culture was requested for investigation due to suspicion of sepsis which confirmed Streptococcus anginosus bacteremia. There was evidence of a distended cystic structure within the right iliac fossa, with extensive expansive portal mesenteric occlusive thrombi and mucinous deposits in the mesorectum and liver concerning for pseudomyxoma peritonei (PMP) with hepatic metastases on computed tomography scan of the abdomen and pelvis, and on magnetic resonance imaging of the liver. Further attempts of obtaining histopathology to investigate the concerns of malignancy were undertaken including a diagnostic laparoscopy, and various endoscopic interventions, however this was negative for malignancy. After receiving two weeks of intravenous antibiotics, the patient showed marked clinical, biochemical and radiological improvement. This case highlights the need to consider sepsis as a rare but significant cause of widespread thrombotic disease.Keywords: Streptococcus anginosus, Bacteremia, Sepsis, Embolism and thrombosis, Abscess, Gram-positive bacterial infections

Introduction

Streptococcus anginosus (milleri) group (SAG) refers to a group of Gram-positive, facultative anaerobic streptococci and comprises of three bacterial species: Streptococcus anginosus (S. anginosus), Streptococcus intermedius (S. intermedius) and Streptococcus constellatus (S. constellatus). SAG are commensal organisms typically found in the oropharynx, gastrointestinal tract (GIT) and urogenital tract [1]. However, with a diverse arsenal of virulence factors, SAG has the ability to become pathogenic [2].

Until the 1990s, SAG has traditionally been underappreciated as a significant pathogen, owing to a lack of microbiological diagnostic techniques to accurately distinguish it from other streptococci species [3]. However, recent literature has recognised its emergence as a cause of invasive infection, specifically in bacteraemia, infective endocarditis and pyogenic abscess formation [4]. Yet, pyogenic thrombotic events from SAG infection remain poorly elucidated in the literature [5].

Pylephlebitis is defined as infective suppurative thrombosis of the portal vein and is a type of septic thrombophlebitis. It arises as a complication from intra-abdominal suppurative processes [6]. In a systematic review conducted by Fusaro et al. [6], pylephlebitis was caused by a single pathogen in 94 (42.8 %) of cases and polymicrobial in 60 (27.2 %) of cases, with most frequently isolated bacteria were Escherichia coli, Bacteroides spp., and Streptococcus spp.

We highlight an atypical case of widespread pylephlebitis, infective thromboembolism and presumed mycotic pseudoaneurysm due to S. anginosus-related appendiceal rupture.

Case Study

A 40-year-old male was transferred to a tertiary metropolitan hospital from a regional hospital with a two-week history of lower abdominal pain, weight loss, fever, nausea and jaundice. He had a history of binge-pattern alcohol use without clinical features of cirrhosis. His past medical history included psoriasis and asthma and he was on no regular medications. He reported no history of intravenous drug use, recent periods of immobilisation or other risk factors for thromboembolism. On examination he was tachycardic with heart rate 117 beats per minute and hypotensive with a blood pressure 94/55 mmHg. He had dermal and scleral icterus and a mildly tender lower abdomen with no signs of peritonism. Initial blood tests demonstrated a lactate 9 mmol/L (0.5–2.0 mmol/L) as well as elevated inflammatory markers with white cell count 34.3 × 109/L (4.0–11.0 ×109/L), neutrophils 22.4 × 109/L (2.0–8.0 x 109/L) and C-reactive protein (CRP) 410 mg/L (0–5 mg/L). Liver function tests demonstrated a mixed cholestatic and hepatocellular pattern with an aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 324 U/L (5–35 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 85 U/L (5–40 U/L), alkaline phosphatase (ALP) 641 U/L (30–110 U/L), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) 386 U/L (5–50 U/L) and bilirubin 68 mcmol/L (0–20 mcmol/L). Initial computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis (CTAP) demonstrated widespread occlusive thrombus of the portal mesenteric venous system. Intravenous fluids and empirical piperacillin-tazobactam were commenced prior to his transfer.

Following transfer, the patient underwent further investigations. A repeat CTAP revealed a distended cystic structure within the right iliac fossa containing gas and dependent fluid density with the medial margin tethered to the sigmoid (Fig. 1A). This was suspected to represent appendiceal mucocele complicated by rupture and possible fistulisation with the sigmoid. Other radiological findings included multiple confluent lobulated hypodense foci within the mesorectum with mass effect (Fig. 1B) and scalloping of rectal wall thought to represent mucinous deposits from pseudomyxoma peritonei (PMP) to the mesorectum. There was further left subphrenic hypodense locules scalloping the splenic surface attributed to PMP (Fig. 1B). There were extensive expansile portal mesenteric occlusive thrombi with intraluminal hypodensities and lobulated venous contour involving superior rectal, sigmoid, left colic, inferior mesenteric, splenic and intra- and extra- hepatic portal veins (Fig. 1C). Multiple cystic hepatic masses were further characterised with Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI). MRI confirmed complex multiloculated cystic hepatic masses with periportal distribution and components contiguous with expanded portal veins (Fig. 2). Hepatic lesions predominantly demonstrated facilitated diffusion centrally and restricted diffusion peripherally. Thus, mucinous hepatic metastases remained a concern as the pattern of diffusion restriction was less indicative of pyogenic hepatic abscesses. A colonoscopy identified a polypoid 2 cm by 1 cm mass in the sigmoid colon, which was postulated to be a secondary tumour deposit from the potential appendiceal-sigmoid fistula found on recent CT, however no fistula was visualised. Histology from the polypoid mass in the sigmoid colon showed active focal inflammation, without features of malignancy. Serum tumour markers showed positive carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (CA19–9) 350 kunits/L (0–35 kunits/L), with a negative carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and alpha-feto protein (AFP) 3.5 microg/L (0–5.0 microg/L) and 1.5 microg/L (<9 microg/L) respectively.

The patient was commenced on a therapeutic dose of enoxaparin given his extensive portal venous thrombus, however developed large volume rectal bleeding with an associated haemoglobin drop from 90 to 64 g/L (130 – 180 g/L). An urgent CT angiography of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated new inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) pseudoaneurysm, as well as an incidental finding of left sub-segmental pulmonary emboli (PE), with no active extravasation of contrast into the large bowel. A repeat flexible sigmoidoscopy and gastroscopy were also performed, neither of which identified any sources of gastrointestinal bleeding or appendiceal-sigmoid fistulisation. Whilst the initial recommendation from the vascular surgery team was to coil the IMA pseudoaneurysm, the patient was deemed a poor surgical candidate due to his significant disease burden from presumed malignancy and extensive thromboembolism. As such, an inferior vena cava (IVC) filter was inserted to balance the risks of bleeding from the pseudoaneurysm with ischaemic risks from the known thrombi.

Blood cultures were requested for investigation due to suspicion of sepsis. Bacteriological laboratory tests revealed an infection with Streptococcus species, with confirmation of Streptococcus anginosus through further testing as previously described [7], [8], [9].

Blood cultures returned positive for S. anginosus and the patient was transitioned to intravenous ceftriaxone and metronidazole. Appendiceal mucinous neoplasm (AMN) complicated by perforation and widespread mucinous tumour deposits remained the working diagnosis, with a subsequent biopsy and aspirate of cystic hepatic mass performed for diagnostic clarification. Histology was negative for malignancy, but did reveal incidental architectural changes consistent with cirrhosis. Liver abscess microscopy revealed significant polymorphonucleocytes, however fluid culture was negative. A diagnostic laparoscopy was performed which revealed blood-stained peritoneal fluid with clots, and mesorectal deposits which were biopsied, revealing granulating fibrinopurulent exudate with no growth on tissue culture. The pelvis and rectum were unable to be safely assessed. There were no lesions seen in the small bowel.

After receiving two weeks of intravenous antibiotics, the patient showed marked clinical, biochemical and radiological improvement. A follow-up CTAP showed volume reduction of the right iliac fossa, peri-splenic and hepatic collections and partial involution of thrombi throughout the portal mesenteric venous system and presumed thrombi within the mesorectum.

Given the clinical features, negative findings of malignancy and S. anginosus bacteremia, these findings supported a final diagnosis of S. anginosus sepsis due to appendix rupture, causing complications of pylephlebitis, infective thromboembolism and mycotic pseudoaneurysm complications.

Following discharge, the patient completed an extended course of ceftriaxone followed by an oral tail of amoxicillin-clavulanic acid. Repeat CT demonstrated resolution of hepatic masses and abdominal collections with residual post-inflammatory adhesion of non-distended appendix adjacent to the sigmoid and small bowel, with complete involution of acute thrombi within the mesorectum and portal mesenteric venous system with cavernous transformation of portal veins. There was improvement in his PE as well as resolution of the pseudoaneurysm. The IVC filter was removed three months later. He continues to be followed up by the gastroenterology team in the outpatient setting for ongoing management of his cirrhosis.

Discussion

SAG is increasingly recognised as a cause of invasive pyogenic infections with abscess formation; most commonly intra-abdominal, cutaneous and soft tissue. Rarely, SAG has been found to cause liver and brain abscesses [1], [9]. There are case reports of S. anginosus liver abscesses with associated portal vein thrombus (PVT), though the thromboembolism was not necessarily attributed to pylephlebitis. In one report, PVT was attributed to severe inflammation secondary to a liver abscess, notwithstanding the patient meeting criteria for SAG pylephlebitis (fever, PVT and SAG bacteraemia) [10].

Pylephlebitis is a rare condition presenting most commonly with fever, abdominal pain, nausea and vomiting. Diagnosis is often made clinically in the presence of radiological evidence of portal vein thrombus coupled with bacteraemia and sepsis. Tracking infection from diverticulitis and acute appendicitis are the two most common causes of pylephlebitis owing to the drainage of the portal vein system [9].

In terms of management, targeted antimicrobial therapy is requisite. Fortunately, selection of antimicrobial agents for the treatment of SAG is relatively uncomplicated. Profiles of SAG isolates have shown high susceptibility to beta-lactams [1], highlighting the appropriateness of ceftriaxone in treating this patient. There is a suggestion, however, that some SAG bacteria may have relative penicillin insensitivity. Therefore, it is recommended to commence vancomycin whilst establishing penicillin minimal inhibitory concentration for patients where penicillin is the antibiotic of choice [11]. Although there is no specific recommendation for duration of treatment in patients with complicated SAG infections, guidelines support prolonged antibiotic therapy of greater than fourteen days in the setting of bacteraemia, presence of abscesses and lack of complete source control [12], [13].

Anticoagulation for PVT should be considered in tandem to antimicrobial therapy, though recommendations are limited due to the rarity of pylephlebitis, and by extension, the lack of randomised controlled trials and guideline recommendations. A retrospective observational study conducted by Naymagon et al. [14] examined the role of anticoagulation in pylephlebitis in 67 patients over a 19-year period that included 47 patients who received anticoagulation and 20 patients who did not. The study found that anticoagulation significantly improved the rate of portal vein thrombus resolution and reduced the rate of chronic symptomatic portal hypertension in patients who were anticoagulated compared to those who were not. Anticoagulation, however, did not appear to affect survival. Long-term sequelae of widespread thromboembolism, particularly in the portal veins system, carries significant mortality and morbidity burden. Potential serious complications include variceal bleeding, splenomegaly and portal biliopathy [15]. Post-thrombotic syndrome and chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension are also complications from venous thromboembolism and patients should be monitored to diagnose and manage these conditions [16]. Based on current evidence, anticoagulation should be considered as adjunctive treatment to antimicrobial therapy. However, further research is required to support this recommendation.

As evidenced in this case, SAG infection can mimic PMP. To our knowledge, there are no cases in the literature denoting SAG infection presenting radiologically like PMP. PMP is a rare disease with high mortality, characterised by diffuse intra-abdominal gelatinous ascites with mucinous implants on peritoneal surfaces, classically arising from perforated AMNs [17]. Presenting symptoms of PMP are often non-specific and include abdominal pain, fatigue and weight loss, making diagnosis exceedingly challenging. Hence, PMP is typically diagnosed incidentally. In this case report, the patient’s symptoms of abdominal pain and nausea may have been consistent with PMP, but the acuity of his symptoms and significant improvement in clinical status and inflammatory markers following antibiotics are discordant with typical progression of PMP [18], [19]. The radiological suspicion of PMP was appropriately explored with a diagnostic laparoscopy. However, in the absence of positive tumour markers and negative histopathology, alternate diagnosis in the form of invasive SAG infection was favoured.

Conclusion

Our case highlights the diagnostic challenge of managing complex disseminated infection caused by SAG. Early consideration of sepsis and the significant entity of S. anginosus pylephlebitis as a differential for widespread atypical thromboembolism is vital. Commencement of appropriate antibiotics when clinically suspecting SAG invasive infection may reduce the risks of complications and improve overall mortality rates for these patients. For patients with SAG pylephlebitis, anticoagulation is also important to consider, though further evidence is required to support this as a recommendation.
