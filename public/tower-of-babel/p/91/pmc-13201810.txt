Integrating self-attention and LSTM into TD3 for robust mobile robot navigation in dynamic environments
Authors: Luyun Chen, Qiang Tang, Rui Xu, You Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMobile robot path planning in dynamic environments is challenging because existing deep reinforcement learning methods lack temporal memory, suffer from inefficient sample utilization under uniform replay, and face credit assignment difficulties with sparse rewards. This paper proposes the Self-Attention LSTM TD3 (SAL-TD3) algorithm, which integrates LSTM networks and multi-head self-attention into the TD3 framework to capture temporal dependencies for proactive obstacle avoidance. A rank-based prioritized experience replay with n-step returns improves sample efficiency, and a composite reward function provides dense feedback for efficient policy learning. Experiments show that SAL-TD3 achieves a 91% success rate (vs. 77% for TD3), reduces path length by 16.6%, and lowers collision rate from 23% to 9%. Generalization tests and real-world robot deployment confirm robust sim-to-real transfer performance.Keywords: Mobile robot path planning, Deep reinforcement learning, Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient, Self-attention mechanismSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Mobile robot path planning in dynamic environments constitutes a fundamental challenge in autonomous systems1, with broad applications spanning warehouse logistics, hospital service robots, autonomous vehicles, and search-and-rescue operations2. The ability to navigate safely and efficiently while avoiding both static obstacles and unpredictably moving agents such as pedestrians directly determines the practical deployment feasibility of mobile robots in human-populated spaces3. As service robots increasingly operate in close proximity to humans in shopping malls, airports, and healthcare facilities, the demand for robust navigation algorithms capable of real-time adaptation to dynamic environmental changes has become critically important4. Unlike controlled industrial settings where robot trajectories can be pre-programmed, real-world navigation requires continuous decision-making under uncertainty, where the consequences of navigation failures range from task inefficiency to potential safety hazards for both robots and humans5.

Traditional path planning algorithms, including A*, Dijkstra, RRT, and their variants, have achieved considerable success in static environments by computing optimal or near-optimal paths based on known map representations6. However, these approaches fundamentally rely on the assumption that obstacle configurations remain fixed during path execution, rendering them inadequate for environments with moving obstacles7. When pedestrians or other dynamic agents intersect the planned trajectory, traditional methods must trigger complete replanning cycles, introducing computational delays that compromise real-time responsiveness8. Furthermore, these algorithms lack the capability to anticipate obstacle motion patterns, resulting in purely reactive avoidance behaviors that often produce inefficient detour paths or, in confined spaces, navigation failures9. The integration of prediction modules with traditional planners has been explored, yet such hybrid approaches suffer from error accumulation between separate prediction and planning components, limiting their effectiveness in highly dynamic scenarios10.

Deep reinforcement learning has emerged as a promising paradigm for addressing the limitations of traditional planning methods by learning end-to-end navigation policies directly from sensory observations11. Actor-critic algorithms such as DDPG, TD3, and SAC enable continuous action control essential for smooth robot motion, while the learned policies can implicitly capture environment dynamics through interaction experience12. Nevertheless, current deep reinforcement learning approaches for robot navigation exhibit several critical deficiencies that hinder their practical deployment. First, standard feedforward neural network architectures process each observation independently without maintaining memory of historical states, fundamentally limiting the agent’s ability to infer obstacle motion trajectories and anticipate future positions13. This memoryless property results in reactive rather than predictive avoidance behaviors, particularly problematic when navigating among multiple moving obstacles with diverse motion patterns. Second, uniform experience replay sampling treats all transitions as equally informative, leading to inefficient utilization of valuable experiences such as successful obstacle avoidance maneuvers or near-collision events that contain rich learning signals14. This sample inefficiency prolongs training time and may result in suboptimal policies that fail to adequately learn from rare but critical situations. Third, single-step temporal-difference learning propagates reward signals slowly through trajectories, causing credit assignment difficulties in navigation tasks where goal-reaching rewards are sparse and delayed15. The combination of these limitations manifests as slow convergence, unstable training dynamics, and degraded generalization performance when deployed in environments differing from training conditions16.

To address the aforementioned challenges, this paper proposes the Self-Attention LSTM TD3 (SAL-TD3) algorithm, a novel deep reinforcement learning framework that synergistically integrates temporal modeling, attention mechanisms, and prioritized learning for enhanced mobile robot path planning. The proposed approach incorporates LSTM networks into both actor and critic architectures to maintain memory of historical observations, enabling the agent to capture temporal dependencies and implicitly predict obstacle motion patterns for proactive avoidance decisions. A multi-head self-attention mechanism complements the LSTM processing by allowing the network to selectively focus on relevant temporal segments, effectively combining the sequential processing strengths of recurrent networks with the long-range dependency modeling capabilities of attention mechanisms17. The framework further employs prioritized experience replay with rank-based prioritization to concentrate learning on high-value transitions, substantially improving sample efficiency and accelerating convergence. Additionally, n-step returns are integrated to enable more effective credit assignment by propagating reward information across multiple time steps, addressing the sparse reward challenge inherent in goal-reaching navigation tasks. A carefully designed composite reward function incorporating heading alignment, distance progress, and obstacle safety components provides dense feedback signals throughout navigation episodes, guiding the agent toward efficient and safe navigation behaviors.

It is worth discussing why the combination of LSTM and multi-head self-attention represents the most suitable architectural choice for mobile robot navigation in dynamic environments compared with alternative temporal modeling strategies. First, pure feedforward architectures (e.g., standard multilayer perceptrons) lack any mechanism for retaining historical information, rendering them fundamentally incapable of inferring obstacle motion trajectories from sequential observations. Second, Transformer-only architectures, while powerful for capturing long-range dependencies through global self-attention, require fixed-length context windows and incur quadratic computational complexity with respect to sequence length, making them less suitable for the online streaming nature of robotic control where observations arrive continuously and inference latency must remain within single-digit milliseconds. Third, convolutional temporal models such as Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN) can capture local temporal patterns efficiently but have limited receptive fields and cannot selectively retain or forget information over varying time horizons as the dynamics of the environment change. In contrast, LSTM networks are specifically designed with gating mechanisms (forget gate, input gate, output gate) that enable selective retention and forgetting of information across time steps, providing a compact and adaptive temporal memory well suited to the variable-duration interactions encountered during dynamic navigation. However, LSTM alone compresses the entire observation history into a fixed-size hidden state, which can become an information bottleneck when the agent must attend to specific past events—for instance, a pedestrian’s sudden direction change several steps earlier. The multi-head self-attention mechanism directly addresses this limitation by enabling the network to compute relevance-weighted combinations over the LSTM output sequence, selectively amplifying the most informative temporal segments while suppressing irrelevant ones. This complementary integration thus combines the sequential memory strengths of LSTM with the selective long-range dependency modeling of self-attention. Our ablation study in section “Experimental analysis” empirically validates this design: removing the LSTM component causes the largest performance degradation (success rate drops from 91% to 83%), while removing self-attention also leads to a measurable 3 percentage point decrease, confirming the necessity of their joint deployment.

Related work

Traditional path planning algorithms

Classical path planning approaches can be broadly categorized into graph-based methods and sampling-based methods18. The A* algorithm represents a foundational graph-based approach that guarantees optimal path finding through heuristic-guided search, widely adopted in grid-based navigation scenarios19. Dijkstra’s algorithm provides optimal solutions without heuristic guidance but suffers from computational inefficiency in large-scale environments20. The D* algorithm and its variant D* Lite extend these approaches to handle dynamic environments through incremental replanning capabilities21.

Sampling-based methods, including Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT) and its asymptotically optimal variant RRT*, have gained prominence for high-dimensional configuration spaces due to their probabilistic completeness guarantees22. The Probabilistic Roadmap Method (PRM) constructs reusable roadmaps for multi-query planning scenarios23. However, these traditional approaches fundamentally assume static or known obstacle configurations and lack mechanisms for real-time adaptation to dynamic environments, limiting their applicability in scenarios involving moving obstacles such as pedestrians or other mobile agents24.

Deep reinforcement learning for robot navigation

The emergence of deep reinforcement learning has revolutionized autonomous navigation by enabling end-to-end learning of navigation policies directly from sensory inputs25. Deep Q-Network (DQN) demonstrated the feasibility of combining deep neural networks with reinforcement learning for high-dimensional state spaces, though its discrete action space limitation restricts applicability to continuous robot control tasks26.

For continuous action spaces essential in mobile robot control, the Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) algorithm introduced an actor-critic architecture that learns deterministic policies through off-policy training27. However, DDPG suffers from overestimation bias in value function approximation and training instability, particularly in complex navigation environments28. The Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3) algorithm addresses these limitations through three key modifications: clipped double Q-learning to mitigate overestimation, delayed policy updates to reduce per-update error, and target policy smoothing for improved robustness29. These enhancements have established TD3 as a preferred baseline for continuous control tasks in robotics30.

The Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm presents an alternative approach based on maximum entropy reinforcement learning, encouraging exploration through entropy regularization while maintaining sample efficiency31. Comparative studies have demonstrated that both TD3 and SAC achieve competitive performance across various robotic manipulation and navigation benchmarks, with TD3 often exhibiting more stable training dynamics in deterministic policy scenarios32.

Recurrent neural networks for temporal modeling

Capturing temporal dependencies in sequential decision-making tasks has motivated the integration of recurrent neural network architectures into reinforcement learning frameworks33. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks address the vanishing gradient problem inherent in vanilla recurrent networks through gating mechanisms that selectively retain or discard information across time steps34. The application of LSTM to deep reinforcement learning was pioneered by Hausknecht and Stone, who demonstrated that recurrent architectures enable agents to handle partial observability by maintaining memory of past observations35.

Prioritized experience replay

Experience replay constitutes a fundamental technique in off-policy reinforcement learning, enabling sample-efficient learning by storing and reusing past transitions36. The original formulation employs uniform random sampling from the replay buffer, which treats all experiences as equally informative regardless of their learning potential37.

Prioritized Experience Replay (PER) improves upon uniform sampling by assigning sampling probabilities proportional to the temporal-difference (TD) error magnitude, ensuring that transitions with higher prediction errors receive increased attention during training38. This prioritization mechanism accelerates learning by focusing computational resources on the most informative experiences. The framework incorporates importance sampling weights to correct for the bias introduced by non-uniform sampling, with an annealing schedule that gradually increases correction strength as training progresses39.

Subsequent research has explored various prioritization strategies, including rank-based prioritization for robustness across different reward scales, and curiosity-driven prioritization for exploration in sparse reward environments40. The integration of PER with actor-critic algorithms has demonstrated consistent improvements in sample efficiency and final policy performance across diverse continuous control benchmarks41.

Attention mechanisms in reinforcement learning

Attention mechanisms, originally developed for sequence-to-sequence modeling in natural language processing, have been increasingly adopted in reinforcement learning to enable selective focus on relevant features or temporal segments42. The self-attention mechanism computes attention weights based on query-key similarity, allowing networks to dynamically weigh the importance of different input elements43.

In robotic applications, attention mechanisms have been employed for multi-modal sensor fusion, enabling agents to selectively attend to relevant sensory modalities based on task requirements44. For temporal modeling, self-attention provides a complementary mechanism to recurrent processing by capturing long-range dependencies through direct attention over the entire sequence history, mitigating the information bottleneck inherent in fixed-size hidden states45.

Recent work has explored the combination of attention mechanisms with LSTM networks for enhanced temporal modeling. The Transformer architecture demonstrates that self-attention alone can achieve competitive performance with recurrent models while enabling parallel computation46. However, for online decision-making in robotics where observations arrive sequentially, hybrid architectures combining LSTM’s sequential processing with attention’s selective focusing have shown promising results47.

N-step returns and multi-step learning

The choice of bootstrapping horizon in temporal-difference learning presents a fundamental trade-off between bias and variance in value estimation48. Single-step TD learning exhibits low variance but high bias due to reliance on potentially inaccurate value function estimates, while Monte Carlo methods provide unbiased estimates at the cost of high variance from trajectory-level returns49.

N-step returns offer a principled interpolation between these extremes by accumulating rewards over multiple consecutive steps before bootstrapping from the value function50. This approach enables more effective credit assignment by propagating reward information further back along trajectories while maintaining bounded variance. The Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) framework provides a theoretically grounded approach to combining multi-step returns with exponential weighting for variance reduction51.

The integration of n-step returns with experience replay requires careful handling of trajectory boundaries and buffer management, as consecutive transitions must be preserved for accurate return computation37. Recent implementations have demonstrated that combining n-step returns with prioritized experience replay yields synergistic benefits, with n-step targets providing more accurate priority estimates while prioritization focuses learning on the most informative multi-step sequences15.

Methodology

Overall model architecture

The proposed Self-Attention LSTM TD3 (SAL-TD3) framework presents an integrated deep reinforcement learning architecture specifically designed for mobile robot path planning in complex environments. The observation processing pipeline begins with the deep policy network, which receives multimodal sensory inputs from the robot’s perception system. The one-dimensional LiDAR scan data is processed through a hierarchical convolutional architecture consisting of two sequential convolutional layers with 32 and 64 filters respectively, each followed by ReLU activation and pooling operations. This convolutional processing extracts spatial features from the range measurements while maintaining computational efficiency. Concurrently, the goal and pose information, representing the robot’s target position and current state, is processed through a separate fully-connected pathway. The extracted features from both modalities are subsequently concatenated and passed through a series of dense layers with progressively reduced dimensionality to form a unified state representation.

The actor network serves as the policy function and generates continuous action outputs based on the processed state representation. The network architecture incorporates three key processing stages designed to capture both immediate spatial relationships and temporal dependencies in the navigation task. The initial stage employs a fully-connected layer with layer normalization and ReLU activation to transform the input state into a higher-dimensional latent space. This representation is then processed by a Long Short-Term Memory layer that captures sequential dependencies across observation history, enabling the network to maintain contextual awareness of the robot’s trajectory. The third stage introduces a multi-head self-attention mechanism with four attention heads, which allows the network to selectively focus on relevant temporal segments of the encoded sequence. The final output layer applies sigmoid activation to produce bounded action values representing linear and angular velocities for robot control.

The value estimation component adopts the twin delayed deep deterministic policy gradient paradigm through the implementation of dual critic networks. Both critic networks share an identical architecture consisting of fully-connected layers with ReLU activation followed by stacked LSTM units for temporal processing. Each network independently estimates the state-action value function, and the minimum of both estimates is utilized during policy updates to mitigate the overestimation bias commonly observed in actor-critic methods. This conservative value estimation strategy contributes to more stable policy improvement throughout the training process.

The experience replay mechanism incorporates prioritized sampling to enhance learning efficiency from stored transitions. The replay buffer maintains a collection of experience tuples containing state, action, reward, next state, and terminal indicators, along with associated priority values. The priority assignment follows a proportional prioritization scheme where transitions with higher temporal-difference errors receive increased sampling probability, ensuring that informative experiences contribute more frequently to gradient updates. Additionally, the framework employs n-step returns for target value computation, which provides a balance between bias and variance in the bootstrapped value estimates by accumulating rewards over multiple consecutive time steps before incorporating the estimated future value.

Training stability is further enhanced through the employment of separate target networks for both actor and critic components. These target networks maintain delayed copies of the online network parameters and are updated gradually through soft parameter updates with a small interpolation coefficient. This delayed update mechanism prevents the rapid propagation of estimation errors and provides stable target values for temporal-difference learning.

The reward shaping mechanism guides the learning process through a composite reward function comprising multiple components. The alignment reward encourages the robot to orient toward the goal direction, while the progress reward provides positive feedback for reducing the distance to the target. Collision penalties discourage contact with obstacles, and a substantial terminal reward is provided upon successful goal reaching. This structured reward design facilitates efficient credit assignment and accelerates convergence toward effective navigation policies.

Prioritized experience replay with N-step returns

To enhance the sample efficiency and accelerate the convergence of the learning process, we incorporate Prioritized Experience Replay (PER) into our framework. Unlike uniform random sampling employed in standard experience replay, PER assigns different sampling probabilities to transitions based on their temporal-difference (TD) errors, which serve as proxies for the informativeness of each experience. Transitions with larger TD errors indicate greater discrepancies between predicted and actual returns, suggesting that these samples contain more valuable information for policy improvement. This mechanism ensures that the agent focuses its learning capacity on the most surprising and potentially informative experiences, thereby improving both the speed and quality of learning. The prioritized sampling strategy has been demonstrated to significantly outperform uniform sampling in various reinforcement learning benchmarks, particularly in environments with sparse rewards or complex dynamics. SAL-TD3 algorithm architecture and training methodology

The proposed Self-Attention LSTM Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (SAL-TD3) algorithm integrates three critical components to address the challenges of continuous control in mobile robot path planning: twin critic networks for value estimation stability, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) units for temporal dependency modeling, and prioritized experience replay for efficient sample utilization. The fundamental innovation of SAL-TD3 lies in its synergistic combination of these mechanisms, which collectively enhance both the learning efficiency and the quality of the learned policies. Unlike conventional TD3 implementations that process state observations independently, our approach leverages LSTM-augmented actor and critic networks to capture the sequential nature of robot navigation tasks, where past observations significantly influence optimal action selection. Experimental analysis

To validate the effectiveness of the proposed SAL-TD3 algorithm, we designed and conducted comprehensive experiments including simulation experiments and real-world robot experiments. The simulation experiments encompass dynamic environment testing with both static and dynamic obstacles, as well as generalization capability assessment. The real-world experiments further verify the practical applicability of SAL-TD3 in actual mobile robot navigation scenarios.

Experimental setup and parameter configuration

The simulation experiments were conducted using ROS (Robot Operating System) on a workstation equipped with an AMD Ryzen 5 5600X processor, NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU with 12GB VRAM, and 32GB RAM, running Ubuntu 20.04 LTS. Conclusion

This paper proposes the SAL-TD3 algorithm for mobile robot path planning in dynamic environments, integrating LSTM-based temporal modeling, multi-head self-attention, and prioritized experience replay with n-step returns into the TD3 framework. Experimental results demonstrate that SAL-TD3 achieves a 91% success rate (vs. 77% for TD3), reduces path length by 16.6%, and lowers collision rate from 23% to 9%. Generalization tests confirm robust performance in unseen environments (84.5% success rate vs. 60% for DDPG), and real-world deployment validates practical applicability with minimal sim-to-real degradation. In future work, we plan to extend the framework to multi-robot coordination, integrate visual perception modalities such as RGB-D cameras, and investigate transfer learning for rapid adaptation to new environments.
