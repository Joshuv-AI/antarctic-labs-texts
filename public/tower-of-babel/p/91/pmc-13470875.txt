Transitions From Parenteral Prostacyclin Analogues to Selexipag in Patients With Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension
Authors: Dalton J. Kuebel, Adele R. Collins, Arun Jose, Maria R. Guido, Jean M. Elwing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a progressive illness that may require therapy with parenteral prostacyclin pathway agents (PPA) (epoprostenol and treprostinil). These parenteral PPA's are continuous ambulatory infusions that require a high level of skill and knowledge to maintain safety and effectiveness. Depending on the clinical context, transition to oral prostacyclin receptor agonist therapy (selexipag) is sometimes pursued. To prevent abrupt withdrawal from parenteral PPA's, overlap transitions are typically used. This report describes 35 inpatient, rapid transitions from parenteral PPA's to oral selexipag. Planned hospital admissions for transition were common (n = 27, 77%), and all patients successfully transitioned without a return to parenteral PPA's in the immediate post‐transition period. Most patients transitioned due to difficulties with current parenteral PPA therapy (n = 20, 57%), followed by an improved clinical status on parenteral PPA therapy (n = 8, 23%). The median length of transition was 36 h (IQR 24–48 h) and hospital stay was 3 days (IQR 2–6 days). Following transition, the cohort demonstrated stability in PAH‐specific risk scores and functional status. All patients survived to outpatient pulmonary hypertension follow up (median 5.5 weeks, IQR 1.4–9.6 weeks), and the majority were alive at 1‐year post‐transition (n = 32, 91%). Based on these results, we conclude that transition from parenteral PPA's to selexipag can be done in the inpatient setting safely, quickly, and effectively.

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a highly complex and progressive disease of the pulmonary vasculature that can lead to morbidity and mortality from progressive right ventricular failure [1]. Medications for management of PAH are currently limited to four main mechanistic pathways: nitric oxide, endothelin, prostacyclin, and activin signaling inhibition. For patients with advanced PAH or high‐risk features, parenteral prostacyclin pathway agents (PPA) are believed to be the most potent medications and frequently serve as the cornerstone of therapy [1, 2]. However, parenteral PPAs are continuous infusion medications that are complicated to use and can be associated with bloodstream infections if administered intravenously, or with infusion site pain if administered subcutaneously. Consequently, patients on these therapies frequently require or request transition to an oral prostacyclin pathway agent [2].

For select patients, transition to an oral prostacyclin pathway agent such as selexipag, a selective prostacyclin receptor agonist, could potentially be a viable alternative to parenteral PPAs [3, 4]. However, there is very little published data on transitioning from parenteral PPAs to selexipag and questions remain about the optimal approach to transition [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13].

This study aimed to describe processes and outcomes associated with inpatient transitions from parenteral PPA to oral selexipag in a small, single‐center cohort of PAH patients.

Methods

This is a retrospective cohort study. All adult patients admitted to the University of Cincinnati Medical Center with a diagnosis of PAH, that underwent a transition from intravenous (IV) or subcutaneous (SQ) parenteral PPA to selexipag between April 2016 and April 2025, were included in this study. Per center protocol, transitioning was accomplished with a stepwise approach that decreased dosage of parenteral PPA's while simultaneously increasing dosage of oral selexipag therapy. Transition plans were individualized for each patient on the basis of clinical status, comorbidities, and prior tolerance to prostacyclin class agents, but aimed to complete transition within 3–5 days, with each transition step executed at 12‐h intervals. Inpatient transitions occurred exclusively in the hospital's dedicated pulmonary hypertension unit with specialty trained nursing, respiratory therapists, and pharmacists. Clinical data (natriuretic peptide levels, 6‐min walk test distance, functional class, right heart catheterization hemodynamics, etc.) was extracted from the medical record. Objective risk classification was done using the United States Registry to Evaluate Early and Long‐term pulmonary arterial hypertension (REVEAL) Lite 2 risk score, which assesses functional classification, naturetic peptides, 6‐min walk distance, vital signs (heart rate, systolic blood pressure), and renal insufficiency [14]. The REVEAL Lite 2 places patients into low, intermediate, or high risk for 1‐year mortality. Vital status was verified from the medical record, with a censor date of April 1, 2025. Data were analyzed using SigmaPlot Version 15.1 (2023), Systat Software Inc. Descriptive statistics are reported as median and interquartile ranges. This research was approved by the University of Cincinnati institutional review board (IRB 2023‐0415).

Results

A total of 35 patients fit inclusion criteria and were included in this study. Baseline characteristics of the cohort are found in Table 1. The median age was 61 years (IQR 42–67 years), with a predominance of female patients (74%) who identify as White race (71%), as expected of an adult PAH cohort and our region's demographic characteristics. The most common etiology of PAH was idiopathic (46%), followed by connective‐tissue‐disease‐associated (29%). Most patients were on intravenous epoprostenol prior to transition (54%), followed by subcutaneous treprostinil (34%). Baseline doses of parenteral PPA can be found in Figure 1. All but one patient was on background oral PAH therapy and most commonly with two agents (57%). No patients within this cohort were on sotatercept at time of therapy transition. Most transitions were planned (77%), and the most common reason for transition was due to a barrier preventing continuation of parenteral PPA therapy (57%). Almost one‐quarter of patients (23%) transitioned per patient preference in the context of improved PAH burden of disease. The transition details are reported in Table 2. The median duration of medication transition was 3 days (IQR 1–4 days), and transitions typically occurred with four planned steps (range 3–7 steps). All patients achieved the individualized transition planned target dose of selexipag, typically in the medium‐intensity dose range. At the time of data extraction, no patients had required transition back to PPA. The median time from transition to censor was 3.2 years (IQR 3.1–5.3 years).

The median time to follow‐up and post‐transition assessment by a pulmonary hypertension specialist was 5.5 weeks (IQR 1.4–9.6 weeks). Post‐transition, the full cohort generally maintained their functional class and risk status (Table 3, Figure 2). Post‐transition 6‐min walk test distance, circulating natriuretic peptide levels, and REVEAL Lite 2 were comparable to pre‐transition values. Patient reported side effects of selexipag at specialist follow up were stable or improved in 30 patients (86%) while five patients (14%) reported side effects that were new or worse compared to PPA. Reported side effects included headaches (n = 2), diarrhea (n = 2), and myalgias (n = 1). No patients had to return to PPA within study follow up. One patient independently stopped selexipag due to reported side effects (nausea and diarrhea). Within 1 year, for those that underwent follow‐up right heart catheterizations, there was a comparable burden of disease compared to pre‐transition values, as measured by pulmonary hemodynamics (Table 3). Most of the cohort survived beyond 1‐year post‐transition (n = 32, 91%) (Figure 3). At the time of data extraction, 12 patients had died at a median time of 28 months (IQR 16–43 months) after completion of transition. Unplanned transitions accounted for 8 (23%) of instances and were primarily performed due disposition requirements (n = 5) or new diagnosis of PAH during hospitalization (n = 3). At follow up, seven patients reported no side effects, and 1 patient reported new myalgia. Unplanned transition patients showed similar REVEAL Lite 2 scores as the total cohort: pre‐transition score of 9 and post‐transition score of 8. Mortality occurred in three patients (38%) from unplanned transitions at time of study completion.

Risk category improvement from pre‐transition to post‐transition was seen in 5 (14%) of patients, with an improvement from high to intermediate‐risk accounting for 4 of said instances. Risk categorization worsening occurred in four patients (11%) with 2 patients each worsening from low to intermediate risk and intermediate to high risk (Figure 2).

There were 14 patients (40%) with high‐risk score in the pre‐transition assessment, and 12 patients (29%) were high‐risk in the post‐transition assessment. The post‐transition, high‐risk patient characteristics were similar to the total cohort: median age of 64.5 years (IQR 55.5–72.3), female sex (n = 11, 92%), median hospital length of stay of 21 days (IQR 3–26), planned transitions (n = 7, 58%), and epoprostenol usage (n = 8, 67%).

Discussion

This report describes successful standardized inpatient transitions from parenteral PPA to oral selexipag in adult patients with PAH. Transitions occurred quickly (median 36 h), and patients appeared to have stable PAH disease post‐transition, suggesting safety and effectiveness. While more work is required to optimize the transition process from parenteral PPAs to oral selexipag, this work suggests an approach focusing on a 1–4‐day window, with 12‐h steps and simultaneous titration of parenteral PPA with oral selexipag in an inpatient setting, may be a promising start to address this clinical problem.

In this cohort, patients who transitioned from parenteral PPAs to selexipag were stable from a PAH standpoint. This cohort represents patients who were not on sotatercept. Although subjects were evaluated by pulmonary hypertension specialists to undergo an inpatient transition based upon their expert evaluation, almost one‐quarter (n = 8, 23%) were unplanned transitions, meaning that the transition decision occurred during hospital admission. The eight patients who underwent unplanned transitions demonstrated a similar change REVEAL Lite 2 Score compared to the total cohort. Although patient selection in the unplanned transitions were based upon patient specific variables (disposition need and new diagnosis/acute management) and not solely on clinical stability, we saw similar outcomes (risk status, mortality, and tolerance) as in the planned transitions.

Despite this heterogeneity, the cohort demonstrated stability across various metrics of PAH risk post‐transition (6‐min walk test distance, functional class, REVEAL Lite 2 score, natriuretic peptide, right heart catheterization (RHC) hemodynamics), and a low 12‐month mortality. This suggests a structured, stepwise transition from parenteral PPAs to oral selexipag can be accomplished in an inpatient setting without increased risk post‐transition, even in unplanned scenarios.

The transition plan utilized in this cohort focused on stepwise titration of parenteral PPA's and oral selexipag simultaneously, in 12‐h increments, over 1–4 days in an inpatient setting. While transition targets for selexipag, and specific titration amounts of parenteral PPAs, were individualized for each patient by the PH specialist and clinical pharmacist, the bulk of the transition plan was standardized. Prior literature on transition from parenteral PPA to selexipag involves small cohorts, with either outpatient protocols or slow transition over weeks to months [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. The retrospective nature did not allow clear extraction of side effects experienced by patients, but we believe that the rate of completion of transition and the rapid time frame is consistent with a high level of tolerability of this rapid titration. This report suggests that a more rapid titration, when occurring in a planned fashion in the inpatient setting, is both feasible and safe. We believe that this experience could help guide clinicians transitioning their patients from parenteral PPAs to oral selexipag.

Limitations of this report include the small, single center cohort. Planned and unplanned transitions were grouped together, limiting generalizability based upon variable patient status at enrollment. Patient selection for planned transitions (77%) likely involved selection bias in favor of successful transition. Undisclosed rationale for transitions accounted for 20% of those included, which can limit the application of the information. Not all subjects had pre‐ and post‐ transition testing (naturetic peptides, walk test, RHC, risk score), which may affect the comparison between pre‐ and post‐ transition values due to selection bias. Transitions occurred in a specialized PAH unit, which limits generalizability to other inpatient transitions. There were limited patients receiving IV treprostinil (N = 4) and oral treprostinil was not evaluated, as it is low use at this center. No patients were on sotatercept in this cohort, which can limit future application of this cohort.

Conclusion

In summary, rapid transition from parenteral PPA to selexipag can be performed in a safe and effective way for patients in a hospitalized setting with careful monitoring. Future research to evaluate candidacy for transition, tolerance of transition, and a protocolized approach to dosage selections are needed to further optimize the transition approach to patients on parenteral PPA's.
