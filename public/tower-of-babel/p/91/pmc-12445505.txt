Influencing factors of influenza vaccination willingness among the elderly in Wuxi city: A study based on the Behavioral and Social Drivers (BeSD) framework and structural equation modeling
Authors: Kun Yang, Shifan Jiang, Jingfeng Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInfluenza vaccination hesitancy among the elderly remains a concern, yet detailed data on influencing factors in Wuxi are limited. To fill this gap and provide a scientific basis for intervention and policy optimization, a questionnaire developed by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention based on the Behavioral and Social Drivers (BeSD) framework was adopted. Stratified random sampling across six districts of Wuxi city yielded 538 valid responses. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was employed to analyze influencing factors. Of the participants, 80.86% (435 individuals) expressed willingness to receive influenza vaccination. SEM fit indices met the standards (RMSEA = 0.054 < 0.08; AGFI, GFI, CFI, NFI > 0.90), indicating a good model fit. Social support was the most significant factor (total effect = 0.774), followed by vaccine safety (total effect = - 0.103) and practical issues (total effect = 0.116). These findings identify social support, vaccine safety cognition, and vaccination convenience as key determinants of influenza vaccination willingness, with social support having the greatest influence. The study underscores the urgent need for multi – dimensional interventions targeting social factors to enhance influenza vaccination uptake among Wuxi’s elderly population.KEYWORDS: Influenza vaccination, willingness, structural equation model, influencing factors, behavioral determinants

Introduction

Influenza poses a significant global public health burden due to its high morbidity and mortality.1 The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that seasonal influenza causes over 1 billion infections and 290,000–650,000 deaths annually.2 Older adults are particularly vulnerable to influenza because of age-related immune system decline, and infection with the influenza virus often leads to serious complications such as pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and other life-threatening conditions. Influenza-associated morbidity and mortality disproportionately affect specific high-risk populations. The elderly face the highest mortality rates, with adults over 75 years experiencing 141.15 deaths per 100,000 people annually.3

Vaccination remains one of the most cost-effective ways to prevent disease, as influenza vaccines effectively reduce the risks of infection, hospitalization, and death.4 Consequently, the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (China CDC) has designated older adults as a priority group for influenza vaccination.5 Wuxi, a city in China with a high aging rate (24.5% of its population was elderly in 2022), faces substantial challenges in managing elderly health. However, the influenza vaccination rate among Wuxi’s older adults stands at less than 5%, far below the WHO-recommended target of 75%.6 Influenza vaccination rates vary significantly across countries. A global meta-analysis of 522 studies from 68 countries found overall vaccination rates of 24.96% in the general population.7 Socioeconomic factors including gender, household income, education level, and residence size significantly influence vaccination uptake, with effects varying between countries.8 Therefore, investigating the factors influencing their vaccination willingness, tailoring interventions based on these factors, and increasing vaccination coverage to mitigate influenza incidence and severe complications are crucial aspects of Wuxi’s elderly health management.

Previous research on Wuxi’s vaccination willingness has largely relied on traditional statistical methods such as multiple linear regression and logistic regression. However, these approaches exhibit constraints in their ability to dissect the precise pathways and quantify the indirect effects inherent within the model. Structural equation modeling (SEM), which integrates factor analysis and path analysis, overcomes these shortcomings by enabling comprehensive examination of complex variable relationships.9 This study utilizes SEM to systematically identify key factors influencing influenza vaccination willingness among older adults in Wuxi and their interconnections, aiming to provide evidence-based insights for developing targeted interventions.

Materials and methods

Study participants

This study included local residents of Wuxi aged 60 years and older. Participants were selected randomly from the population of elderly individuals undergoing annual physical examinations in community health service centers across Wuxi. A stratified sampling approach was adopted to cover all six districts of Wuxi city.

The sample size was determined using the statistical formula for sample size calculation (n=Z2∗P∗1−Pe2), with parameters set as follows: willingness rate (P) was set at 25%, referencing the 25% pneumococcal vaccination rate among the local elderly population; a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), an allowable error of ± 5% (e = 0.05), and a design effect (Deff) of 1.5 for stratified sampling were applied. With a total population of 710,839 individuals aged over 60 in Wuxi city, the finite population correction had minimal impact, resulting in a corrected base sample size of approximately 289. Incorporating the stratified sampling design effect, the adjusted sample size was calculated as n = 289 × 1.5 = 434. Finally, accounting for an assumed 20% non-response rate, the final required sample size was derived as 543.

All six districts of Wuxi city were covered in this study. Specifically, 100 participants were randomly recruited from each of Liangxi, Binhu, Xishan, Huishan, and Xinwu Districts, while Jingkai District – owing to its smaller population (approximately half that of the other districts) – recruited 50 participants. This sampling approach yielded a total sample of 550 participants, ensuring geographic representativeness across the city’s administrative divisions.

Study design

This questionnaire10 is revised from the one previously released by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention. Minor revisions were made to the wording of the questionnaire to enhance comprehension among local elderly individuals, ensuring better adaptability to the study population in Wuxi city. The questionnaire included items on perceptions of influenza risks; vaccine safety; willingness to accept vaccination recommendations from different institutions and personnel; and practical issues encountered during vaccination (20 items scale). The response options were “completely disagree,” “partially agree,” “agree” or “strongly agree.” The questionnaire was provided in electronic format and delivered via WeChat, a widely used app, to ensure the acceptability of our target population. After face-to-face communication with community workers and signing the electronic informed consent form, participants received the questionnaire through a Wechat message and completed it independently on their mobile devices before leaving the community health service centers. Community workers were responsible for implementing quality control measures during the questionnaire completion phase. Data collection was completed between October and November 2024.

Statistical analysis

Following data cleaning, invalid questionnaires were excluded prior to performing exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with SPSS 18.0 statistical software. The criteria for factor analysis applicability included a Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure > 0.7 and a Bartlett’s test of sphericity with P < .05, indicating the presence of common factors. The principal component analysis was employed as the extraction method, and varimax orthogonal rotation was used for factor rotation. A cumulative variance explained rate ≥ 40%11was considered to demonstrate that questionnaire items effectively reflected the variation in vaccination willingness. Based on the extracted common factors, AMOS 21.0 was applied to establish a structural equation model (SEM) to construct path relationships between exogenous and endogenous latent variables, test model fit, and examine the internal mechanisms. The significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Wuxi Municipal Center for Disease Control and Prevention (2024–015). Participants were notified of the purpose and potential use of the collected data in the informed consent form. Only those who signed the electronic informed consent form were permitted to complete the questionnaire.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 559 questionnaires were collected in this study. Based on quality control reports from community workers, 21 questionnaires with extremely short completion time (less than 1 minute) or containing plagiarized responses were excluded, yielding 538 valid responses. Demographic characteristics showed 240 males (44.61%) and 298 females (55.39%), with an average age of 69 years. In terms of medical history, 226 individuals (42.0%) had hypertension, 100 (18.59%) had diabetes, and 164 residents (30.48%) reported influenza infection in the past two years. A total of 303 participants (56.32%) reported a history of influenza vaccination, among whom 4 reported experiencing adverse events following immunization. Finally, 435 participants (80.86% of all valid responses) indicated willingness to receive influenza vaccination.

Structural equation model: factor analysis

Based on relevant literature on vaccine hesitancy and references to the World Health Organization’s behavioral and social drivers, combined with the general analysis results of the questionnaire, reliability analysis and exploratory factor analysis were conducted to derive common factors and construct structural equations. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the questionnaire was calculated as 0.796, the KMO test value was 0.937, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity showed χ2 = 8771.544 (P < .001), indicating correlations among research variables and suitability for further factor analysis. Using the criterion of eigenvalues > 1 and combining with the scree plot, five common factors were extracted, explaining a cumulative variance of 72.165%. The obtained factor loading matrix was subjected to Kaiser-standardized orthogonal rotation. Structural equation model: construction and revision

Taking whether elderly individuals are willing to receive influenza vaccination as the endogenous latent variable, factor analysis yielded five factors: Social Support, Practical Issues, Vaccine Safety, Disease Threat, and Vaccination and Infection History. The development phase of the original questionnaire, which included analyses of construct validity and reliability, also yielded these five factors. Thus, these five factors were taken as latent variables, and the items of each factor were used as observed variables to construct the initial, unrevised structural equation model, as shown in Figure 1.

Structural equation model (initial) of influenza vaccination willingness among the elderly in Wuxi city. Path coefficients are standardized.

The Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Adjusted Goodness-of-Fit Index (AGFI), Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), and Normalized Fit Index (NFI) were selected to evaluate the model. Path coefficients and significance levels were calculated. Three insignificant latent variable paths – with path coefficients having P values above the 0.05 significance level – were removed after checking if any of these paths were reasonable enough to be retained. The insignificant path between Practical Issues and Disease Threat is likely to be caused by the weak relationship between the two factors. The other two insignificant paths are possibly caused by insufficiency of data volume and measurement error.

Structural equation model: result analysis

Factors with direct effects on vaccination willingness included Social Support, Vaccine Safety, and Practical Issues (P < 0.05), with Social support having the most significant impact. While allowing for the correlation between them, disease threat had no statistically significant direct effect on vaccination willingness (P = .526), but indirectly influenced vaccination willingness through vaccine safety. Disease Threat, Vaccination and Infection History, and Social Support all had indirect effects on vaccination willingness. Analysis of the structural equation path diagram revealed that Social Support indirectly influenced vaccination willingness through Vaccine Safety, meaning that elderly individuals more susceptible to social factors promoting vaccination held fewer negative perceptions of vaccines and were thus more likely to receive influenza vaccination. Discussion

Prior studies on influenza vaccination willingness predominantly employed multilinear or logistic regression.12,13 These analyses identified significant correlations between demographic factors (e.g., age, education) and vaccination intention, yet faced three critical limitations:

Inability to model latent constructs : Traditional regression treats abstract concepts (e.g., ‘vaccine safety perception’) as simple composite scores, ignoring measurement error and item-level covariance.14

Omission of mediating pathways: While regression confirmed associations, it could not quantify indirect effects.15

Isolated variable testing: Regression examines predictors in isolation, failing to capture synergistic effects within a holistic theoretical framework.16

While social support, vaccine safety, and practical issues align with global determinants of vaccine hesitancy, our SEM analysis reveals critical interrelationships specific to Wuxi’s elderly. First, social support operated through a dual pathway: directly promoting willingness and indirectly alleviating vaccine safety concerns. This suggests that endorsements from trusted sources (e.g., family, doctors) not only motivate action but also reshape perceived risks – a nuance previously underemphasized in intervention design. Second, disease threat paradoxically suppressed willingness by amplifying safety concerns, despite no direct effect. This indicates that fear-based messaging may inadvertently heighten hesitancy unless paired with safety reassurance. Finally, practical issues (access, cost) had the smallest direct effect, underscoring that logistical improvements alone are inadequate without addressing socio-cognitive barriers.

This study found that social support is the core driving factor influencing influenza vaccination willingness among the elderly in Wuxi city. Recommendations from family members, doctors, and authoritative institutions can significantly enhance vaccination willingness, which is consistent with the findings of many studies at home and abroad. A systematic review and meta-analysis of studies across multiple countries reveals that healthcare provider recommendation is the strongest predictor of vaccine uptake. Provider recommendation increases the odds of maternal vaccination by 10–12 times for pertussis and influenza.17 For the general population, vaccine effectiveness, perceived side effects, and the proportion of vaccinated acquaintances significantly impact immunization decisions.18 A cross-sectional study in Singapore showed that advice from healthcare professionals is crucial for improving vaccination rate. Although better understanding of the disease and vaccines initially led to higher vaccination rate, this effect became insignificant after adjusting for doctor recommendations and other factors.19 Studies in Tunisia,20 Japan,21 Hong Kong,22 and Australia23 have all reached similar conclusions. This indicates that enhancing scientific advice from healthcare professionals and authoritative institutions, rather than simply providing the public with information about influenza vaccination, can more effectively improve vaccination rate. It is recommended that national authoritative institutions should regularly issue scientific guidelines and recommendations on vaccination to provide healthcare professionals with authoritative references. Local regions should strengthen training for medical staff on vaccine-related knowledge and skills to ensure they master the latest vaccine research results and vaccination techniques.

Additionally, the source of vaccination advice provided to the public requires careful consideration. Unlike many developed countries,24 vaccination service in China is primarily managed by community health service centers, where the medical staff responsible for the service are usually public health physicians or nurses rather than clinical doctors. The public tends to trust clinical doctors more than public health physicians regarding vaccination advice, and they are more likely to accept such advice when it comes from clinical doctors. Therefore, it is recommended to implement a “vaccine prescription” system, under which medical institutions open vaccination clinics, or doctors in clinical departments recommend suitable vaccinations based on the health status of patients and issue vaccine prescriptions. Especially for elderly patients with chronic conditions such as cardiovascular diseases, chronic respiratory diseases, metabolic disorders, hepatic or renal insufficiency, hematologic diseases, neurological disorders, neuromuscular dysfunction, or those with immunosuppressive conditions or compromised immune systems, the risk of developing severe influenza-related complications is significantly elevated. Healthcare providers should pay special attention to these high-risk individuals, strongly recommend influenza vaccination to them, and provide disease prevention education. As a supplement to traditional vaccination promotion methods, this approach could effectively improve vaccination rates. Besides, optimize Wuxi’s existing immunization information system’s SMS/WeChat reminder function. In addition to sending appointment schedules, add a ‘family sharing reminder’ option (upon authorization by the elderly individual, reminders can be simultaneously sent to their designated children). Embed brief social support messages, such as: “Your family cares about your health. Timely influenza vaccination is effective protection.”

Consistent with findings from multiple studies,25,26 concerns about vaccine safety represent another critical factor influencing vaccination willingness identified. The World Health Organization’s ‘5C’ model of vaccine hesitancy encompasses Confidence, Complacency, Constraints, Calculation, and Collective Responsibility. Studies have found that confidence in vaccine safety and effectiveness is consistently a significant predictor of vaccination behavior.27 In some recent reviews and meta-analyses, concerns about vaccine safety and efficacy are consistently reported as major barriers to vaccine uptake.28–30 Frequent vaccine-related incidents in recent years have exposed numerous loopholes in the vaccine circulation chain, leading to a decline in the public trust in government agencies. As biological products, vaccines inevitably carry the risk of adverse events following immunization. However, social media proliferation tends to amplify low-probability events and rapidly disseminate information, leading to public misunderstandings or even opposition to vaccines. Therefore, it is essential to strengthen regulation across the entire vaccine production-to-circulation chain, implement a traceability system, improve the transparency of vaccine information, and thereby gradually restore public trust in vaccines. Subsequently, vaccine knowledge should be popularized through authoritative channels to reverse cognitive biases.

Additionally, our study shows that practical issues encountered during vaccination also influence the willingness of elderly people to receive influenza vaccination. An umbrella review shows that vaccine hesitancy is influenced by vaccination inconvenience, cost, and lack of information.28 In developed countries such as the United States, the United Kingdom, and Italy, vaccination can be administered in various settings including pharmacies, schools, hospitals, and clinics. In contrast, most vaccination clinics in China are currently located in community health service centers, resulting in limited vaccination sites. Due to aging issues, elderly people have smaller activity ranges, leading to poor vaccine accessibility. Meanwhile, most domestic health service centers primarily focus on childhood vaccinations, with infrequent adult vaccination sessions. Taking Wuxi city as an example, most health service centers only arrange half a day per week for adults to have vaccination service, and such low frequency further reduces the enthusiasm of the elderly for vaccination. Furthermore, insufficient social security and medical insurance make vaccine costs still a factor contributing to vaccine hesitancy among the elderly. Research indicates that economic factors, including vaccine costs and social support, contribute to hesitancy, with individuals in higher GDP areas showing more concern about vaccine prices.31 It is recommended that government encourage multiple stakeholders to increase the number of health service centers. Meanwhile, other social organizations wishing to provide vaccination services should be encouraged, with appropriate relaxation of requirements for personnel and venues, explore establishing temporary or fixed vaccination sites in qualified settings across Wuxi, subject to policy approval and safety assurance, potential locations include: preventive healthcare/geriatrics departments of secondary or higher-level hospitals, medical clinics in large enterprises/public institutions, and nursing home. Require community health service centers across Wuxi to significantly increase the frequency and availability of adult influenza vaccination services during the peak flu season (e.g., October to January of the following year). For example: Provide services for at least 2–3 full days or afternoon/evening sessions per week to accommodate the varying schedules of the elderly and their working children. promote time-slot appointments to reduce waiting times, thereby reducingtransportation and time cost for elderly vaccination and providing them with convenient vaccination services. In regions with relatively developed economies, pilot programs can also be launched to use medical insurance to cover vaccine costs, or implement free influenza vaccination programs for the elderly, thereby alleviating their financial concerns about vaccination expenses and improving vaccination rates.

Besides, the SEM paths clarify why multidimensional strategies are essential. For example, a ‘vaccine prescription’ system (leveraging clinical doctors’ trust) would simultaneously enhance social support and counter safety misconceptions. Similarly, interventions targeting safety concerns (e.g., traceability system) could amplify the impact of social support and mitigate disease-threat-induced hesitancy. These findings add new perspectives to our determinants, focusing on the interrelationships unveiled by SEM.

This study constructed a structural equation model with five latent variables, which cumulatively explained 72.165% of the total data variance. However, there may be other potential factors influencing influenza vaccination willingness among the elderly, such as perceptions of vaccine efficacy and history of chronic diseases,32 which require further research to refine the model. In conclusion, this study employed Structural Equation Modeling (SEM), which significantly enhanced our understanding beyond traditional regression approaches by revealing the complex interplay and mediating pathways among latent constructs. Specifically, SEM elucidated that social support not only directly promotes vaccination willingness but also indirectly facilitates it by mitigating vaccine safety concerns. It further uncovered the counterintuitive, indirect suppressive effect of disease threat through amplified safety perceptions, which would likely remain obscured in conventional analyses. Therefore, social support, perceptions of vaccine safety, and vaccination accessibility are confirmed as key factors influencing influenza vaccination willingness among the elderly in Wuxi City. Multidimensional intervention strategies should be adopted, including implementing a “vaccine prescription” system, establishing a vaccine traceability mechanism, expanding adult vaccination sites, and providing financial support for vaccination costs. These measures aim to enhance willingness to vaccinate and improve the health outcomes of the elderly in Wuxi.

Limitations and future research directions

First, the cross-sectional nature of our study establishes associations but cannot infer causality or track changes in willingness over time. More importantly, it does not directly measure actual vaccination behavior. Future study should adopt longitudinal designs to track the trajectory of vaccination willingness and its determinants over multiple seasons.

Second, our study did not examine subgroup variations in the impact of vaccine safety perceptions. Future study should identify subpopulations most vulnerable to safety concerns, model interaction effects between safety perceptions and moderators like education level or social support, employ mixed-methods designs to uncover behavioral mechanisms driving safety sensitivity in high-risk groups. Such insights would enable precisely targeted interventions for those most affected by safety-related hesitancy.

Third, Although this study utilized WeChat, a widely used app, for questionnaire completion and with staff assistance throughout the process, the electronic format still excludes some elderly individuals who struggle with mobile phone operation. Furthermore, Having elderly individuals complete electronic questionnaires will inevitably lead to misunderstandings of the questions and incorrect responses. This creates selection bias and self-report bias, which may affect the final results by leading to the inclusion of populations with easier access to online information, thus increasing the effect of factors such as Social Support and Vaccine Safety and potentially underestimating the effect of Practical Issues.
