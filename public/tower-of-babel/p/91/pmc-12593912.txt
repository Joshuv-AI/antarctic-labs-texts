Assessing the quality for integrated guidelines: systematic comparison between the AGREE Ⅱ and AGREE-HS tools
Authors: Gezhi Zhang, Danping Zheng, An Li, Weixuan Bai, Cuifang Liu, Xue Chen, Fangqi Liu, Mengyu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study evaluates WHO guidelines, particularly those integrating health systems guidance (HSG) and clinical practice guideline (CPG), by comparing the AGREE Ⅱ and AGREE-HS tools to identify differences in assessing integrated guidelines (IGs).MethodsThis exploratory evaluation used WHO epidemic guidelines. A comprehensive search in the WHO Institutional Repository for Information Sharing (IRIS) identified CPGs, HSGs, and IGs, which were assessed using AGREE Ⅱ and/or AGREE-HS tools.ResultsA total of 157 guidelines (20 CPGs, 101 HSGs, and 36 IGs) were included. CPGs scored significantly higher than IGs with AGREE Ⅱ (P < 0.001), but no significant difference was found using AGREE-HS (P = 0.185). Significant differences were found in multiple domains of AGREE Ⅱ (P < 0.05), including Scope and Purpose, Stakeholder Involvement, and Editorial Independence. AGREE-HS also revealed differences in cost-effectiveness and ethical criteria (P < 0.05).ConclusionCPGs were of significantly higher quality than IGs when assessed with AGREE Ⅱ, while IGs and HSGs showed similar quality with AGREE-HS. Despite consistent overall scores for IGs across both tools, specific item scores varied. These findings underscore the need for more transparent reporting in IGs, particularly regarding developer information, conflicts of interest, and patient guidance. Future work should focus on developing tools that integrate both AGREE Ⅱ and AGREE-HS to improve guideline evaluation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-025-02690-3.

Introduction

Guidelines are systematic documents providing recommendations for specific situations, based on research, expert consensus, or best practices, to guide decision-making [1]. Clinical practice guidelines (CPGs), the most common type, play a vital role in medicine, offering standardized recommendations for disease prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and management [2]. Health systems guidance (HSG) focuses on broader issues like health policies, resource allocation, and service delivery models, aiming to improve efficiency and equity. HSG is often issued by health authorities like World Health Organization (WHO) for national or regional reforms [3, 4]. Both CPGs and HSGs aim to enhance healthcare quality and public health.

Appropriate methodology and strategy are essential for successful guideline implementation. Tools developed by the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation (AGREE) Collaboration have become internationally recognized for evaluating guideline quality and providing frameworks for their development [5]. The AGREE Ⅱ, derived from the original AGREE Instrument [6], supports CPG evaluation and has been used in various clinical disciplines, including cancer [7], renal disease [8], cardiovascular disease [9], and respiratory disease [10]. The AGREE-HS aids in the development and evaluation of HSGs [11], demonstrating usability, reliability, and validity despite being less widely used than AGREE Ⅱ [11, 12].

Some guidelines blend both clinical and health system elements. For example, HSGs may include health system actions (e.g., quarantine) and clinical advice (e.g., treatment). In such cases, AGREE Ⅱ and AGREE-HS can be applied to the relevant Sect [13]. In an ongoing study of WHO’s epidemic guidelines, we found that some guidelines address both health system and clinical aspects. Thus, we assessed these ‘integrated guidelines’ along with CPGs and HSGs using AGREE Ⅱ and AGREE-HS to evaluate guideline quality and explore the effectiveness of both tools in assessing integrated guidelines (IGs).

Methods

Study design

The literature search was conducted between May 18 and May 20, 2024. Screening and classification of eligible guidelines were undertaken in June 2024. Quality appraisal using the AGREE Ⅱ and AGREE-HS tools was completed in July 2024, and statistical analyses were finalized in August 2024.

Search strategy

The WHO is recognized as a leading authority in guideline development. To reduce quality bias, we conducted a sensitive search in WHO’s Institutional Repository for Information Sharing (IRIS) using keywords “recommendation”, “guide”, “guideline”, “guidance”, “policy”, “plan”, and “strategy”, combined with disease names. The search strategy and results are detailed in Table S1 of the supplementary material, and data were exported to Excel 2019 for screening.

Screening process

Two researchers (GZ, DZ) removed duplicates manually. Four researchers (GZ, AL, WB, XC) cross-screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text review. Each document was reviewed or classified by at least two researchers, with disagreements resolved through discussion with the senior researcher (ML).

Inclusion, Exclusion, and classification criteria

Eligible guidelines must be formally written and provide specific recommendations on health systems challenges and/or clinical management. Multilingual versions were preferred over English versions. Guidelines were assessed as a series if multiple documents were part of a structured guideline package. During full-text review, guidelines were classified into three categories based on their primary focus:

The following types of documents were excluded: incomplete guidelines, summaries, policy briefs, meeting reports, data sets, manuals, technical specifications, and brand-specific vaccine instructions to avoid conflicts of interest.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in this study’s design or dissemination. Future research may include patient perspectives to improve the applicability of guideline assessments.

Evaluation tools

The AGREE Ⅱ tool (2017) consists of 23 items grouped into six quality domains, plus two overall assessments. Each item includes detailed criteria that guide scoring. The AGREE-HS tool (2018) includes five core items and two overall assessments, each accompanied by defined criteria. While AGREE Ⅱ uses expansive descriptions under each item, AGREE-HS outlines required elements more succinctly. The items in AGREE Ⅱ and the criteria in AGREE-HS share conceptual overlap; both tools cover 15 common subjects and one overall assessment. A comparative schematic is provided in Fig. 1, which illustrates the relationship between evaluation items and criteria by comparing AGREE-HS Item 2 Participants and its criteria with AGREE Ⅱ Items 4, 5, 22, and 23 and their respective criteria. This supports that the criteria in AGREE-HS and the items in AGREE Ⅱ correspond to similar dimensions, and also explains our rationale for evaluating not only selected items but also the full set of AGREE-HS criteria in the subsequent analysis. Simplified common elements of both tools are summarized in Table S2, and full texts are listed in Table S3.

Scoring method

Guidelines were evaluated using the following approach: CPGs were assessed using the AGREE Ⅱ; HSGs were assessed using the AGREE-HS; and IGs were evaluated using both tools. This allowed us to capture guideline-type-specific strengths and compare the two appraisal frameworks within the same group (IGs). For scoring, both tools suggest two approaches: (1) calculating the mean score per item or domain, and (2) applying a linear transformation to standardize scores across guidelines. In our study, we used a slightly modified version of the scoring method tailored to our evaluation design. To ensure comparability, we cross-validated our results against those obtained using the original scoring methods from both manuals. No significant differences were found; detailed comparisons are presented in Table S4. The scoring methods used in this study are outlined in Table S5. We added criterion scoring for AGREE-HS to refine the evaluation and applied different calculations without altering the final conclusions for consistency in comparisons.

Neither the AGREE Ⅱ nor AGREE-HS manuals provide explicit thresholds to classify quality as low, moderate, or high. Both instruments are intended for relative or descriptive interpretation. In this study, scores were compared across guideline types and tools rather than categorized into absolute quality levels.

Assessing groups

Six assessors in three groups (CL&WB, AL&XC, GZ&FL) used AGREE-HS and AGREE Ⅱ to evaluate guidelines. After training and pre-evaluating four documents, remaining guidelines were randomly assigned for scoring by two evaluators.

Statistical analysis

The intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) assessed evaluator consistency using SPSS 26.0. Independent samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U-tests compared scores between two guideline groups assessed with the same tool, while paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed rank tests compared scores between tools. A P-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Qualitative analysis

In addition to numerical scoring, assessors were asked to document the rationale and supporting text for each item rated using the AGREE II and AGREE-HS tools. This practice was consistent with guidance from both tools, which encourage appraisers to justify their scores and consider item-specific nuances when interpreting content. The AGREE-HS manual, for example, explicitly states that “judgments by the appraisers are required… appraisers are encouraged to use the comment boxes to provide justification for their scores.”

During the appraisal process, assessors used a pre-designed Excel-based data extraction form that included: (1) the item name, (2) the numeric score (1–7 scale), (3) the corresponding paragraph or location in the guideline used as evidence, and (4) free-text comments explaining the rationale for the score. All assessors independently recorded their observations while evaluating the documents. After completion, the Excel forms were consolidated. For qualitative analysis, thematic synthesis was employed. Comments were reviewed to identify recurring patterns related to tool usability, clarity of guideline content, and perceived differences between AGREE II and AGREE-HS. Codes were inductively generated and organized into overarching themes. The qualitative findings presented under the section “Assessors’ comments” reflect these aggregated themes, enriched by direct quotations from assessors to illustrate key points.

Results

Search results

A total of 4399 documents were exported from IRIS, with 2800 duplicates and 831 non-English versions excluded. After screening titles and abstracts, 180 documents were retained for full-text review, leading to 157 documents included in the study (see Fig. 2). We included 20 CPGs, 101 HSGs, and 36 IGs addressing both health system challenges and clinical management, with characteristics listed in Table S6.

Quality assessment

Assessing consistency

The ICC values for assessor agreement were 0.85 (P < 0.001) for AGREE II and 0.78 (P < 0.001) for AGREE-HS, indicating good reliability (0.75–0.9) and generally good consistency [14].

Assessment with AGREE Ⅱ

As shown in Table 1, the mean overall score for the 20 CPGs was 5.28 (71.4%), with domain scores ranging from 54.9% (Applicability) to 85.3% (Scope and purpose). The 36 IGs scored an average of 4.35 (55.8%) overall, with the highest score for Scope and purpose (76.0%) and the lowest for Editorial independence (31.7%).

Assessment with AGREE-HS

The mean overall score for 101 HSGs was 56.5% (4.19). Topic scored highest (4.91), and Participants scored lowest (3.44). Other item scores were Methods (3.96), Recommendations (4.83), and Implementability (3.81). The transformed average score for 36 IGs under AGREE-HS was 59.6%, with Recommendations scoring highest (5.02), and Participants scoring lowest (3.76).

Recommend for use

The results of the recommended use of all guidelines are shown in Table S7. Most IGs (n = 15, 41.7%) were recommended for immediate use or modification by both assessors. Five guidelines (13.9%) were suggested modifications by one assessor, but not considered for use by the other. Both assessors recognized that four guidelines (11.1%) were ready for immediate use, while 11 guidelines (30.6%) should be modified before use. Only one guideline (2.8%) was not recommended for use by either assessor.

Comparison analysis

Overall scores

The overall score comparisons for the guidelines are presented in Fig. 3A and Table S8. When assessed using AGREE Ⅱ, the overall scores of CPGs were significantly higher than those of IGs (P < 0.001). In contrast, when assessed using AGREE-HS, no significant difference was observed between the overall scores of HSGs and IGs (P = 0.185). Notably, 19 IGs (52.8%) received higher scores under the AGREE-HS compared to their AGREE Ⅱ scores (see Fig. 3B). However, there was no significant difference in the overall scores of IGs between the two tools (P = 0.889).

AGREE Ⅱ Item/domain scores

Comparing the 6 domains and 23 items of AGREE Ⅱ for IGs and CPGs (see Fig. 4A), significant differences were found in Scope and purpose (P = 0.004), Stakeholder involvement (P = 0.004), Rigour of development (P = 0.002), Clarity of presentation (P = 0.002), and Editorial independence (P < 0.001). Specific items with significant differences included item 2 (health questions, P < 0.001), item 4 (development group, P < 0.001), item 7 (systematic methods, P = 0.023), item 8 (evidence selection criteria, P = 0.008), item 9 (strengths and limitations of evidence, P < 0.001), item 10 (recommendation formulation methods, P < 0.001), item 11 (health benefits, P = 0.001), item 12 (link between recommendations and evidence, P = 0.013), item 15 (specific recommendations, P = 0.007), item 16 (management options, P = 0.029), item 17 (key recommendations, P = 0.008), item 19 (practice advice, P = 0.012), item 22 (funding statement, P < 0.001), and item 23 (competing interests handling, P < 0.001).

AGREE-HS Criterion/Item scores

Comparing the 5 items and 35 criteria of AGREE-HS for IGs and HSGs (see Fig. 4B), significant differences were found in criterion 14 (cost-effectiveness, P = 0.022), criterion 21 (ethical principles, P = 0.043), criterion 24 (clear recommendations, P = 0.007), and criterion 25 (actionable recommendations, P = 0.008).

Assessor’s comments

Assessors were encouraged to provide comments on the included guidelines and the evaluation process using both tools to offer different perspectives on the quality assessment of IGs.

Regarding the IGs, assessors agreed that identifying clinical management content was easier, while the content related to health system challenges was broader and less clearly categorized. One assessor noted, “The guidelines are more mature in terms of clinical management, with clear chapters such as ‘XX management’ or ‘XX treatment.’ However, the macro-level content on health system challenges is often less straightforward and requires careful review.”

When using the AGREE Ⅱ tool, assessors spent less time on evaluations, and inconsistencies in understanding the items were less frequent. The assessors felt that many IGs followed a standardized format, making it easier to locate evaluation items in the document. One assessor shared, “Since we’ve used AGREE Ⅱ many times, we assessed the guidelines with ease. The corresponding sections for each domain are usually in sequential order, starting with the purpose and scope, followed by methodology, recommendations, and concluding with funding and team information. While not all guides fully report these, the basis for scoring is largely agreed upon. The manual provides detailed guidance.”

In contrast, using the AGREE-HS tool required more time for assessors to search for scoring support and reach consensus. Despite only five items being scored, the criteria were described in great detail, making it difficult to determine the qualitative differences between guidelines by focusing only on the broad scores. One assessor remarked, “Some of the AGREE-HS criteria were initially unclear, and finding support for scoring in the guidelines was difficult. For example, the TOPIC item requires a description of health system challenge priorities, but most guidelines don’t provide this clearly. Another example is IMPLEMENTABILITY, which requires descriptions of stakeholder acceptability and implementation affordability, which are rarely mentioned.”

Assessors were also asked about items in AGREE Ⅱ and criteria in AGREE-HS with similar meanings but differing scores. They noted, “The content evaluated by both tools partially overlaps. There is consistency in evaluating population involvement, update plans, and conflicts of interest. However, AGREE Ⅱ is more concise, while AGREE-HS tends to be more comprehensive. For instance, AGREE Ⅱ mentions target populations primarily as patients or the public, while AGREE-HS includes all stakeholders, covering a wider range within the health system.”

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first attempt to assess integrated guidelines, which include health system and clinical management content, using both AGREE Ⅱ and AGREE-HS. We also evaluated CPGs and HSGs separately to explore the efficacy of both tools in assessing IGs.

Summary of this study

Different evaluation levels

Although both AGREE Ⅱ and AGREE-HS use the term “item,” they differ in their hierarchical structures. AGREE Ⅱ uses 23 items as the second level of evaluation under six quality domains, while AGREE-HS uses items as the first level, specifying five elements that should be included in guidelines. The items in AGREE-HS correspond broadly to the six domains in AGREE Ⅱ: Topic - Scope and purpose; Participants - Stakeholder involvement; Methods - Rigour of development, Editorial independence; Recommendations - Clarity of presentation; Implementability - Applicability. These differences may stem from the distinct contexts in which the tools were developed. CPGs, which address clinical guidance, require high evidence levels and focus on specific clinical issues. To ensure uniformity, six major domains are defined in AGREE Ⅱ to standardize reporting, with specific evaluation items under each domain. In contrast, HSGs, which are policy-based, address broader health system challenges. Compared to CPGs, HSGs are more macro-level, making it harder to achieve homogeneity across diverse health system challenges. AGREE-HS, therefore, emphasizes a few key elements while providing less stringent criteria than AGREE Ⅱ, with additional requirements placed at the next level for reference.

Significant quality differences with AGREE Ⅱ

Under the AGREE Ⅱ evaluation, CPGs scored significantly higher than IGs overall. Both CPGs and IGs had the highest scores in Scope and purpose and Clarity of presentation, while CPGs scored lowest in Applicability and IGs in Editorial independence. The results of the CPG evaluation were similar to other studies [15, 16]. Low Editorial independence in IGs is due to limited reporting on conflicts of interest or funding influences. CPGs scored higher than IGs in all domains except Applicability, likely due to the fact that implementation-related elements, including describing facilitators and impediments, providing recommendations for practice, as well as evaluating the effects of the guidelines after they have been applied, are common issues that tend to be overlooked in guideline development. More than half of the items showed significant differences, with trends largely matching domain scores.

Approximate quality distribution with AGREE-HS

Under the AGREE-HS evaluation, HSGs and IGs had similar overall scores, with both scoring highest in Topic and Recommendations and lowest in Participants. This trend aligns with previous studies [12, 15]. Unlike other studies, the Methods in this study scored higher, possibly due to the standardized WHO guidelines. No significant score differences were found between CPGs and IGs. IGs scored significantly higher than HSGs in four criteria, mainly related to Recommendations, including evidence of costs and benefits, ethical principles, and recommendation presentation—areas not covered by the AGREE Ⅱ evaluation, where IGs outperformed HSGs.

Differences in specific aspects rather than overall scores in IGs

In general, the IGs scored below 60% on both AGREE Ⅱ and AGREE-HS, with no significant difference between the two tools. Both tools revealed gaps in reporting developer information and conflicts of interest. Significant score differences were found in four areas, with AGREE-HS scoring higher for stakeholder input, evidence selection, and recommendation presentation, while AGREE Ⅱ scored higher for pros and cons of recommendations. Assessors noted that AGREE-HS criteria were more comprehensive, providing more detailed descriptions and relevant assessment basis.

About the integrated guidelines

Health systems face ongoing challenges, such as uneven resource allocation, inefficient services, and patient safety issues, while clinicians deal with diagnostic accuracy, treatment choices, and patient management. To address both health system and clinical challenges, comprehensive guidelines are urgently needed. No study has defined or evaluated guidelines with both CPG and HSG characteristics. Marshall-Andon et al. conducted a systematic review on post-COVID-19 syndrome rehabilitation guidelines, using AGREE Ⅱ and AGREE-HS to assess clinical and service guidelines, respectively [15]. This is the only study found that applied both tools but did not focus on combined characteristic guidelines.

Combined use of the AGREE series of tools

The AGREE tools offer a structured framework for assessing guideline quality, ensuring transparency and rigor in development. However, a single evaluation approach has cognitive limitations. Recognizing that AGREE Ⅱ focuses on the overall methodological quality and reporting of CPGs, AGREE-REX was developed in 2018 to assess the quality and implementability of CPG recommendations [17, 18]. Using both tools together offers a comprehensive framework for evaluating guideline quality and applicability. For example, Triana et al. showed that integrating these tools helps clinicians make informed decisions in urgent situations like the COVID-19 pandemic [19], while Korai et al. emphasized the importance of evaluating both quality and applicability for better healthcare delivery [16]. AGREE-HS, unlike AGREE-REX, was created as a new tool to assess the quality of HSGs. Brouwers et al. highlighted the challenges in developing high-quality HSGs, such as accounting for the complexity of health systems, using diverse evidence, and considering implementation factors [13].

Health system challenges are inherently broader than clinical issues, and HSGs may contain clinical management elements. Using either AGREE-HS or AGREE Ⅱ alone may not sufficiently assess these guidelines. As far as we are aware, our study is the first to jointly use AGREE Ⅱ and AGREE-HS to evaluate guidelines with both CPG and HSG characteristics. The results showed no significant difference in the overall scores of IGs evaluated with both tools, supporting their reliability and stability. Most domains and items of IGs scored lower than CPGs under AGREE Ⅱ, but fewer items showed significant differences under AGREE-HS. This suggests that IGs, though containing both CPG and HSG elements, may align better with AGREE-HS. However, evaluating them with AGREE Ⅱ remains valuable, as high-quality clinical guidance requires robust evidence and processes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Although we conducted a comprehensive search from the WHO IRIS repositories, these guidelines were authoritative but lack generalizability. It is therefore necessary to include more guidelines developed by other countries or organizations for further research in the future. Another limitation is that neither AGREE Ⅱ nor AGREE-HS provides standardized thresholds or cut-off values for categorizing guideline quality. We therefore focused on continuous scores and relative comparisons, which remain valid, though this may limit direct interpretability for individual guideline classification.

Future perspectives

Integrated guidelines aim to optimize resource allocation and improve healthcare efficiency and quality. They should also include patient education to help patients manage their health and reduce system burden. Our study shows room for improvement in IG quality. Both AGREE Ⅱ and AGREE-HS have strengths and limitations—AGREE Ⅱ is concise but lacks comprehensiveness, while AGREE-HS is detailed but sometimes lacks clear counterparts in guidelines. Future research may benefit from improving tools evaluating IGs based on these frameworks, and exploring consensus-based thresholds or interpretive frameworks to enhance the practical application of these evaluation tools.

Conclusion

This study evaluated WHO guidelines with both HSG and CPG content using AGREE Ⅱ and AGREE-HS tools. We found a significant quality difference between CPGs and IGs with AGREE Ⅱ, but similar quality between IGs and HSGs with AGREE-HS. Although overall scores for IGs were consistent across both tools, specific item scores showed notable differences. We recommend more comprehensive reporting of developer information, conflicts of interest, and patient guidance in IGs. Additionally, new evaluation tools building on AGREE Ⅱ and AGREE-HS should be developed to improve guideline quality assessment.
