Fusobacterium nucleatum regulates nicotinamide metabolism via the upregulation of NNMT to activate PARP1/MAPK signalling in colitis-associated cancer
Authors: Jing Lei, Peiyu Yang, Feng Xu, Wenhao Su, Zihao Liu, Juan Li, Siyuan Chen, Yongyu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Colitis-associated cancer (CAC) is a serious complication of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Recent evidence has indicated that Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum) may promote intestinal inflammation and carcinogenesis. However, the effects of F. nucleatum on the cancerous transformation of IBD, along with its underlying mechanisms, remain poorly understood. Our findings revealed that F. nucleatum was enriched in 57.1% of CAC tissues, which was higher than that in ulcerative colitis (UC, 53.0%), Crohn’s disease (CD, 55.6%), and colorectal cancer (CRC, 53.7%) tissues. Furthermore, we demonstrated that F. nucleatum modulated nicotinamide (NAM) metabolism to promote the proliferation of CAC. Mechanistically, F. nucleatum upregulated NNMT to increase PARP1 expression, thereby activating MAPK signalling pathway. These findings provide insight into F. nucleatum-induced metabolic dysregulation and inflammatory cancer development and may propose novel intervention strategies for the prevention and treatment of microbial-associated inflammatory carcinogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06238-6.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a common gastrointestinal malignant tumour, which ranks in third place in terms of incidence but second in terms of mortality [1]. Patients with long-standing ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD) (except limited proctitis) have an approximately 2- to 3-fold increased risk of CRC compared with the general population. The incidence of colitis-associated cancer (CAC) is approximately 20% in UC patients and 8% in CD patients [2]. CAC, which has a poor prognosis, is a leading cause of mortality and is the cause of colectomy in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) patients [3]. Therefore, understanding the pathogenesis of CAC has important implications for the prevention and treatment of CAC.

Previous studies have shown that the gut microbiota plays a substantial role in CRC development and progression [4–6]. Accumulating evidence suggests that the gut microbiota also plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of IBD and has recently been linked to increased CRC risk in patients with IBD [7, 8]. In recent years, a large number of studies have reported a strong association between Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum) and intestinal inflammation and tumour development [9]. F.nucleatum promotes intestinal inflammation by inducing intestinal epithelial cell apoptosis, disrupting the intestinal barrier, and modulating inflammatory pathways [10–12]. F.nucleatum enhances its adhesion to tumour cells via extracellular vesicles or the adhesins RadD, Fap2, and FadA. Moreover, it modulates cellular metabolism and activates multiple signalling pathways, including the TLR4/MYD88/NF-κB, Hippo, Wnt/β-catenin, and MAPK pathways. These mechanisms collectively contribute to the remodelling of the immune microenvironment, thereby promoting tumour progression and chemoresistance [13–18]. A previous study has shown that the abundance of F. nucleatum increases significantly as patients approach a diagnosis of CAC, highlighting its association with the development and progression of colonic diseases, including colitis and colorectal cancer [19]. Nevertheless, the precise role and specific mechanism of F. nucleatum in the development of CAC remain poorly understood.

Nicotinamide (NAM), a product of the non-redox reactions, is a potent enzymatic inhibitor and plays a crucial role in the regulation of cellular energy metabolism. NAM alters nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) levels by inhibiting the activity of key enzymes involved in energy metabolism, such as PARP1 and SIRT1 [20]. PARP1 and SIRT1 can promote cell proliferation, inhibit apoptosis, and affect the progression of colorectal cancer and therapeutic resistance [21]. Nicotinamide N-methyl-transferase(NNMT) catalyses the N-methylation of NAM by consuming SAM, which is the main metabolic pathway for NAM in vivo [22]. Previous studies have shown that NNMT is associated with poor prognosis in colorectal cancer [23–25] and positively affects the viability, proliferation, migration, invasion and chemoresistance of tumour cells [26]. NNMT enhances SIRT1 and PARP1 activity by decreasing and consuming NAM [22], suggesting that NNMT may affect tumour progression by altering NAM metabolism in tumour cells. A negative correlation is observed between NAM and the abundance of F. nucleatum in colorectal cancer tissues [27]. However, the mechanism of NAM metabolism involved in F.nucleatum-associated CAC is currently unclear.

The mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signalling pathway is a kinase cascade system comprising proteins such as ERK, p38, and JNK, has been increasingly shown to play a critical role in the pathogenesis and progression of CAC in humans [28, 29]. NNMT mediates the regulation of energy balance in CRC cells through nicotinamide metabolism, activates the MAPK signalling pathway, and contributes to colorectal cancer cell survival, apoptosis, and cell cycle progression [30]. PARP1 is indispensable for the transcription of pro-survival kinases and for maintaining MAPK activity by suppressing the transcription of MAPK inhibitors [31, 32]. The inhibition of PARP1 transcriptional activity suppresses the activation of the MAPK signalling pathway [33]. Collectively, these findings suggest that PARP1 and MAPK may be involved in the pathogenesis and progression of CAC. However, the specific mechanism between the activation of MAPK and the development of F.nucleatum-associated CAC has not been examined.

In this study, we explored the role and underlying mechanisms of the gut microbiota in the occurrence and development of CAC. We found that the invasion of F. nucleatum and the reduction of NAM were associated with the progression of CAC. We demonstrated that F.nucleatum promoted the progression of CAC by modulating nicotinamide metabolism through NNMT and activating the subsequent PARP1/MAPK signalling pathway.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples

Paraffin-embedded colon tissue samples (from 21 patients with CAC, 41 patients with CRC, 66 patients with UC (59 patients with active UC, 7 patients with remitted UC), 36 patients with CD (32 patients with active CD, 4 patients with remitted CD), and 10 healthy volunteers) used for fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and immunohistochemical staining were collected from the pathology department of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University and Hubei Provincial People’s Hospital. Fresh colon tissue samples (11 UC, 6 CD and 15 CAC) were collected from patients who underwent colonoscopy or surgery at the same hospital for real-time PCR and high-throughput sequencing. All the collected samples were diagnosed as IBD, CAC, or CRC on the basis of clinical manifestations and histological examination. Patients with infectious diarrhoea, pregnancy, recent use of antibiotics (within 3 months) or prednisolone (within 2 weeks), a history of faecal microbiota transplantation, and other recent malignancies were excluded. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and institutional review board approval was obtained (No. 755).

Bacterial strains, cell Lines and treatments

F. nucleatum strain was isolated and confirmed as previously described [34]. F. nucleatum strain was cultured anaerobically at 37℃ in fastidious anaerobic broth. E. coli strain (Tiangen, China) used as the control bacteria was cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium at 37℃ in a shaker at 200–220 rpm/min. The human CRC cell lines HCT116 and SW480 (ATCC, USA) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium-high glucose (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin and streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator.

Cell lines were infected with F. nucleatum or E. coli at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1:100. SB 203580 inhibitor (Sigma, USA) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and cells received treatment with SB 203580 inhibitor at the recommended concentration of 15 μm 1 h before F. nucleatum infection.

Cell transfection

Lentiviruses harbouring plasmids containing short hairpin RNA (shRNA) against NNMT and PARP1 were purchased from GeneChem (Shanghai, China), and the sequences were as follows: shNNMT-5’-GCTCAAGAGCAGCTACTACAT-3’; shPARP1-5’-GCAGCTTCATAACCGAAGATT-3’. A vector containing non-silencing short hairpin RNA was used as the control. Lentiviral infection was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were screened with puromycin (2 µg/ml) for four weeks to establish knockdown and control cell lines.

Cell proliferation and colony formation assays

Cell proliferation was assessed by Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) (MedChemExpress, USA) assay. HCT116 cells (1 × 103 cells/well) or SW480 cells (2 × 103 cells/well) were grown in 96-well plates for 24 h before F. nucleatum infection. After incubation for 0, 6, 12, or 24 h under suitable conditions, 10 µl of CCK8 solution and 90 µl of medium were added to each well. Then, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to evaluate cell viability. For the colony formation assay, cells treated with F. nucleatum or PBS for 24 h were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 500 cells per well and cultured for 10 to 14 days. The cells were subsequently washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 5% crystal violet for 30 min at room temperature before the number of colonies formed was counted.

Flow cytometry analysis

For cell cycle detection, 1 × 106 cells were harvested and fixed with 75% ethanol at 4℃ overnight after being washed with PBS. Then, the cells were washed with PBS and stained with propidium iodide. A FACSCalibur instrument (BD Biosciences, USA) and CellQuest software were used to analyse the cell cycle.

Mice

Three to four-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were obtained from the Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Company (China) and maintained in a specific pathogen-free environment with a 12-h light/dark cycle. After one week of acclimatization, the mice were provided with drinking water containing antibiotics of 200 mg/kg ampicillin, 200 mg/kg neomycin sulfate, 200 mg/kg metronidazole, and 500 mg/L vancomycin for 7 days to homogenize the gut microbiome and facilitate the colonization of F. nucleatum. Subsequently, the mice were randomly assigned to different treatment groups using a random number table and were treated with PBS-resuspended F. nucleatum (109 CFU/ml) or PBS alone by gavage daily throughout the entire period of the animal model. To establish an AOM/DSS model as a mimic of CAC, mice were administered with a single intraperitoneal injection of 10 mg/kg AOM (Sigma, USA) combined with three cycles of 2.5% DSS (MP Biochemicals, USA) in the drinking water for one week followed by regular drinking water for two weeks. The mice were subsequently euthanized by cervical dislocation, after which the number of colorectal tumours was recorded. The tumour sizes (diameters) were quantified as < 2 mm, 2–4 mm, or > 4 mm.

Female BALB/c nude mice aged four–five weeks were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Company (China). HCT116 cells (5 × 106) with stable knockdown of NNMT or PARP1 or mock cells were respectively injected into the right armpits of mice. When the tumour volume was approximately 100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into different groups that were untreated or treated with F. nucleatum, SB 203580, or NAM by using a random number table. F. nucleatum was given by multipoint intratumoral injection twice per week for 2 weeks. SB 203580 was administered by intraperitoneal injection twice per week for 2 weeks, and the concentration of SB 203580 was 30 mg/kg. The tumours were measured every 3 days with callipers and calculated using the following formula: volume = [length×width2]/2. After 2 weeks, the tumours were removed surgically, weighed, fixed, and sectioned for subsequent haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemical staining. During the experiment, the observers and subsequent experimenters were blinded to the group assignments of the animals. All animal protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (No. 755).

Immunohistochemical staining (IHC)

The paraffin-embedded tissue sections were stained using an UltraSensitiveTM SP (mouse/rabbit) IHC Kit (Maxim, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Three random 200× magnification fields per sample were evaluated by three pathologists who were blinded to the experimental protocols. The immunoreactive score is calculated as the product of the proportion of positive cells (scored 0–4) and the staining intensity (scored 0–3), yielding a range of 0 to 12. Sections were categorised into low and high expression according to the median immunoreactive score.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

The paraffin-embedded colon tissue was sectioned into 5 micrometer slices. We utilised the DNA Bacteria Universal FISH Kit (Focobio, China) for the detection of F. nucleatum colonisation in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The specific probe sequence used for F. nucleatum (FUS664; FITC-labeled) was 5’-CTT GTA GTT CCG C(C/T) TAC CTC-3’. The sequence of the “universal bacterial” probe (EUB338; Cy3 labeled) was 5’-GCT GCC TCC CGT AGG AGT-3’. Each sample was evaluated by three researchers blinded to the experimental protocol. Five random fields at 200× magnification were assessed per sample, and the mean bacterial count per field was calculated to assess the abundance of F. nucleatum. Samples were categorised as low abundance (< 20 probes), or high abundance (> 20 probes) based on the average number of probes per field.

DNA extraction and high-throughput sequencing

Genomic DNA from frozen clinical human samples was extracted using a TIANamp Bacteria DNA Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, PR China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. gDNA from each sample was subjected to qPCR to determine the amounts of F. nucleatum. The Ct values for F. nucleatum were normalized to the amount of human biopsy gDNA in each reaction by using PGT as a reference gene. Besides, DNA was sent to Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for sequencing. DNA concentration and integrity were measured by a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. PCR amplification of the V3-V4 variable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was carried out in 25 µL reactions using the universal primers 343 F (5’-TACGGRAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 798R (5’-AGGGTATCTAATCCT-3’). The PCR products were purified with Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter Co., USA) and quantified using a Qubit dsDNA assay kit. The concentrations were then adjusted for sequencing. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 with two paired-end read cycles of 250 bases each (Illumina Inc., CA; OE Biotech Company; China). Paired-end reads were preprocessed using Trimmomatic software to detect and cut off ambiguous bases(N). The software program also cut off low-quality sequences with average quality scores below 20 using a sliding window trimming approach. After trimming, paired-end reads were assembled using FLASH software. The parameters of assembly were 10 bp of minimum overlap, 200 bp of maximum overlap and a 20% of maximum mismatch rate. Sequences were further denoised as follows: reads with ambiguous, homologous sequences or sequences smaller than below 200 bp were abandoned. Reads with 75% of bases above Q20 were retained using QIIME software (version 1.8.0). Then, reads with chimaeras were detected and removed using VSEARCH. Clean reads were subjected to primer sequence removal and clustering to generate operational taxonomic units (OTUs) using VSEARCH software with a 97% similarity cut-off. The representative read of each OTU was selected using the QIIME package. All representative reads were annotated and blasted against the Silva database (Version 132) using the RDP classifier (70% confidence threshold). The DNA sequence data have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database (accession number: PRJNA541040 and PRJNA1440639).

RNA Extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from fresh tissues and cells by TRIpure Total RNA Extraction Reagent (ELK Biotechnology, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Japan) was used to synthesize first-strand cDNA. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed in triplicate with EnTurbo™ SYBR Green PCR SuperMix Kit (ELK Biotechnology, Eq. 001) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex System PCR instrument from Life Technologies. The 2−ΔΔCt method was used to compare the Ct values obtained from different samples. The internal reference transcript was GAPDH. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blotting (WB)

Proteins were extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer supplemented with 1% phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). Total protein was separated via sodium dodecyl sulfate‒polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE, 10% gel) and then transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (PVDF, Millipore, USA). The membranes were then blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The membranes were subsequently incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, after which the bands were visualized using an ECL kit (Beyotime, China). Primary antibodies against the following targets were used: NNMT (1:2000, Cat No. 15123-1-AP, Proteintech, China), PARP1 (1:2000, Cat No. 13371-1-AP, Proteintech, China), P38 (1:1000, Cat No. 8690, Cell Signaling Technology (CST), USA), phosphorylated-P38 (1:1000, Cat No. 4511, CST, USA), ERK (1:1000, Cat No. 4695, CST, USA), phosphorylated-ERK (1:2000, Cat No. 4370, CST, USA), JNK (1:1000, Cat No. 24164-1-AP, Proteintech, China), phosphorylated-JNK (1:1000, Cat No. 80024-1-RR, Proteintech, China), Cyclin D1 (1:200, Cat No. 26939-1-AP, Proteintech, China), c-Myc (1:1000, Cat No. 10828-1-AP, Proteintech, China) and GAPDH (1:10000, Cat No. MB001, Bioworld, USA).

Statistical analyses

Comparisons of quantitative data between different groups were conducted using unpaired or paired Student’s t tests and Mann–Whitney U tests. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way ANOVA was used. Linear regression analysis was used to analyse the relationship between F. nucleatum abundance and mRNA expression levels. Associations between F. nucleatum abundance and patient characteristics were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All P values were two-tailed, and differences for which the P value of less than 0.05 were considered significant (*P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, and *** P < 0.001). All the statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad software, Inc., USA) and SPSS Statistics 26.0 software (IBM, Inc., USA).

Results

F. nucleatum is associated with the progression of colitis-associated cancer

We used 16S rDNA high-throughput sequencing to examine the differences in the intestinal microbiota among 17 intestinal tissue biopsies from active IBD patients (UC = 11 and CD = 6) and 15 biopsies from CAC patients. Microbial structure analysis showed that Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Prevotella, Prevotellaceae, and Fusobacteria were enriched in the CAC tissue compared with the IBD tissue (P < 0.05; Fig. 1A, B). Fusobacteria, Peptostreptococcaceae, Tissierellaceae, and Coriobacteriales were abundant in CAC tissues, whereas Actinobacteria, Bacillales, Rhizobiales, and Sphingomonadaceae were abundant in UC tissues (P < 0.05; Fig. 1C). Compared with those in the CD tissues, the abundances of Tissierellaceae, Coriobacteriales, Erysipelotrichales, and Xanthomonadaceae in the CAC tissues were significantly higher (P < 0.05; Fig. 1D). Furthermore, cluster analysis revealed a significant increase in the relative abundance of Fusobacterium in the CAC samples (P < 0.05; Fig. 1E). Given the established role of Fusobacterium, particularly F. nucleatum, in inflammatory processes and colorectal carcinogenesis, we further investigated F. nucleatum. The qPCR results revealed that the abundance of F. nucleatum in UC, CD, CRC and CAC tissues was significantly increased compared to normal tissues (P < 0.001), whereas the abundance of F. nucleatum in CAC tissues was significantly increased compared to UC tissues (P = 0.0013) and CD tissues (P = 0.0146; Fig. 1F). Furthermore, the abundance of F. nucleatum in the CAC tissues was greater compared with that in the CRC group (P = 0.022; Fig. 1F). We further examined the F. nucleatum abundance in 66 UC tissues (59 active UC, 7 remitted UC), 36 CD tissues (32 active CD, 4 remitted CD), 21 CAC tissues, 41 CRC tissues, and 10 normal tissues from patients using FISH. Consistent with the real-time PCR results, F. nucleatum was detected in a higher percentage of CAC tissues (57.1%) than UC tissues (53.0%, AUC = 55.9%, RUC = 28.6%), CD tissues (55.6%, ACD = 59.4%, RCD = 25.0%), and CRC tissues (53.7%, P < 0.05; Fig. 1G; Table 1). F. nucleatum is related to nicotinamide metabolism in CAC

On the basis of the 16S rDNA results, we performed functional prediction using PICRUSt analysis, which revealed significant alterations in microbial-associated nicotinamide metabolism in CAC tissues compared with IBD tissues (P < 0.05; Fig. 2A). High performance liquid chromatography demonstrated a reduction in the NAM content in HCT116 cells after F. nucleatum intervention compared with that after PBS treatment (P = 0.0224; Fig. 2B). We further evaluated the expression of key enzymes involved in nicotinamide metabolism using RT-PCR. The results indicated the gene expression of NNMT (P < 0.001; Fig. 2C), SIRT1 (P = 0.0348; Fig. 2D), and PARP1 (P = 0.0044; Fig. 2E) was significantly upregulated in HCT116 cells infected with F. nucleatum compared with that in cells treated with PBS. Given the more marked upregulation of NNMT and PARP1 and the established evidence that NNMT directly modulates PARP1 expression, we further investigated the role of NNMT in modulating F. nucleatum-related nicotinamide metabolism. FISH and IHC were used to visualize the abundance of F. nucleatum and NNMT expression in CAC tissues. A high abundance of F. nucleatum in tissues was often accompanied by high levels of NNMT expression (P = 0.0421; Fig. 2F, G). F. nucleatum promotes colon tumour proliferation through the regulation of NAM metabolism in vivo

We established an AOM/DSS-induced inflammatory carcinogenesis model in C57BL/6J mice by administering F. nucleatum and/or NAM. Compared with the control group, the AOM + DSS group presented greater body weight loss and an increased number of tumours (P < 0.05; Fig. 3A-C). Compared with mice in the AOM + DSS group, mice in the F. nucleatum + AOM+DSS group exhibited greater weight loss (P < 0.05; Fig. 3A), a significantly increased number of colon tumours (P = 0.0004; Fig. 3B and C) and a higher proportion of colon tumours larger than 4 mm (P < 0.05; Fig. 3D). Furthermore, F. nucleatum infection led to marked upregulation of NNMT, c-Myc, and Cyclin D1 expression in tumour tissues (Fig. 3E), along with a significant increase in Ki67 expression (Fig. 3F). On the basis of these findings, NAM treatment alleviated weight loss (P < 0.05; Fig. 3A), significantly reduced both the number of colon tumours (P = 0.0118; Fig. 3B and C) and the proportion of colon tumours larger than 4 mm (20.00% vs. 36.35%; Fig. 3D), and downregulated the expression of NNMT, c-Myc, Cyclin D1, and Ki67 in tumour tissues from the F. nucleatum + AOM+DSS group(P < 0.05; Fig. 3E and F). These results demonstrate that F. nucleatum markedly enhances the proliferation of CAC tumours, an effect that can be counteracted by NAM. To further confirm this conclusion, we established a mouse xenograft tumour model using HCT116 cells (Fig. 3G). The F. nucleatum group exhibited significantly increased tumour weight (P = 0.0019; Fig. 3H), tumour volume (P < 0.05; Fig. 3I, I), and Ki67 expression (Fig. 3J) compared to the PBS group. NAM treatment attenuated the F. nucleatum-induced increase in tumour weight (P < 0.0001; Fig. 3H) and tumour volume (P < 0.05; Fig. 3I) and reduced Ki67 expression (Fig. 3J). Discussion

Over recent decades, the prevalence of IBD has been increasing globally, particularly in newly industrialized regions [35]. Inflammation is a well-established pathway to carcinogenesis [36]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the existence of a sustained inflammatory response in IBD is an independent risk factor for the development of colitis-associated colorectal cancer [28]. The concept of cumulative inflammatory burden highlights the importance of considering histologic inflammation over time as an important risk factor for CAC [3]. Patients with IBD are at an elevated risk of developing CAC, and CAC arising in IBD is often considered the prototype of inflammation-induced carcinogenesis. CAC is a leading cause of both mortality and colectomy in patients with IBD [37]. And compared with sporadic colorectal cancer, CAC is associated with more severe pathological types, poorer prognosis, and higher mortality rates [38]. Therefore, investigating the pathogenesis of CAC is critical for improving its diagnosis and clinical management.

The gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in maintaining intestinal physiological homeostasis and in the pathophysiology of diseases, including IBD and CRC. Previous studies have suggested a potential role for the microbiome in the development of CAC. High-fat diet drives the development of colitis-associated cancer by inducing gut microbial dysbiosis, suppressing butyrate metabolism, and promoting the M1 polarization of proinflammatory macrophages [39]. Escherichia coli improves intestinal inflammation and restricts tumour formation by inducing IL-10 production by macrophages [40]. However, the understanding of the structure and function of the microbiota in CAC, as well as how it differs from the microbiota of CRC patients without IBD, remains limited. Our previous study has demonstrated that F. nucleatum is positively associated with increased inflammatory activity in IBD [34]. One study reported that F. nucleatum was detected in 6.3% of low-grade dysplasia, 33.3% of high-grade dysplasia, and 39.5% of CAC tissue samples [19]. Another study reported that the sporadic cancer group exhibited higher levels of Fusobacterium than the CAC group [41]. In the present study, we utilized RT-PCR and FISH to validate the abundance of F. nucleatum in tissue samples and found that compared with that in normal tissues, the abundance of F. nucleatum markedly increased in UC, CD, CRC (sporadic CRC), and CAC tissues. And the F. nucleatum abundance was increased in CAC group compared with the CRC group. In addition, F. nucleatum was detected in a higher percentage of CAC tissues (57.1%) than in UC tissues (53.0%), CD tissues (55.6%), and CRC tissues (53.7%, P < 0.05). Thus, we revealed that a high abundance of F. nucleatum is present in CAC tissues, whose abundance is even higher than that in IBD and CRC tissues. These contrasting results can potentially be due to the combination of a larger collection of CAC specimens and the diversity in the pathological stages of the lesions obtained. Evidence suggests that the gut microbiota composition can change across the stages of CRC [42]. Nevertheless, a limitation of our study is the relatively small sample size of the CAC group.

Our high-throughput sequencing results demonstrated that the relative abundance of Fusobacterium was increased in CAC tissues compared to IBD tissues. Numerous studies have demonstrated that F. nucleatum promotes the progression of both colitis and CRC and is closely associated with treatment resistance [10, 16–18, 43]. However, the underlying mechanisms between F. nucleatum and CAC remain poorly understood. Numerous studies have demonstrated that F. nucleatum promotes the proliferation of colorectal cancer cells [44, 45]. Consistent with previous researches, our findings indicated that F. nucleatum enhanced the proliferation and clonogenic ability of colorectal cancer cells, increased the proportion of cells in the proliferative phase, and upregulated the expression of c-Myc and Cyclin D1. Furthermore, we established an AOM/DSS mouse model and found that F. nucleatum exacerbated weight loss, increased the number and size of colonic tumours, and upregulated the expression of c-Myc, Cyclin D1, and Ki67 in tumour tissues. And in a mouse xenograft tumour model, the F. nucleatum group exhibited significantly increased tumour weight, tumour volume, and Ki67 expression compared with the control group. This is in line with previous studies reporting that F. nucleatum infection exacerbates weight loss, accelerates tumour growth, increases Ki67 expression, and shortens colon length in mice [45–47]. Thus, we revealed that F. nucleatum may promote the progression of CAC.

NAM is the main precursor of NAD⁺, a coenzyme essential for DNA repair, glycolysis, and oxidative phosphorylation. NAD⁺ is degraded by NAD⁺-consuming enzymes such as sirtuins and PARPs, cleaving it into ADP ribose and NAM, which can be recycled to reconstitute NAD⁺ [48]. NAM is an inhibitor of both SIRT1 and PARP1. NAM inhibits tumour cell proliferation, cell cycle progression, and DNA replication, while enhancing tumour cell apoptosis and suppressing DNA damage repair through the inhibition of SIRT1 and PARP1 [49]. NAM is methylated by NNMT, which represents the primary metabolic pathway for NAM in vivo [50]. NNMT is overexpressed in colorectal cancer and is associated with poor prognosis [24, 25]. Besides, our previous research has revealed that F. nucleatum infection increased the levels of NAM in intestinal epithelial cells [43]. However, whether and how nicotinamide metabolism influences the F. nucleatum-mediated progression of CAC remain unclear. Our PICRUSt analysis demonstrated that microbial-related nicotinamide metabolism is linked to the progression of CAC. We found that F. nucleatum reduced the level of NAM and stimulated the expression of NNMT and PARP1 in CRC cells. NAM supplementation suppressed the proliferation of CAC induced by F. nucleatum in vivo. NNMT Knockdown decreased F. nucleatum-induced proliferation and the expression of c-Myc, Cyclin D1, and PARP1 in CRC cells. Furthermore, PARP1 knockdown suppressed F. nucleatum-mediated tumour proliferation both in vitro and in vivo. Therefore, we demonstrated that F. nucleatum promotes colonic tumour proliferation by modulating nicotinamide metabolism via NNMT to regulate PARP1.

Furthermore, the mechanisms by which F. nucleatum influences colorectal tumour proliferation via nicotinamide metabolism were investigated. Extensive research has demonstrated that MAPK activation contributes to the pathogenesis of IBD [51–53]. Moreover, MAPK signalling affects the progression of colorectal cancer by regulating processes such as apoptosis, proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [54]. Consistent with the findings of previous studies, our transcriptomic study revealed that differentially expressed genes subsequent to F. nucleatum infection of colorectal cancer cells were significantly enriched in the MAPK signalling pathway. We found that F. nucleatum induced the expression of phospho-p38 and phospho-ERK in colorectal cancer cells. Pharmacological inhibition of the MAPK signalling pathway attenuated F. nucleatum-induced cell proliferation both in vitro and in vivo. Moreover, SB significantly attenuated the F. nucleatum-mediated upregulation of phospho-P38 and phospho-ERK. In addition, following NNMT knockdown, the shNNMT + SB+F. nucleatum group exhibited reduced expression of NNMT, PARP1, phospho-p38, and phospho-ERK, along with a significant decrease in the size and volume of subcutaneous xenografts and reduced Ki67 expression in tumour tissues, compared with the NC + SB+F. nucleatum group. Collectively, these data indicated that F. nucleatum promoted colorectal cancer proliferation by upregulating NNMT expression and subsequently increasing PARP1 expression, thereby activating the MAPK signalling pathway.

Our investigation revealed that the abundance of F. nucleatum was significantly higher in both CAC and CRC tissues than in IBD tissues. Moreover, the abundance of F. nucleatum was higher in CAC tissues than in CRC tissues. Measuring the abundance of F. nucleatum could therefore serve as a potential biomarker for predicting the risk of CAC development. Furthermore, our findings suggest that targeting F. nucleatum, inhibiting the MAPK signalling pathway or supplementing with NAM may represent effective strategies to block F. nucleatum-induced CAC progression. Consequently, the detection of F. nucleatum abundance and nicotinamide levels, combined with differentiated management strategies for patients with varying levels of F. nucleatum, may play a critical role in the prevention and management of CAC. However, this study lacks the safety and long-term consequences of systemic NAM manipulation or NNMT/PARP1 inhibition. Future studies will need to utilize more physiologically relevant models, combined with systems pharmacology and metabolomics methods, to comprehensively evaluate long-term safety and efficacy.

In conclusion, our current findings elucidate the molecular mechanism through which F. nucleatum modulates nicotinamide metabolism to influence the progression of CAC. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for the clinical understanding and treatment of CAC, and provide a novel direction and target for the prevention and treatment of microbiota-induced CAC.
