Alexander disease mutations differentially sensitize glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) to posttranslational modifications and network disruption by oxidants
Authors: Nuria Goya-Iglesias, Per Hägglund, Paula Martínez-Cenalmor, Tina Nybo, Lasse G Lorentzen, María A Pajares, Michael J Davies, Dolores Pérez-Sala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe type III intermediate filament protein glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) plays a key role in astrocyte and brain homeostasis. Mutations in GFAP can result in Alexander's disease (AxD), a severe neurodegenerative disease. Studies on AxD models indicate that oxidative stress may be an important pathogenic factor. Cellular expression of certain GFAP AxD mutants can provoke oxidative stress and contribute to a pathogenic cycle, as GFAP itself is an important oxidant target. To understand the molecular mechanisms involved, we have carried out a detailed LC-MS/MS characterization of posttranslational modifications formed on recombinant GFAP wild-type and AxD-relevant mutants (R79C, R239C and E373K) in response to in vitro treatment with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), hypochlorous acid (HOCl) and peroxynitrous acid (ONOOH). These data indicate that cysteine residues are key targets. AxD mutants show increased susceptibility to modification, with sulfinic and sulfonic acids detected at both the wild-type cysteine (C294), and cysteine residues introduced by disease-related mutations. Mutation-selective variations in nitrations (from ONOOH) and chlorinations (from HOCl) were also detected. Formation of disulfide bonds, some of which involved mutation-introduced cysteine residues, results in GFAP oligomerization in vitro and in cells. Astrocytoma cells transfected with GFP-GFAP wild-type undergo morphologically-distinct remodeling in response to oxidants. In contrast, cells expressing the R239C mutation showed persistent or increased aggregates, particularly after H2O2 treatment. Together, these data show that AxD-associated GFAP mutations favor reversible and irreversible oxidative protein modifications, potentially contributing to impaired protein filament assembly and more severe responses to oxidative stress.Keywords: Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), Alexander disease (AxD), Vimentin, Protein oxidation, Proteomics, Posttranslational modifications (PTMs), Hydrogen peroxide, Cysteine oxidation

Introduction

Alexander disease (AxD) is a rare leukodystrophy caused by single point mutations in the gene encoding glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), for which no cure is available. Most disease-causing variants are de novo missense mutations, although some inherited cases have been also reported; mutations are heterozygous and show autosomal dominant inheritance [1,2]. Although previously considered an extremely rare disease (frequency: 1 in 2-3 million), a recent analysis of the UK biobank dataset calculated a much higher prevalence of 68 per million individuals [3]. The onset of the disease is variable with the most severe, and fatal, forms affecting infants (up to 4 years-of-age), while less severe forms are diagnosed later in life, and may manifest after injury to the brain, including head trauma, excessive alcohol consumption or fever. All these factors increase oxidant production, suggesting a role for oxidative stress in late-onset AxD (reviewed in Ref. [4]).

AxD begins in astrocytes, the major site of GFAP gene expression. Given the key role of astrocytes in brain homeostasis, astrocyte dysfunction due to GFAP mutations subsequently impacts other brain cell types, leading to demyelination and neurodegeneration [5]. GFAP is a member of the intermediate filament type III (type III IFs) protein class that also includes vimentin. These proteins can form heteropolymers in astrocytes constituting a filament network that expands from the perinuclear area to the cell membrane [6,7]. The structure of type III IFs includes a core helical rod domain and disordered N- (head) and C-terminal (tail) domains (Fig. 1A) [8]. Due to the high sequence similarity between GFAP and vimentin (Fig. 1B), GFAP filament assembly is expected to mimic that described for vimentin. This involves parallel association of monomers into dimers, followed by antiparallel assembly of the dimers into tetramers and the lateral association of tetramers into unit-length filaments (ULFs), which then connect longitudinally [9]. Nevertheless, the exact nature of the heteropolymers and the precise distribution of GFAP and vimentin within them are poorly understood. AxD-related GFAP mutations result in aberrant organization of the GFAP network and accumulation of protein aggregates (Rosenthal fibers), which may also contain vimentin, ubiquitin or αB-crystallin [10]. Importantly, an increase in GFAP wild-type (wt) levels, arising from stress or damage, also results in aberrant GFAP organization and Rosenthal fiber formation [11,12]. The complexity of this picture is further increased by the occurrence of 12 GFAP isoforms from alternative splicing of the GFAP gene [13], most of which differ from the canonical form (α-GFAP, hereinafter GFAP) in the N- or C-terminal domains. Since most AxD mutations map to the rod domain, they are also likely to affect several splicing isoforms. Importantly, the age of onset and severity of AxD clearly correlate with the mutation site and/or the nature of the residue introduced. Infantile AxD frequently associates with arginine (R) to cysteine (C) mutations [2], with ∼30% of the reported AxD mutations occurring at R79, R88, R239 or R416 [14]. While disease onset occurs consistently at early ages for the R79C and R239C variants, others such as R416W display a greater age variability [15].

Both GFAP and vimentin are targets of multiple posttranslational modifications (PTMs), including phosphorylation, ubiquitination, acetylation, and oxidation, which modulate their assembly, stability and interactions [16,17]. Both proteins contain an important and conserved single cysteine residue (C294 in GFAP), which serves as a key site for PTMs that markedly affect filament and network organization and contribute to the redox regulation of the intermediate filament system [7,[17], [18], [19]]. Reversible modification of C294 may constitute a protective mechanism against sustained oxidative stress and irreversible protein damage. Consistent with this hypothesis, oxidation and phosphorylation of GFAP have been detected in aggregates in various neurodegenerative diseases (e.g., Alzheimer's disease [20]).

The thiol group of cysteine is a powerful nucleophile and prone to modification. As evidence for oxidative stress has been detected in tissue samples from AxD patients and in animal models [12,21,22], the presence of additional cysteine residues in some of the highly pathogenic GFAP variants (e.g. R79C, R239C) may result in these species having a greater susceptibility to modification and enhanced dysregulation of protein networks [23,24]. Consistent with this, GFAP R239C expressed in astrocytoma cells displays increased susceptibility to (lip)oxidation and forms an abnormal network containing protein aggregates, which are not detected for GFAP wt, and/or more intense bundling and retraction from the cell periphery [23]. Interestingly, expression of GFAP R239C in cells, in the absence of exogenous oxidant exposure, elicits oxidative stress as evidenced by free thiol depletion and increased mitochondrial superoxide production [23]. This suggests a pathogenic cycle in which oxidants can provoke GFAP misfolding and aggregation, thus amplifying mitochondrial damage and astrocyte dysfunction. Additionally, oxidation of several purified AxD GFAP mutants by hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) treatment in vitro has also been reported [24].

Given the importance of oxidative PTMs in the disruption of intermediate filament networks, the current study has analyzed the impact of several physiological oxidants on the presence and pattern of PTMs formed on GFAP wt and three AxD GFAP mutants (Fig. 1), with a particular emphasis on C294 modifications, due to its key role in network organization [7]. The data obtained show that in vitro oxidant exposure elicits multiple PTMs along the GFAP sequence, including at C294. These are particularly extensive in the AxD mutants, and may be involved in their aberrant reorganization, especially under conditions of oxidative stress.

Materials and methods

Materials

IPTG was from Calbiochem (La Jolla, CA, USA). DNase I, aprotinin and Pefablock were from Roche (Mannheim, Germany). HiTrap DEAE FF (5 ml) and HiTrap CM FF (5 ml) cartridges were acquired from Cytiva (Uppsala, Sweden). SimplyBlue SafeStain was a product from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA). Urea was from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Restriction enzymes (XbaI and HindIII), DNA ligase and phosphatase TSAP were acquired from Promega (Madison, WI). Antibiotics (ampicillin and kanamycin), protease inhibitors, leupeptin and trypsin inhibitor were from Sigma (St. Louis, MO). Other chemicals were from Sigma-Merck, unless stated otherwise. Purified recombinant GFAP wt and the C294S mutant were purchased from Abvance Biotech S.L. (Madrid, Spain).

Synthesis of peroxynitrous acid (ONOOH)

ONOOH was synthesized in a two-phase system using isoamyl nitrite and H2O2, with unreacted H2O2 removed using manganese dioxide [25]. Stock solutions of ONOOH were stored at −80oC. Before use, the concentration of ONOOH (diluted in 0.1 M NaOH) was determined by UV–vis spectrophotometry (UV-2600, Shimadzu, Japan) at 302 nm (ε302 1670 M−1 cm−1). The concentration of HOCl was determined by UV absorbance at 292 nm at pH 11 using ε292 of 350 M−1 cm−1 [26].

Site-directed mutagenesis for generation of GFAP mutants

The plasmid containing the codon optimized sequence of human GFAP (NM_002055.5), cloned into the XbaI/HindIII sites of pET28a (pET28a-GFAPmod) for its bacterial expression was obtained from Abvance Biotech S.L. This open reading frame was subcloned in the same sites of pBS-Blue-T2 [27] to generate pBS-GFAPmod. Both pET28a-GFAPmod and pBS-GFAPmod were used as templates for site-directed mutagenesis with the NZYTech mutagenesis kit (NZYTech) and the corresponding oligonucleotides with sequences are indicated in Table S1.

The PCR program included: i) denaturation for 2 min at 95oC; ii) 16 cycles of 1 min denaturation at 95oC, 1 min annealing at 60oC and 15 min elongation at 68oC; and iii) a final elongation step of 15 min at 68oC. Mutagenesis reactions were digested with DpnI (NZYTEch) for 2 h at 37oC and later used to transform NZY ultracompetent cells that were seeded into LB kanamycin (LBK) plates and incubated at 37oC overnight. Three to four of the colonies grown were used to inoculate LBK medium for the isolation of the corresponding plasmids using the NZYTech MAXIprep kit. The presence of the desired mutation was verified by automatic sequencing at Secugen SL (Madrid, Spain). Only the R239C mutant was directly obtained using pET28a-GFAPmod (Fig. S1), whereas production of R79C and E373K mutants required two steps. In the first step, mutagenesis was carried out on pBS-GFAPmod using the procedure described above, but with selection in LB medium with ampicillin (LBA). In the second step, these two mutants were subcloned into the XbaI/HindIII sites of pET28a. The absence of additional changes in the final mutant plasmids was verified by sequencing of the whole GFAPmod ORF at Secugen S.L.

Mammalian expression vectors coding GFP-GFAP wt and the R239C mutant have been described previously [18]. The GFP-GFAP mutants R79C and E373K were obtained by site directed mutagenesis as specified above using the NZYTech kit and the oligonucleotides depicted in Table S1, but with two exceptions; i) the number of PCR cycles was reduced to 18; and ii) the DpnI digestion of the PCR product was carried out for 1 h. Successful mutagenesis was confirmed by DNA sequencing (Secugen S.L.) following plasmid amplification in Escherichia coli strain XL-10 Gold (Stratagene), grown in the presence of the appropriate selection antibiotic. Plasmids were isolated using the High Pure Plasmid Isolation Kit (Roche), and those intended for transfection were further purified with the EndoFree Plasmid Maxi Kit (Biotools). DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer.

Purification of recombinant GFAP mutants

The pET28a-GFAPmod plasmids encoding mutant GFAPs were transformed in E. coli BL21(DE3) competent cells that were seeded in LBK plates and incubated overnight at 37oC. One colony was used in each case to inoculate 20 mL of Terrific Broth medium containing 50 μg mL−1 kanamycin (TBK) and grown overnight at 37oC. This inoculum was added to 500 mL of TBK medium and the culture grown until A600 ∼0.3, when expression was induced with 1.5 mM IPTG for 18 h at 37oC. The bacterial pellet was obtained by centrifugation at 4200 g for 10 min, washed with deionized water and centrifuged again, before freezing with dry ice for storage at −80oC (Fig. S1).

A single bacterial pellet (∼0.9 g) was resuspended in 5 mL of 20 mM potassium phosphate pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM PMSF by vortexing. Cell lysis was carried out by sonication on ice (30 cycles of 20 s on/40 s off; 55-65% potency of microtip) in a Sonifier 450 (Branson, Inc.). The lysate was incubated with DNase I (10 U mL−1) for 30 min at 37oC and centrifuged at 10000 g for 15 min at 4oC. The pellet was washed with 1.5 M NaCl and centrifuged at 10000 g for 20 min at 4oC and washed again with deionized water. The washed pellet was resuspended in 5.5 ml of 5 mM Tris/HCl pH 7.6, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1% (v/v) β-mercaptoethanol, 0.4 mM PMSF containing 9.5 M urea and centrifuged at 114000 g for 1 h at 25oC. The supernatant was collected and the volume measured.

Purification of GFAPs was carried out on an Äkta Prime system at a flow rate of 1 mL min−1. For the first step, the cleared lysate was diluted 2.5 fold with 5 mM Tris/HCl pH 7.6, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 0.4 mM PMSF containing 8 M urea (buffer A), before injection into a HiTrap DEAE FF (5 mL) cartridge equilibrated in buffer A. Extensive washing with buffer A (10 vol or until A280∼0) was carried out before elution with a 6 vol (30 mL) linear gradient from 0 to 0.36 M NaCl in buffer A. Fractions (1 mL) were collected during elution and 20 μl samples prepared for analysis on 12.5% SDS-PAGE gels that were stained with SimplyBlue SafeStain. The fractions containing GFAP mutant proteins were collected and kept at room temperature for the next purification step on a HiTrap CM FF cartridge (5 mL) also equilibrated in buffer A. For the second step, the DEAE peak was diluted (1:5 v/v) with buffer A for its injection into the CM cartridge. After extensive washing (10 vol or until A280∼0), elution was carried out with a 6 vol (30 mL) gradient from 0 to 0.3 M KCl in buffer A. Fractions (1 mL) were collected during the elution and 20 μL samples were prepared for analysis on 12.5% SDS-PAGE gels for detection of GFAP. The peak was collected and its concentration estimated from A280 measurements on a Nanodrop spectrometer using ε280 20450 M−1 cm−1. Aliquots (1 mL) were kept at −80oC until use.

Refolding of recombinant GFAP wt and mutants

Purified recombinant human GFAP wt and mutants stored in buffer A containing ∼0.2 M KCl as described above, were filtered using 10 kDa pore size Amicon Ultra filter units (Millipore) to remove EDTA, as described previously [28], and dialyzed in a step-wise manner against 5 mM Pipes (pH 7.0) containing 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT; Invitrogen) and decreasing concentrations of urea (6, 4, 2 and 0 M). Finally, two dialysis steps were performed against 5 mM Pipes (pH 7.0) containing 0.25 mM DTT. The dialyzed proteins were ultracentrifuged at 120000 g for 15 min at 4oC and the protein concentration of the supernatant was estimated spectrophotometrically using ε280 20450 M−1 cm−1. Aliquots of the protein were kept at −80oC until use.

Oxidation of recombinant GFAP wt and mutants for LC-MS/MS analysis

DTT removal prior to treatment with oxidants was achieved by loading the protein (125 μg) onto a 3 kDa MWCO filter (Sartorius), and 50 mM phosphate buffer pH 7.4 was added up to 500 μL. Sample was centrifuged at 12000 g for 15 min, then 500 μL phosphate buffer was added and the sample was centrifuged as above. The spin filter was inverted into fresh 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes and the retentate was collected by spinning at 3000 g for 2 min, and the volume was adjusted to 250 μL with phosphate buffer. Eighteen microliters (corresponding to 9 μg protein) from this solution was mixed with 2 μL of either ONOOH, HOCl, H2O2 or H2O (control) in 0.5 mL Eppendorf tubes to achieve final concentrations of 0.5 mM, 50 μM and 1 mM, respectively. Samples were incubated at 37oC with orbital shaking (600 rpm) on a thermomixer for 15 min (ONOOH, HOCl) or 30 min (H2O2, control). Single concentrations and time points were employed in these experiments due to the time-consuming nature and complexity of these analyses.

Sample clean up and purification for LC-MS/MS analysis

Magnetic beads were added to the sample at a 2:1 (w/w) bead:protein ratio and mixed, followed by addition of acetonitrile to a final concentration of 70%. The samples were then incubated at room temperature for 20 min at 1000 rpm on a thermomixer and subsequently mounted in a magnetic rack and left for 1 min. Supernatants were removed and the pellet was washed in three cycles with 800 μL 10% acetonitrile, 70% ethanol, and 100% acetonitrile, respectively. Subsequently the pellet was dried in air for ∼1 min and resuspended in 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.8 to achieve a protein concentration of 1 mg mL−1, then 2-chloracetamide was added (to alkylate free cysteine residues) to a final concentration of 40 mM, and samples incubated at 37oC for 30 min. Lys-C was added at a 1:200 (w/w) enzyme:protein ratio and samples were incubated for 1 h at 37oC. Then, trypsin was added at a 1:100 (w/w) enzyme:protein ratio and the samples then incubated for 16 h at 37oC. Afterwards the tubes were mounted in a magnetic rack and left for 1 min before supernatants were aspirated and acidified with 0.5% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). For stage tip purification, 200 μL pipette tips were packed with discs of C18 material (AttractSPE Disks Bio C18, Affinisep). The acidified samples were then loaded and washed with 0.1% TFA in water and subsequently eluted with 80% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid in water.

Sample analysis by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry

Samples were reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid in water and analyzed by LC-MS/MS on an Impact II ESI-QTOF (Bruker Daltonics) mass spectrometer in a positive ion data-dependent acquisition mode with a captive spray source connected on-line to a Dionex Ultimate 3000 chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a NanoLC pump. Analytes were separated using a Bruker Pepsep column (length 15 cm, 75 μm internal diameter, bead size 1.9 μm, pore size 120 Å) at 40oC with a flow rate of 400 nL min−1, and gradient elution using 0.1% formic acid in water (Solvent A) and 80% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid/water (Solvent B) over 22 min. The initial concentration of Solvent B was 2.5% followed by gradual increases from 2.5 to 12% (3 min), 12−35% (17 min), 35-80% (1 min). Afterwards the column was purged and re-equilibrated at isocratic Solvent B concentrations of 80% (4 min), and 2.5% B (9 min), respectively.

Raw data files were converted to mzML files using MSConvert (Proteowizard) and subjected to database searches using Fragpipe 21.1 (with MSFragger 4.0, IonQuant 1.10.12 and Philosopher 5.1.0) with the following parameters: trypsin (2 missed cleavages), mass tolerance 20 ppm (both precursor and fragment ions), variable modifications [+15.99 Da (Met oxidation), +33.96 Da (Tyr/Trp chlorination), +44.98 Da (Tyr/Trp nitration), +67.92 Da (Tyr/Trp di-chlorination), +89.97 Da (Tyr/Trp di-nitration), +31.99 Da (di-oxygenation at cysteine to give the sulfinic acid), +47.98 Da (tri-oxygenation at cysteine to give the sulfonic acid), +57.02 Da (cysteine alkylation), −17.03 Da (pyroglutamate formation from N-terminal Gln)] and match-between-runs. Cross-linked peptides were identified using the Byonic module in the Byos software suite (version 5.1.1; Protein Metrics). The database search parameters were as follows: trypsin 2 missed cleavages; parent ion mass tolerance: 20 ppm; fragment ion mass tolerance: 20 ppm; fixed modifications: 17.03 Da (N-terminal Gln); variable modifications: +15.99 Da (Met oxidation), crosslinks: -2.015650 Da (disulfide). FASTA files containing the human proteome (UP000005640) wt and mutant GFAP sequences (Uniprot accession P14136) and common contaminants were used, with the protein false discovery rate (FDR) set to 1%. Only modified peptides that were identified in at least 2 out of 3 replicates were considered. MS/MS spectra for all modified species were visually inspected, and the isotope profiles and retention times for quantified features validated using Skyline (version 24.1.0.414). The occupancy of modifications (relative site occupancy, RSO) was calculated by dividing the summed intensity of matching peptides features containing the relevant modification at a particular site by the summed intensity of all peptides (modified and non-modified) containing the residue involved. For the peptide 288QLQSLTCDLESLR300 all quantifications were based on features where the N-terminal Gln has been converted to pyroglutamate as this was the dominant form detected. Intensities for disulfide-bonded peptide features were normalized against total protein intensity. Annotated mass spectra for all identified modified and crosslinked peptides are provided in the Supplementary information.

Oxidation of recombinant GFAP wt and mutants for SDS-PAGE analysis

Protein (4 μM final concentration) was incubated for 1 h at room temperature with 1 mM H2O2 or H2O (control) in the presence of varying concentrations of DTT, as indicated in each figure. When indicated, the final DTT concentration was adjusted to 0.1 mM before the treatment with H2O2. For incubations in the absence of DTT (theoretical concentration <0.6 μM), proteins were subjected to two rounds of 1:20 (v/v) dilution with DTT-free buffer and ultrafiltration using Amicon 10 kDa 0.5 mL filters (Merck) at 14000 g for 10-30 min. After incubations, aliquots from the mixtures were diluted with Laemmli sample buffer with (reducing) or without (non-reducing) 5% (v/v) β-mercaptoethanol and incubated at 95oC for 5 min. To examine the reversibility of disulfide crosslinks, aliquots from the oxidation mixtures were incubated with 1 M DTT in Laemmli buffer, for 15 min at 95oC. Samples (4.3 μg of protein per lane) were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and stained with SimplyBlue SafeStain according to the manufacturer's protocol. Representative grayscale images of at least two independent experiments are presented. Oligomer-to-monomer ratios quantified using the ImageJ Gel analysis software were calculated considering all the oligomeric forms and are presented below each corresponding lane.

Plasmids for expression of GFAP wt and mutants in mammalian cells and transfections

Vectors used for mammalian expression include those coding GFP-GFAP wt and the R239C mutant previously described [23], as well as those coding GFP-GFAP R79C and E373K mutants generated in the present study (see Materials and Methods 2.3). Transfections were performed at 70% cell confluence in 60 mm culture dishes, using 1.8 μg of plasmid DNA and Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) in antibiotic-free medium for 5 h. Following transfection, cells were maintained in antibiotic-free medium for 48 h to allow recovery. Transfected cells were selected by culturing in medium containing 500 μg mL−1 geneticin (G418) for at least five passages post-transfection, and the transfected population was further enriched via fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) at the Flow Cytometry facility of CIB Margarita Salas. Expression of GFP-GFAP was verified by fluorescence microscopy and western blot analysis.

Cell culture and treatments

The U-87 MG glioblastoma astrocytoma cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling via microsatellite amplification at Secugen, SL (Madrid, Spain). Routine testing for mycoplasma contamination was carried out at the Cell Culture Facility of CIB Margarita Salas. Cells were maintained in DMEM with high glucose, supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and antibiotics (100 U mL−1 penicillin and 100 μg mL−1 streptomycin; Gibco), at 37oC in a humidified incubator with 5% (v/v) CO2.

For treatments with oxidants, cells were incubated with 1 mM H2O2 for 30 min, 0.5 mM ONOOH or 50 μM HOCl for 15 min, in serum-free medium, at 37oC. For antioxidant pre-treatment, cells were incubated with 3 mM N-acetylcysteine (NAC) in complete culture medium for 16 h at 37oC. Subsequently, cells were exposed to 1 mM H2O2 for 30 min at 37oC in serum-free medium. Cell integrity under these conditions was >95% in all fields analyzed, as assessed by optical microscopy. The effect of the various treatments was analyzed through western blot and confocal microscopy.

Cell lysis and western blotting

U-87 MG cells expressing GFP-GFAP wt or mutant variants were grown in 60 mm dishes and harvested under standard culture conditions, or after treatment in the absence or presence of oxidants as specified above. After two washes with ice-cold PBS, lysis was carried out on ice by addition of 120 μL lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM EGTA and 0.5% (w/v) SDS) containing 50 mM sodium fluoride, 0.1 mM sodium orthovanadate, 2 μg mL−1 each of protease inhibitors (leupeptin, aprotinin and trypsin inhibitor) and 1.3 mM Pefablock. Cell homogenization was carried out on ice by initial rocking of plates, rubber scraping of the cell monolayer, and finally, repeated passages through a 26½ gauge needle. Protein concentration was determined in total cell lysates using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Pierce). Aliquots of the lysates were denatured in Laemmli buffer either in the presence (reducing conditions) or absence (non-reducing conditions) of 5% (v/v) β-mercaptoethanol. Protein samples containing similar amounts of the GFAP forms, as indicated in the figure legends, were resolved in 10% SDS-PAGE gels and electrotransferred to Immobilon-P membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 2% (w/v) low-fat milk in 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 0.05% (v/v) Tween-20 (T-TBS), followed by incubation with a 1:5000 (v/v) dilution of anti-GFAP (DAKO) in T-TBS containing 1% (w/v) BSA. After three washes in T-TBS, membranes were incubated with a 1:2000 (v/v) dilution of HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit antibody (DAKO) in T-TBS containing 1% (w/v) BSA. Antibody incubations were typically performed for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were developed using the Enhanced Chemiluminescence detection system (Cytiva) and captured on Agfa X-ray films.

Determination of glutathione levels in cells

Total (GSH + GSSG) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) concentrations were determined by the method of Tietze as modified by Rahman et al. [29]. Briefly, U87-MG cells expressing GFP-GFAP wt or the R79C, R239C or E373K mutants were grown in p100 plates until 80-90% confluence. After gentle washing, cells were recovered by scraping and centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min at 4oC. Cell pellets were immediately frozen with dry ice and kept at −20oC until processing. Each cell pellet was extracted with 250 μL of potassium phosphate/EDTA (KPE) buffer containing 0.6% (w/v) sulfosalicylic acid (Sigma) and 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100 and centrifuged at 8000 g for 10 min at 4oC. Supernatants and pellets were separated and used in parallel to measure glutathione and protein concentrations, respectively. A 25 μL aliquot of the supernatant was diluted 1:10 (v/v) in KPE buffer for total glutathione determination, with another 150 μL used for GSSG measurement. This last aliquot was modified with 2-vinylpyridine (Aldrich) for 1 h, the reaction stopped with triethylamine (Sigma) for 10 min and then diluted 1:2 (v/v) with KPE buffer for GSSG detection. Reduced glutathione (GSH) was calculated by subtraction of GSSG concentrations from total glutathione values, considering that each pmol of GSSG corresponds to 2 pmol of GSH. Pellets were employed for protein determination using Bradford reagent (Bio-Rad) with bovine serum albumin as standard. Three independent assays were carried out in triplicate for each cell line.

Confocal microscopy

Astrocytoma cells expressing GFP-GFAP wt or the R239C mutant were grown on 35 mm glass-bottom dishes (Mattek Corp.) and subjected to the oxidant treatments described above before fixation with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde for 25 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100 for 20 min and blocking with 1% (w/v) BSA in PBS for 1 h. Subsequently, cells were stained simultaneously with Phalloidin-Alexa Fluor 568 (1 μg mL−1, Invitrogen) and DAPI (3 μg mL−1, Sigma), both in blocking solution for 30 min. Preparations were observed using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope with a X63 objective. Phalloidin staining was used to outline the contours of individual cells. Single sections were acquired every 1 μm and overall projections were obtained.

Image analysis

For the assessment of filament network retraction in cells expressing GFP-GFAP wt upon oxidative treatment, 20 cells per experimental condition were analyzed. Image settings were optimized using the LUT tool of Leica software to avoid signal saturation. Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually delineated using ImageJ software to encompass (i) the entire cell body, defined by phalloidin staining, and (ii) the GFAP-positive area, identified as GFP-positive regions exhibiting filamentous structures. The percentage of GFAP-covered area was calculated as the ratio of GFAP-positive area to total cell area.

To quantify the proportion of cells exhibiting aggregates, at least 100 cells expressing the GFP-GFAP wt or the R239C, R79C or E373K mutants were evaluated across a minimum of two independent experiments. Cells were categorized based on the presence or absence of aggregates under control and treatment conditions. For each experiment, the percentage of cells containing aggregates was calculated, and individual experiments are shown as separate data points.

For quantification of the average number of aggregates per cell, 25 GFP-GFAP R239C mutant cells per experimental condition across three independent experiments were analyzed. Using ImageJ software, the threshold for aggregate detection was set manually to ensure inclusion of all aggregates while excluding bundle formations. All graphical representations were generated using GraphPad Prism v9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Prediction of the putative localization of the GFAP residues of interest extrapolating the information from available vimentin structures

Although the crystal structure of a tetrameric assembly of GFAP rod 1B is available (PDB entry: 6A9P [30]), it only comprises residues 110-223 and hence lacks many of the residues of interest. Consequently, and making use of the fact that type III intermediate filament proteins have conserved residues in equivalent positions, the data published for vimentin structures (compact and elongated tetramers [31]) was used to explore the impact of GFAP mutations at residues conserved between both proteins. For this purpose, a BLASTP alignment of human vimentin and GFAP sequences was performed (Fig. 1B), with this indicating that GFAP residues R79, R239, Y285 and Y324 occupy equivalent positions to vimentin residues R113, R273, Y319, and Y358, respectively. Rendering of the structures was then carried out using PyMOL (v1.7.435; Schrödinger LLC, New York, NY, USA) with the mutagenesis feature used to introduce the desired mutations at equivalent positions in the vimentin structure (R113C and R273C) and the measurement tool to obtain distances between side chains of interest.

Statistical analysis and data availability

Statistical differences were examined using t-tests (where only 2 data groups were present) and one-way ANOVA with post hoc analysis using Tukey's multiple comparison test, on data from three independent replicates, with p < 0.05 considered significant. The raw MS data files have been deposited with PRIDE with the following project accession code: PXD069155.

Statistical analysis of the glutathione results was performed using Kruskal-Wallis test and Dunn's post-hoc analysis. For all cell-based quantifications, either a two-tailed unpaired parametric (for Gaussian-distributed datasets) or a non-parametric (for non-Gaussian datasets) t-test was applied. Gaussian distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. All statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism v9 software. Data are presented as mean ± SEM.

Results

Oxidants elicit modifications on GFAP wt at various sites

In order to examine whether oxidants generate modifications on purified GFAP wt, control and oxidant-modified protein samples were analyzed by LC-MS/MS using sequential digestion with Lys-C and trypsin as detailed in the Materials and Methods. Initial experiments indicated a sequence coverage for unmodified refolded recombinant GFAP wt of 83-91%, under the conditions employed, with detection of peptides across all domains of the protein (Fig. S2).

Samples of refolded purified recombinant GFAP wt were also treated with fixed concentrations of H2O2 (1 mM, 30 min), ONOOH (0.5 mM, 15 min) or HOCl (50 μM, 15 min) for the indicated time periods. Under these conditions, it is likely that all the ONOOH and HOCl will be completely consumed based on literature data for the reactivity of these species [[32], [33], [34]]. As consumption of H2O2 was unlikely to be complete under the reaction conditions employed, excess oxidant was removed from the samples during the initial precipitation step of the processing. The sequence coverage for these samples is presented in Fig. S2B.

Each of the oxidants induced consistent modifications (detected in at least two out of three independent replicates) at multiple sites on GFAP wt, with these including oxidation at cysteine (with each oxidant), and also nitration (ions with mass increase of +45 Da, with ONOOH as oxidant) and chlorination (ions with mass increase of +34 Da, for HOCl), with the latter modifications also detected on multiple Tyr (and possibly Trp) residues including Y14, Y83, Y116, Y172, Y242, W256/Y257, Y285, Y324, Y349 and Y366. The most frequent oxidative modifications found under these conditions and their sites are illustrated in Fig. 1B and may represent hot spots for modification in the protein. The full list of modifications is provided in Table S2.

C294 of GFAP wt is a major site of modification by various oxidants

Since the single C294 residue of GFAP has been proposed as a hot spot for modifications with important implications for filament remodeling [7], we performed a detailed examination of the species generated at this site, as previous studies on C294 modification relied on immunodetection or derivatization techniques, which may not provide a complete and unbiased characterization of the species present [35,36] (reviewed in Ref. [18]). Thus, certain potential products (e.g., cysteic acid, RSO3H) are difficult to detect, and the specific residues involved in cross-links are challenging to characterize using these approaches. In the current LC-MS/MS analyses, a peptide containing C294 (288QLQSLTCDLESLR300), was readily detected in control samples (non-oxidized, see Table S2). The MS/MS spectrum of this peptide is presented in Fig. 2A and shows a near complete sequence of y ions, confirming its identity. However, analysis of the oxidant-treated samples resulted in varying degrees of loss of this species, with a significant decline of this peptide detected on treatment with H2O2, HOCl and ONOOH (Fig. 2B, left panel). These data are consistent with the available kinetic data for these oxidants, which exhibit rate constants for reaction with cysteine that decrease along the order HOCl > ONOOH ≫ H2O2 [32,34,37,38].

(B) Quantitation of peak area for the 288QLQSLTCDLESLR300 peptide of GFAP wt (left panel) and the corresponding peptide from the C294S mutant (right panel), treated under the same conditions. As a control for these effects, the GFAP C294S mutant was treated and analyzed under the same conditions. Interestingly, upon treatment with oxidants, the ion signal from the peptide 288QLQSLTSDLESLR300 was not decreased in intensity to the same extent as the wt peptide (the C294-containing peptide: 288QLQSLTCDLESLR300) (Fig. 2B, right panel), consistent with a lower or lack of oxidant-mediated modification of any of the residues present in the former peptide. Similar extents of site occupancy (i.e. % modification at specific residues) were detected for the wt and C294S proteins with regards to chlorination and nitration, although a lower chlorination level was observed at Y285 in the C294S mutant, and non-significant trends towards an increase at other sites (Fig. 2C and D). These data indicate that the C294 residue is a major site of modification in GFAP wt, and particularly with HOCl and ONOOH, though these oxidants can also induce chlorination (with HOCl) and nitration (with ONOOH) at Tyr (and maybe the single Trp) residues. The levels of these alternative products (cf. the site occupancy data in Fig. 2C and D) are modest when compared to the extent of consumption of the wt peptide under identical conditions (∼50-95% consumption, Fig. 2B). Mutation of the C294 residue to Ser prevented loss of this peptide but did not result in significant increases in alternative products, probably as a result of experimental variation arising from the low levels of these species. Taken together, these results show that C294 is a major site for oxidative modifications in GFAP and its presence or absence can impact on the modifications detected at other sites.

Effects of recombinant Alexander disease GFAP mutations on the extent of modification by various oxidants

Modifications formed on the AxD GFAP mutants (R79C, R239C and E373K point mutants) were evaluated under the same incubation conditions used for GFAP wt. Modifications at both the cysteine and other sites were examined, including chlorination and nitration at the Tyr residues identified in GFAP wt. An inverse estimate of the global modifications at C294 was obtained from the availability of this peptide for alkylation (Fig. 3A).

Chlorinated (B) and nitrated (C) peptides detected on the R79C, R239C and E373K mutant proteins in comparison to GFAP wt. In general, the extent of modification of C294 was slightly lower for the R239C variant and higher for the E373K variant. These data are analyzed in more detail below. Levels of chlorination induced by HOCl across each of the mutant GFAP species, together with comparable data for the parent wt protein are depicted in Fig. 3B. The extent of chlorination at Y83 in the R239C mutant, and at Y285 in the R79C variant, were significantly decreased with respect to GFAP wt and the E373K mutant with the levels in the latter two protein variants being comparable across all sites (Fig. 3B). Chlorination at Y285 in the R239C mutant also showed a trend towards a decrease. For the corresponding nitrations, a statistically-significant increase was detected at Y83 for the R239C mutant (Fig. 3C), but the levels at all other sites across each of the variants examined were consistent and not statistically different. Thus, the point mutations present in two of the variants examined (R79C and R239C) appear to decrease the extent of chlorination and enhance nitration even though the mutation sites are not in the direct proximity of the Tyr residues that are subject to chlorination/nitration. In contrast the E373K mutant protein behaves similarly to GFAP wt. Therefore, AxD mutations can impact modifications of the corresponding protein variants to different extents.

Study of the modification of C294 by various oxidants in GFAP wt and several AxD mutants

In the light of the above data, the changes provoked by oxidants on the C294-containing peptide (QLQSLTCDLESLR) were studied in greater detail. In line with the data presented above, occupancy of the alkylated peptide (showing a +57 Da mass shift due to modification of C294 by 2-chloroacetamide) was lower in oxidant-treated GFAP wt samples compared to controls (Fig. 4A, left panel). Therefore, a significant decrease in the concentration of the free thiol of the cysteine residue can be deduced, with this being particularly notable for the HOCl and ONOOH treated samples, in which the proportion of remaining free thiol (occupancy) was <20%. Higher levels (∼40%) were detected for the H2O2-treated protein (Fig. 4A). Ions with other mass changes were also detected for the C294-containing peptide with these assigned to oxidation of C294 to sulfinic acid (+32 Da) and sulfonic acid (+48 Da) species (Fig. 4A, middle and right panels, and Table S2). Interestingly, the intensity of the ions assigned to the C294 sulfinic acid increased along the series H2O2 < HOCl < ONOOH, with the ions from the latter accounting for ∼50% of the detectable peptide (Fig. 4A). All of the oxidizing systems converted C294 to the corresponding sulfonic acid to a similar extent (∼30%, Fig. 4A). These data indicate that the loss of the free thiol of the C294-containing peptide occurs in parallel with an increase in both reversible and irreversible oxidized products.

Left, middle and right panels show occupancy for the alkylated (+57 Da), sulfinic acid (+32 Da) and sulfonic acid (+48 Da) forms of the C294-containing peptides, respectively. The GFAP mutant proteins were also incubated with oxidants, as described above, prior to evaluation of their respective C294 free thiol content. Treatment with H2O2 led to similar alkylation levels for the GFAP mutants and GFAP wt, with these being ∼40% of the untreated protein (i.e. site occupancy of ∼0.4; Fig. 4A–D, left panels). Moreover, as with the wt protein, the C294 occupancy levels after treatment with HOCl or ONOOH were generally lower in all mutants (between 10 and 40%). Nevertheless, the effect of HOCl was less dramatic in the R239C mutant (occupancy ∼40%, compared to 10-20% for the other mutant proteins). However, it needs to be noted that both the R79C and R239C mutants contain an extra cysteine residue, which does not improve C294 free thiol availability upon oxidant treatment as deduced from their similar degree of alkylation to the wt protein. Notably, the free C294 thiol content of the E373K mutant was dramatically decreased on treatment with HOCl and ONOOH, with <10% of the thiol detected in its alkylated form after oxidant treatment (Fig. 4).

In addition to determining the loss of the C294 thiol, formation of sulfinic (+32 Da) and sulfonic acid (+48 Da) products from this residue were analyzed across all the recombinant mutant proteins. Under control conditions (no added oxidant), no significant formation of these oxidized species was detected (Fig. 4). After H2O2 treatment, sulfinic acid formation at C294 was detected at similar levels for GFAP wt and the R79C and R239C proteins, whereas the levels of this species were slightly lower for the E373K mutant (Fig. 4, middle panels). Similar C294 sulfinic acid occupancy values to those detected for GFAP wt were found upon HOCl treatment of the R79C mutant, whereas lower levels were detected at this site for the R239C and E373K mutants, this decrease reaching nearly 50% of the levels of the wt in the E373K protein. Treatment with ONOOH gave rise to a high sulfinic acid occupancy of C294 and similar levels were detected for the wt, R79C and R239C proteins, whereas this increase was more moderate for the E373K protein. With regards to the formation of the sulfonic acid species, similar occupancy levels were detected at C294 for the wt, R79C and R239C proteins with all the oxidants employed, whereas for the E373K mutant increased levels were found especially upon HOCl treatment (Fig. 4, right panels). The ratio of the sulfonic + sulfinic acid forms of the C294 residue relative to the alkylated species (i.e. non-oxidized cysteine), as well as the other cysteine residues present in the mutant forms, was calculated as an index of the occurrence of higher oxidation states of these residues relative to the parent species. This analysis for each of the proteins examined showed no dramatic changes for the R79C and R239C mutants, with values similar to those obtained for the GFAP wt protein independently of the oxidant (Table 1). However, this ratio increased notably for the E373K mutant protein under any of the conditions tested, suggesting a clear influence of the residue occupying this position on the extent of modification that occurs at C294.

As the additional cysteine residues present in the R79C and R239C mutants are also potential targets for the oxidants, modification at these residues was also assessed (Fig. 5). As expected, the occupancy of the additional thiol (as quantified by the ion intensity of the alkylated, m/z +57, species) was close to 1 for the control samples (no oxidant treatment). The levels of alkylation of the R79C peptide decreased significantly with oxidant treatment (Fig. 5A, left panel), indicating a loss of this free thiol, with H2O2 producing an ∼30% decrease, compared with 60-70% for HOCl and ONOOH. Assessment of disulfide bond formation in recombinant GFAP wt and AxD mutant proteins induced by oxidants

As the cysteine residues discussed above may also be involved in disulfide bonds within the GFAP filaments, and the potential formation of these species would be likely to increase with the presence of additional cysteine residues in certain AxD mutants, including the R79C and R239C variants, the potential existence of such crosslinks was explored by use of LC-MS/MS (for all three oxidants), and by gel electrophoresis for H2O2-treated samples.

The LC-MS/MS data showed that an intermolecular disulfide bond is formed between C294 in GFAP wt both under control conditions (no added oxidant) and upon oxidant exposure, with a lower presence in ONOOH-treated samples (Fig. 6A). This disulfide is also formed in the different GFAP mutants, although extent of formation of this species varies across mutants (Fig. 6B–D). Thus, the intensity of ions arising from the C294–C294 crosslink in the R239C protein was significantly higher upon H2O2 incubation and lower for ONOOH treatment, relative to control (Fig. 6C).

Disulfide bonds detected in GFAP wt and AxD mutants treated with various oxidants. Disulfide bond C294–C294 detected in GFAP wt (A) and mutants R79C (B), R239C (C) and E373K (D). Additional disulfide bonds were detected in the R79C (E) and R239C (F) mutants. The intensities of the disulfide bonded peptide features were normalized by protein abundance. Please note that in order to show the variation in occupancy levels, the scales for the Y axis are very different between the figure panels.

In addition to this C294–C294 crosslink, the R79C and R239C mutant proteins also showed two additional disulfides with lower normalized intensities. In both cases, the disulfide involved crosslinking of the conserved C294 with the additional cysteine arising from the point mutation (i.e., C79–C294 for the R79C protein; C239–C294 for the R239C protein) (Fig. 6E), and a second species arising from intermolecular crosslinking between two molecules of the new cysteine residue (i.e. C79–C79 for the R79C protein; C239–C239 for the R239C protein) (Fig. 6F).

For the R79C protein, ONOOH and H2O2 induced disulfide formation with the yield of these species following the order C294–C294 (native homodimer) > C79–C294 (crossed-dimer) > C79–C79 (homodimer), as determined by the MS ion intensities, though these values need to be interpreted with caution due to potential differences in ionization efficiency between the peptides. HOCl followed the same pattern, except that the levels of the C79–C79 species were higher than for ONOOH (Fig. 6E). For the R239C mutant, the normalized intensities for all the disulfides were significantly lower than those for the R79C protein, though the propensity for formation of individual species followed a similar pattern (i.e. with C294–C294 being the dominant species, followed by the crossed-dimer C239–C294, and the homodimer C239–C239 being the least abundant). Comparison of the normalized intensities of the peptide ions derived from the species with the C239–C239 and C239–C294 disulfide bonds generated by the three oxidants used showed a higher propensity for the generation of these species in incubations with H2O2, whereas no significant differences were detected between the HOCl and ONOOH treatments (Fig. 6F).

The second method used to assess disulfide bond formation involved analysis of the recombinant protein samples on SDS-PAGE gels (Fig. 7), with two different approaches employed. In the first approach, proteins were incubated with H2O2 in the presence of 0.1 mM DTT to avoid excessive oxidation (Fig. 7A). In the second approach, the DTT concentration in the protein preparations was lowered by successive ultrafiltration steps to <0.6 μM (Fig. 7B). In the first approach, analysis of the incubation mixtures under non-reducing conditions revealed only the monomer band and negligible levels of oligomeric species in GFAP wt under control conditions. In contrast, several oligomeric species of variable sizes were detected in the mixtures from all AxD mutants under control conditions, with the most intense bands on the gels having an apparent molecular mass of ∼100 kDa in the case of R79C, and >150 kDa in the case of R239C, with these being less abundant for the E373K mutant protein (Fig. 7A). Oxidant treatment elicited the appearance of oligomers for GFAP wt, as well as an increase in the abundance of the oligomeric species for all of the mutant proteins. Most of these oligomers disappeared when the samples were analyzed under reducing conditions (with 5% (v/v) β-mercaptoethanol, Fig. 7A and B, right panels). Interestingly, the oligomers formed from R239C appeared to be particularly resistant to reduction and were only eliminated by incubation at 95oC for 15 min in the presence of 1 M DTT prior to gel electrophoresis (Fig. 7C).

These results further supported the presence of disulfide bonds which, according to the estimated molecular mass of the bands, should be established between monomers to form dimers and tetramers (Fig. 7A–C). These high molecular mass bands became even more apparent when the DTT-free incubations (theoretical concentration <0.6 μM) were carried out, both in the absence and presence of oxidants. Under these conditions, additional oligomers could be detected on the non-reducing gels, and particularly in the cases of the R79C and R239C mutants, that were again mostly undetectable under reducing conditions, except for the R239C > 150 kDa oligomer mentioned above. Quantitation of the proportion of oligomeric vs monomeric species confirms that all AxD mutants assayed are more prone to form disulfide crosslinked species than the wt protein, and particularly on treatment with H2O2. Although this type of analysis does not provide information on the identity of the residues involved in the crosslinking, GFAP wt can only form C294–C294 disulfide bonded dimers. In this regard, it should be noted that GFAP dimers may display an apparent molecular mass higher than that corresponding to their theoretical mass, due to a large proportion of α-helix in the protein, as it has been demonstrated for a vimentin dimer migrating above 150 kDa [39]. On the other hand, it appears that the oligomer at ∼100 kDa is specific for the R79C mutant, and therefore, likely involves C79, whereas the oligomeric species formed by the R239C mutant could involve C294–C294 (lower band, migrating at the same position as the GFAP wt dimer), and C239–C239 or C239–C294 disulfides (upper band). While the single cysteine residue of GFAP wt only allows the formation of inter-molecular dimers through disulfide bonding, in the case of the mutants containing two cysteine residues, the possibility exists that some of the bands correspond to trimers or tetramers or higher order oligomers in different configurations.

Recent crosslinking studies combined with molecular modeling and small angle X-ray scattering have proposed the existence of two configurations of vimentin tetramers in solution: elongated and compact [31]. These models provide the opportunity of extrapolating the approximate positions of some of the cysteine residues in GFAP wt or mutants. Fig. 7D–F shows that the distances between the single cysteine residues of GFAP wt, or the positions equivalent to the R239C mutation, are in the range of 9.4 to 10.7 Å within a tetramer or between monomers of nearby tetramers, which are not incompatible with their involvement in disulfide bonds. Taken together, these data suggest differences in the susceptibility and/or accessibility of the disulfides to the reducing agent, since some of them may be hidden in the core of the corresponding oligomer as suggested from the examination of analogous positions in vimentin structures.

AxD GFAP mutants show altered disulfide crosslinking in cells under basal conditions and upon exposure to oxidants

Given the importance of the oxidative modifications at cysteine observed in vitro, we explored disulfide bond formation on GFAP wt and the various mutants, expressed in U-87 MG astrocytoma cells as GFP-tagged proteins. Cell lysates were analyzed by SDS-PAGE under non-reducing and reducing conditions, and GFAP detected by immunoblotting (Fig. 8A). Under non-reducing conditions, lysates of control cells (no oxidant treatment) revealed the presence of high molecular weight oligomers (∼250 kDa) in the GFP-GFAP R79C and R239C samples (Fig. 8A, left lanes). These oligomers were attenuated under reducing conditions, consistent with the cleavage of an existing disulfide bond in these cysteine mutants (Fig. 8A, right lanes) and confirming that both show altered disulfide formation also in cells.

Expression of GFAP AxD mutants may cause alterations in the redox state of the cells by various mechanisms [23], among them changing the content of glutathione forms. In order to analyze the possibility that GFAP AxD mutants alter cellular glutathione content, total (GSH + GSSG) and oxidized (GSSG) glutathione levels were measured in each cell line (Fig. 8B). GFAP wt, R79C and R239C expressing cells displayed notable variability between experiments in their total glutathione values, as well as in their calculated GSH concentrations (Fig. S3), whereas data in E373K-expressing cells were more consistent. Total glutathione levels of E373K cells tended to be higher compared to the rest of the cell lines and were significantly elevated relative to R239C cells (Fig. 8B, top panel). Reduced glutathione levels showed a comparable pattern, consistent with its predominant contribution to total glutathione content (Fig. S3). Mean GSSG values tended to be lower in R79C and R239C cells compared to GFAP wt expressing cells and significantly diminished versus E373K cells (Fig. 8B, bottom panel). Altogether the results show higher glutathione availability in E373K-expressing cells than in the other cell lines, especially the R239C-expressing cells.

Given the increased resistance to reduction of the GFAP R239C disulfide bonded oligomer, even under basal conditions, we next specifically examined the effects of oxidative challenge in GFP-GFAP wt or R239C expressing cells. Treatment with the various oxidants (H2O2, ONOOH or HOCl) did not elicit appreciable oligomerization of GFP-GFAP wt, and only the monomer was detected (Fig. 8C). In contrast, treatment of cells expressing GFP-GFAP R239C with certain oxidants elicited an increase in the intensity of the band from the ∼250 kDa oligomer, which was more apparent after treatment with H2O2. The high-molecular-mass GFP-GFAP R239C bands were abolished under reducing conditions, except in samples that were treated with H2O2, where these species were still visible though with lower intensity. Together, these data are consistent with the presence of disulfide bonds especially resistant to reduction in the GFAP R239C mutant protein in cells.

As expected, expression of GFP-GFAP wt led to the formation of a uniform network of filaments extending from the nuclear periphery towards the plasma membrane. In contrast, and consistent with our previous observations [23], under control conditions cells expressing the R239C mutant displayed thick filament bundles coexisting with bright aggregates and areas apparently devoid of filaments, or showing a diffuse signal, consistent with a profound alteration of the GFAP network. Treatment of cells expressing GFP-GFAP wt with H2O2 resulted in a marked retraction of the filament network and its accumulation near the nucleus (Fig. 9).

Remarkably, cells expressing GFP-GFAP R239C showed further bundling and a prominent increase in the number of aggregates in response to H2O2 (Fig. 9). Curiously, ONOOH treatment elicited cellular alterations with the appearance of weaker network retraction in cells expressing GFP-GFAP wt, and the filaments appeared preserved. These differences to the behavior detected with H2O2 may arise from the relative propensity of this oxidant to form nitrated Tyr residues vs disulfide bonds. HOCl treatment induced a retraction of the GFP-GFAP wt network similar to that produced by ONOOH, with this being consistent with the high rate constant for reaction of HOCl with thiol groups [37]. Interestingly, in cells expressing GFP-GFAP R239C all compounds elicited mixed patterns, with perinuclear bundles, aggregates and some diffuse protein. Taken together, these results illustrate the deleterious effect of the R239C mutation on the distribution of the GFAP network and in the response to oxidants, characterized by further alterations of protein assembly and/or impaired redistribution.

Effect of N-acetyl-cysteine on the oligomerization and distribution of GFAP AxD mutants

The results shown above indicate that expression of certain GFAP AxD mutants associates with alterations in glutathione availability and/or the appearance of oxidized GFAP oligomers, even under basal cell culture conditions. Therefore, it was of interest to explore whether supplementation of cells with glutathione precursors could mitigate GFAP mutant-associated alterations. For this, we used N-acetyl-Ll-cysteine (NAC), a widely used antioxidant that acts both as a precursor for intracellular glutathione synthesis and as a free thiol donor and scavenger of reactive oxygen species. Effects of NAC pretreatment on GFAP disulfide oligomerization and network morphology under basal conditions and on subsequent challenge by H2O2 were examined. We observed that pretreatment with NAC moderately decreased the abundance of disulfide-linked oligomeric species in R79C and R239C mutants, as revealed by non-reducing electrophoresis and western blot analysis of cell lysates (Fig. S4). However, NAC pretreatment was not sufficient to counteract the enhanced disulfide oligomerization provoked by exposure to H2O2 in any of the mutants. Overall, these data indicate that NAC exerts only a partial impact on GFAP disulfide-mediated oligomer formation and does not effectively prevent H2O2-induced oligomerization. In turn, the morphology of the GFAP network was not significantly altered by NAC (Fig. S5). In cells expressing wt GFAP wt, NAC elicited a weak increase in the proportion of cells displaying GFAP filament network retraction, and tended to attenuate the effect of H2O2. In cells expressing R79C, R239C, or E373K GFP-GFAP mutants, treatment with NAC did not produce significant changes in the proportion of cells showing GFAP aggregates, either in the absence or presence of H2O2 challenge. Together, these results indicate that NAC pretreatment did not substantially modify GFAP oligomerization or the associated cellular phenotypes, indicating that providing glutathione precursors is not sufficient to reverse the mutant-associated alterations in this experimental model.

Discussion

AxD is a devastating neurodegenerative disease caused by mutations in the type III intermediate filament protein GFAP. Type III intermediate filaments are highly sensitive to redox alterations and oxidative modifications, with their single cysteine residue playing an important role [40,41]. Although redox sensing is important for regulation under physiological conditions, susceptibility to oxidation may have a downside in pathologies associated with oxidative stress.

Accumulating evidence indicates that AxD is accompanied by thiol depletion and redox imbalance, which the antioxidant capacity of cells appears to be insufficient to counteract [12,23,42]. Moreover, mutant GFAP could act both as trigger and target of oxidative stress, as suggested by the observations that expression of GFP-GFAP R239C in cells (in the absence of added exogenous stress) increases superoxide production from mitochondria, and at the same time this mutant is highly prone to oxidation [23]. By using MS, here we show that AxD mutations not only alter the PTM landscape of GFAP, but also confer increased susceptibility of the proteins to oxidation, favoring oxidative modifications that compromise C294, the single cysteine residue in GFAP wt, which is required for correct assembly of the protein structure [7]. Therefore, in addition to alterations in protein folding or organization due to the amino acid substitution, the modification of other critical residues by PTMs may contribute to the deleterious effects of the mutations.

Our results confirm that C294 undergoes extensive modification upon treatment with various oxidants. In this study, we have been able to identify certain modifications, including sulfinic and sulfonic acid formation, as well as C294–C294 disulfide crosslinking. The detection of sulfinic and sulfonic acid species from C294 indicates the existence of stable or irreversible modifications, and the presence of additional negative charges at sites that are critical for filament assembly. This is particularly interesting in the case of the E373K mutant, in which oxidants elicit a marked increase in the oxidation of C294, with a high proportion of this residue being detected as the sulfonic acid form. At present, the reason why this mutation influences the modification of C294 is not known, although it is possible that if the protein is poorly folded, the accessibility of C294 to over-oxidation to the irreversible species could be enhanced more than in the wt.

Some of the consequences of C294 modifications can be extrapolated from data available for vimentin, which has been more extensively studied [40]. Generation of sulfinic and sulfonic species will introduce negative charges at position 294 in the sequence, which in the case of the equivalent residue of vimentin (C328) appears to hamper filament formation [19]. Moreover, disulfide crosslinks with the additional cysteine residues in some AxD mutants would likely constrain the position of C294. Our results clearly show that certain AxD mutants display increased susceptibility to modification by various oxidants. In particular R79C and R239C show increased disulfide crosslinking, both in vitro and in cells. Some of these disulfide crosslinks appear to be highly stable, and their reversal requires incubation with high DTT concentrations, consistent with these being poorly accessible (buried) within dense structures. Furthermore, these linkages also appear to be present and persist in cells exposed to oxidative stress [23], implying that they cannot be (readily) reversed by the cellular antioxidant systems. Hypothetically, the generation of modifications at C294 could also impact on the formation of modifications at other sites. In this context, treatment of a mutant protein lacking this residue, C294S, with HOCl, resulted in a decreased extent of chlorination at the nearby Tyr residue, Y285.

Other modifications of C294, that could potentially occur in vitro and in vivo, such as sulfenylation, have not been monitored in this study. Additional modifications can take place in cells upon treatment with both oxidants and electrophiles, including mixed disulfide formation, such as cysteinylation, glutathionylation, CoAlation and alkylation, and may also contribute to a decrease in free thiol availability in cells. Interestingly, previous results from our group in which the thiol group occupancy of GFP-GFAP wt and R239C in cells was assessed by means of biotinylated iodoacetamide, indicated that the R239C mutant displayed a lower proportion of free thiol group than the wt under control conditions (occupancy 1.2 vs 0.9 in the wt; i.e. a 1.3 fold change) in spite of having two cysteine residues [23]. Some of these modifications, and in particular glutathionylation, have been shown to have functional effects and to hamper vimentin filament formation in vitro [43].

In AxD, mutant GFAP acts in a dominant manner [44]. Moreover, the mutant form is usually overexpressed, potentially due to compensatory mechanisms provoked by defective functionality. It should be noted that overexpression of GFP-GFAP wt alone may be deleterious and lead to network alterations similar to those provoked by the mutants, at least in cellular models. Therefore, levels of GFAP need to be tightly controlled for optimal functionality. GFAP is a long-lived protein, with a half-life of weeks to months in the nervous system [45]. However, incorporation of PTMs, such as citrullination by PAD4 [46] and its phosphorylation at S13 [47] cause GFAP instability and degradation. In addition, increased GFAP ubiquitination has been detected in AxD brains and in a rat model of AxD, as well as in primary astrocytes transfected with the AxD E373K mutant [24]. On the other hand, the half-life of GFAP in a transgenic GFAPR236H/+ mouse model has been found to be shorter than that of GFAP in the wild type mice GFAP+/+ [48], although in primary cortical astrocytes isolated from these mice the half-lives of total GFAP were similar [48].

At present, there is little information on the impact of oxidative modifications on GFAP stability or degradation, although oxidation and phosphorylation of GFAP have been detected in aggregates found in neurodegenerative diseases (e.g., Alzheimer disease [20]). In addition, accumulation of high molecular mass species, positive for GFAP and ubiquitin have been reported in cellular models expressing GFAP AxD mutants, as well as in brain tissue from AxD patients [24]. It should be noted that protein degradation has been found to be impaired in models of AxD. In particular, an inhibition of proteasome activity by GFAP R239C oligomers has been shown in AxD astrocytoma cells [49], in which lysosomes also appear to be defective [50]. Thus, the impact of the modifications detected in the current study on the biological half-life of GFAP is unclear and this would clearly be an area worthy of further study. Of particular interest may be the poorly-reducible disulfide cross-links as these (as indicated above) may be both deficiently repaired or removed by the machinery within cells and inhibit proteasome (and other degradative) activities.

Of particular interest is the observation in this study that oxidants impact the organization of the GFAP network in U-87 MG astrocytoma cells expressing GFP-GFAP wt or the AxD mutant GFP-GFAP R239C. In particular, oxidants elicited a major remodeling of GFP-GFAP wt consisting of a retraction of filaments from the cell periphery and accumulation near the nucleus in the case of H2O2. This was less apparent in the case of HOCl, and especially ONOOH, which may indicate that the extensive nitration of Tyr residues induced by this oxidant are better tolerated in terms of filament organization. In contrast, the already aberrant distribution of GFP-GFAP R239C was not markedly altered by oxidants, except for the clear increase in the number of aggregates upon treatment with H2O2. Therefore, mutant GFAP did not appear to undergo the perinuclear condensation typical of the wt. Interestingly, this remodeling has been interpreted as a defense response, since this rearrangement implies a concentration of the cellular repair machinery associated with intermediate filaments, which includes chaperones, proteostasis machinery and signaling molecules (reviewed in Ref. [40]). Moreover, we have previously observed that the perinuclear concentration of GFAP in astrocytoma cells upon H2O2 treatment is reversible [23]. This effect is analogous to the reversible perinuclear condensation of vimentin induced by treatment with the ONOOH donor SIN-1 in fibroblasts [17], and reinforces the idea of a protective response.

Previous reports have shown a reduction of the free thiol content, mainly GSH, in GFAP R239C-expressing cells [23]. Here we detected changes in total glutathione concentrations, which are consistent with these previous observations and indicate significant differences in GSH availability between GFAP R239C and E373K expressing cells. Such disparities may differentially influence GSH-dependent antioxidant defense mechanisms in both mutant-expressing cells when exposed to a challenge. However, pretreatment with NAC in an attempt to replenish the cellular pool of cysteine for glutathione synthesis, mitigate the background oxidative stress associated with cell culture conditions, as well as potentially counteracting the effects of oxidant challenge, did not afford significant protection against GFAP oligomerization or network alterations in our experimental model. Based on these data we cannot exclude the existence of additional GSH-independent contributors favoring or inducing GFAP oxidative modifications although, given the increasing evidence on the occurrence of oxidative modifications in AxD, the potential protective effect of antioxidant strategies may deserve further investigation.

Together with previous evidence, our results delineate a pathogenic cycle in which mutant GFAP alters cellular organelle homeostasis leading to oxidative stress. This in turn would further damage GFAP and potentiate protein oxidation and aggregation, therefore compromising proteasomal and lysosomal degradation. It would be necessary, and important, to confirm whether this pathogenic cycle operates in vivo, and if so, what therapeutic strategies can be used to break it.

In summary, the data reported here provide novel and in-depth insights into how the presence of several mutations, including additional cysteine residues in GFAP variants present in patients with AxD, may impact on the structure and function of this protein, and the filament network within cells, and how modification of these cysteine and other residues may contribute to an enhanced oxidative stress, and susceptibility to cellular damage and dysfunction in AxD.
