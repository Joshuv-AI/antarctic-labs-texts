Apatinib enhances anti-PD-1 efficacy by inhibiting Exo70-mediated exosome secretion in pMMR/MSS colorectal cancer
Authors: Lingying Zhao, Chunyi Gao, Xiaoxue Qiao, Fanzhuoran Lou, Bowen Zheng, Miao Fu, Xintian Huang, Xiaowen Xie, Wenqing Zhang, Yongxiang Hong, Kaiyi Rong, Huibo Shi, Li Xiao, Tianhui Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer is a malignant disease with high morbidity and mortality. In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors have emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy; however, patients with proficient mismatch repair/microsatellite-stable (pMMR/MSS) colorectal cancer derive limited benefit from immunotherapy. We conducted a single-arm, exploratory clinical study to evaluate the efficacy of camrelizumab combined with apatinib in patients with advanced metastatic colorectal cancer who had received third-line or later treatment. This trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04067986; registered August 20, 2019). Our results demonstrated that apatinib significantly enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy in patients with pMMR/MSS colorectal cancer. Mechanistically, apatinib improved immunotherapy outcomes by modulating the tumor microenvironment in vivo. Further in vitro experiments revealed that apatinib reduced levels of exosomal PD-L1 in the tumor microenvironment by inhibiting tumor-derived exosome secretion. This inhibitory effect was mediated through the regulation of Exo70. Collectively, our findings indicate that apatinib enhances the efficacy of camrelizumab in pMMR/MSS colorectal cancer and provide a theoretical rationale for the combined use of camrelizumab and apatinib in this patient population.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a malignant tumor originating from the epithelial cells of the mucosa of the colon or rectum and is one of the most common cancers worldwide. Among various malignant tumors, CRC has been associated with high morbidity and mortality. Although most primary tumors can be surgically resected, the 5-year survival rate of patients with advanced CRC remains below 20%1,2.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are novel anti-tumor therapies that enhance T cell-mediated immune responses, restore immune function suppressed by tumor cells, and offer a promising strategy for cancer immunotherapy3,4. PD-L1 on the surface of tumor cells interacts with PD-1 on the surface of T cells to inhibit the activity of effector T cells, leading to immune escape. Currently, PD-1/PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have been developed and approved for clinical use. However, their therapeutic efficacy in CRC, especially in MSS patients, remains limited5,6. Potential synergistic anti-tumor activity of ICIs in combination with anti-angiogenic therapy has been reported7. Therefore, the anti-tumor effect and mechanism of camrelizumab combined with apatinib in CRC deserve further investigation.

Exosomes (Exo) are a subtype of extracellular vesicles (EVs) encapsulated by a double layer of phospholipid membrane molecules with a diameter of approximately 30–150 nm8. By transferring functional cargo to recipient cells, Exo serve as critical mediators of intercellular communication and contribute to the regulation of numerous physiological processes, such as immune responses, signal transduction, and antigen presentation9. It has been shown that in vitro co-culture of PD-L1-overexpressing Exo with T cells leads to inhibition of T cell activation10. In CRC, microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) or mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) is a subtype characterized by a high mutation rate, associated with a better prognosis and a higher likelihood of responding to immunotherapy. However, the majority of CRC cases are microsatellite-stable (MSS), which do not have the high mutation burden that leads to the generation of numerous neoantigens. As a result, pMMR/MSS CRC has historically been less responsive to immunotherapy. Improving immunotherapy efficacy in pMMR/MSS CRC remains a major challenge.

Combining immunotherapy with other treatments, such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy, to enhance the immune response in pMMR/MSS CRC may be a promising approach. Recently, a few targeted therapeutic agents have been evaluated for their efficacy in metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC). Studies such as FRESCO, CORRECT, J003, and RECOURSE have demonstrated that fruquintinib, regorafenib, and TAS-102 monotherapy achieve objective response rates (ORR) ranging from 1% to 4% in patients with mCRC11–13. However, in a single-arm, prospective, multicenter study, apatinib monotherapy achieved an ORR of 8.3% in 48 patients14. Currently, clinical survival benefits from combinations of tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKIs) targeting VEGFRs and Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) vary considerably among patients with pMMR/MSS mCRC. For instance, a Phase Ib/II study reported an ORR of 20.0% for fruquintinib plus sintilimab (a PD-1 inhibitor), and another Phase II study demonstrated an ORR of 21.4% with regorafenib plus sintilimab15,16. In contrast, the REGOMUNE study showed an ORR of 0% among 43 patients receiving regorafenib combined with avelumab (a PD-L1 inhibitor)17. These discrepant findings suggest that further investigation into the differential efficacy of various TKIs targeting VEGFRs in combination with ICIs is warranted.

Apatinib (a VEGFR2 inhibitor) and camrelizumab (a PD-1 monoclonal antibody) are two drugs that have shown potential promise in combination therapy for treating certain cancers18–21. A recent study showed that apatinib, in combination with camrelizumab, significantly improved the outcomes of MSI-H CRC patients22. However, the combination effects of the two drugs in pMMR/MSS CRC patients have not been studied. Moreover, how apatinib may enhance the response of pMMR/MSS CRC to camrelizumab has not been fully elucidated. Therefore, the anti-tumor effect and mechanism of camrelizumab combined with apatinib in CRC deserve further investigation.

Our previous research revealed that apatinib could remodel the TME, potentially enhancing immune cell infiltration and therapeutic efficacy23. We also found that apatinib inhibits the secretion of tumor-derived exosomes, which might be a possible mechanism for TME remodeling24. As mentioned, Exo are key mediators of intercellular communication and immune regulation8,9, and PD-L1 carried by tumor-derived Exo may contribute to immune suppression and reduced response to PD-1 blockade10,25,26.

Exo secretion is regulated by various molecular components, including the exocyst complex, an octameric protein complex composed of Sec3, Sec5, Sec6, Sec8, Sec10, Sec15, Exo70, and Exo84. This complex mediates vesicle tethering, docking, and fusion with the plasma membrane27,28. Exo70, in particular, has been implicated in tumor progression. Our previous studies found that ULK1 can phosphorylate Exo70 in breast cancer cells, inhibiting cell protrusion formation and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) secretion, thereby suppressing invasive metastasis29. We also demonstrated that Exo70 induces drug resistance in ovarian cancer cells by effluxing chemotherapeutic drugs (cisplatin) through extracellular secretion of exocyst30, and it is involved in the release of tumor Exo in pancreatic cancer and promotes the invasion and metastasis31. All the above studies suggest that Exo70 plays a key role in Exo secretion and tumor progression.

In this study, we conducted a phase II, open-label, single-arm clinical study (NCT04067986) to evaluate the efficacy of apatinib mesylate combined with camrelizumab as third-line or later treatment for patients with recurrent or metastatic CRC, which showed promising clinical outcomes. Meanwhile, we explored the underlying anti-tumor mechanisms of this combination, with a particular focus on the role of Exo and the exocyst component Exo70. These findings may provide a theoretical basis for the clinical application of apatinib combined with PD-1 inhibitors in the treatment of pMMR/MSS CRC.

Results

A single-arm exploratory clinical study of Camrelizumab in combination with apatinib for the treatment of advanced metastatic colorectal cancer in the third line and beyond (NCT04067986).

Treatment

All 19 patients received apatinib at a daily oral dose of 250 mg in combination with camrelizumab administered every 14 days, with regular imaging assessments performed during treatment. The median progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were 4.3 months (95% CI, 2.8–10.1) and 18.3 months (95% CI, 10.0–not estimable [NE]), respectively (Fig. 2A, B). The disease control rate (DCR) in this single-arm study was 57.9% (95% CI, 35.7–80.1%) (Fig. 2C). As of the data cutoff date (July 14, 2024), 15 patients (78.9%) discontinued study treatment due to disease progression, while 4 patients (21.1%) discontinued because of adverse events. The best therapeutic effect for 11 patients was SD, and currently, However, the effect of PD was observed in 8 patients. (Fig. 2D). Treatment-Related Adverse Events

Apatinib Enhances Anti-PD-1 Efficacy by Modulating the Immune Microenvironment of pMMR/MSS CRC In Vivo

To investigate the specific effects of the combination of anti-PD-1 and apatinib on pMMR/MSS CRC progression, we established an orthotopic colorectal tumor model using the MSS CRC cell line CT26-Luc in mice. Seven days after tumor implantation, tumor progression was assessed using an in vivo imaging system, and tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into four groups (Fig. 3A). Apatinib treatment was initiated at the time of group allocation and administered daily by oral gavage, while anti-PD-1 antibody was administered intraperitoneally every three days until the experimental endpoint (Day 21). Tumor burden was monitored by bioluminescence imaging at 7-day intervals throughout the study. In the CT26-Luc mouse model, neither apatinib nor anti-PD-1 monotherapy showed a significant difference in tumor growth compared to the control group. However, combining apatinib with anti-PD-1 resulted in a significant reduction in tumor weight and bioluminescence signal compared to the monotherapy and control groups (Fig. 3B, C), which is consistent with our clinical observations (Fig. 2). Notably, the divergence between treatment groups became more apparent at later time points, with statistically significant differences observed at Day 21, suggesting a delayed but enhanced antitumor effect of the combination therapy. Previous studies have suggested that low-dose apatinib can modulate the tumor immune microenvironment32. In the CT26-Luc mouse model, we further analyzed which immune cells contributed to the observed antitumor immune response. We found that the combination of anti-PD-1 and apatinib created a more favorable immune-supportive environment compared to other treatment groups. There was a reduction in CD34+ vessel density and CD163+ macrophage infiltration (Fig. 3D, E). Additionally, Flow cytometry results showed increased infiltration of CD3+ T cells and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3F, G), along with decreased infiltration of CD11b+ macrophages and CD206 + M2-like macrophages (Fig. 3H–K).

Overall, these results imply that the combination of anti-PD-1 and apatinib has enhanced antitumor efficacy, which is mechanistically related to remodeling the immune microenvironment.

Apatinib remodels the tumor microenvironment via exosome-mediated regulation

Extensive studies have demonstrated that exosomal PD-L1 exerts immunosuppressive functions within the tumor microenvironment33–35. Apatinib significantly reduces the expression of PD-L1 on the exosomes derived from CRC cells (Fig. 4A). To investigate the relationship between exosomal PD-L1 function and immune cell infiltration, we conducted T cell functional assays in vitro. Jurkat T cells were activated with 50 ng/ml phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) and then exposed to exosomes derived from CRC cells, either with or without apatinib treatment. Compared to the control group, the Apatinib-treated group showed a significant reduction in the apoptosis rate of Jurkat T cells (Fig. 4B). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from healthy human donors were activated using anti-CD3/CD8 magnetic beads and co-incubated with exosomes from HCT116 cells for T cell stimulation modeling. Anti-CD3/CD8 beads significantly increased the expression of the early T cell activation marker CD69. Treatment with Exo-control resulted in a marked decrease in CD69 expression in T cells, whereas exosomes derived from apatinib-treated blocked the action of PD-L1, rescuing the suppressed CD69 expression in T cells (Fig. 4C). The late T cell activation marker CD25 showed consistent results (Fig. 4D). Macrophages constitute the largest proportion of tumor-infiltrating immune cells and play a critical role in modulating the immune state of the TME and tumor progression36. In vivo experiments showed a decreased proportion of M2-type macrophage infiltration following apatinib treatment (Fig. 3). To further explore the regulatory effect of apatinib on macrophages, we used standard methods to induce human monocytic THP-1 cells into a macrophage polarization model. To determine whether apatinib directly regulated macrophage polarization, THP-1 cells were treated with various concentrations of apatinib (based on the IC50 of 34.31 μM; Fig. S1A), and the expression of key M2 macrophage polarization markers was analyzed. The results showed no significant changes in the expression of CD163 (Fig. S1B, C).

Collectively, these findings suggest that apatinib does not directly modulate immune cell polarization. Instead, it remodels the tumor microenvironment by alleviating CRC cell–mediated immunosuppression, primarily through the reduction of exosomal PD-L1, thereby restoring effective immune cell responses.

Apatinib regulates exosomal secretion via Exo70

In our previous study, we found that Exo70 promotes invasive metastasis of pancreatic cancer cells by regulating the secretion of exosomes in pancreatic cancer31, and that Exo70 promotes tumourigenesis in CRC cells37. However, the effect of Exo70 on exosomes in CRC cells has not been reported in any relevant studies. To clarify whether Exo70 affects exosome secretion in CRC cells, we constructed stable transcripts knocking down Exo70 in RKO and HCT116 cells. Exosomes were isolated from cell culture supernatants using differential ultracentrifugation, and transmission electron microscopy revealed that exosomes derived from both control (shCtrl) and Exo70 knockdown (shExo70) cells exhibited typical oval or cup-shaped morphology (Fig. S2A). Quantitative analyses demonstrated a significant reduction in exosome abundance following Exo70 knockdown in CRC cells (Fig. S2B, C). To further investigate whether Exo70 influences exosomal cargo composition, we performed nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) to assess both exosome concentration and particle size. While Exo70 knockdown markedly reduced exosome numbers, no significant differences in exosome size distribution were observed (Fig. S2D). Consistently, the expression of classical exosomal markers was significantly decreased in Exo70-deficient RKO and HCT116 cells (Fig. S2E). Notably, Exo70 protein was also detected within isolated exosomes, suggesting that Exo70 itself may be incorporated into exosomal cargo.

To determine whether apatinib regulates exosome secretion via Exo70, we next examined the effects of apatinib on Exo70 expression. Apatinib treatment resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in Exo70 protein levels (Fig. 5A), while Exo70 mRNA expression remained unchanged (Fig. 5B), indicating that apatinib does not affect Exo70 transcription but rather promotes its post-translational regulation. Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays confirmed that apatinib accelerated Exo70 protein degradation (Fig. 5C). Further mechanistic investigations revealed that apatinib enhanced Exo70 degradation through the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway (Fig. 5D). To further validate the role of Exo70 in apatinib-mediated inhibition of exosome secretion, we established CRC cell lines with doxycycline (Dox)-inducible overexpression of Exo70. Following apatinib treatment, the expression of multiple exosomal markers, including Alix, CD63, TSG101, CD9, and CD81, was markedly reduced. Importantly, Dox-induced re-expression of Exo70 significantly rescued the apatinib-induced reduction of these exosomal markers (Fig. 5E, F). Discussion

In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have gained substantial attention as a cornerstone of cancer therapy. Immunotherapy targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis has demonstrated significant clinical efficacy across a broad spectrum of malignancies and has become an essential treatment modality alongside chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and anti-angiogenic therapy39–41. Accordingly, clinical investigations evaluating the combination of apatinib with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors have been increasing18,32,42. However, for patients with metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC), therapeutic options after progression on PD-1/PD-L1–based regimens remain poorly defined, and robust evidence supporting effective post-immunotherapy strategies is still limited.

Our team conducted a single-arm exploratory clinical study of camrelizumab (PD-1 inhibitor) in combination with apatinib for the treatment of advanced mCRC in the third line and beyond (NCT04067986). In this Phase 2 trial, camrelizumab combined with apatinib demonstrated promising antitumor activity in patients with advanced pMMR/MSS mCRC who had failed at least two lines of chemotherapy with manageable toxicity. In the present study, although no objective responses were observed with the combination of camrelizumab and apatinib in patients with pMMR/MSS mCRC (an ORR of 0%), the median PFS was 4.3 months and the median OS was 18.3 months. This suggests that the combination regimen may still confer survival benefits for a subset of patients through disease stabilization.Previous studies have shown that patients with mCRC treated with camrelizumab combined with apatinib had better survival outcomes compared to those treated with apatinib alone21,43. Our study also observed similar results. Substantial heterogeneity in treatment outcomes has been observed across prior studies in this setting. The REGOMUNE study, which evaluated regorafenib plus avelumab, also reported an ORR of 0%, with a median PFS of 3.6 months and a median OS of 10.8 months, showing a trend consistent with our findings44. In contrast, a phase II clinical trial (NCT04745130) assessing regorafenib combined with sintilimab in 103 patients with MSS mCRC demonstrated an ORR of 21.4%, a median PFS of 4.1 months, and a median OS of 14.1 months16. This heterogeneity suggests that the efficacy of TKIs targeting VEGFRs in combination with ICIs may be highly dependent on factors such as the specific TKIs and ICIs agents used, patient baseline characteristics, and the tumor immune microenvironment.

Beyond clinical efficacy, we systematically explored the underlying mechanisms by which apatinib enhances the therapeutic effects of PD-1 blockade. Using MSS CRC mouse models, we confirmed that apatinib significantly alters immune cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment (TME). Accumulating evidence indicates that tumor cells actively remodel the TME and promote tumor growth, invasion, and immune evasion largely through the secretion of exosomes45,46. Tumor-derived exosomal PD-L1 is functionally analogous to membrane-bound PD-L1, as it can engage PD-1 on T cells, suppress T cell activation, and facilitate immune escape47. Although a previous study has suggested that apatinib inhibits exosome secretion in CRC24, the immunological consequences of this effect have not been fully elucidated. In the present study, we observed a coordinated reduction in PD-L1 and CD63 levels in CRC-derived exosomes following apatinib treatment. Functional in vitro assays further demonstrated that exosomes derived from apatinib-treated attenuated T cell apoptosis, restored PBMC activation, and reversed macrophage polarization toward an immunosuppressive phenotype. These findings suggest that apatinib reduces exosomal PD-L1 within the TME by suppressing exosome secretion from CRC cells, thereby alleviating PD-1/PD-L1–mediated immune inhibition and enhancing antitumor immune responses.

Mechanistically, the docking and fusion of multivesicular bodies (MVBs) with the plasma membrane share key similarities with exocyst-mediated vesicular trafficking. Building on our previous work demonstrating a critical role for Exo70 in MVB docking and exosome secretion in pancreatic cancer31, we investigated whether Exo70 exerts a similar function in CRC. We found that Exo70 knockdown in CRC cells markedly impaired MVB docking and fusion with the plasma membrane, resulting in reduced exosome secretion. Importantly, apatinib promoted Exo70 degradation via the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway, leading to a concomitant decrease in exosome release. Restoration of Exo70 expression following apatinib treatment partially rescued exosome secretion, providing direct evidence that apatinib regulates exosome release in an Exo70-dependent manner. Furthermore, consistent with previous reports identifying an interaction between Exo70 and the SNARE protein SNAP23 during vesicle tethering and membrane fusion38, we demonstrated that Exo70 interacts with SNAP23 in CRC cells. Apatinib treatment significantly disrupted the co-localization of Exo70 and SNAP23, suggesting that apatinib interferes with exocyst–SNARE complex assembly by reducing Exo70 availability, thereby inhibiting exosome secretion at the level of vesicle fusion. We acknowledge that analysis of paired patient plasma samples was not included in the current mechanistic study due to limited sample availability and high inter-individual heterogeneity of circulating exosomes, which will require larger cohorts for meaningful validation in future investigations.

In summary, this study provides preliminary insights into the potential value of combining apatinib with a PD-1 inhibitor in patients with pMMR/MSS mCRC, both from a clinical and mechanistic perspective. Clinically, although the combination regimen did not yield objective responses, the observed trends in PFS and OS were consistent with those reported in the REGOMUNE study, suggesting that the combination may still confer survival benefits through disease control. Mechanistically, our findings indicate that apatinib may modulate exosome secretion via the regulation of Exo70, thereby reducing exosomal PD-L1 levels in the tumor microenvironment. This reduction likely attenuates immune evasion mediated by the PD-L1/PD-1 interaction, ultimately enhancing the antitumor immune response of PD-1 inhibitors. These mechanistic insights offer a novel perspective for understanding the clinical efficacy of this combination therapy.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small (n = 19), and the single-arm design precludes a direct assessment of the independent contribution of the ICIs to the combination regimen. Second, the mechanistic findings are primarily derived from in vitro and animal models and require further validation in clinical samples. Future studies should incorporate biomarker analyses to identify patient populations most likely to benefit from this combination strategy.

Methods

Clinical cohorts

This study was a single-arm, single-center clinical trial conducted at Zhongshan Hospital of Xiamen University (Study ID: Arise-FJ-R301). Eligible patients had histologically confirmed colorectal cancer, had previously received at least two lines of systemic therapy, and had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–1. The key eligibility criteria included: age ≥ 18 years; histopathologically confirmed recurrent or metastatic colorectal adenocarcinoma; ECOG performance status 0–1; and prior failure of at least two lines of standard therapy. The key exclusion criteria included: history of other malignancies within the past 5 years (with the exception of cured skin cancer and cervical carcinoma in situ); receipt of systemic chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgical intervention, or hormonal therapy within 2 weeks prior to enrollment, or prior treatment with apatinib mesylate; and dMMR/MSI-H CRC patients. Among the 19 enrolled patients, all exhibited a proficient mismatch repair/microsatellite-stable (pMMR/MSS) phenotype.

This was a single-arm, open-label phase II study. The sample size was initially calculated based on the primary endpoint of progression-free survival (PFS). Based on previous studies, we hypothesized that treatment with apatinib plus camrelizumab would improve the median PFS to 4.0 months, corresponding to a hazard ratio of 0.625, assuming a historical control median PFS of 2.5 months. Using a one-sample log-rank test with a one-sided α of 0.05 and 80% power, and accounting for an accrual period of 12 months and a minimum follow-up of 6 months, the required number of PFS events was estimated to be 48. Considering the study design, the censoring rate was expected to be approximately 14%. Therefore, 56 evaluable patients were required. After adjusting for a 10% dropout rate, the final target sample size was set at 62 patients. However, due to the exploratory nature of this study and the practical feasibility of patient accrual in this heavily pretreated population, a total of 19 patients were ultimately enrolled.

All patients received camrelizumab at a dose of 200 mg administered intravenously on days 1 and 15, in combination with oral apatinib at a dose of 250 mg once daily on days 1–28. Each treatment cycle lasted 4 weeks, and therapy was continued until disease progression or the occurrence of intolerable treatment-related adverse events.

The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the time from treatment initiation to the first documented evidence of disease progression or death from any cause. Secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), defined as the time from treatment initiation to death from any cause, and disease control rate (DCR), defined as the proportion of patients achieving complete response, partial response, or stable disease following treatment. As of the data cutoff date, all patients were evaluable for efficacy analysis. Tumor responses were assessed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST), version 1.1. The safety of the investigational drug was evaluated based on the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0.

This trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04067986; registered August 20, 2019). The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital (Approval No.: ChiECRC T20190218) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national regulations governing human research. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. For participants unable to provide consent independently, informed consent was obtained from legally authorized representatives in accordance with institutional policies.

Cell culture

RKO (RRID: CVCL_0504), HCT116 (RRID: CVCL_0291), THP-1 (RRID: CVCL_0006), Jurkat T (RRID: CVCL_0367) and 293 T (RRID: CVCL_0063) cell lines were all purchased from the Cell Bank of Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai Institute of Cell Biology). The murine colon carcinoma cell line CT26-Luc was kindly provided by Prof Xiaoting Hong (School of Medicine, Xiamen University, Xiamen, China). RKO and 293 T cells were cultured in DMEM medium (10566016, GIBCO, USA). HCT116 cells were grown in McCoy’s 5a medium (12330031, GIBCO, USA). CT26-Luc, THP-1, Jurkat T, and PBMCs cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (11875093, GIBCO, USA). All cells were cultured with 10% High quality fetal bovine serum (A3160901, GIBCO, USA) in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

Tumor models

Female BALB/c mice (RRID: IMSR_CRL:028, 5-6 weeks old) were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Center. Experimental mice were housed in individual ventilated cages under specific-pathogen-free conditions. Mice were acclimatized for at least 7 days before experimentation to reduce stress-related variability. All animal care and experimental protocols were carried out according to the Chinese Animal Management Rules of the Ministry of Health. All animal studies were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Xiamen University (XMULAC20220195). All methods used for animal experiments were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. In animal experiments, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (100 mg kg−1). Euthanasia was performed by CO₂ oversaturation, in strict accordance with institutional and international animal welfare guidelines. Each mouse was considered one experimental unit. Animals were monitored daily for general health, body weight, and signs of distress throughout the study. Investigators performing tumor measurements and imaging analyses were blinded to group allocation.

Establishment of subcutaneous tumor model

A total of 5 × 105 CT26-Luc cells were inoculated into the right axilla of BALB/C mice. 10 days later, solid tumors with a diameter of 1 cm developed. Mice were included if they successfully developed subcutaneous tumors.

Establishment of the orthotopic colorectal cancer model

An orthotopic colorectal cancer (CRC) mouse model was established using a tumor fragment implantation approach. Mice bearing subcutaneous tumors were euthanized, and the skin was sterilized prior to tumor dissection. Excised tumor tissues were immersed in pre-cooled sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), carefully trimmed to remove surrounding connective tissue, and sectioned into approximately 1 mm3 fragments.

A separate cohort of BALB/c mice was anesthetized via inhalational anesthesia and placed in the supine position with limbs secured. A 1 cm incision was made in the lower left abdomen, and the colon was gently exteriorized. A single prepared tumor fragment was randomly selected and implanted onto the surface of the colon using a purse-string suture technique. The colon was then returned to the abdominal cavity, and the abdominal wall was closed by suturing. Following recovery from anesthesia, mice were transferred to a specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal facility for routine husbandry.

Seven days after surgery, in vivo bioluminescence imaging was performed to confirm successful tumor engraftment. Mice without detectable tumor signals were excluded prior to randomization. Animals with confirmed tumor establishment were subsequently randomized into experimental groups to initiate treatment protocols. Mice were assigned to treatment groups in an alternating order based on tumor burden to ensure balanced baseline tumor size across groups. Each experimental group consisted of 5 mice (n = 5 per group).

Sample sizes for animal experiments were determined based on prior experience and previously published studies employing CT26 tumor models; no formal a priori statistical power calculation was performed. The exact number of animals allocated to each experimental group, as well as the total number of mice used in each experiment, is provided in the corresponding figure legends. The primary outcome measure was tumor burden, assessed by serial in vivo bioluminescence imaging over a 21 day experimental period.

Humane endpoints were predefined to minimize animal suffering. Mice were euthanized if body weight loss exceeded 20%, if tumors exhibited ulceration or necrosis, or if animals showed signs of severe distress, including reduced mobility or impaired feeding behavior. All remaining mice were euthanized on day 21 following tumor implantation, and tissues were collected for subsequent analyses and statistical evaluation. All experimental procedures and outcome assessments were conducted according to predefined protocols to ensure consistency across experimental groups. This study was performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines for the reporting of animal research.

Histologic analysis

Tumor tissues were fixed in formalin, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and baked. Tissue sections were subsequently deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval. After blocking with 2% goat serum, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibodies against CD34 (RRID: AB_2864384; ab185238, Abcam, USA) and CD163 (RRID: AB_2722503; ab182422, Abcam, USA). On the following day, sections were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) working solution and examined under a light microscope.

PBMCs separation and cell culture

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained from three healthy volunteers. PBMCs were isolated from 5 mL of peripheral venous blood by density gradient centrifugation. Briefly, blood samples diluted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were carefully layered onto Ficoll reagent (PC8610, Solarbio, China) and centrifuged at 400 × g for 30 min at room temperature. The PBMC layer was collected and washed twice with PBS.

For T cell activation, a total of 3 × 106 PBMCs were incubated with 20 μL of CD3/CD28 magnetic beads (A25789NFB, Abclonal, China) and recombinant interleukin-2 (IL-2; 100 U/mL; RP01039, Abclonal, China) in 24-well plates for 48 h. Following activation, cells were divided into three experimental groups: a control group, a wild-type CRC cell-derived exosome-treated group, and an apatinib-treated CRC cell-derived exosome-reated group. Cells were subsequently cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Flow cytometry analysis

Processed PBMCs were centrifuged and collected, then divided into two tubes per well. Each tube was incubated with 0.5 μL of BV650-labeled Anti-Human CD3 (RRID: AB_2573522, 416-0038-42, Thermo, USA) / APC -labeled Anti-Human CD4 (RRID: AB_2562612, 317416, Biolegend, USA)/ FITC -labeled Anti-Human CD8a (RRID: AB_10063406, 301006, Biolegend, USA)/ PE-labeled Anti-Human CD25 (RRID: AB_465610, 12-0259-42, Thermo, USA) and CD3 /CD4 /CD8a /PE-labeled Anti-Human CD69 (RRID:AB_465751, 12-0699-42, Thermo, USA) antibodies, respectively, in the dark at 4 °C for 30 min. The expression levels of CD25 and CD69 were analyzed using flow cytometry.

For tumor tissue processing, sterilely dissected tumor tissues were placed in a sterile petri dish and finely minced using ophthalmic scissors. The tissue was ground to achieve a single-cell suspension in the culture medium. The suspension was filtered through a 300 mesh stainless steel cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension of the tumor tissue. Cell counting was performed, and the cell density was adjusted to 2 × 106 cells per 100 μL. 50 μL of the cell suspension was transferred to an EP tube and incubated with 0.5 μL of FITC-labeled Anti-Mouse CD11b (RRID: AB_312789, 101205, Biolegend, USA) and APC-labeled Anti-Mouse CD206 (RRID: AB_10640380, 141707, Biolegend, USA). Another 50 μL of the cell suspension was transferred to a separate EP tube and incubated with 0.5 μL of FITC-labeled Anti-Mouse CD3 (RRID: AB_312685, 100204, Biolegend, USA) and APC-labeled Anti-Mouse CD8 (RRID: AB_312750, 100712, Biolegend, USA). Both sets of cells were incubated in the dark at 4 °C for 30 min. Following incubation, 500 μL of staining buffer was added to resuspend the cells, and flow cytometry analysis was performed.

Cell apoptosis assay

Jurkat T cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested, counted, and seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. The cells were then treated with CRC cell-derived exosomes and apatinib-treated CRC cell-derived exosomes. An equal volume of PBS was used as a control. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Post-incubation, the cells were collected and resuspended in 100 μL of PBS. Subsequently, 5 μL each of annexin V-FITC and PI dyes (40303ES20, Yeason, China) were added. The cells were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. Following this, the cells were resuspended in 300 μL PBS and analyzed using flow cytometry.

Cytokine assay

After treating PBMCs for 48 hours, the culture supernatant was collected. The levels of TNF-α and IFN-γ in the supernatant were then measured using specific ELISA kits (RK00030, RK00019, Abclonal, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Extraction of exosomes

Cells were seeded in 15 cm culture dishes and allowed to grow to the logarithmic phase. After removal of the culture medium, cells were washed three times with 5 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at room temperature. Serum-free medium was then added to the control group, while serum-free medium supplemented with apatinib was added to the treatment group. After 48 h of incubation, cell culture supernatants were collected for exosome isolation.

Exosomes were isolated from conditioned media by sequential differential centrifugation. Briefly, collected supernatants were first centrifuged at 300 × g for 10–15 min at 4 °C to remove residual cells. The supernatants were transferred to new tubes and centrifuged at 2000 × g for 15–25 min at 4°C to eliminate dead cells and large cellular debris. The resulting supernatants were then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 50–60 min at 4 °C to remove large vesicles and protein aggregates. Subsequently, the clarified supernatants were transferred into ultracentrifuge tubes compatible with a Beckman ultracentrifuge (L-100XP, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) and centrifuged at 120,000 × g for 1.5–3 h at 4 °C.

Following ultracentrifugation, the supernatants were carefully discarded, and the exosome pellets were resuspended in 50-150 μL of pre-cooled PBS at 4°C by gentle pipetting. The resuspended exosomes were subsequently used for downstream experiments.

Western blot

Western blot was performed as previously described in ref. 31. Antibody Exo70 (RRID: AB_10861503, ab118792), Alix (RRID: AB_2864376, ab186429) and TSG101 (RRID: AB_10699469, ab133586) were purchased from Abcam (USA). β-actin (RRID: AB_262011, a3854) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). CD63 (RRID: AB_627812, sc-5275) and B7-1(RRID: AB_2819118, sc-376012) was purchased from Santa Cruz (USA). PD-L1 (RRID: AB_2687655, 13684) was purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (USA). F-actin (RRID: AB_10708040, PHDR1) was purchased from Cytoskeleton (USA). Molecular weight markers were visualized using a prestained protein ladder (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 26616). For Fig. 5C, chemiluminescent signals of Exo70 and β-actin in HCT116 cells were visualized using X-ray film.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After fixation, cells were washed three times with PBS and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Non-specific binding was blocked with bovine serum albumin (BSA; #A610903, Sangyo Biologics, Shanghai, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were then incubated with the indicated primary antibodies (1:100 dilution) overnight at 4 °C.

Following primary antibody incubation, cells were washed with PBS and incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies, anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 594 (1:500; Thermo Fisher Scientific) or anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500; Thermo Fisher Scientific), for 4 hours at room temperature in the dark. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime). Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 880, ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with a 60 × oil-immersion objective.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis

The extracted exocytosis was resuspended in 1 ml of PBS in a quartz cuvette. Exosome size and number were detected using a nanoparticle size analyser (Zetasizer Nano ZS90, Malvern Panalytica, UK).

Construction of vectors and stable cell lines

Construction of vectors and stable cell lines was performed as previously described in ref. 31.

Statistical analysis

The median PFS and the respective 95% confidence intervals were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and the P values were estimated using a two-sided log-rank test. All statistical data were calculated and analyzed by R Software v.4.3.3 (The R Project for Statistical Computing, www.r-project.org) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (RRID:SCR_002798), and univariate and multi-factor analysis was conducted by paired T-test. Statistical differences were represented by ns and *, ns indicates no statistical significance, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.
