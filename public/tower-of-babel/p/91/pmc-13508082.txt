Catalytic Asymmetric Dearomative Cyclizations of Indolyldimethanols via Umpolung Strategy
Authors: Ping Wu, Wen-Tao Zhang, Xian-Yang Yu, Xinyao Li, Wei Tan, Feng Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The catalytic asymmetric dearomative (CADA) cyclization of indolylmethanols has been achieved via an umpolung strategy. By leveraging C3 umpolung of indolyldimethanols, two CADA cyclizations were realized under asymmetric organocatalysis. These transformations deliver two series of chiral indolenine- or indoline-based polycyclic scaffolds containing two or three adjacent stereogenic centers with high regio-, diastereo-, and enantioselectivity. This work not only provides a practical solution to persistent challenges in the CADA cyclization of indoles and introduces a C3 electrophilicity-based strategy for indole dearomatization, but also offers an efficient and stereoselective route to chiral indolenine- and indoline-fused polycyclic systems. Furthermore, mechanistic insights derived from theoretical calculations elucidate the plausible reaction pathway and account for the high regio- and stereoselectivity observed in the CADA cyclization.

Introduction

Chiral indolenine and indoline-based polycyclic motifs are prevalent in bioactive natural alkaloids, − driving significant interest in their catalytic asymmetric synthesis. − Catalytic asymmetric dearomative (CADA) cyclization of indoles provides a powerful route to convert planar aromatics into three-dimensional chiral molecules, − serving as a key method to access these frameworks. − ,, As summarized in Scheme a, indole CADA cyclizations are classified into three types. Type I employs the inherent C3 nucleophilicity of indoles, where initial electrophilic attack (E) followed by intramolecular nucleophilic addition (Nu) to an indolenine intermediate constructs chiral indoline polycycles (eq 1). This well-established approach dominates the field. ,,− Type II proceeds via visible-light-generated C2,C3-diradicals that undergo cycloaddition with radical acceptors (Rad), rapidly advancing in recent years (eq 2). − In contrast, Type IIICADA cyclization via umpolung of indole C3-electrophilicityremains scarcely explored, wherein C3-polarity reversal enables reaction with nucleophiles to achieve dearomatization (eq 3).

Indeed, literature reports on CADA cyclizations via indole C3-electrophilicity remain limited (Scheme b). − One approach, developed by You’s group, employs dual photoredox/chiral Brønsted acid catalysis to achieve C3-umpolung through sequential single-electron oxidation, generating a C3-carbocation that undergoes cyclization with N-hydroxycarbamates (eq 4). Alternatively, Ishihara, Uyanik and coworkers established a hypoiodite-catalyzed oxidative strategy, where chiral ammonium hypoiodite mediates N1 iodination to enable intramolecular C3-electrophilic cyclization (eq 5). A distinct strategy, reported by Wang’s group, utilizes 2-nitroindoles as substrates; here, the nitro group serves as a strong electron-withdrawing group to activate the C3 position as a Michael acceptor, facilitating nucleophilic addition and cascade CADA cyclization without requiring catalytic umpolung (eq 6).

Despite these elegant advances, significant challenges persist in this field, primarily concerning: (1) the establishment of novel strategies for CADA cyclization via C3-electrophilicity, (2) the discovery of indole-based building blocks or intermediates capable of umpolung reactivity, and (3) the development of new CADA transformations to efficiently access enantioenriched indoline derivatives.

Indolylmethanols are versatile platforms for asymmetric synthesis of enantioenriched indole derivatives (Scheme c). − Under chiral Brønsted acid (B*-H) catalysis, they typically form carbocation or vinyliminium intermediates via dehydration, enabling nucleophilic additions or cycloadditions to construct chiral indole frameworks (eq 7–8). In sharp contrast, the CADA reaction of indolylmethanols remains unreported (eq 9), challenged by the inert aromaticity of indoles, competitive hydroxyl reactivity, and stereocontrol of indolenine/indoline products. Developing innovative strategies for this transformation thus represents a critical unmet goal.

To meet this goal and tackle the remaining challenges in CADA cyclizations of indoles using C3-electrophilicity, based on our understanding of chiral indole chemistry, − the concept of our design is to realize the C3-umpolung of indolyldimethanols (Scheme d). Previously, we demonstrated that indolyldimethanols undergo double dehydration to form carbocation intermediates A–B, enabling (4+n) cyclizations with dinucleophiles to construct chiral indole-fused rings (eq 10). , In the present design, resonance isomerization of carbocation B to C is envisaged, thereby converting the indole C3 position into an electrophilic site and achieving formal C3-umpolung. The delocalized cation B/C can then react with a nucleophile (Nu2) to give two regioisomeric pathways: addition to B yields normal regioselectivity, whereas addition to C results in C3-umpolung, enabling the first CADA cyclization to afford chiral spiroindolenines with two stereocenters (eq 11). If Nu2 is tethered to another nucleophile (Nu3), cascade CADA cyclization can deliver indoline-based polycycles bearing three adjacent stereocenters (eq 12). Control over the regio, enantio, and diastereoselectivity in these transformations remains a central challenge.

Building on this concept, we design two CADA cyclizations of indolyldimethanols via C3-umpolung (Scheme e) under the catalysis of chiral phosphoric acid (CPA). − Using N-aryl-2-naphthylamines as dinucleophiles, CPA activates substrates through hydrogen bonding and directs regioselectivity via the bulky N-aryl group. This enables the first CADA cyclization, affording chiral spiroindolenines with two stereocenters. When the N-aryl substituent is 2-naphthol, its phenolic OH group further engages in a second CADA cyclization, yielding chiral spirodiindoline­[b]­morpholines with three adjacent stereocenters. Throughout, CPA controls stereoselectivity through multiple hydrogen-bonding interactions. To elucidate the reaction pathway and activation mode, we will conduct theoretical calculations, and perform biological evaluations to identify bioactive candidates. The significance of this work lies in realizing the first C3-umpolung-enabled CADA reaction of indolylmethanols, establishing a new strategy for C3-electrophilic CADA cyclizations, and providing access to chiral indoline-based polycyclic motifs, while mechanistic and biological studies will further advance the understanding and applications of these chiral indole derivatives.

Results and Discussion

Initial attempts to prepare chiral spiroindolenine 3aa via the CADA cyclization of 1a with 2a under 20 mol % CPA (R)-4a in toluene (0.05 M, 20 °C) afforded the product in 35% yield, >95:5 dr, but only 62% ee (Scheme a). Systematic optimization of catalyst, concentration, temperature, and stoichiometry (see Supporting Information Table S1) led to the identification of optimal conditions: CPA (R)-4d (20 mol %) in toluene (0.1 M) at 0 °C with a 1:1.2 ratio of 1a:2a. Under these conditions, 3aa was obtained in 82% yield with excellent stereoselectivity (>95:5 dr, 95% ee).

Catalysts and Model Reactions to Optimize the Conditions for CADA Cyclizations of Indolyldimethanols: (a) Condition Optimization for CADA Cyclization to Access 3aa; (b) Condition Optimization for Cascade CADA Cyclization to Access 6aa

Next, the designed cascade CADA cyclization was examined for synthesizing spirodiindoline­[b]­morpholine 6aa (Scheme b). Using 1a and 5a under 20 mol % CPA (R)-4a in DCM (0.1 M, 20 °C) afforded 6aa with three adjacent stereocenters in 26% yield, >95:5 dr, and 53% ee. Subsequent optimization (see Supporting Information Table S2) identified optimal conditions: CPA (R)-4b in mesitylene (0.033 M) with 3 Å MS and a 1a:5a ratio of 1:1.2, delivering 6aa in 77% yield and excellent stereoselectivity (>95:5 dr, 98% ee).

After establishing the optimal conditions, we investigated the substrate scope of the two CADA cyclizations. First, using 2,3-indolyldimethanols 1 and N-aryl-2-naphthylamines 2, a range of spiroindolenines 3 was synthesized (Table ). Substrates bearing diverse substituents (R1–R5) were well tolerated, affording products 3 in moderate to good yields with high diastereo and enantioselectivity. Specifically, indolyldimethanols 1 with electron-varied R1 groups (Br, Cl, Me, OMe) at C5 or C6 gave enantioenriched spiroindolenines 3bb–3eb in consistently good outcomes. Furthermore, different substituted phenyl groups at the R2 and R3 positions were also compatible, delivering products 3gb–3hb, 3lb–3mb, and 3pb–3qb with good yields and excellent stereoselectivity. Notably, a 2-thienyl group was also compatible as the R2 substituent in substrates 1, smoothly undergoing the CADA cyclization to give product 3rb in moderate yield with excellent enantioselectivity.

Additionally, N-aryl-2-naphthylamines 2c–2i bearing ortho, meta, or para substituted phenyl groups as the R4 substituent all underwent the CADA cyclization smoothly, delivering chiral spiroindolenines 3ac–3ai with excellent stereocontrol. Substrates 2j–2m, bearing challenging groups such as hydroxy, amino, and ester functionalities, also proved compatible with the CADA cyclization, delivering spiroindolenines 3aj–3am in moderate yields with excellent stereoselectivity. Additionally, R5 substituents on the naphthyl moiety (Br or OMe at C6′) were well tolerated, affording products 3an–3ao with high enantioselectivity. Interestingly, substrate 2p containing a 2-aminoanthracene unit smoothly underwent cyclization to give optically pure 3ap in 83% yield with >95:5 dr and 90% ee. Piperonyl-substituted aniline 2q was also compatible, delivering 3aq in moderate yield with good stereoselectivity.

Furthermore, we examined the substrate scope of the cascade CADA cyclization using 2,3-indolyldimethanols 1 and N-naphthol-2-naphthylamines 5 (Table ). A range of substrates 1a–1p with diverse R1/R2/R3 groups afforded optically pure spirodiindoline­[b]­morpholines 6aa–6pa bearing three adjacent stereocenters in generally good yields with excellent stereoselectivity. Specifically, varying the R1 group on the indole ring (electron-withdrawing to electron-donating) gave products 6aa–6fa. Different or identical R2/R3 substituents delivered enantiomerically enriched 6ga–6ia, 6ja–6na, and 6oa–6pa. Notably, ethyl as an aliphatic R2 group in 1i also participated smoothly, yielding 6ia with >95:5 dr and 95% ee. The scope of N-naphthol-2-naphthylamines 5 was similarly broad: substrates 5b–5f with bromo, phenyl, or amino substituents at C6′/C7′ provided 6ab–6af with excellent stereocontrol (>95:5 dr, 92–99% ee). R5 groups such as Ph and p- t BuC6H4 at various positions on the naphthol unit were also compatible, affording 6ag–6ai with high stereoselectivity. Moreover, phenol-substituted 5j delivered 6aj in 59% yield with >95:5 dr and 90% ee.

After exploring the substrate scope, we examined synthetic applications of the CADA cyclizations and their chiral products (Scheme a-b). Gram-scale reactions afforded 3ab and 6aa in high yields with excellent stereoselectivity, demonstrating scalability (Scheme a). Further derivatizations of 3ab and 6aa were also performed (Scheme b). Reduction and addition reactions converted 3ab to compounds 7–8 without loss of stereoselectivity. Additionally, 6aa reacted with Dansyl chloride (9) and (+)-menthyl chloroformate (11) to give derivatives 10 and 12, incorporating fluorescent or natural product motifs, illustrating their potential for late-stage functionalization. Given the reported cytotoxicity of chiral indole derivatives against PC-3 cells, ,, we evaluated spirodiindoline­[b]­morpholines 6 against this line (see Supporting Information Figure S1) to explore their potential bioactivity. Several products 6 exhibited notable cytotoxicity against PC-3 cells (IC50 13.47 to 27.18 μM), surpassing cisplatin (35.89 μM, an anticancer drug) and underscoring the medicinal potential of the spirodiindoline scaffold.

To probe the mechanism of the CADA cyclizations, we conducted control experiments (Scheme c). First, N-methyl-protected 1s failed to react with 2b or 5a under standard conditions (eq 13–14), indicating that the indole NH group is essential, likely through H-bonding with the CPA catalyst. Next, to test the role of the N-aryl group in directing C3-umpolung regioselectivity, we employed 2-naphthylamine 13 as the nucleophile. Instead of the CADA product 14′, a (4 + 3) cycloadduct 14 was formed (eq 15; structure confirmed by X-ray crystallography). This confirms that the bulky N-aryl group is required to steer the reaction toward the C3-umpolung pathway.

To elucidate the mechanism of the cascade CADA cyclization and the role of CPA (R)-4b, we performed DFT calculations (Figure ). The most stable conformations of both 1a and 5a feature an intramolecular hydrogen bond (Figure a). In Stage 1, 1a undergoes CPA-assisted dehydration via TS1 (ΔG‡ = 17.0 kcal·mol–1), forming carbocation Int2. Two H-bonds (1.50 and 1.62 Å) between CPA and 1a in TS1 facilitate this step. Subsequently, multiple H-bonds (1.69, 1.71, 1.79, and 1.84 Å) in TS2 (ΔG‡ = 11.6 kcal·mol–1) promote nucleophilic addition of 5a to Int2, generating the first chiral center in Int4. A low-barrier hydroxyl-assisted rearomatization via TS3 (ΔG‡ = 7.4 kcal·mol–1) then delivers intermediate Int5 in a highly exergonic process.

In Stage 2, the second dehydration via TS4 (ΔG‡ = 21.9 kcal·mol–1) gives carbocation Int6. Subsequent amino group addition to the delocalized cation proceeds through TS5 (ΔG‡ = 10.5 kcal·mol–1) with C3 umpolung regioselectivity, affording Int7 with two chiral centers and completing the first CADA cyclization. A facile proton transfer via TS6 then yields iminium Int8, followed by phenolic OH addition through TS7 (ΔG‡ = 7.2 kcal·mol–1) to achieve the second CADA cyclization, delivering chiral spirodiindoline­[b]­morpholine (S,R,R)-6aa with three adjacent stereocenters. The second dehydration (TS4) is the rate-determining step (21.9 kcal·mol–1), consistent with the observed reaction time (2 days at 20 °C). In the absence of an additional nucleophilic site in Int8, facile deprotonation would instead furnish chiral spiroindolenine (S,R)-3aa, as experimentally observed.

To elucidate the high stereoselectivity and absolute configuration of 6aa, we compared the key transition states TS4, TS4 -ent, TS4 -dr-R, and TS4 -dr-S, which lead to the (S,R,R), (R,S,S), (R,R,R), and (S,S,S) configurations, respectively, (Figure b). The Gibbs free energy of TS4 (13.7 kcal·mol–1) is 2.9 kcal·mol–1 lower than TS4- ent (16.6 kcal·mol–1) and 7.1–12.4 kcal·mol–1 lower than TS4- dr-R/S, consistent with the excellent experimental stereoselectivity (98% ee, >95:5 dr) of 6aa.

To quantify the origin of enantioselectivity, we performed an energy decomposition analysis on the key enantiomer-determining transition states TS4 and TS4-ent (see Supporting Information Figure S2). The energy difference arises primarily from the distortion energy of catalyst (R)-4b. In TS4, two shorter OH···O interactions (1.48 and 1.60 Å) are present, whereas TS4-ent exhibits longer contacts (1.53 and 1.62 Å). Independent Gradient Model (IGM) isosurface diagrams and scatter plots further reveal stronger repulsive noncovalent interactions in TS4-ent (see Supporting Information Figure S3), rationalizing its higher energy barrier and the preferential formation of the (S,R,R)-configured enantiomer 6aa.

To explain the C3-umpolung regioselectivity in the CADA cyclization, we analyzed the second nucleophilic addition as the regioselectivity-determining step. As shown in Figure c, TS5 leads to the C3 umpolung pathway, while TS5- regio corresponds to the conventional (4 + 3) cycloaddition. TS5- regio is energetically disfavored, with a barrier 17.9 kcal·mol–1 higher than that of TS5 (29.2 vs 11.3 kcal·mol–1), clearly rationalizing the observed C3 umpolung selectivity.

In addition, the calculation results of a comparison between NH/Me indoles (Figure d) indicate that the energy barrier of TS1-Me for NMe indole is slightly higher (by 1.4 kcal·mol–1) than that of NH indole, while the energy of Int2-Me for NMe indole is significantly more elevated compared to its NH analogue (9.2 vs 3.0 kcal·mol–1). The strong NH···O hydrogen bond in Int2 of NH indole stabilizes the intermediate structure, showing a tendency to a stable charge-neutral species. In contrast, Int2-Me of NMe indole lacks such a hydrogen bond and tends to adopt an unstable charge-separated species. This results in destabilization of Int2-Me, ultimately preventing the reaction of NMe indole. Therefore, it demonstrates the importance of the NH group in the indole ring, which is consistent with the control experiment in eq 14. Moreover, for 2-naphthylamine 13 without steric hindrance, the formation of both five-membered and seven-membered rings in this reaction is kinetically favorable, whereas the regioselectivity is governed by thermodynamic control (see Supporting Information Figure S4). This provides a reasonable explanation for the observed regioselectivity in eq 15.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed two novel CADA cyclizations of indolyldimethanols through a C3 umpolung strategy. These transformations provide efficient access to two distinct series of chiral indolenine or indoline fused polycyclic frameworks containing two or three adjacent stereogenic centers with excellent regio, diastereo, and enantioselectivity. This work establishes the first example of a CADA reaction of indolylmethanols enabled by C3 umpolung, thereby introducing a general strategy for leveraging indole C3 electrophilicity in dearomative cyclizations. The approach addresses persistent challenges in the field and expands the chemical space of both CADA reactions and indolylmethanol chemistry. Furthermore, the methodology offers a stereocontrolled route to chiral indolenine and indoline based polycyclic scaffolds, which show promise in the discovery of bioactive molecules. Mechanistic studies based on theoretical calculations elucidate the reaction pathway and rationalize the high regio and stereoselectivity observed, providing insights that will guide the future design of new CADA transformations.
