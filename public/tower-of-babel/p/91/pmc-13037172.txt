Endoscopic-assisted transoral atlantoaxial joint release using plasma radiofrequency ablation in management of irreducible atlantoaxial dislocation: a proof-of-concept case series with technical notes
Authors: Zhechen Li, Ruomiao Chen, Rongsheng Chen, Qingcong Zheng, Zhibin Chen, Chuanrong Chen, Weihong Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIrreducible atlantoaxial dislocation (IAAD) is a potentially life-threatening condition and remains surgically challenging. In selected patients, anterior C1-2 release followed by posterior reduction and fusion is effective, but traditional transoral anterior release is technically demanding. Endoscopy and plasma radiofrequency ablation (PRA) may improve visualization and soft tissue handling in a confined corridor, but their combined use for IAAD has been rarely reported. This study aimed to describe an endoscopic-assisted transoral atlantoaxial joint release using PRA and to report preliminary feasibility, procedural safety, and clinical/radiographic outcomes in patients with IAAD.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed five consecutive patients with IAAD who underwent endoscopic-assisted transoral C1–2 release using PRA, followed by posterior reduction and fusion. Clinical outcomes were assessed with the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score and Odom criteria. Radiographic correction was evaluated using the clivus–axial angle (CAA) and cervicomedullary angle (CMA). Perioperative outcomes included operative time, blood loss, airway management, wound healing, and approach-related complications. Fusion was assessed on follow-up computed tomography.ResultsThe mean age was 57.4 years, and the mean follow-up was 33 months. Two patients had restricted mouth opening. Mean total operative time was 229 min (transoral stage 130 min), with a mean estimated blood loss of 170 mL. All patients were extubated in the operating room; none required reintubation, tracheostomy, or intensive care. No cerebrospinal fluid leak, neurological deterioration, posterior pharyngeal wound complication, surgical site infection, dysphagia, or implant-related failure occurred. CAA improved from 117.9° to 143.0°, and CMA improved from 128.5° to 152.9°. Solid fusion was achieved in all cases. JOA scores improved from 8.2 to 14.2, and 80% of patients were rated excellent/good by Odom criteria.ConclusionsThis proof-of-concept series suggests that endoscopic-assisted transoral C1–2 release using PRA can facilitate subsequent posterior reduction and fusion in selected IAAD patients, including those with restricted mouth opening, with encouraging early safety and clinical/radiographic improvement. Given the small retrospective, non-comparative design and the inability to disentangle PRA-related effects from adjunctive drilling, larger multicenter comparative studies are needed to validate these preliminary findings and clarify the specific contribution of PRA within the procedure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03619-3.Keywords: Irreducible atlantoaxial dislocation, Endoscopy, Plasma radiofrequency ablation, Transoral approach, Case series

Bachground

Atlantoaxial dislocation (AAD) is a pathological condition characterized by abnormal instability or misalignment between the atlas (C1) and axis (C2) [1]. It can arise from a variety of etiologies, including congenital malformations, trauma, inflammatory diseases, and neoplasms [2]. The condition poses a serious threat to the medulla oblongata and upper cervical spinal cord; if left untreated, progressive myelopathy, quadriparesis, or even sudden death may occur [3, 4], highlighting the importance of timely diagnosis and effective surgical intervention.

Irreducible atlantoaxial dislocation (IAAD) refers to atlantoaxial dislocation in which satisfactory realignment cannot be achieved after high-weight skull traction under general anesthesia, often due to chronic fibrosis, osteophyte formation, or joint ankylosis around the atlantoaxial joints [5, 6]. Multiple surgical strategies have been described for IAAD, yet there is no consensus regarding the optimal surgical treatment [5, 7–11]. A commonly used strategy is anterior C1–2 joint release to restore mobility, followed by posterior reduction and fixation to achieve stable realignment and arthrodesis [5, 6, 9]. The transoral route provides the most direct ventral access to the craniovertebral junction and has been widely used for anterior release [12, 13]. However, the traditional transoral approach is technically demanding because of a deep and narrow surgical corridor, limited visualization, and close proximity to critical neurovascular structures [14, 15]. Although satisfactory outcomes with acceptable complication rates have been reported in experienced centers [5, 16, 17], adequate exposure may require wide mouth opening and prolonged oropharyngeal retraction, which can be associated with approach-related morbidity, including wound complications and delayed extubation [12, 15]. These limitations underscore the need for technical refinements that improve visualization and potentially reduce soft-tissue trauma while achieving the same surgical goals in IAAD.

Recent advances in minimally invasive spinal surgery have introduced new technologies that may help overcome the limitations of traditional transoral approaches. Endoscopic techniques offer superior illumination and magnification, enabling precise dissection in deep and confined spaces with minimal soft tissue disruption [18–20]. Meanwhile, plasma radiofrequency ablation (PRA) permits controlled tissue ablation and hemostasis at lower thermal profiles than standard electrocautery. This technology ablates soft tissue at 40–70 °C with simultaneous saline irrigation, which minimizes collateral thermal injury to surrounding structures and may reduce edema and postoperative pain [21–23]. Endoscopic-assisted transoral release followed by posterior reduction and fusion has been reported with encouraging outcomes, including the larger cohort by Song et al. [16, 24]. However, evidence regarding the integration of PRA into endoscope-assisted transoral release for IAAD remains limited. Therefore, we describe an endoscopic-assisted transoral atlantoaxial joint release using PRA and report our proof-of-concept experience, focusing on technical notes and clinical and radiological outcomes.

Methods

This case series was reported in accordance with the CARE guidelines; the completed CARE checklist was provided as Supplemental File 1. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital (IEC-FOM-013-2.0). The study was performed under the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Each included patient signed a written informed consent form to participate in this work and to publish personal data and images.

We retrospectively reviewed consecutive patients with IAAD who underwent endoscopic-assisted transoral C1–C2 joint release with PRA followed by posterior reduction and fixation at our hospital between June 2022 and April 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients diagnosed with IAAD, confirmed by unsuccessful realignment under general anesthesia with skull traction (usually one-fifth to one-sixth of the body weight for 10 min) [6]; (2) minimum follow-up of 24 months; and (3) availability of complete clinical records and imaging data. The exclusion criteria were: (1) reducible atlantoaxial dislocation achieving anatomic reduction with skull traction under general anesthesia; (2) AAD secondary to tumor or infection; (3) incomplete clinical data or a follow-up of less than 24 months.

Surgical procedures with technical notes

All the surgeries were performed by the same senior surgeon. Each surgery consisted of two main steps (A schematic overview of the two-stage workflow is provided in Supplementary File 2).

Step 1: endoscopic-assisted transoral atlantoaxial joint release using PRA

Under general anesthesia with orotracheal intubation, the patient was positioned supine with mild head extension. Skull traction was applied with a load not exceeding one-sixth of the patient’s weight. After povidone-iodine preparation of the face, oral cavity, and nasal cavity, the surgical field was draped to allow oral and nasal access. A Davis-Boyle mouth gag was gently placed to open the mouth. Excessive opening was avoided to minimize pressure-related injury to the teeth and tongue. The soft palate was elevated using rubber catheters passed through the nostrils, providing exposure of the posterior oropharyngeal wall. A 4-mm endoscope (0° or 30° lens) (ShenDa, China) was used throughout this step. The endoscope was handheld by the operating surgeon; no endoscope holder was used. The anterior tubercle of C1 was identified by digital palpation and confirmed with lateral fluoroscopy before the mucosal incision. Under endoscopic visualization, a vertical midline incision was made in the posterior pharyngeal wall from the anterior tubercle of C1 to the inferior border of C2, using PRA (Coblation, ArthroCare Corp, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). PRA served as the primary tool for soft-tissue dissection and hemostasis, allowing cutting and coagulation simultaneously. A Coblation wand (EIC5874-01) was set to ablation 5 and coagulation 3, with continuous saline irrigation. Subperiosteal dissection exposed the anterior arch of C1, C2 body and odontoid, and bilateral C1–2 facet joints. Retraction of the incision was provided intermittently by an assistant; a self-retaining retractor was avoided to minimize sustained mucosal compression. PRA was then used for soft-tissue release, including C1–2 facet joint capsular release and ablation of fibrous tissue (see Supplemental Video 1). Based on preoperative computed tomography (CT), bony osteophytes that contributed to irreducibility were identified. A high-speed burr was used to remove these targeted bony impediments, and partial resection of the anterior arch of C1 was performed as needed to facilitate reduction. Each C1–2 facet joint was then gently opened and mobilized with a small curette. Restoration of atlantoaxial alignment was confirmed with lateral fluoroscopy. Hemostasis was achieved by coagulating bleeding bone and soft tissue surfaces with PRA, followed by local packing with hemostatic materials. No intra-articular grafting was performed through the transoral corridor; fusion was achieved via posterior decortication and grafting. The posterior pharyngeal wall was closed in layers with interrupted resorbable sutures, and a nasogastric feeding tube was inserted before completing this step (Fig. 1).

Step 2: posterior reduction, instrumentation, and fusion

The patient was repositioned prone while maintaining skull traction, and a standard posterior midline approach was performed. C1–2 posterior instrumentation and fusion following the Harms technique [25] was used in Patient 1, 3, and 4, whereas occiput-C2 instrumentation and fusion [26] was performed in Patient 2 and 5 due to atlas occipitalization of C1. Final alignment was verified with lateral fluoroscopy. Morselized autogenous cancellous iliac grafts were applied over the decorticated posterior elements of C1-2 (or occiput-C2) to facilitate fusion. No intra-articular bone grafting was performed within the C1–C2 facet joints.

Postoperative management

Extubation was individualized based on preoperative neurological status, respiratory reserve, and intraoperative airway assessment, and was planned in the operating room when patients demonstrated adequate spontaneous ventilation and airway protective reflexes without concern for significant oropharyngeal bleeding or swelling. In this series, all patients were extubated in the operating room immediately after the surgery, and none required delayed extubation or postoperative ventilatory support. Prophylactic intravenous cefuroxime sodium plus metronidazole was administered 30 min before incision and continued for 6 days, following our institutional protocol and a previously published regimen [27]. Enteral nutrition was provided via a nasogastric tube for 7 days. Oral liquid intake was initiated on postoperative Day 8 and gradually advanced to a regular diet. A rigid cervical orthosis was worn for at least 3 months until radiographic evidence of solid fusion was obtained.

Clinical and radiological data evaluation

Clinical data, including patient age, sex, and underlying pathology were retrospectively reviewed. Neurological function was evaluated using the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score preoperatively and at final follow-up [28]. The recovery rate was calculated using the Hirabayashi formula [28]: (postoperative JOA score -preoperative JOA score)/(17 - preoperative JOA score) × 100%. In addition, at the final follow-up, patients also rated their outcomes using the Odom criteria [5] (see Supplemental File 3).

Surgery-related variables, such as operative duration (total and for the transoral step), blood loss, length of hospital stay, and any complications were documented. Specific complications of interest included airway or ventilation issues (need for reintubation or tracheostomy), wound problems (infection or dehiscence of the posterior pharynx), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, neurological deterioration, and instrumentation-related problems. Because no validated grading system exists for postoperative oropharyngeal/tongue edema, we did not perform objective quantification in this retrospective series. Airway-related events (prolonged intubation, reintubation, or tracheostomy) and wound complications were documented as pragmatic clinical surrogates.

Preoperative, postoperative, and follow-up imaging studies, including radiographs, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), were collected and analyzed. The clivus axial angle (CAA) and cervicomedullary angle (CMA) were measured to evaluate craniovertebral alignment. The CAA was defined as the angle between Wackenheim’s line and a line drawn along the posterior margin of the dens and C2 vertebral body [29] and was measured on mid-sagittal CT reconstructions. The CMA was defined as the angle between lines along the ventral surface of the medulla oblongata and along the ventral surface of the upper cervical spinal cord, measured on the midline sagittal T2-weighted MRI image [30]. Measurements were obtained independently by two observers, and the mean of the two values was used for analysis. Bony fusion was deemed successful if follow-up CT demonstrated continuous bridging trabecular bone across the operated segments.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations for continuous variables; counts and percentages for categorical variables) were calculated using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA). Given the small sample size (n = 5), only descriptive statistics were reported, and no hypothesis testing was performed.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between June 2022 and April 2023, 24 patients with AAD were screened and five consecutive patients (3 males and 2 females) met the eligibility criteria. The mean age was 57.4 ± 7.1 years, and the mean follow-up duration was 33.0 ± 4.4 months. Three patients presented with IAAD associated with malunion of odontoid fracture, and two had IAAD combined with basilar invagination. Notably, two patients (Patients 1 and 5) had restricted mouth opening (inter-incisor distance < two fingerbreadths) and an enlarged tongue that obscured the posterior pharyngeal wall, narrowing the operative corridor and making traditional transoral exposure challenging [20]. In these cases, the use of endoscopic assistance provided adequate visualization and working space, enabling successful completion of the procedure (Fig. 2).

Perioperative data

The mean total operative time was 229.0 ± 54.1 min, including 130.0 ± 20.9 min for the transoral stage. Patients with malunited odontoid fracture tended to require a longer transoral stage than those with basilar invagination, which likely reflected the need for more extensive scar release and adjunctive drilling to address ventral bony impediments. Intraoperative blood loss averaged 170.0 ± 67.1 mL. The average length of hospital stay was 9.2 ± 1.9 days, and no patients required intensive care (Table 1).

All patients were extubated immediately after surgery without the need for prolonged endotracheal intubation, and none developed postoperative respiratory distress requiring reintubation or tracheostomy. No airway compromise suggestive of clinically significant tongue/oropharyngeal edema was observed. No posterior pharyngeal wound complications, surgical site infection, CSF leakage, postoperative dysphagia, or neurologic deterioration occurred.

Radiological outcomes

Postoperative imaging confirmed successful reduction and stable fixation in all patients. As shown in Table 2, the mean CAA and CMA improved markedly after surgery. Specifically, the mean CAA increased from 117.9 ± 10.4° preoperatively to 143.0 ± 8.8° postoperatively, and the mean CMA increased from 128.5 ± 12.9° preoperatively to 152.9 ± 9.8° postoperatively, respectively (Figs. 3 and 4). Solid bony fusion was observed on follow-up CT scans, characterized by continuous bridging trabecular bone across the fusion sites. No evidence of implant loosening, hardware failure, or loss of reduction was detected during follow-up.

Clinical outcome

At the final follow-up, all patients showed significant neurological improvement. The mean JOA score increased from 8.2 ± 2.8 preoperatively to 14.2 ± 1.3 at the last follow-up, corresponding to a mean recovery rate of 69.6 ± 7.6%. Based on Hirabayashi’s criteria, this outcome indicates an overall good recovery, with most patients achieving excellent or good results. According to the Odom criteria, clinical outcomes were rated as excellent in two patients (40%), good in two patients (40%), and fair in one patient (20%), with no poor results.

Discussion

In this proof-of-concept case series, we reported the technical feasibility of endoscopic-assisted transoral atlantoaxial joint release using PRA, followed by posterior reduction and instrumented fusion, in five patients with IAAD. Adequate anterior release was achieved in all cases, enabling subsequent posterior reduction and fixation. At the final follow-up, all patients demonstrated solid fusion with improved radiological parameters (CAA and CMA) and neurological status. No major complications such as airway compromise, infection, CSF leakage, neurological deterioration, or dysphagia were observed. Given the retrospective, non-comparative design and small sample size, these findings should be interpreted as feasibility and preliminary safety in selected patients, and comparative effectiveness remains to be determined.

The surgical treatment of IAAD remains technically demanding because of complex anatomy, a deep operative corridor, and proximity to critical neurovascular structures [14, 23]. Although the transoral corridor provides direct access to the craniovertebral junction and is widely used for anterior release [12], adequate visualization in a narrow field often requires wide mouth opening and prolonged oropharyngeal retraction, which may contribute to tongue edema, soft-palate injury, and delayed extubation [12, 15]. Endoscopic assistance provides improved illumination and magnification and may reduce the need for excessive mouth opening and soft-tissue retraction [31]. In our series, endoscopic assistance was particularly helpful when oral access was limited and the posterior pharyngeal wall could not be reliably visualized directly, facilitating landmark identification and C1–2 facet access without widening mouth opening or increasing retraction. This is consistent with prior reports suggesting that endoscopic techniques may extend the applicability of transoral surgery in anatomically constrained settings [24, 32].

All patients in this study were extubated immediately after surgery, and none required reintubation or tracheostomy. No posterior pharyngeal wound complications, surgical site infection, CSF leakage, or dysphagia were recorded. Importantly, postoperative airway and respiratory outcomes after craniovertebral junction surgery are multifactorial and depend on patient factors (such as preoperative neurological status and respiratory reserve) and perioperative management [33, 34], rather than surgical techniques alone; therefore, airway management should remain individualized. Although causal attribution cannot be established in this small retrospective series, the low rate of approach-related complications may plausibly relate to reduced local soft-tissue trauma during the transoral phase. Potential contributors include: (1) low-temperature PRA with irrigation, which may limit collateral thermal injury while providing effective ablation and hemostasis [23]; (2) endoscopic visualization, which may reduce the need for excessive mouth opening and mechanical tongue compression; and (3) gentle, intermittent retraction avoiding continuous pressure from a self-retaining retractor, which may reduce mucosal ischemic compression and support wound healing. Postoperative infection risk is also multifactorial and depends on patient-related factors, contamination control, and perioperative protocols. Antibiotic courses after transoral surgery vary widely, typically ranging from 2 to 6 days [5, 27, 29]. In our practice, cefuroxime plus metronidazole was continued for 6 days, within the upper range reported in the literature.

Published series provide context for our findings. Song et al. [16] reported a retrospective cohort of 59 patients with IAAD treated with either traditional transoral release (n = 35) or endoscopic-assisted transoral release (n = 24), followed by posterior fixation and fusion. Radiological reduction and neurological recovery were comparable between groups, whereas the endoscopic group had lower blood loss, earlier oral intake, and shorter hospital stay. Technically, their endoscopic transoral release was performed through a working cannula with continuous saline irrigation, with both radiofrequency and drilling instruments introduced via the same cannula. In contrast, our transoral phase was performed without a dedicated working cannula; the endoscope was handheld, and instruments were not constrained by a cannula, allowing the use of a broader range of conventional transoral instruments as needed. Yadav et al. reported a prospective series of 34 patients undergoing endoscopic transoral odontoidectomy for IAAD followed by posterior fixation [20]. Similar to our setup, they did not use a dedicated working cannula and achieved immediate extubation in 33 patients; one delayed extubation occurred after difficult intubation, and one CSF leak was managed with lumbar drainage. Because odontoidectomy involves ventral decompression close to the dura, its anterior approach–related risk profile differs from that of C1–2 facet release, which typically avoids manipulation in close proximity to the dura.

In addition to endoscopic-assisted transoral release followed by posterior reduction and fusion, several other endoscopic-assisted surgical strategies have been described for IAAD. Endoscopic-assisted transoral anterior release with anterior fixation and fusion has been reported in selected patients [24], offering the advantage of avoiding patient repositioning and a posterior incision. However, anterior constructs may provide less powerful reduction control in rigid deformities; incomplete reduction may necessitate odontoidectomy to achieve adequate cervicomedullary decompression, increasing the risk of dural tear, CSF leakage, and infectious complications [24]. The endoscopic endonasal corridor is often considered less disruptive to oral structures and may allow earlier extubation and oral intake compared with transoral surgery [11, 35]. Nevertheless, the endonasal corridor is narrower and instrument maneuverability is more limited, increasing technical complexity. Endoscopic-assisted transcervical atlantoaxial joint release can avoid transoral or endonasal contamination and may reduce infection risk [7, 10]. However, patient selection is critical: this transcervical technique may be unsuitable for patients with high basilar invagination, a mandibular angle lies below C2, or traumatic or other lesions involving the C1/C2 facet joints [10]. Recently, a posterior-only reduction strategy has gained popularity for selected patients with IAAD [36, 37]. This technique involves posterior C1–2 facet joint release, stepwise facet distraction, placement of intra-articular bone graft or a cage, and definitive posterior instrumentation. Facet distraction may release the anterior tension band and obviate the need for anterior release in selected patients [36, 37]. However, this technique requires meticulous handling around the vertebral artery and may be less effective when reduction is impeded by a rigid anterior bony block or extensive ventral scarring; in such settings, an anterior release may be required to enable reduction [17].

We did not adopt the posterior-only strategy because several patients had prominent anterior osteophytes between the C1 anterior arch and the odontoid, constituting a rigid anterior block that is difficult to address via a posterior-only corridor; therefore, we favored an anterior release to remove the dominant ventral constraint. Once the ventral impediment is addressed, definitive stabilization is still required to achieve and maintain reduction. Accordingly, we performed posterior instrumentation and fusion, as posterior fixation provides reliable reduction control in rigid deformities, and anterior C1–2 fixation instrumentation was not available at our institution during the study period. With respect to fusion, we acknowledge that C1-2 intra-articular fusion is regarded as providing a more reliable fusion construct when combined with rigid screw–rod fixation. However, because the anterior C1–C2 joint capsule is opened during the transoral stage and may introduce a contamination concern, we avoided intra-articular grafting either through the transoral corridor or by opening the posterior C1–2 facet joints for graft placement. Instead, graft material was applied over the decorticated posterior elements of C1–C2 (or occiput–C2) during the posterior stage, and all patients achieved solid fusion at final follow-up.

Our incremental contribution lies in the anterior workflow: handheld endoscopic–assisted transoral C1–2 facet release without a dedicated working cannula, using plasma radiofrequency ablation mainly for soft tissue release and hemostasis, with adjunctive drilling when bony impediments are present. Because our workflow differs in both corridor and key procedural components from the aforementioned strategies, our findings should be interpreted as proof-of-concept feasibility and preliminary safety, pending validation in larger comparative cohorts.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it is a small retrospective case series without a control group, which limits statistical power and precludes comparative effectiveness versus conventional transoral techniques or other established strategies. Second, all procedures were performed by a single senior surgeon, introducing potential selection and expertise bias; multicenter studies are needed to confirm reproducibility. Third, none of the five consecutive patients presented with vertically oriented C1–2 facets or severe basilar invagination with marked cranial migration of C2; therefore, the applicability of our approach to these complex phenotypes remains uncertain. For these complex phenotypes, delineation and opening of the C1–2 joint space may be difficult, careful preoperative CT-based assessment of facet orientation and the joint line is essential, and navigated osteotomy techniques may be considered. Fourth, operative duration is a practical limitation. As an initial five-case experience, the mean transoral stage lasted 130 min, which may be longer than traditional open transoral release in experienced hands. With increasing experience, operative time may decrease. Finally, because adjunctive drilling was performed when bony impediments were encountered, the incremental contribution of PRA cannot be isolated from that of mechanical drilling. Moreover, PRA-related soft-tissue trauma and edema were not objectively quantified. Therefore, any proposed technical advantages should be considered hypothesis-generating and warrant validation in larger, comparative cohorts.

Conclusions

In this proof-of-concept series, endoscopic-assisted transoral C1–2 joint release using PRA followed by posterior reduction and fusion was feasible in selected IAAD patients, including those with restricted mouth opening. The technique provided adequate anterior release to enable posterior reduction, with encouraging radiological correction and neurological improvement and a low rate of approach-related complications. Larger multicenter comparative studies are warranted to define indications and to quantify any incremental benefits relative to alternative strategies.
