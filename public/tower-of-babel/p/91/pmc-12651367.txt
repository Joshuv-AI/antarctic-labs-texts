A conformal self-healing organogel with synergistic supramolecular crosslinking
Authors: Yiyang Gao, Qihao Jiang, Jing Chen, Yanming Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThrough molecular design, this study developed a conformal self-healing organogel (CSHO) via molecular design. Utilizing acryloylmorpholine (ACMO) and hydroxyethyl acrylamide (HEAA) as monomers, tributyl citrate (TBC) as the solvent, and lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) as an electrolyte salt, the organogel was polymerized by UV curing. A dual supramolecular network of hydrogen and lithium bonds provided excellent mechanical strength without chemical crosslinkers, while also enabling self-healing. TBC enhanced flexibility and established continuous ion channels, boosting ionic conductivity. The CSHO exhibited excellent conformability to complex surfaces, replicating fine textures, and was recyclable through a water-based process. Mechanical sensing tests showed that the CSHO could respond sensitively and in real time to pressing and stretching. This work presents a strategy for multifunctional ionic conductors, advancing flexible sensors and wearable devices.Subject areas: Chemistry, Supramolecular chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

With the rapid development of wearable flexible devices and smart electronics, flexible rechargeable batteries with long service life and excellent mechanical properties1,2,3,4,5,6 as well as flexible electronic devices7,8,9,10,11,12,13 have gradually become research hotspots. In these applications, the choice of electrolyte material is crucial to the overall performance of the device. As emerging materials, ionogels have garnered significant attention in both academia and industry in recent years. Combining the electrochemical stability, non-volatility, and flame retardancy of organic solvents, ionogels overcome the limited temperature range of traditional hydrogels, making them a preferred alternative. The exceptional properties of ionogels have demonstrated immense potential across various fields. In the realm of solid-state electrolytes, the high ionic conductivity and electrochemical stability of ionogels offer broad prospects for applications in batteries,14,15,16,17,18 supercapacitors,19,20,21,22,23 and thermoelectric devices.24,25,26 Additionally, ionogels play a vital role in emerging fields such as ionic skins27,28,29,30 and soft robotics.31,32,33,34 These applications benefit from the ionogels’ ability to withstand extreme temperatures, flexibility, and tunability, enabling them to operate in harsh environments beyond human tolerance, showcasing significant advantages. However, ionogels still face challenges in practical applications. For instance, their toughness, fatigue resistance, and insensitivity to crack propagation are often limited by the rigid networks formed through covalent crosslinking. To address these limitations, researchers have made notable progress in developing high-strength and high-toughness ionogels by incorporating strategies such as sacrificial bonds, double-network structures, micro-nano composites, and reversible bonds. These approaches not only enhance the mechanical properties of ionogels but also provide robust guarantees for their stability and durability in practical applications. Furthermore, molecular design to endow ionogels with self-healing capabilities has increasingly become a focus of research.

Self-healing ionogels are typically composed of polymer networks and ionic liquids or salt-based electrolytes. The polymer chains contain specific functional groups, such as carboxyl, amino, and hydroxyl groups, which enable the formation of reversible interactions, including hydrogen bonds and ion-dipole interactions. These interactions allow self-healing ionogels to spontaneously recover at room temperature without requiring external stimuli (e.g., heat or light).35,36,37,38 This property enables self-healing ionogels to rapidly restore their integrity and functionality after damage, thereby extending their service life. For instance, Masayoshi Watanabe’s team developed a supramolecular micellar ionogel composed of a diblock copolymer with an ionic liquid-phobic block and a hydrogen-bonding block containing both hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, which exhibited rapid self-healing at room temperature.39 Similarly, Li’s team synthesized a linear polyurethane (SHPU) with multiple hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, inspired by sea cucumber peptides, which demonstrated excellent self-healing properties and puncture resistance.40 However, these reversible interactions are often limited in variety and strength. Particularly in gel systems, the presence of a large amount of solvent molecules can reduce the material’s mechanical strength, often necessitating stronger chemical bonds to maintain structural integrity. Yet, increasing the degree of chemical crosslinking often compromises the reversibility of these interactions. To address this, we propose an alternative approach: involving both the solvent and electrolyte components in the reversible interactions. This strategy enhances physical crosslinking, significantly improving mechanical strength while preserving the material’s self-healing capabilities.

Based on the design strategy of multiple supramolecular crosslinking interactions, this study successfully developed a conformal self-healing organogel (CSHO) that combines self-healing properties with mechanical strength, offering a novel approach for the design of flexible functional materials. CSHO is constructed using acryloylmorpholine (ACMO) and hydroxyethyl acrylamide (HEAA) as copolymer monomers, tributyl citrate (TBC) as the solvent, lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) as the electrolyte salt, and phenylbis(2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl) phosphine oxide as the photoinitiator (photoinitiator 819) with polymerization achieved through UV curing technology. The structural formulas of all raw materials are shown in Figure S1. Within this gel system, multiple intermolecular hydrogen bonds can form between ACMO, HEAA, and TBC. The introduction of LiTFSI not only endows the material with excellent ionic conductivity but also provides abundant lithium ions, which can form stable lithium-bond interactions with the oxygen atoms in the monomers,41,42 as illustrated in Figure 1A. Consequently, CSHO incorporates both hydrogen bonds and lithium bonds (Li-bonds) as supramolecular crosslinking mechanisms, enabling high mechanical strength without the need for chemical crosslinking agents. Additionally, CSHO exhibits exceptional conformability, allowing it to tightly adhere to irregular surfaces and form seamless interfacial contact, thereby effectively reducing interfacial voids and contact resistance. This property enables CSHO to adapt to complex dynamic deformations, significantly enhancing the durability and stability of devices under repeated mechanical stress. We anticipate that this material holds great promise for applications in biomedical engineering, flexible electronics, and soft robotics, with the potential to drive technological innovation and performance breakthroughs in these fields.

Results and discussions

Construction and characterization of CSHO

ACMO and HEAA, as photocurable monomers, exhibit characteristics such as low volatility, low irritability, and rapid curing. From a molecular structure perspective, the ACMO molecule contains abundant hydrogen bond acceptors, which can form multiple hydrogen bond networks with the hydroxyl (-OH) and secondary amine (-NH-) groups at the terminal of the HEAA molecule, as illustrated in Figure 1B. Simultaneously, LiTFSI demonstrates excellent solubility in the system, and its dissociated cations and anions provide the material with good conductive properties. Notably, Li+ can coordinate with the carbonyl (C=O) and ether (C–O–C) groups in the monomer molecules, forming stable Li-bonds, as shown in Figure 1C. Although this coordination significantly enhances the mechanical strength of the material, it inevitably introduces spatial hindrance to Li+ migration, thereby affecting ionic conductivity. To optimize ion transport performance, this study introduces TBC as a plasticizer. TBC not only adheres to the principles of green chemistry, being non-toxic, odorless, and environmentally friendly, but also its active -OH groups in the molecular structure can form additional hydrogen bond networks with ACMO and HEAA (as shown in Figure 1D), thereby enhancing the material’s flexibility while further improving mechanical strength. More importantly, the addition of TBC constructs continuous ion transport channels in the system, effectively compensating for the negative impact of Li-bonds on ionic conductivity. Based on the aforementioned intermolecular interactions, CSHO successfully establishes a reversible supramolecular crosslinked network with synergistic hydrogen and Li-bonds, achieving a balanced regulation of mechanical and conductive properties.

To verify the formation of Li-bonds and their mechanism of action, we characterized the CSHO system using lithium-7 nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (7Li NMR) and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). The 7Li NMR results (Figures 1E and 1F) show that in the CSHO with a LiTFSI concentration of 0.1 mol L−1, the 7Li NMR spectrum exhibits a single characteristic peak at 0.10 ppm with an intensity of 4,181. However, when the LiTFSI concentration is increased to 1 mol L−1, the chemical shift of the characteristic peak significantly moves downfield to −0.99 ppm, and the peak intensity increases to 12,601. This phenomenon can be attributed to the coordination between lithium ions and the carbonyl groups (C=O) in the monomer molecules: as the LiTFSI concentration increases, more Li+ coordinate with the carbonyl groups. The strong electron-withdrawing effect of the carbonyl groups reduces the electron density around the lithium nuclei, causing the chemical shift to move downfield.43 Additionally, the significant increase in peak intensity is positively correlated with the change in Li+ concentration, further confirming the reliability of the test results. To supplement the verification of Li-bond formation, we analyzed the characteristic vibration peaks of the carbonyl groups using FTIR spectroscopy (Figure 1G). Although the overall trends of the infrared spectra for the two CSHO concentrations are similar, differences are observed in the carbonyl stretching vibration region between 1,600 and 1,700 cm−1 (Figure 1H). Specifically, at a LiTFSI concentration of 0.1 mol L−1, the carbonyl characteristic peak is located at 1,640 cm−1 whereas at a concentration of 1 mol L−1, the peak shifts to 1,636 cm−1. This shift in the characteristic peak is consistent with literature reports,44 indicating that coordination interactions between lithium ions and carbonyl groups indeed exist, thereby altering the vibrational frequency of the carbonyl groups. Combining the 7Li NMR and FTIR analysis results, the formation of Li-bonds in the CSHO and their impact on the material’s properties are conclusively confirmed. Besides, we performed Raman spectroscopy characterization on the CSHO sample (Figure S2). The spectral analysis revealed distinct characteristic peaks at 1,607 and 1,718 cm−1, which can be attributed to the C=O stretching vibrations of amide and ester groups, respectively. Notably, these peaks exhibit a significant redshift compared to the typical C=O stretching vibrations of amide (1,640–1,680 cm−1) and ester (1,730–1,750 cm−1) groups. This shift is primarily attributed to the formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds and Li-bonds, which reduce the force constant of the C=O bond, thereby lowering its vibrational frequency.

While maintaining a fixed monomer content of 30%, we replaced LiTFSI with 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium TFSI to ensure anion consistency while varying only the cation species. However, the material prepared under these conditions failed to form a stable gel structure (Figure S3). This result indirectly confirms that lithium ions play an essential role in the formation of the physical crosslinking network by engaging in dynamic reversible bonds with polymer chains, which is critical for the structural integrity of the CSHO.

Electrical properties of the CSHO

After determining the composition of the system, we systematically investigated the conductive properties of the CSHO. Figure 2A shows the impedance spectra of CSHOs with a solid content of 50% and LiTFSI concentrations of 0.5, 0.75, and 1 mol L−1. The results indicate that as the LiTFSI concentration increases, the intrinsic resistance of the CSHO initially decreases and then rises, while the ionic conductivity follows a trend of first increasing and then decreasing. This phenomenon can be attributed to a transition in the ion transport mechanism: in the low concentration range, ionic conductivity is primarily controlled by carrier concentration. As the LiTFSI concentration increases, the number of mobile Li+ and TFSI− ions increases, leading to enhanced conductivity. However, when the concentration exceeds 0.75 mol L−1, the ion transport channels become crowded, and interactions between ions intensify, resulting in reduced migration efficiency, which is macroscopically manifested as a decrease in conductivity. Therefore, the optimal conductivity is achieved at a LiTFSI concentration of 0.75 mol L−1. The ionic conductivity, calculated based on the electrode area and sample thickness, is shown in Figure 2B. The conductivity of the 0.75 mol L−1 sample is 2.7 × 10−8 S cm−1, which is significantly lower than the requirements for practical applications. To optimize the conductive performance, we reduced the solid content to 30% and increased the solvent content to 70%, while ensuring that the mechanical strength meets basic usage requirements. Figure 2C shows the impedance spectra of this series of CSHOs. Compared to the system with 50% solid content, the intrinsic resistance is reduced by at least three orders of magnitude. The corresponding conductivity calculations are shown in Figure 2D. The trend of conductivity with LiTFSI concentration remains unchanged (first increasing and then decreasing), with the maximum value still occurring at 0.75 mol L−1, where the conductivity reaches 2.43 × 10−5 S cm−1. Although this value is still lower than the requirements for gel electrolytes used in batteries (typically >10−3 S cm−1), it is sufficient for many applications such as flexible sensors. This optimization result demonstrates that by adjusting the solid content and solvent ratio, the conductive performance of the material can be significantly improved while maintaining its mechanical properties.

Figure 2E shows the frequency-impedance characteristic curve of the CSHO with a LiTFSI concentration of 0.75 mol L−1, where the vertical axis represents the impedance modulus (|Z|). At the characteristic frequencies of 0.1 Hz, 1 kHz, and 1 MHz, the corresponding impedance moduli are 73.4, 4.3, and 2.4 kΩ, respectively. As the frequency increases, the impedance modulus shows a decreasing trend, with a more pronounced decline in the low-frequency region (<1 kHz) and a gradual stabilization in the high-frequency region (>1 kHz). This phenomenon indicates that the CSHO exhibits typical frequency-dependent behavior. Notably, when the frequency exceeds 1 kHz, the impedance significantly decreases, suggesting that the CSHO can fully utilize its ionic conductive properties within this frequency range. To further elucidate the electrical characteristics of the CSHO, we analyzed the frequency-phase angle curve (Figure 2F). The results show that the phase angle first decreases and then increases with increasing frequency. Specifically, as the frequency increases from 0.1 Hz to 1 kHz, the phase angle decreases significantly from 60° to 2°, indicating a transition from predominantly capacitive behavior to predominantly resistive behavior. However, as the frequency continues to rise to 1 MHz, the phase angle increases again to 36.5°. This phenomenon can be attributed to the motion characteristics of ions under high-frequency electric fields: at extremely high frequencies, the migration distance of cations and anions under the electric field is significantly shortened, causing the ions to only undergo short-range reciprocating motion as the electrode polarity switches. When the frequency is sufficiently high, this restricted ion motion can be approximated as localized vibrations, causing the material to exhibit capacitive characteristics again in the high-frequency region. This explanation is consistent with the stabilization of impedance in the high-frequency region observed in Figure 2E, further confirming the frequency-dependent conductive mechanism of the CSHO.

Mechanical properties of the CSHO

In addition, we systematically characterized the mechanical properties of the CSHO. Figure 3A shows the physical appearance of the CSHO in its original and stretched states, indicating that the material exhibits excellent stretchability, capable of accommodating the deformation requirements of flexible devices. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to observe the microstructure during the stretching process (Figure 3B), revealing that the CSHO undergoes significant structural fragmentation upon deformation. This phenomenon confirms the rupture of the physical crosslinking network within the CSHO and indirectly indicates that the CSHO is a viscoelastic material. To quantitatively investigate the effect of LiTFSI concentration on mechanical properties, we prepared a series of CSHOs with a solid content of 30% and LiTFSI concentrations of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mol L−1, respectively, and tested their stress-strain curves (Figure 3C). Since the CSHO relies solely on physical crosslinking and exhibits typical viscoelastic behavior, we focused on the mechanical properties within the first 150% strain range. The results show that the mechanical strength of the CSHO significantly increases with the increase in LiTFSI concentration. However, when the hydrogen bonds and Li-bonds in the supramolecular crosslinking networks break during stretching, the stress-strain curve enters a plateau region. Based on this, we calculated the Young’s modulus from the linear region within the first 10% strain range, as shown in Figure 3D. Specifically, the Young’s moduli corresponding to LiTFSI concentrations of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mol L−1 are 1.97, 9.77, 44.18, 75.04, and 102.75 MPa, respectively. This significant increasing trend confirms the crucial role of LiTFSI in enhancing the mechanical properties of the material: it not only provides conductivity to the CSHO but also significantly improves the mechanical strength through the formation of a Li-bond crosslinking network. This dual functionality makes LiTFSI a key component in optimizing the overall performance of the CSHO.

Furthermore, we conducted cyclic tensile tests on the CSHO at different strain levels while simultaneously monitoring the changes in electrical resistance. As shown in Figure 3E, the material was subjected to three consecutive stretching cycles at 50%, 100%, and 150% strain, respectively. Due to the inherent viscoelasticity of the CSHO, the stress gradually reduced with increasing cycles, which is consistent with the typical mechanical behavior of such materials. Meanwhile, it can be clearly observed from the resistance response in Figure 3F that the change in resistance closely corresponds to the stretching behavior, demonstrating notable and reproducible synchrony. The figure exhibits three distinct resistance peaks with different intensities, corresponding to the tensile cycles at 50%, 100%, and 150% strain, respectively. These results further support the potential of the CSHO as a reliable signal-responsive component in mechanical sensing applications.

In Figures S4 and S5, we present the complete stress-strain curves of the ionic gels with LiTFSI concentrations of 0.2 and 1.0 mol L−1, respectively. The experimental results indicate that the elongation at break for the two samples is 1,750% and 627%, respectively. This significant difference reveals the effect of LiTFSI concentration on the ductility of the CSHO: while the mechanical strength of the CSHO increases significantly with the increase in LiTFSI concentration, the elongation at break shows a distinct downward trend. Notably, during the tensile testing, when the strain exceeds 1,000%, the CSHO exhibits a mucus-like stringing behavior, with the stress either ceasing to increase or even decreasing, indicating that the CSHO has lost its self-supporting capability. Therefore, the stretching behavior beyond 1,000% strain loses its engineering significance in practical applications. However, the elongation at break exceeding 600% is sufficient to meet the requirements of most flexible electronic devices, fully demonstrating its potential for application in the field of flexible electronics. In the subsequent tests, the solid content will be fixed at 30%, and the concentration of LiTFSI will be fixed at 0.75 mol L−1.

Moreover, we performed thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) on this CSHO, as shown in Figure S6A. The TGA results demonstrate that the CSHO material exhibits excellent thermal stability. No significant mass loss was detected below 302°C. When the temperature exceeded 302°C, substantial weight loss occurred due to the evaporation of residual solvent molecules in the system. A second distinct weight loss step was observed at approximately 380°C, corresponding to the thermal decomposition of the polymeric framework. Based on this, we tested the effects of temperature on the mechanical and electrochemical properties. Due to the conformability of CSHO, which may lead to gradual deformation over time, we introduced 0.1% crosslinking agent into the system to ensure that the samples maintain their structural integrity and shape throughout the testing process. Although the incorporation of the crosslinking agent slightly reduced the elongation at break and self-healing properties of the samples, it did not alter the fundamental trends regarding the influence of temperature on the mechanical performance of CSHO. As can be seen from Figure S6B, the variation in impedance follows a general trend. As temperature increases, the intrinsic resistance of CSHO gradually decreases, which is specifically reflected in the impedance curve shifting closer to the origin at its intersection with x axis. This phenomenon can be attributed to the following three reasons: first, the conductive behavior of ionic conductors exhibits significant frequency dependence. The increase in temperature intensifies molecular thermal motion, promoting ion migration toward the electrodes, which macroscopically manifests as reduced impedance and enhanced conductivity. Second, under elevated temperatures, the physical crosslinking network within the CSHO (such as hydrogen bonds and Li-bonds) undergoes dissociation, leading to a decrease in crosslinking density. The loosening of the network structure provides more pathways for ion migration, further improving ion mobility. Third, the breaking of Li-bonds releases previously coordinated lithium ions into free ions, increasing the concentration of mobile lithium ions in the system and thereby enhancing the overall conductivity of CSHO. Moreover, the mechanical strength of CSHO decreases to some extent with increasing temperature, accompanied by a tendency for reduced elongation at break (Figure S6C). This behavior can be attributed to the disruption of dynamic crosslinks within the CSHO at elevated temperatures, which leads to a reduction in the number of physical crosslinking sites and a consequent decline in the load-bearing capacity of the network. Simultaneously, the intensified thermal motion of polymer chains promotes easier relative slippage between chains, resulting in a loosened local structure and thereby facilitating fracture.

Self-healing capability and conformability of CSHO

After systematically characterizing the mechanical and electrical properties of the CSHO, we further investigated its self-healing characteristics. To facilitate observation of the healing process, the CSHO was dyed red and blue, respectively. Two pieces of CSHO with different colors were joined and observed under an optical microscope (Figure 4A). Initially, a distinct black crack was visible at the seam. Over time, the crack gradually narrowed, indicating that the healing process was underway. After 15 h of resting, the crack had almost completely disappeared. It should be further explained that the images at 12 and 15 h exhibit a color difference compared to the first five images. This discrepancy is due to uncontrollable factors during the experiment that caused the microscope to shut down and restart, resulting in minor changes in the illumination conditions and focus adjustment. In fact, the position of the CSHO sample remained unchanged throughout all the time. To verify the healing effect, we conducted a tensile test on the repaired sample. The results showed that the two pieces of CSHO had completely fused into a single entity (Figure 4B).

As shown in Figure 3C in the main text, the CSHO exhibits significant viscoelastic characteristics, with notable viscoelastic behavior appearing after approximately 10% strain. As a result, the mechanical performance before and after self-healing is nearly identical on the macroscopic level, making it difficult to distinguish differences using conventional stress-strain curves. To enhance the elastic behavior of the material and facilitate the observation of self-healing effects in mechanical tests, we introduced 0.1% crosslinking agent into the system. This modification increased the modulus and elasticity of the CSHO, thereby improving the detectability of subtle differences in mechanical properties before and after self-healing.

As can be seen from Figure 4C, the CSHO exhibits nearly identical strength up to 100% strain both before and after self-healing. However, in the subsequent stretching stage, a slight decrease in strength is observed in the healed sample. This behavior can be attributed to the chemical crosslinking network formed by the introduced crosslinking agent, which cannot effectively self-heal, resulting in incomplete recovery of this part after damage. In the initial stretching stage, the weak interactions dominated by physical crosslinks dissociate first and dissipate energy, allowing the material to maintain same strength macroscopically. As the strain further increases, and the sacrificial bonds within the physical crosslinking network are gradually exhausted, the non-recoverable nature of the chemical crosslinks becomes apparent, leading to the observed difference in strength. Moreover, the increased elongation at break observed in the self-healed samples is due to testing variability rather than an anomalous phenomenon (original sample: 410%, healed sample: 485%). Inconsistencies in the clamping force applied by the fixtures make it difficult to ensure that fracture occurs precisely at the center of the gauge length every time, resulting in certain fluctuations in the measured elongation at break.

To further validate the self-healing properties of the CSHO, we prepared six block-shaped samples of CSHOs, which were dyed red and blue alternately. The samples were then arranged and assembled in an alternating color sequence (as shown in Figure S7). After allowing the sample to rest overnight, we observed that it not only successfully self-healed into a single entity but also exhibited a notable characteristic: due to the effect of gravity, the sample lost its initial bulk shape and underwent deformation. Based on this phenomenon, we demonstrated the remarkable conformability of the CSHO. As shown in Figure 4D, an irregularly shaped CSHO was placed in a spherical silicone mold and left to rest overnight, after which it took on a perfect hemispherical shape. Subsequently, the hemispherical CSHO was placed in a mold with a leaf texture and rested overnight once again. Upon removal, it was evident that the side in contact with the mold had perfectly replicated the leaf texture. We then cut the CSHO into four pieces and placed each piece into a mold with Chinese characters. After another overnight rest, the Chinese characters were clearly visible on the surface of the four pieces of CSHO. Notably, these four pieces could be reassembled into a single entity using their self-healing properties, thus achieving a closed loop.

To further verify this property, we constructed an artificial slit using two glass slides and a 1-mm-thick U-shaped silicone mold. The CSHO was placed at the top of the slit and left undisturbed for 12 h (Figure 4E). It was observed that the gel gradually infiltrated into the slit over time (Note: The color change in the CSHO above the slit is due to exposure to atmospheric moisture, while the CSHO within the slit remained colorless and transparent). These results demonstrate that the CSHO exhibits remarkable conformability, enabling it to slowly yet effectively fill even finely confined spaces through autonomous deformation. The conformability of the CSHO indicates its ability to closely adhere to complex or irregular surfaces, ensuring uniform coverage. This property endows it with significant potential in applications such as electronic devices, sensors, and biomedical equipment, where precise fitting is required, and it can also effectively simplify manufacturing processes.

In addition to its excellent conformal capabilities, the CSHO also demonstrates remarkable recyclability. As previously mentioned, the crosslinked network of the CSHO primarily relies on weak interactions such as hydrogen bonds and Li-bonds. This non-covalent bonding approach enables the reversible recycling of the CSHO. As shown in Figure S8A, we immersed the CSHO in deionized water. Water molecules, acting as highly active hydrogen bond donors, competitively bind to the hydrogen bond acceptors in the monomer molecules, gradually disrupting the original supramolecular crosslinked network and ultimately causing the CSHO to dissolve completely. Subsequently, the resulting solution was poured into a polytetrafluoroethylene mold and dried at 60°C to remove the water, yielding a transparent CSHO film once again (Figure S8B). Notably, the recycled CSHO retains tensile properties (Figure S8C). Water is an environmentally friendly, safe, and non-toxic solvent; second, the process is simple and does not require complex equipment or harsh conditions. This characteristic not only highlights the material’s sustainability but also provides a feasible solution for efficient resource utilization. Such recyclability broadens the application prospects of the CSHO in fields such as flexible electronics and wearable devices, while also aligning with the principles of green chemistry and sustainable development.

However, we systematically evaluated the mechanical and electrical properties of the CSHO after dissolution and re-film formation. The results indicate a significant degradation in the performance of the regenerated film. As shown in Figure S9A, the impedance spectrum shown in the left figure reveals that the intrinsic resistance of the as-prepared CSHO sample was 7,865 Ω (corresponding to the intersection of the blue curve with the x axis). After reprocessing, the intrinsic resistance increased to 39,791 Ω (intersection of the red curve with the x axis), representing a 5-fold increase in resistance and a corresponding decrease in conductivity to only 20% of the initial value, indicating a substantial decline in electrical performance. Furthermore, the stress-strain curve presented in the right figure demonstrates a notable deterioration in the tensile properties of the reformed CSHO film, with the elongation at break falling below 100% (Figure S9B). Thus, while the CSHO exhibits recyclability, its reusability remains limited.

Sensing applications of CSHO

Finally, we experimentally demonstrated the mechanical sensing performance of the CSHO. According to classical electrical theory, the resistance of a material is directly proportional to its length and inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area. Therefore, changes in the material’s shape will directly lead to variations in resistance. As a material with a flexible structure, the CSHO exhibits high sensitivity of its resistance to deformation. As shown in Figures 5A and 5B, we investigated the resistance changes of the CSHO under two typical mechanical stimuli: pressing and stretching. The experimental results indicate that when the CSHO is subjected to pressing or stretching, its resistance increases rapidly. Upon removal of the external force, the resistance quickly returns to its initial state, and a clear spike signal is observed at the output terminal. This demonstrates that the CSHO can respond to mechanical stimuli in real time and with high sensitivity. The complete mechanical sensing process can be viewed in Videos S1 and S2. Additionally, Video S3 showcases the mechanical sensing performance of the CSHO after being dissolved in deionized water, recycled, and re-evaporated into a film. From the video, it is evident that the CSHO retains excellent sensing capabilities.

We have supplemented two types of sensing performance tests. Figure 5C shows glass bottles filled with different volumes of water, with their total weights controlled at 20, 30, and 40 g, respectively. Figure 5D displays the resistance variation curves obtained by pressing the CSHO three times sequentially using these bottles of different weights. The results indicate that as the applied weight increases, the magnitude of resistance change also increases: the resistance changes induced by 20, 30, and 40 g loads are approximately 7.7, 11.6, and 15.8 Ω, respectively, and a clear linear correspondence is observed between the resistance change and the applied weight. Additionally, Figure 5E shows the resistance response of the CSHO during 200 consecutive rapid tapping events (divided into 4 groups of 50 times each). The initial resistance value was 3,046.7 Ω. After each group of taps, the resistance values were 3,048.8, 3,037.0, 3,033.0, and 3,045.8 Ω, respectively. The overall resistance variation range (ΔR) was 15.8 Ω, corresponding to a variation rate of only 0.52%. These results demonstrate that CSHO maintains high response stability under repeated mechanical stimulation, indicating excellent reliability for strain sensing applications.

Conclusions

This study successfully developed a CSHO with synergistic supramolecular crosslinking. ACMO and HEAA, as photocurable monomers, provided excellent mechanical strength and self-healing properties through a multiple hydrogen bond network. The introduction of LiTFSI not only endowed CSHO with superior ionic conductivity but also further enhanced its mechanical strength via Li-bonds. TBC, as a plasticizer, not only improved the flexibility of CSHO but also optimized ionic conductivity by constructing continuous ion transport channels. The CSHO achieved optimal conductivity (2.43 × 10−5 S cm−1) at a LiTFSI concentration of 0.75 mol L−1, and its conductive behavior exhibited significant frequency dependence. With increasing LiTFSI concentration, the Young’s modulus of the CSHO significantly increased, reaching up to 102.75 MPa, while maintaining excellent elongation at break, meeting the application requirements for flexible electronic devices. Additionally, CSHO demonstrated complete self-healing of cracks within 15 h, and the repaired samples retained excellent mechanical properties. In addition, CSHO could tightly conform to complex or irregular surfaces, showcasing outstanding conformability. Moreover, CSHO exhibited excellent recyclability, enabling multiple cycles of use through a simple dissolution-reformation process, aligning with the principles of green chemistry and sustainable development. Finally, CSHO demonstrated high sensitivity and real-time responsiveness in mechanical sensing, rapidly increasing resistance under external force and quickly recovering to its initial state upon force removal. This characteristic makes it highly promising for applications in flexible sensors, wearable devices, and other fields. In summary, the CSHO provides new insights into the design of flexible functional materials and is expected to drive technological innovation and performance breakthroughs in fields such as flexible electronics, biomedicine, and soft robotics.

Limitations of the study

This study has made significant progress in multifunctional integration but still exhibits limitations in performance balance and practical applications. The optimal ionic conductivity of the material (2.43 × 10−5 S cm−1) remains somewhat lower than that typically required for gel electrolytes in batteries. More notably, the material shows significant performance degradation after recycling and reprocessing, with conductivity retaining only 20% of its initial value and elongation at break dropping below 100%, which considerably restricts its potential for cyclic utilization. Furthermore, mechanical properties markedly decline at elevated temperatures due to the dissociation of the physically crosslinked network. These factors collectively hinder its prospects for practical applications in harsh environments.

Method details

Materials

All the reagents in this work were purchased from Aladdin, they are analytically pure and can be used directly without further purification.

Synthesis of PSE

All the chemicals used in this research were of analytical grade. For the typical synthesis of CSHO, ACMO, HEAA, TBC and LiTFSI were mixed under magnetic stirring for 5 min to form a homogeneous solvent mixture. Photo-initiator 819 were then added to the mixture and continuously stirred for another 2 min at room temperature to develop a transparent precursor solution. The molar percentages of photo-initiator 819 was controlled at 0.2%, respectively. Finally, the as-prepared precursor solution was injected into a glassy mould and cured by ultraviolet light irradiation (365 nm, 400 W power) for 2 min to obtain the CSHO samples.

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) tests

FTIR was performed on a Nicolet 6700 IR spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and scanning time of 32.

Lithium-7 nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (7Li NMR) tests

7Li NMR measurements (600 MHz) were conducted using a JNM-ECZ600R spectrometer (JEOL RESONANCE Inc., Japan).

Impedance tests

The CSHO samples were sandwiched between two stainless steels. The thickness of CSHO samples was 1.0 mm and the area of stainless-steel electrodes was 2 cm2. The AC impedance measurements were performed on an electrochemical workstation (CHI660E) with the frequency ranging from 0.1 Hz to 1 MHz.

Mechanical tests

PSEs were cut into dumbbell-shaped tensile samples with the dimensions of 16 mm × 4 mm (length × width). The thickness of each sample is measured individually. Then, these tensile samples were stretched on an electronic tensile machine (CMT6503, MTS) with a 50 N sensor to evaluate their mechanical performance under the stretching speed of 30 mm min−1.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) tests

The morphologies of samples were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss, Gemini 500).

Optical microscope (OM) tests

The morphologies of samples were examined by Nikon-Ti2-E.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All the data in this manuscript were plotted using Origin software.
