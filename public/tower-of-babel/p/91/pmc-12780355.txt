The Influence of Hemodialysis on Ocular Pressure Dynamics: An Observational Study on IOP and OPP Changes
Authors: Venkata Sai Sweeya Donepudi, M Meera Alias Devasena, Radha Annamalai, Pavan Krishna Reddy Sannareddy, E Ram Prasad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHemodialysis (HD) is a life-saving therapy for patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD), yet its impact on ocular hemodynamics remains insufficiently characterized. Intraocular pressure (IOP) and ocular perfusion pressure (OPP) are critical determinants of ocular health, and their fluctuation during HD may predispose patients to complications, particularly in glaucoma.ObjectiveTo assess the effect of HD on IOP and OPP in patients on maintenance dialysis.Materials and methodsThis observational study included 50 ESRD patients undergoing regular HD. IOP was measured using a noncontact tonometer, and OPP was calculated before and after dialysis sessions. Demographic, clinical, and hemodynamic parameters were recorded. Pre- and postdialysis values were compared using paired statistical tests.ResultsHemodialysis significantly increased IOP [right eye (RE): 13.56 ± 2.75 to 16.06 ± 1.97 mm Hg, p < 0.001; left eye (LE): 13.68 ± 2.67 to 16.51 ± 1.53 mm Hg, p < 0.001] and reduced OPP (RE: 49.2 ± 6.5 to 42.7 ± 5.8 mm Hg; LE: 47.6 ± 6.1 to 41.5 ± 5.7 mm Hg; both p < 0.001), with a mean decline of 7.6 mm Hg.ConclusionsHemodialysis significantly alters ocular pressure dynamics, with increased IOP and reduced OPP suggesting compromised ocular perfusion. Hypertension (HTN) and diabetes mellitus (DM) may exacerbate these effects. Although no acute ocular complications were observed, reduced OPP may increase the risk in patients with glaucoma or vascular disease. Introduction

End-stage renal disease (ESRD) is a major contributor to global morbidity, mortality, and healthcare-related economic burden.1 Chronic kidney disease (CKD), a precursor to ESRD, is a significant public health concern, with approximately 17% of the global CKD population residing in India.2 A 2006 population-based study estimated the age-adjusted incidence of kidney failure in India at 232 per million population.3 By 2018, approximately 1,75,000 and 8,500 individuals were receiving hemodialysis (HD) and peritoneal dialysis (PD), respectively.4 Patients undergoing HD are at increased risk of glaucoma and other ocular complications.5

Changes in intraocular pressure (IOP) during HD are among the most commonly reported ophthalmological abnormalities in chronic HD patients.6,7 The effect of HD on IOP has been investigated for nearly six decades. Sitprija and Holmes first reported an increase in IOP during HD in 1962,8 but subsequent studies have yielded variable results, with some observing IOP elevation7,9 and others reporting a decrease or negligible change.7,10,11 Recent advances in dialysis techniques, including high-flux HD, hemofiltration, and improved urea control, may help maintain osmolar balance and prevent significant IOP elevations.7,12 These fluctuations are clinically relevant, particularly in patients with glaucoma, as sudden changes may compromise optic nerve function and vision.

Ocular perfusion pressure (OPP), determined by systemic blood pressure (BP), is essential for maintaining adequate ocular tissue oxygenation. Abrupt reductions in OPP during HD can precipitate optic nerve ischemia, contributing to the development or progression of glaucomatous damage. The Early Manifest Glaucoma Trial reported that low baseline systolic perfusion pressure increased the hazard ratio for glaucoma development to 1.42.13 While previous studies have primarily assessed long-term OPP, the transient effects induced by HD remain poorly characterized.

Patients with CKD frequently have compromised ocular blood flow,14,15 and HD-induced fluctuations in IOP may exacerbate retinal or optic nerve ischemia, potentially leading to irreversible hypoxic damage. Despite decades of research, the effects of HD on IOP and OPP remain inconsistent.5,7,16,17 Some studies report transient IOP reductions due to decreased plasma osmolarity, whereas others note negligible changes. Similarly, the impact of intradialytic BP fluctuations on OPP is not well defined, underscoring the need for systematic evaluation.

Several limitations are apparent in prior studies, including the absence of standardized protocols for IOP and OPP measurement and limited consideration of systemic comorbidities such as hypertension (HTN) and diabetes mellitus (DM), which influence ocular autoregulation and may amplify HD-induced fluctuations.

The present study aims to address these gaps by systematically evaluating bilateral changes in IOP and OPP before and after a single HD session in patients with ESRD, using standardized measurement protocols in a cohort with a high burden of vascular comorbidities. This study is designed to provide a comprehensive understanding of the ocular hemodynamic consequences of HD and to inform strategies for optimizing ocular care in high-risk populations.

Materials and Methods

This observational study was conducted in the Department of Ophthalmology in collaboration with the Department of Nephrology at a tertiary care hospital. The objective was to evaluate the effect of a single HD session on IOP and OPP in patients with ESRD. A total of 50 patients aged 18 years and above, diagnosed with ESRD and undergoing maintenance HD, were recruited after obtaining written informed consent. Both male and female patients were included. Patients with a history of corneal abnormalities or prior corneal surgery, allergy to topical anesthetic agents, active ocular infection, or ocular surface disease interfering with accurate IOP assessment were excluded.

All participants underwent IOP measurement under aseptic precautions using a noncontact tonometer at two standardized time points during a single HD session: Within 30 minutes prior to initiation and immediately after completion. For consistency, three readings were taken in each eye at every time point, and the mean value was recorded. Systemic BP was measured simultaneously using a calibrated sphygmomanometer. All examinations were performed by the same investigator to minimize variability.

Ocular perfusion parameters were derived from BP and IOP values using standard formulae: Mean arterial pressure (MAP) = DBP + 1/3 (SBP–DBP); ocular perfusion pressure (OPP) = MAP–IOP; systolic OPP (SOPP) = SBP–IOP; diastolic OPP (DOPP) = DBP–IOP; and mean OPP (MOPP) = 2/3 (MAP–IOP). These indices provided a comprehensive assessment of ocular hemodynamics in the setting of HD.

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 21 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, United States). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as percentages. Pre- and postdialysis values were compared using paired t-tests for continuous variables, while Chi-square tests were used for categorical data where appropriate. Normality of data distribution was assessed prior to test selection, and a p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The mean preoperative IOP of the right eye (RE) was 13.56 ± 2.75 mm Hg, which increased to 16.06 ± 1.97 mm Hg postoperatively. The difference was found to be statistically highly significant (p < 0.001), indicating a meaningful rise in IOP following the intervention. The mean preoperative IOP of the left eye (LE) was 13.68 ± 2.67 mm Hg, which increased to 16.51 ± 1.53 mm Hg postoperatively. The difference was observed to be statistically highly significant (p < 0.001), demonstrating a marked elevation in IOP following the procedure (Table 4).

Discussion

The effects of HD on ocular hemodynamics remain incompletely characterized. While IOP has been extensively studied, results are inconsistent, and OPP has received comparatively limited attention.7 Variability in prior findings likely reflects differences in study design, timing of measurements, dialysis protocols, patient demographics, and ocular comorbidities such as glaucoma.

Previous studies report heterogeneous IOP responses. Sitprija et al.18,19 documented elevations of 4–8 mm Hg due to osmotic disequilibrium between plasma and intraocular compartments, whereas De Marchi et al.20 noted significant rises in patients with narrow anterior chamber angles. In contrast, Hojs and Pahor21 observed no significant changes, and Gutmann and Vaziri22 described early declines followed by modest increases. More recent studies, including Dinc et al.10 and Yang et al.,23 reported slight postdialysis reductions. These discrepancies highlight the influence of ultrafiltration volume, hydration status, dialysis duration, and systemic comorbidities on ocular pressure dynamics.

In the present study, we observed a modest but statistically significant postdialysis rise in IOP (RE: +2.5 mm Hg; LE: +2.8 mm Hg; p < 0.001). Although absolute values remained within the normal physiological range, even small increases may be clinically relevant for patients with glaucoma or ocular HTN, in whom disease progression is pressure-dependent.

A key novel finding of this study is the consistent and statistically significant reduction in OPP across both eyes, with a mean decline of 7.6 mm Hg (p < 0.001). As OPP reflects the balance between mean arterial pressure (MAP) and IOP, this reduction is attributable to HD-induced systemic hypotension compounded by concurrent IOP elevation. Reduced OPP is a recognized risk factor for glaucomatous optic neuropathy and impaired retinal perfusion. The uniform decline observed highlights the susceptibility of HD patients to ocular ischemic stress—a feature not extensively emphasized in previous studies.

The study cohort had a high prevalence of systemic comorbidities—HTN (90%) and DM (52%)—both associated with vascular dysregulation and impaired ocular autoregulation.5,10 HTN may amplify perfusion fluctuations during HD, while DM-related microvascular damage can compromise retinal and optic nerve head perfusion. This patient profile likely contributed to the pronounced OPP reduction observed, providing new insights into the interplay between systemic comorbidities and ocular hemodynamics during dialysis.

Overall, these findings corroborate prior reports of transient IOP elevation post-HD,5,7,17 while highlighting the novel observation of simultaneous and consistent OPP reduction. Clinically, this underscores the importance of monitoring both IOP and OPP in high-risk patients. Although no acute ocular complications occurred in this cohort, repeated hemodynamic fluctuations may increase the risk of long-term ocular morbidity, including glaucoma progression, ocular HTN, and ischemic retinal injury. Routine ophthalmic assessment, including both IOP and OPP monitoring, should therefore be integrated into dialysis care, particularly for patients with vascular or ocular comorbidities.

Limitations

This study is limited by its modest sample size, single-center design, and cross-sectional nature without longitudinal follow-up. Dialysis-related factors such as ultrafiltration volume, session duration, and dialysate composition were not analyzed in detail. Potential confounding effects of systemic medications, including antihypertensives and antidiabetics, were not assessed.

Future Directions

Longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate whether repeated fluctuations in IOP and OPP accelerate glaucoma or retinopathy. Advanced imaging modalities, such as optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA), may provide detailed insights into retinal and optic nerve head perfusion. Large multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings and inform evidence-based guidelines for ocular monitoring in patients undergoing HD.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that HD significantly affects ocular hemodynamics in patients with ESRD. In 50 participants—predominantly male (74%) with a high prevalence of HTN (90%) and DM (52%)—postdialysis IOP increased significantly (RE: 13.56 ± 2.75 to 16.06 ± 1.97 mm Hg; LE: 13.68 ± 2.67 to 16.51 ± 1.53 mm Hg; p < 0.001), while OPP decreased consistently (49.4 ± 6.1 to 41.8 ± 5.9 mm Hg; mean difference −7.6 mm Hg; p < 0.001). These findings highlight the combined impact of HD-induced hypotension and IOP elevation, underscoring the risk of compromised optic nerve and retinal perfusion. Notably, the simultaneous evaluation of IOP and OPP provides novel insight into the dual hemodynamic changes occurring during dialysis. Routine ophthalmic monitoring incorporating both IOP and OPP assessment is therefore recommended for early detection and management of ocular complications in high-risk dialysis patients.
