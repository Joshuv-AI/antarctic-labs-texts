Oral prolonged-release dienogest 2 mg and ethinylestradiol 0.02 mg in a 24/4-day regimen for polycystic ovary syndrome-associated hirsutism: a double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial
Authors: Roger Lobo, Alicyoy Angulo, Alejandro Muñoz, Enrico Colli, Héctor F Escobar-Morreale, Manuel Luque-Ramírez, Janos Zatik, Pedro-Antonio Regidor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundCurrent guidelines for polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) recommend combined oral hormonal contraceptives as first-line pharmacological treatment to reduce androgen excess and to treat clinical hyperandrogenic skin manifestations, such as hirsutism. However, these are used off-label. We aimed to demonstrate the efficacy of a prolonged-release oral formulation in a 24/4-day regimen for up to nine 28-day consecutive cycles compared with placebo for the clinical management of hirsutism in women with PCOS.MethodsThis was a multinational, multicentre, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial done across 46 sites in Czech Republic, Hungary, Lithuania, Poland, Serbia, Slovakia, Spain and Ukraine. Eligible participants were 2-year-post-menarche women (14–40 years old) with a body mass index (BMI) of 18 to <35 kg/m2 who were diagnosed with clinical hyperandrogenism (adapted modified Ferriman-Gallwey [mFG] score ≥7) plus oligomenorrhea of ≤6 menses per year and/or polycystic ovary morphology. Enrolled participants were randomised (4:1) to prolonged-release once-daily oral dienogest 2 mg (DNG) + ethinylestradiol 0.02 mg (EE) in a 24/4-day regimen (DNG + EE group) or placebo for up to nine 28-day cycles. Co-primary endpoints were: (1) the change from baseline in the adapted mFG score at the end of Cycle 9 or at the early discontinuation visit (EDV), and (2) proportion of responders, defined as participants with an adapted mFG score reduction of 50% from baseline to the end of Cycle 9/EDV. An adapted mFG score was used to assess for specific regions of body hair. No facial hair was assessed. We used a mixed-model repeated measure (MMRM) analysis that was repeated for BMI subgroups ≤30 or >30 kg/m2 but without BMI stratification. Adverse events and safety were monitored throughout. This study is registered with EudraCT, 2021-002178-17.FindingsBetween Nov 2, 2021, and Dec 5, 2023, of 500 patients screened, 305 participants were enrolled and randomised to study groups and 202 participants completed the trial to the end of Cycle 9. The safety set comprised 291 participants (DNG + EE, n = 234; placebo, n = 57) and 256 participants were included in the full analysis set (FAS; DNG + EE, n = 209; placebo, n = 47). 76 (DNG + EE) and 27 (placebo) participants discontinued the trial early, including 18 and four who discontinued due to adverse events; 168 and 34 participants, respectively, completed the trial. Least squares (LS) mean changes from baseline to end of Cycle 9/EDV in adapted mFG score were −3.8 and −1.5 for the DNG + EE and placebo groups, respectively (difference −2.24 ± 0.49 (98.75% CI: –∞, −1.14; p < 0.0001; MMRM, FAS). In the ≤30 and > 30 kg/m2 BMI subgroups, the LS mean changes from baseline in adapted mFG score were −3.8 and −3.7 for DNG + EE–treated participants, and −1.9 and −0.6 for placebo, respectively; LS mean treatment group differences were −1.9 (98.75% CI –∞, −0.7; p = 0.0004) and −3.14 (98.75% CI –∞, −0.9; p = 0.0011), respectively (MMRM, FAS). No new safety signals for DNG + EE were observed. The proportion of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) was similar between study groups. The most frequent TEAEs were headache (n = 49, 17%), intermenstrual bleeding (n = 41, 14%), and nasopharyngitis (n = 33, 11%). Serious TEAEs were reported for six participants (2%): five participants (2%) in the DNG + EE group (abdominal pain [n = 2], pain in extremity [n = 1], hypoesthesia [n = 1] and pulmonary embolism [n = 1]) and one participant (2%) in the placebo group (vestibular neuronitis).InterpretationOur findings show that oral prolonged-release DNG 2 mg plus EE 0.2 mg in a 24/4-day regimen for up to 9 cycles is an effective treatment for hirsutism in women with PCOS, with an acceptable safety profile. Further research is required to assess DNG + EE in treating hirsutism among different cultural and ethnic populations as well its impact on mood disorders (depression and anxiety) in women with hirsutism-associated PCOS.FundingChemo Research S.L.Keywords: Hirsutism, Polycystic ovary syndrome, Combined oral contraceptive, Modified Ferriman-Gallwey score, Dienogest and ethinylestradiol

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Hirsutism is the main manifestation of clinical hyperandrogenism associated with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) affecting between 65% and 80% of women with PCOS. We searched PubMed, Cochrane and CNKI from database inception to Sep 31, 2025 using the search terms ‘hirsutism’, ‘polycystic ovary syndrome’, ‘hyperandrogenism’, ‘combined oral contraceptive∗’, and included alternative abbreviations. These searches yielded over 200 articles. Current international PCOS guidelines state that combined oral hormonal contraceptives (COCs) could be used in reproductive adults and adolescents with PCOS for the management of hirsutism and/or irregular menstrual cycles, yet these are used in an off-label manner as there are no approved pharmacological treatments to manage hirsutism. Previous randomised controlled trials support the efficacy of COCs for reducing hirsutism symptoms in women with PCOS; however, specific types or doses of progestins, oestrogens, or combinations of COCs cannot currently be recommended owing to the very low quality of evidence available. Current data support COCs with the lowest effective oestrogen doses (such as ethinylestradiol [EE] 0.02–0.03 mg or equivalent) as a first choice COCs for the treatment of PCOS, balancing efficacy, metabolic risk profile, and side effects. A prolonged-release formulation of dienogest (DNG) 2 mg plus ethinylestradiol EE 0.02 mg has been shown to improve unscheduled bleeding (versus an immediate-release COC), but it has not yet been studied in women with PCOS. Obesity often increases the severity of clinical hyperandrogenism symptoms. Well-designed randomised controlled trials stratified by BMI are needed to evaluate pharmacological treatments for hirsutism. We aimed to address this knowledge gap.

Added value of this study

The findings of our multicentre, double-blind, randomised placebo-controlled trial show, for the first time, that prolonged-release DNG 2 mg + EE 0.02 mg is effective at reducing PCOS-associated hirsutism regardless of BMI using an adapted modified Ferriman-Gallwey scoring system that utilises body hair assessments. Facial hair was not considered in the evaluation for feasibility reasons. Moreover, treatment with DNG + EE normalised serum testosterone levels and other androgens and improved the menstrual bleeding cycle profile after up to nine 28-day cycles of treatment. There were no clinically relevant changes in metabolic parameters. Moreover, improvements in hirsutism were associated with improvements in PCOS-specific quality-of-life assessments.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings are suggestive of prolonged-release DNG 2 mg + EE 0.02 mg being considered as an effective treatment option to manage hyperandrogenism, specifically hirsutism in women with PCOS. We used an adapted version of the modified Ferriman-Gallway score, which is the gold standard for assessing and screening for clinical hirsutism; however, because of known variability of hair growth in various skin regions, and that not all body areas could be assessed, more clinical data regarding hirsutism are needed from future treatment studies.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a heterogeneous endocrine and metabolic disorder defined by androgen excess and ovarian dysfunction, provided that other specific diagnoses have been excluded.1 The prevalence of PCOS ranges from about 6–20% when using evolving diagnostic criteria, making it possibly the most common endocrine and metabolic disorder in women of reproductive age.2, 3, 4 PCOS arises from complex interactions between genetic and environmental factors,2 with diverse clinical manifestations affecting reproduction (abnormal menstrual bleeding, infertility, hyperandrogenism), metabolism (insulin resistance, impaired glucose tolerance, type 2 diabetes mellitus, adverse cardiovascular risk profiles), and psychological health (increased anxiety, depression, and worsened quality of life [QoL]).5,6 Hirsutism, characterised by the presence of the thick growth of excessive terminal hair on a woman's face and body, is the main manifestation of clinical hyperandrogenism associated with PCOS,4,7 and affects 65–80% of women with PCOS depending on how both PCOS and hirsutism are defined.4,7, 8, 9, 10 Ethnicity and cultural norms also affect the reported overall prevalence of 14.2% for hirsutism among unselected populations from various countries across different continents globally.11

To date, most medications prescribed for PCOS are used in an off-label fashion,1 including combined oral contraceptives (COCs) for reducing excess androgens, which remain the first-line pharmacological treatment of hyperandrogenic skin symptoms.12,13 The progestin component of COCs suppresses luteinising hormone (LH) secretion, resulting in a decline in ovarian androgen production, protects the endometrium, regulates menstrual cycles, and reduces hyperandrogenism.14 The oestrogen component of COCs increases circulating levels of sex-hormone binding globulin (SHBG), further decreasing serum free testosterone concentrations15 and contributes to gonadotropin suppression. The combined effect of the progestin and oestrogen components on the endometrium maintains menstrual cyclicity.14

Combined oral dienogest (DNG) 2 mg plus ethinylestradiol (EE) 0.02 mg has high antiandrogenic activity by reducing androgen levels, increasing serum SHBG levels, and significantly decreasing serum free testosterone levels.16 In addition DNG provides some degree of antiandrogen activity at the level of the androgen receptor.17 The objective of this trial was to demonstrate the efficacy of a prolonged-release formulation of oral DNG 2 mg plus EE 0.02 mg in a 24/4-day regimen for up to nine 28-day consecutive cycles compared with placebo for the clinical management of hirsutism in women with PCOS.

Methods

Study design and ethics

In this multicentre, multinational, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, eligible participants were women aged 14–40 years old who were 2-years post-menarche, and had a body mass index (BMI) of between 18 kg/m2 and <35 kg/m2 plus a diagnosis of PCOS from a specialist clinic.

The trial was registered at EudraCT (2021-002178-17) and formed part of a paediatric investigational plan (EMEA-002229-PIP02-21). The trial protocol, written materials provided to participants, and recruitment advertisements were approved by the relevant Independent Ethics Committee for each trial centre. The specific names of the authorities that granted ethical approval for the study are available in the Supplementary Materials. See Appendix S1 for the full protocol and ethical approval documents. The trial was performed in compliance with Good Clinical Practices and International Council for Harmonisation guidelines and in accordance with all national laws of participating countries and the Declaration of Helsinki (2008). Written informed consent/assent was received from all participants/parents of minors (<18 years) prior to enrolment.

Participants

At screening, all participants were assessed for the diagnosis of clinical hyperandrogenism (adapted modified Ferriman-Gallwey (mFG) score of ≥7 (a score of 28 was considered the most severe; see below for full description) plus oligomenorrhea (≤6 menses per year) while not using hormonal contraceptives and/or polycystic ovary morphology defined as the presence of ≥12 follicles measuring 2–9 mm located throughout the entire ovary or an ovarian volume ≥10 cm3 determined by intravaginal ultrasound (transabdominal ultrasound examination could be used for adolescents). Participants with other disorders and aetiologies of ovulatory dysfunction and hyperandrogenism were excluded. In this trial, all participants were in good physical and mental health, with a BMI 18–<35 kg/m2 and systolic/diastolic blood pressure of ≤140/90 mmHg. Women who were pregnant, wishing to get pregnant or were breastfeeding were not eligible to enrol, neither were current smokers ≥36 years with a BMI >30 kg/m2. All participants consented to use reliable non-hormonal contraception methods (condoms, female or male sterilisation or sexual abstinence) for the duration of the trial. Women with abnormal gynaecological examination findings on pelvic, breast or intravaginal ultrasound examination (or transabdominal ultrasound examination for adolescents) that in the investigator's opinion contraindicated participation in the trial were excluded. In addition, participants with type 2 diabetes and haemoglobin A1C > 6.5% (treatment with metformin was allowed only if the dose had been stable for at least 3 months prior to screening), anaemia with haemoglobin <10 g/dL, or the presence or risk of venous or arterial thromboembolism were also not eligible for enrolment. A full list of inclusion and exclusion criteria are listed in Appendix S2.

Randomisation, trial drugs and masking

Participants were randomised (4:1; stratified by age and centre) to receive either a prolonged-release oral formulation of DNG 2 mg + EE 0.02 mg (active treatment: DNG + EE arm) or placebo for up to nine consecutive 28-day cycles. Each participant was allocated a randomisation number using a validated interactive web response system (GxP brain GmbH, Berlin, Germany).

One treatment cycle comprised 24 active or placebo white tablets followed by 4 green placebo tablets (24/4-day regimen) in identical blister packs. The participants were asked to take the trial medication at approximately the same time each day for nine consecutive 28-day cycles.

Randomisation codes were not revealed until all data had been collected, all protocol deviations had been documented, the trial databases had been locked, and a blind data review had been completed. Only the trial safety manager, who was not involved with any other aspect of the trial, was aware of the trial drug allocations.

Trial procedures

For the trial duration participants were not permitted to take non-trial medications that may affect hirsutism or androgen levels, hormonal contraceptives, or drugs affecting blood pressure control, serum lipids or carbohydrate metabolism, except for metformin as already described.

Hirsutism assessments using the adapted mFG scoring system were conducted at screening, during Cycle 6 (Day 20 ± 7) and end of Cycle 9 (Day 29 ± 3). Each participant could decide whether to shave their hair in the assessed body areas during the trial, but they were required to shave exactly 15 days before each adapted mFG assessment, and the shaving regime had to be identical for each adapted mFG assessment throughout the trial. Site staff telephoned participants who shaved their hair 15 days before the Cycle 6 and end of Cycle 9 visits to remind them to shave their hair and that shaving was not allowed during the next 14 days. At all other timepoints, the participant could decide to shave any body hair as often as they liked. For each adapted mFG evaluation, the assessed body areas were photographed by the investigators using a smart phone with a pre-installed app provided by the sponsor. During the adapted mFG assessments, hairs could be plucked and these were also photographed. Participants were not allowed to pluck hairs from the body areas assessed for hair growth at any other time during the trial.

Laboratory evaluations

All laboratory evaluations were performed centrally (Laboratorium für Klinische Forschung GmbH, Hamburg, Germany). Hormone concentrations were analysed by quantitative electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) for insulin, TSH, β-HCG, β-HCG, testosterone, androstenedione, dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-S) and SHBG. Blood samples for baseline assessment of testosterone, androstenedione, 17⍺-hydroxyprogesterone (17-OHP), DHEA-S and SHBG were drawn 21–28 days before the study medication dispensation visit. All blood samples were drawn between 6am and 10am to take into account serum concentration fluctuations over the course of the day for parameters with a circadian rhythm. Albumin was measured by the bromocresol green method. The free androgen index (FAI) was calculated from serum total testosterone, and SHBG concentrations and the free testosterone concentrations by the method of Vermeulen et al.18

Outcomes

The co-primary endpoints were: (1) the change from baseline in the adapted mFG score at the end of Cycle 9 or at the early discontinuation visit (EDV), and (2) proportion of responders, defined as participants with an adapted mFG score reduction of 50% from baseline to the end of Cycle 9/EDV. The adapted mFG score was completed by each investigator based on seven body areas (chest, upper abdomen, lower abdomen, thighs, back, arm, and lower back/buttocks); however, to ensure the study remained feasible for patients, exposed facial areas such as the upper lip and chin were excluded from evaluation.

Secondary efficacy endpoints comprised the change in laboratory parameters from baseline to the end of Cycle 9/EDV for serum concentrations of total and calculated free testosterone, FAI, androstenedione, 17-OHP, DHEAS and SHBG. Changes from baseline to end of Cycle 9/EDV in insulin resistance were measured using the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA2-IR) based on fasting serum glucose and insulin levels. Serum lipid profiles were assessed at baseline and the trial end and comprised total cholesterol, high- and low-density lipoprotein (HDL and LDL) cholesterol and triglycerides. Adverse events were monitored throughout the trial at trial visits, via e-diary prompts, and calls to all participants on Day 10 of each cycle to collect information on any adverse events. Safety assessments included changes from baseline to the end of the trial for vital signs, clinical laboratory parameters, gynaecological examination and cervical cytology, and bodyweight. Bleeding patterns (self-reported using an e-diary) and QoL based on the polycystic ovary syndrome questionnaire (PCOSQ) were also recorded. The PCOSQ questionnaire comprises 26 items measuring five domains: emotions [8 items], body hair [5 items], weight [5 items], infertility problems [4 items] and menstrual problems [4 items]. PCOSQ assessments are made using a 7-point Likert-type scale in which 7 represents optimal function and 1 the poorest function.19

Statistical analysis

For the sample size calculation, a relative reduction in the adapted mFG score of 50% in the DNG + EE arm and 30% in the placebo arm was assumed with a conservative standard deviation of 25%. Expected responder rates were 60% and 20% in the DNG + EE and placebo arms, respectively. Based on these assumptions, along with a 4:1 randomisation ratio, a sample size of 165 evaluable adult participants (DNG + EE, n = 132; placebo, n = 33) would provide an overall power of ∼90%. Considering a drop-out rate of 35%, 255 adult participants (DNG + EE, n = 204; placebo, n = 51) had to be randomised. Additionally, 20 adolescent patients were also randomised (4:1) to fulfil the regulatory requirements for drugs used in adolescents.

All statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Analysis System (SAS) for Windows version 9.4 or higher (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The first co-primary outcome was analysed using a mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM), with visit and treatment as factors and baseline adapted mFG score as a covariate to take account of fixed effects (i.e., treatment) and random effects (i.e., participant variation) over time. Each participant was included as a random effect. The MMRM analysis was repeated in an exploratory manner for BMI subgroups (≤30 or >30 kg/m2). The least squares (LS) mean associated with treatments were presented overall at the end of Cycle 9/EDV with 97.5% confidence intervals (CIs) and corresponding p-values (Fisher's exact test was determined; 1.25% one-sided significance level). LS mean treatment differences ± standard error of the mean (SE) at the end of Cycle 9/EDV were estimated with a one-sided 98.75% CI and p-values. A fully conditional specification regression model was used to impute the missing values, assuming that the data was missing at random. The model included the variables of treatment, adapted mFG at baseline, adapted mFG at the end of Cycle 6, and adapted mFG at the end of Cycle 9/EDV. The number of imputations was 10. For the subgroup analyses, any intercurrent event occurrences were ignored.

A two-group Chi-squared test was used for the second co-primary endpoint (treatment responders, defined as a 50% improvement in the adapted mFG score) and was repeated for same subgroups as the first primary endpoint, and trial completers. The difference in the response rates was demonstrated with 98.75% CI, and the p-value of Fisher's exact test was determined (1.25% one-sided significance level).

The primary analysis was conducted using the full analysis set (FAS; defined as all participants who received at least one dose of the trial drug and who had at least one post-baseline assessment of primary efficacy measurement), and supportive analyses were repeated on the per-protocol set (PPS). The relative change of the adapted mFG total score from baseline to end of Cycle 9/EDV was assessed for the overall population and for the trial completers only.

All secondary endpoints were analysed using descriptive statistics based on the FAS. All statistical tests for secondary endpoints were exploratory. P-values (estimated using a t-test as the continuous data sets did not strongly violate a normal distribution) and 95% CIs were calculated for treatment differences in serum laboratory parameters changes from baseline to end of Cycle 9/EDV. Treatment differences in scheduled bleeding or spotting by reference period were analysed using a t-test.

All participants exposed to the trial medication were assessed for safety outcomes (safety set).

A post hoc analysis sensitivity analyses were conducted for the primary endpoints to adjust for the stratification variables (age or site); treatment differences for responder rates were analysed using a Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test for the association between two binary variables while controlling for a third categorical confounding variable and 97.5% CIs. Other post-hoc analyses comprised repeating the MMRM analysis according to hirsutism severity subgroups defined by the adapted mFG total score at baseline (<14, 14–19 or >19) and trial completers, as well as calculating the relative change of the adapted mFG total score from baseline to Cycle 9/EDV according to baseline hirsutism severity subgroups and an exploratory analysis by BMI subgroup (≤30 and > 30 kg/m2) for the change from baseline to end of Cycle 9 for serum hormone levels and serum SHBG concentration. A further post-hoc exploratory analysis assessed the relationship between the change from baseline to end of Cycle 9/EDV in the adapted mFG total score and the change of PCOSQ scores (in each domain and the total score). This analysis was based on Pearson's correlation coefficient r-values with corresponding 95% confidence limits and p-values.

Role of the funding source

The trial sponsor initiated and financed the trial, was responsible for all regulatory documents (including trial protocol, statistical analysis plan, clinical trial report) and drug safety, and provided oversight to the contract research organisation responsible for trial data management (Scope International AG, Mannheim, Germany).

Results

Between Nov 2, 2021, and Dec 5, 2023, 500 patients were screened across 46 sites in Czech Republic, Hungary, Lithuania, Poland, Serbia, Slovakia, Spain and Ukraine, of whom 305 provided consent and were randomly allocated to study groups (Fig. 1). 202 participants completed the trial (DNG + EE, n = 168; placebo, n = 34). 291 participants were included in the safety set (DNG + EE, n = 234; placebo, n = 57), 256 in the FAS (DNG + EE, n = 209; placebo, n = 47) and 233 in the PPS (DNG + EE, n = 188; placebo, n = 45). Demographics and baseline characteristics were mostly similar between groups except for some differences in the severity of hirsutism (Table 1). Most participants (207/256 [80.9%)] had an adapted mFG score of 7<14 indicating mild hirsutism. Overall, the mean ± SD participants’ age at menarche was 12.6 ± 1.5 years. Bleeding patterns during the 6 cycles prior to screening were similar in both groups: 84.1% of participants (244/291) reported no regular scheduled bleeding, 25.9% (75/291) reported spotting and/or unscheduled bleeding and 61.0% (177/291) reported the absence of more than one menstrual bleeding. The median time since last scheduled/menstrual bleeding was 20.0 days in both treatment groups.

In the ≤30 and >30 kg/m2 BMI subgroups, the LS mean changes from baseline in adapted mFG score were −3.8 and −3.7 for DNG + EE–treated participants, and −1.9 and −0.6 for placebo, respectively; the LS mean treatment group differences were −1.92 (98.75% CI –∞, −0.7; p = 0.0004) and −3.14 (98.75% CI –∞, −0.9; p = 0.0011), respectively (MMRM analysis, FAS). Exploratory post-hoc analysis for participants with mild (DNG + EE, n = 166; placebo, n = 34), moderate (DNG + EE, n = 32; placebo, n = 8) or severe (DNG + EE, n = 3; placebo, n = 2) baseline hirsutism showed a LS mean treatment difference of −1.27 (p = 0.0053) −4.21 (p = 0.0011) and −13.37 (p = 0.1292), respectively (Table S1).

We also assessed the relative reduction from baseline in the adapted mFG score. A total of 31.3% (63/201) versus 15.9% (7/44) were treatment responders in the DNG + EE and placebo groups, respectively (defined as participants with a relative ≥50% reduction in adapted mFG score from baseline to the end of Cycle 9/EDV) with a treatment difference of 15.5% (98.75% CI –3.8, ∞; p = 0.0271; co-primary endpoint; FAS; participants with missing values not included). Among trial completers, response rates at end of Cycle 9/EDV were 35.1% in the DNG + EE group and 20.6% in the placebo group (treatment group difference 14.5%; p = 0.0709, 98.75% CI: −7.7, ∞).

The post-hoc sensitivity analysis adjusting for site or age supported the predefined analysis; site-adjusted LS mean changes from baseline to end of Cycle 9/EDV in adapted mFG scores of −4.0 and −1.5 for the DNG + EE and placebo groups, respectively (difference −2.5 ± 0.46 (97.5% CI: −3.5, −1.4; p < 0.0001) and age-adjusted LS mean changes from baseline to end of Cycle 9/EDV in adapted mFG scores of −4.1 and −1.9 for the DNG + EE and placebo groups, respectively (difference −2.2 ± 0.49 (97.5% CI: −3.3, −1.4; p < 0.0001).

The mean ± SD relative change in adapted mFG score from baseline to the end of Cycle 9/EDV was significantly greater in the DNG + EE group (−34.5 ± 29.1%) versus the placebo group (−16.6 ± 28.3%; p < 0.001). Among the DNG + EE group, 19.6% (41/206) achieved a relative adapted mFG score reduction of ≥60% compared with 2.1% (1/47) of the placebo group (post-hoc analysis, FAS). Of the DNG + EE and placebo groups who had moderate or severe hirsutism at baseline (i.e., with an adapted mFG score of 14–28), 75.7% (28/37) and 25.0% (3/12) of participants, respectively, achieved mild hirsutism or better (adapted mFG score <14) by Cycle 9/EDV (post-hoc analysis; FAS). By the end of Cycle 9/EDV, 45.5% (95/209) and 29.8% (14/47), respectively, had adapted mFG scores <7, indicating no hirsutism (Table S2 and Figure S2).

Overall, there were statistically significant favourable mean serum hormone changes for the DNG + EE group for FAI, serum free testosterone, androstenedione, 17-OHP, DHEAS and SHBG at the end of Cycle 9/EDV, but not for the placebo group (Table 2), with no clinically relevant differences observed between BMI subgroups ≤30 kg/m2 and >30 kg/m2 (data not shown). Neither group showed a negative impact on insulin resistance with all mean HOMA2-IR values within the normal range (<2.1). At baseline, 80.0% of participants in the DNG + EE and placebo groups had high serum free testosterone levels (≥15 pmol/L). By the end of Cycle 9/EDV, 65.1% (127/195) of participants in the DNG + EE group had normal serum free testosterone levels (<15 pmol/L) but there was no change among the placebo group (Table S3; Figure S3).

At baseline and at the end of Cycle 9/EDV, all mean serum lipids were within the normal range in both groups, and there were no changes in the mean ratios of HDL:LDL and total HDL:cholesterol (Table S4).

A higher proportion of participants in the DNG + EE group reported scheduled bleeding/spotting during all reference periods (Cycles 2–4, 5–7, 8–9 and 2–9) compared with the placebo group (p < 0.0001 for each reference period; Fig. 3B). For the DNG + EE group the overall proportion of participants with no bleeding/spotting increased each cycle from 10.0% (24/228) in Cycle 1 to 23.3% (37/159) in Cycle 9, but there was no clear pattern in the placebo group with the lowest proportion of 27.7% (10/46) in Cycle 4 and the highest proportion of 44.2% (23/52) in Cycle 2.

The proportion of women with treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were similar in both treatment groups: 166 (70.9%) and 42 (73.7%) participants in the DNG + EE and placebo groups, respectively. The most frequent TEAEs were headache (49 participants, 16.8%), intermenstrual bleeding (41 participants, 14.1%), and nasopharyngitis (33 participants, 11.3%) with similar proportions in both treatment groups (Table 3). Most TEAEs were of mild or moderate intensity. No deaths occurred during the trial. Serious TEAEs were reported for six participants (2.1%): five participants (2.1%) in the DNG + EE group (abdominal pain [n = 2], pain in extremity [n = 1], hypoesthesia [n = 1], pulmonary embolism [n = 1]) and one participant (1.8%) in the placebo group (vestibular neuronitis). The investigators assessed all serious TEAEs as unrelated or unlikely to be related to trial medication, except for the pulmonary embolism, which was considered related to treatment with DNG + EE. The participant who developed the pulmonary embolism approximately 3 months after starting DNG + EE had other risk factors for thromboembolism, including recent prior COVID-19 infection, a BMI of 30.5 kg/m2 and type 2 diabetes mellitus (diagnosed while at the hospital, and had been untreated). The trial medication was withdrawn, antithrombotic treatment started, and the patient was discontinued from the trial. Uncontrolled diabetes was a trial exclusion criterion as it is associated with a hypercoagulable state that may increase the risk of thromboembolism. One patient in the DNG + EE group with a medical history of insulin resistance developed an exacerbation which the investigator reported as possibly related to the trial medication; insulin resistance is frequently associated with PCOS.1 There were no clinically relevant changes in vital signs, electrocardiogram, gynaecological examination cervical cytology or bodyweight.

Discussion

This randomised controlled trial assessed treatment responses to oral prolonged-release DNG 2 mg + EE 0.2 mg in a 24/4-day regimen versus placebo for the management of hirsutism in women with PCOS for up to 9 cycles. The trial demonstrates that DNG plus EE significantly improves hirsutism severity, assessed using an adapted mFG score at the end of Cycle 9/EDV, compared with placebo. Favourable mean serum hormone changes were also observed for the DNG + EE group versus the placebo group, including decreases in FAI and serum free testosterone; a high proportion of participants (63%) had normal serum free testosterone levels with DNG + EE treatment. There were minor changes in glucose, insulin or lipid metabolism among the DNG + EE group, most of which were not clinically significant. Participants in the DNG + EE group showed greater QoL improvements across all domains of the PCOSQ (including the emotions domain consisting of feeling depressed, moody or worried because of PCOS) versus the placebo group. There was a significant correlation between total PCOSQ score and adapted mFG score for participants in the DNG + EE group that was not seen with placebo. Significantly more participants in the DNG + EE group had regular scheduled bleeding compared with the placebo group. No new safety concerns occurred, and the safety profile of DNG + EE was similar to a previous trial of the same prolonged-release formulation20 and other COCs.21 One patient with multiple risk factors, including undiagnosed type 2 diabetes, developed a pulmonary embolism. The trial discontinuation rate (33.8%) was in line with the anticipated rate (35%).

Every participant in our trial had hirsutism (defined as an adapted mFG score of ≥7) plus oligomenorrhea and/or polycystic ovarian morphology (defined as ≥12 follicles throughout the ovary measuring 2–9 mm or an ovarian volume ≥10 cm3). This criterion for polycystic ovary morphology has been shown to have a specificity of 99% and sensitivity of 75% for the major hormonal and metabolic features of PCOS.22 We did not specify what type of ultrasound technology was used to examine ovary morphology in our trial. As ultrasound technology has improved, more recent guidelines consider the threshold for polycystic ovarian morphology as ≥20 follicles in at least one ovary if using high frequency ultrasound (≥8 MHz) or, alternatively, an ovarian volume of ≥10 cm3 if using older ultrasound technology.13

Although we did not require participants to have biochemical hyperandrogenism, at baseline our trial participants had mean free testosterone serum levels of 30.85 pmol/L and 33.79 pmol/L and FAI of 5.81 and 7.61 in the DNG + EE and placebo groups, respectively, which is similar to levels reported in other PCOS cohorts.9,23, 24, 25

The FG and mFG are the current gold standard for the evaluating and screening for clinical hirsutism.4,12,13,26, 27, 28 The original FG score uses 11 body areas (upper lip, chin, chest, upper and lower back, upper and lower abdomen, upper arms, forearms, thighs, and leg) to assess hair growth.26 The mFG excludes forearms and legs.4 Current international PCOS guidelines recommend using a standardised visual score system, such as the mFG with scores ≥4–6 defining hirsutism depending on ethnicity and acknowledging that self-treatment (i.e., hair removal) is common and can limit clinical assessment.12,13 In contrast, the adapted mFG scoring system uses a combination of body areas that have been shown to predict hirsutism, the most pertinent areas being the upper and lower abdomen, thighs and buttocks,7,28 but excludes the upper lip and chin for feasibility reasons and we used a higher cut-off of ≥7 to define hirsutism. Nevertheless, as both the upper lip and chin are highly androgen-sensitive regions, we may have seen greater adapted mFG score reductions if these facial areas had been included and could have observed higher hirsutism severity at baseline. Moreover, most participants had mild hirsutism at baseline, defined by an adapted mFG score of 7<14, making it more challenging to detect a notable reduction in the adapted mFG score than would be expected in a population with hirsutism of moderate or severe intensity. Indeed, we observed greater improvements in adapted mFG scores among participants with moderate and severe baseline hirsutism.

Obesity exerts a significant impact on the PCOS phenotype, particularly on metabolic associations and complications, including increased risk for metabolic syndrome and its constellation of cardiovascular risk factors.29,30 Moreover, greater bodyweight can have an additive effect, increasing hair growth in women with PCOS, with the maximum mFG scores observed in women with high BMI compared with women with a low BMI.31 A recent Cochrane review found substantial heterogeneity in BMI and treatment response among ten trials comparing the effects of metformin and/or COCs on PCOS-associated hirsutism.27 In our trial, reductions in LS mean adapted mFG scores were observed with DNG + EE regardless of BMI subgroup but we observed a greater treatment difference for the BMI >30 versus ≤30 kg/m2 subgroups; the placebo effect appears lower among participants with a BMI >30 kg/m2, possibly because lifestyle modifications are less effective in this subgroup.

PCOS is associated with metabolic disorders, including hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidaemia, which are considered risks factors for cardiovascular disease.12 In our trial, although serum markers of carbohydrate and lipid metabolism remained within normal ranges and HDL:LDL and total cholesterol:HDL ratios were unchanged, there was a non-significant trend towards very small increases in mean fasting insulin levels and mean concentrations of blood lipids in the DNG + EE group. These findings are supported by a meta-analysis of RCTs (33 studies; 1521 women with PCOS) that showed no significant differences between oral contraceptives and metformin in post-intervention standardised mean differences for serum glucose and odds ratios for abnormal glucose tolerance, even though the insulin sensitiser, metformin, was superior in improving surrogate indices of insulin resistance.21 Furthermore, the meta-analysis showed no significant differences among therapies in lipid profile, blood pressure or prevalence of hypertension.21 Our findings are also supported by the results of another trial of 29 women treated with an immediate-release DNG 2 mg + EE 0.2 mg in a 21/7 regimen for 6 cycles, for whom there were small increases in mean fasting serum levels of insulin and C-peptide among the women, but the values remained within the normal range, and there were no significant increases in glucose levels, glycosylated haemoglobin levels, or the insulin/glucose ratio.32 The small changes in lipid metabolism caused by the DNG-containing formulations appeared to be more favourable than those observed with levonorgestrel 0.1 mg + EE 0.02 mg due to the difference in progestin androgenicity.32 As such, prolonged-release DNG 2 mg + EE 0.02 mg is not expected to induce any clinically relevant deterioration in glucose tolerance or cardiometabolic risk factors in women with PCOS. Moreover, DNG has a thromboembolic risk similar to that of first generation progestins such as levonorgestrel,33 but without levonorgestrel's androgenic properties, making it particularly appropriate for managing PCOS.

Patients with PCOS also have altered coagulation factors,29 making it important to consider potential VTE risk associated with COC use. Existing evidence suggests that the benefits of oral contraception outweigh the risks for most women with PCOS when using the lowest effective dose of oestrogen as it mitigates any potential additional thromboembolic risk from using a COC.34 In a population-based case–control study, in patients using different types of COC preparations, the relative thrombotic risk was 0.8 (95% CI, 0.5–1.2) and 1.9 (95% CI, 1.1–3.4) for oestrogen doses of 0.02 and 0.05 mg respectively, compared to the reference oestrogen dose of 0.03 mg.35 Current international guidelines for managing women with PCOS recommend using a COC with the lowest effective oestrogen dose, such as EE 0.02–0.03 mg, balancing efficacy, metabolic risk profile, side effects, cost and availability. The relative and absolute contraindications and side effects of COCs need to be considered in relation to PCOS-specific risk factors such as high BMI, hyperlipidaemia and hypertension.12,13 Careful assessment and ruling out additional risk factors before starting treatment with COCs are needed to mitigate VTE risk among women with PCOS.36

Although current international guidelines recommend COCs to regulate menses and control clinical hyperandrogenism in women with PCOS,12,13 none are approved specifically for managing PCOS-associated hirsutism. The ideal contraceptive for women with PCOS should: (1) limit antral follicle development by inhibiting the secretion of gonadotropin releasing hormone (GnRH) and reducing LH levels resulting in less androgen production from the ovaries37; (2) counteract the effect of androgens on the pilosebaceous unit4; (3) restore the balance between oestrogens and progesterone in the endometrium, ensuring control of the menstrual cycle34; and (4) provide adequate protection against the milieu of unopposed oestrogen that may exist in some women with PCOS which predisposes them to endometrial hyperplasia and adenocarcinoma of the endometrium.38

Current PCOS guidelines state that COCs could be used in reproductive adults and adolescents with PCOS for the management of hirsutism and/or irregular menstrual cycles, but the guidelines do not currently recommend specific types or doses of progestin, oestrogen, or combinations in oral COC pills because there is only very low quality of evidence available.12,13 In terms of effectiveness in patients with hirsutism associated with PCOS, the lowest effective oestrogen doses (such as EE 0.02–0.03 mg or equivalent) can be considered first-choice COCs for the treatment of PCOS.12,13,25 In the case of the progestin DNG, it has been estimated that the antiandrogenic activity is about 30% of that of cyproterone acetate (CPA),39,40 the most potent antiandrogenic progestin.17 DNG + EE has been shown to be non-inferior to CPA + EE for the treatment of acne in a large randomised controlled trial.41 Moreover, the combination of low dose DNG + EE, as well as other antiandrogenic progestins in combination with EE, significantly reduces androgen levels, increases serum SHBG levels, and decreases total testosterone and free testosterone levels.14,42 Therefore, the DNG + EE combination has strong antiandrogenic potential but without the explicit warnings in the latest PCOS guidelines against first-line use of EE + CPA due to adverse effects including venous thromboembolic risks.12,13,25 In our current trial, favourable changes in circulating hormone levels were observed for participants in the DNG + EE group from baseline to the end of Cycle 9/EDV for free testosterone, testosterone, androstenedione, and FAI, as well as an increase in mean serum SHBG levels, which were not seen in the placebo group. Moreover, significant favourable decreases were observed for 17-OHP and DHEAS in the DNG + EE group compared with the placebo group. By using prolonged-release formulation of DNG + EE, the peak of exposure is reduced compared to immediate-release formulations and delayed by a few hours. This leads to a similar total systemic exposure with reduced peak intensity and therefore reduced hormonal fluctuations that result in a more predictable bleeding profile.20

Other clinical trials have measured improvements in PCOS-associated hirsutism using FG or mFG score reductions for COCs; however, these have used different combinations and doses of EE and progestins as well as different treatment durations and hirsutism score cut-off values, and were used in diverse, and often small, trial populations.24,25,27,28,43 There is a need for head-to-head trials with adequate sample size and standardised endpoints to help elucidate whether a particular COC formulation provides any benefit (or harm) over another for treating hirsutism associated with PCOS.

One RCT comprising 221 participants conducted in India compared desogestrel 150 μg (low androgen receptor binding), CPA 2 mg (high anti-androgen activity), and drospirenone (DRSP) 3 mg (considered high anti-androgen activity) for up to 12 months of treatment.24 Patients had been diagnosed with PCOS using the Androgen Excess Society criteria10 and included patients with oligomenorrhea (≤6 menses per year) who thought that their hair growth was excessive. There was no cut-off or quantitative definition of hirsutism for the trial. At baseline, participants had mean mFG scores of 5.55, 6.84 and 6.14 in the desogestrel (n = 58), CPA (n = 56) and DRSP (n = 57) groups, respectively. There were no significant differences after 6 months of treatment, but after 12 months of treatment the CPA group showed a significant reduction in mFG score (mean change from baseline in mFG score, −5.29) compared with either the desogestrel (−1.69; p = 0.003) or DRSP (−2.12; p = 0.02) groups. Both the CPA and DRSP groups had significant increases in serum SHBG levels after 12 months of treatment compared with baseline and compared with the desogestrel group.

Another small RCT conducted in China compared DRSP 3 mg plus EE 30 μg + metformin with CPA 2 mg plus EE 35 μg + metformin.43 All participants met the Rotterdam diagnostic criteria for PCOS44 and baseline median mFG scores of 2.0 (DRSP + EE group, n = 50) or 3.0 (CPA + EE group, n = 49). Of the 68 patients who completed the study, improvements from baseline at 6 months in mGF scores were reported for both treatment groups, but there was no difference between the two groups.

One mechanism by which DNG + EE improves hyperandrogenism may be due to the effects that 19-nor-derivatives have on inhibiting 5-alpha reductase in the skin,45 an enzyme that converts testosterone into dihydrotestosterone and is linked to hyperandrogenism and hirsutism; DNG is a 19-norprogestogen derivative. Moreover, EE directly increases SHBG,46 affecting FAI calculations and free testosterone levels (Fig. 4). In addition, the use of the COC per se is likely to be responsible for improvements in bleeding symptoms in patients with PCOS, and in our trial, these improvements were associated with the amelioration of the negative quality of life experienced by participants, as observed by the linear correlations of the PCOSQ domain scores with the adapted mFG total score. The mean PCOSQ scores improved across domains (emotions, body hair, weight, and menstrual problems), all important aspects of managing PCOS symptoms.

Rationale for managing PCOS with dienogest 2 mg plus ethinylestradiol 0.02 mg. DNG, dienogest; EE, ethinylestradiol; LH, luteinising hormone; SHBG, sex hormone binding globulin; VTE, venous thromboembolism.

Although our multinational trial was randomised and had many trial sites, which improves the generalisability of the results and provides confidence that the effects of DNG + EE can be applied across different populations, we did not prespecify or explore differences between sites as the investigators were provided with detailed instructions on how to use the adapted mFG scoring system. Nevertheless, the post-hoc sensitivity analyses by age and site support the primary analysis.

With no approved drug for managing hirsutism in PCOS, published data to inform our trial's sample size calculations were limited. Furthermore, this is the first randomised, placebo-controlled clinical trial to use the adapted mFG score. The lack of published literature to define treatment responders and predict response rates may have led to an extremely conservative assumption in our calculations, potentially impacting the observed treatment responder rates.

Caution should be taken when applying our results to other patient populations as mFG scores and hirsutism severity vary considerably.4 Moreover, in an analysis of diagnosing hirsutism among unselected diverse populations, the mFG cut-off values ranged from ≥4 to ≥8 across 11 diverse ethnic groups across eight countries, with a score of ≥7 considered ‘abnormal’ for all ethnicities combined,11 highlighting that the adapted mFG score cut-off values used in our trial for defining hirsutism severity may have also impacted interpretation of the trial results. The adapted mFG score cut-off values were prespecified in our trial. Although widely used, the FG and mFG scoring systems have limitations, including their subjective nature, high inter-reader variability, the failure to account for a locally high score that does not disproportionally skew the total score, and the lack of consideration of such androgen sensitive areas such as the sides of the face from the hairline to below the ear (sideburns) and buttocks.7 Our use of the adapted mFG attempts to mitigate some of these limitations by using standardised assessments, fewer body areas so that locally high scores are proportionally better represented in the total score, and using the lower back/buttock area as it is sensitive for predicting hirsutism. Nevertheless, it is important to highlight that the adapted mFG scoring system does not include any facial hair assessments. In our placebo-controlled trial, we wanted participants to be allowed to use their usual hair-removal regimen for their face as facial hair in women can have a detrimental social impact affecting mental health. Although it would be helpful to know the impact of DNG + EE on facial hair in PCOS-associated hirsutism, the feasibility of conducting such a trial may be problematic as many women may not choose to allow facial hair to grow, particularly if there is a placebo arm in the trial.

Although this clinical trial primarily focused on the improvement of hirsutism, we also assessed the global change in participants' quality of life using the PCOSQ score. We observed that patients with improvements in hirsutism symptoms also reported improvements in their quality of life, including improvements in the ‘emotions’ domain. Nevertheless, the PCOSQ lacks validation for assessing depression and anxiety in patients with PCOS. As there were two patients in the DNG + EE group who discontinued the study early due to mood swings, further research is required using validated psychological tools to determine the effect that the prolonged release formulation of DNG + EE has on both depression and anxiety symptoms in patients with hirsutism associated with PCOS. All patients with PCOS should be regularly screened for depressive and anxiety symptoms using regionally validated screening tools.13

In our trial we used quantitative ECLIA to measure serum parameter concentrations, including insulin, TSH, β-hCG, testosterone, androstenedione, DHEA-S and SHBG. This type of assay is used routinely in clinical practice and requires standard procedures for sample preparation; however, these serum parameter concentrations may have been more accurately measured using high-performance liquid chromatography combined with mass spectrometry.47 Nevertheless, none of the parameters measured in the serum samples analysed in our trial were below the lower limit of detection for ECLIA. Moreover, our central laboratory was able to detect free testosterone in the pmol/L range, which was sufficient for the exploratory analysis conducted in this trial.

In conclusion, the results of this trial show that oral prolonged-release DNG 2 mg plus EE 0.2 mg in a 24/4-day treatment regimen for up to 9 cycles is an effective treatment for hirsutism for women with PCOS, and was generally well-tolerated. Patients with PCOS should be screened for thromboembolism risk prior to being treated with oral COCs and should be regularly monitored in case they develop any condition, such as type 2 diabetes, that increases the risk of thromboembolism.
