Occupants and surface types drive microbial dynamics in controlled indoor environments
Authors: Jianjian Hou, Makiko Nakajima, Yukiko Nishiuchi, Daisuke Ogura, Atsushi Teramoto, China Kuratomi, Yoko Iwamoto, Yoshiko Okamura, Kazuki Moriguchi, Mateja Dovjak, Kento Takayama, Yusaku Tsugami, So Fujiyoshi, Fumito Maruyama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndoor microbial communities play a critical role in influencing indoor environmental quality and human health and are shaped by occupant activity, surface characteristics, and environmental conditions. While previous studies have examined these factors individually, systematic evaluations of their combined interactions, particularly involving Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning (HVAC) and drainage systems, remain limited. This controlled, long-term (1.5-year) investigation assessed how human occupancy, surface moisture (dry vs. wet), aquaponics (soilless plant-aquarium systems), and environmental parameters (humidity, ventilation, and seasonal variations) influence bacterial and eukaryotic dynamics in tightly sealed residential units.ResultsContinuous air-conditioner operation without fresh-air intake led to elevated CO₂ levels during occupancy and pronounced seasonal humidity fluctuations, emphasizing the need for improved ventilation and adaptive humidity control in compact urban residences. Amplicon sequencing revealed higher microbial diversity on dry surfaces (aerosols, air-conditioner filter dust, and floor dust) than on wet surfaces (waste drains and showerheads). Wet environments supported biofilm-associated taxa adapted to moist conditions (e.g., Methylobacterium, Vermamoeba). Human occupancy significantly enriched air-conditioner filter dust with opportunistic bacteria (e.g., Finegoldia and Streptococcus), underscoring occupant-driven microbial accumulation via recirculated air. Additionally, the small-scale aquaponic system had minimal measurable influence on microbial composition at the room scale, suggesting limited aerosolization or dispersal under typical usage conditions. Indoor relative humidity was significantly correlated with microbial diversity in air systems, notably enhancing moisture-adapted taxa such as Sphingomonas during humid seasons. Seasonal variations markedly influenced eukaryotic communities (e.g., pollen influx), whereas bacterial communities were more strongly influenced by human occupancy.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the critical role of human-driven microbial accumulation in air-conditioner filters and the distinct microbial profiles associated with dry and wet indoor surfaces. Although small-scale aquaponics demonstrated minimal room-wide microbial impact, its potential localized influence warrants further exploration. These insights offer practical guidance for targeted hygiene protocols, HVAC system maintenance, and building design strategies aimed at improving indoor microbial quality and supporting occupant health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-025-00775-6.

Introduction

Microbial communities in indoor environments are shaped by complex interactions involving human activity, surface characteristics, environmental conditions, and seasonal fluctuations [1–10]. Since people spend most of their time indoors, understanding how these factors influence indoor microbial diversity is essential for developing strategies to manage indoor environmental quality (IEQ) and reduce associated health risks [4, 11–14]. Indoor microbiomes originate predominantly from human occupants, pets, outdoor air infiltration, and various indoor surfaces, each contributing to distinct microbial assemblages [15]. Routine occupant behaviors, such as cooking, cleaning, personal hygiene, and interactions with indoor surfaces, significantly influence microbial emissions, particularly in compact residential spaces where limited ventilation and spatial constraints may amplify microbial exposure.

In small urban flats, environmental parameters such as temperature, relative humidity, and carbon dioxide (CO2) concentrations further affect microbial survival, growth, and distribution, particularly in moisture-prone areas like air-conditioner (AC) filters, drains, and showerheads. Wet surfaces, especially in bathrooms and drains, have gained attention as potential reservoirs for pathogens including SARS-CoV-2, highlighting the need for thorough investigations of these environments to better understand microbial transmission routes [16, 17]. In contrast, dry indoor sources such as aerosols, settled dust, and Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning (HVAC) filters accumulate distinct microbial communities over time, influenced by human activities and air circulation patterns [4, 11]. Despite advances in microbial community profiling techniques [4, 18–20], there remains a lack of comprehensive, controlled studies comparing microbial behavior in dry versus wet indoor environments.

The HVAC system plays a critical yet often underappreciated role in shaping indoor microbial communities, acting both as a pathway for microbial entry and as a reservoir where bacteria and fungi can proliferate and subsequently disperse into the occupied environment [21, 22]. Beyond regulating thermal comfort and air quality, HVAC systems significantly influence microbial dispersal, accumulation, and persistence indoors [4, 5, 11, 23]. In particular, HVAC filters have been demonstrated to effectively capture airborne microorganisms, making them valuable tools for characterizing the indoor microbiome and assessing human exposure to pathogens and allergens [24, 25]. However, the impact of heat-pump AC systems in compact urban residences, characterized by low air change rates, tight building envelopes, and limited space, remains poorly understood. Our study addresses this knowledge gap by examining a heat-pump AC system operating at a realistic but relatively low air change rate per hour (ACH of 0.31), representative of typical small urban apartments. Investigating microbial dynamics under these controlled ventilation conditions is essential for developing targeted ventilation strategies that optimize microbial management and promote occupant health, especially as more people live in energy-efficient but poorly ventilated housing [7, 26, 27].

In addition to occupant activity and HVAC-mediated microbial dispersal, indoor plants are widely recognized as significant contributors to indoor microbial diversity, with potential benefits for occupant health [4, 28–32]. However, the microbial implications of emerging soilless greenery systems, such as aquaponics (referred to in this study as"biotopes"), remain largely unexplored. Aquaponic systems, where ornamental plants interact symbiotically with aquatic organisms without soil, are gaining popularity in urban living spaces due to their aesthetic appeal and perceived health benefits [33]. Yet, their microbial contributions and potential impacts on the broader indoor microbiome have not been thoroughly investigated.

To address these critical knowledge gaps, we conducted a controlled, long-term study of indoor microbiome dynamics in actual urban apartments with carefully controlled environmental settings. Over a 1.5-year period, samples were systematically collected from multiple indoor locations, allowing for detailed tracking of microbial communities under controlled conditions. This approach differs from traditional household surveys or observational studies [3, 6, 34–36], which typically rely on participants to submit samples or observations without controlled environmental parameters. Our study rigorously examines microbial community composition, dispersal mechanisms, and environmental interactions under defined occupancy conditions. Specifically, we aimed to: (1) characterize the interactive effects of human occupancy and aquaponic systems on microbial communities across diverse indoor surfaces (air, dry surfaces, and wet surfaces); (2) elucidate how key environmental parameters (humidity, ventilation, and seasonal variation) influence microbial distributions; and (3) identify prevalent microbial taxa in distinct indoor microenvironments that may pose health risks. By systematically investigating these dynamics, this study provides insights into microbial selection pressures and dispersal pathways within the built environment, guiding practices to improve indoor microbial management and support healthier living environments.

Materials and methods

Characteristics of the experimental site, air conditioning system, and ventilation system

This study was conducted at Yamanaka Hall, a guesthouse located on the Hiroshima University campus in Higashi-Hiroshima City, Japan. The building is a four-story reinforced-concrete structure. Each floor consists of four apartment units equipped with standard residential facilities such as a bathroom, toilet, and mini kitchen, making the guesthouse comparable in amenities to a typical compact urban residence.

The experiment, outlined in Fig. 1 and Table S1, utilized three distinct apartment units on the fourth floor, designated Units #401, #402, and #403. Each unit, referred to as a “private residential room”, included a living space and bathroom, as illustrated in Figure S1. All units had identical dimensions and layouts (net floor area: 17.8 m2; conditioned volume: 42.5 m3) and were each equipped with a split-type heat pump air conditioner. This type of air conditioner is the most commonly used in Japanese residences and is designed solely to circulate indoor air for heating and cooling; it does not include any ventilation function.Fig. 1Sampling overview for indoor microbiome analysis and sampling day

Sampling overview for indoor microbiome analysis and sampling day

The building employs a mechanical exhaust-only ventilation system, with exhaust fans installed in the bathroom and mini-kitchen. Throughout the study period, the bathroom exhaust fan operated continuously, providing an ACH of 0.31 (approximately 13 m3/h). The kitchen exhaust fan was operated at the occupant’s discretion, primarily during cooking. In Japan, the installation of 24-h mechanical ventilation systems and provision of an ACH of at least 0.5 have been mandatory for new residential buildings since July 2003. However, this building was constructed before this regulation came into effect and thus conformed to the building standards in place at that time. Consequently, while exhaust fans are present in the bathroom and kitchen, there are no dedicated air inlets on the exterior walls; fresh air is assumed to enter through gaps around window frames and doors when the exhaust fans are operating. Notably, residential buildings constructed before 2003 still account for approximately 60–65% of the total housing stock in Japan. Therefore, conducting this study in such an environment provides realistic and representative insights into current residential conditions.

To minimize the influence of external environmental factors, all windows remained closed throughout the experiment, and the indoor thermal environment was controlled using air conditioners. The main distinction between Units #402 and #403 was their orientation: one faced east and the other west. This directional difference was addressed by alternating the placement of the aquaponic system (biotope) between units throughout the study.

Experimental period, occupancy conditions, and sampling schedule

The study was conducted over six distinct experimental periods between May 25, 2021, and December 18, 2022. The participation periods were defined as follows: June–July and October–November for 2021, and April–May, June–July, September–October, and November–December for 2022. Each participation period spanned two months and included two sampling events per unit, conducted approximately one month apart (Fig. 1 and Table S1). In total, 12 sampling events were conducted per unit over the course of the study.

Each “sampling session” involved collecting air and surface microbiome samples immediately following one month of continuous occupancy or exposure to a controlled condition (occupied or unoccupied). During the second month of each participation period, the same unit was occupied by a different volunteer and re-sampled after one additional month.

To assess the effect of human presence on microbial dynamics, Unit #401 remained unoccupied and served as a control throughout the study. Units #402 and #403 were occupied and used in alternating configurations–with and without the biotope—to test the impact of aquaponics on the indoor microbiome. To minimize confounding effects from occupant or unit variation, the two volunteers residing during each participation period switched rooms after one month. In total, 11 volunteers participated across the six periods, with one volunteer participating twice (Table S1).

On each sampling day, occupants temporarily vacated the units. Sterile sampling protocols were employed: trained personnel wearing disposable sterile gowns entered each unit to collect microbiome samples from designated surfaces and air. Detailed sampling procedure, schedule, and volunteer behavior guidelines are provided in Figure S2 and the Supplementary Methods.

The biotope setup, as detailed in Fig. 2, was sourced from Seseragi Biotope (https://seseragi-store.jp/pages/landing-pages), Saijo Gardens Corporation (Higashi-Hiroshima, Japan). It consisted of a small aquarium system (tank dimensions: 30 × 30 × 30 cm) featuring decorative plants placed in bamboo charcoal and water without soil. Throughout the experiment, the water level in the tank was kept consistent, with periodic additions to compensate for evaporation. No water was drained during the study. This setup was strategically placed on a television stand in the designated biotope unit, as shown in Figure S1, ensuring visibility and stability.Fig. 2The biotope setup consisting of a fish tank with decorative plants in bamboo charcoal was used in the biotope unit to assess its impact on indoor microbial communities

Measurement of IEQ parameters

Continuous measurements of selected IEQ parameters were conducted in all three units throughout the experiment from May 25, 2021, to December 18, 2022. Parameters measured included indoor air temperature, Tai (℃), relative air humidity, RHai (%), and concentration of carbon dioxide, CCO2 (ppm). Measurements were recorded at 10-min increments using a data logger (TR-76Ui, T&D Corporation, Nagano, Japan) placed on the top of the closet, 172 cm above the floor in the living spaces (Figure S1).

Samples were collected from five specific indoor locations within the three designated units: aerosols, air-conditioner (AC) filter dust, and floor dust as dry sources; and the showerhead and waste drain as wet sources. DNA extraction and sequencing

DNA extraction and library preparation were performed in a biological safety cabinet, incorporating appropriate negative controls. DNA was extracted using the repeated bead-beating and column method with the DNeasy PowerBiofilm Kit (QIAGEN, Germantown, MD, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol with some modifications [37], as detailed in the Supplementary Methods.

To comprehensively characterize the bacteria, fungi, protists, house dust mites, pollens, and other organisms associated with the indoor environment, we targeted the bacterial 16S rRNA gene and the eukaryotic 18S rRNA gene for amplicon sequencing. The bacterial 16S rRNA gene, spanning a hypervariable V3-4 region, was amplified using primers 341 F (forward: 5’- CCT ACG GGN GGC WGC AG-3’) and 805R (reverse: 5’- GAC TAC HVG GGT ATC TAA TCC-3’) [38]. The eukaryotic 18S rRNA gene, spanning a hypervariable V4-5 region, was amplified using primers 616*f (forward: 5’-TTA AAR VGY TCG TAG TYG-3’) and 1132r (reverse: 5’- CCG TCA ATT HCT TYA ART-3’) [39]. A two-round polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed to reduce potential bias introduced by MiSeq adaptor overhang [40]. Detailed descriptions of the PCR conditions and processing steps are provided in the Supplementary Methods. The finished amplicon libraries were quantified, normalized, pooled at equimolar concentrations, and sequenced using a 300 bp paired-end on the Illumina MiSeq platform.

Bioinformatics

Bioinformatics analysis was performed using the QIIME2 program (qiime2–amplicon-2023.9) [41]. Raw paired-end FASTQ reads were demultiplexed using the q2-demux plugin, followed by the removal of primer sequences from the demultiplexed data as part of quality control. The DADA2 pipeline [42] was employed to filter, trim, denoise, and merge the sequences. Chimeric sequences were removed using the consensus method, and singleton sequences were also excluded to prepare the data for further analysis.

Taxonomic classification was assigned using the SILVA database (https://www.arb-silva.de/documentation/release-138/), specifically the silva-138–99-nb-classifier. All taxonomic assignments were conducted in QIIME2 using the naïve Bayesian algorithm. For the 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequences, amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) classified as Eukaryotes, Chloroplasts, or Mitochondria were excluded from the dataset.

Each sample was randomly rarefied to a sequencing depth that achieved Good’s coverage greater than 96% for diversity analyses. Alpha diversity metrics, including observed ASVs and the Shannon index, and beta diversity based on Bray–Curtis distance were calculated at the ASV level, incorporating all observed taxa.

Statistical analysis

Data for IEQ parameters and alpha diversity metrics, including observed ASVs and Shannon indices for bacterial and eukaryotic communities, were analyzed using non-parametric tests due to the non-normal distribution of the datasets. Sample sizes for microbial community analyses are provided in the supplementary table (Table S2). Pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, while group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test. The false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure for both the Wilcoxon and Kruskal–Wallis tests, while the familywise error rate for Dunn’s multiple comparisons was controlled using the Holm–Bonferroni method.

Beta diversity was assessed using Bray–Curtis distances, and significant differences between sample groups were evaluated via permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with 999 permutations. The principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was used to visualize microbial community structures. Additionally, linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) was employed to identify biologically significant taxa among different sample groups, based on a Kruskal–Wallis test (P < 0.01, unadjusted) and a threshold logarithmic LDA score > 4.0, aiming to minimize false positives and highlight significant differences.

A correlation plot of Spearman’s rank coefficients was generated to illustrate relationships among stay rate, IEQ parameters, alpha diversities, and the top ten bacterial and eukaryotic genera in aerosol and AC filter dust samples.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.2 (www.rproject.org), using the following packages: phyloseq version 1.46.0 [43], vegan version 2.6–4, corrplot version 0.92, and the microeco version 1.4.0 [44].

Constructing co-occurrence networks

Network analysis of the association patterns between taxa was performed to infer ecological correlations among taxa within each sample source (aerosols, AC filter dust). Data were preprocessed by retaining only taxa present in at least 20% of the samples. ASVs were agglomerated at the genus level, and the analysis was conducted using the NetCoMi (Network Construction and Comparison for Microbiome Data) package (v.1.1.0) in R [45].

Association networks were constructed using SParse InversE Covariance Estimation for Ecological Association Inference (SPIEC-EASI) [46], applying the Meinshausen and Bühlmann (MB) neighborhood selection method along with the bounded Stability Approach to Regularization Selection (B-StARS) for stability selection. The resulting networks were analyzed using the netAnalyze function and visualized with the plot function.

Results

IEQ parameter profiles

IEQ parameters—including Tai (℃), RHai (%), and CCO2 (ppm)—were continuously recorded over 12 monthly periods in three residential units. Summary statistics are presented in Table S3 and visualized in Figs. S3 and S4. Due to device malfunctions, data from Unit #401 were partially missing for Sampling −2, −3, and −5.

To estimate occupancy levels, the stay rate (%) was calculated based on indoor CCO2 concentrations exceeding outdoor levels by 100–150 ppm, serving as a proxy for occupant presence during each monitoring period.

Tai and RHai exhibited clear seasonal patterns. During summer (June–July), Tai often exceeded 25 ℃, while in autumn and early winter it dropped below 20 ℃, reflecting seasonal AC use. RHai frequently exceeded 60% during the rainy season and summer, indicating higher moisture loads, and occasionally fell below 40% in spring and winter, potentially contributing to respiratory discomfort.

CCO2 levels varied significantly by occupancy. In occupied Units #402 and #403, CCO2 occasionally exceeded 1500 ppm, whereas levels remained lower and more stable in the unoccupied control unit (#401). Statistical comparisons revealed no significant differences in Tai or RHai between units (P > 0.05; Figure S5), but CCO2 was significantly elevated in occupied units (P < 0.001; Figure S5), affirming its sensitivity to human activity.

Overall, these results highlight the challenges of maintaining healthy IEQ in tightly sealed flats with limited ventilation (ACH = 0.31). Although thermal and humidity conditions were moderately regulated by air-conditioning, elevated CO2 levels in occupied flats underscore the need for enhanced ventilation strategies. Seasonal fluctuations further emphasize the importance of integrated heating, cooling, and humidity control to ensure healthy IEQ in compact urban residences.

The sequencing data and predominant bacterial and eukaryotic phyla are detailed in the Supplementary Results (Table S4 and S5).

Alpha diversity analysis revealed no significant differences (P > 0.05) between unit types across all sample sources (data not shown). LEfSe further identified significant differences in bacterial taxa between unit types in the AC filter dust and waste drain samples (Fig. 3C). In the AC filter dust of the control unit, Proteobacteria, particularly the genus of Sphingomonas, were more abundant. In the NoBio unit, an increased abundance of Streptococcus was observed, while in the Bio unit, elevated levels of the phylum Firmicutes and the genus of Finegoldia were detected.

In contrast, NoBio unit showed increased levels of Enhydrobacter, Pseudomonas, and Rhizobium, while Elstera and Gordonia were more prevalent in the Bio unit.

Beta diversity analysis using PERMANOVA revealed significant differences among the five sample sources (F = 11.942, R2 = 0.237, P = 0.001) (Fig. 5B and C). Pairwise comparisons showed the most significant differences between floor dust and showerhead samples (F = 22.261, R2 = 0.255, P = 0.001), followed by AC filter dust and showerhead samples (F = 18.194, R2 = 0.216, P = 0.001). The smallest difference, indicating closer similarity, was observed between floor dust and AC filter dust (F = 6.451, R2 = 0.090, P = 0.001), followed by aerosols and AC filter dust (F = 6.329, R2 = 0.094, P = 0.001).

These observations were further supported by PCoA plots, which displayed distinct clusters between showerhead and floor dust samples, as well as between showerhead and AC filter dust samples. Partial overlaps in microbial diversity were observed between floor dust and AC filter dust, between aerosols and AC filter dust, as well as between aerosols and waste drains. Bar plots of bacterial phyla and genera indicated that aerosols and floor dust also shared more similar bacterial compositions with AC filter dust (Figure S8).

LEfSe further identified numerous bacterial taxa with significantly different abundances across the five sample sources, demonstrating that specific bacterial phyla dominate distinct indoor environments (Fig. 5D). At the phylum level, aerosols exhibited a higher RA of Bacteroidota; AC filter dust was rich in Actinobacteriota; floor dust was dominated by Firmicute; the showerhead was characterized by Proteobacteria; and the waste drain contained high levels of Planctocycetota and Bdellovibrionota. At the genus level, aerosols were notably enriched with Bacillus, Lysinibacillus, Pelomonas, Acinetobacter, Escherichia-Shigella, Chryseobacterium, and Gardnerella. AC filter dust contained higher levels of Corynebacterium, Enhydrobacter, Pseudomonas, Streptococcus, and Finegoldia. Staphylococcus, Lactobacillus, and Shimwellia were more common in floor dust. Showerhead samples showed elevated levels of Sphingomonas, DSSF69, Bradyrhizobium, and Gemmata, while waste drain samples were enriched with Methylobacterium, Rhizobium, Phreatobacter, Caulobacter, and Bdellovibrio. These results suggest that human-associated bacteria such as Corynebacterium, Pseudomonas, Streptococcus, Staphylococcus, and Shimwellia are more prevalent in indoor dust sources.

Eukaryotic composition – sample source variations

Similarly to bacterial diversity, significant variations in alpha and beta diversities were observed among eukaryotic compositions across four sample sources (Fig. 8). These results revealed that opportunistic fungal genera such as Cladosporium and Aspergillus are more common in indoor aerosol sources, whereas allergen-associated mites like Acari and Dermatophagoides farinae dominate floor dust habitats.

Discussion

This study systematically characterized microbial dynamics within controlled residential units, emphasizing how human occupancy, aquaponic systems, and IEQ parameters influence indoor microbiomes. Key findings include the following: human occupancy significantly shaped microbial communities in AC filter dust, with notable enrichment of opportunistic bacteria such as Finegoldia and Streptococcus; aquaponic systems exhibited minimal broader influence on microbial distributions; wet and dry environments distinctly differed in microbial composition, emphasizing moisture-driven microbial selection; seasonal variations impacted microbial diversity, particularly among eukaryotic taxa such as pollen; and IEQ parameters, particularly RHai, showed clear correlations with microbial diversity and the abundance of specific taxa.

Human activity and its impact on indoor microbiomes

Human activity is a well-established factor influencing indoor microbiomes, primarily through the introduction of skin- and respiratory-associated microbes via direct contact, particle shedding, and aerosolization [4, 5, 11, 47, 48]. However, in this study, human presence showed limited impact on aerosol and floor dust microbiomes, contrary to well-documented patterns in previous research. It is presumed that this reduced influence is partly due to the design and maintenance of the indoor environment, including regular cleaning and the use of well-maintained air-conditioning systems, which likely prevented the accumulation of occupant-derived microbes on indoor surfaces and in the air. Specifically, the air conditioner filters were cleaned and dried monthly after each sampling session, potentially removing microbial residues and limiting carryover between occupancy periods.

To support this assumption, occupant influence was more evident in AC filter dust, where opportunistic bacterial genera such as Finegoldia and Streptococcus were prevalent. These bacteria, commonly associated with human skin and mucosa [5, 49, 50], suggest that occupant movement and indoor air circulation facilitate microbial transfer into AC filters. Notably, these opportunistic bacteria were present in greater abundance in units with human occupancy, implying that human activity indirectly contributes to microbial build-up in air systems. This finding aligns with previous research showing that microbial diversity in AC filters correlates with occupancy rates [8, 23, 51], highlighting occupant movement as the primary driver of microbial diversity in these environments. LEfSe analysis (P < 0.01, effect size > 4.0) further illustrated the relationship between human activity and microbial diversity. The results showed that bacteria such as Finegoldia and Streptococcus were more abundant in AC filter dust from occupied units, suggesting strong interactions between human occupants and their immediate surroundings. This pattern of microbial accumulation, driven by human presence, emphasizes the importance of routine maintenance and targeted filtration strategies in HVAC systems to mitigate exposure to opportunistic bacteria and maintain IEQ.

A notable limitation of this study relates to the limited scope of daily human activity in the apartments. Unlike typical households, the study participants primarily used the units for sleeping and were absent during the day and on weekends. Consequently, the overall stay rate was relatively low (Table S3), and opportunities for sustained microbial deposition were reduced. In particular, kitchen use was minimal: cooking activities were reported only in Unit #402 during June 2022 and in Unit #403 during July and November 2022 (Table S3). No discernible changes in aerosol or surface microbiomes were observed during these periods. However, given that cooking is a known contributor to indoor particulate matter and microbial emissions, the absence of routine kitchen use limits the generalizability of our findings. This may explain the weak signal of occupant impact on the airborne and settled dust microbiota observed in our study. This limitation underscores the importance of designing future studies that incorporate more realistic occupancy patterns, including prolonged daytime presence and diverse household activities such as cooking and cleaning. Such scenarios will better reflect microbial dynamics in typical residential environments and provide a more comprehensive understanding of human contributions to indoor microbial ecosystems.

Aquaponics: a novel but locally limited influence on indoor microbial communities

This study presents one of the first controlled investigations into how aquaponic systems—defined here as compact, self-contained “biotopes” integrating ornamental plants and aquatic components—may influence indoor microbial communities. While previous research has demonstrated that soil-based plants and proximity to green spaces can affect indoor microbiota and potentially enhance human health [4, 28–32, 52], the microbial impact of soilless systems such as aquaponics remains largely unexplored. The growing popularity of aquaponics in compact urban residences, particularly due to their aesthetic appeal and association with biophilic design, makes understanding their microbial implications increasingly important [53–55].

In our study, the aquaponic system did not produce measurable changes in the overall microbial community composition of aerosols or settled dust at room scale. This finding is significant because it suggests that passive exposure to soilless plant systems without active disruption (e.g., manual handling or water exchange) may have minimal influence on airborne or surface microbiota—at least under typical living conditions. The limited impact may also be attributed to the small size of the aquaponic setup and the absence of targeted sampling near the biotope. Moreover, no microbial samples were taken directly from the aquaponic water or plant surfaces, which constrains our ability to evaluate the system’s internal microbial ecology. Although these findings suggest minimal room-wide influence, they should not be interpreted as dismissing the microbiological relevance of aquaponic systems. Comparative studies examining traditional potted plants, hydroponic systems, and aquaponics would provide deeper insights into how different greening strategies influence indoor microbial ecology.

Interestingly, while differences in bacterial community structure were observed in waste drains between biotope and non-biotope units, these patterns were more strongly linked to moisture level and occupant behavior rather than the presence of the aquaponic system. Fungi such as Malassezia and Naganishia, known for their association with humid environments and biofilm formation, were prevalent in drains regardless of biotope status [56, 57]. This supports the interpretation that microbial communities in drains are shaped predominantly by localized environmental conditions, rather than distant plant systems.

Taken together, our findings suggest that small-scale aquaponic systems, although novel and increasingly common in urban apartments, may not substantially alter the broader indoor microbiome under normal usage scenarios. However, their potential for influencing localized microbial niches remains an open and promising area of investigation. This work provides an initial foundation for future studies exploring how indoor plant-aquatic systems contribute to microbiome dynamics in residential settings and interact with occupant health, IEQ, and broader principles of biophilic design.

Area-specific microbiome composition: dry vs. wet surfaces

This study compared microbial communities from dry (airborne) and wet (water-associated) indoor environments to explore how microhabitat characteristics influence microbial diversity and potential exposure risks. These two environments—air conditioner (AC) filters and waste drains—represent critical microbial reservoirs in compact urban residences [57–60], particularly those with limited ventilation (ACH = 0.31). AC filters accumulate airborne particles over time via recirculated air [8, 61–63], while drains are shaped by sustained moisture and inputs from hygiene-related activities [57, 60, 64–68]. Examining these contrasting environments in parallel provides insights into distinct microbial dispersal and persistence pathways within indoor settings.

As anticipated, dry and wet sites exhibited distinct microbial compositions driven by their respective environmental conditions. Dry samples (aerosols, floor dust, and AC filter dust) exhibited greater microbial richness and taxonomic diversity. This aligns with prior findings that HVAC systems and settled dust serve as accumulation points for a broad range of airborne microorganisms over time [69]. In contrast, moist sites (showerheads and waste drains) supported specialized, biofilm-associated taxa, consistent with stronger environmental selection pressures and narrower niche specialization [70]. These findings advance our understanding of how surface types and moisture conditions shape microbial niches and inform hygiene practices in compact residences.

Dry site: aerosols, floor dust, and AC filters

Aerosols and AC filter dust shared similar microbial profiles, with abundant bacterial taxa such as Bacillus, known for resilience to desiccated conditions in urban and indoor environments [71–74]. Common indoor fungi like Aspergillus and Cladosporium were also prevalent in aerosols, consistent with findings that aerosolized particles facilitate fungal dispersal indoors [11, 75, 76]. Notably, AC filter dust exhibited skin-associated bacteria like Staphylococcus, Corynebacterium, and Streptococcus, as well as gastrointestinal and genitourinary tract-associated bacteria like Finegoldia, highlighting the role of human activity in shaping AC filter microbial communities [6, 36, 77, 78]. This pattern suggests that HVAC systems function as passive collectors of occupant-derived microbes, continuously accumulating biological material from recirculated air.

In contrast, floor dust differed in microbial composition due to its proximity to surfaces where larger particles settle, such as carpet over wood flooring, which act as reservoirs for skin cells, pollen, and dust mites. House dust mites (Dermatophagoides farinae) were particularly abundant in floor dust, implicating their role in exposing occupants to allergens that can cause respiratory distress [79, 80].

Wet site: showerheads and waste drains

Wet environments supported microbial assemblages adapted to high-moisture, nutrient-rich conditions. Showerhead communities were dominated by water system-associated genera such as Sphingomonas and Hankyongella aquatica, which tolerate disinfection and survive in low-nutrient aquatic environments [81]. Waste drains harbored a wider range of microorganisms, including biofilm-forming bacteria such as Methylobacterium, known to produce pink biofilms and considered a potential opportunistic genus for immunocompromised individuals [82, 83]. Additionally, we observed a high prevalence of the amoeba Vermamoeba vermiformis, a known host of Legionella spp. [84, 85]. Although Legionella itself was only detected at low levels, the presence of its reservoir organism signals a need for attention in drain-associated microbial risk assessments.

While microbial communities in drains appeared compositionally distinct, the observed overlap with aerosols (Fig. 5) suggests possible microbial exchange or shared sources, potentially driven by splashing, evaporation, or aerosolization [17, 60, 86, 87]. This underscores the contribution of water-associated pathways to broader microbial dispersion and connectivity across indoor compartments in tightly sealed flats.

Collectively, these findings provide fundamental insights into microbial ecology and offer evidence-based guidance for targeted cleaning protocols and HVAC maintenance strategies to reduce potential microbial exposure risk in compact residential settings. Future studies should incorporate spatially resolved sampling (e.g., near drains or HVAC outlets) to better assess microbial migration between indoor compartments. Such efforts would clarify how building design, ventilation, and occupant behavior interact to shape microbial exposure, informing urban housing standards and public health policies.

Influence of building ventilation and air exchange

Ventilation plays a fundamental role in shaping IEQ and microbial community dynamics [8, 61, 88, 89]. Effective ventilation can dilute human-derived contaminants, reduce pollutant buildup, and influence microbial transport and persistence in indoor environments [89–92]. In our study, the apartment units were equipped with heat-pump-type air conditioners and mechanical exhaust ventilation systems located in bathrooms and mini-kitchens. The overall air exchange rate was relatively low (ACH = 0.31), typical for tightly sealed residential flats in Japan. As expected, this limited air exchange contributed to elevated CO2 concentrations during periods of occupant presence. Positive correlations between CO2 levels and human-associated genera, including Finegoldia, Streptococcus, and Malassezia, in AC filter dust (Fig. 9B) suggest that restricted ventilation may permit the accumulation of human-derived microbes, raising potential IEQ concerns under long-term occupancy [8, 61, 88, 89]. These findings underscore the importance of evaluating the performance limitations of heat-pump-only air conditioning systems, which are designed primarily for temperature control and do not inherently provide fresh air exchange.

Interestingly, despite the low ACH, microbial profiles in aerosols remained relatively unaffected by occupancy. This might be explained by the continuous operation of bathroom exhaust fans and the controlled building envelope, which may have enabled indirect cross-ventilation through shared hallways or minimized microbial disturbances overall. Additionally, the relatively low occupancy rate (average stay rate of 42.7%) likely reduced the overall microbial input from human activity. These factors may have collectively buffered microbial accumulation in the airborne fraction, although the effect was more visible in surface-accumulated samples such as AC filter dust. Nevertheless, these observations should be interpreted with caution. The stability of aerosol microbiomes may reflect limitations of our sampling resolution rather than true environmental uniformity. Moreover, our findings should not be generalized to typical residential conditions with higher occupancy, open windows, or variable use of kitchen and bathroom ventilation. The lack of dynamic ventilation adjustment and absence of a mechanical fresh-air supply (e.g., via energy recovery ventilation or dedicated outdoor air systems) represents a notable constraint of this setting.

In contrast, the influence of ventilation was minimal in high-humidity microenvironments such as waste drains, where microbial communities were shaped predominantly by moisture and organic material accumulation. These findings suggest that ventilation, while essential for overall IEQ, may have a limited effect in wet niches unless coupled with source control and regular cleaning.

Overall, while our study design enabled control over air exchange and ventilation parameters, the low ACH and reliance on exhaust-based systems highlight important limitations in achieving optimal IEQ in compact urban flats. Future investigations should compare alternative ventilation strategies, including demand-controlled systems or balanced natural and mechanical ventilation, and assess their efficacy in shaping both airborne microbial communities and occupant health outcomes.

Seasonal variation in indoor microbiomes

Seasonal fluctuations are a well-recognized driver of microbial dynamics in outdoor environments [93–97], but their influence on indoor microbiomes, particularly within controlled residential settings, remains less well understood. In our study, which spanned multiple seasons and years in a temperate region of Japan, we observed clear seasonal patterns, particularly among eukaryotic taxa. During spring (April–May), for example, an influx of plant-derived pollen, especially from Magnoliophyta and Pinophyta, was detected across aerosols, AC filter dust, and floor dust. These findings suggest that airborne pollen can penetrate indoor spaces despite closed windows and contribute significantly to indoor microbial diversity. The seasonal introduction of allergenic eukaryotic materials is particularly relevant for respiratory health, especially for sensitized individuals.

In contrast, indoor bacterial communities demonstrated less pronounced seasonal variation. While certain bacterial taxa, such as Streptococcus, were more abundant during colder months (November and December), overall bacterial diversity appeared more closely associated with human presence than with environmental seasonality. This suggests that occupant-related factors, such as personal hygiene, microbiome composition, and daily behaviors, may exert a stronger influence on bacterial community dynamics indoors than external seasonal inputs alone. Notably, year-to-year variability was more evident in bacterial profiles than in eukaryotic ones, especially during the June-July and October–November periods across different years. These fluctuations did not appear to align consistently with climatic data or occupancy density, further supporting that interpersonal variability (e.g., in skin microbiota) may drive subtle changes in bacterial community structure over time.

Eukaryotic communities, by contrast, exhibited more consistent seasonal trends, reinforcing the importance of external environmental inputs like pollen. However, a major limitation of our eukaryotic profiling was the use of 18S rRNA gene sequencing, which has limited taxonomic resolution for many fungi. As a result, several fungal taxa could not be confidently identified at the genus level [98], restricting the depth of ecological interpretation. To better capture the diversity and functional relevance of indoor fungi, future studies should incorporate higher-resolution phylogenetic markers, such as tefA, cadM, or rpb2, to improve taxonomic resolution and potentially reveal additional seasonal patterns [99, 100].

Overall, our findings contribute to the growing body of evidence that seasonal inputs, particularly pollen, can shape indoor eukaryotic microbiomes even in relatively closed environments. Meanwhile, bacterial communities appear more strongly shaped by human occupancy and behavioral factors. While our controlled residential model offers valuable insight into these dynamics, further studies incorporating broader geographic and architectural diversity are needed to generalize these patterns and better inform IEQ management practices.

IEQ parameters influencing microbial dynamics in air systems

IEQ parameters, especially humidity and temperature, are key determinants of microbial growth, dispersal, and survival [70, 101–104]. In this study, RHai was especially influential, showing a negative correlation with bacterial diversity in aerosols and a positive correlation with specific genera such as Sphingomonas in both aerosols and AC filter dust. For example, elevated levels of Sphingomonas in aerosols during the June–July 2022 period (Figure S6) corresponded with increased RHai (Figure S3), characteristic of Japan’s humid rainy season. This seasonal peak suggests that high ambient moisture may support the proliferation of certain environmental bacteria adapted to moist conditions [70], emphasizing the role of humidity control in HVAC systems as a potential microbial risk mitigation strategy.

Conversely, fungal genera such as Trametes and Sistotrema, commonly associated with drier or woody substrates [105], were more abundant during relatively dry periods, such as April–May and November–December 2022 (Figure S9). This pattern implies that seasonal shifts toward lower humidity may favor different fungal communities, adding complexity to indoor microbial dynamics over time.

While these observations suggest meaningful associations between IEQ conditions and microbial compositions, several limitations warrant careful interpretation. Notably, microbiome samples (e.g., aerosols and AC filter dust) were collected monthly as single-point “snapshot” samples, while IEQ parameters were monitored continuously. For correlation analyses, we used the monthly average values of these IEQ parameters to represent general conditions during each sampling interval. However, this mismatch in temporal resolution introduces uncertainty, particularly for airborne microbiome, where community composition can shift rapidly due to short-term events such as occupant activity, ventilation operation, or outdoor air intrusion [106, 107]. Thus, while correlations between average monthly IEQ data and microbiome composition help identify broader trends, they may overlook transient fluctuations and short-term environmental drivers of microbial change.

Additionally, positive correlations among human-associated taxa in AC filter dust, such as Staphylococcus, Corynebacterium, Finegoldia, and Malassezia, suggest that the relatively stable microenvironment within HVAC systems may facilitate co-occurrence or interaction among certain microbes. These associations may reflect cumulative exposure to human-derived aerosols and skin particles over the course of each month, but could also be influenced by shared ecological preferences or biofilm formation in filter materials. The complex interplay between environmental factors, occupancy patterns, and microbial community assembly within air systems remains an important area for further research.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that seasonal variations in temperature and humidity do influence indoor microbial patterns, particularly among airborne and filter-associated communities. However, methodological limitations related to sampling frequency and temporal resolution highlight the need for caution in drawing causal inferences. Future studies incorporating higher-frequency microbiome sampling, along with real-time environmental monitoring and metadata on daily occupant activity, will be essential to more fully unravel the dynamic interactions between indoor climate and microbial ecology.

Implications, limitations, and conclusions

This study provided insight into how human activity, moisture, and aquaponic systems influenced microbial communities in compact urban apartments with relatively low ventilation (Fig. 11). Even at low occupancy, human presence contributed to the accumulation of opportunistic bacteria in AC filters, highlighting the need for regular HVAC maintenance and the benefits of improved ventilation to mitigate potential microbial exposure risks. In contrast, the presence of a small-scale aquaponic system did not notably alter microbial communities on room-scale airborne or surface samples under typical usage conditions. However, localized microbial interactions near aquaponics warranted further investigation using spatially targeted sampling strategies.

IEQ parameters, particularly RHai, showed significant correlations with shifts in microbial composition, especially within airborne and HVAC filter-associated communities. Several limitations should be considered. Microbiome samples were collected monthly, while environmental data were recorded continuously, limiting temporal alignment. These factors may have reduced the generalizability of findings to more active households or better-ventilated apartments.

In conclusion, microbial communities in compact living spaces are shaped by a combination of human behavior, surface moisture, and environmental conditions. Routine maintenance, effective humidity control, and improved ventilation strategies can support healthier microbial environments, even in spatially constrained homes. Future studies should explore a broader range of occupancy patterns, multiple ventilation strategies, and building types to refine microbial risk mitigation strategies and promote safe, comfortable indoor environments in urban residential contexts.
