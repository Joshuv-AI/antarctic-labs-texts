The role of analytical treatment interruptions in shaping HIV-specific immunity and HIV cure
Authors: Mihiri Weerasuria, James H. McMahon, Sharon R. Lewin, Jillian S.Y. Lau
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose of reviewWhile existing guidance supports the use of analytical treatment interruptions (ATIs) in HIV cure clinical trials, their design must be tailored to the intervention and scientific question. As immunologically based cure strategies gain prominence, understanding how ATIs interact with HIV-specific immune responses is critical for their safe and effective implementation.Recent findingsTime to rebound ATIs evaluate how quickly HIV returns after stopping treatment and are generally safer due to limited viraemia duration. In contrast, set point ATIs measure the level at which viraemia stabilizes after rebound and may pose greater risks, as participants can experience higher viraemia before reaching a set point or demonstrating post intervention control. Shorter ATIs appear to cause only transient effects on the HIV reservoir, immune function, and inflammation. However, the long-term consequences of prolonged ATIs remain unclear due to limited data.SummaryAs HIV cure research progresses, carefully designed ATIs are essential for evaluating new therapies. Longer follow up post virological suppression should be considered, despite potential cost and logistical burdens. When collected, these data and outcomes should be reported in trial publications and shared with stakeholders.

INTRODUCTION

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) has significantly improved the life expectancy and quality of life of people with HIV (PWH) [1]. ART must be taken lifelong due to the early formation of reservoirs of latently infected cells that are the source of viral rebound when ART is stopped [2,3]. At present, biomarkers to predict viral dynamics following interventions are being investigated [4–7] but lack reliability to be utilized in clinical research. Therefore, to evaluate potential HIV cure therapeutics, analytical treatment interruptions (ATIs), wherein clinical trial participants temporarily cease ART under close monitoring, are now commonly used. ATIs are also used to investigate HIV reservoir changes, study viral rebound dynamics, and evaluate immune responses [8,9]. However, ATIs are not without risks to participants and their sexual partners [10–12]. While guidance exists on incorporating ATIs into HIV cure clinical trials [13▪,14▪,15], ART interruptions and restart criteria will differ based on interventions being tested and related research questions.

Multiple strategies for achieving an HIV cure are currently under investigation. To date, elimination of all latently infected cells has been achieved by 10 individuals following stem cell transplantation in people with concurrent haematological malignancies [16–25]. One other approach is to reduce the pool of latently infected cells and/or enhance immune control with some recent success in some but not all participants using broadly neutralising antibodies and other immune modulators, including immune checkpoint blockade [26–29]. These trials often require participants to undergo longer ATIs and tolerate higher viraemia as the presence of viral antigen is critical to immune-mediated viral control [30]. An understanding of ATI design choices and their impacts on viral reservoirs and immune responses is essential to advancing safe and effective HIV cure strategies.

NATURAL HISTORY OF HIV FOLLOWING ART CESSATION

Several studies have described the impacts of planned and unplanned treatment interruptions on PWH [8,31–34]. Following ATI, almost all PWH who have previously undetectable plasma HIV RNA levels on ART will experience a rapid viral rebound within 2–3 weeks [35–38]. A recent meta-analysis conducted by Gunst et al. found only 4% of participants maintained plasma HIV RNA <50copies/ml 84 days after ATI and median time to plasma HIV RNA >10 000copies was 32 days [39▪]. A minority of PWH will be post treatment controllers (PTC) who demonstrate sustained suppression of plasma HIV RNA after ART is ceased [40▪,41,42]. The first large group of PTCs described were the VISCONTI cohort of 14 patients who maintained plasma HIV RNA <400copies/ml for at least 24 months after ART cessation [42]. In these individuals, after an initial period of precipitous increase, plasma HIV RNA will plateau to the new viral set point [43]. Post intervention controllers (PICs), who demonstrate sustained viral suppression without ART following experimental intervention, have also been identified in recent immunotherapy HIV cure studies [29,40▪,44,45].

HIV cure therapeutics initiated during clinical trials aim to delay the time to viral rebound, reduce the viral set point or induce PIC. These therapeutics may be initiated concurrently with ART initiation [29], during stable ART [46,47], or during ATI [45,48]. The viraemia and subsequent immune response following ATI may be necessary for cure therapeutics which operate through immunological mechanisms and to detect PIC [41,49]. Upon ART recommencement, plasma HIV RNA returns to undetectable levels, for the majority this will occur within 12 weeks [50▪].

TYPES OF ANALYTICAL TREATMENT INTERRUPTIONS

ATIs are usually classified by the primary HIV virological endpoint measured following ART cessation. The two most commonly used endpoints are time to viral rebound and the establishment of a lower viral set point. TIME TO VIRAL REBOUND

Time to viral rebound ATIs measure the time a participant maintains virological control below a prespecified plasma HIV RNA threshold off ART [30]. This threshold, determined at the discretion of the trial investigators, is usually between 50 and 10 000 copies/ml [38,46,50▪] but thresholds of 100 000 copies/ml have been used [26,44,51,52]. Viral rebound is defined as when consecutive plasma HIV RNA measurements exceed a threshold (usually 50–5000 copies/ml) or if a single plasma HIV RNA measurement exceeds a much higher threshold, for example 10 000–100 000 copies/ml. After viral rebound, ART is recommenced, and the participant continues to be monitored for viral re-suppression. During the trial, participants undergo regular HIV RNA measurements according to a prespecified schedule to detect viral rebound. Commonly, this is weekly for 12 weeks and then fortnightly if virological control is maintained [13▪,50▪]. Viral rebound is thus only detectable at study visits and the exact point at which rebound occurs is unknown.

Time to viral rebound ATIs are useful to demonstrate the ability of a therapeutic to delay HIV viral rebound [53]. Several recent HIV cure clinical trials have incorporated time to viral rebound as the primary outcome measure [45,46]. Time to viral rebound may be the safest virological outcome measure as participants are able to promptly restart ART after viral rebound is detected [54]. This minimizes time spent off ART while viraemia is present and also reduces other risks such as HIV transmission. The drawback of these shorter ATIs is that for some interventions, some antigen is needed to activate the immune response and therefore a longer time of observation off ART is required to identify ART-free viral control [41].

VIRAL SET POINT

In contrast to time to viral rebound ATI trials, another approach is to assess the viral set point following ART cessation and allow for some low level viral replication [55]. Several recent HIV cure clinical trials have incorporated viral set point as the primary outcome measure [27,44,52]. Viral set point ATIs may expose participants to greater risks as they may need to tolerate some viraemia (commonly up to 100 000 copies/ml) before viral set point or PIC is observed [56], typically, these new viral set points are seen 8–12 weeks after ATI [41]. Risks include participants experiencing acute retroviral syndrome, progressive decline in CD4+ T cell count, sequelae of increased inflammation as well as the psychosocial impacts of not being on ART [30,36,57]. Additionally, participants who were initially commenced on ART during Fiebig stage I or II and are antibody negative may experience seroconversion following ATI [58].

There has been one participant death [12] in a HIV clinical trial with a prolonged ATI. In this trial, investigating a novel therapeutic HIV vaccine, a participant in the placebo arm died of a myocardial infarction after 43 weeks off ART. There have also been two confirmed HIV transmissions during ATIs [10,11] in HIV clinical trials of therapeutic vaccines. In the ANRS-LIGHT trial, a male participant transmitted HIV to his female partner after approximately one month off ART. In the second trial a male participant transmitted HIV to his male partner during an eight week ATI (viral rebound at week four). In both trials, participants and their partners were extensively counselled regarding risks of HIV transmission during ATI, however notably, preexposure prophylaxis was not offered to partners despite being available [52,59].

UPDATED GUIDANCE ON ANALYTICAL TREATMENT INTERRUPTION IMPLEMENTATION

There have been two large workshops in 2019 and 2024 during which stakeholders have generated consensus guidance on the implementation of ATIs in clinical research [13▪,14▪]. A summary of the ATI eligibility and ART restart criteria from these workshops is included in Figures 2 and 3, respectively. Inclusion criteria to ATI trials have largely remained similar, however, exclusion criteria have undergone significant changes. In the 2024 workshop, certain exclusion criteria were removed, including a CD4+ T cell nadir cutoff, and a history of HIV-associated dementia or progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy. Other criteria were relaxed – for example, only AIDS-defining illnesses indicative of severe immunosuppression, such as CMV retinitis and cryptococcal meningitis – are now excluded. New exclusion criteria were added, including prior receipt of long-acting ART or broadly neutralizing antibodies within the past two years. At the most recent workshop, there was consensus that ATI trials may safely permit longer periods of viraemia, provided they are scientifically justified by the study hypothesis [14▪]. Additionally, the diagnosis of new sexually transmitted infections (STIs), which denotes unprotected vaginal or anal intercourse, has been removed as an automatic ART restart criteria. However, this should prompt intensive participant counselling to assess and reduce the risk of HIV transmission to sex partners. Other recommended ART restart criteria - such as a CD4+ T cell count <350 cells/μl or <15%, clinical signs of HIV progression (e.g., opportunistic infections or acute retroviral syndrome), pregnancy, or participant request - remain unchanged from the 2019 guidance.

Despite the presence of consensus guidance there is still significant heterogeneity amongst trial protocols [50▪]. Participant safety should be the key priority when design ATI trials. Additionally, ATI protocols should be developed according to study objectives and the intervention's mechanism of action. Overly stringent ART restart criteria results in shorter ATIs [60] and reduced participant risk but limits assessment of therapeutics working through immunological mechanisms and the detection of people with ART-free viral control [30]. If ART has not been restarted by the conclusion of the clinical trial, investigators must have a separate protocol incorporating appropriate clinical and laboratory monitoring while the participant remains off ART.

ANALYTICAL TREATMENT INTERRUPTION EFFECTS ON VIRAL RESERVOIR

Clarridge et al. demonstrated that in shorter ATIs of under four month duration there is a transient expansion of the HIV reservoir which resolves 6–12 months after restarting ART [8]. The same finding has been noted in several other studies [33,61–63]. This temporary expansion is more pronounced and demonstrates delayed normalisation in PWH who commenced ART in established chronic infection compared to those treated within 3–6 months of infection [9]. Furthermore, short ATIs that resulted in brief periods of viraemia do not significantly alter the diversity or composition of the HIV reservoir [64]. These studies also did not find an increase in drug resistance mutations within the intact HIV proviral DNA sequences of the HIV reservoir after ART was reinitiated [8,63]. The effect of longer ATIs on the HIV reservoir is less clear. A prospective study demonstrated that following a long ATI of 48 weeks, there was an increase in integrated HIV DNA which was not recovered by 2 years post ART recommencement [65].

ANALYTICAL TREATMENT INTERRUPTION EFFECTS ON IMMUNE FUNCTION AND INFLAMMATION

Following short ATI there is a transient decrease in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells which resolves following ART resumption [8,66]. Similarly, proportions of B cells, natural killer (NK) cells, activated CD8+ T cells, most cytokines, chemokines and inflammation markers are similar pre and post ATI [8]. This suggests that short ATIs do not result in lasting immunologic abnormalities. Less is known about the impacts of prolonged ATIs on immune function and inflammation. A retrospective analysis by Richart et al. of ten prospective studies found an fourfold increase in non-AIDS events, likely inflammation driven, including malignancies, infections, and end-organ disease in participants who underwent ART interruptions longer than one year [67]. However, the study had a small sample size drawn from older trials conducted between 1999 and 2018, with over half of the ATIs lasting more than a year. Furthermore, there was no adjustment for important risk factors such as smoking status and obesity.

The true long-term impacts of ATIs on the HIV reservoir, immune function and inflammation and resultant clinical outcomes may not be captured due to many trials having little or no participant follow up after plasma HIV RNA re-suppression [26,27,50▪]. Long-term follow up after virological suppression should be considered - especially in set point ATI trials - though it may entail prohibitive costs and logistical challenges for both participants and investigators. If collected, such data and viral suppression outcomes should be reported in trial publications and communications.

ANALYTICAL TREATMENT INTERRUPTIONS IN PLACEBO-CONTROLLED TRIALS

Randomized placebo-controlled trials are considered the gold standard for assessing treatment efficacy in clinical research [68]. Single arm studies, wherein all participants receive the intervention and there is no placebo arm undergoing ATI, remain useful in early clinical research to estimate efficacy, safety and tolerability of newer therapies [30,69]. Additionally, they are usually less costly and focus resources on participants receiving interventions [69]. Historical controls from early ART therapy trials frequently serve as the comparison group for single arm studies [70]. However, virological and immunological factors including larger HIV reservoir size, higher transcriptional activity, elevated pre-ART plasma HIV RNA levels, lower pre-ART CD4+ T cell counts, and certain HIV subtypes increase the risk of viral rebound following treatment interruption [71–73]. Therefore, historical controls who commenced ART in chronic infection may not be an appropriate comparator group to modern-day participants who are more likely to start ART earlier [74]. Furthermore, sex influences HIV viral rebound dynamics following treatment interruption; thus, reliance on predominantly male historical control data may limit the validity of comparisons in trials that include female participants [71,75].

Single arm studies may also lack generalizability to populations not represented in the study limiting comparison to other trials. Additionally, these studies are liable to bias and confounding from the trial methodology from which historical trials are sourced affecting results interpretability. Placebo-controlled trials have additional advantages of blinding researchers and often participants to intervention allocation. This allows for an unbiased assessment of efficacy and adverse events and thus reduces the possibility of confounding.

Therefore, the choice to incorporate ATIs in placebo arms of HIV clinical research should depend on the stage of research, the research question being assessed, and the risks to participants. Single arm studies should be used in early phases of clinical research for efficacy and safety data, or when a randomized placebo-controlled model is not safe or practical. However, if a study requires a placebo arm to be interpretable, for instance, if appropriate historical controls cannot be found, then placebo controls should be used. At the recent ATI guidance workshop, there was strong consensus that HIV cure trials should have placebo-control arms if scientifically justified [14▪]. The perspectives of people living with HIV should be a key focus when designing clinical trials. PWH may be motivated to volunteer for clinical trials in the hopes of receiving a novel treatment and the use of a placebo arm incorporating ATI may deter them.

CONCLUSION

As HIV cure research continues to evolve, evaluating the effectiveness of novel strategies increasingly relies on the discerning implementation of ATIs in clinical research. Short ATIs appear to have self-limiting impacts on the HIV reservoir, immune function, and inflammation; however, there is a lack of data on the consequences of longer ATIs. This is concerning as assessment of immune-based cure therapeutics often requires longer ATIs with resultant higher levels of viraemia. Going forward, if feasible, longer follow up of participants after viral re-suppression should be considered to capture the long term effects of ATIs. If collected, this data, along with viral re-suppression information following ART recommencement, should be made widely available to stakeholders. Gaining a deeper understanding of how ATIs impact viral and immunological outcomes will be essential for optimizing trial methodologies and advancing toward a safe, scalable, and durable HIV cure.
