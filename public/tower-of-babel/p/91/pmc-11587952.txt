Analgesic efficacy of sacral neuromodulation for chronic pelvic cancer pain
Authors: Khaled M. Fares, Sahar A. B. Mohamed, Diab F. Hetta, Moaaz Mohamed Tohamy, Nourhan A. Elgalaly, Nayira M. Elhusseini, Fatma Adel El Sherif
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Neuromodulation has provided promising results in chronic pain management. Sacral neurostimulation (SNS) is a neuromodulatory technique, where the sacral nerve roots are electrically stimulated.Objective:Evaluate the efficacy of the trial phase to control severe cancer pain in pelvic organs.Design:Pilot study.Methods:Chronic pelvic cancer pain patients were managed with morphine ≥ 60 mg daily, duloxetine 30 mg, and celecoxib 200 mg twice daily. Twenty-two adults were divided randomly into two equal groups: the SNS group (N = 11), received a sacral neurostimulator device and opioid analgesics, and the opioid group (N = 11), received increased opioid analgesics to control pain.Results:Visual analog scale scores were decreased in the SNS group at 24 h, 4 days, 1, and 2 weeks in SNS compared to the opioid group, medians (IQR) [4 (3–5) vs 6 (5–7), 3 (3–4) vs 5 (5–6), 3 (3–4) vs 5 (5–6), and 3 (3–3) vs 5 (4–6) P < 0.001], daily morphine consumption was reduced in the SNS group compared to the opioid group, median (IQR) [90 (60–90) mg vs 120 (120–150) mg, P < 0.001] and [30 (30–60) mg vs 150 (120–180) mg] during the first and second weeks. The SNS group had a better life quality compared to the opioid group after 2 weeks, median (IQR) was (123 (122.5–124) vs 117 (117–118.5) P < 0.001), respectively.Conclusion:The SNS trial device showed effectiveness in managing severe chronic pelvic cancer pain.

Introduction

The pelvic organs are heavily innervated by diversity of network of sympathetic, parasympathetic, and somatic fibers, so when cancer arises in this region, the pain might reach 95% and control of the pain becomes a difficult task.[1] Because of the particular neuroanatomical and functional variations that are specific to the pelvis and result in a diversity of pain generators, managing pain associated with pelvic malignancies is challenging.[2] Furthermore, the persistence and chronic nature of pain perception is supposed to be the overlap and convergence between somatic and visceral pain circuits.[3]

Previous well-designed studies on managing pain owing to malignancies in the pelvis revealed that, 60% to 70% of patients improved their pain after treatment with superior hypogastric plexus (SHP) neurolysis, and that this benefit is increased when pulsed radiofrequency is applied to the sacral roots,[4] Furthermore, ganglion impar denervation has been shown to control perineal pain from both oncologic and benign causes in 51% to 90% of patients.[56] However, these therapies were only short-term, lasting 3 to 6 months at most.[46]

The scientific background of sacral neurostimulation (SNS) is through electrically stimulating the sacral dorsal root ganglion with an implanted device, primarily at the level of S3 ganglia.[7] However, the changes that occur by the device in neurotransmitters and in pain-transmitting fibers are not fully understood. However, it has been proposed that it modulates the spinal cord reflexes and brain-perceiving neurons.[89]

Although the usage of SNS for pain management is still considered off-label,[10] it has been included in the therapy protocol for refractory pelvic pain.[11] In this preliminary trial, we evaluated the effect of the trial phase, SNS on cancer-related severe persistent pelvic pain.

1. Enrollment and Eligibility

1. Enrollment and Eligibility

1. Enrollment and Eligibility

This pilot study was a prospective, randomized, clinical trial. The study has been approved by the South Egypt Cancer Institute’s Institutional Review Board for Anaesthesia and Pain Management at Assiut University, IRN number: IORG0006563.

The authors registered the trial prospectively at Clinical Trials.gov with an ID number; NCT04549818. Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statements were used to properly report the results of this trial patient received an explanation of the procedure’s technological advantages, expectations, and risks. Written informed consent was signed by each included patient before enrollment.

Adult patients with chronic cancer pain in the pelvic region alone or pelvic and perineal region (pain duration ≥ 6 months), complaining of severe pain score [≥6 out of 10 on the standard visual analog scale (VAS) pain score] despite treatment with strong opioids (oral morphine equivalents ≥ 60 mg daily) and adjuvant treatment (duloxetine 60 mg daily, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, celecoxib 200 mg daily) were enrolled in this study. Importantly, we enrolled patients who had a previous operation for pelvic cancer and completed their chemoradiation, and the expected survival was more than two years.

The exclusion criteria were patients with distant metastasis, inoperable cancer that had not been resected, patients with a mental or psychological illness that impairs their judgment, infection at the site of insertion, coagulopathy, and abnormal sacral anatomy that masks visualization of the sacral foramina.

2. Randomization and Allocation

2. Randomization and Allocation

2. Randomization and Allocation

We randomly divided the participants into two groups, equal in size using electronically generated tables, masked in opaque sealed envelopes that were opened immediately before patient allocation by an independent researcher who is not involved in the study, SNS group (N = 11), where the sacral neuro-stimulator device had been implanted and postoperative opioid analgesics had been delivered according to analgesic response and opioid group (N = 11), where the opioid analgesic had been increased to control pain without insertion of SNS.

3. Interventions

3. Interventions

3. Interventions

A prophylactic antibiotic (ceftriaxone 2 gm) was given 30 min before the sacral nerve stimulator (InterStimTM) implantation procedure, which was performed under sterile conditions with C-arm fluoroscopic guidance.

4. Technique of Sacral Nerve Stimulation

4. Technique of Sacral Nerve Stimulation

4. Technique of Sacral Nerve Stimulation

Patients were placed in a prone position on the operating table, with a pillow placed under the pelvic region and another pillow placed under the ankle region. The entry point was determined in the region between S2–S4 with the anteroposterior view of C-arm fluoroscopic guidance, targeting the S3 or S4 foramen [Figure 1]. Then, the entrance point was locally anesthetized with 2–3 ml lidocaine 1%. A 20-gauge needle, 15 cm in length was inserted 2 cm cephalic to S3 or S4 foramen accordingly, with a 60-degree angle in a caudal direction, then the depth of the needle was checked in the lateral view [Figure 2]. After placement of the needle, to confirm the desired needle placement, we connected the test stimulation cable to a non-insulated portion of the foramen. Once the test stimulation cable was connected, pair the programmer to an external nerve stimulator (ENS).

Fluoroscopic AP view of the sacrum showing the sacral foramina and the stimulation needle targeting the S4 foramen

Fluoroscopic lateral view of the sacrum showing the pulse generator electrode inserted at the S4

The ENS gradually increased its amplitude to obtain the desired motor and sensory response at a low amplitude level. The desired S3 motor response of the pelvic floor muscle was inward and deepening of the intergluteal fold (the Bellows response), and the foot response of S3 was plantar flexion of the big toe. In female patients, S3 sensory response was vaginal sensation extended to the perineum and rectum. In male patients, it was paranesthesia at the base of the penis that extended to the scrotum and rectum. In some patients, S4 sensory stimulation was observed as a perianal sensation. The stylet of the needle was removed, and replaced by a directional guide wire.

At that moment, the needle was removed leaving the guide wire in place, upon which an introducer sheath and a dilator were advanced to the S3 foramen. Then, the wire and the dilator were removed, leaving the introducer sheath in place. However, a tined lead with 4 electrodes was inserted through the introducer sheath and advanced to the proper depth, which was confirmed by obtaining a good response upon stimulating the 4 electrodes. The introducer sheath was removed along with the lead stylet, and another test for confirming the position of the lead was conducted [Figure 3].

Fluoroscopic AP view of the lumbosacral region: On side (a), the electrode of the pulse generator inserted at the 4th sacral nerve root, and on side (b), the site of the implanted pulse generator

An identification and incision of the connection site were done below the iliac crest, and lateral to the edge of the sacrum. A tunnel from the lead to the connection site was created in the subcutaneous fat, to allow for the passage of the lead to the connection site. Another tunnel from the connection to the exit site was created, through which a percutaneous extension was passed. Then, the lead was inserted and connected to the percutaneous extension, and then the connection was inserted in a pocket created in the subcutaneous fat at the connection site, the pocket was irrigated with an antibiotic-containing solution, and finally, the skin incision was sutured. The percutaneous extension was connected to the ENS and the patients were instructed on how to use the external stimulator [Figure 4].

The lower back of the patient showing sites of needle insertion, percutaneous extension exit site, and the pocket for the implanted pulse generator

Patients were deemed to have a positive test response if a 50% or greater improvement in their pain was noted compared to baseline during the 2-weeks trial. Patients with a positive test response were candidates for implantation of a permanent stimulator.

5. Post-Procedure Follow-Up

5. Post-Procedure Follow-Up

5. Post-Procedure Follow-Up

The change in pain intensity on a standard 10-cm VAS[12] at 24 h and 4th day then one- and two-weeks post-procedure, the daily oral morphine consumption, the change of life quality measured by the life quality questionnaire (QLQ C-30),[13] at two weeks post-procedure were gathered and analyzed. QLQ C-30 was administered to the patients before the initiation of treatment and then once again during the treatment. Patients with higher scores have better life quality.

Post-procedural analgesic treatment consisted of a continuation of a pre-procedure dose of strong opioid drugs that were titrated according to the patient’s pain intensity. For the opioid group, the opioid (morphine) has been increased to control pain.

Study Outcomes

The authors considered that the primary outcome of the current study was the number of participants who showed a reduction in their pain intensity by >50% when measured by VAS score compared with baseline values. The secondary outcomes were the pain severity measured by VAS score at 24h, 4th day, and one week post-procedure. Morphine consumption by (mg) at one week and two weeks post-procedure. The change in quality of life was measured by (QLQC-30) at two weeks post-procedure. Ethical committee IRB approval number: 199, approval dated on 1/1/2015.

Statistical methodology

We entered the data of the patient, case by case, through the SPSS sheet. After completion of the project, we proceeded to statistical analysis, we arranged the data to numerical data such as age, BMI, VAS pain scale, morphine consumption, and QLQ C-30 and categorized using means and standard deviations or medians, interquartile ranges and Categorical data such as gender and type of cancer arranged as numbers and percentages. Before proceeding to inferential statistics, we applied tests of normality, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and the Shapiro–Wilk test to obtain accurate results. For age and BMI, we used the student’s t-test, while for the VAS pain scale, morphine consumption, and QLQ C-30, comparisons were conducted by the Mann-Whitney test. Chi-square was used for gender and type of cancer. The significance level was set at P values < 0.05.

Sample size

The use of pelvic neurostimulators for cancer pain is a novel technique and had not been studied before so this trial is a preliminary pilot study of 10 cases in each group. Further studies can use our trial for sample size calculation.

Results

Thirty-five patients were assessed for eligibility to participate in this study. Five patients were excluded from the study as they refused to participate. Eight patients were excluded because they were not candidates for the inclusion criteria. A final number of twenty-two patients completed the study and were divided into two groups with 11 patients in each group.

Discussion

In our pilot study, we found that the group of patients who complained of severe pelvic pain or pelvic pain associated with perineal chronic cancer pain treated with a sacral neurostimulator device achieved ≥ 50% reduction in pain intensity with a significant reduction of opioid analgesics in comparison to their preoperative values, resulting in a significant improvement in quality of life after two weeks of implantation of sacral neurostimulator device. In contrast, the opioid group who were treated medically with an increased opioid dose failed to lower pain intensity by > 50% of their basal values, resulting in no improvement in life quality.

SNS has been licensed by the Food and Drug Administration to treat refractory overactive bladder symptoms and non-obstructive urine retention.[1415]

SNS has been recently a popular form of neuromodulation used to treat chronic pelvic pain.[10] The use of SNS for pain alleviation was based on the fact that pain symptoms decreased in individuals who had SNS for urine frequency and urgency management.[1617] Although the use of SNS for pain management is still considered off-label,[10] it has been included in the therapy protocol for refractory pelvic pain.[11]

Previous research has shown that electrical stimulation of sacral roots has analgesic properties in a variety of genitourinary pain conditions. According to Feler and colleagues,[18] sacral electrical stimulation is an efficient analgesic method in the treatment of chronic pelvic and perineal discomfort caused by non-bacterial prostatitis, epididymo-orchialgia, and vulvodynia. However, McJunkin et al.[19] found that SNS was effective in treating neuropathic testicular discomfort. Furthermore, Kim et al.[20] reported that sacral stimulation effectively treated intractable pain of cauda equina syndrome. In some investigations, sacral stimulation application is shown to give adequate pain relief in individuals with anorectal pain.[2122] Martellucci et al.[23] indicated that sacral stimulation is helpful in patients with various chronic pelvic pain. In systematic research, neurostimulation applications are recommended to be effective, safe, and sustainable treatment in chronic pelvic pain.[24]

Neuromodulation is thought to reduce the firing of pain to the brain.[252627] The underlying mechanism of action is most likely consistent with Melzack and Wall’s[26] gate control theory, which states that neuromodulation may stimulate large myelinated afferent nerve fibers of the dorsal horn to mask pain signals transmitted through the primary afferent nociceptive fibers, thereby closing the gate and inhibiting further transmission of pain signals. Hanai’s work on cats verified this theory.[28] Other studies have argued that a pain signal can be modulated in the neural system other than the dorsal horn neurons.[29] Furthermore, the neuronal circuitry of pain has been proposed as the fundamental biological mechanism for neuromodulation.[25]

Because of the complexity of pain transmission, there is no single theory that can explain the mechanism of pain relief when stimulating the sacral roots. Creating an electric field through the sacral neurostimulator near these nerves could inhibit the transmission of all impulses from the pelvic and perineal areas. Particularly concerning was the fact that one of eleven instances was exacerbated by infection, and the device was removed. It is worth noting that we had two patients who, in addition to their pelvic pain, complained of post-surgical fecal incontinence and reported that their continence had improved two weeks after SNS implantation.

In this study, we assessed the short-term analgesic efficacy of the SNS device in the trial phase, and future research should focus on the long-term analgesic effect of the permanent implant.

Study Limitation: The study is limited by the limited number of participants and a large size multicenter study is required for further studies, the short follow-up period, and the high cost of the device. Owing to the high cost of the device, it is better to be reserved for patients operated on for pelvic cancer, and the expected survival was estimated to be more than two years, with intractable pelvic pain that showed poor response to opioid analgesics. Opioid consumption as a parameter has no added value because we increased opioids in the opioid group. Life quality can be influenced by increased opioid dose. Moreover, the study was designed with a superiority concept, so we can state that neuromodulation is more effective than increasing opioids.

Conclusion

The authors concluded that the trial phase of the SNS device was successful in controlling severe chronic pelvic cancer pain; as it achieved ≥ 50% reduction of pain intensity with a significant reduction in opioid consumption and improvement of life quality.
