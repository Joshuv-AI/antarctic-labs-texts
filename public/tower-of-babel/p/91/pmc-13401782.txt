Variation in the access and use of biologic and targeted synthetic DMARDs in juvenile idiopathic arthritis: insights from a national UK survey
Authors: Samantha Cooray, Kalindi Rajani, Sandrine Compeyrot-Lacassagne, Ethan S. Sen, Catriona Anderson, Andrew Fell, Dearbhla McKenna, Clara Danielsen, Mario Cortina-Borja, Coziana Ciurtin, Ameenat Lola Solebo, Paul Brogan, Arthritis UK Paediatric Rheumatology Clinical Studies Group (CSG), JIA Topic Specific Group and the Scottish Paediatric and Adolescent Rheumatology Network (SPARN)
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBiologic and targeted synthetic disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (b/tsDMARDs) have transformed outcomes for juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA). However, contemporary understanding of real-world treatment selection, and access across the United Kingdom (UK), remains limited. Commissioning pathways, service configuration, and age- and specialty-based prescribing frameworks may contribute to variations in care. We describe current perspectives of paediatric and adolescent rheumatology multidisciplinary teams (MDTs) on the selection and access to b/tsDMARDs for JIA across the four UK nations.MethodsA 26-question survey was distributed to MDTs across UK paediatric and adolescent rheumatology centres via national networks. Data on MDT composition, b/tsDMARD choice, commissioned pathways, dosing constraints and barriers to access were analysed by JIA subtype, UK region, age, and specialty pathway. Heatmaps and non-parametric permutation testing were used to quantify treatment patterns. Free-text responses underwent qualitative thematic analysis.ResultsResponses were received from 13 of 17 (76%) tertiary and quaternary centres across the four UK nations, representing 123 MDT professionals. Adalimumab was most commonly used first-line for all JIA subtypes except systemic JIA (sJIA)/Still’s disease. Tocilizumab was frequently used second-line across multiple subtypes, including polyarticular disease and uveitis, and was widely used first- and second-line in sJIA/Still’s. Interleukin-1 inhibition with anakinra was commonly used in sJIA, particularly in macrophage activation syndrome (MAS). In England and Wales, access to canakinumab for sJIA was unavailable despite supportive trial evidence, and adalimumab was the only commissioned therapy for JIA-associated uveitis. Paediatric access to anti–IL-17 agents for enthesitis-related arthritis (ERA) was limited. Broader therapeutic choice and higher biologic dosing were more accessible via dermatology or gastroenterology pathways, for example, for psoriatic JIA and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)-associated arthritis. Adult/adolescent rheumatologists reported wider biologic access, though reclassification to adult diagnoses was sometimes required. Biosimilars were universally adopted, but specialist pharmacy support for implementation was inconsistent. External advice was sought for refractory disease, including consideration of haematopoietic stem cell transplantation and combination biologic therapy.ConclusionsSelection and access to effective b/tsDMARD therapies for JIA varies considerably across the UK, shaped by commissioning structures, age thresholds and specialty pathways rather than clinical need. National alignment of services is urgently required to ensure equitable, timely access to evidence-based therapies for children and young people with JIA.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12969-026-01235-2.

Introduction

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is the most common chronic inflammatory joint disorder in children, with an annual incidence of 5.61 and prevalence of 43.5 per 100,000 children, approximately 1 in 2,300 children in the United Kingdom (UK) [1]. Active disease persists into adulthood in up to 60% of cases [2, 3], although epidemiological data on adult prevalence of JIA is lacking. JIA encompasses several clinically-distinct subtypes, including oligoarticular, rheumatoid factor (RF) positive/negative polyarticular, psoriatic, enthesitis-related arthritis (ERA) and systemic JIA (sJIA)/Still’s disease, each with distinct clinical features [4]. Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)-associated arthritis with childhood onset, although not an International League of Associations for Rheumatology (ILAR) subtype, often requires specialist rheumatology input for joint disease [4, 5]. Uveitis is a common extra-articular manifestation affecting 10–30% of patients that can precede arthritis, particularly in anti-nuclear antibody (ANA)-positive individuals [6]. Systemic JIA, distinct for its autoinflammatory features, may include macrophage activation syndrome (MAS), a life-threatening hyperinflammatory complication.

The therapeutic landscape for JIA has been transformed by the introduction of biologic and targeted synthetic disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (b/tsDMARDs), that target tumour necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, IL-12, IL-17, IL-23, CD20 and Janus activated kinase (JAK) pathways. Randomised-controlled trials, though limited in children, have demonstrated b/tsDMARD efficacy and safety across JIA subtypes and JIA-uveitis [7–17], informing international treatment guidelines [18–22]. Despite this evidence base, access to b/tsDMARDs in the UK remains inconsistent. Availability is influenced by commissioning frameworks, age- and indication-specific licensing, and specialty-based prescribing pathways, leading to substantial variation in access across the UK. These challenges are further underscored by the recent ‘Getting It Right First Time’ (GIRFT) report on paediatric rheumatology services in England, which identified unwarranted variation in b/tsDMARD access and prescribing delays due to commissioning and infrastructure constraints [23]. These discrepancies highlight major challenges in providing equitable care for children with JIA across the UK, potentially delaying or limiting access to therapies, risking long-term complications.

To better understand these challenges, we conducted a national survey through the Arthritis UK Paediatric Rheumatology Clinical Studies Group (CSG) and the JIA Topic Specific Group (JIA-TSG), with input from the Scottish Paediatric and Adolescent Rheumatology Network (SPARN). Established in 2007, the CSG supports high-quality paediatric rheumatology research across the UK, including disease-specific paediatric rheumatology working groups like the JIA-TSG [24]. SPARN promotes equitable care across Scotland through coordinated service delivery, shared clinical guidance and specialist pharmacy support. This study aimed to evaluate real-world access to b/tsDMARDs for paediatric/adolescent rheumatology services across the four UK nations (England, Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland), identify regional, age-related, and specialty-specific variations in use, and highlight disparities that could inform commissioning policy and guideline development to support equitable and timely care for patients with JIA across the UK.

Methods

Survey design

A 26-question cross-sectional survey, comprising both closed-response and optional free-text fields, was developed in consultation with members of the CSG, JIA-TSG and SPARN, including allied health professionals, patient advocates and charities. Survey completion instructions were discussed during UK JIA TSG meetings and outlined within the survey introduction. The survey was designed for completion by MDTs rather than individuals to reflect institutional prescribing practice. A single respondent submitted responses on behalf of each centre following prospective discussion during routine paediatric rheumatology MDT meetings, with invited participation from cross-specialty colleagues including gastroenterology, dermatology and ophthalmology, or alternatively following respondent participation within the relevant cross-specialty MDT.

The main section of the survey focused on b/tsDMARD treatment preferences for JIA subtypes and related conditions: rheumatoid factor (RF) positive/negative polyarticular JIA, oligoarticular/extended oligoarticular JIA, psoriatic JIA, ERA, JIA-associated uveitis, sJIA and MAS, as well as IBD-associated JIA. Respondents were asked to indicate first- to fourth-line b/tsDMARD selection preferences for each condition. Individual therapies could be selected for multiple treatment lines, to capture therapeutic flexibility. Multiple agents could also be chosen per line to reflect equivalent/interchangeable use (e.g. anti-TNF agents). The b/tsDMARD list included all therapeutic agents licenced and commissioned (funded via agreed National Health Service-NHS pathways) for JIA or associated conditions (e.g. IBD/psoriasis); post-pubertal and adult rheumatological indications; or off-label use (Supplementary Table 1). Free-text fields captured reasons for order of preference, desired but non-commissioned drugs, patient factors e.g. tolerance, off-label use and other access limitations.

The survey also captured MDT demographics, specialist pharmacy support, biosimilar use, combination therapy, disease reclassification in transitioning adolescents, sources of external advice, and the influence of side effects and safety considerations on treatment choice.

Distribution

The survey was distributed via email to UK paediatric and adolescent rheumatology centres via national and regional networks. Responses were collected over three months, mid-January to mid-April 2025, using Google Forms (Google LLC, 2024). A single MDT member was asked to input the responses for their centre.

Data review and statistical analysis

Survey responses were collated and processed in Excel and R (version 4.4.1) [25]. For each MDT, JIA subtype and treatment line, binary matrices were generated for each b/tsDMARD selection, which served as the input for statistical analyses. Heatmaps were produced in GraphPad Prism (v10.3.1) to visualise aggregated first- to fourth-line selection frequencies across JIA subtypes, consistent with published survey visualisation methods [26, 27].

To assess whether particular b/tsDMARDs were selected significantly more than expected under the null hypothesis of random choice, a custom permutation testing framework was implemented in R (tidyverse suite of packages including dbplyr and tidyr) [28]. For each b/tsDMARD and JIA subtype–treatment line combination, the observed frequency of selection across MDTs was compared to null distributions generated from 10,000 constrained permutations. In each permutation, b/tsDMARD choices were randomly reassigned within each centre, while preserving the total number of centre selections made. This permutation design accommodated the non-independent nature of the data by preserving the dependence-structure inherent to multi-therapeutic, multi-line selection and controlling for centre-level variation. p-values were computed as the proportion of permutations in which the frequency of selection for a b/tsDMARD equalled or exceeded the observed count. This methodology aligns with published frameworks for analysing multivariate and dependent survey data [29, 30].

Free-text responses were analysed thematically, using an inductive approach, to contextualise quantitative findings. Two investigators (SC, KR) independently coded and consolidated themes. Data were managed in Excel, and representative quotes were used to illustrate key themes.

Results

Demographic and MDT information

Responses were received from 13/17 tertiary/quaternary centres (76%): 12 specialist paediatric rheumatology centres and one dedicated adolescent/young adult rheumatology centre across UK nations [England 8, Scotland 2, Wales 1, Northern Ireland (NI) 1; Supplementary Table 2]. Total MDT contributions comprised 123 professionals: 54 consultants (including paediatric and adult rheumatologists, gastroenterologists, dermatologists and ophthalmologists), 18 resident doctors, 24 clinical nurse specialists (CNS), 9 specialty pharmacists and 18 allied health professionals (Supplementary Figure 1). All centres included at least one CNS and ≥2 rheumatology consultants; 5/13 (39%) provided patient care beyond the age of 18 years. Specialist rheumatology pharmacists were present in 9/13 (69.2%) centres. Centres in Scotland lacked specialist pharmacists but were recruiting, and had SPARN network pharmacy support, commenting: “It can be difficult to attract pharmacists to acute in-hospital settings” and “though funding exists, it isn’t always straightforward to access” (Supplementary Table 3). A centre in Wales had a business case for specialist pharmacy provision rejected, with an experienced pharmacist overseeing multiple paediatric departments, a practise also observed in some centres in England (Supplementary Table 3).

Selection of b/tsDMARD for JIA subtypes across the UK

Oligoarticular and extended oligoarticular JIA

Biologic therapy was generally reserved for severe joint disease, in line with NHS England commissioning, though centres noted ‘severe disease’ was undefined (Supplementary Tables 5–6) [31]. Three centres managed oligoarticular and extended oligoarticular JIA similarly, with one noting “Transcriptomic studies suggest they are part of the same spectrum” (Supplementary Table 5) [32, 33]. Adalimumab and etanercept (13/13 and 9/13 centres respectively, p < 0.0001) were preferred first-line agents (Fig. 1A–B; Supplementary Permutation Tables A–B). Intravenous (IV) and subcutaneous (SC) tocilizumab were the most common second-line agents (each 8/13 centres, p < 0.0001), with anti-TNF agents also common. SC/IV tocilizumab and infliximab were common third-line, with abatacept and tofacitinib used third-fourth line choices (Fig. 1A–B; Supplementary Table 5–6). Etanercept was avoided in patients with concomitant uveitis (Supplementary Tables 5–6). Centres in Scotland reported the ability to “Escalate to biologics early to control aggressive disease” and “Patients do not have to have had an inadequate response to methotrexate first”. JIA uveitis

Adalimumab was first-line for JIA-associated uveitis (13/13 centres, p < 0.0001), at a two-weekly dosing interval (Fig. 1C; Supplementary Permutation Table C; Supplementary Tables 4 and 7). Two centres also accessed local funding (i.e. paid directly from their hospital rather than NHS-commissioned ‘pass-through’ funding) for weekly adalimumab dosing (Supplementary Table 7). Second-line choices included IV/SC infliximab (11/13 centres, p < 0.0001 and 6/13 centres, p = 0.0004, respectively); and third-line IV/SC tocilizumab (8/13, p < 0.0001 and 5/13, p = 0.001, respectively). Tofacitinib was mainly used after other agents. Access was limited in England and Wales (but not in NI or Scotland) for children without arthritis and adults with persistent uveitis, often requiring local funding for therapies beyond adalimumab (Supplementary Table 7).

RF-positive and RF-negative polyarticular JIA

First-line preferences for RF-positive polyarticular JIA were adalimumab (13/13, p < 0.0001) and etanercept (7/13, p = 0.0002) (Fig. 1D; Supplementary Permutation Table D; Supplementary Tables 4 and 8). IV/SC tocilizumab was the predominant second-line choice, with infliximab/tofacitinib used third line and rituximab used third and fourth-line (8/13, p < 0.0001; Fig. 1D). Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) was considered for refractory cases following b/sDMARD treatment failure (Supplementary Table 8). RF-negative disease followed a similar pattern; however, oral tofacitinib was used earlier in some centres, particularly if poor tolerance to IV/SC therapy (second-line 6/13, p = 0.0034) (Fig. 1E; Supplementary Permutation Table E; Supplementary Tables 4 and 9). Third-fourth line therapies included tocilizumab, anti-TNF switching and use of abatacept, rather than rituximab (Fig. 1E). Centres also reported occasional later-line use of secukinumab, ustekinumab and golimumab, typically via adult services or local funding (Fig. 1E; Supplementary Table 9).

Systemic JIA (sJIA) and macrophage activation syndrome (MAS)

Anakinra and IV tocilizumab were predominant first- and second-line therapies and anakinra was preferred in the context of MAS, reflecting national commissioning rules (Fig. 1F; Supplementary Permutation Table F; Supplementary Tables 4 and 10) [31, 34]. Tocilizumab was generally reserved for sJIA without MAS, with one respondent noting: “Ideally, measure IL-18; if very high there may be an increased risk of MAS with tocilizumab” (Supplementary Table 10) [35]. Anti-interferon-gamma (IFNγ) antibody emapalumab for sJIA-MAS was restricted, available only via compassionate-use request to the pharmaceutical company (SOBI) or via a recent pharma-sponsored clinical trial. [9] Centres commented on few available treatments for MAS beyond glucocorticoids, older DMARDs e.g. cyclosporine/etoposide. Allogeneic HSCT was considered in refractory-MAS. Canakinumab was available and used second/third line in Scotland/NI; it was not commissioned in England and Wales despite high-level evidence of efficacy and safety from a randomised-controlled trial [15], (Fig. 1F). Where available, JAKi, was used by some centres, usually for persistent arthritis in the absence of systemic features (Supplementary Table 10).

Psoriatic JIA

Adalimumab and etanercept predominated first- and second-line choices (both p < 0.0001; Fig. 1G; Supplementary Permutation Table G; Supplementary Table 4). Dermatology respondents noted that older anti-TNFs were less favoured due to limited efficacy (etanercept) and risk of paradoxical psoriasis (infliximab/etanercept) in some patients (Supplementary Table 11 [36]). Secukinumab was widely used second- to third-line (11/13 and 8/13 centres, respectively; both p < 0.0001); ustekinumab was reserved for third- or fourth-line use (7/13 and 6/13 centres, p = 0.0006 and p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1G). Respondents highlighted discrepancies in specialty-specific prescribing: dermatology services reported higher dosing thresholds for secukinumab (up to 300 mg weekly for patients > 50 kg with moderate–severe plaque psoriasis, versus a licenced maximum of 150 mg weekly for arthritis) (Supplementary Table 11). Pharmacists noted: “More medications are licenced for psoriasis than for psoriatic JIA and rheumatology services are trying to access these medications by requesting licenses are extended for psoriatic arthritis (e.g. guselkumab and ustekinumab), which are now available but previously only accessible via trials” (Supplementary Tables 11 and 11).

b/tsDMARDs for ERA were largely limited to anti-TNF agents (Fig. 1H; Supplementary Permutation Table H; Supplementary Table 4). Secukinumab and tofacitinib were selected second-fourth line a few centres. Concern was expressed about inequitable access between paediatric and adult services, with newer IL-17 inhibitors available to adults, but not children, under current NHS England commissioning criteria: “Secukinumab is commissioned for ankylosing spondyloarthritis (AS) in adults, but not for ERA, as ERA is not recognised as equivalent”. In contrast, centres in Scotland had “Early access to (a range of) b/tsDMARDs for axial disease” (Supplementary Table 12).

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)-associated JIA

Adalimumab and IV infliximab were the predominant first-line choices, with infliximab also common second line (8/13 centres; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1I; Supplementary Permutation Table I; Supplementary Table 4). Etanercept was avoided due to poor efficacy in intestinal disease (Supplementary Table 13). Treatment decisions were made jointly with paediatric gastroenterology, ensuring concordance with IBD-management pathways. In England, two-weekly dosing of up to 80 mg adalimumab is only available for IBD indications, however, NI reported “In a severe case, we have used equivalent to gastroenterology dosing for adalimumab” (Supplementary Table 13). Access to newer agents e.g. ustekinumab, risankizumab and upadicitinib were mainly via gastroenterology services, reflecting IBD commissioning pathways; arthritis is off-licence. However, some paediatric gastroenterologists noted “We have little experience beyond infliximab”. Rheumatology services in Scotland could access ustekinumab and JAKi (e.g. upadacitinib) for this indication (Supplementary Table 13).

Disease reclassification in older patients

Contributing adult rheumatologists in some centres in England/Wales reported reclassifying JIA e.g. ERA as spondyloarthritis (SpA) and RF-positive polyarticular JIA as adult RA to access a broader range of b/tsDMARDs. Comments included “There is a wider selection of biologics for people with RA than JIA” and “Reclassification reflects genetic, pathogenic and clinical similarities between PsA/ERA/seropositive polyJIA and their adult counterparts”. More caution was expressed for reclassification of oligoarticular/RF-negative polyarticular JIA due to different outcome measures e.g. “Poor performance of DAS28” in these subtypes. In psoriatic JIA and adult psoriatic arthritis (PsA), treatment decisions are often guided by clinical presentation e.g. the presence of axial involvement. In contrast, centres in Scotland, NI and some areas of England continued to manage patients beyond 16 years without reclassification, with Scottish centres noting: “Disease reclassification is not required” and “No issues with biologic options when patients transition. Many patients remain stable on anti-TNF therapy and adult rheumatologists have a broader range of b/tsDMARDs; they are not restricted by National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in Scotland” (Supplementary Table 3).

Use of biosimilars

All UK centres reported biosimilar use, with 13 agents (including IV/SC formulations) employed for JIA treatment (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table 14). Anti-TNF biosimilars were most common, with increasing uptake of adalimumab biosimilar - Yuflyma® (8/13 centres, 62%). Truxima® was the predominant rituximab biosimilar (9/13, 69%), while tocilizumab Tyenne® was used in 3/13 centres, with further centres planning to switch (Supplementary Table 14). Exclusive biosimilar use occurred in 7/13 centres (54%), though poor tolerability prompted reversion to originators. Wales used infliximab biosimilar Flixabi® due to central procurement, and NI returned to originators after adverse experiences with biosimilars in 2020–2021, with selective reintroduction thereafter. Formulation issues were noted by some centres: “10–20% of patients had to switch back to Humira from Amgevita®due to injection site issues,” and “paediatric formulations of etanercept biosimilars were unavailable, requiring unlicenced decanting” (Supplementary Tables 3 and 14).Fig. 2Use of Biosimilars. Safety and external consultation

A range of potential side effects were reported e.g. infection and cytopenias, which were manageable but reinforced the need for robust pharmacovigilance and consistent reporting (Supplementary Table 15). Centres, unless quaternary, commented that they sought advice for complex or >third-line treatment decisions (7/12, 58%), or combination b/tsDMARD use (5/12, 42%). Both tertiary and quaternary centres specifically sought external clinical opinion for consideration of HSCT (8/12 centres, 67%) (Supplementary Table 16). Respondents consistently emphasised a cautious, collaborative approach, involving national MDTs and consortia, as well as adult rheumatology services when paediatric options were limited.

Funding differences between Uk nations and patient factors

Access to b/tsDMARDs varies across the UK. England and Wales face stricter commissioning controls and delays in approving high-cost or off-label therapies, requiring patients to first be treated with, and show an inadequate response to, conventional DMARDs (e.g., methotrexate) despite side effects, poor tolerability, and high-level evidence of inefficacy for systemic sJIA manifestations [37]. Centres reported that biosimilars for agents like adalimumab were less expensive than some DMARDs, yet “patients must still show an inadequate response to methotrexate first before access is allowed” (Supplementary Table 3). Further comments mentioned dosing constraints: “increased frequency is not permitted despite clinical need” and age-based prescribing limitations: "the post-pubertal dosing rule [mandating use only in post-pubertal adolescents] is not ethical when data exist for younger children" (Supplementary Table 3). Like Scotland, NI reported freer access: "we have no restrictions for off-label b/tsDMARDs" (Supplementary Table 3). Wales noted, "we can get licenced drugs readily; and if unlicenced, can get access via individual patient request forms (IPFR), which are approved rapidly" (Supplementary Table 3). Patient factors like needle phobia, family logistics, co-morbidities and adherence also influenced therapy selection, with oral agents like JAKi considered for injection-averse patients despite higher costs (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

This national survey, encompassing responses from the majority of paediatric rheumatology centres across the UK, highlights significant variation in access to b/tsDMARDs for JIA. While these therapies are supported by trial evidence and expert consensus, their availability remains inconsistent across UK devolved healthcare systems. Variations relate to differing commissioning processes and care pathways, and age- and specialty-specific licensing restrictions.

Although our study was not designed to directly measure patient-level outcomes, delays or restrictions in access to b/tsDMARDs are likely to have clinically meaningful consequences. Early initiation of biologic therapy in JIA has been associated with improved disease control, reduced cumulative corticosteroid exposure, and higher rates of sustained remission [38–41]. Data from observational studies and international registries support a “window of opportunity” in early disease, whereby timely intervention with b/tsDMARD therapy is associated with improved long-term outcomes [38, 39, 41–43]. Conversely, delays may prolong active disease and heighten the risk of irreversible joint damage and extra-articular complications such as uveitis-related visual impairment and MAS end-organ damage.

In England and Wales, access for high-cost therapeutics is governed by the Medicines for Children in Specialised Services (M4C) framework [44]. Under M4C, funding is restricted to medicines supported by NICE appraisals or NHS England commissioning policies, restricting access to newer therapies, even where high-level evidence and specialist consensus exist. A key component of this process is the ‘Prioritisation of Products Process’ (PPP), which determines whether therapies are selected for evaluation and funding. While designed to ensure cost-effective allocation of resources, PPP may contribute to delays in access, as availability depends not only on clinical evidence but also on prioritisation relative to competing therapies [45–48]. These challenges are particularly pronounced in rare paediatric diseases like JIA, where the evidence base is often limited to small trials and observational studies, making it difficult to meet conventional thresholds for evaluation [45, 46].

Consequently, the process for national commissioning approval of individual b/tsDMARDs is lengthy, with no guarantee of approval, even where robust evidence exists. This explains the predominant use of anti-TNF agents across JIA subtypes except sJIA. Access to b/tsDMARD therapy for JIA-associated uveitis remains particularly restricted; agents such as infliximab, tocilizumab and JAKi are not licenced or commissioned, despite evidence of efficacy [11, 13, 18, 49, 50]. This necessitates local funding or individual funding request (IFR) routes; however, success is inconsistent as it is often difficult to demonstrate “exceptionality” for individual patients. Notably, canakinumab for sJIA remains uncommissioned in England and Wales, despite high-level evidence, having failed cost-prioritisation on three occasions as of June 2026 [51].

Consistent with UK emphasis on cost-containment, biosimilar uptake was widespread. Specialist paediatric rheumatology pharmacists play a key role in biosimilar switching, medicines optimisation, and reducing delays in treatment initiation; however, their availability was variable across centres. This aligns with findings from the GIRFT review, which also highlighted inconsistent pharmacy support across the UK and recommended enhanced specialist pharmacy input to standardise b/tsDMARD prescribing pathways and reduce delays in medicines management [23].

Paediatric commissioning and access to b/tsDMARDs lags behind adult care, even where market authorisation exists for post-pubertal or younger age groups. NHS England policy stipulates that funding for medicines approved for adults may only extend to paediatric use if: the medicine holds a paediatric licence for the same indication; or is included in the British National Formulary for Children (BNFc) with age-appropriate dosing; or the patient is a post-pubertal child or adolescent. While intended to ensure safety, this approach can delay access to effective therapies for younger patients and rarer JIA subtypes. Recent approvals via the M4C route, including for ixekizumab, ustekinumab, golimumab, certolizumab pegol, guselkumab and upadacitinib for psoriatic JIA/juvenile PsA have begun to address this gap (Supplementary Table 10). However, where licenced or BNFc-listed dosages are unavailable for pre-pubescent patients, access is restricted to post-pubertal children only. In such circumstances, prescribing responsibility falls to clinicians, in the absence of supporting paediatric trial data [44].

Discrepancies between paediatric and adult commissioning frameworks were most apparent for ERA and RF-positive polyarticular JIA, which, in England and Wales, are not considered equivalent to adult SpA or RA, respectively. As a result, several agents available in adult services, including IL-17 inhibitors and JAKi, remain uncommissioned for paediatric use. This structural mismatch contributes to the reclassification of JIA diagnoses during adolescence or early adulthood to enable broader access, a practice reported by some centres in England and Wales but not in Scotland/NI (where reclassification is not required). Specialty-specific differences were noted, with gastroenterology and dermatology able to access higher b/tsDMARD dosing for some agents for psoriasis/IBD in the presence or absence of arthritis, highlighting challenges in aligning cross-specialty treatment pathways. In contrast to England and Wales, centres in Scotland and NI reported fewer restrictions and earlier access to b/tsDMARDs. Although Wales operates under the same framework as England, rapid IPFR (IFR) approvals were reported, a pragmatic approach that likely reduces access delays.

These findings support several actionable policy recommendations. Commissioning pathways should better align with contemporary clinical evidence, including removal of the mandatory requirement for an inadequate response to methotrexate prior to b/tsDMARDs in sJIA and earlier escalation in moderate-severe disease, consistent with international recommendations from the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) and the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) [19–21]. Appraisal processes should be adapted to reflect the realities of rare paediatric conditions, enabling more timely and flexible decision-making where high-quality evidence exists but large-scale trials are not feasible [45, 46]. Greater engagement with industry and a more streamlined process may facilitate the submission of paediatric data for NICE appraisal.

Reform of IFR pathways is needed to reduce administrative burden and improve access for patients with refractory disease. Incorporation of clinicians with relevant expertise into IFR decision-making panels may improve the clinical validity and consistency of funding decisions. Commissioning frameworks should support continuity of care across the life course, avoiding the need for diagnostic reclassification during transition to adult services. Greater pathway flexibility is also required to support patient-centred prescribing, including earlier use of oral agents such as JAKi in patients with significant needle phobia or intolerance to parenteral therapies. In addition, commissioning of therapies with established efficacy and/or NICE approval e.g. canakinumab for sJIA and infliximab/tocilizumab for JIA-associated uveitis should be prioritised.

This study has several strengths, including broad national paediatric and adolescent rheumatology community representation and detailed, subtype-specific analysis of prescribing hierarchies across devolved UK healthcare systems. The use of novel permutation testing allowed evaluation of consensus prescribing patterns despite modest sample sizes. Limitations include sensitivity to individual-centre effects, inability to assess within-centre variation due to composite responses, and influence of national commissioning rules on early-line treatment choices. Although surveys were completed collaboratively and contemporaneously within MDT meetings by senior clinicians directly involved in prescribing decisions, some degree of reporting bias cannot be excluded. However, by integrating quantitative data with qualitative MDT insights, this study provides novel evidence on access barriers that are not captured in clinical trials, registries, or service reports.

Conclusions

Taken together, these findings indicate that inequitable access to b/tsDMARDs represents a major barrier to optimal JIA care across the UK, consistent with the GIRFT report on paediatric rheumatology services in England [23]. Urgent action is required to harmonise commissioning frameworks in England and Wales with those in Scotland and NI, improve cross-specialty alignment, and develop national, evidence-based life-course guidelines. Such measures could reduce reliance on disease reclassification and IFR processes, minimising delays. The GIRFT report similarly advocated for revised commissioning policies and standardised prescribing pathways to streamline care and improve outcomes.

Although the specific barriers identified arise within the UK system, the underlying challenges are relevant internationally. Access to b/tsDMARDs is shaped by the interplay between regulatory approval, funding policies, and clinician prescribing autonomy, with substantial variation reported even among high-income countries with comparable treatment approaches [42, 43, 52, 53]. Compared with the UK, many European systems allow greater clinician discretion and earlier access to b/tsDMARDs, although funding varies between countries. In contrast, insurance-based systems such as in the United States may enable faster uptake of newer therapies for those with insurance, but create inequities, with some patients unable to afford or access treatment [54]. In lower-resource settings, including parts of Asia and Africa, high drug costs and regulatory timelines further limit access, leading to lower biologic use and continued reliance on conventional DMARDs [53, 55].

These comparisons highlight key trade-offs: centralised systems such as the UK, which provide care that is ‘free at the point of use’ promote financial equity and protect patients from high costs but may delay access to new therapies, whereas insurance-based systems may enable more rapid uptake at the expense of equity. For rare paediatric diseases like JIA, there is a need for more flexible, evidence-responsive frameworks that better align clinical data with funding decisions. Future multinational studies linking access to b/tsDMARDs with longitudinal clinical outcomes are needed to inform policies that support timely and equitable treatment for all children and young people with JIA.
