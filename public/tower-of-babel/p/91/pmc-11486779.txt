Chrysin mitigates neuronal apoptosis and impaired hippocampal neurogenesis in male rats subjected to D-galactose-induced brain aging
Authors: Ram Prajit, Rasa Saenno, Kornrawee Suwannakot, Soraya Kaewngam, Tanaporn Anosri, Nataya Sritawan, Anusara Aranarochana, Apiwat Sirichoat, Wanassanun Pannangrong, Peter Wigmore, Jariya Umka Welbat
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Oxidative stress-induced neuronal apoptosis is primarily involved in brain aging and impaired hippocampal neurogenesis. Long-term D-galactose administration increases oxidative stress related to brain aging. Chrysin, a subtype of flavonoids, exhibits neuroprotective effects, particularly its antioxidant properties. To elucidate the neuroprotection of chrysin on neuronal apoptosis and an impaired hippocampal neurogenesis relevant to oxidative damage in D-galactose-induced brain aging, male Sprague Dawley rats were allocated into vehicle control, D-galactose, chrysin, and cotreated rats. The rats received their respective treatments daily for 8 weeks. The reactions of scavenging enzymes, protein regulating endogenous antioxidant defense, and anti-apoptotic protein expression were significantly reduced in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of the animals receiving D-galactose. Conversely, product of oxidative damage and apoptotic protein expressions were significantly elevated in both cortical areas of the D-galactose group. In hippocampal neurogenesis, significant upregulation of cell cycle arrest and decrease in differentiated protein expression were detected after D-galactose administration. Nevertheless, chrysin supplementation significantly mitigated all negative effects in animals receiving D-galactose. This study demonstrates that chrysin likely attenuates brain aging induced by D-galactose by enhancing scavenging enzyme activities and reducing oxidative stress, neuronal apoptosis, and the impaired hippocampal neurogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10522-024-10140-8.

Introduction

Currently, the increasing number of aging populations, inversely correlated with birth rates, present critical health problems worldwide. Cognitive impairment, characterized by declined memory and thinking, is due to neurodegeneration in brain aging (Tu et al. 2018; Ahmad et al. 2021). The brain, which is primarily composed of lipids, is highly susceptible to oxidation by free radicals produced by mitochondria. This oxidative stress can induce neuronal apoptosis, contributing progressively to brain aging (Cobley et al. 2018; Jelinek et al. 2021). The damaged hippocampus and prefrontal cortex due to oxidative damage exhibit cognitive impairments. Additionally, hippocampal neurogenesis, the development of neural stem cells in the subgranular zone (SGZ), is counteracted by neuronal apoptosis, which is liked to memory deficits (Nieuwenhuis and Takashima 2011; Bond et al. 2015; Prajit et al. 2020).

Several mechanisms of aging have been studied using animal models. The observation of animals undergoing natural aging is time-consuming and costly (Azman and Zakaria 2019; Holtze et al. 2021). D-galactose, a reducing sugar, stimulates aging mechanisms via oxidative stress-induced apoptosis and is extensively used in aging research because of its convenience and minimal side effects. Furthermore, animals demonstrate a high survival rate after receiving D-galactose (Shwe et al. 2018). Cognitive impairment, a sign of brain aging, is a result of D-galactose-induced neuronal apoptosis due to oxidative stress resulting in hippocampal neurogenesis depletion (Prajit et al. 2020; Ahmad et al. 2021).

Flavonoids, ubiquitous in various plants, have utilities for human health. A subdivision of flavonoid found in honey, propolis, Passiflora caerulea, and Pleurotus ostreatus is chrysin (5, 7 dihydroxyflavone). It possesses antioxidant properties because of the presence of meta-hydroxylation, a double bond, and a carbonyl group. These characteristics make it useful for pharmacological treatments in liver diseases, neurodegenerative disorders, and reproductive impairments (Naz et al. 2019). The neuroprotection of chrysin on memory deficits includes ameliorating the reduction of hippocampal neurogenesis and oxidative harmful effects in both natural and D-galactose-induced brain aging (Anand et al. 2012; Souza et al. 2015; Prajit et al. 2020).

Although some research suggests that chrysin possesses antioxidant properties and can slow the reduction of hippocampal neurogenesis associated with memory deficits in brain aging (Anand et al. 2012; Souza et al. 2015; Prajit et al. 2020), the neuroprotective effects of chrysin on the apoptotic pathway related to oxidative stress have not been studied in this context. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate whether chrysin attenuates neuronal apoptosis related to oxidative damage in the hippocampal and prefrontal cortical regions. Additionally, we observed the potential effects of chrysin on cell cycle arrest in the SGZ and protein regulating differentiation in hippocampal neural stem cells in D-galactose-induced brain aging in rats.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and antibodies

The chemicals and antibodies were listed in Table S1-S4.

Animals and drug administration

The permitted animal ethics (Record no. IACUC-KKU-106/64) was certified by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Khon Kaen University. Twenty-four male Sprague Dawley rats (8 weeks old) were housed under the controlled environment as describe previously (Prajit et al. 2020). The rats were raised with accessible diet and acclimated for 4 weeks without any interventions.

This study was an extension of our previous work, which found no significant differences in the neuroprotective potential of chrysin (10 and 30 mg/kg) on hippocampal neurogenesis in animals who underwent D-galactose-induced aging (Prajit et al. 2020). Consequently, the dose of 30 mg/kg chrysin was omitted to decrease cost and the number of animals required. In present study, animals were randomly allocated into four groups (6 animals per group): vehicle, D-galactose, chrysin, and cotreatment. The vehicle animals received an oral administration of propylene glycol and an intraperitoneal injection of normal saline at 1 ml/kg. The D-galactose group was intraperitoneally injected with D-galactose at 50 mg/kg, dissolved in normal saline. The chrysin group was orally administered chrysin (10 mg/kg), dissolved in propylene glycol. The cotreated animals received equal doses of D-galactose and chrysin with the same dissolving reagents and routes. All animals were administered the treatments daily for 8 weeks.

Tissue preparation

Following treatment, the animals were rapidly stunned and decapitated. One hemisphere of the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex was immediately separated and flash-frozen for the analysis of biochemical assays and protein expressions. The other hemisphere was immersed in a sucrose solution (30%), embedded in cryomold using optimal cutting temperature compound, and flash-frozen for subsequent serial sectioning and immunofluorescence staining.

Biochemical assays

Homogenates of the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex were centrifuged and then the supernatant was used to assess scavenging enzyme activities and markers of oxidative stress in triplicate as described previously (Suwannakot et al. 2022). Protein concentration was measured and expressed in units of mg/ml.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined by inhibiting cytochrome C reduction (McCord and Fridovich 1969). The supernatant was mixed in a cocktail and xanthine oxidase to estimate SOD activity as described previously (Suwannakot et al. 2022). Absorbance was evaluated at 540 nm from 0 to 5 min. The activity of SOD was expressed in units/mg protein.

Catalase (CAT) activity was detected by the absorbance of the reaction between potassium permanganate and hydrogen peroxide (Ming et al. 2009). The supernatant was mixed with a solution containing hydrogen peroxide, potassium phosphate buffer, and sulfuric acid. The samples were mixed with potassium permanganate as described previously (Suwannakot et al. 2022) and evaluated the absorbance at 540 nm. Data were reported in units/mg protein.

Glutathione peroxidase (GPx) reaction was assessed by the glutathione reductase catalyzed nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate hydrogen (NADPH) (Eyer and Podhradský 1986; Monostori et al. 2009). The supernatant was blended with a cocktail and reactive solutions as described previously (Suwannakot et al. 2022), followed by incubating in a dark room for 10 min before measuring absorbance at 405 nm. Data were expressed in units/mg protein.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were evaluated using the hydrolyzed 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane as a standard (Gupta et al. 2003; Wang et al. 2022) to assess lipid oxidation. The supernatant was mixed with a solution containing thiobarbituric acid and then heated at 95 °C. At room temperature, an n-butanol/pyridine mixture was added to the samples as described previously (Suwannakot et al. 2022). The mixtures were collected for absorbance detection at 540 nm. Data were expressed in nmol/mg protein.

Western blotting

Homogenates of both cortical parts of the brain were prepared using lysis buffer containing protease inhibitor tablets. The supernatant was separated after centrifugation and measured protein concentration expressed in mg/ml. The supernatant was mixed with loading buffer and then denatured as described previously (Welbat et al. 2020). Electrophoresis was used to separate the proteins through a polyacrylamide gel. Then, the separated proteins were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane and blocked for non-specific protein binding using 5% bovine serum albumin. The target proteins on the membrane were assessed by incubating with primary antibodies, including anti-nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2, 1:1000), anti-B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2, 1:2000), anti-Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax, 1:1000), anti-caspase-3 (1:2000), anti-doublecortin (DCX, 1:500), and anti-glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH, 1:20,000). Subsequently, the membranes were probed with polyclonal goat anti-mouse (1:2000) to detect caspase-3, DCX, and GAPDH, while Nrf2, Bcl-2, and Bax were probed with polyclonal goat anti-rabbit (1:2000). The immunoblotted protein bands were exposed using chemiluminescence reagent and detected with a chemiluminescence imager. All optical density of the targeted protein expression was measured using Image J 1.53e software and presented as a percentage of the target proteins relative GAPDH expression. All protein evaluations were carried out in triplicate.

Immunofluorescence staining

The snap-frozen hemispheres were immersed in distilled water to remove the optimal cutting temperature compound and then placed in paraformaldehyde solution (4%) at 4 °C overnight. The hemispheres were subsequently serially sectioned to cover the entire area of the dentate gyrus, at a thickness of 40 μm, using a cryostat microtome (Cryostat Series HM 550, Microm International GmbH, Walldorf, DE) (Sirichoat et al. 2022). Every eighth section was preserved in cryoprotective solution at 4 °C. Nine selected sections were blocked using 20% normal goat serum, followed by incubation with primary p21 antibody (1:100) at 4 °C. The next day, the sections were probed with Alexa Fluor 488 rabbit anti-mouse IgG (1:500) 1 h. Subsequently, the nuclei were stained using propidium iodide (PI, 1:6000), mounted, and cover-slipped for cell counting. Nine stained sections were examined using a fluorescence microscope. p21-positive cells were manually counted in the SGZ, specifically within an area encompassing three cell bodies adjacent to the internal part of the granular cell layer of the dentate gyrus (Welbat et al. 2020). The total number of p21-positive cells from 9 sections was multiplied by 8 for cell cycle arrest estimation.

Statistical analysis

Data analyzed by GraphPad Prism program Version 8 were expressed as mean ± SEM with significance set at p < 0.05. One-way ANOVA was used to analyze significant differences and comparison among all groups were made using Bonferroni correction.

Discussion

Brain aging leads to cognitive impairments, and numerous investigates have suggested that D-galactose administration induces age-related cognitive deficits (Shwe et al. 2018). The mechanism underlying D-galactose-induced brain aging is linked to neuronal apoptosis associated with oxidative stress (Tu et al. 2018; Ahmad et al. 2021). This study found that animals receiving D-galactose at a dose of 50 mg/kg exhibited diminished activities of scavenging enzymes, reduced Nrf2 expression, and increased MDA production. Additionally, there was an upregulation in apoptotic protein expressions, and a decline in Bcl-2 expression in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of the animals receiving D-galactose. The impeded hippocampal neurogenesis, including cell cycle arrest and diminished DCX expression, was observed after D-galactose administration. However, chrysin treatment mitigated the adverse effects of D-galactose-induced brain aging.

Continuous administration of D-galactose is metabolized through accessory pathways (Shwe et al. 2018). It is reduced to galactitol, which increases osmotic stress, or oxidized to hydrogen peroxide, initiating free radical generation and subsequent oxidative stress (Conte et al. 2021). D-galactose can induce the formation of advanced glycation end products, which activate their specific receptor to stimulate NADPH oxidase, producing free radicals (Kuzan 2021; Yuvaraj et al. 2021). This study observed significant reductions in SOD, CAT, and GPx activities in both cortical areas of the D-galactose group. MDA, a final product of lipid peroxidation, is generated from oxidized lipids (Cobley et al. 2018; Jelinek et al. 2021). Animals receiving D-galactose showed significant increases in MDA levels in both cortical areas. Nrf2, a transcription factor involved in maintaining intracellular redox balance and antioxidant enzyme production (Ho et al. 2019), was significantly downregulated in both cortical areas of the D-galactose group. Previous studies have documented that D-galactose promotes brain aging through the downregulation of Nrf2 expression, antioxidant enzyme activities, and the upregulation of MDA production (Ho et al. 2019; Ahmad et al. 2021). Chrysin possesses neuroprotective effects due to its antioxidant properties. The chemical structure of chrysin features meta-hydroxylation, a double bond, and a carbonyl group, which protects against free radicals and oxidative damage (Naz et al. 2019). Previous research has indicated that chrysin enhances Nrf2 gene expression and scavenging enzyme activities and inhibits MDA production in brain aging and myocardial oxidative damage (Anand et al. 2012; Yuvaraj et al. 2021) indicating that chrysin not only exhibits antioxidant properties but also improves endogenous antioxidant synthesis by stimulating Nrf2 in the cells. Similar to this study, chrysin supplementation was able to attenuate the detrimental effects of brain aging caused by D-galactose.

Oxidative stress significantly promotes apoptosis by activating the p53 transcription factor. The downstream pathway of p53 stimulates Bax expression, increasing mitochondrial permeability and cytochrome c release. The cytosolic cytochrome c forms an apoptosome, subsequently activating caspase-3-induced apoptosis (Kole et al. 2013; El-Far et al. 2022). In this study, the D-galactose group showed significant upregulations in Bax and caspase-3 expressions and downregulation in Bcl-2 expression in both hippocampus and prefrontal cortex. These findings align with the concept that D-galactose induces brain aging by promoting neuronal apoptosis (Tu et al. 2018; Ahmad et al. 2021). Conversely, chrysin supplementation protects against neuronal apoptosis by downregulating Bax and caspase-3 expressions and attenuating reduced Bcl-2 expression in traumatic brain injury, renal ischemia/reperfusion, and myocardial damage (Xu et al. 2019; Rashno et al. 2020; Yuvaraj et al. 2021). Additionally, chrysin inhibits activation of p53 pathway in cardiomyopathy (Mantawy et al. 2017), which may correlate in our finding, suggesting that chrysin treatment attenuated the expressions of apoptotic proteins and upregulated anti-apoptotic protein expression in both cortical areas of animals treated with D-galactose.

Hippocampal neurogenesis, the growth and development of neural stem cells in the SGZ, occurs throughout life but gradually declines over time (Bond et al. 2015). Cell cycle arrest, a hallmark of aging, is characterized by antiproliferative features. The p21 is a biomarker of cell cycle arrest (Welbat et al. 2020; Aranarochana et al. 2021), and its activation is linked to oxidative stress-induced p53 (Al Bitar and Gali-Muhtasib 2019; El-Far et al. 2022). D-galactose administration increases p21 gene and protein expression levels in the brain, liver, and kidney (Sha et al. 2021; El-Far et al. 2022). In this study, cell cycle arrest was evaluated using immunofluorescence staining and quantified in the SGZ. The increased number of p21-positive cells were found in the animals receiving D-galactose. While the effect of chrysin supplementation on p21-stained cells in the SGZ had not been previously studied, it is known to protect against decreased cell division in the SGZ in models of brain aging and chemotherapy (Prajit et al. 2020; Sirichoat et al. 2022). This study is the first to demonstrate that chrysin ameliorated cell cycle arrest in the SGZ in D-galactose-induced brain aging. Chrysin suppresses oxidative stress-induced p53 pathway demonstrating in a previous study (Mantawy et al. 2017), which may be a possible mechanism that subsequently inhibits p21 activation. Additionally, DCX is a cytoskeletal protein essential for the migration of immature neurons, making it a marker for tracing hippocampal neurogenesis (Bond et al. 2015). The reductions in DCX-positive cells have been reported in several studies involving D-galactose administration (Yoo et al. 2012; Prajit et al. 2020; Saenno et al. 2022). Due to the antioxidant and anti-apoptotic effects of chrysin (Anand et al. 2012; Rashno et al. 2020; Yuvaraj et al. 2021), they support neural stem cell differentiation beneficially, as presented by chrysin’s restoration of DCX expression in animals treated with D-galactose. Furthermore, chrysin treatment alleviated the reduced DCX-positive cells in models of brain aging and chemotherapy (Prajit et al. 2020; Sirichoat et al. 2022), which strongly aligns with the DCX result of this study.

Due to cost and time constraints, we could not use naturally aged animals for comparison with D-galactose-induced brain aging. Moreover, our previous study demonstrated that chrysin supplementation improves cognitive function by reducing impaired spatial and recognition memories associated with brain aging (Prajit et al. 2020). Consequently, this study aims to extend the investigation into the molecular mechanisms and bridge gaps in “Seven knowledge gaps in modern biogerontology" as described recently (Rattan 2024). The current study highlighted that chrysin supplementation primarily recovered endogenous antioxidant defense and subsequently reduced oxidative damage-induced neuronal apoptosis in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of rats with D-galactose-induced aging. In hippocampal neurogenesis, chrysin improved phenotypes associated with brain aging, including neuronal apoptosis and cell cycle arrest, which counteracted to new neuron generation in the SGZ in rats receiving D-galactose. This study did not examine the development of neural stem cells into newborn neurons during hippocampal neurogenesis. We plan to investigate all stages of hippocampal neurogenesis with chrysin supplementation in a rat model in D-galactose-induced brain aging.

Conclusions

The current study suggested that the neuroprotective effects of chrysin attenuated brain aging induced by D-galactose. Chrysin restored antioxidant status, inhibited oxidative stress, and the apoptotic pathways in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of D-galactose-induced aging rats. Furthermore, chrysin offered protection against cell cycle arrest and preserved the expression of cytoskeletal protein, which is essential for hippocampal neurogenesis compromised by D-galactose. These findings enrich our understanding of the neuroprotective mechanisms of chrysin on brain aging induced by D-galactose in a rat model.
