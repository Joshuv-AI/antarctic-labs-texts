Optimizing stability and controlled release of co-encapsulated probiotics and vitamin B12 using fluidized bed coating: impact of plasticizers on tailored HPMCAS-coated granules
Authors: Yuqian Yan, Dandan Song, Linlin He, Yanna Zhao, Lili Wang, Xiao Wang, Hui Yan, Zhuang Ding, Zhengping Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProbiotics and vitamins face substantial challenges in preserving their viability and stability during processing, storage, and gastrointestinal transit. This study aimed to develop co-encapsulated formulations comprising Lactiplantibacillus plantarum JYLP-326 and vitamin B12 (vitB12) using a fluidized bed coating. The coatings used included a single hydroxypropyl methylcellulose acetate succinate (HPMCAS) and two composite HPMCAS systems incorporating either the hydrophilic triacetin (TR) or lipophilic oleic acid plasticizer. The physicochemical properties of the HPMCAS films were modulated by the inclusion of different plasticizers, thereby influencing the performance of the coated granules. The fluidized bed-coating process effectively preserved probiotic viability and vitB12 stability. The integration of TR into HPMCAS enhanced the pH-responsive controlled release by reinforcing the hydrogen bonding network. This feature resulted in only 3.3 % of vitB12 released in simulated gastric fluid and 94.7 % released in simulated intestinal fluid, while maintaining probiotic viability at over 95 % survival. All coated granules demonstrated high probiotic survival rates, exceeding 97 %, during a 10-day storage period at 4 °C and 25 °C, although the presence of plasticizers reduced thermal resistance at 40 °C. This study demonstrates that HPMCAS-based fluidized bed coating, optimized with specific plasticizers, represents an effective approach for encapsulating sensitive bioactive components while preserving their functional efficacy throughout manufacturing, storage and consumption. The extensive data obtained in this study offer a basis for established strategies aimed at developing oral fortified foods that enhance stability and facilitate controlled release.Keywords: Fluidized bed coating, Encapsulation, Hydroxypropyl methylcellulose acetate succinate, Plasticizer, Release characteristics

Introduction

Probiotics and vitamins are increasingly preferred as nutritional supplements among consumers due to their extensive health benefits. Many individuals use oral probiotics and vitamin products to enhance their physical and psychological well-being (Li et al., 2021). Recently, the co-supplementation of probiotics and vitamins has been recognized as important due to their synergistic effects across several health problems (Guetterman et al., 2022; Sultana et al., 2024). However, both probiotics and vitamins are susceptible to degradation and can easily lose their activity when exposed to adverse environmental and processing conditions (Tao et al., 2019; He et al., 2024). Consequently, the co-encapsulation of probiotics and vitamins is essential for maintaining their viability and stability during product manufacturing, storage, and gastrointestinal digestion (Bu et al., 2024).

Co-encapsulation is a versatile technique that involves encapsulating different labile substances within a protective shell, thereby shielding them from detrimental conditions. This process can be implemented through various industrial methods, including freeze-drying, spray-drying, electrospinning, and fluidized bed coating (Niño-Vásquez et al., 2022; Jia et al., 2025). In contrast to solid dispersions produced through other encapsulation methods, the microencapsulated structure generated via fluidized bed coating is analogous to that of a pill/pellet coating (Dreu et al., 2012). This structure facilitated complete encapsulation of the drug-loaded core material. Additionally, fluidized bed-coating technology is prominent in pharmaceutical and functional food development, owing to its high encapsulation efficiency, substantial drug-loading capacity, high production yield, and flexibility in selecting encapsulation materials (Yan and Kim, 2024). In a comparative study of the preparation of simvastatin-loaded dry emulsion systems using various encapsulation technologies, Pohlen et al. (2022) reported that products obtained through the fluidized bed process exhibited superior yield, encapsulation efficiency, storage stability, and relative oral bioavailability compared with those produced via spray drying. Yang et al. (2022) used three different anionic polysaccharides (sodium alginate, sodium carboxymethylcellulose, and xanthan gum) as the outermost layer to encapsulate chlorophyll-loaded particles using fluidized bed-coating technology, thereby enhancing the stability of chlorophyll during storage and facilitating its controlled release in gastrointestinal fluids.

Selecting appropriate encapsulation materials allows for the precise modification and regulation of the surface characteristics and functional properties of drug-loaded particles. Exposure to oxygen, acidic conditions, and a moist environment are the primary factors contributing to the inactivation of anaerobic probiotics and degradation of vitamins in food products. Due to its acid-insoluble properties, oxygen and water barrier capabilities, and safe, non-toxic profile, hydroxypropyl methylcellulose acetate succinate (HPMCAS) has potential use in the microencapsulation of probiotics and vitamins (Choudhari et al., 2023). Typically, cellulose-based polymers, including HPMCAS, are commonly used as coating materials in conjunction with small-molecule plasticizers. The plasticizers used in the food industry are primarily categorized into two groups: hydrophilic plasticizers, such as polyols and triethyl citrate, and hydrophobic plasticizers, such as fatty acids. The incorporation of the plasticizers enhances the film-forming capabilities and processability of cellulose-based polymer coatings. However, the modulating effects on the permeability and dissolution characteristics of the coating materials are contingent upon the interaction between the selected polymer and plasticizer (Eslami et al., 2023). Park et al. (2016) developed probiotics (Lactobacillus acidophilus and Bifidobacterium animalis ssp. lactis) encapsulated in pellets using HPMCAS and a combination of plasticizers (triethyl citrate and acetylated monoglyceride) using a dry powder coating technique. Their findings indicated that the encapsulated pellets exhibited significantly enhanced acid resistance, storage stability, and intestinal colonization by viable bacteria. Klar and Urbanetz (2016) used HPMCAS in conjunction with three distinct plasticizers to prepare theophylline-coated pellets using a rotary fluidized bed process. Films incorporating triethyl citrate or triacetin (TR) as plasticizers demonstrated substantial resistance to gastric juice, with less than 10 % release. In contrast, including polyethylene glycol 400 resulted in a 20 % release over 120 min in acidic buffer conditions. Deshpande et al. (2018) investigated the influence of plasticizers, specifically triethyl citrate and polyethylene glycol, on the physicochemical and dispersion stability properties of HPMCAS aqueous coating dispersion. They found that the plasticizers inhibited aggregation and enhanced the processability of HPMCAS dispersions. Although some studies have explored the combination of HPMCAS and plasticizers to coat bioactive molecules, these investigations have primarily concentrated on a limited range of hydrophilic plasticizers, such as triethyl citrate and polyethylene glycol (Choudhari et al., 2023). Furthermore, existing research has predominantly used dry or aqueous dispersion coating methodologies, with insufficient attention allocated to the effects of solvent-based coating on plasticization.

Lactiplantibacillus plantarum JYLP-326 is a novel probiotic strain that ameliorates sleep disorders in an insomnia rat model and alleviates behaviors associated with spontaneous depression in a depression rat model. This strain was isolated from traditionally fermented sticky rice in China and has not undergone any domestication or screening processes. Consequently, their tolerance to acidic and oxidative stress is relatively limited (Zhu et al., 2024). Cyanocobalamin, a cyano-form of vitamin B12 (vitB12), has been extensively used in food and pharmaceutical products to prevent and treat vitB12 deficiency. Additionally, vitB12 can be metabolized and used by most probiotic bacteria to facilitate their growth and colonization (Guetterman et al., 2022). However, the molecular structure of vitB12 is easily degraded when exposed to acidic, thermal, photolytic, or oxidative stress conditions, thereby limiting its application (Mazzocato et al., 2019). Therefore, we selected probiotic strain, JYLP-326, and small-molecular vitB12 as co-encapsulated model components. This study aimed to develop innovative formulations using a fluidized bed-coating technique, with ethanol-dissolved HPMCAS as primary coating material, to enhance the viability and bioactivity of probiotics and vitamins during storage and digestion. The commercially available HPMCAS is primarily classified into three types: LF, MF, and HF. The primary differentiation among these types is the relative proportions of four substituent groups: hydrophobic methyl, acetyl, hydrophilic hydroxypropyl, and succinyl (Table S1). In this study, the HPMCAS-LF variant was selected due to its superior hydrophilicity, reduced viscosity, and more versatile release characteristics compared with those of the other types (Choudhari et al., 2023). Two distinct plasticizers, hydrophilic TR and lipophilic oleic acid (OA), were incorporated as co-encapsulants. The effects of plasticizer incorporation on the properties and performance of the HPMCAS-based coating was investigated, a topic not previously reported in the literature. Furthermore, the influence of these formulation factors on process stability, storage viability, in vitro release, and in vitro-simulated digestive survival rate of L. plantarum JYLP-326 and vitB12 were systematically examined. This study is important for the comprehensive utilization and synergistic development of probiotics and vitamins for various applications. It proposes a promising strategy and compatible formulations for the co-encapsulation of multiple bioactive ingredients with varying properties and functions to enhance the efficacy of functional foods and nutritional products.

Materials and methods

Materials

The probiotic strain, L. plantarum JYLP-326 (lyophilized powder using maltodextrin as a matrix, 108 CFU/g concentration of probiotic strain), was purchased from Zhongke-Jiayi Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Weifang, Shandong, China). VitB12 (Cyanocobalamin; >99 % purity) was purchased from Yuxing Bioengineering Co. Ltd. (Xingtai, Hebei, China). HPMCAS (AQOAT AS-LF) was provided by Shin-Etsu Chemical Co. Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). OA and TR plasticizers were obtained from Spectrum Chemical Mfg. Corp. (Gardena, CA, USA) and Macklin Biochemicals (Shanghai, China), respectively. De Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) broth and agar were obtained from Hope Bio-Technology Co. Ltd. (Qingdao, Shandong, China). Digestive enzymes included porcine pepsin (≥3000 U/mg, BBI Life Sciences, Shanghai, China) and bovine trypsin (≥250 U/mg, BBI Life Sciences). Fluorescent labeling (fluorescein isothiocyanate isomer I, FITC) and all cell culture reagents were obtained from Solarbio Technology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). Commercially available ultrapure water (Wahaha Group, Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, China) was used for all experiments.

The preparation of coating films

HPMCAS, either alone or in conjunction with a plasticizer (OA or TR), was dissolved in an 80 % (w/w) aqueous ethanol solution and subjected to continuous stirring overnight at 500 rpm to prepare the respective coating solutions. The three coating formulations are listed in Table 1. A 10-g aliquot of the coating solution was evenly spread onto a glass plate and subsequently dried in a thermostatic blast dryer (LC101, Lichen Instrument Technology Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 50 °C for 90 min. Following the drying process, an approximately 100-μm thick coating film was obtained for further characterization.

Physicochemical characterization of coating films

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The FTIR spectra of the coating films and each component were recorded using the attenuated total reflection technique on a Nexus 870 spectrometer equipped with a smart iTX model (Nicolet, Waltham, MA, USA). All samples were scanned in the wavelength range of 400–4000 cm−1.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

The DSC thermograms of the coating films were obtained using a Q2000 DSC system (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA). Each sample (5.0 mg) was accurately weighed and sealed in an aluminum pan with a pierced lid. The analysis was performed at 20–200 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C/min.

Dynamic contact angle

Static water contact angles (WCA) of the coating films were evaluated using a DSA100 digital goniometer (KRUSS, Hamburg, Germany). Deionized water (2 μL) was dispensed using a microsyringe and the needle was carefully lifted once the droplet made contact with the coating film. The shape of the droplet was captured using a high-speed optical camera, and the precise contact angle between the film surface and tangent of the droplet was determined through contour fitting.

Oxygen permeability (OP)

The oxygen transmission rates of the coating films were assessed using a GTR-701M OP apparatus (Beijing Harke Test Instrument Factory, Beijing, China). The films were cut into circular shapes with a diameter of 10 cm to ensure the absence of holes or scratches. The exposure area for the tests was maintained at 38.48 cm2. The OP measurements were conducted at 23 °C and relative humidity (RH) of 50 %.

Simulation method

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were executed to examine the interactions between HPMCAS and various plasticizers. Initial configurations of three distinct systems, including HPMCAS, HPMCAS-TR, and HPMCAS-OA, were generated using Packmol software (version 20.3.3), and simulations were conducted using the GROMACS package (version 2023.3) using the all-atom OPLS force field. Energy minimization was achieved through the steepest descent method. Subsequently, dynamics simulations were performed under constant pressure and temperature ensemble (NPT type) at 298 K for a duration of 5 ns. The H-bond numbers and pore size distribution were calculated using the ‘‘grid point in L-sphere’’ method. System configurations were visualized using Visual Molecular Dynamics software (version 1.9.3). Further details on the MD simulations are provided in the Supplementary Information.

The preparation of core granules

Core granules containing L. plantarum JYLP-326 and vitB12 were prepared using wet granulation. The probiotic powder and vitB12 raw material were first sieved through a 200-mesh stainless steel sieve (75-μm aperture, ASTM standard). The sieved components were subsequently blended at a 99:1 mass ratio (JYLP-326:vitB12). The powder mixtures were granulated using ultrapure water, which was filter-sterilized through 0.22-μm filters. Ultrapure water, constituting 15 % of the dry powder mass, was atomized onto the powder mixture via a pneumatic spray nozzle with a diameter of 0.8 mm. The resulting hydrated mass was consistently kneaded to ensure uniform moistening and subsequently extruded through a 40-mesh sieve to form wet granules. These granules were then dried in a convection oven (37 ± 0.5 °C, 6 h), achieving a residual moisture content of approximately 4.0 % (w/w), as determined using a HE53 moisture analyzer (Mettler Toledo, Zurich, Switzerland; Table S2). After drying, the granules underwent sequential size fractionation through stacked sieves: initial classification through a 40-mesh sieve followed by removal of fine granules using a 60-mesh sieve. The average size of the resulting granules were 559.0 ± 10.1 μm, respectively, as measured using a Mastersizer 3000 equipped with an Aero S dry powder disperser (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK; Fig. S1). The resulting core granules were immediately transferred to a desiccator containing allochroic silica gel and stored at 4 °C prior to coating.

Fluidized bed coating of core granules

Core granules comprising JYLP-326 and vitB12 were coated using a Wurster-type bottom-spray fluidized-bed apparatus (Model FPCG-2, Fuyao Electromechanical Technology Development Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China). A batch of 150 g of core granules was introduced into the drying chamber and fluidized using hot airflow maintained at 50.0 ± 2.0 °C. The temperature and humidity in the preparation workshop were controlled at 25.0 ± 1.0 °C and 45–50 %, respectively. For each batch, 833.3 g of the coating solution (containing 50 g of solid coating material) was delivered to the drying chamber via a peristaltic pump at a rate of 1.0 g/min. The coating solution was uniformly sprayed onto the continuously fluidized core granules through a nozzle at an atomizing pressure of 1500 mbar. The product temperature was maintained at 37.0 ± 2.0 °C. Following a 14-h coating process, the coated granules were collected and stored at 4 °C in a desiccator until further evaluation. The granules coated with the various formulations were labeled HPMCAS, HPMCAS-TR, and HPMCAS-OA. Furthermore, to assess the impact of the fluidization process on probiotic activity and vitB12 stability, the core granules were subjected to fluidization in a hot airflow at 37.0 ± 2.0 °C for 14 h without any coating. The granules obtained from this process, which remained uncoated, were used as blank samples.

Probiotic viability in coated granules

Enumeration of viable L. plantarum JYLP-326 in the coated granules was conducted following the methodology described by Liu et al. (2018) with minor modifications. Coated granules (0.25 g; corresponding to 0.2 g of core granules) or 0.2 g of the blank sample was suspended in 9.8 mL of phosphate buffer solution (PBS, pH 7.5). This mixture was then subjected to vortexing at 4000 rpm for 10 min to ensure the thorough dissolution of the coated granules and complete release of encapsulated bacteria. The resulting bacterial suspension was serially diluted, and 100 μL of each dilution was plated onto MRS agar. Storage stability

The storage stability of the coated granules was evaluated by determining the viable counts of L. plantarum JYLP-326 after short-term storage. Granule samples were placed in aluminum foil-sealed bags and stored in a climate chamber (MKF720, Binder GmbH, Tuttlingen, Germany). Three different storage conditions were tested: refrigeration (4 °C, 20 % RH), room temperature (25 °C, 50 % RH), and high temperature storage (40 °C, 50 % RH). After 10 days of storage, the SR of the JYLP-326 strain in the coated granules was calculated using the method described in Section 2.6.

In vitro-simulated digestion

Release of vitB12

The protocol for simulated digestion, which encompasses the preparation of simulated gastric fluid (SGF, pH 2.0) and simulated intestinal fluid (SIF, pH 7.0), was conducted in accordance with the methodology outlined by Brodkorb et al. (2019), with certain modifications. The SGF, comprising 2000 U/mL of pepsin and 0.15 mM of CaCl2, and the SIF, containing 100 U/mL of trypsin, 10 mM of bile salts, and 0.6 mM of CaCl2, were prepared and sterilized by filtration through a 0.22-μm membrane. Subsequently, these solutions were preheated to 37 °C for 10 min prior to use. The coated granules or blank sample (0.2 g) was initially introduced into 9.8 mL of SGF and incubated in a thermostatic bath shaker (SHA-CA, Putian Instruments, Changzhou, China) with agitation at 100 rpm at 37 °C for a duration of 2 h. After exposure to SGF, the digested chyme was combined with SIF and incubated for 4 h. Morphological observation of coated granules

The morphological characteristics of the selected coated granules during storage or digestion were examined using a SU8010 scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi, Kyoto, Japan). An appropriate amount of the coated granules was dispersed onto conductive silicone and coated with gold. The gold-coated samples were analyzed using cold-field emission at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. Morphology was observed at magnifications of×100 and × 500.

Adhesion capability to Caco-2 cells

The ability of L. plantarum JYLP-326 to adhere to Caco-2 cells was assessed using the FITC fluorescent labeling technique, as described by Jia et al. (2021), with minor modifications. Freshly cultured bacteria strain, JYLP-326, was harvested using centrifugation at 3000×g for 5 min at 4 °C and washed twice with sterile PBS (pH 7.5). The harvested bacteria were resuspended in an FITC solution (500 μg/mL) and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C in the dark. After incubation, the bacteria were collected using centrifugation and washed twice with sterile PBS to eliminate unbound FITC. Maltodextrin (2.0 % w/w) was added to MEM broth to simulate the environment the bacteria in the blank sample were in. Either HPMCAS alone (0.5 %, w/w) or a combination of HPMCAS (0.4 %, w/w) and a plasticizer (0.1 %, w/w) was continually added to MEM broth to simulate the environment of the bacteria in coated granules. The bacteria were subsequently resuspended in MEM broth containing the coating components at a concentration of 5 × 105 CFU/mL. The unlabeled strain, JYLP-326, was similarly resuspended in the aforementioned MEM broth to serve as negative controls for background subtraction purposes. Owing to its strong adhesion properties to the intestinal mucosa, the commercial Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG strain (LGG) underwent the same labeling procedure in MEM broth and was used as a positive control (Leser and Baker, 2024). Subsequently, the cultures were washed three times with sterile PBS and digested with 300 μL of pancreatin at 37 °C for 5 min. MEM (300 μL) was added to terminate the reaction. Results and discussion

Physicochemical properties of the coating film

Molecular interactions

To investigate the intermolecular interactions between HPMCAS and plasticizers within the composite films, the FTIR spectra of each component and two composite films containing plasticizers were analyzed (Fig. 1A). Our analysis focused on alterations in the positions of the characteristic peaks near 3400 and 1750 cm−1, corresponding to hydroxyl (-OH) and ester carbonyl (-C=O) stretching vibrations, respectively (Liu et al., 2022) (Fig. 2). The pure HPMCAS film exhibited typical -OH and -C=O stretching vibration peaks at 3469.4 and 1739.5 cm−1, respectively. In comparison, the -OH peak of the HPMCAS-OA was shifted to 3475.9 cm−1, representing a blue-shift of 6.5 cm−1, while the -C=O peak remained relatively unchanged at 1739.3 cm−1. Therefore, the hydrophobic long-alkyl chain of OA induced spatial site-blocking effects on the -OH groups of HPMCAS, thereby disrupting the hydrogen-bonding network among the HPMCAS molecules (Fig. 2). Although the -C=O groups of OA may establish new hydrogen bonds with the -OH groups of HPMCAS, the overall hydrogen bonding effect initially formed by HPMCAS may be weakened (Zhang et al., 2024). Conversely, FTIR of the HPMCAS-TR demonstrated a 6.2-cm−1 red-shift in the characteristic -C=O peak to 1733.3 cm−1, indicating that the -C=O of TR participates in the formation of additional hydrogen bonds with the -OH groups of HPMCAS (Fig. 2).

Schematic view of molecule-molecule interactions in hydroxypropyl methylcellulose acetate succinate (HPMCAS) film, along with two composite films containing the triacetin (HPMCAS-TR) or oleic acid (HPMCAS-OA) plasticizers.

To achieve a deeper understanding of the molecular interactions between HPMCAS and plasticizers, their associations were examined using MD simulations (Fig. 3). The MD simulation analyses revealed that HPMCAS molecules are capable of forming a network structure through intermolecular hydrogen bonding. Due to the presence of multiple carbonyl groups, TR established H-bonds with two HPMCAS molecules, thereby enhancing the network structure. Similarly, the monocarbonyl group in the OA structure formed H-bonds with the -OH on HPMCAS. MD simulations indicate that, compared with the HPMCAS, more H-bonds were formed in the HPMCAS-TR, potentially enhancing the network strength of HPMCAS-TR film. In contrast, the number of H-bonds in the HPMCAS-OA system was reduced. This result attributed to the steric hindrance introduced by the incorporated OA, which impedes hydrogen bonding between the polymers (Fig. 3D). The outcomes of MD simulations align with those derived from FTIR analysis.

Simulated number of H-bonds in a pure HPMCAS system, as well as in two composite systems containing either triacetin (HPMCAS-TR) or oleic acid (HPMCAS-OA) as plasticizers (D).

Thermal property

Fig. 1B illustrates the thermal behavior of the pure HPMCAS film and two composite films with plasticizers. The DSC curves show an endothermic transition within a specific temperature range, indicating the glass transition of the samples, and good compatibility between HPMCAS and the plasticizers. The glass transition temperature (Tg) and onset temperature of glass transition (Tgonset) were determined as the midpoint and inflection point of the thermal transition, respectively. For the pure HPMCAS film, the Tg and Tgonset were 120.6 ± 0.2 and 114.1 ± 1.7 °C, respectively (Table S1). The incorporation of plasticizers resulted in a significant reduction in the Tg and Tgonset of the composite films, with values of 62.7 ± 0.2 and 52.2 ± 0.7 °C for HPMCAS-TR, and 70.8 ± 0.5 and 58.6 ± 0.6 °C for HPMCAS-OA. HPMCAS-TR demonstrated a low Tg, which can be attributed to the molecular structure of TR, characterized by a higher content of polar ester groups. This structural feature likely enhances its compatibility with the polymer matrix. Previous studies have demonstrated that plasticizers can lower the Tg of materials by inserting themselves between polymer chains, enhancing molecular mobility, and reducing intermolecular forces. The reduction in Tg improves polymer flexibility and spreadability, which is crucial for lowering film-forming temperatures and enhancing film toughness and uniformity (Zhao et al., 2019; Lan et al., 2023). Furthermore, a lower Tg may facilitate the absorption of heat energy, thereby offering enhanced protection to the heat-sensitive active ingredients. However, an excessively low Tg can diminish the resistance of coating films and increase their permeability when subjected to elevated temperature environments. This phenomenon may undermine the protective efficacy of the coating films on embedded components in high-temperature conditions, particularly in environments that exceed the onset temperature of glass transition (Tgonset) (Halloran et al., 2021).

Surface hydrophobicity

The surface hydrophobicity of the coating materials is another critical property that can be assessed using WCA measurements. The WCA values of the HPMCAS film and two composite films containing plasticizers are presented in Fig. 1C. The WCA of the HPMCAS film was 69.4 ± 0.7°. The increased hydrophobicity of HPMCAS relative to cellulose was attributed to the modification of partial -OH groups through methylation and acetylation, which results in enhanced moisture resistance and extended release properties. Adding hydro-soluble TR did not significantly alter the WCA of the composite film. This is primarily due to the presence of a substantial number of hydrophilic groups within HPMCAS molecules, including hydroxyl (∼50 %), hydroxypropyl (∼8 %), and succinyl groups (∼6 %). Furthermore, TR lacks strong hydrophilic groups, and the interaction between the -C=O group of TR and -OH group in HPMCAS reduces their affinity for water molecules. Compared with HPMCAS, the HPMCAS-OA film exhibited a slightly higher WCA, which was attributed to increased hydrophobicity resulting from the incorporation of OA (Fig. 2). Similarly, Shorey et al. (2024) reported a 37.9 % increase in the WCA value for OA-modified lignin-loaded films compared with samples with unmodified lignin.

OP

The oxygen barrier properties of the coating materials are crucial for regulating OP to safeguard oxygen-sensitive bioactive components. Fig. 1D shows the OP of the HPMCAS film and two composite films containing plasticizers. The unmodified HPMCAS film exhibited the lowest OP at 383.1 ± 11.7 cm3/(m2·24 h·0.1 MPa). The incorporation of TR and OA resulted in a notable increase in OP, reaching 420.8 ± 8.9 cm3/(m2·24 h·0.1 MPa) for HPMCAS-TR and 859.5 ± 34.8 cm3/(m2·24 h·0.1 MPa) for HPMCAS-OA. To confirm the underlying mechanism, three-dimensional molecular packing models of the three coating matrices were constructed through MD simulations (Fig. 4). The free volume fraction within a unit cell was quantified to simulate the porosity of the coating film system. The HPMCAS system exhibits the lowest porosity at 28.9 %. The addition of TR and OA led to an increase in porosity by 0.7 % and 2.7 %, respectively, which corresponds with the observed increase in the OP of the HPMCAS-TR and HPMCAS-OA systems. HPMCAS molecules can form a dense network structure through hydrogen bonding between -OH and -C=O groups (Zhao et al., 2019). The increased OP observed in the composite films suggests that the incorporation of plasticizers, particularly OA with its hydrophobic long chains, expanded the pore size of the network structure, thereby resulting in a pronounced increase in OP (Fig. 2).

Characterizations of coated granules

The characterizations of coated granules prepared with various coating matrices, in terms of particle size, moisture content, weight analysis, product yield, and coating efficiency, are presented in Fig. S1 and Table S2. Following the coating process, the average size increased from approximately 560 μm for the uncoated granules to 610 μm for coated granules (Fig. S1). The coating efficiency was assessed by quantifying the surface content of vitB12, revealing that the coating efficiency of all coated granules exceeded 99.6 % (Table S2). Therefore, fluidized bed-coating technology successfully formed a complete protective coating layer on the surface of the uncoated granules. Following the coating process, the mass of each granule batch increased from 150.0 g to between 195.6 and 197.5 g, with yields surpassing 97.8 % for all batches. This can be attributed to the effective fluidized state of the granules and preservation of granule integrity, underscoring the high efficiency of fluidized bed-coating technology (Yang et al., 2022; Pohlen et al., 2022). Additionally, the moisture content of the granules decreased from 4.0 % prior to the coating process to 3.3 % after, suggesting that the particles underwent further drying during the fluidization process, particularly in the initial stage.

Stability of probiotic and vitB12 in coating process

The probiotic, JYLP-326, viability and vitB12 content of granules coated with various formulations are detailed in Table 2. After wet granulation, the viable bacterial count and vitB12 content in all the prepared core granules remained stable at approximately 108 CFU/g and 10 mg/g, respectively, indicating that the granulation process did not adversely affect the stability of these bioactive components. Importantly, during the 14 h fluidized coating process, there was no significant reduction in viable bacterial count or vitB12 content, when adjusting for weight gain due to the coating material. The fluidized coating technique effectively controls system temperature and sustains the suspension of core particles through the application of a moderately heated airflow. In this study, the temperature of the coated granules in the fluidized chamber was maintained at 37.0 ± 2.0 °C when exposed to a 50.0 ± 2.0 °C airflow. This process prevents localized overheating and thermal damage to bacteria and thermally sensitive compounds. Consequently, fluidized bed coating offers a significant advantage in encapsulating heat-sensitive ingredients compared with that of high-temperature encapsulation methods (Pohlen et al., 2022; Ramachandran et al., 2024).

Storage stability

The viability of probiotic strain JYLP-326 and chemical stability of vitB12 across four granule formulations were examined during a 10-day short-term storage period under various conditions: refrigeration (4 °C, 20 % RH), room temperature (25 °C, 50 % RH), and high-temperature storage (40 °C, 50 % RH). The vitB12 content in all tested samples remained stable throughout the 10-day storage period (Fig. 5A), which was attributable to the encapsulation effect achieved through the granulation and fluidized coating processes (He et al., 2024). Even in the absence of further fluidized coating, microencapsulated granules based on the maltodextrin matrix were initially formed during core material processing in the blank formulation. Consequently, vitB12 demonstrated substantial chemical stability across all tested samples during short-term storage.

HPMCAS, hydroxypropyl methylcellulose acetate succinate; OA, oleic acid; TR, triacetin.

Assessment of probiotic viability demonstrated notable variations in the protective efficacy of the different formulations (Fig. 5B). Under refrigerated conditions, all experimental groups maintained a high SR with minimal decline, indicating the robust stability of JYLP-326 under low-temperature and low-humidity conditions. At room temperature, the probiotic SR in the uncoated granules decreased to 90.9 ± 0.2 %. In contrast, the groups treated with HPMCAS, HPMCAS-TR, and HPMCAS-OA exhibited significantly enhanced protection, with SRs of 99.4 ± 0.3 %, 98.6 ± 1.0 %, and 99.7 ± 0.5 %, respectively. The physical appearance and moisture adsorption of all experimental groups were assessed both before storage and after 10 days of storage (Fig. S4 and Table S4). The uncoated granules, when stored at room temperature (25 °C, 50 % RH) for 10 days, exhibited significant agglomeration and caking, along with the highest moisture adsorption. This behavior is attributed to the high hygroscopicity of maltodextrin (Tao et al., 2019). In contrast, the coated granules retained their free-flowing properties and did not gain weight due to moisture absorption. The hydrophobic HPMCAS matrix effectively formed coating films that hindered the penetration of environmental moisture and oxygen, thereby ensuring the SR of probiotics (Stukalin et al., 2010; Moreno et al., 2020). The variation in probiotic stability across different formulations was further exacerbated at elevated temperatures. After a storage period of 10 days, the SR of probiotics in the uncoated granules significantly decreased to 26.3 ± 0.7 %, whereas the HPMCAS group exhibited the highest SR at 82.0 ± 1.1 %. This enhanced stability is attributed to the structural integrity maintained by its relatively high Tg of 122.6 °C. In contrast, formulations incorporating plasticizers demonstrated a marked decline in performance with the SR of 74.1 ± 0.6 % for HPMCAS-TR and 72.6 ± 0.8 % for HPMCAS-OA.

Following short-term storage under three distinct conditions, the morphological alterations in the coating layer of coated granules were evaluated using SEM (Fig. 6). All freshly prepared granules exhibited an intact spherical shape and a rough surface with circular patches, characteristic of products manufactured via the Wurster-type bottom-spray fluidized-bed process. The surface of the granules displayed no visible defects or pores, indicating a high degree of structural integrity. During a 10-day storage period at refrigerated and room temperature conditions, no significant changes were observed in the surface morphology of the three coated granules compared with that at their initial states. However, under elevated temperature conditions, the initially rough surfaces of the two coated granules containing plasticizers became smooth, accompanied by the formation of small pores. This transformation was attributed to the reduced Tg of the composite films, which increases the molecular mobility and decreases mechanical strength at high temperatures (Coimbra et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2025). This phenomenon contributes to the disruption of the composite film structure and enhances permeability, thereby explaining the reduced probiotic SRs of HPMCAS-TR and HPMCAS-OA under high-temperature conditions.

In vitro simulated gastrointestinal digestion

Release behavior of vitB12

In the food and pharmaceutical industries, the primary objective of using coating processes is to modulate the release behavior of coated components during digestion. The release characteristics of vitB12 in the uncoated and three differently coated granules were assessed in a simulated gastrointestinal environment (Fig. 7A). The vitB12 in the uncoated granules was completely released within 10 min in SGF, whereas all three coated granules demonstrated significantly slower release properties over a 120-min period in SGF. The HPMCAS-TR formulation exhibited the greatest resistance in SGF, with a cumulative release rate of only 3.4 ± 0.1 % for vitB12 over 120 min, which was significantly lower than the 40.7 ± 0.7 % observed in the HPMCAS group. This effect is attributed to the enhanced hydrogen bonding network present in the HPMCAS-TR formulation, which prolonged the relaxation time of polymer chains and consequently inhibited film swelling and erosion. The HPMCAS-OA group demonstrated a final release rate of 14.0 ± 0.7 % for vitB12 in SGF. While the presence of OA may enhance the resistance to moisture penetration by increasing the film hydrophobicity, its highly flexible chain structure can disrupt the tight interchain alignment of the polymer (Fig. 4). This disruption leads to the formation of irregularly dispersed gaps within the film structure, thereby increasing the permeation rate of gastric juice and accelerating the diffusion of active ingredients (Majoudi et al., 2025). The release profiles of vitB12 from three coated granules during SGF exposure were analyzed using mathematical dissolution kinetic models, including zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, and Korsmeyer–Peppas models (Table S5). The ranking of release rate correlation coefficients for the three granule types was HPMCAS > HPMCAS-OA > HPMCAS-TR, aligning with their observed release behaviors. Based on the coefficient of determination (R2), these release profiles demonstrated a strong fit with both the zero-order and Korsmeyer–Peppas kinetic models, thereby confirming the slow-release characteristics of all coated granules in SGF (Chang et al., 2023). The diffusion dissolution index (n) derived from the Korsmeyer–Peppas model exceeded 0.45 for all formulations, indicating that vitB12 release was controlled by a combination of coating layer erosion and drug diffusion through the coating layer (Thakkar et al., 2020).

The uncoated granules served as a Blank. SGF: simulated gastric fluid, SIF: simulated intestinal fluid.

During the 120–240 min interval of the SIF, all three coating groups exhibited a significant pH-responsive triggered release, which was attributed to the characteristics of HPMCAS (Nogami et al., 2021). Under neutral pH conditions, the ionization of the -COOH groups in HPMCAS disrupts the intramolecular hydrogen bonds, leading to the disintegration of the film structure, thereby facilitating the release of the coated components (Zhang et al., 2018). However, formulations containing different plasticizers exhibited varying release rates. Notably, the HPMCAS-TR group displayed a marked increase in release from 3.4 ± 0.1 % at 120 min to 90.4 ± 6.1 % at 135 min when exposed to SIF. Under neutral conditions, the hydrogen bond network of HPMCAS-TR can be more rapidly disrupted because the introduction of TR increases film porosity and consequently accelerates the penetration of intestinal fluid. The observed release behavior aligns with the findings of a previous study that used HPMCAS in conjunction with the plasticizer, TR, for the protection of diltiazem hydrochloride (Fukui et al., 2001). Compared with the HPMCAS-TR group, the HPMCAS-OA group demonstrated a relatively gradual release, increasing from 14.0 ± 0.7 % at 120 min to 76.8 ± 5.9 % at 135 min. This behavior is likely attributable to the formation of a temporary diffusion barrier caused by hydrophobic aggregation of the OA chains.

Survival of probiotics in digestion

The impact of various coating formulations on the SR of L. plantarum JYLP-326 during simulated gastrointestinal digestion was assessed (Fig. 7B). The viability of the uncoated group significantly declined from 7.96 ± 0.08 log CFU/g to 4.99 ± 0.19 log CFU/g after 120 min of exposure to SGF and subsequently stabilized at approximately 5.0 log CFU/g in SIF, indicating a high sensitivity of the uncoated JYLP-326 bacteria to gastric acidity. Conversely, all coated formulations provided substantial protection to the probiotic, with the SR in the HPMCAS group increasing to 6.43 ± 0.03 log CFU/g, while the composite coating formulations maintained a bacterial viability exceeding 7.0 log CFU/g throughout digestion. Previous research on the probiotic characteristics of JYLP-326 has demonstrated that gastric acidity is the primary barrier affecting the survival of JYLP-326 in the digestive tract (Zhu et al., 2024). Hence, maintaining a high SR of JYLP-326 and ensuring its physiological functions necessitates the avoidance of exposure to gastric acid environments through an intact coating. Compared with the uncoated and HPMCAS groups, the complexed formulation containing HPMCAS and a plasticizer provides enhanced protection for probiotic bacteria against degradation in SGF. The observed improvement is ascribed to the incorporation of the plasticizer into HPMCAS, which augments the controlled-release properties of the composite matrix under acidic conditions. This observation is consistent with the results obtained from the vitB12 release test.

Morphological changes of coated granules during digestion

To accurately assess the release behavior of the coated components, granules coated with HPMCAS or composite films were collected at 120 min (the end of the SGF phase) and 135 min (the onset of the SIF phase) during the digestion test, and subsequently analyzed using SEM (Fig. 8). Micrographs of the three different granules were obtained prior to simulated digestion to enable a morphological comparison. Consistent with the aforementioned observations, all coated granules exhibited structural integrity, devoid of any defects or breakages, before undergoing simulated digestion. After 120 min of exposure to SGF, the surfaces of the granules in the HPMCAS group exhibited increased microcracks and wrinkled regions, with some film layer edges showing signs of peeling. This suggests that the structural integrity of the HPMCAS film is compromised in acidic environments. In contrast, the surface structure of the HPMCAS-OA group remained relatively intact during the SGF phase. However, high-magnification observations revealed the presence of some irregular small pores. This observation suggests that the hydrophobic aggregation of OA may compromise the film density, thereby facilitating the penetration of small-molecule compounds. This structural characteristic could explain the 14.0 % release rate of vitB12 in the HPMCAS-OA group and its nearly complete protection of probiotic bacteria. Conversely, the HPMCAS-TR group maintained very high film density and integrity in SGF, exhibiting no obvious cracks or holes and presenting a homogeneous structure, which demonstrated excellent gastric acid tolerance. Outstanding structural stability determines the controlled release of both probiotics and small-molecule vitB12.

Upon exposure to SIF, the coating films of all the three formulations were disrupted. The surface of the HPMCAS group exhibited peeling and fragmentation of the film layer, indicating rapid destabilization in the alkaline environment. The composite films containing plasticizers demonstrated distinct morphological changes compared with those of the single HPMCAS film. Due to the leakage of core components through perforations, the granules in HPMCAS-TR and HPMCAS-OA exhibited significant crumpling and collapsing; however, fragmentation did not occur. This phenomenon can be attributed to the increased resistance of the composite films resulting from the incorporation of the plasticizers. Nevertheless, after 30 min of exposure to SIF, the integrity of all three coated granules was largely compromised, indicating the substantial release of encapsulated probiotics and vitB12. Additionally, it should be noted that the granules containing HPMCAS at the end of the SGF phase, along with those containing HPMCAS-TR and HPMCAS-OA at the onset of exposure to SIF, had already demonstrated obvious fragmentation of the coating film. The granules selected for SEM observation were relatively intact, as these were more readily collectible.

Adhesion capacity on Caco-2 cells

To assess the effect of the coating materials on the ability of probiotics to colonize the intestinal mucosa, the adhesion capacity of JYLP-326 in various formulations was evaluated using intestinal epithelial Caco-2 cells (Fig. 9). Compared with the positive control strain LGG (71.7 ± 7.8 %), the free JYLP-326 bacteria demonstrated a comparable adhesion rate of 65.6 ± 3.1 % to Caco-2 cells (P > 0.05), indicating its substantial potential for intestinal mucosal colonization (Zhu et al., 2024). The JYLP-326 strain in the blank sample achieved an adhesion rate of 66.4 ± 4.9 %, suggesting the presence of maltodextrin and vitB12 did not adversely affect the viability and adhesion capacity of JYLP-326. In contrast, the adhesion rate of the probiotic in the coated granules exhibited a slight reduction, ranging from 54.3 ± 7.3 % to 59.0 ± 6.5 %. This minor decrease in adhesion can be attributed to the presence of HPMCAS, which likely occupied the hydrophobic sites on the bacterial surface, thereby diminishing the interaction between the cells and bacteria (Alkalbani et al., 2022). However, no significant differences were observed between the formulations (P > 0.05). This suggests that HPMCAS coating material alone or in conjunction with plasticizers, such as OA or TR, did not induce any structural disruption of bacterial adhesion-related regions. Consequently, it did not significantly alter the mucosal adhesion properties of the bacterial surface. Hoffmann and Daniels (2019) reported the use of oral dispersible tablets for delivering L. plantarum and L. paracasei, and found that the inclusion of the cellulose derivative, HPMC, did not compromise the mucoadhesive function of the probiotics.

Conclusion

This study initially evaluated the properties of HPMCAS matrix films used in the fluid bed coating process, with a particular focus on the effects of TR or OA as plasticizers. The HPMCAS-TR formulation exhibited enhanced hydrogen bond interactions, further decreasing film permeability, whereas the HPMCAS-OA formulation resulted in increased film permeability and surface hydrophobicity. The incorporation of the plasticizers notably reduced the Tg of the composite films. Furthermore, the co-encapsulation of the L. plantarum JYLP-326 probiotic and vitB12 was achieved through the fluid bed-coating technology with different coating matrices. Owing to the mild preparation conditions, the probiotic viability and vitB12 content were well preserved following the granulation and coating processes. During simulated gastrointestinal digestion, granules coated with the HPMCAS-plasticizer formulation exhibited superior gastric retention and targeted intestinal release, thereby ensuring higher probiotic viability and controlled release of vitB12. All granules coated with the HPMC matrix demonstrated greater probiotic survival during storage at both refrigerated and room temperature. However, the incorporation of a plasticizer into HPMCAS was less effective in resisting relatively high temperatures due to a decreased Tg of the composite films. The current research has certain limitations. First, the functional assessment conducted in this study primarily concentrated on encapsulated granules produced using laboratory-grade equipment. Consequently, the findings may be predominantly applicable to the development of relatively simple food products, such as premixed powders and granules. Further verification of their functionality is required when these granules are applied to complex food systems and large-scale production. Second, long-term stability and in vivo evaluation remains necessary for future investigations. Finally, HPMCAS has not yet received approval for direct use in food products. Nevertheless, this research enhances the understanding of the role of two food-grade plasticizers in cellulose-based polymer coating agents. Additionally, the fluid bed-coating process using the HPMCAS-plasticizer coating agent demonstrated substantial benefits in maintaining probiotic viability and stability of labile components throughout manufacturing, storage, and digestion, indicating promising potential for the development of nutrition-coated products.
