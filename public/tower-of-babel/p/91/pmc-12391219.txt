Study of the release kinetics of dalbavancin from bone allografts
Authors: Roger Erivan, Nicolas Bourzat, Mouloud Yessaad, Guillaume Villatte, Stéphane descamps, Stéphane Boisgard, Philip Chennell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone infections are common and difficult to treat, and secondary bone defects, which are often observed, may require a bone allograft. In this case, the surgeon will add antibiotics (usually vancomycin) in direct contact with the bone graft during the procedure, in order to allow in-situ release after implantation in the operating site. Dalbavancin is a novel antibiotic indicated for treating acute bacterial infections resistant to vancomycin. Its modified chemical structure grants it an increased half-life that could modify its release kinetics from the bone allograft. The aim of this study was to determine the release kinetics of dalbavancin from bone grafts after they were immersed in a dalbavancin solution. The study was conducted using a Design of Experiments (DoE) protocol. Decellularized and delipidated allograft bone cubes were preliminarily characterized and put into contact with dalbavancin solutions. The parameters that were studied where the allograft mass, initial dalbavancin concentration and contact time. The samples were then transferred into the release media, which was sampled over time and dalbavancin was quantified using a high pressure liquid chromatography with diode array detector method that was developed for the occasion. Our results showed that on average, dalbavancin was fully released after 5 min for the lower mass bone grafts, but after 60 min for the high mass and high concentration conditions. Contact time had no impact, thus indicating a fast loading process of dalbavancin into the allograft. Although our study revealed the possible benefits of using dalbavancin in bone grafting, an in-vivo study is required to confirm our hypotheses.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Osteoarticular infections (OAIs) involve bones, joints, or osteoarticular prostheses. They are difficult to eradicate and usually require long-term antibiotic therapy combined with extensive surgical excision of the infected tissues. Their pathophysiology involves complex interactions between the infectious agent and the host’s osteoarticular tissue and immune system. One of the bacterial persistence mechanisms leading to the relapse of chronic bacterial infections is biofilm formation [1]. Biofilms are microbial communities that adhere to inert surfaces (i.e., bone grafts, prosthetic implants, or fixation hardware) and are embedded within an extracellular matrix, which enhances bacterial adhesion and survival. It was found that these biofilms can reduce antibiotic penetration and form quiescent intracellular reservoirs, with phenotypic switching of certain strains to slow-growing antibiotic-resistant small colony variants. The chronic phase of OAI involves major bone tissue destructive factors and osteolysis that may cause significant bone defects.

Bone allografts are part of the promising armamentarium for treating these bone defects [2, 3]. These grafts are harvested from living (surgical waste) or deceased patients. As the penetration of antibiotics in the bone and joints can be challenging [4], a proven way of preventing infections in these inert tissues is to immerse the allografts in an antibiotic solution, which have been proven as being suitable vectors for delivering local antibiotics [5, 6]. Research and clinical practices currently focus on using old and well established antibiotics like glycopeptides, cephalosporins or aminosids like tobramycin [7–9]. Whilst cefazolin (a first generation cephalosporin) has proven difficult to adequately manipulate due to its structurally-linked aqueous chemical instability [10], and tobramycin seemed to affect implant fixation when high doses were used [11], most published studies have been conducted with the glycopeptide vancomycin, which has been shown to be safe (even with extremely high quantities such as 1 g of vancomycin for 300 g of allograft [12]) and to be able to effectively prevent bacterial colonization [13] and clinically decrease the risk of deep surgical site infections [14, 15]. Unfortunately, vancomycin’s short half-life means it is rapidly eliminated and does not remain in the bone. These factors suggest that its clinical efficacy could be enhanced even further and that using another molecule with slower release and diffusion from the graft could be an alternative. Likewise, inconclusive trials have been conducted with teicoplanin in bone grafts, but the quantity released was too low [16]. Also, antibiotic release and resistance could be influenced by factors such as the patient’s characteristics, liver function and type of bacteria [17]. Additionally, any complementary treatment of the material characteristics such as for example polymer surface coating or the use of co-adjuvants mixed with the allograft have been shown to be able to influence antibiotic release [18–20].

Dalbavancin (Xydalba©) is a semisynthetic lipoglycopeptide antibiotic derived from teicoplanin. This molecule has a molecular weight of 1816.7 g/mol and a long carbon chain (10-methyl-1-oxo-undecanoyl) grafted onto the amino-β-D-glucopyranosyl group, making it more lipophilic (with a calculated octanol-water partition coefficient of 3.8) and giving it a longer plasmatic half-life (around 372 h) than vancomycin (4 to 6 h) and teicoplanin (100 to 170 h) [21, 22]. It is indicated for treating acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections (ABSSSI) in adults and pediatric patients aged 3 months and older. Its in vivo activity inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding to the terminal D-alanyl-D-alanine residues of peptidoglycan precursors of the nascent cell wall, preventing cross-linked peptide chains. Its efficacy has been proven on Gram-positive bacilli, such as methicillin-susceptible and methicillin-resistant staphylococci and certain streptococci [23]. It should be noted that Gram-negative bacilli are inherently resistant to this molecule. Its antitoxin and bactericidal effects against biofilms are very interesting [24].

The 2 intravenous treatment regimens available are either a single dose (1500 mg on day 1) or 2 doses (1000 mg on day 1 and 500 mg on day 8) infused over 30 min. The long half-life of this molecule is particularly interesting for treating patients with OAIs who have limited venous access since the antibiotic therapy is often extended (6 to 12 weeks) [22]. Substituting vancomycin for dalbavancin to immerse the bone allografts would perhaps, in conjunction with the lipophilicity conferred by its carbon chain, increase retention and promote the extended release. The aim of this study was to determine the release kinetics of dalbavancin from bone grafts after they were immersed in a dalbavancin solution for a set period of time and assess the impact of certain variables (bone allograft mass, dalbavancin concentration, and contact time) have on these kinetics and the quantities released.

Materials and methods

Materials

Allograft bone harvesting

Both organ and bone tissue donations were anonymous and free and only required the donor’s consent or the family’s agreement. The musculoskeletal tissues harvested postmortem included bone, tendons, and menisci. Each bone graft was then processed and identified (donor label, tissue type, and lateralization). All the harvested musculoskeletal tissue samples were stored at −80 °C in a tissue bank (Ostéobanque, Clermont-Ferrand, France), which is an authorized independent structure whose processes are regularly audited by the French Health Agency. The bone graft processing steps ensured optimal preservation of bone tissue and microbiological safety. The steps followed for these grafts were:

Pulse lavage with sterile water, followed by sonication and centrifugation to perform a deep cleaning and remove all poor-quality tissues while preserving the healthy tissue.

Pulse lavage with sterile water, followed by sonication and centrifugation to perform a deep cleaning and remove all poor-quality tissues while preserving the healthy tissue.

Supercritical carbon dioxide (CO2) cleaning to decellularize and delipidate without chemicals.

Supercritical carbon dioxide (CO2) cleaning to decellularize and delipidate without chemicals.

Sample preparation and analytical considerations

The bone allografts were in the shape of small cubes measuring around 125 mm3 (5 × 5 × 5 mm). The cubes were weighed on a previously tared analytical balance to obtain the total weight of bone allografts desired for each experiment: 350, 700, and 1400 mg. A qualitative visual characterization of the bone allograft cubes was carried out in parallel to this study. The cubes were individually weighed and classified into 3 categories: low porosity, medium porosity, and high porosity. The water weight they could absorb was also assessed for each category.

ADVANZ PHARMA kindly supplied the dalbavancin in the form of Xydalba® 500 mg vials (powder for concentrate for solution for infusion, batch W060096, expiry date 11/2023). The content of each vial was reconstituted by slowly adding 10, 12.5, and 25 mL of sterile water for injection (WFI) to obtain dalbavancin solutions of 50, 40, and 20 mg/mL, respectively.

Three different variables that were hypothesized to impact dalbavancin release kinetics were tested:

Experimental design

If we had wanted to conduct all the experiments for these 3 variables, the total number of experiments would have been 33 (i.e., 27). However, the limited availability of the raw material (dalbavancin) due to the high price per vial (around 1000 euros) resulted in us developing a design of experiments (DoE) using MODDE® Pro software (version 13.0.2.34314, Sartorius Stedim Data Analytics AB, Göttingen, Germany). This DoE is a statistical approach for optimizing experiments by simultaneously modifying different variables while maintaining the capability to analyze and interpret responses. Practical implementation

For this study, we developed and validated a liquid chromatography (LC) method to quantify dalbavancin using a modular LC system comprised of an AS-4150 autosampler, a PU-4180 LPG pump, a CO-4060 oven and an MD-2018 Plus diode array detector (Jasco France, Lisses, France).

The method was validated following recommendations by the International Council for Harmonization of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use [26]. A calibration curve of 5 concentration levels ranging from 5 to 50 µg/mL of dalbavancin was analyzed daily for 3 days. Repeatability and intermediate precision were evaluated by analyzing 6 samples of the target analytical concentration (10 µg/mL) for 3 days. To verify the precision of this method, repeatability was estimated by calculating the intraday relative standard deviation (RSD), and the intermediate precision was evaluated using the interday RSD. Both RSDs were considered acceptable if less than 5%. The accuracy of this method was verified by evaluating the recovery of 5 theoretical concentrations to experimental values found using the mean curve equation, and results should be within the range of 95–105%. The accuracy profiles were also computed, as described by Hubert et al. [27, 28], and the results were considered acceptable if within the range of 90–110%.

Therefore, a total of 15 solutions were reconstituted and poured into a crystallizer-type container according to the desired DoE: 5 with a concentration of 20 mg/mL, 5 with a concentration of 40 mg/mL, and 5 with a concentration of 50 mg/mL.

The cubes were then transferred into one of the reconstituted dalbavancin solutions using dissecting forceps and were kept fully immersed for a set period of time (5, 10, and 20 min).

Meanwhile, we prepared new containers containing 10 mL of WFI that were weighed on a tared balance for each of the 15 experiments (n = 1 for each experiment). At the end of the contact time allotted for each experiment, the soaked allografts were transferred 1 by 1, using metal forceps, into this new WFI solution. This step made it possible to determine the weight of the soaked allografts. Prior knowledge of the density also helped us calculate the volume and quantity of dalbavancin retained in the allograft cubes for each experiment.

Each container of bone grafts was placed on a ping-pong shaker at a speed of 150 rpm to ensure homogenization of the medium.

Samples were then collected at 5, 10, 15, 30, and 60 min to measure dalbavancin concentrations released by the bone grafts at each time interval (Fig. 1).

Experimental design diagram. WFI: Water for injection. RPM: rounds per minute

All data were analyzed using either MODDE® Pro-DOE software or Prism software (version 10.1.2, GraphPad Software, Boston, USA). When applicable, an ordinary one-way analysis of variant (ANOVA) was performed, assuming a Gaussian distribution of the residuals and equal standard deviations. Multiple comparisons were performed if statistically significant differences were observed (p < 0.05). All results were presented as mean ± standard deviation unless stated otherwise.

Complementary study

In order to compare the kinetics of dalbavancin release to a benchmark drug, a complementary study using vancomycin (Vancomycin Sandoz, 1 g, powder for solution for injection, batch F0332, expiring Nov 2025, Levallois-Perret, France) was performed using the same methodology as described for the dalbavancin study. The same weights of allografts, concentrations and impregnation were used, for a total of 7 combinations, equivalent to conditions E1, E2, E3, E4, E8, E10 and E11 described in Table 1 and henceforth described as conditions EV1, EV2, EV3, EV4, EV8, EV10, and EV11. Vancomycin was quantified using a method adapted from Plaidy et al. [29].

Results

Dalbavancin quantification

With the LC method used, dalbavancin had a retention time of 9.5 ± 0.2 min (Fig. 2). The method was linear for concentrations ranging from 5 to 50 µg/mL. The mean regression equation was y = 1997x - 618, where x was the dalbavancin concentration (in µg/mL), and y was the peak area ratio of the corresponding chromatogram. The intercept was not significantly different from 0, and the coefficient of determination (R²), which took into account the 3 calibration curves, was 0.9983. The relative mean bias coefficients were less than |1.17%|, except for the 5 µg/mL calibration point, which was −4.68%. The mean repeatability and intermediate precision RSD coefficients were of 2.88% and 3.14%, respectively. The accuracy profile constructed with the data showed that the limits of the 95% confidence interval coefficients were all within 9% of the expected value. The method was, therefore, considered to be accurate.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to analyze the release kinetics of dalbavancin and the quantities released from the bone grafts, as well as investigate the variables that might affect these kinetics. Three different variables, with 3 levels each, were studied.

The choice of using 3 different allograft masses was a compromise between real clinical applications and the feasibility of performing these measurements in a laboratory environment. In fact, it is very common to use 125 mm3 bone grafts weighing close to 1500 mg in our clinical practice. The same reasoning was used to determine the contact time. The surgeon is limited by time constraints in the operating theater, and in our department, bone allografts are usually immersed in an antibiotic solution for approximately 20 min. The solubility of dalbavancin powder in WFI limited the choice of initial concentrations, and we could not use concentrations greater than 50 mg/mL.

Overall, our measurements showed that the dalbavancin release kinetics during our experiments followed a logarithmic function, with total release usually achieved within the first 5 min, especially for the low mass bone allografts. Concentration and mass seem to be the variables that could affect the release kinetics. This is coherent with previously published data (also obtained in an in-vitro setting) that showed that bone mass and type could impact vancomycin uptake and release [6], and also with the data obtained in this work using the same methodology applied to vancomycin. Interestingly, it can be seen that in both cases (for dalbavancin and vancomycin) the release profile was almost complete even after only a couple of min, except for the conditions yielding the highest amount of dalbavancin released, ie conditions E4 and E8 (1400 mg allograft soaked in a loading solution of 50 mg/mL of dalbavancin), for which an additional 20–30% (approximatively) amount of dalbvancin was released slowly over the following 55 min. This could indicate that most of the antibiotics (dalbavancin or vancomycin) is not strongly linked or adherent to the allograft surface, and therefore that the release rate is not particularly impacted by either their chemical structure or biological pharmacokinetics of the drugs, except perhaps when high quantities are loaded and/or perhaps the porosity of the allograft allows for multiple in-depth canals that require more time to empty themselves once loaded.

Conversely, our study found that the initial contact time in the dalbavancin solution had no statistically significant impact on the kinetics. This information is critical since surgeons in our hospital must wait an average of 20 min before introducing antibiotic-impregnated bone grafts into the recipient site. Our study simulated the most favorable conditions for obtaining a long and sustained release. However, we noted that the ideal conditions simulated at 60 min did not match those at the other times. This can be explained by the fact that all the dalbavancin was released at 60 min in our simulation.

As mentioned previously, local antibiotic delivery is often the best option, but must take into account the nature of the microorganisms most likely to be the cause of surgical site infection [30]. From a clinical perspective, a recently published study indicated that Staphylococcus aureus was responsible for more than 70% of infections in pediatric bone and joint infections among children [31]. A similar proportion (67.5%) was found in a systematic review (last updated in February 2023) of bone and joint infections covering 23 studies and 450 patients [32] and in a recently published study American study [33] indicating a proportion of 70.7%, including 40.0% of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus). Even if Dalbavancin retains the same activity as vancomycin and does not act on gram-negative bacilli, as they are less frequently the cause of deep surgical site bone and joint infections this element should only be a minor issue.

Other ways of achieving sustained antibiotic in-situ delivery have been investigated. Hornyak et al. studied the use of a biodegradable coating of chitosan and alginate impregnated with three different antibiotics over human bone allografts and found that the duration of the delivery was greatly extended [18], but such a method couldn’t be easily used in clinical practice and would need to be prepared at an industrial level. The osteoconductive properties of such a product would also need to be thoroughly checked as the coating could reduce interactions with the bone cellular media. In another study, Bormann et al. evaluated the release kinetics of gentamycin from different human bone allograft types. They showed that even if all grafts released the antibiotic for 2 to 21 days, in all cases the concentrations measured in the release media decreased by more than ten-fold in 24 h, thus indicating that more than 90% of all the loaded antibiotic was released very quickly (burst-effect) [34]. Very similar fast-release kinectis were also found in an interesting study by Coraça-Hubert et al. [35], who also demonstrated in an in-vivo study that even residual amounts of antibiotics eluting out of the allogenic bone blocks in which they had been loaded could still have an antimicrobial effect.

More exotically, some authors have also envisioned the use hydrogel or micelles or titania nanotubes for the antibiotics release [36–39], but these materials do not possess sufficient mechanical properties, that the allografts on the other hand do have.

Our study’s limitations were the small number of experiments performed for our DoE and the lack of correlation between the in vitro and in vivo dalbavancin release mediums (a bleeding bone cavity within a living organism versus WFI). These results, therefore, need to be confirmed on a larger scale. Given the molecule’s very interesting pharmacokinetics [40], in-vivo local efficacy studies seem highly relevant. These various experiments showed that there were initially very high concentrations in the surrounding environment that rapidly decreased with a significant residual level that persisted after several days. Some factors of the in-vivo environment could possibly have a major influence on the release kinetics such as (hypothetically) the size of the graft (linked to the size of the bone defect), local vascularization, or also perhaps the additional presence of an implant with or without cement.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated the possible advantages of using dalbavancin in bone grafts, but an in-vivo study should be performed to confirm our hypotheses.
