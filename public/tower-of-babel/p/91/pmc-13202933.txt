The impact of misdiagnosed heart failure and cardiac or extra-cardiac disease burden on the prognosis in patients with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Nurlan B Yeshniyazov, Igor N Posokhov, Vadim V Medovchshikov, Gulnara L Kurmanalina, Dariga E Kushimova, Iliada Zh Talipova, Kulsin K Tokbayeva, Alma S Akhmetova, Bekbolat Zholdin

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe examined how misdiagnosed heart failure (HF) and the burden of cardiac and extra-cardiac diseases prognostically impact 1-year outcomes (all-cause mortality, rehospitalization, and their composite) in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).MethodsA retrospective analysis of a prospective observational cohort study was performed. Background

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and chronic heart failure (HF) are two common conditions encountered in clinical practice [1]. The presence of T2DM increases the risk of HF, whereas HF significantly affects the outcomes of patients with T2DM. Despite recent therapeutic advances, morbidity and mortality rates after HF onset remain substantial [2]. HF in T2DM patients often presents atypically or is masked by other diabetes-related complications, posing significant diagnostic challenges. Of particular concern is the problem of misdiagnosis (both over- and underdiagnosis) of HF in such patients, especially in primary care settings [3]. Misdiagnosed HF occurs when the syndrome is not accurately diagnosed, either because diagnostic data are misinterpreted or appropriate diagnostic tests are not used. This includes missing diagnoses, false positive diagnoses, and misclassification. These issues potentially lead to inadequate management, which may adversely affect the prognosis. To reduce the morbidity and mortality rates, understanding this prognostic impact is crucial for developing targeted screening strategies, improving diagnostic accuracy, and optimizing management protocols in this vulnerable patient population.

Although several studies have explored HF misdiagnosis, few have specifically addressed this issue longitudinally in T2DM patients. While existing research identifies various contributing factors like cardiovascular conditions and non-cardiovascular comorbidities [4], a critical limitation of these studies on HF misdiagnosis lies in their methodological design, often confined to case reports [3], cross-sectional studies [5], or descriptive reviews [6]. This highlights a critical gap, as few studies have assessed the prognostic impact based on longitudinal data, especially in T2DM patients.

We believe that the presence of multiple comorbidities, encompassing both cardiac and extracardiac conditions, significantly influences the overall prognosis of patients with HF. However, how this “disease burden” specifically modulates outcomes when HF is initially misdiagnosed, particularly in a T2DM cohort, is a critical area that requires further investigation. A distinctive feature of our study is the assessment of the prognostic impact of HF misdiagnosis in T2DM patients based on longitudinal study data. Our objective was to examine the prognostic impact of misdiagnosed HF (both under- and overdiagnosis) and the burden of cardiac and extracardiac diseases on 1-year outcomes (all-cause mortality, rehospitalization, and their composite) in patients with T2DM.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective analysis of a prospective observational cohort study was performed. We re-analyzed data from a prospectively maintained registry. This database collected comprehensive clinical, demographic, and outcome data for all patients admitted to the Department of Cardiology and Internal Medicine Department of the University Hospital from January to December 2018, ensuring the 1-year follow-up period concluded before December 2019. Data reanalysis involved extracting specific variables relevant to T2DM, HF diagnosis, MAGGIC score components, and clinical outcomes. This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the research protocol was approved by the relevant ethics committee. Informed consent was obtained from all the participants (or their legally authorized representatives).

Patient grouping and misdiagnosis definition

Consecutive patients with established T2DM who were referred to our hospital were prospectively enrolled. Exclusion criteria included: age < 40 years (to focus on an age group with higher T2DM and HF prevalence and exclude early onset or atypical HF), type 1 diabetes (due to significant differences in pathophysiology and management from T2DM-related HF), and acute coronary syndrome (to avoid confounding acute prognosis with immediate post-ACS complications).

All patients underwent a comprehensive diagnostic workup at baseline, including clinical evaluation, echocardiography, N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) analysis, and a review of their medical history. We reviewed medical histories, assessing prior cardiovascular events, comorbidities, and current medication use. The final HF diagnosis was established in the hospital according to the ESC HF guidelines [7, 8] and confirmed by consensus of two experienced HF specialists. The interrater reliability between the observers showed 97% agreement in identifying HF (kappa coefficient, 0.91). We divided the enrolled patients into two groups: (1) patients with HF (HF+) and (2) patients without HF (HF-).

Misdiagnosis of HF was defined as the difference between the diagnosis of HF contained in the primary care medical records of the patient upon admission and the final diagnosis established in the hospital at discharge. Primary care medical records were obtained from referring general practitioners or internal medicine specialists and included documented diagnoses, medication lists, and relevant clinical notes at the time of the patient referral. Misdiagnosis was assessed by comparing the documented HF status in the records with the final hospital diagnosis.

In each group (HF + and HF-), the following subgroups were distinguished: (1) subgroup of patients without a HF diagnosis in their primary care records who subsequently had a final HF diagnosis established (HFPC-/HF+), and (2) subgroup of patients with a diagnosis of HF in primary care records who subsequently had HF excluded from their final diagnosis (HFPC+/HF-).

Calculation of cardiac and extra-cardiac burden

To quantify the demographic, cardiac, and noncardiac comorbid risk components across the entire cohort, including both HF + and HF- patients, we adapted the externally validated MAGGIC risk score [9]. The MAGGIC score is an optimal model for predicting death in patients with HF, with 13 variables identified as being highly significant, and an integer risk score created using model estimates of the predictive strength of each variable. In the current analysis, we used all 13 baseline risk factors identified in the MAGGIC score, which were extracted directly from the registry data and grouped into three domains representing demographic burden (age and sex), cardiac burden (left ventricular ejection fraction, New York Heart Association class, systolic blood pressure, HF duration, β-blocker use, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibition), and noncardiac risk factors (body mass index, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, creatinine, and current smoking). Using the MAGGIC integer score, we calculated the sum of each domain for all patients. This approach, similar to [10], allowed us to consistently evaluate these risk domains. While originally developed for established HF, we applied its component factors to all patients to provide a comparable measure of disease burden, acknowledging this as a potential limitation later discussed.

Clinical outcomes

Outcomes were evaluated based on patient-structured questionnaires with telephone follow-up consultations. Follow-up calls were conducted by trained research nurses 12 months post-discharge. Structured questionnaires were used to gather information on vital status, rehospitalizations for any reason (number, dates, and primary reasons), and changes in medication. The primary outcomes were all-cause mortality, rehospitalization, and a composite of rehospitalization and all-cause mortality.

Statistical analysis

We described continuous variables as mean and standard deviation (SD) and tested for normal distribution using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Otherwise, the median and interquartile range were used. Analysis of variance with a post hoc test was used to compare continuous variables that satisfied normal distribution, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare continuous variables that did not satisfy normal distribution. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The multivariable models were adjusted for covariates (MAGGIC demographic burden, AFib/AFl [atrial fibrillation/flutter], NT-proBNP level, and presence of anemia). Covariates for the multivariable models (MAGGIC demographic burden, AFib/AFl, NT-proBNP level, and presence of anemia) were selected due to their established clinical relevance as independent predictors of HF outcomes. These specific factors were included to account for confounding variables not fully encompassed within the MAGGIC score’s cardiac and noncardiac domains, such as the acute phase marker NT-proBNP and specific arrhythmias, thereby enhancing the precision of our risk estimations. To test the proportional hazards assumption for the Cox regression model, we used graphical methods (log-minus-log plots). Furthermore, the 1-year mortality rates of the groups and subgroups were evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using log-rank tests. All data processing and statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS statistical package, and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A review of primary care records revealed that the preliminary diagnosis of HF was established primarily based on the history, complaints, and physical examination. Only 47 patients (8.4% of the HF+ group) had records indicating reduced or preserved ejection fraction. Among patients without a final hospital diagnosis of HF (HF-), 41.9% (n = 135) had an overdiagnosis of HF in their primary care records (HFPC+/HF- subgroup). Conversely, 18.7% (n = 128) of patients with a final hospital diagnosis of HF (HF+) were underdiagnosed in primary care (HFPC-/HF+ subgroup).

Overall 1-year outcomes

The 1-year loss-to-follow-up rate among the participants was 8.2% (n = 83). Patients lost to follow-up had similar baseline characteristics compared to the study group, leading us to assume that this proportion did not cause significant bias. The primary outcome, a composite of rehospitalization and all-cause mortality during the 1-year follow-up, occurred in 48.6% (334/686) of patients with HF and 13.7% (44/322) of patients without HF. Both 1-year mortality and the risk of rehospitalization were significantly (p < 0.05) higher in the HF+ group than in the HF- group (Fig. 1A–C).

Prognostic impact of misdiagnosis on 1-year outcomes

To further investigate the impact of misdiagnosis, we analyzed the 1-year outcomes within the specific misdiagnosed subgroups. Patients whose primary care records were inconsistent with the final diagnosis of HF showed worse all-cause mortality and hospitalization-free survival (Fig. 1D). In the subgroup (both HFPC-/HF + and HFPC+/HF) multivariate Cox regression analysis, the MAGGIC noncardiac burden was the most significant predictor of the composite outcome (adjusted HR: 1.217 [1.079–1.461]; p < 0.001, and adjusted HR: 1.305 [1.119–1.561]; p < 0.001, respectively) during the 1-year follow-up (Table 3).

Discussion

It is well known that the incidence of misdiagnosis of HF, including over- or under-diagnosis, varies widely. Thus, a systematic review of misdiagnosis showed that the rates of HF misdiagnosis ranged from 16.1% in the hospital setting to 15.7–68.5% when the general practitioner referred patients to a specialist setting [4]. At the same time, more than one-third of primary care patients labelled with HF may not have HF, and such overdiagnosis may result in inadequate management [5]. The misdiagnosis rate obtained in our study was close to these values.

The most important finding of our study was worse survival in subgroups of patients who had a difference between the diagnosis in the primary care medical records and the final hospital diagnosis according to current HF guidelines. Several authors highlight that HF misdiagnosis leads to inadequate patient management. For instance, underdiagnosis delays HF diagnosis placing patients at risk of harm from prolonged congestion [11, 12]. This delayed diagnosis can have significant prognostic implications, as demonstrated in studies evaluating predictors of improvement in left ventricular ejection fraction. In such studies, de novo heart failure — and therefore a shorter disease duration — has been associated with a greater likelihood of improvement in ejection fraction, as well as better outcomes [13]. In the case of overdiagnosis, patients are at risk of harm from incorrect and unnecessary therapy [4, 5].

Despite receiving comprehensive diagnostic work-up and appropriate management following initial misdiagnosis, patients in the HFPC-/HF + and HFPC+/HF- subgroups exhibited worse survival outcomes. This persistent prognostic disadvantage, even after appropriate hospital diagnosis and management, suggests that initial misdiagnosis may lead to irreversible physiological changes or delayed interventions that cannot be fully compensated for later, potentially contributing to outcomes less favorable than those consistently diagnosed correctly. For instance, prolonged exposure to congestion in underdiagnosed patients (HFPC-/HF+) might have contributed to subclinical organ damage, while overdiagnosed patients (HFPC+/HF-) might have experienced delays in investigating and managing the true underlying cause of their symptoms, leading to higher noncardiac burden-related mortality. Our analysis revealed that the noncardiac burden emerged as the most significant predictor of the composite outcome within these subgroups, suggesting that this underlying comorbidity significantly attenuates the potential benefits of timely HF management in these patients.

Indeed, studies have reported that common causes of HF misdiagnosis include several comorbidities, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, obesity, deconditioning, and old age [4, 14, 15]. This is particularly pronounced in patients with diabetes, in whom diagnosis of HF can be challenging with subtle symptoms and signs in the case of indolent HF in the early phases of the syndrome [4, 16]. The prominence of the noncardiac burden as a predictor in misdiagnosed subgroups underscores the complexity of distinguishing HF from other conditions, particularly in T2DM patients who often present with multiple comorbidities. These noncardiac conditions, such as chronic kidney disease, anemia, or severe respiratory disease, may mimic HF symptoms, delay accurate diagnosis, and independently contribute to adverse outcomes, potentially diminishing the benefits of even optimized HF-specific therapy.

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the single-center design from a university hospital setting may limit the generalizability of our results to other healthcare contexts, such as smaller community hospitals or primary care clinics, where patient demographics and diagnostic resources might differ significantly. Second, the mean ejection fraction (45.6%) in our cohort suggests that the MAGGIC cardiac burden might have exerted a more pronounced prognostic influence had the study population included a higher proportion of patients with severe ventricular dysfunction. Third, despite adjustments for baseline characteristics, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Furthermore, the potential for partial overlap and thus double adjustment for certain variables (e.g., NT-proBNP, anemia, AFib/AFl) with the MAGGIC domain scores should be acknowledged. Fourth, while the MAGGIC risk score is externally validated for established HF, its application to the HF- group to quantify ‘noncardiac burden’ was done without prior validation in this specific context. However, we are confident that the non-cardiac burden in such patients can be used for statistical comparison with those who have HF.

Our findings underscore several critical avenues for future research. Prospective studies are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of interventions aimed at improving diagnostic accuracy for HF in primary care settings, specifically within T2DM populations. This could involve exploring the utility of enhanced biomarker screening protocols, point-of-care diagnostics, or integrated care pathways that facilitate closer collaboration between primary care providers and cardiology specialists. Furthermore, detailed mechanistic studies are warranted to elucidate how specific noncardiac comorbidities contribute to adverse outcomes in misdiagnosed HF patients, particularly regarding their interaction with delayed or inappropriate HF management. Understanding these pathophysiological links will be crucial for developing more precise, personalized therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

Under- and overdiagnosis of HF is common in everyday clinical practice, with rates of 18.7% for underdiagnosis and 41.9% for overdiagnosis observed in our T2DM cohort. In patients with T2DM, HF is associated with more frequent rehospitalizations and lower survival rates. Misdiagnosis of HF may lead to inadequate patient management; however, the most important predictor of outcomes in such patients is the burden of noncardiac diseases. This longitudinal study highlights that the misdiagnosis of heart failure, both under- and overdiagnosis, is common in T2DM patients and is linked to less favorable long-term outcomes, even among those receiving appropriate hospital-based care. Crucially, the burden of noncardiac diseases was the primary determinant of prognosis in these misdiagnosed subgroups. Our findings underscore the importance of a holistic approach to the diagnosis and management of this complex patient population, given that the noncardiac disease burden appears to be a major determinant of prognosis.
