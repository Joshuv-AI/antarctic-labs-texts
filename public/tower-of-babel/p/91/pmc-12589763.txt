A combined in-silico and in-vitro evaluation of cyanoacrylate-based dental materials as an adhesive for applications in prosthodontics: molecular docking and cytocompatibility analysis
Authors: Divyansh Sinha, Subhabrata Maiti, Suresh Venugopalan, Sathan Raj Natarajan, Artak Heboyan, Selvaraj Jayaraman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study combined in-silico molecular docking with in-vitro cytocompatibility testing to evaluate the safety of cyanoacrylate adhesives. Molecular docking was performed for four cyanoacrylate monomers (ethyl, n-butyl, isoamyl, and 2-octyl) against key proteins associated with inflammation (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, CRP), bone metabolism (BMP4), and tissue repair (NOTCH2, fibronectin). Docking scores and binding residues were analyzed using PyRx and BIOVIA Discovery Studio. Degradation products (formaldehyde, ethyl-2-cyanoacetate) were also evaluated. In-vitro cytocompatibility of isoamyl 2-cyanoacrylate was assessed on 3T3-L1 fibroblasts using MTT and LDH assays at concentrations of 25–200 μg/mL over 24 and 48 h. Docking scores ranged from –4 to –6 kcal/mol, suggesting weak to moderate binding and low likelihood of interference with protein function. Longer-chain cyanoacrylates showed weaker binding compared to ethyl cyanoacrylate, indicating more favorable stability and lower toxicity. Degradation products demonstrated reduced binding, though ethyl-2-cyanoacetate showed comparatively higher affinities than formaldehyde. In-vitro assays revealed cell viabilities above the ISO 10993–5 threshold at clinically relevant concentrations, with mild cytotoxicity only at higher concentrations and longer exposures. The combined findings provide a molecular and cellular basis for their clinical use, suggesting that longer-chain cyanoacrylates are likely safer adhesives for denture applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44445-025-00085-0.Keywords: Biocompatible materials, Complete denture, Cyanoacrylates, Molecular docking, Cytotoxicity

Introduction

Complete dentures (CDs) are among the most often used prosthodontic rehabilitation modalities for fully edentulous individuals.(Goodacre 2024; Goodacre and Goodacre 2022; Arora et al. 2024) Nonetheless, concerns with inadequate retention, masticatory deficits, and discontent with stability are prevalent with complete dentures, as is the detachment of artificial tooth from underlying base.(Prpić et al. 2020; Aydin et al. 2023) Estimates indicate that between 22 to 30% of denture repairs, result from the debonding of tooth from the underlying denture base, that occur especially in the anterior region. There is a greater incidence that is seen in implant-supported overdentures owing to elevated occlusal stresses.(Aydin et al. 2023) The physicochemical interaction formed between the denture base and teeth establish a robust connection. Denture base and standard prosthetic teeth vary structurally and are manufactured independently. Isolated manufacturing procedures are regarded as a primary cause contributing to tooth failure.(Prpić et al. 2020; Arora et al. 2024) Findings linked to the bonding strength between denture base and denture teeth differ.(Andrade de Freitas et al. 2018) Technological innovations have guaranteed digital technologies for denture base manufacture printing.(Aydin et al. 2023) Digital workflows offer the construction of a denture base as a single unit, enabling the attachment of premanufactured teeth or CAD/CAM teeth using appropriate adhesives or by bonding via cold- or heat-polymerization (traditional method). Nonetheless, 3D printing has several benefits over milling, such as reduced material waste, the capacity to produce multiple dentures concurrently, and the capability to create intricate patterns that are unfeasible to mill. (Goodacre and Goodacre 2022) The majority of vendors advocate usage of glue as a superior choice, however there is no approved bonding process. (Srinivasan et al. 2021) The literature results on the shear bond strength of 3D printed denture base and 3D printed prosthetic teeth, coupled with their bonding technique are sparse.

The field of medical grade adhesives possesses an extensive history, is quite wide-ranging, and involves a range of compounds that may be used for perform a variety of medical procedures that include surgery. (Üstün et al. 2020) Over the full spectrum of surgical adhesives, cyanoacrylates offer unique qualities that distinguish them apart from others and have been utilized for topical tissue healing and closure consequently replacing sutures due of their mechanical strength and quick cure rates. (Aravind et al. 2024) Also, they have the added benefits of having a simple curing mechanism with good handling characteristics, high-tensile strength with excellent adhesion to a variety of substrates. However, they have relatively higher material costs, exothermic reaction, potentially toxic byproducts and solubility in polar solvents are the disadvantages of these. (Petrie 2014).

To be used as an adhesive for bonding 3D printed denture base and tooth, one must have to be absolutely certain about the biocompatibility and toxicity of cyanoacrylates compounds. Though the toxic impact of cyanoacrylates is relatively low, certain of its components or breakdown products may represent a danger to oral tissues. (Sankari and Nambi 2020) Cyanoacrylate polymers generate formaldehyde and cyanoacetate as by-products of hydrolytic reaction, that are hazardous to adjacent tissues. Substantial inflammation has resulted in unsatisfactory outcomes when short-chain cyanoacrylates have been utilized for vascular anastomoses or nerve adaptations. (Viana et al. 2024) While formaldehyde generation may be minimized using longer-chain derivatives, (Sohn et al. 2016; Üstün et al. 2020) tensile strength is often inferior. (Singer et al. 2019).

Biocompatibility is a crucial feature for any material for the use of any living organism by determining the organism’s responses to the material. It may be described as the capacity of a biomaterial to induce an acceptable host response when administered with a conventional technique. (Bapat et al. 2021; Shahi et al. 2019) To be categorized as biocompatible, biomaterials must be non-toxic, cause no physiologically unwanted damaging effects, like cytotoxicity, oxidative stress production, initiation of a hypersensitivity or inflammatory reaction, and rejection of the material due to immunological factors. (Bapat et al. 2021; Shahi et al. 2019; Arab-Nozari et al. 2023) Biocompatible materials must also not disrupt the healing mechanism. (Arab-Nozari et al. 2023) In the realm of dentistry, a wide range of products are being utilized which includes restorative materials like amalgam, cements, composites, endodontic materials, intracanal medicines, prosthodontic materials, and different implant materials. (Srinivasan et al. 2021) The growing pace in the discovery of new materials for dental uses has led to a heightened realization of the biological hazards and constraints of such biomaterials. Biological and immunological complications linked to dental materials are few and the observed adverse reactions are not severe. However, this fully relies on the nature of the products utilized and the procedure employed by personnel. In certain rare occasions serious responses have been documented. (Shahi et al. 2019).

Conventional assessment techniques for the biocompatibility and cytotoxicity of materials used in dentistry are inadequate in delivering molecular-level insights into the relationships among evaluated biomaterials and the human body. (Hosseinpour et al. 2022) The present study used contemporary in-silico analytical methods, such as molecular docking, to assess the biocompatibility and cytotoxicity of cyanoacrylate-based materials. The advancement of diverse in-silico methodologies enables a comprehensive and systematic comprehension of the molecular-level interactions between these dental materials with the cells in human body, including potential binding sites, affinities, and periodic structural changes. (Saini et al. 2024; Cheriyan et al. 2024).

Choosing the group of receptors included a thorough evaluation of their functions in the oral environment, emphasizing bone metabolism and tissue contact resulting from the use of cyanoacrylate-based products for applications in dentistry. Receptors involved in inflammatory responses were also examined, taking into account the possible immunological reactions elicited by dental materials. To reflect the biological challenges associated with denture adhesives, receptors were selected across three functional domains. IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP were chosen as they are central mediators of oral inflammation and denture stomatitis. BMP4 was included for its established role in bone metabolism and craniofacial development, which are critical in implant-supported prosthodontics. NOTCH2 and fibronectin were evaluated because of their involvement in tissue differentiation, wound healing, and extracellular matrix organization, processes essential for the integration of dental biomaterials. (Dalton and Lemmon 2021; Manokawinchoke et al. 2020) This panel of receptors thus represents the key biological pathways likely to interact with cyanoacrylate-based adhesives.

Existing research has investigated the biocompatibility of materials used in dentistry. (Hosseinpour et al. 2022; Wiertelak-Makała et al. 2023; Saini et al. 2024; Rao et al. 2024) But the investigations had drawbacks, like a deficiency of knowledge of molecular interactions between the studied dental materials and physiological structures. There have been investigations that have assessed the biocompatibility and toxicity of cyanoacrylate adhesives. (Sohn et al. 2016; Lee et al. 2015) However, no prior research have investigated it utilizing in-silico molecular docking approaches. Eventually, this would enable more accurate and educated choices about what materials to use for employing cyanoacrylates as an adhesive for gluing 3D printed denture base and teeth.

This study aims to evaluate the affinity for binding and possible toxicity hazards linked with various cyanoacrylate compounds in interactions with receptors involved in inflammation, metabolism of bones and formation of tissues.

Materials and methods

Molecular docking

The molecular docking and cell culture analysis were performed at Centre of Molecular Medicine and Diagnostics (COMManD), Department of Biochemistry, Saveetha Dental College, Chennai, India. A systematic approach was employed starting with the selection of the receptors to be analysed. First, a particular category of receptors known to be essential for biocompatibility of dental materials as molecular docking sites was identified. Following that, molecular docking experiments were undertaken utilizing various cyanoacrylate molecules to understand the molecular relationships and dynamic behaviors arising from the interactions.

Protein preparation

The justification for utilizing these approaches was addressed, highlighting the need of precisely modelling ligand structures for later molecular docking investigations. The 3D molecular structures of cyanoacrylate compounds were created to replicate the atomic arrangement in three-dimensional space, demonstrating a realistic portrayal of receptor-ligand relationships. The molecular binding of the selected cyanoacrylate molecules with the selected target proteins were evaluated with PyRx software (version 0.8). (Dewani et al. 2024) Protein Data Bank (PDB) was used to derive the structures of the selected target proteins. A grid box measuring 90 Å × 90 Å × 90 Å, with a spacing of 0.45 Å, was used to aid in precise protein-compound communication indication during the docking procedure. Lamarckian genetic algorithm (LGA) was used to obtain binding energy scores, with 100 genetic algorithm runs performed for making sure the complete analysis of the three-dimensional space.

Ligand selection

To identify and characterise high-level communications between cyanoacrylate molecules and inflammation, bone and tissue metabolism regulating target proteins, post-docking analyses were performed. Moreover, binding energy scores of the compounds with target proteins were carefully investigated to report the type of binding. Degradation products of formaldehyde and ethyl-2-cyanoacetate were also docked to demonstrate the molecular interactions between the molecules and proteins.

Software visualization

Resultant three dimensional molecular structures of the compound-protein complexes from docking simulations were visualised and analysed using BIOVIA Discovery Studio software (version 16), providing a comprehensive evaluation of spatial configurations and molecular binding analysis. (Ahmad et al. 2022) This extensive strategy helps elucidate the possible mechanisms by which the cyanoacrylate compounds influence inflammatory, and metabolic pathways, highlighting its therapeutic significance through interactions with specific protein targets. The binding energy of the cyanoacrylate molecules to the targets was evaluated and analysed along with the amino acid residues.

In vitro analysis

Cell culture

The mouse fibroblast cell-line, 3T3-L1 was procured from the National Centre for Cell Sciences (Pune, India). Passaging and maintenance of the cells were performed within a CO2 incubator, provided with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin and streptomycin combination antibiotics, and Dulbecco Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Himedia Labs, Chennai, India).

Cell viability assay

The current study analyzed the cytocompatibility of isoamyl 2-cyanoacrylate, also known as isoamyl cyanoacrylate, were tested against fibroblast cell line, 3T3-L1 using the MTT and LDH colorimetric assay, following the standardized protocol of ISO 10993–5. Cells were seeded into a 96-well plate with a density of 5 X 104 cells in each well and incubated overnight for cell attachment. For each concentration, three samples were used for carrying out the assessment. Sterilized isoamyl 2-cyanoacrylate in an increased concentration (25–200 μg/mL) for 24 and 48 h of treatment were then diluted in a 1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)-DMEM media mixture were incubated in a CO2 incubator. After treatment, the supernatant fluid was taken away and Phosphate Buffered Saline was used to wash the wells. MTT and LDH solutions were added to each well and incubated for 1 h. DMSO was kept as negative control for both MTT and LDH experiments (0 µg/ml isoamyl cyanoacrylate). The formed formazan crystals were solubilized with DMSO, and the absorbance was evaluated at 495 nm.

Statistical analysis

Cell viability (MTT and LDH) data were analyzed using SPSS (version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Shapiro–Wilk test of normality reported the data to be normally distributed; hence, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed for both MTT and LDH assay data individually, with concentration and time as fixed factors to assess their independent and interaction effects on cell viability, followed by Tukey’s post hoc pairwise comparison analysis. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study offered a detailed investigation of the biocompatibility of cyanoacrylates, applying a variety of advanced computational approaches. By combining in-silico docking with in-vitro cytocompatibility testing, the results look at the safety and potential clinical applicability of cyanoacrylates in prosthodontics. Through molecular docking, we got significant observations of the behaviour and safety profiles of distinct cyanoacrylate molecules coupled with a knowledge of how a material acts at the macromolecular scale with biological molecules relevant to inflammation, bone metabolism, and tissue repair. The procedure is justified by highlighting the necessity of assessing numerous proteins, involving bone metabolism, tissue development, and inflammatory reactions. Interleukins are primary markers of tissue inflammation. IL-1β, also called human leukocyte pyrogen due to its fever inducing property, is a known pro-inflammatory agonist. IL-1β, TNF-α are used to simulate the formation of IL-6, which in turn cause release of fibrinogen and C-reactive protein (CRP), commonly known pro-inflammatory mediators. (Tanaka et al. 2014) Higher values of IL-6 have been seen in people suffering from denture stomatitis. (Pesee and Arpornsuwan 2015) TNF-α is a versatile cytokine molecule that performs a vital role in immunological and host defence responses to infection. It increases angiogenesis, impacts tissue remodelling, and controls cell division and proliferation. (Al-Rashed et al. 2019) C-reactive protein is an inflammatory protein, usually seen in acute stages of inflammation. Its majorly aids in the activation of the complement pathway resulting in the opsonization of infectious agents and by causing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines. (Baumeister et al. 2016) BMP4 was chosen since it acts like a regulator for osteoblast differentiation and chondrocyte proliferation that will in turn be responsible for bone formation and regeneration, both of which are critical in maintaining the underlying residual alveolar ridge supporting the retention and stability of complete dentures over a long period of time. It has also been evaluated in odontogenesis and craniofacial development that takes place in embryonic stages. (Ye et al. 2022) Another study by Son et. al. observed that BMP4 has significantly higher bone formation in alveolar bone as compared to long bone, which can be useful in complete dentures as well as in implant-supported rehabilitations of the completely edentulous arches. (Son et al. 2020) Likewise, NOTCH2, is a receptor essential for determination of lineage and tissue development. It enhances osteoclastogenesis by downregulating osteoprotegerin and stimulating RANKL pre-stimulated osteoclast precursors via the RANK-RANKL pathway. (Nikolic et al. 2019) Fibronectin plays multiple key roles, one of which is being a key component of wound healing, especially in the formation of the extracellular matrix (ECM) and reepithelialization. (Saini et al. 2024) A study evaluated the distribution patterns of fibronectin in denture stomatitis and reported a strong linear expression of fibronectin in hyperplastic denture stomatitis as compared to a more disorganised structure in atrophic denture stomatitis. Fibronectin stained darker in both these conditions as compared to a healthy palatal mucosa, suggestive of signs of earlier signs of denture stomatitis. (Bars and Soueidan 2012) Since successful denture bonding depends not only on mechanical adhesion but also on a biologically compatible interface with oral tissues, evaluating cyanoacrylate interactions with NOTCH2 and fibronectin provide insight into how these adhesives may influence wound healing and mucosal compatibility around the prosthesis.

The findings from the molecular docking provided information on the chemical stability and molecular affinities of cyanoacrylate molecules with important biological targets and emphasize the prospective importance of in-silico methodologies for directing the designing and development of prosthetic materials to increase biocompatibility. In molecular docking, a more negative binding energy indicates stronger interaction between a ligand and its target protein. Strong binding to inflammatory markers like TNF-α, IL-1β or CRP could suggest a higher potential to form strong or stable interactions with the target protein, leading to triggering of unwanted immune responses. Conversely, weak or moderate binding energies reflect a low likelihood of such activation. In this study, the docking scores of cyanoacrylates ranged between –4 and –6 kcal/mol, corresponding to weak to moderate affinities. This indicates that cyanoacrylates are unlikely to form stable complexes with inflammatory or tissue-related proteins, causing a low risk to provoke inflammatory or metabolic disturbances, thereby, supporting their biocompatibility. (Saini et al. 2024) Hence, these molecules will not act as pro-inflammatory compounds nor alter the bone or tissue metabolism significantly. Upon comparison, we can see that the longer chained cyanoacrylate molecules like isoamyl and 2-octyl cyanoacrylates had a lesser negative binding energy as compared to the short-chained ethyl 2-cyanoacrylate. This could be ascribed to the short-chained cyanoacrylate molecules possessing inflammatory or carcinogenic potential due to their rapid degradation, that could be a factor in the oral environment. This accelerated degradation contributes to greater local tissue irritation and cytotoxicity, consistent with clinical reports of inflammation and mucosal discomfort. In contrast, longer-chain cyanoacrylates (n-butyl, isoamyl, and 2-octyl) degrade more slowly, resulting in a gradual release of byproducts at concentrations less likely to cause irritation and support their suitability for intraoral application. This is in accordance to previous observations that the rate of breakdown of cyanoacrylate compounds is inversely related to the length of their aliphatic (-COOR) side chains. (Hida et al. 2021) Thus, n-butyl cyanoacrylate and 2-octyl- cyanoacrylate having lengthier aliphatic side chain lengths have demonstrated safety profiles adequate for human topical use cleared by the FDA. (Hida et al. 2021; Athavale et al. 2023) On the contrary, ethyl 2-cyanoacrylate exhibited stronger binding affinities, and with its known degradation products, could explain its tissue toxicity, due to which it is only used for industrial grade applications. (Sohn et al. 2016; Hida et al. 2021) The molecular docking of degradation products adds further insight in this study. Compared with the parent compound ethyl-2-cyanoacrylate (–4 to –6 kcal/mol), formaldehyde exhibited much weaker binding (–1.6 to –2.2 kcal/mol), indicating that its irritant effects are less likely to result from stable protein interactions. Ethyl-2-cyanoacetate showed moderately stronger binding (–3.5 to –4.5 kcal/mol) than formaldehyde, approaching the affinities of the parent molecules, which could be attributed to its bigger molecular size, that could lead to it possessing greater number of binding sites than the latter, suggesting that ethyl 2-cyanoacetate poses a higher risk of causing inflammatory and metabolic disturbances.

The in-vitro cytocompatibility assays supported computational predictions. There is literature evidence supporting the biocompatibility of n-butyl and 2-octyl cyanoacrylate, however, isoamyl 2-cyanoacrylate was chosen for performing cell viability assays as there is scarce evidence in this domain. (Hida et al. 2021; Athavale et al. 2023) The 3T3-L1 mouse fibroblast cell line was chosen for MTT and LDH assays due to its sensitivity to oxidative stress, making it a good model for detecting sub-lethal inflammatory responses to chemicals, providing insight into the potential inflammatory interactions of isoamyl 2-cyanoacrylate. Both MTT and LDH assays demonstrated clear dose- and time-dependent effects, with higher concentrations exposed over longer time periods causing greater cytotoxicity. The assays showed that isoamyl 2-cyanoacrylate was non-toxic below 175 µg/mL according to the ISO 10993–5 standardization of 70% as clinically acceptable cell viability, which supported the computational predictions. At higher concentrations and over longer durations, the material appears to shift into a mildly cytotoxic range, which may be attributed to degradative byproducts such as formaldehyde and cyanoacetate which showed higher binding affinities (e.g., ethyl-2-cyanoacetate with CRP: −4.5 kcal/mol). These byproducts are known contributors to the cytotoxicity of cyanoacrylates, but the longer alkyl side chain in isoamyl derivatives appears to moderate their release rate compared to shorter-chain molecules. Taken together, the weak protein binding observed computationally, along with the acceptable cell viability in fibroblasts, strengthens the conclusion that longer-chain cyanoacrylates are comparatively safer for denture bonding applications. These findings support the need for precise formulation of adhesives or biomaterials to balance efficacy with biocompatibility, ensuring safety in dental applications and reducing potential adverse effects on surrounding tissues. In clinical application, the quantity of adhesive required for bonding denture bases and teeth is minimal (less than 0.5ml) and confined to localized interfaces, substantially reducing the risk of systemic exposure. Moreover, polymerized isoamyl cyanoacrylate undergoes rapid surface polymerization, which further limits residual monomer diffusion into surrounding tissues. (Sankari and Nambi 2020) These aspects suggest that, at clinically relevant doses, isoamyl cyanoacrylate is unlikely to induce cytotoxic responses, especially when applied under controlled conditions.

Recognizing clinical consequences of the results was vital for their use in clinical prosthodontic routine. In a recent shear bond strength study conducted according to ISO-TS 19736 standards, isoamyl cyanoacrylate exhibited the strongest bonding between 3D-printed denture base and teeth. N-butyl cyanoacrylate followed, while ethyl cyanoacrylate reported the least sheasr bond strength values. The failure modes further supported these results, with, isoamyl and n-butyl groups showed mixed or cohesive failures, indicative of stronger bonding, whereas ethyl cyanoacrylate and control groups demonstrating predominantly weaker adhesive failures. (Sinha et al. 2025) These mechanical observations complement the present molecular docking and cytocompatibility results, reinforcing that longer-chain cyanoacrylates not only possess lower toxicity potential but also provide superior adhesive performance in denture applications. Due to its biocompatibility and mechanical properties, long-chain cyanoacrylates have been used in various maxillofacial, cleft and periodontal surgeries, including guided bone regeneration. They have shown to be an excellent alternative to suturing in rat and human models. (Dipika and Nambi 2021; Borie et al. 2019) By establishing the molecular docking results and in-vitro biocompatibility assays of cyanoacrylate-based adhesives, it can be said that longer-chain cyanoacrylate compounds are less likely to provoke significant inflammatory responses or cytotoxicity as compared to their shorter-chain counterparts. For dental practitioners and laboratory personnel, it could translate into better informed judgments about selection and utilisation of cyanoacrylate-based materials in more reliable bonding of denture bases and teeth, fewer complications such as denture stomatitis, and improved long-term prosthesis performance. (Wiertelak-Makała et al. 2023) Importantly, the molecular insights from docking complement traditional in-vitro biocompatibility tests, providing an additional predictive tool for material selection. One of the limitations of this study was the lack of use of any known control ligands to validate the molecular docking analysis, however, the docking parameters were standardized according to previous literature. (Saini et al. 2024) Although the research gives useful information, more experimental validation is essential to validate the veracity of these findings, as this study design relies only on in-silico molecular docking. More experimental research can be done in the field using in-vitro, followed by in-vivo assessments of cytotoxicity, biocompatibility, long-term thermal and mechanical stability and dynamics simulation.

Conclusion

This study provides preliminary evidence on the molecular interactions and potential biocompatibility of cyanoacrylate-based adhesives. In-silico docking demonstrated weak binding affinities with proteins involved in inflammation, bone metabolism, and tissue repair, while in-vitro assays with isoamyl 2-cyanoacrylate showed acceptable cytocompatibility at clinically relevant concentrations. These results suggest that longer-chain cyanoacrylates may offer favourable safety profiles for dental applications. However, given the inherent limitations of docking and short-term cell viability studies, further in-depth in-vitro and in-vivo investigations are required before definitive clinical recommendations in guiding material selection can be made.

Clinical significance

This study emphasizes the molecular interactions and biocompatibility of cyanoacrylate adhesives in dental applications, especially for bonding 3D-printed denture bases and teeth. The results indicate that longer-chain cyanoacrylates, including n-butyl, isoamyl and 2-octyl cyanoacrylate, possess reduced pro-inflammatory potential and enhanced safety, reinforcing their suitability as dental adhesives for 3D-printed prostheses.
