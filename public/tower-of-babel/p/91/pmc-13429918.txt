Potential of biosynthesized nanoparticles as disinfectants against microbial contamination of chicken eggshells
Authors: Mária Vargová, Ľudmila Balážová, Ľudmila Tkáčiková, Peter Váczi, František Zigo, Yaroslav Shpotyuk, Kamil Szmuc, Grzegorz Gruze, Ladislav Takáč, Zdenka Bedlovičová
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiosynthesized silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) exhibit antibacterial activity and can be applied by spraying for the disinfection of the external eggshell surface. This study evaluated the effects spraying of Ag NPs synthesized using Stachys recta L. extract on eggs from Plymouth Rock chickens (Gallus gallus domesticus). Ninety-six fresh eggs were randomly divided into four treatment groups: Ag NPs, AgNO₃, Stachys recta extract, and Virocid, a broad-spectrum disinfectant based on glutaraldehyde and quaternary ammonium compounds. Microbial load was assessed on the eggshell surface before (0 minutes) and after disinfection (1, 5, and 15 minutes). By comparing microbial loads before (0 min) and after disinfection (1, 5, and 15 min), a significant effect of treatment was observed (P < 0.0001). After 15 min of exposure, Virocid achieved complete microbial elimination (3.47 log₁₀ CFU/egg reduction) and was significantly more effective than all other treatments (P < 0.0001). Ag NPs reduced microbial load by 1.34 log₁₀ CFU/egg and were significantly more effective than AgNO₃ (0.39 log₁₀ CFU/egg reduction) and the plant extract (0.20 log₁₀ CFU/egg reduction) (P < 0.0001). By microbiological culture examination of eggshells, we identified 8 bacterial species, Escherichia coli, Enterobacter cloacae, Enterococcus faecalis, Enterococccus faecium, Micrococcus luteus, Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus saprophyticus, and Staphylococcus xylosus. Reducing microbial contamination on the eggshell may help decrease the risk of egg spoilage and microbial penetration. However, the effectiveness of sanitation procedures must also be considered in relation to the preservation of the eggshell cuticle and other natural protective barriers that contribute to egg quality and shelf life.Keywords: Eggshell, Microbial load, Silver nanoparticles, Stachys recta L

Introduction

Silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) represent one of the most extensively investigated nanomaterials, as evidenced by the enhancing the number of recent review articles (Baláž et al., 2017; Dos Santos et al., 2014; Rizzello and Pompa, 2014). The most interesting seems to be their multidisciplinary application, particularly their potent antibacterial properties (Franci et al., 2015; Girase et al., 2011; Ovais et al., 2016). Synthesis of Ag NPs can be achieved via different methods categorized into physical (laser ablation, evaporation-condensation, gamma and electron irradiation (Guzel, 2018)) chemical (chemical reduction, mechanochemistry (Baláž et al., 2017; Iravani et al., 2014)), and biological approaches (green synthesis (Altammar, 2023; Vijayaram et al., 2024)). Green synthesis is based on the bioreduction of silver from its ionic form into nanoparticles mediated by diverse biological entities, including, bacteria, fungi, algae, lichens (Noga et al., 2023) or animal-derived materials, such as blood (Kakakhel et al., 2021), or fur (Akintayo et al., 2020).

The development of alternative methods with minimal adverse effects on human health, animal health, and the environment remains an important scientific challenge. In this context, plant-derived bioactive compounds have attracted considerable interest as potential disease-control agents due to their biological activity and application in environmentally friendly approaches, including the green synthesis of nanoparticles (Altammar, 2023; Vijayaram et al., 2024). Although biosynthesized nanoparticles are produced using biological reducing and stabilizing agents, they should not be regarded as inherently natural compounds. Despite the advantages associated with green synthesis, the potential toxicological effects, environmental fate, and possible accumulation of nanoparticles in living organisms remain important considerations (Bundschuh et al., 2018). Therefore, comprehensive safety assessments are required prior to their large-scale application in food production systems (McClements and Xiao, 2017).

One of such agent supposed to be the medicinal plant Stachys recta L. Stachys species are also used in European folk medicine for external wound treatment, while internally they are applied for inflammatory diseases (Háznagy‐Radnai et al., 2012), reduction of abdominal, menstrual, and other types of pains, relieve cough (Hartwell et al., 1982) and the symptoms of asthma, reduction of anxiety (Camangi et al., 2003; Karioti et al., 2010) and antibacterial properties (Grujic‐Jovanovic et al., 2004; Tomou et al., 2020).

A healthy hen’s egg is generally considered sterile prior to laying; however, following oviposition, the eggshell becomes colonized by a diverse microbial community originating from the surrounding environment (Moyle et al., 2016; North and Bell, 1990). Shortly after laying, the eggshell surface may harbour approximately 300 – 500 microorganisms, although a substantial proportion of these are commensal or environmental microflora rather than pathogenic species (North and Bell, 1990). Freshly laid egg is warm and wet, the cuticle is not mature, some pores may be open, and in the presence of sufficient temperature and humidity, the number of microorganisms may increase up to 20.000 or 30.000 bacteria (North and Bell, 1990). Due to higher susceptibility, organisms can penetrate the eggshell pores with negative consequences such as poor chick quality, posthatch infection in growing birds, increased mortality, poor growth, and decreased hatchability (Bruce and Drysdale, 1994). Frequently presented microorganisms are Pseudomonas, Salmonella, (Jones et al., 2004), E. coli and coliforms (Singh et al., 2009). Lactic acid bacteria, viruses, yeasts, and moulds have also been detected in food production environments and may contribute to contamination and spoilage (Jones et al., 2011). Microbiological contaminants may be divided into two groups - pathogens (Salmonella enteritidis) and spoilage bacteria (Aeromonas, Enterobacter, Proteus, and Pseudomonas). Protection of human and animal health at every stage of the food production process represents one of the top priorities for public health and the economy (Vargová et al., 2020).

Therefore, it is obvious that the highest standards of hygiene must be practiced in hatcheries to minimize the contamination of eggs, and sanitation is essential to limit bacterial numbers and in successful hatching egg production (Coufal et al., 2003). For the devitalisation of microorganisms several sanitation methods are available. Fumigation, spray application, U.V. light, and washing with appropriate sanitizer are used in practical conditions (Adler et al., 1979; Arhienbuwa et al., 1980; Coufal et al., 2003; Kuhl, 1989; Proudfoot et al., 1985; Sacco et al., 1989). Spray application of disinfectant solution is widely used sanitation method. Among the synthetic chemical sanitizers, formalin and Virocid were most commonly tested. Disinfection of hatching eggs with formalin is currently used in practice. The use of formalin is limited due to its mutagenic and carcinogenic effects, which threaten the health of hatchery personnel (Songur et al., 2010). For these reasons, it is necessary to look for new disinfectants, which would have the same or better disinfection effect. Many researchers have begun investigating the use of biosynthesised nanoparticles as an alternative to conventional disinfectant products produced by chemical companies. When a biosynthesis process, green synthesis is utilised, biological materials are used as reducing agents and stabilising agents for the production of nanoparticles. This reduces the amount of chemicals required for nanoparticle production, and thus makes the production of nanoparticles more sustainable (Altammar, 2023;Vijayaram et al., 2024). Silver nanoparticles provide multiple antimicrobial mechanisms (Franchi, et al., 2015 and Ovais et al., 2016) which include the destruction of membranes and the interaction with microbial proteins and DNA. Silver nanoparticles produced by biological processes could very well be a promising alternative for the applications of biosynthesized silver nanoparticles in agricultural and food hygiene.

The aim of this study was the determination of the incidence of pathogens on eggshells of Plymouth Rock chickens as well as the effect of different disinfectants on the microbial load of surfaces of eggs collected from laying farm. Biosynthesized silver nanoparticles were tested as one of the potential disinfectants. Before assessing their potential use in eggshell disinfection, it is essential to verify that the biosynthesized nanoparticles are properly formed and exhibit stable physicochemical and biological characteristics. For this reason, the present study combines detailed characterization of Ag NPs with in vitro evaluation of their antimicrobial properties, followed by testing under practical conditions using naturally contaminated eggs collected from a commercial poultry farm. This sequential approach allows a more reliable interpretation of their antimicrobial performance and supports the transition from laboratory-based synthesis to application-oriented testing.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of silver and gold nanoparticles

The dried stems with leaves, and flowers of Stachys recta L. (Juvamed, Rimavská Sobota, Slovakia) were stored at room temperature and utilized for the preparation of a plant extract. For the green synthesis of nanoparticles, silver nitrate (AgNO3, 99.8%, Mikrochem Trade, Pezinok, Slovakia) and gold chloride salt (HAuCl4·3H2O, 99%, Sigma Aldrich, USA) was used for synthesis of silver, respectively gold nanoparticles.

A water extract from Stachys recta L. was prepared at a 1:20 (w/v) ratio by two methods. First was sonication (Bandelin Sonorex Digitec, Germany) for 15 minutes at room temperature and second was maceration for 2 hours at room temperature. The followed procedure in both cases was filtration through Whatman filter paper grade 2 to remove plant debris. The extract was freshly prepared prior to use. A 2.2 mM solution of silver nitrate was heated to 80°C using a Peltier block connected to a Cary 60 spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, USA). In case of preparation of gold nanoparticles 2.2 mM gold chloride salt (HAuCl4·3H2O) was used according to previous description. The Stachys recta extract was added drop by drop to the warm AgNO3 solution in a 1:9 ratio. The synthesis was monitored in situ every minute by scanning the wavelength range of 350 - 750 nm with the spectrophotometer Cary 60 spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, USA).

Phytochemical characterization of extracts and nanoparticles

Total polyphenolic content

For this determination of total polyphenol content (TPC) of aqueous plant extracts and synthesized Ag NPs and Au NPs was used Folin-Ciocalteau method (Waterhouse, 2002). Briefly, 20 μL of tested solution (extracts, Ag NPs, Au NPs, gallic acid with the concentration range of 0.5–1.5 mg.mL−1 (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, M.O., USA)), 1.6 mL of distilled water and 100 μL of Folin-Ciocalteau reagent (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, M.O., USA) were mixed and incubated 5 minutes. Afterwards, 300 μL of sodium carbonate (Mikrochem, Slovakia) was added and such prepared samples were incubated in the dark at laboratory temperature for 2 hours. The absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 765 nm by spectrophotometer Cary 60 spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, USA). The equation obtained from the gallic acid calibration line (y = 0.3007x − 0.0237; R2 = 0.9985) gave total phenolic content expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (mg GAE.mL−1). All measurements were in triplicate, average and standard deviations were calculated.

Total flavonoids content

Flavonoids in extract, Ag NPs, and Au NPs were determined spectrophotometrically at wavelength of 410 nm by modified method Do et al. (2014). Reagent containing 1 mL of sample (extracts, Ag NPs, Au NPs, 50 – 1,000 mg.mL−1 quercetin) and 50 μl of a 5% NaNO2 were incubated for 6 min at room temperature. Afterwards 300 μL of 10% AlCl3 were added to tested solution and incubated again for 5 minutes at same conditions. After incubation, 2 mL of 1M NaOH was added, filtered through Whatman 1 filter and measure absorbance at the wavelength of 410 nm Cary 60 spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, USA).

Microscopical characterization of nanoparticles

The microstructure and morphology of the nanopowders were characterized using an FEI Tecnai Osiris scanning/transmission electron microscope (S/TEM) with a field-emission gun (FEG) operating at 200 kV. Imaging was performed in conventional bright-field (B.F.) transmission electron microscopy (TEM) mode, selected-area electron diffraction (SAED), and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) modes. STEM observations were carried out using high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) and dark-field (D.F.) detectors. The chemical composition and elemental distribution were also analysed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS).

The FRAP working solution was prepared by mixing an acetate buffer with pH 3.6, 10 mM TPTZ solution (2,4,6-tripyridyl-S-triazine, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) in 40 mM HCl (Mikrochem, Slovakia), and 0.02 mol·dm⁻³ ferric chloride solution in a volumetric ratio of 3:1:1. 75 μL of sample (extract, Ag NPs, Au NPs and 0.1–2 mM ferric sulphate) was added to 2250 μL of FRAP solution, incubated for 5 min and measured at wavelength 593 nm by Cary 60 spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, USA).

Antibacterial activity in vitro

For the determination of antimicrobial activity, were used cultures of E. Coli CCM 3988 and S. aureus CCM4223 (Czech Collection of Microorganisms, Brno, Czech Republic). Following cultivation, the bacteria were diluted to a concentration of 0.5-1 on the McFarland scale in phosphate buffer. Subsequently, 0.1 mL of this suspension was inoculated into 100 mL of emulsified liquid agar, from which 20 mL was poured into 90 mm diameter Petri dishes. After the agar had solidified, wells of 5 mm diameter were cut into which 50 μl of the sample, gentamicin at a concentration of 50 μg/mL, which served as a positive control, and the extract, which was a negative control, were pipetted. Testing of antifungal activity

Reference strain

The in vitro susceptibility testing was carried out on the reference strain of Candida albicans ATCC 10231 (Czech Collection of Microorganisms, Brno, Czech Republic).

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration and quality control testing

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of tested substances (AgNO3, HAuCl4, extracts of Stachys recta, Ag NPs, Au NPs) was determined by using the broth microdilution method M27-A3 (CLSI, 2008), with some modifications. The quality control of the test was verified by the sensitivity of the reference strain Candida albicans ATCC 10231 to fluconazole. The MIC of fluconazole was 4 μg/mL, which is in line with the criteria of the M27-A3 method (<8 μg/mL – sensitive, 16 – 32 μg/mL – dose-dependent, ≥ 64 μg/mL – resistant).

Preparation of the inoculum

A pure, 24-hour-old culture of C. albicans growing on Sabouraud dextrose agar with chloramphenicol at 35°C was used to prepare the inoculum. The stock suspension of concentration 1 Mc Farland (106 CFU/mL) was prepared in PBS (phosphate-buffered saline) and diluted to 103 CFU/mL with Sabouraud-dextrose broth (Hi Media Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India) supplemented with glucose (10 mM).

Preparation of substances tested

96-well microtitre plates with a U-shaped bottom (Brand GmbH + C.O. K.G., Weithem, Germany) were used for testing. A stock solution of each substance was diluted with Sabouraud-dextrose broth enriched with glucose directly in microtitre plates by double-fold dilution ranging from 1000 mg/mL to 2 mg/mL. Columns 1 – 10 contained the concentration gradient of the tested substances, the 11th column represented the negative control (only Sabouraud-dextrose broth) while the 12th column served as a positive control (Sabouraud-dextrose broth with the inoculum). The final volume of diluted compounds in the wells was 100 μL.

Susceptibility testing

Afterward, 100 µL of inoculum (103 cells/mL) was added to each well containing a concentration gradient of tested substances, thereby halving their concentration (500 mg/mL – 1 mg/mL) and to the column serving as a positive control. The microtitre plates were incubated at a temperature of 35°C for 24 hours. After this time, the MICs were evaluated. For better visualization of the results, 0.15% solution of resazurin dye (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) of volume 15 μL was added to each well 4 hours before reading them. The susceptibility of reference strain to each substance was tested 4-times. Descriptive statistics were evaluated using Microsoft Excel.

In vivo method

Farm, Sampling and Storage

The research took place on a commercial laying farm (one male per ten females) in Eastern Slovakia, using a barn system with a flock of 150 hens. A total of 96 fresh eggs, unwashed and free from feces, were gathered from 24-week-old Plymouth Rock chickens housed in a free-range flock raised in standard environmental conditions under controlled daily lighting conditions. Egg collection occurred twice a day over two consecutive days, with eggs stored overnight at room temperature (18 ± 0.5°C) before processing at the Department of Animal Nutrition and Husbandry, UVMP in Košice. Each collection session included a random selection of 48 visibly clean eggs from the nest boxes.

Antibacterial activity on eggshell

Eggs were randomly assigned to four disinfectant groups: silver nitrate (AgNO₃), biosynthesized silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs), Stachys recta L. plant extract, and the commercial disinfectant Virocid. Biosynthesized Ag NPs and the other disinfectant solutions were applied by spraying onto the external surface of chicken eggs to ensure uniform coverage of the eggshell. After treatment, the eggs were left to dry under standard laboratory conditions. Microbial sampling was performed from the eggshell surface using sterile swabs. For each disinfectant, independent groups of six eggs were sampled before disinfection (0 min) and after disinfection following exposure times of 1, 5, and 15 min. Each egg was sampled only once and was not repeatedly swabbed at different time points. Thus, 24 eggs were analyzed for each disinfectant (6 eggs × 4 time points). In total, 96 eggs were included in the study. Only visibly clean eggs, free of visible faecal contamination, were included in the study. No mechanical cleaning was carried out before the application of the tested disinfectants. Spraying of eggs was carried out using 2.2 mM AgNO3; biosynthesized Ag NPs; plant extract from Stachys recta L. 1:20 (w/v); disinfectant Virocid at a dilution of 0.4%. Disinfectant Virocid is broad-spectrum disinfectant with a synergistic effect of 4 active substances: dodecyldimethylammonium chloride (78.0 g/L), alkyldimethylbenzylammonium chloride (170.6 g/L), glutaraldehyde (107.3 g/L) and isopropanol (146.3 g/L). The spraying was carried out thoroughly while wetting the entire eggshell surface. The solution was kept at room temperature.

Microbiological swabs (n = 96) were collected from the entire surface of each egg using sterile cotton swabs (Fisherbrand™ Swabs) moistened with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific), ensuring complete coverage of the eggshell surface. Microbiological analyses were carried out exclusively on the external surface of the eggshell. The internal content of the eggs was not subjected to microbiological examination in this study. Swabs were taken before and after disinfection by rotating the cotton swab when it was in contact with the surfaces being evaluated. The swabs were placed in a sterile container with 10 mL of PBS, and they were vortexed for 2 min to dislodge the bacteria. Swabbed samples were serially diluted in PBS. The dilutions (100 µL) were then plated using the pour plate method on the selective diagnostic medium.

The total count of bacteria (TCB) was enumerated on non-selective diagnostic medium Nutrient agar No. 2 (N.A., HiMedia, India) and for coliform bacteria (C.B.) was used selective diagnostic medium Endo agar (E.A., HiMedia, India). Detection of TCB was performed according to ISO 18593:2004. Detection of the number of C.B. was performed according to ISO 4832:2006. The results from E.A. and N.A. were obtained after 24 hours of incubation at 37°C. After incubation, the number of colony-forming units (CFU) per plate was counted manually, and the results were expressed as log10 CFU/egg. Suspected colonies of Staphylococci were isolated according to the instructions of STN ISO 6888–1:1999. From the first two consecutive dilutions, a 100 µL spread was inoculated onto the surface of Baird-Parker agar (BPA), selective diagnostic medium (Hi-Media, India). The inoculated samples were incubated at 37°C for 24 – 48 hours. Based on their typical appearance, characteristic staphylococcal colonies gathered from the Petri dishes were used for next investigation. Identification of species of the genus Staphylococcus was performed by biochemical identification which was achieved by using the STAPHYtest 24 (Erba Lachema, Brno, Czech Republic), and evaluation was according to the manufacturer’s instructions by the software TNW Pro 7.0 (Erba-Lachema, Brno, Czech Republic).

Microccocus spp. were isolated on blood agar (Columbia Blood Agar Base with 5% of defibrinated blood), cultivated at 37°C for 24 h. Identification was biochemically using the ENTEROtest, the software TNW Pro 7.0 (Erba-Lachema, Brno, Czech Republic), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Detection of bacteria count of the family Enterobacteriaceae was carried out according to ISO 21528–1:2017 using the selective diagnostic medium Violet Red Bile Agar (VRBL; HiMedia, India), incubation period was 24 hours and temperature 37°C. Strains of the Enterobacteriaceae family were biochemically recognized at species level using the ENTEROtest 24 (Erba Lachema, Brno, Czech Republic), according to the manufacturer’s instructions by the software TNW Pro 7.0 (Erba-Lachema, Brno, Czech Republic) with a probability of correct identification of the species over 90%. Species identification of bacteria was afterwards performed with the aid of MALDI-TOF M.S., according to the standard sample preparation protocol of manual Bruker Daltonics. The Bruker MALDI-TOF M.S. system measures the mass spectra of the test strain and compares with a database of mass spectra of different bacteria and computes a score value that express the similarity between the obtained spectrum and the spectrum from the database (i.e., the quality of the match. A score rate of 2.00 and above this value express species identification. For isolates with the score value < 2.00, there was no reliable species identification. For a more exact identification of isolates, they were inoculated onto the surface of blood agar (Columbia Blood Agar Base), ensured a higher cleanliness of the isolates. Subsequently, MALDI-TOF M.S. identification was repeated. Analysis of the results was ensured in an Ultraflex III device (Bruker, Billerica (M.A.) USA). The results were processed using Flex Analysis software, version 3.0 and evaluated using BioTyper software, version 1.1 (Bruker, Billerica, M.A., USA).

Statistical analysis

Microbial counts were expressed as log10CFU/egg prior to statistical analysis. Data were analysed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to evaluate the effects of the tested disinfectants on eggshell microbial load. When significant differences were detected, Tukey’s multiple comparison test was applied for pairwise comparisons among treatment groups. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software, and differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Building on the promising in vitro results, an in vivo model was designed to assess the antibacterial efficacy of the nanoparticles on eggshell surfaces collected from a laying farm, which represent an ecologically and economically relevant system. To achieve this, the prevalence and composition of pathogenic microorganisms colonizing the eggshells of Plymouth Rock chickens were analysed. Furthermore, the antimicrobial performance of the biosynthesized nanoparticles was systematically compared with commonly used commercial disinfectants, allowing a direct assessment of their potential as sustainable and efficient alternatives for microbial control in the poultry industry.

This research highlights an innovative green approach that combines principles of nanotechnology and natural product chemistry to address real-world challenges in agricultural hygiene and food safety, underscoring the significant applied value and multidisciplinary relevance of the study.

Biosynthesis of silver and gold nanoparticles

In this work, we successfully prepared silver and gold nanoparticles using the extract of Stachys recta L. through a green synthesis method. The mechanism of synthesis of silver and gold nanoparticles by plant extract is based on the reduction of silver nitrate for Ag NPs and HAuCl4 for Au NPs by plant compounds to silver or gold in the nano range. The metabolites responsible for reduction from Stachys recta are iridoid components such as aucubin, ajugoside, acetylharpagide, harpagide and harpagoside (Háznagy-Radnai et al., 2012), phenylethanoid glycosides (verbascoside, fgorsythoside, betonyoside), quinic acid derivatives (chlorogenic acid, diterpenes and flavonoids (isoscutellarein and its derivatives) (Karioti et al., 2010). Different essential oil constituents (cis-muurola-4(14),5-diene; (E)-caryophyllene) are minor constituents, however, they are not very good extracted by water as we used in our case (Ascrizzi et al., 2019; Giuliani et al., 2008; Hegnauer, 1989).

A distinct color change during biosynthesis of Ag NPs from yellow to brown was observed, further confirming the synthesis of Ag NPs. In case of Au NPs prepared with 2.2 mM or 5.5 mM HAuCl4 the color appeared purple. The yellow color is attributed to the water plant extract, while the transition to brown, respectively purple, and signifies the successful formation of Ag, respectively Au, nanoparticles. This visual and spectral evidence collectively supports the effective synthesis of Ag NPs and Au NPs using the extract of Stachys recta L. through green synthesis methods.

The synthesis was monitored in situ using U.V.-Visible spectroscopy, confirming the formation of Ag NPs and Au NPs by observing characteristic surface plasmon resonance peaks. During the synthesis of silver nanoparticles, we observed a notable increase in absorbance in the range of 400 - 450 nm (Fig. 1). The maximum absorbance was observed at a wavelength of 426 nm. Notably, the U.V.-Vis measurements indicated absorption peaks at 498 nm for Stachys spectabilis (Erenler et al., 2023) and 469 nm for Stachys annua (Dag, 2021), both of which are higher than the observed wavelength of 426 nm in our study.

Biosynthesis of Ag NPs from Stachys recta measured spectrophotometricaly every minutes.

Plasmon surface resonance also increases during the synthesis of gold nanoparticles. The main absorption peak was observed at a wavelength of 534 nm for extract prepared by 2 hours of maceration at room temperature. For Au NPs synthetized using the extract obtained after 15 min of sonication, the absorption peak weas very similar 538 nm. The purple color, as we observed during synthesis, and similar maximum of SPR (528 nm) were determined in Au NPs prepared by Stachys lavandulifolia Vahl extract (Khademi-Azandehi and Moghaddam, 2015).

Phytochemical characterization of extracts and biosynthesized nanoparticles

During the green synthesis of silver and gold nanoparticles, the reduction of metal ions (Ag⁺/Au³⁺) to their neutral atomic states is facilitated by bioactive organic compounds present in plant extracts exhibiting intrinsic redox properties. Among these, polyphenolic compounds serve as potent reducing agents and play a crucial role in the biosynthesis of metallic nanoparticles. This diverse group of phytochemicals includes phenolic acids, tannins, flavonoids, lignans, and anthocyanin. The polyphenols are characterized by the presence of hydroxyl functional groups attached to aromatic benzene rings, which enable electron transfer and drive the reduction process during nanoparticle formation. In this study, we quantified the total polyphenol and flavonoid contents in plant extracts prepared using two different extraction techniques, maceration and ultrasound-assisted extraction, to identify the more efficient method for obtaining bioactive compounds. Additionally, we performed phytochemical characterization of the biosynthesized silver and gold nanoparticles to compare the compositional changes in the phytochemical profile that occurred during the biosynthesis process.

Total phenolic compounds were determined spectrophotometrically using the gallic acid equivalence method with Folin-Ciocalteau reagent (Singleton, Orthofer, Lamuela-Raventos 1999). The results for differently prepared extracts and nanoparticles (Ag NPs, Au NPs) in different concentrations (2.2 mM and 5.5 mM) vary considerably. In the extract prepared by maceration at laboratory temperature for 2 hours and the nanoparticles synthesized from it; a decreased number of phenolic compounds at a 5.5 mM concentration of the Ag NPs (0.2593 ± 0.1236 mg GAE/mL) and in 2.2. mM Au NPs (0.2767 ± 0.0310 mg GAE/mL) was observed, that corresponds to the assumption that phenolic substances were the main reducing agent in the formation of the nanosuspension. However, in the case of 2.2 mM Ag NPs, an increase concentration of phenolic substances (0.4393 ± 0.0291 mg GAE/mL) was observed in comparison to the extract (0.3538 ± 0.0438 mg GAE/mL). In the case of the extract prepared by sonication, 0.2909 ± 0.0177 mg GAE/mL for the extract, 0.3901 ± 0.1862 mg GAE/ml for 2.2 mM Ag NPs and 0.3602 ± 0.0162 mg GAE/ml for 5.5 mM Ag NPs were determined. A similar trend was observed for Au NPs (Table 1).

A decrease in phenolic substances was observed in Au NPs compared to the extract. Similar observations have been reported previously and have been associated with the reduction of gold ions by phenolic compounds (Balážová et al., 2024). On the other hand, the results for Ag NPs were the opposite; an increase number of phenolic substances was observed, that may be due to the composition of St. John's wort, in which flavonoids, phenethanol glycosides, and quinic acid derivatives were identified (Karioti et al., 2010). All these compounds contain hydroxyl groups, which can be grouped mainly on the outer side of the nanoparticle surface and thus lead to an increase in the detected volume of phenols, while in the extract itself they were freely distributed in the organic matrix.

Flavonoids, as a subclass of phenolic compounds, were quantified spectrophotometrically using modified analytical methods with quercetin (Q.E.) as standard (Do et. al, 2014). Compared to the determination of total phenolic substances, the quantification of flavonoids yielded more definitive results. In the case of both extracts (0.0428 ± 0.0035 mg Q.E./mL for sonication and 0.0492 ± 0.0029 mg Q.E./mL for maceration) and the 2.2 mM Ag NPs (0.0335 ± 0.0013 mg Q.E./mL) and 5.5 mM Ag NPs (0.016 ± 0.0088 mg Q.E./mL) prepared from extract by sonication, respectively maceration (0.0351 ± 0.0037 mg Q.E./mL; 0.0256 ± 0.0044 mg Q.E./mL). We observed a decrease in the number of flavonoids in case of NPs compared to the extracts themselves. These results can be explained by the reduction of flavonoids compounds during the biosynthesis of NPs. Based on the measured quantities of phenolic compounds and flavonoids, it can be inferred that flavonoids were more extensively used in the biosynthesis process than other type of phenolic compounds.

In vitro activities

Antioxidant activities

The determination of antioxidant activity is among the most widely used analytical approaches in vitro, as it is rapid, straightforward to perform, and provides highly informative and valuable results. It was determined by two methods, concretely DPPH scavenging activity and FRAP – ferric reducing ability. Determination of antioxidant activity using DPPH is one of the most used methods for determining antioxidant activity, which is based on measuring the reducing ability of antioxidants against the DPPH• radical. DPPH is an organic nitrogen radical that reacts with hydrogen donors (Kedare and Singh, 2011).

The DPPH antioxidant activity of the extracts was significantly higher (84.03 ± 0.4615% maceration at laboratory temperature and 66.68 ± 0.3159% for the sonicated extract) than that of the nanoparticles (Table 1). We probably observed this result because the substances responsible for the formation of these nanoparticles (polyphenolic compounds) contribute significantly to antioxidant activity. Au NPs showed no antioxidant activity against the DPPH radical. Contradictory results can be found in the literature; some authors observed, in agreement with our results, lower antioxidant activity of nanoparticles compared to the extract itself (Ahn et al., 2019; Demirbas et al., 2016). The authors assume that the reduced antioxidant capacity of nanoparticles is due to the fact that the antioxidant activity of the extract itself was used to reduce silver ions to elemental silver. However, other authors point to the higher antioxidant activity of nanosuspensions compared to extracts (Balážová et al., 2024; Elemike et al., 2017; Khorrami et al., 2018; Vijayan et al., 2018). The increased scavenging ability against the DPPH radical may be related to the adsorption of bioactive plant metabolites on the surface of Ag NPs (Vijayan et al., 2018).

It was found by antioxidant activity using the FRAP method, that all prepared Ag NPs had a higher reducing capacity than the extract itself (2.4 ± 0.1137 mg/L for sonication or 2.07 ± 0.0114 mg/L for maceration). The best results were obtained with Ag NPs prepared using 2.2 mM AgNO3 (2.93 ± 0.4427 mg/L for sonication and 2.69 ± 0.0697 mg/L for maceration). Higher reduction activity was demonstrated with this method for Ag NPs and Prosopis farcta fruit extract (Salari et al., 2019). However, another study showed a reduced reduction capacity of both Ag NPs and Au NPs compared to the extract when producing nanoparticles using Capsicum chinense (Lomelí-Rosales et al., 2022). The authors did not provide a relevant explanation, but we assume that, as with other methods, this phenomenon is related to the substances contained in a particular plant or its extract and, therefore, to its ability to reduce the compounds in question. Therefore, we assume that the surface of the nanoparticles we prepared contains a higher proportion of substances that are capable of reducing ferric cations, such as reducing the DPPH radical, as the antioxidant capacity was reduced there.

In conclusion, the antioxidant activity of the Stachys recta extract, as well as of the biosynthesized silver and gold nanoparticles from it, was found to be insignificant, indicating their limited potential as antioxidant agents for applications in veterinary or human medicine, or for food preservation purposes. Consequently, further in vitro experiments were conducted to explore alternative biological activities of the synthesized nanoparticles. As a result, the focus shifted toward the assessment of their antimicrobial properties, specifically antibacterial and antifungal activities, to identify more promising and practically relevant applications.

Antibacterial activity

In our study, we investigated the antibacterial activity of biosynthesized Ag NPs and their precursors (silver nitrate), respectively Au NPs and their precursors HAuCl4 and Stachys recta, against representative Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. The agar diffusion method was employed to evaluate the effectiveness of these agents on the Gram-positive bacterium Staphylococcus aureus (CCM4223) and the Gram-negative bacterium E. coli (CCM3988).

Antimicrobial activity has been documented for several species within the genus Stachys, including S. recta, S. germanica, S. byzantina, S. cretica, S. scardica, S. officinalis, S. sylvatica and S. guyoniana (Ferhat et al., 2017; Lazarevic et al., 2013; Paun et al., 2018; Saeedi et al., 2008; Sarbu et al., 2013; Serbetci et al., 2010; Skaltsa et al., 2003). However, in our study, no antibacterial activity of both prepared extracts against E. coli or Staphylococcus aureus was observed. The discrepancies in results may be attributed to the extraction methods. For instance, extracts from S. officinalis, S. germanica, S. byzantina, S. sylvatica, S. palustris, and S. recta were prepared using 1 g of dried plant material in 100 mL of 70% ethanol, subjected to ultrasound extraction for 30 minutes at 60°C (Benedec et al., 2023). In contrast, our study utilized water as the extraction solvent, which is more polar and likely extracted only a limited number of essential oils that are primarily responsible for antibacterial effects. Essential oil from eight Stachys species (St. alopecuros (L.) Bentham., St. scardica (Griseb.) Hayek, St. cretica L. ssp. cretica, St. germanica L. ssp. heldreichii (Boiss.) Hayek, St. recta L., St. spinulosa L., St. euboica Rech. and St. menthifolia Vis.) have demonstrated antimicrobial activities against various bacteria (Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC 27853), E. coli (ATCC 35210), Bacillus subtilis (ATCC 10907), Bacillus cereus (clinical isolates), Micrococcus flavus (ATCC 10240), Staphylococcus epidermidis (ATCC 2228) and also fungi (Aspergillus niger (ATCC 6275), Penicillium ochrochloron (ATCC 9112), Epidermophyton floccosum (clinical isolates), Candida albicans (clinical isolates) and Trichophyton mentagrophytes (clinical isolates). The observed activity was generally stronger against bacteria than fungi (Benedec et al., 2023). In our case, however, no antifungal or antibacterial effects were detected, likely due to the use of a water extract rich in polar substances but lacking the volatile compounds typically found in non-polar essential oils.

In our study by agar diffusion methods, the water extract of Stachys recta did not exhibit any antibacterial activity against both Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (CCM4223) and Gram-negative E. coli (CCM3988). However, Ag NPs synthesized through a green method using this extract demonstrated moderate, but promising antibacterial activity. The bactericidal effect, measured as the Relative Inhibition Zone Diameter (RIZD), was notably stronger against the Gram-negative bacteria, with an inhibition rate of 64.20 ± 0.7%, compared to 18.52 ± 1.1% for the Gram-positive bacteria. These findings suggest that while the water extract itself lacks antibacterial properties, the Ag NPs produced from it can effectively inhibit bacterial growth. The differences in antibacterial activity between Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria are primarily attributed to the structural characteristics of their cell membranes. Gram-negative bacteria possess a thin peptidoglycan layer (approximately 2 – 3 nm) situated between their inner and outer membranes, whereas Gram-positive bacteria have a significantly thicker peptidoglycan layer (around 30 nm) and lack an outer membrane (Murray, 1965; Shockman, 1983). The mechanism is not fully understood however it is known that ionic silver is strongly interacts with the thiol and phosphorus groups of enzymes or DNA, which leads to their inactivation (Gupta, 1998; Hatchett, 1996; Xie, 2023). Additional effects include membrane destruction, the creation of granules from silver and sulphur, and loss of DNA's ability to replicate (Feng, 2000; Nover, 1983). Moreover, NPs compared to ionic silver have increased catalytic activity (More, 2023; Morones, 2005; Somorgaj, 2004).

On the other hand, Au NPs showed no or negligible antibacterial activity against both strains. Similar results have been reported in the literature, where gold nanoparticles showed no antibacterial activity up to a concentration of 5 mg. mL−1 (Aljabali et al., 2018).

Antifungal activity

Based on the promising antibacterial activity of biosynthesized silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) prepared using Stachys recta extract, we further evaluated their antifungal activity in vitro. The antifungal tests were carried out for both types of nanoparticles, Ag NPs and Au NPs, as well as for the Stachys recta extract and the corresponding precursors used in nanoparticle synthesis, namely AgNO₃ and HAuCl₄.

During evaluation of minimal inhibitory concentrations of plant extract (Table 2), we found that Stachys recta extracts prepared by different methods (Fig. 2, rows E – H) did not show sufficient antifungal activity against the reference strain of Candida albicans (MIC ≥ 25 mg/mL). Contradictionary, antifungal effect against candida albicans was determined using extract of Romanian Stachys species: S. officinalis, S. germanica, S. byzantina, S. sylvatica, S. palustris, and S. recta (Benedec et al., 2023). The method of extract preparation and thus the content of antifungal components differed in our case. Evidently, higher antifungal activity was observed with nanoparticle precursors AgNO3 (Fig. 2, rows A, B) at average MIC 0.14 mM and HAuCl4 (Fig. 2, rows C, D) at average MIC 0.21 ± 0.099 mM.

Microscopical characterization of silver nanoparticles

Ag NPs prepared in water by sonication for 30 min exhibited the most effective antibacterial and antifungal activity. For this reason, these nanoparticles were selected for further studies, including microscopy examination and evaluation of their effect on microbial load on chicken eggshells.

The morphological and structural properties of the synthesized silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (Fig. 5). Microscopic analysis confirmed the successful formation of discrete, nanometric-scale particles (Fig. 5 (A)). Interestingly, a detailed evaluation of the micrographs revealed a monomodal size distribution (Fig. 5 (D)). This degree of uniformity is noteworthy for biosynthesized nanoparticles, which typically exhibit polydisperse or bimodal size distributions due to the inherent complexity of biological reducing agents.

Microscopic analysis of Ag NPs prepared by 30 min sonication. (A) EDS analysis of whole particles. (B) Ag elemental distribution. and (C) corresponding spectrum. TEM images at different magnifications (D: 50 nm scale bar. E: 20 nm scale bar) and (F) selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern.

In contrast to the uniform size distribution, the sample displayed a bimodal morphology. The population is dominated by two distinct geometric forms: prismatic particles and planar triangular nanoplates. High-resolution imaging of the triangular nanoplates further revealed that they possess truncated or rounded edges rather than sharp vertices (Fig. 5(E)).

The crystalline nature of the material was confirmed by selected area electron diffraction (SAED) (Fig. 5 (F)). The observed diffraction rings were indexed to the (111), (200), (220), (311), and (222) lattice planes, which are characteristic of the face-centered cubic (fcc) metallic silver structure. Notably, no diffraction rings corresponding to silver chloride (AgCl) or other impurity phases were detected, confirming the high phase purity of the metallic Ag NPs.

EDS analysis provided further insights into the chemical composition and surface properties of the nanomaterial (Fig. 5 (B)). The spectra exhibit prominent peaks for silver, while the characteristic peak for chlorine is absent, corroborating the SAED results regarding the lack of AgCl formation. Strong signals for carbon and copper were attributed to the carbon-coated copper TEM grids used for support (Fig. 5 (C)). However, the presence of a distinct oxygen peak suggests the existence of adsorbed organic material on the nanoparticle surface. This organic layer appears to function as an effective capping agent, providing steric stabilization. Consequently, the micrographs show well-dispersed particles with negligible aggregation, indicating that surface-bound biomolecules effectively prevent the metallic cores from coalescing.

Microbial load of eggshell

Based on the previously reported in vitro results referenced in this article, silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) demonstrated significant antibacterial and antifungal activity, whereas gold nanoparticles (Au NPs) exhibited negligible effects. Consequently, only Ag NPs were selected for subsequent antibacterial assays targeting bacteria present on the eggshell surface.

The initial objective was to determine the bacterial spectrum present on the surface of the eggshells. By microbiological culture examination of eggshells and subsequent species identification using MALDI-TOF M.S., 8 bacterial species were identified, namely E. coli, Enterobacter cloacae, Enterococcus faecalis, Enterococcus faecium, Micrococcus luteus, Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus saprophyticus, and Staphylococcus xylosus (Table 3). The most common pathogens from evaluated eggshells were Staphylococcus spp. (Abdullah, 2010) noted that the highest degree of eggshell contamination was found with Gram-positive bacteria, particularly Staphylococcus spp. Results from our study showed that 3 species of Staphylococcus (S. aureus, S. saprophyticus and S. xylosus) were obtained from the eggshells. These results agree with the results previously published (Abdullah, 2010; Chaemsanit et al., 2015).

The presence of total bacterial counts and coliform bacteria is commonly used as an indicator of general hygienic conditions and surface contamination; however, they do not directly correlate with the presence of specific pathogens such as Salmonella spp. (ISO 4832:2006). Bacteria on eggshell surfaces typically originate from air, water, and soil, so the types of bacteria present vary depending on the environment of the egg production facility (Moyle et al., 2016). The removal of organic matter such as wastes, dust, faeces and soil and, ensuring adequate hygiene of the environment and the washing of the eggs decrease the microbial contamination on the eggshells, however, these processes are inadequate.

In the present study, statistical analysis indicated significant effect of disinfectants on the microbial load of eggshells (P < 0.0001). Conclusions

In this study, Ag NPs, and Au NPs were successfully biosynthesized using the extract of Stachys recta L., as confirmed by the characteristic colour change and distinct surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peaks at approximately 430 nm for Ag NPs and 530 nm for Au NPs. Although the biosynthesized nanoparticles and the plant extract exhibited limited antioxidant activity in vitro, further biological evaluations revealed significant antimicrobial potential, particularly in the case of Ag NPs. The Ag NPs demonstrated pronounced antibacterial activity against both Gram-negative (represented by E. coli) and Gram-positive (represented by Staphylococcus aureus) strains, while Au NPs and the extract alone showed negligible effects. Comparable results were obtained in antifungal assays against Candida albicans.

Beyond conventional in vitro testing, the practical applicability of Ag NPs was further validated on the surface of eggs collected from a laying farm, which were found to host diverse bacterial populations dominated by Staphylococcus spp. The effective reduction of microbial contamination on eggshell surfaces by Ag NPs underscores their potential as a sustainable and efficient disinfectant with low risk of antimicrobial resistance development.

The findings provide valuable insight into the antimicrobial potential of biosynthesized Ag NPs and support their further investigation as alternative disinfectants. However, the present study was based on samples collected from a single poultry farm and production system. In addition, long-term storage effects, biosafety aspects, and comparisons with a wider range of commercial sanitation methods were not evaluated. Another limitation is that no validated neutralizing agent was used following the defined disinfectant exposure times. Consequently, residual antimicrobial activity during sample processing cannot be completely excluded, which may have influenced the microbiological results. Therefore, the current findings should be considered preliminary, and further studies under different production conditions, together with toxicological and long-term safety assessments, are required before the practical use of biosynthesized Ag NPs in poultry production or other industrial applications can be recommended. Future investigations should also incorporate an appropriate validated neutralization step before microbiological analysis to eliminate this potential source of bias.
