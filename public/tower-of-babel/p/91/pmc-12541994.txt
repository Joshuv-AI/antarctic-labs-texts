Understanding mental health literacy, stigma, and help-seeking attitudes among university students in the Maldives: a mediation analysis
Authors: Mariyam Hudha Hussain, Nur Arfah Binti Zaini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite heightened awareness of mental health concerns among university students, considerable disparities remain in their help-seeking behaviors and use of mental health services. Previous research has highlighted the potential role of mental health literacy in shaping help-seeking attitudes and reducing stigma related to seeking mental health help, but there is a lack of empirical data from the Maldives. This study aimed to address this gap by examining the associations between mental health literacy, stigma related to seeking mental health help (self-stigma and societal stigma), and help-seeking attitudes among Maldivian university students. A quantitative cross-sectional design was employed, involving 225 university students aged 18–35 from five tertiary institutions in the Maldives. Data were collected via an online survey comprising demographic questions, the Mental Health Literacy Scale (MHLS), the Stigma Scale for Receiving Psychological Help (SSRPH), the Self-Stigma of Seeking Help Scale (SSOSH), and the Mental Help Seeking Attitudes Scale (MHSAS). Statistical analyses, including multiple linear regression and parallel mediation analysis, were conducted using SPSS. The results showed that higher mental health literacy was positively associated with more favorable help-seeking attitudes and with lower levels of self-stigma and societal stigma related to seeking help. Conversely, greater levels of self-stigma and societal stigma were associated with less favorable help-seeking attitudes. Additionally, self-stigma and societal stigma showed significant statistical mediating associations in the relationship between mental health literacy and help-seeking attitudes. These results underscore the importance of enhancing mental health literacy and addressing help-seeking stigma to support positive attitudes toward seeking help. While the cross-sectional design limits causal inference, this study provides valuable preliminary insights for developing mental health interventions and policies in the Maldives.Keywords: Mental health literacy, Stigma, Help-seeking attitudes, University students

Introduction

Mental health and psychological help seeking among university students

The university years are a time of significant emotional and intellectual challenges, often surpassing those encountered in other educational stages. Numerous studies have highlighted the prevalence of mental health issues among university students globally. For example, an international systematic review reported that 25% of university students experience depression, and 14% show symptoms related to suicide during their academic years [1]. In Asia, research in Bangladesh has indicated that university students there face higher rates of depression and anxiety compared to global averages [2]. Similarly, in Pakistan, the prevalence of depression, anxiety, and stress among university students is notably high, with rates of 75%, 88.4%, and 84.4%, respectively [3]. In the Maldives, significant levels of anxiety and depression have been observed among students, with 58.9% experiencing moderate to severe anxiety and 28.9% exhibiting moderate to severe depression or higher [4].

Despite the increasing recognition of mental health issues among university students, there are significant disparities in help-seeking behaviors and the utilization of mental health services within this group [5]. For instance, a study in Malaysia found that younger students generally hold more negative attitudes toward seeking mental health support compared to older students [6]. Male students also tend to be less inclined to seek help compared to female students [7]. Furthermore, ethnic factors play a role, as studies indicate that Asian students are less likely to seek treatment, resulting in about 80% of cases going untreated [8]. Additionally, students are less likely to seek help when experiencing severe mental health symptoms, showing a higher propensity to seek assistance for minor issues, as severe depression, anxiety, and stress are significantly associated with reduced intention to seek help [9].

Factors affecting help seeking attitudes

One major factor contributing to the gap in help-seeking among university students is the challenge of recognizing symptoms, often referred to as inadequate mental health literacy [10, 11]. Mental health literacy involves the ability to distinguish between mental health conditions and general stress, knowledge about mental disorders, perceptions of their causes, and understanding of risk factors and available professional support [12]. Recently, the concept of mental health literacy has broadened to include maintaining positive mental health, understanding disorders and treatments, reducing the stigma associated with mental illness, and increasing awareness of mental health resources for managing mental illnesses [13, 14].

Evidence from cross-sectional studies strongly indicates that mental health literacy is linked to various aspects of psychological help-seeking [15]. Hierarchical regression analysis has demonstrated that mental health literacy significantly contributes to better attitudes towards seeking help [16]. Supporting this, Kim [17] also found a positive correlation between mental health literacy and attitudes towards seeking mental health support. Students who could accurately identify mental health symptoms were more likely to have favorable views on seeking professional psychological assistance. Moreover, interventions aimed at promoting mental health literacy have shown promising results in enhancing help-seeking behavior and preventing mental disorders, as mental health literacy is connected to the goal of preventing mental illness Jorm[18–20]. Researchers have also examined aspects related to mental health literacy, such as its association with stigma [21, 22]. However, there is also evidence that mental health literacy promotion alone does not necessarily increase university students’ intention to seek help for mental health issues [23].

Another significant factor influencing help-seeking behaviors and attitudes among university students is the stigma associated with mental health. Stigma can manifest at multiple levels, but two particularly influential forms are societal stigma and self-stigma. Societal stigma refers to negative stereotypes and prejudices about mental illness that are reinforced by the broader community, often leading to discrimination, marginalization, and fear of being labeled “mentally ill” [24]. Such external pressures create barriers to seeking support, as students may anticipate judgment, labeling, or negative feedback from peers and family [25]; Schomerus & Angermeyer, 2008; Shi et al., [26]. In contrast, self-stigma, or the internalization of these negative societal beliefs, is strongly linked to reduced help-seeking. Self-stigma often manifests as feelings of shame, embarrassment, or inadequacy about needing professional psychological support [27]. Empirical research demonstrates that higher levels of self-stigma are associated with less favorable attitudes toward counseling and greater reliance on self-reliance or problem denial rather than professional help [28].

Recent studies among university populations reinforce these findings, showing that students who experience greater stigma are less likely to have favorable attitudes toward seeking professional psychological help [16]. Self-stigma, such as feelings of shame, significantly influences students’ attitudes toward treatment-seeking Kim [17]. At the same time, research indicates that higher levels of mental health literacy are associated with lower stigma and more positive attitudes toward professional support [29, 30, 31]. Awareness interventions that enhance literacy have been shown to reduce stigma and encourage more active help-seeking among students [32]; Kim [17].

Mental health literacy, mental health stigma and help seeking attitude

According to Yang et al. [33], stigma plays a mediating role between mental health literacy and attitudes toward professional psychological help-seeking. Kim [17] further supports this by showing that mental health stigma acts as a mediator in the relationship between mental health literacy and the inclination to seek help for mental health issues. The study’s findings suggest that both social and self-stigma contribute to lower levels of mental health literacy, subsequently decreasing the willingness to seek help Kim [17]. Notably, Kim [17] and Kim [34] highlight that self-stigma has a more substantial impact than social stigma as a mediating factor between mental health literacy and attitudes toward seeking help. Given the evidence suggesting that stigma can distort knowledge of mental illness and its connections with both mental health literacy and help-seeking behaviors, understanding the mediating role of stigma in this relationship is crucial.

Theoretical framework

To better understand these dynamics, the current study draws upon Rosenstock’s [35] Health Belief Model (HBM). The HBM posits that health behavior decisions are guided by individuals’ perceptions of their susceptibility to a condition, the perceived severity of the condition, perceived benefits of taking action, perceived barriers, and cues to action [36]. Within this framework, mental health literacy can be understood as contributing to perceived susceptibility, as students who are more literate are more aware of their vulnerability to mental health challenges. Conversely, stigma functions as a perceived barrier, which discourages individuals from seeking care despite recognizing its necessity. By applying the HBM, this study investigates how these constructs influence university students’ intentions to seek psychological help, with specific attention to the Maldivian context where research is scarce.

Current study

In the Maldives, there is a lack of understanding regarding the specific attitudes of university students toward seeking help for their mental health concerns, as well as their level of familiarity with mental health topics and the stigmatization of mental health. Moreover, there has been relatively little empirical research focused on mental health literacy among university students in the Maldives. Existing reports indicate that stigma is a major barrier to seeking treatment, particularly in smaller communities and islands, where patients often prefer traditional healers and are hesitant to access formal mental health services due to concerns about confidentiality and social judgment [37]. Hence, the objective of this study is to examine the association between mental health literacy, mental health stigma, and help-seeking attitudes among Maldivian university students. Building upon past research, this study proposes that mental health stigma may act as a potential intervening factor between mental health literacy and help-seeking attitudes. Specifically, the study analyzes the separate statistical mediating roles of societal stigma and self-stigma to explore which form of stigma is more strongly related to the association between mental health literacy and help-seeking attitudes among Maldivian university students.

Hence, the study hypothesizes as follows: First, mental health literacy will be positively associated with help-seeking attitudes among university students (path c’). Second, mental health literacy will be negatively associated with mental health stigma (path a1 and a2). Third, mental health stigma will be negatively associated with help-seeking attitudes (path b1 and b2). Method

Research design

A quantitative cross-sectional study design was utilized for this research project. Due to the decentralized distribution of colleges and universities across various islands in the country, the study was conducted online. This online approach was chosen as the most practical option and guaranteed participant anonymity.

Procedure

A convenience sampling approach was used to recruit university students in the Maldives, primarily due to the absence of a centralized student database and logistical constraints related to accessing the entire student population. This method is commonly applied in exploratory research, particularly in under-researched contexts such as the Maldivian higher education system. Efforts were made to increase the diversity of the sample by engaging multiple institutions and academic disciplines.

Seven tertiary education institutions were contacted via email with a formal request to participate in the study. Of these, five universities responded positively and agreed to disseminate the online survey link to their enrolled students. The survey link included an information sheet that explained the purpose of the study, participant rights, and contact details for the research team. Informed consent was obtained electronically prior to participation.

The survey comprised items related to demographic characteristics, mental health literacy, mental health-related stigma (both social and self-stigma), and help-seeking attitudes. It required approximately 10 min to complete. Data collection was carried out between March 2023 and May 2023.

Participants

Participants eligible for this study were individuals aged between 18 and 35 years. Those actively seeking any form of psychological health service were excluded. A total of 235 students completed the online questionnaires, with 10 excluded from the analysis due to not meeting the inclusion criteria. Thus, the final sample consisted of 225 students, including 40 males (17.8%) and 185 females (82.2%), with an average age of 22.28 years (SD = 3.828, range 18–35). The majority of participants were from The Maldives National University (80.9%). All participants provided informed consent for participation. Measures

Demographic data questionnaire

The sociodemographic data collected included age, gender, academic year, university enrollment, and department. Additionally, information was gathered on whether participants were actively seeking psychological help.

Mental health literacy scale (MHLS)

The Mental Health Literacy Scale (MHLS) comprises 35 items designed to evaluate individuals’ understanding of mental health, utilizing 4-point and 5-point Likert scales [38]. The scale encompasses six attributes derived from Jorm et al.‘s [12] conceptualization of Mental Health Literacy: recognition of mental health problems, knowledge and beliefs about risk factors and causes, awareness of self-help interventions, knowledge and beliefs about available professional help, attitudes supporting recognition and appropriate help-seeking, and knowledge about how to seek relevant mental health information. The total score is computed by summing responses to all items, with certain items (number 10, 12, 15, and 20–28) reverse scored. Scores range from 35 to 160, with higher scores indicating greater mental health literacy. A typical item example is, “To what extent do you think it is likely that Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT) is a therapy based on challenging negative thoughts and increasing helpful behaviors.” The MHLS has demonstrated high validity and reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.87 [38].

Stigma scale for receiving psychological help (SSRPH)

The Stigma Scale for Receiving Psychological Help (SSRPH) was developed to measure individuals’ perceptions of societal stigma associated with seeking professional assistance [28]. This scale comprises 5 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). Responses to these items are summed, with higher scores indicating stronger perceptions of public stigma. An example item is, “People generally show less favorable attitudes towards individuals who are undergoing professional psychological support.” The SSRPH has demonstrated good internal consistency, validity, and reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.72 [21].

Self-Stigma of seeking help scale (SSOSH)

The Self-Stigma of Seeking Help (SSOSH) Scale consists of 10 items designed to measure the belief that seeking help from a psychologist or other mental health professional undermines an individual’s self-esteem, self-satisfaction, self-assurance, and overall sense of personal value [39]. Respondents rate their agreement with each item using a 5-point Likert-style format. Scores are then summed, with higher scores indicating greater perceptions of self-stigma. An example item is, “I would feel worse about myself if I could not solve my own problems.” The SSOSH Scale has demonstrated high validity and reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.91 [39].

Mental help seeking attitudes scale (MHSAS)

The Mental Help Seeking Attitudes Scale (MHSAS) is a 9-item tool designed to assess respondents’ overall evaluation, whether favorable or unfavorable, of seeking help from a mental health professional if they were dealing with a mental health concern [40]. The total score is calculated by summing responses to all items and dividing by 9, with certain items (number 2, 5, 6, 8, and 9) reverse scored. A higher score indicates a more positive attitude toward seeking help. The MHSAS employs a 7-point semantic differential scale. For instance, respondents mark the circle closest to “useless” if they feel seeking help would be extremely useless, mark “0” if undecided, and mark the “1” circle closer to “useful” if they perceive seeking help as slightly useful. The MHSAS has demonstrated high validity and reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.92 [6].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 25.0; IBM). Preliminary analyses included descriptive statistics and assessments of normality, skewness, and kurtosis for all continuous variables. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated to examine bivariate relationships between variables and to assess potential multicollinearity. To test the primary hypotheses, a multiple linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate whether mental health literacy, self-stigma, and societal stigma significantly predicted help-seeking attitudes. In addition, two separate simple linear regressions were conducted to determine whether mental health literacy predicted levels of self-stigma and societal stigma. A parallel mediation analysis was conducted using Hayes’s PROCESS macro for SPSS (Model 4), employing 5,000 bootstrap samples to test indirect effects. This analysis examined whether self-stigma and societal stigma mediated the relationship between mental health literacy and help-seeking attitudes. Additional exploratory analyses were performed to assess demographic differences. An independent samples t-test was conducted to compare help-seeking attitudes between males and females. Finally, Pearson’s correlation was used to examine the relationship between age and help-seeking attitudes.

Assumptions of linearity, normality, homoscedasticity, and independence of errors were evaluated through standardized residual plots, histograms, and normal probability (P–P) plots. No serious violations were detected, though some evidence of heteroscedasticity was noted and is addressed in the limitations.

Results

The multiple regression analysis examining the relationship between mental health literacy, self-stigma and societal stigma related to seeking mental health help, and help-seeking attitudes yielded significant results (p < .000). The predictors; mental health literacy, self-stigma, and societal stigma, were collectively associated with 50% of the variance in help-seeking attitudes (R² = 0.50, F(3, 221) = 74.93, p < .001). Mental health literacy was positively associated with help-seeking attitudes (β = 0.333, p < .000), indicating a moderate effect size. Conversely, societal stigma (β = − 0.120, p < .023) and self-stigma (β = − 0.391, p < .000) were negatively associated with help-seeking attitudes, with self-stigma showing a relatively strong negative association. These results suggest that these variables are meaningfully related to help-seeking attitudes. This finding aligns with the first hypothesis that higher levels of mental health literacy are associated with more positive attitudes toward seeking help for mental health concerns, and with the third hypothesis that higher levels of self-stigma and societal stigma are associated with decreased willingness to seek help for mental health issues among university students. Discussion

The present study investigated how mental health literacy, stigma related to seeking mental health help (both self-stigma and societal stigma), and help-seeking attitudes intersect among university students in the Maldives. This study demonstrated a clear positive association between mental health literacy and help-seeking attitudes, consistent with findings from Cheng et al. [16] and Kim [17]. Higher levels of mental health literacy were positively associated with more favorable attitudes toward seeking professional psychological help, suggesting that students with increased knowledge about mental health conditions, symptoms, and treatments tend to report more willingness to seek help. The observed association between mental health literacy and help-seeking attitudes (β = 0.333) represents a moderate effect size, indicating a relationship that may be relevant in real-world contexts. This aligns with previous studies indicating that inadequate mental health literacy is associated with difficulties recognizing symptoms and seeking help [10, 11].

The present study is also consistent with the notion that higher mental health literacy is associated with lower stigma related to seeking mental health help, which in turn relates to more positive help-seeking attitudes among students. Consistent with previous research, enhanced mental health literacy has been associated with lower self-stigma and societal stigma toward seeking professional psychological help [29, 30, 19, 20]. This potential reduction in help-seeking stigma is important, as stigma has been identified as a barrier that may prevent students from accessing mental health services. However, evidence also suggests that improving mental health literacy alone may not always be linked to increased intention to seek help, highlighting the need for holistic approaches that address emotional and psychological barriers in addition to knowledge [23].

This study also found that both self-stigma and societal stigma related to seeking mental health help were significantly associated with help-seeking attitudes, corroborating prior research [41, 42]. This finding is consistent with prior studies that emphasize the strong association between internalized help-seeking stigma and help-seeking behavior Kim [17]. This suggests that personal beliefs and perceptions about mental illness—such as feelings of shame or inadequacy—may be more strongly related to deterring individuals from seeking help than external societal attitudes alone. Moreover, research by Shi et al. [26] found that students who seek psychological assistance often report facing social stigmas, such as being labeled by others, which can further discourage help-seeking behaviors. This finding aligns with our current study, suggesting that societal stigma related to seeking help may be linked to reluctance to seek professional psychological support.

In exploring the statistical mediating patterns of stigma, the present study found that both self-stigma and societal stigma related to seeking mental health help partially mediated the association between mental health literacy and help-seeking attitudes. This finding aligns with prior research, which suggests that while knowledge is related to attitudes towards help-seeking, stigma—both internalized and external—continues to pose significant barriers [34, 33, Kim [17] . Understanding these statistical mediation patterns highlights the complex interplay between cognitive awareness and emotional responses in decisions related to seeking psychological support.

Importantly, in line with Kim [17], the current study found that self-stigma related to seeking mental health help showed a stronger statistical mediating association than societal stigma in the relationship between mental health literacy and help-seeking attitudes. This indicates that the internalized belief against oneself in help-seeking, manifesting as feelings of shame or inadequacy, may be more immediately related to negative help-seeking attitudes than external societal judgments. The relatively strong negative association of self-stigma (β = 0.391) highlights its potential role as a key factor linked to help-seeking behavior. Approaches that foster self-acceptance and address these internalized beliefs could be explored as potentially useful in improving help-seeking attitudes among university students.

Comparing trends in help-seeking attitudes among university students in the Maldives, the present study reaffirmed previous literature [7, 6]. It was observed that younger students and males were less likely to exhibit positive help-seeking attitudes compared to older students and females. This aligns with findings from Ibrahim et al. [6], who reported that younger students generally hold more negative attitudes toward seeking mental health support compared to their older counterparts. Younger students might lack the maturity or awareness to recognize the importance of seeking professional help, or they may feel more stigmatized by their peers.

Similarly, Hubbard et al. [7] found that male students are generally less inclined to seek help compared to female students. This gender disparity in help-seeking behavior is consistent with broader societal trends where males are often socialized to view seeking help as a sign of weakness or vulnerability, leading to a reluctance to seek mental health support. Cultural norms and expectations about masculinity might discourage men from acknowledging mental health issues and seeking professional help, contributing to the lower rates of help-seeking among male students [7, 6].

These findings are consistent with the application of the Health Belief Model (HBM), previously outlined, in explaining help-seeking behavior. Mental health literacy aligns with perceived susceptibility, as students with greater knowledge are more likely to recognize symptoms and perceive themselves at risk. In contrast, self-stigma and societal stigma related to seeking mental health help function as perceived barriers, being associated with reduced help-seeking even when vulnerability is acknowledged.

Clinical and practical implications

The findings of this study have several clinical and practical implications for improving mental health support among university students in the Maldives. First, the significant positive relationship between mental health literacy and help-seeking attitudes highlights the need for psychoeducational programs within university settings. These could include mandatory mental health orientation workshops, peer-led seminars, or integration of mental health content into academic curricula. Such initiatives can help students recognize mental health symptoms, understand available services, and reduce misconceptions.

Second, the stronger mediating role of self-stigma compared to societal stigma indicates the importance of addressing internalized beliefs through targeted interventions. Strategies such as cognitive-behavioral workshops focusing on self-compassion, online self-help modules, and narrative-based campaigns (e.g., student mental health testimonials) may help reduce feelings of shame and inadequacy. These programs can foster self-acceptance and reduce barriers to accessing professional help.

Third, given that males and younger students demonstrated more negative help-seeking attitudes, mental health interventions should be tailored to these vulnerable subgroups. For example, gender-sensitive outreach programs and peer mentoring could help normalize help-seeking behaviors among male students. Mobile-based mental health platforms might be more effective for younger students who prefer digital communication.

Finally, broader stigma-reduction campaigns on campus could focus on creating a culture of openness and psychological safety. Collaborations between student unions, counseling centers, and academic departments can help embed mental health visibility into campus life.

Limitations and future research

Despite its contributions, the present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the use of self-report measures may have introduced response biases, such as social desirability or inaccurate recall, which could affect the validity of the findings. Second, the study employed a cross-sectional design, which limits the ability to infer causality or determine the temporal sequence of relationships among variables. Although a mediation model was tested, the data was collected at a single point in time, and as such, the results should be interpreted as exploratory. Mediation implies a temporal and causal process that cannot be conclusively established without longitudinal or experimental data.

Another limitation involves the sample characteristics. The participants were exclusively university students in the Maldives, and a large proportion were enrolled in mental health and healthcare-related programs such as psychology, nursing, and medicine. This overrepresentation may have influenced the results, as students in these fields might possess higher mental health literacy and more favorable attitudes toward help-seeking compared to students in other disciplines. Furthermore, a disproportionate number of participants were from a single institution, Maldives National University (MNU), which may have introduced sampling bias. The institutional culture, curriculum emphasis, or mental health initiatives at MNU may not be representative of other universities in the country, limiting broader generalizability.

The sample also exhibited a gender imbalance, with female students being overrepresented. Given that gender differences are well-documented in mental health stigma and help-seeking attitudes, this imbalance may have influenced the strength or direction of the observed associations. Future research should strive for more gender-balanced samples to better understand potential gender-specific patterns in help-seeking behavior.

Additionally, the study focused on attitudes toward help-seeking but did not assess actual help-seeking behavior. As attitudes do not always translate into action, future studies should consider incorporating behavioral outcomes to capture a more accurate picture of mental health support-seeking patterns.

The study also did not control several potentially influential variables such as socioeconomic status, previous mental health history, or access to care, which may confound the relationships observed. For instance, students from lower-income backgrounds may face greater structural barriers to accessing mental health services, while individuals with prior treatment experience may report less stigma or more favorable help-seeking attitudes.

Moreover, the study did not explore how cultural norms and beliefs specific to the Maldivian context such as collectivist values, religious influences, or societal expectations, might shape mental health stigma and influence help-seeking behavior. Understanding these culturally embedded factors would provide richer context and help tailor more culturally sensitive mental health interventions.

Finally, some variability in participants’ responses was not consistent across levels of predicted values, suggesting minor heteroscedasticity. While the overall patterns remain interpretable, this should be considered when evaluating the strength of the observed relationships.

Future research should build on these findings using longitudinal designs to better understand the temporal relationships between mental health literacy, stigma, and help-seeking attitudes. Mixed-methods approaches could offer deeper insight by capturing students’ lived experiences and the contextual factors influencing help-seeking behavior. Additionally, examining intersecting variables such as socioeconomic background, cultural identity, and family mental health attitudes would provide a more comprehensive understanding of help-seeking among university students.

Conclusion

In summary, this study investigated the association between mental health literacy, stigma related to seeking mental health help, and help-seeking attitudes among university students in the Maldives. The findings revealed that higher levels of mental health literacy were linked to more positive help-seeking attitudes, while greater levels of self-stigma and societal stigma related to seeking help were associated with less favorable attitudes toward seeking help for mental health concerns. Furthermore, self-stigma and societal stigma were found to statistically mediate the observed association between mental health literacy and help-seeking attitudes. The study also highlighted demographic differences, with younger students and males exhibiting less positive help-seeking attitudes compared to their older and female counterparts. While providing valuable insights into the factors associated with help-seeking attitudes among university students in the Maldives, this study also has limitations, including its reliance on self-report measures and cross-sectional design. Therefore, these associations should be interpreted with caution as causality cannot be inferred. Future research should employ longitudinal designs, consider diverse samples, and explore interventions to address stigma and improve mental health literacy, ultimately fostering a more supportive environment for mental health help-seeking among university students in Maldives.
