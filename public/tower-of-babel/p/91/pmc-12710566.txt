Galvanic‐Cell‐Based Self‐Powered Bioelectronic Devices
Authors: Yu Xin, Longfei Chen, Bin Sun, Fangping Tang, Yamin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBioelectronic devices hold significant promise for advancing biomedical technologies, addressing critical healthcare challenges, and improving the quality of human life. Conventional bioelectronic devices are typically powered by external, bulky batteries connected by extended electrical wires, which limit the compactness and miniaturization of bioelectronics, restrict patient mobility, and increase the risk of complications such as infections and device‐related failures. This perspective discusses the emerging concept of galvanic‐cell‐based self‐powered bioelectronic devices, in which galvanic electrodes serve directly as the tissue‐contacting interfaces. We provide an overview of the principles and working mechanisms of biocompatible galvanic cells in galvanic devices for diverse biomedical applications, including electrical modulation, chemical and biochemical modulation, and hybrid electrical/(bio)chemical modulation, along with their control strategies: passive control and active control. Furthermore, we envision that the advancement of galvanic devices will represent a promising direction for biomedical applications, despite the presence of inherent challenges.Keywords: battery, bioelectronics, biomedical devices, galvanic cell, self‐powered

Introduction

Benefiting from the latest breakthroughs in advanced materials and modern biomedical technologies, a variety of novel bioelectronic devices are emerging to enhance the quality of human life.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] Numerous wearable and implantable systems have been developed to address increasingly complex clinical demands.[ 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 ] These devices have demonstrated broad applications across contemporary medical care, including cardiac pacing,[ 10 , 11 , 12 ] nerve stimulation,[ 13 , 14 , 15 ] wound healing[ 16 , 17 , 18 ] and drug delivery.[ 19 , 20 , 21 ]

Among the critical components of contemporary bioelectronics, the power supply module significantly influences both the functionality and operational lifespan of bioelectronic devices. Batteries, which convert chemical energy into electrical energy through spontaneous oxidation‐reduction (redox) reactions, are extensively utilized due to their reliable performance, substantial power output, and high energy density.[ 22 ] A typical battery comprises one or more galvanic cells connected in series or parallel. Each galvanic cell consists primarily of three main components: an anode, a cathode, and an electrolyte bridging these two electrodes. Conventional bioelectronic devices are usually powered by external, bulky batteries (e.g., lithium [Li] batteries) connected by extended electrical wires to tissue‐contacting electrodes (Figure 1a). Representative examples include commercially available cardiac pacemakers[ 23 ] and deep‐brain stimulators.[ 24 ] These wired power supplies impose inherent limitations in device compactness and miniaturization, raising significant practical concerns. Clinical complications include an elevated risk of infection, restricted patient mobility, increased likelihood of device failure, and safety issues in vivo, such as electrolyte leakage and wire dislodgement.[ 25 ]

In recent years, there has been growing interest in galvanic‐cell‐based self‐powered bioelectronic devices (hereafter referred to as galvanic devices), featuring biocompatible galvanic electrodes directly interfacing with biological tissues. In such systems, the galvanic cell simultaneously functions as both the power source and the active therapeutic element, thereby eliminating the requirement for external power supplies or wired connections (Figure 1b). Contemporary galvanic devices incorporate various biocompatible electrode materials, including metals (e.g., magnesium [Mg], zinc [Zn], iron [Fe], molybdenum [Mo], and tungsten [W]) and hydrogel‐based composites. Additionally, these systems exploit biocompatible electrolytes derived from natural biofluids and biological tissues or conductive hydrogels. By removing wired connections, galvanic devices can achieve substantial miniaturization, significantly mitigating risks associated with infection, reducing patient discomfort, and enhancing overall safety and biocompatibility.

Galvanic devices can achieve continuous electrical output without requiring external energy inputs, unlike other self‐powered strategies such as triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs)[ 26 ] and piezoelectric nanogenerators (PENGs), which rely on friction or mechanical compression.[ 27 ] Furthermore, galvanic devices can achieve higher output power, ranging from several hundred microwatts to a few milliwatts, compared to biofuel cell (BFC)‐based devices, whose performance is inherently constrained by the limited availability and concentration of biomolecules (e.g., glucose) and oxidants (e.g., O2).[ 28 ] However, a primary limitation of galvanic‐cell‐based devices lies in their finite operational lifetime due to the restricted quantity of active electrode materials. Research efforts on enhancing energy density and improving energy utilization would extend the lifetime and functionality of these galvanic devices.

Given the advancements in materials and innovative strategies for the fabrication of bioelectronic devices, several comprehensive reviews[ 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 ] have discussed advanced materials and self‐powered technologies utilized in bioelectronics. This perspective specifically focuses on the development of galvanic devices, offering an extensive overview of their operational mechanisms and device designs. We begin by introducing biocompatible galvanic cells, followed by classifying galvanic devices according to their modulation approaches for biomedical applications: electrical modulation, chemical and biochemical modulation, and hybrid electrical/(bio)chemical modulation. Furthermore, galvanic devices are categorized based on control strategies, including passive control and active control, for optimized safety and advanced modulation. Finally, we highlight emerging trends, current challenges, and future prospects in the development of galvanic devices.

Biocompatible Galvanic Cells

In galvanic cells, the open‐circuit potential arises from differences in electrochemical potentials established by two spatially separated half‐reactions occurring at the electrodes. The anode, possessing a higher tendency to lose electrons, undergoes oxidation, whereas the cathode, exhibiting a higher tendency to gain electrons, undergoes reduction. During discharge, electrons flow spontaneously from anode to cathode through an external circuit, accompanied simultaneously by ionic conduction within the electrolyte to complete the internal circuit. Galvanic cells comprise essential components, including an anode, cathode, electrolyte, separator, and encapsulation packaging. The careful selection and precise integration of biocompatible materials are critical for fabricating these components, thereby ensuring the overall biocompatibility of galvanic devices. Strategically tailoring the composition, morphology, and quantity of these constituent materials enables the optimization of device biocompatibility, enhancement of functional performance, and precise fulfillment of specific physiological demands and clinical requirements.

Figure 1c summarizes representative anodic oxidation reactions and cathodic reduction reactions. Anode materials include biocompatible metals (Zn, Mg) and LiTi2(PO4)3 (LTP). The standard electrochemical potentials of Mg, Zn, and LiTi2(PO4)3 are −2.37, −0.76, and −0.52 V, respectively, which indicates higher voltage outputs of Mg‐based cells than the cells based on the other two anode materials when coupled with the same cathode. Mg, Zn, and LiTi2(PO4)3 possess high theoretical volumetric capacities of 3833, 5845, and 2062 mAh cm−3, respectively, building their competitive advantage as anode materials for batteries.[ 33 ] As for cathodic reactions, the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) with a standard electrochemical potential of 1.23 V can provide a relatively higher voltage output compared to others. However, its intrinsically poor kinetics, severe impedance by oxygen mass transfer, and requirement for expensive electrocatalysts all limit the widespread use of such fuel cells in galvanic devices. Pairing different electrode reaction couples enables the construction of galvanic cells exhibiting diverse outputs across multiple performance metrics, including voltage, current, power, capacity, energy, stability, and operational lifetime. Customizing suitable electrode pairs allows the design of galvanic devices optimized for diverse biomedical applications, precisely addressing their unique performance requirements and clinical demands.

The anodic materials, including Zn, Mg, and LiTi2(PO4)3 have been reported to be biocompatible, with Zn and Mg being bioresorbable. As for cathodic materials, MoO3, MnO2, and AgCl, and conductive viologen‐based hydrogels (VHs)[ 34 ] are biocompatible, while MoO3 is bioresorbable. In addition, cathodic electrodes made from bioresorbable Fe, Mo, W, and nondegradable Pt and Pt/C are used for gas‐related electrochemistry, such as hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and ORR, as these materials possess catalytic performance toward HER and ORR. Regarding the bioresorbability, these electrodes can either be oxidized or dissolve to form soluble products in the biofluid. Specifically, Fe, Mo, and W typically react with O2, while Zn and Mg react with water and generate H2. These processes produce degradation products, including Fe(OH)3, H2MoO4, H2WO4, Zn(OH)2, and Mg(OH)2 in the human body. According to the degradation tests of nanometer‐thick metal films in Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) (pH 7.4 at 37 °C), Mg, Zn, W, Mo, and Fe exhibit degradation rates of 480, 300, 20, 0.7, and 7 nm day−1, respectively.[ 35 ] When selecting bioresorbable anode materials, trade‐offs must be carefully considered. A relatively high electronegativity can enhance the voltage output of the resulting galvanic cell, but it also correlates with a faster biodegradation rate and thus a shorter operational lifespan in the body. In addition, dissolvable metal oxides are considered bioresorbable as they form resorbable metal oxide hydrates (e.g., MoO3 + H2O → H2MoO4) in the human body.

On the other hand, employing aqueous electrolytes (i.e., saline and hydrogel) significantly enhances the in vivo safety of galvanic devices. Any leakage of such aqueous electrolytes containing appropriate salt ions (i.e., sodium chloride [NaCl]) poses minimal risk to patients.[ 36 ] Biofluids, such as sweat and extracellular fluid, as well as tissues, can function as electrolytes for galvanic cells during the operation of devices, thus completely eliminating the risk of electrolyte leakage.[ 37 ]

Selection of appropriate packaging materials requires careful consideration of material properties, including flexibility, adhesiveness, and biosafety, to ensure compatibility with targeted biomedical applications within complex physiological environments. Soft polymeric materials, such as polyanhydride (PA), polycaprolactone (PCL), and poly(lactic‐co‐glycolic acid (PLGA), have attracted considerable attention in bioelectronic device encapsulation owing to their excellent biocompatibility.[ 38 ] For instance, randomly oriented fibrous PCL has been employed as an encapsulation layer for nerve guidance conduits, effectively mitigating potential mechanical deformation and enhancing structural stability during neural regeneration.[ 39 ] Bioresorbable PA has been utilized as a packaging material in implantable temporary pacemakers, where its gradual biodegradation following completion of therapeutic cardiac pacing eliminates the need for secondary surgical extraction procedures.[ 40 ]

Modulation Approaches for Biomedical Applications

The emerging galvanic devices address the limitations of traditional wired bioelectronic devices and present new possibilities for biomedical applications. Depending on the therapeutic mechanisms required in different biomedical scenarios, these emerging galvanic devices can be categorized into three subgroups: 1) electrical modulation; 2) chemical and biochemical modulation; and 3) hybrid electrical/(bio)chemical modulation. Electrical Modulation

Ever since Galvani discovered the existence of bioelectricity in a frog's sciatic nerve, human's understanding of electricity in physiological activities (e.g., nerve conduction, cardiac rhythms, wound healing, etc.) has progressively advanced.[ 41 ] Accordingly, electrical stimulation generated in galvanic devices can be utilized for electrical modulation in these biomedical contexts. Traditional electrostimulation therapy relies on electrode pads to deliver electrical stimulation for treatment, such as muscle recovery or wound healing. However, wired systems and bulky power supplies limit the compactness and miniaturization of these electrostimulators and increase the risk of complications such as infections and device‐related failures. Galvanic devices offer a promising alternative for certain electrostimulation applications, addressing these challenges. In the galvanic device, the galvanic anode and/or cathode are directly utilized as the tissue‐contacting electrodes. The electrochemical potential difference between the two electrodes generates an electric field in the surrounding tissue, facilitating electrical stimulation for modulation purposes Figure 2a,i).

In general, power levels ranging from several hundred microwatts to a few milliwatts are sufficient for neurostimulators,[ 39 , 42 , 43 ] cardiac pacemakers[ 40 ] and devices for wound healing.[ 17 , 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 ] Galvanic devices can effectively deliver these power levels, rendering them suitable for these biomedical applications. For example, a tubular Zn‐Mo galvanic cell‐powered nerve guidance conduit has been developed, primarily comprising a Zn anode, a Mo cathode, and biofluid electrolyte (Figure 2a,ii).[ 39 ] An inner layer composed of aligned PCL fibers topographically guides axonal outgrowth, while an outer layer of randomly oriented PCL fibers imparts mechanical stability and prevents deformation during nerve regeneration. The Zn‐Mo cell exhibits an output voltage of ≈0.5 V at a discharge current density of 25 µA cm−2 for up to 50 h (Figure 2a,iv). The conduit, featuring an inner diameter of ≈1.5 mm, was validated experimentally by implanting it around a ≈10 mm sciatic nerve defect in Sprague‐Dawley (SD) rats. This configuration effectively delivered electrical stimulation for nerve tissue regeneration (Figure 2a,iii,v). The conduit completely degraded within 33 days upon immersion in PBS at 65 °C. After the implantation to bridge sciatic nerve defects for 12 weeks, histological analysis using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunofluorescent imaging demonstrated that the presence of the conduit positively influenced the myelination of regenerated nerve fibers. Furthermore, no significant inflammatory response was observed throughout the 12‐week implantation period, underscoring the biocompatibility and therapeutic potential of this conduit in peripheral nerve repair applications.

In addition, a cardiac pacemaker placed on the heart muscles generates electrical stimulation to regulate heart rate and treat conditions such as bradycardia. A recent work has reported a bioresorbable, millimeter‐scale pacemaker made of a Mg‐MoO3 or Zn‐MoO3 galvanic cell with only 3.5 mm in length, 1.8 mm in width, and 1 mm in thickness.[ 40 ] This pacemaker can be activated by light and provide temporary cardiac pacing, in which tissue and biofluids act as electrolytes and galvanic electrodes serve directly as pacing electrodes. When implanted at a tissue depth of 40 mm, the pacemaker generates an output current of 0.64 mA with light intensities of ≈61.7 mW mm−2 at the surface of the skin. The pacemaker completely degrades within 41 days upon immersion in PBS at 95 °C. Upon implantation onto the heart, the pacemaker demonstrates stable in vivo operation exceeding 6 days with the Mg–MoO3 electrode pair and over 20 days with the Zn–MoO3 pair. However, the pacing threshold increases significantly from day 2 onward for the Mg–MoO3 configuration and from day 19 for the Zn–MoO3 configuration, possibly attributable to the synergistic effect of local inflammation and electrode degradation. In vivo biocompatibility and toxicity assessments indicate no observable adverse effects following a 4‐week implantation period, underscoring the safety and biological compatibility of the device.

For wound healing scenarios, the exogenous electrical stimulation from devices can enhance the natural endogenous electrical potential around wounds, accelerating the healing process. Implantable, body‐fluid‐driven Mg‐Mo‐based electronic bandages have been successfully applied in intermuscular spaces[ 44 ] and the intestine.[ 45 ] Various Zn‐O2 and Mg‐O2‐based wearable wound healing patches have been reported for superficial tissues, such as skin regeneration and healing.[ 17 , 46 , 47 , 48 ] All of these devices utilize galvanic electrodes as electrical stimulating electrodes, thus significantly reducing device complexity. Moreover, some of these devices can naturally degrade after use, eliminating the need for secondary removal surgeries and thereby significantly reducing patients’ burden.[ 40 , 45 ]

Chemical and Biochemical Modulation

In addition to providing electrical stimulation via direct contact with tissues or organs, tissue‐contacting electrodes can also modulate bioprocesses and biochemistries through programmable release of chemicals and biochemicals. For example, corrodible anodes can serve as gates for drug reservoirs (Figure 2b,i). These gates can undergo corrosion during discharge and then release drugs sealed in the underlying reservoir.[ 49 ] One example reports light‐controlled, bioabsorbable drug delivery devices with Mg anodes serving as gates for drug reservoirs (Figure 2b,ii).[ 49 ] Mg anodes and a Fe cathode are interconnected through phototransistors. Light illumination on the phototransistor leads to a short circuit of the galvanic cell and thus corrosion of the anodes, which allows the drug release from the underlying reservoir (Figure 2b,iii). The Mg‐Fe cell delivers an average short circuit current of ≈31 mA cm−2 (Figure 2b,iv). Wavelength division strategy allows programmable control of a triple‐reservoir drug delivery device by utilizing three light‐selective optical filters placed atop the phototransistors. The device has been implanted near a rat's sciatic nerve. Released lidocaine successfully blocks the transduction of nerve signal (Figure 2b,v). The device completely degrades within 33 days upon immersion in PBS at 95 °C. Histological analysis reveals only a minimal presence of fibroblasts at the 1‐week time point, progressing to observable deposition of loose fibrous tissue by 4 weeks postimplantation. These findings collectively indicate a mild inflammatory response followed by reparative processes in response to the implanted device.

Tissue‐contacting electrodes are able to generate chemicals (e.g., H2) through electrochemical reactions[ 50 ] and release drugs (e.g., dexamethasone and adenosine triphosphate disodium salt) through valence change of drug‐adsorbed electrodes.[ 34 , 51 ] Exemplary works demonstrate the use of VHs (loaded with dexamethasone or adenosine triphosphate disodium salt) as both drug reservoirs and cathode materials in a Mg‐cell‐based drug delivery patch.[ 34 , 51 ] The discharge of the cell leads to a valence change in the VHs, resulting in reduced interactions between viologen and drug molecules, thereby facilitating the drug release. Furthermore, an implantable Mg‐based galvanic cell with on‐site H2 (the electrochemical reaction product) release can inhibit tumor growth, providing an emerging method for hydrogen therapy.[ 50 ] While these chemical and biochemical modulations can enhance therapeutic precision, fine‐tuning release kinetics to align with biological needs remains a challenge. More precise and intelligent controlled or even programmed release of chemicals and biochemicals in vivo is expected to further improve the efficacy and reduce the need for patient’ adherence.

Hybrid Electrical/(Bio)Chemical Modulation

The galvanic cell generates electrical energy through electrochemical reactions, enabling tissue‐contacting electrodes to simultaneously provide electrical stimulation and chemical/biochemical modulation. We refer to this combinatorial therapeutic strategy as hybrid electrical/(bio)chemical modulation, an approach that could facilitate enhanced regulatory precision and therapeutic efficacy through synergistic interactions (Figure 2c,i). For instance, a Mg‐cell‐based wound healing bioelectronic patch (Figure 2c,ii,iii) generates electrical stimulation for cell migration and proliferation while releasing Mg2+ and H2 from electrochemical reactions to modulate inflammation and oxidative stress.[ 52 ] The Mg‐based galvanic cell exhibits an average discharge voltage of ≈0.63 V for up to 94 h when connected to a 9 kΩ resistor, generating an electric field sufficient to promote wound healing (Figure 2c,iv,v). By integrating suitable mechanisms and designing electrode reactions, hybrid electrical/(bio)chemical modulation could evolve into synergistic and patient‐specific systems that can significantly improve therapeutic outcomes while reducing the need for external interventions. However, research on galvanic devices for hybrid electrical/(bio)chemical modulation remains relatively limited, highlighting an essential area for further investigation and technological advancement. We envision that in the future, various new types of galvanic devices for synergistic electrical stimulation and (bio)chemical modulation will be developed and investigated.

Control Strategies for Biomedical Devices

To enable on‐demand biomedical modulation, effective control strategies must be incorporated into bioelectronic devices. In galvanic systems, the discharge behavior and resulting electric current can be precisely regulated by tuning the resistance within the circuit, thereby controlling the extent of modulation achieved. Depending on the modulation mechanisms employed, control strategies can generally be classified into three distinct categories: 1) no control (open‐circuit configuration), 2) passive control (local resistance adjustment), and 3) active control (remotely adjustable switching).

No Control

Galvanic devices have been reported to perform modulation with the galvanic cell being open‐circuited and without any control. In this context, the electric field generated from the galvanic cell is directly utilized for modulation purposes. Such a design simplifies the device structure, encompassing almost only two electrodes as the core components Figure 3a,i). For instance, a recent work reports a self‐powered electronic bandage (E‐bandage) composed of a PCL outer layer, a tissue‐adhesive chitosan hydrogel layer, and bioresorbable Mg and Mo electrodes with an interdigital structure (Figure 3a,ii,iii).[ 45 ] This E‐bandage spontaneously generates a direct‐current (DC) electric field with voltages of ≈0.94 V on day 1, 0.39 V on day 2, and 0.04 V on day 3 upon contact with biofluids serving as electrolytes under open‐circuit conditions, thereby enabling effective intestinal wound healing (Figure 3a,iv,v). However, though such an E‐bandage can induce an electric field, a prominent limitation of such a device without control is that its voltage and current output are solely dependent on the intrinsic properties of the galvanic cell and are hard to fine‐tune.

Passive Control

As an escalated version of the open circuit state, interconnecting electrodes with resistors enables passive control of galvanic devices by locally adjusting the resistance of the circuit. Such passive control allows for tuning of device performance according to specific functional applications, i.e., the integrated resistor in the circuit can reduce electric current to a suitable level for chemical and biochemical modulation, such as the on‐demand drug delivery (Figure 3b,i). For instance, a recently reported iontophoresis patch uses an Mg anode, a 20% p‐styrene‐bipyridine (SV) polymerized viologen‐based hydrogel (VH20) cathode, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)/PBS gel electrolyte, and an external resistor to achieve on‐demand release of the dexamethasone sodium phosphate (Dex) drug (Figure 3b,ii,iii).[ 34 ] The chronopotentiometric analysis of the Mg‐based galvanic cell exhibits a discharge voltage of ≈0.62 V for up to 170 h when connected across a resistor of 104 Ω (Figure 3b,iv). The anionic Dex can be incorporated as counter‐ions within the skin‐connected VH20 cathode (quaternary ammonium cations, VH2+) via electrostatic interactions. During the discharge process, ammonium cations in the VH20 cathode are reduced to VH° form and weaken the electrostatic interactions between Dex with the VH20 cathode, allowing Dex molecules to be released. The release of Dex can reach ≈25.8% when discharging with a 103 Ω resistor over a span of 180 min (Figure 3b,v). The rate of release can be adjusted by altering the resistance value of the resistor.

Active Control

Active control mechanism represents an appealing strategy due to its unique user‐defined programmability. This mechanism requires the use of remotely adjustable electric switches, e.g., resistors. For example, external signals such as light can reduce the resistance of light‐sensitive phototransistors in circuits to achieve active control of galvanic devices (Figure 3c,i). Such light‐controlled modulation relies on the use of light‐responsive phototransistors. For instance, the previously mentioned millimeter‐scale Mg‐MoO3 cell‐based pacemaker with biofluids as electrolyte utilizes a silicon bipolar junction phototransistor, which can be activated by tissue‐penetrating near‐infrared (NIR) light (Figure 3c,ii,iii).[ 40 ] The Mg‐MoO3 pacing pair exhibits stable discharge behavior for up to 16 days within agarose gel at 37 °C, delivering currents of ≈0.4–0.5 mA with pacing rates of 60 bpm and pulse widths of 5 ms, thus surpassing the performance requirements typically necessary for temporary cardiac pacing (Figure 3c,iv). When implanting such a pacemaker into the superficial subepicardial layer of the myocardium, the inactive phototransistor exhibits a megaohm level resistance, making this pacemaker non‐functioning. Shining NIR light significantly lowers the resistance of the phototransistor to a level of approximately a few hundred ohms, thereby enabling the discharge of the galvanic cell for actively‐controlled cardiac pacing through the pacing electrodes (Figure 3c,v). Similarly, the previously mentioned light‐controlled programmed drug delivery system also utilizes this mechanism to achieve actively‐controlled drug release.[ 49 ] Exposing the phototransistor to light decreases its resistance and leads to a short circuit of the Mg‐Fe cell, the corrosion of the Mg anode (gate for the underlying drug reservoir), and thus the drug release event. These works also demonstrate autonomous, on‐demand closed‐loop operation in various complex biomedical scenes.[ 40 ]

Beyond light‐triggered activation, other external stimuli such as magnetic fields,[ 53 ] ultrasound,[ 54 ] thermal variations,[ 16 ] and chemicals[ 55 ] may also be explored for the active control of galvanic devices in future biomedical applications. Although currently unexplored, the development of electrical control switches responsive to these stimuli represents a promising direction for future device designs. Compared to optical stimuli, magnetic fields and ultrasound possess superior tissue‐penetration capabilities, enabling device implantation and therapeutic modulation in deeper tissues. Thermal‐responsive switches, activated by temperature fluctuations, are particularly suitable for wearable devices subject to environmental temperature variations and for implantable devices sensitive to local physiological temperature changes caused by diseases or disorders. Furthermore, chemically responsive switches hold potential for autonomous, chemically driven closed‐loop treatments within the body. For instance, a galvanic device responsive to localized pH variations could open new and innovative avenues for precision medicine.

Conclusion and Future Perspective

The advancement of self‐powered technologies represents a significant and emerging trend in the bioelectronic and biomedical sectors. Among these, galvanic devices have gained considerable attention due to their minimally invasive nature and effectiveness in addressing diverse biomedical challenges. Galvanic devices allow for electrical modulation, chemical and biochemical modulation, and hybrid electrical/(bio)chemical modulation, demonstrating broad applicability in biomedical fields such as cardiac pacing, neuromodulation, and controlled drug delivery.

Despite these promising developments, critical challenges remain for clinical translation and commercialization. Key areas requiring attention include: 1) enhancing device safety by attenuating chronic inflammatory responses and reducing fibrotic capsule formation associated with long‑term implantation; 2) optimizing galvanic cell performance across multiple dimensions, including energy density, power density, voltage output, and operational stability; 3) miniaturizing device size to simplify implantation procedures, mitigate associated risks, reduce patient burden, and accelerate postoperative recovery; and 4) addressing the inherent conflict between the short lifespan of bioresorbable materials and the demand for prolonged device functionality, particularly in bioresorbable systems intended for long‐term applications.

Moreover, the distinct requirements posed by different biomedical applications further compound these challenges. For instance, neuroprosthetic devices typically require cylindrical and flexible configurations to accommodate anatomical constraints, necessitating specific design considerations. In drug delivery applications, precise control over dosage release rates is essential to prevent overdosing or inadequate therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, tailored strategies must be developed to meet these unique functional and performance demands across diverse biomedical uses.

To address the aforementioned challenges, the following efforts are essential to advance biomedical galvanic devices toward broader clinical adoption and streamlined commercialization Figure 4 ).

Opportunities and challenges associated with galvanic bioelectronic devices. Future research directions encompass advanced material design, device miniaturization, diversification of functionalities, development of bioadhesive interfaces, integration of wireless technologies, and incorporation of artificial intelligence.

Material Design

Material design is central to optimizing galvanic bioelectronic devices, with particular focus required for anodes, cathodes, electrolytes, and packaging materials. Integration of theoretical modeling with advanced material‐processing techniques enables the fabrication of electrodes exhibiting high electrical conductivity, superior electrochemical performance, robust mechanical integrity, and excellent biocompatibility. For passive devices, careful electrode pairing necessitates comprehensive knowledge of the electrochemical characteristics of candidate materials to ensure optimal electrochemical behavior. State‐of‐the‐art characterization techniques facilitate the establishment of correlations between material structure and electrochemical performance, thereby advancing the fundamental understanding of emerging electrode materials. Furthermore, the selection and processing of materials for device assembly must balance cost‐effectiveness with rigorous requirements for electrochemical performance and biocompatibility. While bioresorbable metals such as Mg, Zn, and Fe degrade readily in vivo, their corrosion byproducts, particularly elevated local hydroxide ion (OH−) concentrations, may induce undesirable tissue alkalization and negatively influence cellular activity. Thus, meticulous consideration of both electrode precursors and their degradation products or electrochemical byproducts is essential. In addition, chronic inflammatory responses and the formation of fibrotic capsules remain long‐standing challenges associated with device implantation. Therefore, galvanic device materials must be carefully engineered and rigorously tested for long‐term biocompatibility to minimize inflammation.

Device Miniaturization

Device miniaturization and compactness represent emerging trends, enabling substantial reductions in the volume and weight of bioelectronic devices. Importantly, such miniaturization does not necessarily compromise the device's power output or therapeutic efficacy. Long‐term wearable and implantable applications demand ever‐smaller devices with high energy capacity. Therefore, some specific strategies need to be developed to achieve both device miniaturization and long‐term operation in the future: 1) utilizing advanced and rational electrode architecture design (e.g., 2D slurry‐coating method[ 56 ] and 3D printing technology)[ 57 ] to enhance specific power density, cycling stability and mechanical performance; 2) developing advanced materials with high energy density to enable the long‐term operation of miniaturized devices;[ 58 ] 3) incorporating advanced electrolyte systems, such as eutectic liquids, to improve electrode stability and thereby extend the lifetime of the galvanic cell;[ 59 ] 4) integrating galvanic cells with energy harvesting modules into a hybrid energy system enables continuous collection of energy, such as biochemical, thermal, or mechanical energy, from the body, environment, or other sources to support long‐term operation.[ 60 ] Additionally, device miniaturization imposes rigorous demands on configuration design and assembly techniques. Advanced structural designs, such as fiber‐based, interdigital, and thin‐layer configurations, can enhance the functional capabilities of miniaturized devices while preserving their high performance.[ 61 ]

Diversified Functionalities

Galvanic devices spontaneously generate electric fields, enabling intrinsic electrical stimulation. Moreover, these devices can simultaneously release chemicals or biochemicals through electrochemical reactions, facilitating synergistic modulation of biological processes. Through precise control over electrode materials and reaction pathways, galvanic devices hold significant potential to enhance therapeutic efficacy across a wide spectrum of biomedical applications.

Bioadhesive Interfaces

Establishing a stable interface between bioelectronic systems and living tissues is critical for biomedical galvanic devices. The development of advanced bioadhesive materials is essential, with tailored electrical conductivity, depending on the intended application. Additionally, these materials must effectively and conformably adhere to both electronic components and anatomically complex organ surfaces, without inducing discomfort or causing tissue injury. The application of bioadhesive interfaces, such as hydrogels, offers an effective strategy to reduce mechanical mismatches between devices and tissues, thereby mitigating fibrotic capsule formation and enhancing the stability and efficacy of implantable devices.

Wireless Technology

Wireless bioelectronic devices offer substantial advantages by removing physical connectors, thereby minimizing infection risks, enhancing patient comfort, and enabling greater flexibility in device placement and extended monitoring capabilities. Galvanic devices employing active control modalities can be wirelessly activated and programmed via external stimuli. Looking forward, wireless bioelectronics integrating autonomous closed‐loop operation are expected to become mainstream, featuring control systems capable of collecting and analyzing signals from the device to provide real‐time feedback, data logging, and adaptive modulation.[ 62 ] Therefore, continued development and refinement of feedback‐control algorithms are essential to enable increasingly efficient wireless control strategies for future biomedical applications.

Artificial Intelligence

The biological signals acquired by bioelectronic devices can be challenging to analyze due to their inherently continuous and complex nature, resulting in the generation of extensive datasets. Artificial intelligence (AI) offers powerful tools to address these challenges, capable of rapidly processing and interpreting large volumes of biological data. However, despite the promise of AI‐assisted biomedical bioelectronics, this technology remains in its early stages of development, requiring further research to enhance practicality and efficacy, and to address critical considerations regarding AI‐human interactions and ethical implications in medical AI applications.

Successful clinical translation and commercialization of galvanic devices, both wearable and implantable, will require rigorous validation of their safety, biocompatibility, and efficacy through extensive preclinical and clinical studies. Common essential steps include material optimization, standardized manufacturing processes, regulatory approvals, and robust performance evaluation. Implantable galvanic devices will necessitate additional considerations, such as advanced sterilization protocols, long‐term stability assessments, compatibility with diagnostic imaging techniques, and refined surgical implantation procedures to ensure optimal clinical outcomes and patient acceptance. Multidisciplinary collaboration among engineers, clinicians, and regulatory experts will accelerate iterative design cycles, streamline translation, and ultimately clinical adoption.
