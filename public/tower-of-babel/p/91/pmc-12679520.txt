Applying DanGer Shock Eligibility Criteria to a Real‐World Cohort of Cardiogenic Shock Patients
Authors: Raffaela Miriam Planka, Lukas Herold, Theresa Glantschnig, Gudrun Sommer, Jonathan Pavek, Stefan Hatzl, Nicolas Verheyen, Christoph Strohhofer, Johannes Gollmer, Ewald Kolesnik, Martin Benedikt, Markus Wallner, Andreas Zirlik, Gabor Toth‐Gayor, Dirk von Lewinski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe DanGer Shock trial investigated the effects of adding a microaxial flow pump to guideline‐recommended therapy in patients with ST‐elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI)‐related cardiogenic shock (CS), finding a lower all‐cause mortality at 180 days in the intervention group.MethodsDanGer Shock eligibility criteria were applied to the prospective single‐center PREPARE CardShock registry, which consecutively enrolled 721 patients between April 2019 and August 2024 with advanced CS—classified as stages C to E according to the SCAI shock classification—who were on vasoactive medication and underwent cardiac catheterization.ResultsAmong 721 patients in the registry, 384 (53.3%) had STEMI‐related CS. Of those, 117 (30.5%) met DanGer Shock trial eligibility criteria, accounting for 16.2% of the registry cohort. Median age of the eligible patients was 68 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 58–78), and 62.4% were male, compared to 68 years (IQR: 60–76) and 79.2% in DanGer Shock. PREPRARE‐CS DanGer Shock eligible patients had a higher median systolic blood pressure (SBP) (92 mmHG [78–107] vs. 83 mmHG, [72–91]) and higher median lactate levels (5.2 mmol/L [3.6–7.6] vs. 4.6 mmol/L [3.3–7.0]) at baseline, and were more frequently resuscitated (56.4% vs. 20.3%). Device use was 42.7% in eligible patients. Renal replacement therapy (RRT) was more frequent among trial participants (34.4% vs. 17.1%). The 180‐day mortality rate among eligible patients was 71.8%, compared to 52.1% in trial participants.ConclusionsAmong STEMI‐related CS patients in our cohort, only one‐third met the DanGer Shock eligibility criteria. Consequently, trial outcomes may not be broadly applicable.Keywords: AMICS, cardiogenic shock, MCS, microaxial flow pump, mortality, STEMI‐related CS

Introduction

Cardiogenic shock (CS) represents the most severe form of acute heart failure (AHF). Cardiac impairment leads to low cardiac output (CO), resulting in inadequate end‐organ perfusion. Hypoperfusion with tissue hypoxia and elevated lactate levels trigger a pathophysiological cascade that often culminates in multiorgan failure and death [1].

The underlying causes of CS are highly heterogeneous. In the absence of a uniform definition, the Shock Academic Research Consortium (SHARC) has proposed the following definitions of CS: Clinically, CS is defined as cardiac disorders, characterized by clinical signs and biochemical markers indicating inadequate tissue perfusion. For research purposes, SHARC defines CS as cardiac disorders associated with a systolic blood pressure (SBP) < 90 mmHG for ≥ 30 min—or requiring vasopressors, inotropes, or mechanical circulatory support (MCS) to maintain a SBP ≥ 90 mmHG—accompanied by signs/evidence of insufficient tissue perfusion [2]. The Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI) defines five stages of CS severity, ranging from stage A (at risk) to stage E (extremis). Clinically manifest CS begins at stage C, and most clinical studies include patients classified as SCAI shock stages C to E [3].

Phenotyping and classifying CS based on its underlying mechanism can help improve clinical management and enable more precise therapeutic interventions [1]. Given the current state of research, distinguishing between CS caused by acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and CS due to other aetiologies is clinically important for guiding treatment decisions.

Approximately 5%–10% of patients with AMI develop CS [4, 5, 6, 7]. Despite advances in the therapeutic management of CS, mortality remains high, including AMI‐associated cases. While early mortality in AMI‐related CS has decreased from historical rates of 70%–80%, it still remains substantial, with current estimates ranging from 30% to 50% [8, 9, 10]. Early vascularization is the key in the treatment of acute myocardial infarction complicated by cardiogenic shock (AMICS), as it has been shown to improve survival [11, 12, 13]. Temporary mechanical circulatory support (tMCS) devices are used as an adjunctive to percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in an attempt to provide haemodynamic stability. These devices have been increasingly employed in patients with refractory CS as a means of bridging to decision or recovery. The neutral IABP‐SHOCK II trial led to a revision of recommendations regarding the use of intra‐aortic balloon pumps (IABP) [14]. Since then, their use has drastically decreased [15, 16, 17]. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) and microaxial flow pump (mAFP) devices, namely Impella devices, remain in clinical use.

Although early use of MCS in AMICS appears promising from a haemodynamic perspective, safety concerns–particularly peripheral ischemic as well as bleeding complications–and inconclusive evidence regarding a mortality benefit of MCS in AMICS patients temper their widespread adoption. Until recently, large randomized controlled trial (RCTs), such as the IABP‐SHOCK II trial and the ECLS‐SHOCK trial, failed to demonstrate a mortality benefit associated with MCS use [14, 18].

However, the recently published DanGer Shock trial represents a breakthrough, being the first RCT to show a significant mortality benefit of MCS in patients with STEMI‐related CS. This multicentre trial compared the use of mAFPs (Impella CP) adjunctive to standard care to guideline recommended medical therapy in patients with ST‐segment elevation (STEMI)‐related CS. The primary endpoint, all‐cause mortality at 180 days, was significantly lower in the mAFP group (45.8%) compared to the standard care group (58.5%) (Hazard ratio 0.74, 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.55–0.99, p = 0.04, NNT = 8). Despite these beneficial effects, complications were more frequent in the Impella group, including higher rates of major bleeding, limb ischemia, stroke, sepsis, renal replacement therapy (RRT), and cardioversion following ventricular tachycardia. The Impella group also had a higher rate of composite safety events (including severe bleeding, limb ischemia, hemolysis, device failure, and worsening of aortic regurgitation) (24.0% vs. 6.2%, Relative risk [RR] 4.74, 95% CI 2.36–9.55, NNH = 6) [19].

Due to the trial′s strict eligibility criteria, applicability of the findings to a broader, real‐world CS population remains uncertain. Certain patient groups were underrepresented, and as such, we hypothesize that the results may not be directly generalizable to more heterogeneous clinical populations.

Methods

Registry and Trial Population

This analysis is based on the PREPARE CardShock registry—a PRospective single‐center REgistry of PAatients in REfractory cardiogenic shock—established at the University Heart Center Graz, Medical University of Graz. Since its initiation in April 2019, the PREPARE CardShock registry has prospectively and consecutively enrolled patients. Inclusion criteria are: age between 18 and 99 years, haemodynamic instability requiring vasopressors or inotropes at the time of admission to the catheterization laboratory (cath lab)—with cath lab admission itself being a mandatory criterion. This corresponds to cardiogenic shock patients in SCAI shock stages C, D, or E, as defined by the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI), which classifies shock severity into stages A (at risk) to E (extremis) [3].

Device use in the registry cohort was based on a local standard operating procedure (SOP), which specifies both relative and absolute contraindications for MCS use, based on factors such as patient age, comorbidities, haemodynamic biomarkers, and peri‐procedural aspects (e.g., CPR duration), and which has been previously published in Catheterization and Cardiovascular Interventions [20].

Registry patients received MCS via the Impella CP® device (Abiomed, Aachen, Germany) and the Xenios ECMO platform (Xenios, Heilbronn, Germany). The IABP was used only to a limited extent, namely in four registry patients. The devices were implanted through femoral access by the interventional cardiologist. In patients receiving venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA‐ECMO), an antegrade arterial sheath (6 F) was inserted and connected to the arterial cannula to ensure adequate limb perfusion. The choice of MCS modality was primarily based on the patients′ residual circulatory function and ventilatory capacity. Patients with moderate‐to‐severe circulatory impairment were typically considered for Impella CP®, while those with profound circulatory collapse and/or severe oxygenation impairment—as indicated by a low Horowitz index (arterial oxygen partial pressure to fractional inspired oxygen ratio [PaO₂/FiO₂])—were more likely to receive VA‐ECMO [20].

The registry received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Graz, Austria (EK 31–323 ex 18/19), and data collection was performed in strict adherence to the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments, as well as in compliance with the International Conference on Harmonization′s Good Clinical Practice (ICH GCP E6) guidelines.

Eligibility criteria in the DanGer Shock trial were strictly defined. Inclusion criteria were: new‐onset STEMI or STEMI‐equivalent within 36 h, or STEMI complicated by CS developing within 12 h post‐revascularisation. CS had to be present for less than 24 h, and was defined by peripheral hypoperfusion (lactate ≥ 2.5 mmol/L and/or mixed venous oxygen saturation [SvO2] < 55% with normal partial pressure of arterial oxygen [PaO2]), SBP < 100 mmHG, and/or the need for vasopressors (dopamine, norepinephrine, or epinephrine), and a left ventricular ejection fraction < 45%. Patients were excluded if CS lasted over 24 h, was unrelated to STEMI, or resulted from mechanical complications of myocardial infarction (MI). Further exclusion criteria included a persistent Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score < 8 after out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) as well as severe right ventricular failure (assessed individually via echocardiography), significant aortic valve disease, mechanical aortic valve prothesis, vascular anatomy unsuitable for Impella placement, left ventricular thrombus, infective endocarditis, life expectancy under 1 year, or inability to provide informed consent [19].

This analysis compared baseline characteristics and key outcome variables—adverse events and all‐cause mortality at 180 days—between registry patients and DanGer Shock trial participants. Registry patients with STEMI‐related CS who met the DanGer Shock trial′s eligibility criteria were classified as PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock eligible, while those who did not meet those criteria were categorized as PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock ineligible.

Data Collection and Statistical Analysis

Variables were collected retrospectively from the PREPARE CardShock registry, which compiles data from the local hospital information system and the intensive care unit program, and compared with published data from the DanGer Shock trial. For comparison with the registry cohort, the two DanGer Shock trial groups (mAFP plus standard care and standard care alone) were combined by averaging their data. Categorical variables are shown as counts (frequencies) and compared using the Chi‐square (χ2) test. Continuous variables are shown as medians (interquartile range [IQR]), and compared using the Mann‐Whitney U test. To explore associations in more detail, regression analyses were performed. For statistical analyses, IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 29 was used. Tables were created using Microsoft Word (Microsoft 365), Version 2505. Figures were created using Microsoft Word (Microsoft 365), Version 2505 and IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 29.

Results

Patient Characteristics

During the observation period from April 2019 to August 2024, a total of 721 patients were enrolled in the registry. Among them, 384 (53.3%) had STEMI‐related CS. Of these, 117 (30.5% of STEMI‐related CS patients; 16.2% of the total registry) met the DanGer Shock trial′s eligibility criteria and were therefore classified as PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock eligible. The remaining 267 (69.5% of STEMI‐related CS patients; 37.0% of the total registry) did not fulfill the criteria and were classified as PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock ineligible (see Figure 1).

PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock Ineligible Patients Compared to Trial Participants

Compared with trial participants, those who did not meet DanGer Shock trial eligibility criteria (PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock ineligible) were younger (63 years [IQR: 55–74]), had a higher SBP (99 mmHG [IQR: 83–120]), a lower HR (76 bpm [IQR: 65–92]), and lower lactate levels (2.3 mmol/L [IQR: 1.4–5.9]). They were also more frequently resuscitated (67.0%). Coronary artery disease (CAD) was less extensive in PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock ineligible patients, with higher rates of no CAD (2.2% vs. 0.3%) and single‐vessel CAD (37.5% vs. 27.9%), while multivessel CAD occurred less frequently (59.6% vs. 71.8%). This PREPARE Shock subgroup had the highest proportion of male patients among all registry groups. With 75.3%, the proportion of male patients was still lower than in the trial cohort (see Table 1).

PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock Eligible Patients Compared to PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock Ineligible Patients

PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock eligible patients were older (p = 0.009) and included more females (p = 0.010) compared to PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock ineligible patients. Regarding risk factor distribution, PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock eligible patients had less diabetes (p = 0.002) and did not suffer from CKD as much (p = 0.012). They were less likely to have undergone resuscitation (p = 0.046). Their median SBP was lower (p = 0.003), as was their median HR (p < 0.001). They had higher median lactate levels (p < 0.001) (see Table 2).

Total PREPARE CS Cohort Compared to Trial Participants

In the overall PREPARE CS cohort, when compared to DanGer Shock trial participants, patients were less often male (71.8%) and more frequently had HF (16.4% vs. 9.3%), and CKD (18.4%) as comorbidities. They were more frequently resuscitated than trial participants (63.4%). PREPARE CS patients more frequently presented without CAD (12.6%), and where less likely to suffer from multivessel CAD compared to trial participants (59.1%). PREPARE CS patients were, on median, 1 year younger (67 years [IQR: 58–76]) and had a higher SBP (99 mmHG [IQR: 81–117]), a lower HR (74 bpm [IQR: 62–90]), and lower lactate levels (3.6 mmol/L [IQR: 1.7–7.1]) (see Table 1).

MCS Use

MCS use was 21.2% in the overall PREPARE CS registry cohort. The PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock eligible subgroup had the highest device utilization rate. Of the PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock eligible patients, 1 (0.9%) received an IABP, 23 (19.7%) an Impella, 19 (16.2%) a VA‐ECMO, and 6 (5.1%) were implanted an ECMELLA device. In the DanGer Shock cohort, 173 of the 355 participants (48.7%) initially received an Impella device (see Tables 1 and 2).

Outcomes and Adverse Events

In the overall PREPARE CS cohort, all‐cause mortality at 180 days was 53.1%. With 71.8%, 180‐day mortality was significantly higher in PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock eligible patients when compared to PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock ineligible patients, who showed a 180‐day mortality of 37.1% (p < 0.001). Participants enrolled in the DanGer Shock trial had a 180‐day mortality of 52.1% (see Figure 2 and Table 3).

Discussion

In this real‐world cohort of CS patients, fewer than one in five would have been eligible for the DanGer Shock trial. This was largely driven by the inclusion criterion of a GCS > 8, excluding the frequent group of comatose patients arriving at the hospital in CS and suffering from the worst prognosis.

Patients from the PREPARE CS registry—especially PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock ineligible patients and the broader group of STEMI‐related CS registry patients—were generally younger, more frequently resuscitated, and exhibited less extensive CAD when compared to trial participants, with the exception of the PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock eligible subgroup, whose coronary profiles resembled those of the trial participants, with a similar tendency toward multivessel CAD.

In comparison to trial participants, the overall registry population and defined subgroups showed more favorable baseline haemodynamic profiles, characterized by a higher SBP, a lower HR, and also lactate levels were generally lower in registry patients, with the exception of the PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock eligible group, which showed the highest median lactate levels.

When comparing PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock eligible to PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock ineligible patients, PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock eligible patients showed higher lactate levels, suggesting more severe shock. However, they had a less burdened overall risk profile, with lower rates of CKD and resuscitation. The resuscitation rate as a main driver of outcome was still almost three times higher in the PREPARE CS‐DanGER Shock eligible patients than in trial participants.

The DanGer shock trial group had the lowest frequency of resuscitated patients and the highest proportion of male patients.

The DanGer Shock trial demonstrated that the Impella device provided effective haemodynamic support, evidenced by a more stable mean arterial pressure (MAP) in the early phase (up to 12 h), a reduced need for vasopressors and inotropes, as well as a delayed necessity for their initiation, and a lower overall requirement for vasoactive medication. Improved tissue perfusion was suggested by the faster normalization of lactate levels [19, 21]. Furthermore, subgroup analysis of the primary endpoint showed that among patients with a mean arterial pressure of ≤ 63 mm Hg, treatment with a mAFP was associated with a significantly lower risk of death at 180 days [19]. MCS devices are frequently considered a last‐resort therapy in patients with severe and/or refractory CS. Both the optimal timing of MCS initiation and the identification of appropriate candidates remain unclear [22, 23]. In the absence of robust data from prospective RCTs, clinical decision‐making continues to rely predominantly on registry data, single‐center experiences, and local standard operating procedures (SOPs). The current ESC guideline offers a Class IIa, Level C recommendation for the use of tMCS in patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and severe and/or refractory CS [24]. The ESC guideline came out before the DanGer Shock trial results were published, and primarily recommends VA‐ECMO use, based on the results of the ECMO‐CS RCT from Ostadal et al. [24, 25]. The 2025 ACC/AHA/ACEP/NAEMSP/SCAI guideline for the management of patients with ACS based their Class IIa, Level B‐R recommendation for the temporary use of mAFPs in selected patients with STEMI and severe and/or refractory CS on the DanGer Shock trial results [19, 26]. IABP devices and VA‐ECMO are not recommended, mainly based on the results of the IABP‐SHOCK II trial, the ECMO‐CS trial and the ECLS‐Shock trial [14, 18, 25, 26]. The aforementioned guidelines only provide limited guidance on patient selection criteria, broadly suggesting that decisions on tMCS use should be based on factors such as age, comorbidities, neurological function, and that certain haemodynamic aspects should be considered. There is an ongoing gap in evidence‐based risk stratification models to guide individualized treatment strategies that might benefit the outcome in patients with ACS associated with CS.

In the DanGer Shock trial randomization was allowed up to 12 h after leaving the cath lab, however if patients were randomized pre‐PCI, the Impella device was implanted before revascularisation [19]. As in the DanGer Shock trial, the majority of Impella pumps in the STEMI‐related CS registry cohort were implanted pre‐PCI. The timing of MCS was retrospectively reviewed across the entire STEMI‐related CS cohort: among 59 patients for whom the data were available, MCS was initiated pre‐PCI in 44 cases and post‐PCI in 15 cases. The DanGer Shock trial was not designed to address aspects on patient selection and the optimal timing for MCS implantation. In the context of AMICS, key uncertainties remain regarding whether MCS should be initiated before or after revascularisation, as well as on the patient selection criteria, on which device choices should be based [20].

In the DanGer Shock trial, the study population was described as particularly homogeneous. Comatose patients following cardiac arrest were excluded based on the rationale that metabolic derangements in such cases could stem either from global ischemia due to circulatory arrest, or from left ventricular failure caused by myocardial injury—with no reliable way to distinguish between the two. It was further argued that these patients typically die from irreversible hypoxic brain injury rather than from persistent cardiac failure. Consistent with guideline recommendations, which emphasize that decisions regarding MCS should take neurological function into account, MCS is unlikely to alter outcomes in cases of irreversible anoxic brain damage [19]. The exclusion criterion in the DanGer Shock trial—a GCS score of less than 8 following out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA)—resulted in only resuscitated patients after in‐hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) being eligible. OHCA registry patients were systematically excluded. This was largely attributable to the fact that nearly all patients had been sedated and intubated before transfer to the cath lab, and therefore, by definition, exhibited a GCS score of less than 8. Given the growing use of MCS in the cardiac arrest setting—during and after resuscitation—the broad exclusion of post‐OHCA patients with a GCS score below 8 before randomization limits the external validity of trial findings and their applicability to more heterogeneous CS populations.

Women were disproportionately underrepresented in the DanGer Shock trial cohort, especially when compared with our registry. This underrepresentation is noteworthy given that biological sex differences might influence not only the distribution of cardiovascular risk factors, but also the incidence and outcomes of cardiovascular events. Existing data suggest that women may be at increased risk of developing AMICS [27]. However, subgroup analysis of the DanGer Shock trial′s primary endpoint did not show a significant benefit of mAFP in females [19].

Although the age distribution of patients in the DanGer Shock trial relatively closely matched that of our registry cohort, neither the trial nor our registry may fully represent octogenarians—a population that may be at increased risk for adverse outcomes of CS [28]. This particularly vulnerable group, however, had no benefit in DanGer Shock [29] and was excluded by the PREPARE CS SOP [20].

Outcomes

The trial population represents a highly selected subgroup of CS patients. Over a 10‐year enrollment period, 355 patients were included, during which more than 1200 STEMI patients with CS were screened and approximately 70% were excluded due to stringent inclusion criteria [19]. Despite this rigorous selection process, the fragility index for the primary endpoint was only four, indicating that the p‐value for mortality at 180 days would have been greater than 0.05, if either only four more patients in the control group would have survived until day 180 or four more patients in the Impella group would have died [30].

Mortality was significantly higher in the PREPARE‐CS DanGer Shock eligible patients. These patients had a significantly higher rate of prior resuscitation, were intubated more frequently, had elevated lactate levels, and were more often female. This points to a selection bias in the DanGer Shock trial not fully accounted for by its stated inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Bleeding was the second most frequent complication in the DanGer Shock trial [19], with comparable rates observed in the PREPARE CS registry. Although the incidence of limb ischemia was low in the DanGer Shock trial [19] as well as in the PREPARE CS registry—albeit numerically higher in DanGer—systematic documentation of limb ischemia was lacking in PREPARE CS, raising the possibility of detection bias and underreporting.

A secondary analysis of the DanGer Shock trial showed that patients receiving an Impella device experienced a higher rate of acute kidney injury (AKI) and required RRT more often than those in the control group, with 61% of Impella patients developing AKI compared to 45% in the control group. AKI and RRT were linked to a higher 180‐day mortality in both groups, though Impella use was associated with a lower overall mortality despite the kidney complications. Key predictors of AKI in the Impella group included suction events and higher pump speed, which are modifiable factors [31]. A survivor bias cannot be ruled out as a partial explanation for the higher rate of AKI and RRT in the Impella group. Registry patients overall and the subgroup of PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock eligible patients showed a higher prevalence of CKD. Despite this, the need for RRT as a complication was significantly lower, even when considering only the subgroup of PREPARE CS‐DanGer Shock eligible patients with Impella (relatively small simple size of 23). This could be due to more aggressive management of kidney function or MCS use may have mitigated the progression of CKD in our cohort.

Limitations

This analysis is limited by the comparison of registry data with data from a randomized controlled trial. LVEF was not systematically assessed in the PREPARE CS registry. Initial LVEF was not documented numerically but as a semi‐quantitative visual estimation (e.g., mildly, moderately or severely reduced). Only in 4.4% of cases, the exact and numerical LVEF was documented during the index catheterization. An LVEF < 45% was assumed for all patients with moderately or severely reduced LVEF. A sensitivity analysis was performed by detailed review of the medical charts: within 48 h after intervention, LVEF could be determined in 324 of 384 cases (84.4%), among which 234 patients (60.9%) had an LVEF < 45%. Importantly, because LVEF often improves after STEMI and intervention, documentation of a higher EF up to 48 h after the index MI on post‐procedural echocardiography does not contradict the initial classification of these patients as DanGer Shock eligible.

Conclusion

This analysis demonstrates that the eligibility criteria of the DanGer Shock trial, particularly the GCS > 8 threshold, would have excluded more than 80% of the patients in a real‐world CS cohort. Notably, this exclusion disproportionately affects comatose patients, a subgroup with the poorest prognosis and high clinical relevance.
