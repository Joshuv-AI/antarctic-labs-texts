Impact of an Ophthalmic Cooling Device on Corneal Immune and Sensory Nerve Features in Chronic Ocular Surface Pain: A Prospective, Feasibility Study
Authors: Jeremy Chung Bo Chiang, Mengliang Wu, Ji-Hyun Lee, Alyssa Curkpatrick, Cristos Ifantides, Thanh Nguyen, Anat Galor, Helen Wu, Richard Lindstrom, Vance Thompson, Laura E. Downie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionA safe and effective treatment for chronic ocular surface pain (COSP) remains an unmet need. This prospective feasibility study investigated the impact of an ophthalmic cooling device (ETX-4143) on ocular pain, as well as corneal nerve and immune cell features, in individuals with COSP.MethodsFor this clinical trial (Clinicaltrials.gov:NCT07059754), the more symptomatic (study) eye was treated with the ETX-4143 device, involving two metallic probes, internally cooled by a −20 °C fluid, applied to the bulbar conjunctiva posterior to the limbus at 3 and 9 o’clock for 4 min, with a fellow (untreated) eye control. Ocular pain, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), tear osmolarity, corneal sensitivity, slit lamp biomicroscopy, ocular surface staining, and static and functional in vivo confocal microscopy (Fun-IVCM) were conducted at baseline and 2-, 6-, and 12-weeks posttreatment. Corneal nerve and immune cell parameters were quantified.ResultsFive eligible participants were enrolled and completed the study (mean age 66.4 ± 9.9 years, 60% female/40% male). Relative to baseline, ocular pain, measured with the Chronic Ocular Pain Questionnaire (COP-Q) Eye Pain Severity Module, was significantly lower at 2 weeks (mean change −4.4 units, 95% CI −7.4 to −1.4, P = 0.02), 6 weeks (−5.0 units, 95% CI −7.0 to −3.0, P = 0.002), and 12 weeks posttreatment (−5.8 units, 95% CI −7.8 to −3.8, P = 0.001); benefit progressed with time. Clinical ocular surface signs, BCVA, and corneal nerve morphology remained stable. In the study eye, corneal Fun-IVCM showed an increase in putative intraepithelial T-cell motility up to 6 weeks posttreatment (P < 0.001), followed by a reduction at 12 weeks (P = 0.03) that was accompanied by reduced probing of putative dendritic cells (P = 0.049), relative to baseline.ConclusionsThe ETX-4143 device showed a favorable safety profile, including preserved corneal nerve morphology, while improving pain outcomes and modulating corneal immune dynamics up to 12 weeks posttreatment, acknowledging these findings are exploratory. Randomized controlled trials are warranted to evaluate treatment efficacy and mechanisms.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier, NCT07059754.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40123-026-01454-6.

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Chronic ocular surface pain (COSP) is a debilitating condition, and a safe and effective treatment remains an unmet need as symptoms can be severe, persistent, and inadequately relieved by standard therapies.This prospective, feasibility, paired-eye clinical trial sought to investigate whether unilateral treatment with the ETX-4143 ophthalmic cooling device could reduce pain symptoms while preserving ocular surface structure and function, including corneal nerve and immune cell features, in individuals with COSP.What was learned from the study?In the treated eye, ocular pain was significantly reduced at 2, 6, and 12 weeks posttreatment, with no significant negative impact on clinical ocular surface measures throughout the 12-week study.Increased in vivo motility of putative corneal intraepithelial T cells at 2 and 6 weeks posttreatment, indicative of heightened tissue surveillance, was followed by an attenuation in both T-cell motility and the probing behaviors of putative dendritic cells at 12 weeks posttreatment in the study eye, suggesting a corneal immunomodulatory effect, although the findings are exploratory and should thus be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.While these findings provide initial evidence to support the clinical safety and efficacy of the ETX-4143 investigational cooling device, randomized controlled studies are required to further evaluate the clinical effects, durability, and mechanisms of treatment.

Introduction

Chronic ocular surface pain (COSP) is a debilitating condition that commonly presents as spontaneous ocular burning, irritation, dryness, foreign body sensation, and/or evoked pain to wind or light that persists for at least 3 months [1]. Although it often arises in the setting of ocular surface disease, growing evidence suggests that COSP may also involve peripheral and central somatosensory dysfunction, helping explain why symptoms can be severe despite limited ocular surface signs, and why conventional dry eye therapies may be inadequate in some patients. In individuals whose symptoms outweigh clinical signs, COSP has also been described as “corneal pain without stain,” recognizing that ocular pain severity is often disproportionate to ocular surface findings [2]. While the prevalence of COSP remains ill defined, it is increasingly recognized as a significant morbidity, with a recent survey of ophthalmologists and optometrists in specialty clinics estimating that up to one-third of their patients may present with COSP symptoms [3]. The quality of life of affected individuals is often severely impacted, including detrimental effects on mental health, social life, and work productivity [1]. However, the mechanisms underlying COSP remain poorly understood, with high rates of dissatisfaction among patients and eye care practitioners reflecting often inadequate control of the condition with current treatment options [3, 4]. A safe and effective treatment for COSP remains a major unmet need.

The use of cold or freezing temperatures has been proposed as a potential avenue to address chronic pain elsewhere in the body, such as in the prevention of chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy [1], as well as the treatment of chronic peripheral neuropathy [2] and knee pain [3]. In the eyes, cyclocryotherapy has been used for treating intractable glaucoma through the application of a cryoprobe (at −60 °C to −85 °C) at multiple positions posterior to the limbus, which targets the ciliary body to reduce aqueous humor production [4]. More recently, topical cooling devices (with temperatures ranging from −5 °C to −20 °C) applied to the conjunctiva posterior to the limbus for 10–20 s have been shown to provide acute pain relief after intravitreal injections [5, 6]. Application of a gel packet (−20 °C) over a gauze on the closed eye has also been shown to reduce discomfort and intraocular inflammation following cataract surgery compared with the contralateral untreated eye [7].

The topical ophthalmic cooling device (ETX-4143 device) investigated in this study has been shown to be nonirritating, nonsensitizing, and not cytotoxic through in vitro and in vivo biocompatibility studies (unpublished data). A preclinical study on rabbit eyes demonstrated minimal impact on corneal epithelial wound healing, conjunctival redness, and tear volume when a modified version of the device was applied to the bulbar conjunctiva about 1–2 mm posterior to the limbus at 3 o’clock and 9 o’clock overlying branches of the long ciliary nerves for 10 min [8]. Temporary cooling of these nerve branches, which are responsible for sensory innervation of the ocular surface, is hypothesized to induce reversible inhibition of sensory nerve signaling and a reduction in the release of neuropeptides, such as substance P and calcitonin-gene-related peptide (CGRP); these neuromediators have been implicated in neuroinflammation and corneal pain [8]. A double-masked, randomized controlled trial, following a first-in-human study, showed that treatment with the device was well tolerated when applied on-eye for 4 min, with sustained reductions in eye pain for up to 8 weeks posttreatment [9]. In the present clinical trial, we sought to further investigate the safety and efficacy of the device in treating COSP while assessing potential changes to corneal sensory nerve and immune cell features.

Methods

Study Design and Study Population

The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of St Vincent’s Hospital (085/25), registered prospectively on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07059754), and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent to participate. Participants were aware of the study hypothesis that the topical cooling treatment is intended to address and treat COSP. The study was conducted from 26 August 2025 to 4 February 2026. Eligible participants were aged between 22 and 90 years of any sex, with documented COSP symptoms (e.g., ocular burning, stinging, light sensitivity, foreign body sensation, discomfort, or pain) for at least 3 months. A score of ≥ 5 out of 10 on the Eye Pain Severity Module of the Chronic Ocular Pain Questionnaire (COP-Q), and more than 50% improvement in eye pain score on a visual analog scale (VAS, range 0–10) after topical 0.5% proparacaine hydrochloride at the screening visit, were required for eligibility. To be eligible, participants also needed to be willing to forgo participation in any other clinical studies during their participation, and to demonstrate sufficient cognitive awareness to comply with the study process. As this was a feasibility study, a sample size of five participants was chosen without specifying an inferential statistical power.

Exclusion criteria included known hypersensitivity to aluminum or topical anesthetic; COSP symptoms directly related to contact lens wear that were completely ameliorated by contact lens removal; active ocular infection or inflammation; history of any of: corneal transplant, significant corneal endothelial disease, corneal thinning, corneal ectasia, keratorefractive surgery (such as laser assisted in situ keratomileusis [LASIK] or photorefractive keratectomy [PRK]), herpetic keratitis, nonhealing corneal epithelial defects, neurotrophic keratopathy due to corneal herpetic disease, stem cell deficiency, diabetic keratopathy, severe lagophthalmos, topical anesthetic abuse, or any other cause; significant corneal or conjunctival surgery (such as pterygium or scleral buckle surgery) within the past 90 days; or presence of any of: subconjunctival implant, planned eye surgery during the entire study period, participation in any clinical study of an investigational product within 30 days prior to enrollment, neuropsychiatric disorder (such as psychosis, schizophrenia, mania, or depression) or major psychiatric illness that required hospitalization within the last 6 months prior to screening, significant corneal scarring that could limit in vivo confocal microscopy (IVCM) imaging and analysis, an insufficient number of corneal nerves to enable functional-IVCM (Fun-IVCM) corneal imaging, and any history of serious, poorly controlled systemic or ophthalmic condition or circumstances, including those identified in the screening that could compromise the subject’s ability to comply with the protocol or that could compromise the subject’s safety or the interpretation of the clinical trial results.

Testing Protocol

Participants attended eight study visits (Vs) across two locations in Melbourne, Victoria, Australia (Sunshine Eye Surgeons: baseline [V1a], treatment visit [V2], 1 week posttreatment [V3], and 12 weeks posttreatment [V6a]; the University of Melbourne: baseline [V1b], 2 weeks posttreatment [V4], 6 weeks posttreatment [V5], and 12 weeks posttreatment [V6b], Fig. 1). A topical anesthetic challenge test was performed at V1a, with the percentage change (pre- versus post-anesthetic) in VAS eye pain score calculated. The eye with more severe COSP symptoms was allocated to treatment (study eye); the fellow eye was the untreated control. Eye pain (assessed with the COP-Q Eye Pain Severity Module [range 0–10] and VAS [range 0–100] questionnaires, administered by unmasked investigators), distance best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), tear osmolarity, slit lamp biomicroscopy (to grade eyelid erythema, eyelid edema, bulbar conjunctival erythema, bulbar conjunctival hyperemia, corneal transparency and corneal neovascularization using a standardized clinical grading scale and FDA-accepted format: 0: none, 1: mild, 2: moderate, 3: severe), anterior chamber inflammation (SUN grading scale [range 0–4+ for cells and flare]), corneal fluorescein staining (Oxford grading scale, range 0–5) [10] (including the presence of late “whorl-like” staining for limbal stem cell dysfunction [11]), and static IVCM and Fun-IVCM were conducted at V1b, V4, V5, and V6b. As additional safety measures, bulbar conjunctival Lissamine green staining (Oxford grading scale, range 0–5) [10], Cochet Bonnet esthesiometry (range 0.4–15.9 g/mm2), gonioscopy (using the Spaeth grading system), and intraocular pressures (using Goldmann applanation tonometry) were assessed bilaterally at V1a, and in the study eye at V3 and V6a. Treatment Device and Procedure

The ETX-4143 device is a handheld investigational device that contains two metallic probes, internally cooled by a −20 °C fluid mixture of purified water and glycerol. For the study treatment, the device was applied to the bulbar conjunctiva, posterior to the limbus, at the 3 and 9 o’clock positions for 4 min, avoiding direct contact with the cornea. These positions were targeted in recognition that the long ciliary nerves primarily branch and enter the corneoscleral limbus to innervate the cornea at these locations [12]. Topical anesthetic eye drops (oxybuprocaine minims 0.4%) and gel (preservative-free lidocaine gel 2%) were administered prior to ocular placement of the device. A custom speculum was used to open and insulate the eyelids and facilitate correct device positioning. A corneal shield was applied to maintain ocular lubrication during treatment. Slit lamp biomicroscopy and bulbar conjunctival Lissamine green staining were performed prior to, and immediately after, treatment at V2, as safety assessments.

Corneal In Vivo Confocal Microscopy

Bilateral corneal IVCM imaging was performed using the Heidelberg Retinal Tomograph-3 with Rostock Corneal Module (Heidelberg Engineering GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany). For static imaging, scans were taken at the level of the central corneal epithelial (sub-basal) nerve plexus. A total of 8–12 well-focused, non-overlapping scans (400 µm × 400 µm) per eye were analyzed for the density of immune cells, comprising putative T cells and dendritic cells. Sensory nerve parameters, comprising corneal nerve fiber length (CNFL, mm/mm2), nerve fiber density (CNFD, fibers/mm2), nerve branch density (CNBD, branches/mm2), total branch density (CTBD, branch points/mm2), nerve fiber area (CNFA, mm2/mm2), nerve fiber width (CNFW, mm/mm2), and nerve fractal dimension (CNFrD) were quantified using ACCMetrics software (University of Manchester, Manchester, UK) [15]. Volume scans were obtained at the temporal and nasal peripheral cornea (three scans per side) and limbus (four scans per side); the presence of stromal nerves was subjectively assessed.

Corneal Fun-IVCM was performed, per published protocols [16, 17], to quantify the morphodynamic features of corneal immune cell subsets (Table 1), as exploratory endpoints. IVCM sequence and volume scans were acquired at the whorl and inferior cornea, over four to five consecutive timepoints, spaced 3–8 min apart. Time-lapsed videos were created using custom MATLAB software (Version R2023a; The MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) [17]. Immune cell subsets were classified on the basis of their characteristic morphometry and motility [16], with putative T cells as small, motile, non-dendriform cells (herein referred to as T cells), putative dendritic cells (DCs) as larger, relatively sessile cells that extend and retract their dendrites (herein referred to as DCs), and putative stromal macrophages as ameboid-shaped cells in the anterior stroma that shift their cell conformation around static keratocytes (herein referred to as macrophages). As these immune cell subsets are defined in vivo on the basis of validated morphodynamic characteristics, given the inability to undertake molecular phenotyping, the term “putative” (i.e., inferred) is used to describe immune cell subsets quantified using Fun-IVCM. Corneal immune cell morphodynamics were quantified from Fun-IVCM videos using manual cell tracing and tracking in ImageJ by a single masked observer (J.C.B.C. or A.C.), following masking of image sequences with custom MATLAB code. Quantified parameters included T-cell motility metrics (mean instantaneous speed and meandering index), DC attributes (cell perimeter, field area, and dendrite surveillance extension and retraction cycling habitude [dSEARCH] index), macrophage features (cell perimeter, field area, and dancing index), and immune cell subset densities. Interobserver reliability has been demonstrated in a prior study for manual analysis of immune cells, namely stromal macrophage density (ICC = 0.94, 95% CI 0.854–0.976) and field area (ICC = 0.94, 95% CI 0.870–0.967) [13]. Statistical Analysis

Paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, informed by the normality of the data using Shapiro–Wilk tests, were conducted to compare eye pain symptom scores between visits. These tests were also used to assess the change from baseline at each follow-up visit for: BCVA, slit lamp biomicroscopy signs, tear osmolarity, and corneal nerve parameters for the study and fellow eyes, and bulbar conjunctival Lissamine green staining, corneal sensitivity, and intraocular pressures for the study eye only. Cell-level data were derived by pooling immune cells sampled from both the whorl and inferior cornea to maximize available data capture, except for cell densities that were analyzed separately in corneal regions. These data were then analyzed using linear (for parametric data) or generalized (for nonparametric data) mixed-effects models with a random intercept for participant to account for repeated measurements. Fixed effects included treatment, study visit, and their interaction, and pairwise comparisons were performed using estimated marginal means with Holm adjustment for multiple testing. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Immediately Pre- and Post-intervention

Pre-intervention with the investigational device at V2, temporal and nasal bulbar conjunctival Lissamine green staining were noted in the study eye of four participants (two participants with grade 3 nasal and temporal staining, one participant with grade 2 nasal and temporal staining, and one participant with grade 1 nasal and grade 2 temporal staining). These gradings remained unchanged immediately following intervention with the investigational device. Intervention-related adverse events in the study eye were observed in four participants and included bulbar conjunctival inflammation, characterized by mild chemosis and hyperemia. Mild corneal edema was reported in two participants, increased corneal fluorescein staining (by at least one grade) in two participants, and a mild central corneal abrasion in one participant, all of which resolved by V3 (1 week posttreatment). Per protocol, all participants received topical corticosteroid eye drops (dexamethasone 0.1% or prednisolone acetate 1.0%, four times daily for 24 h) following the intervention. The adverse events resolved without further treatment (Supplementary Table S1).

Ocular Pain

Relative to baseline, ocular pain, measured with the COP-Q Eye Pain Severity Module, was significantly lower at 2 weeks (mean change −4.4 units, 95% CI −7.4 to −1.4, P = 0.02, range 0–10), 6 weeks (−5.0 units, 95% CI −7.0 to −3.0, P = 0.002), and 12 weeks (− 5.8 units, 95% CI −7.8 to −3.8, P = 0.001) posttreatment (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table S2). VAS eye pain scores were also lower at 2 weeks (−52.0, 95% CI − 99.8 to −4.2, P = 0.04) and 12 weeks (−62.0 units, 95% CI −91.3 to −32.7, P = 0.004, range 0–100) posttreatment (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table S2).Fig. 2Scatterplots showing the mean and standard deviation (SD) for eye pain symptom scores measured with a the Eye Pain Severity Module of the Chronic Ocular Pain Questionnaire (COP-Q) and b visual analog scale (VAS), at baseline (V1a), 2 weeks posttreatment (V4), 6 weeks posttreatment (V5), and 12 weeks posttreatment (V6b). Clinical Ocular Surface Measures

There were no significant changes to clinical ocular surface signs, including late corneal fluorescein staining, tear osmolarity or BCVA, in either eye during the study (Supplementary Table S3). Bulbar conjunctival Lissamine green staining, corneal esthesiometry, and intraocular pressures, assessed in the study eye, did not change over the study duration (Supplementary Table S4). Gonioscopy revealed no changes at either follow-up visit (V3 and V6a). Dilated retinal fundus examination showed no posterior ocular changes in the study eye at 12 weeks posttreatment (V6a).

Corneal Nerve Parameters

Relative to baseline, most corneal nerve parameters, including CNFL, CNFrD, CNFA, and CNFW, remained stable in both the study and fellow (control) eyes over the 12-week follow-up period. At 12 weeks posttreatment, increases in CNBD and CTBD were observed in the study eye, and in CNFD in the fellow eye (Fig. 3). Discussion

This paired-eye feasibility clinical trial evaluated a novel topical ophthalmic cooling device for treating COSP. While sustained improvements in ocular pain were observed up to 12 weeks posttreatment, these findings should be considered exploratory in nature given the small sample size of this feasibility trial. Nonetheless, such reductions in ocular pain are consistent with prior studies showing that transient, topical application of cold temperatures to the bulbar conjunctiva can reduce ocular pain in settings such as intravitreal injection [10] and cataract surgery [11], although investigations of those effects were limited to the immediate peri-procedural period. The hypoalgesic effects of cooling are thought to involve reduced sensory nerve signaling, potentially through decreased nerve conduction velocity and modulation of ion channel activity [18–20]. However, the mechanisms by which localized cooling produces sustained reductions in COSP remain incompletely understood.

The safety of a modified version of the investigational device used in the current study has been established in preclinical studies, with findings for a stable corneal epithelium, tear production, and intraocular pressures in treated rabbit eyes [8]. The current clinical feasibility study involving adults with COSP lends further support to the device’s positive safety profile. In particular, stability was observed for multiple clinically meaningful safety endpoints, defined by the absence of late corneal staining (that can indicate limbal stem cell dysfunction), stable corneal mechanosensitivity thresholds, and intraocular pressure measures, as well as the absence of any adverse ocular surface, gonioscopic, or retinal funduscopic findings up to 12 weeks posttreatment. Only mild transient conjunctival inflammation was noted immediately following treatment in four participants, mild corneal edema and increased fluorescein staining in two participants, and a mild corneal abrasion in one participant, all of which resolved without sequelae.

Corneal epithelial sensory nerve architecture, assessed using IVCM, was also maintained following treatment in both eyes. Central CNFL, a well-established indicator of corneal nerve health and a clinically relevant marker of nerve injury [14–16], remained stable in both the study and fellow eyes over 12 weeks. At baseline, CNFL values in two study eyes and two fellow eyes (a pair from the same participant, and the other study eye and fellow eye from separate participants) were below published normative thresholds [17], consistent with prior reports of reduced corneal nerve parameters in patients with COSP, suggestive of underlying neural dysfunction [18, 19]. The involvement of morphological disruption and dysfunction of corneal nerves in the development or progression of COSP requires further investigation.

Small increases in CNBD and CTBD were observed at 12 weeks posttreatment in the study eye. While such changes have been associated with corneal nerve regeneration in both preclinical and clinical settings [20–22], their clinical significance remains uncertain, particularly given the known variability and lower repeatability of these parameters compared with CNFL [23]. Nevertheless, the observed stability in both corneal nerve structure (from IVCM analyses) and function (from corneal esthesiometry thresholds) in the present study support the overall clinical safety profile of the investigational device.

The current study also provides early insight into the in vivo modulation of corneal immune cells following treatment with the device, although it should be noted that these findings are exploratory. Ocular surface inflammation and neuroinflammation are thought to contribute to the pathophysiology of COSP [24]. In addition to immune cell density, we evaluated longitudinal changes in corneal immune cell morphodynamics as potential markers of immune activity [25]. In the study eye, corneal T-cell motility increased at earlier timepoints (up to 6 weeks) and subsequently decreased below baseline (at 12 weeks posttreatment), suggesting a biphasic response. Increased T-cell motility has been associated with heightened immune surveillance in inflammatory states [25–27], while normalization of these dynamics has been observed following antiinflammatory treatment and may parallel symptomatic improvement [25]. In parallel, a reduction in DC dSEARCH index was observed at 12 weeks posttreatment, reflecting decreased cell probing activity. This finding occurred alongside a reduction in T-cell meandering index, consistent with more confined immune cell movement. Together, these findings suggest a potential shift toward reduced immune activation over time and support the notion that corneal immune cell dynamics may serve as biomarkers of therapeutic response. However, these observations require validation. As several immune cell parameters were evaluated, the possibility of residual type I errors cannot be excluded. Further research into the physiological variability of corneal immune cell morphodynamics, and the influence of potential variations in the actual corneal area imaged between visits, should also be considered.

Baseline inter-ocular differences may also provide insight into corneal immunological characteristics in COSP. The study eye had a higher corneal T-cell meandering index and lower anterior stromal macrophage density compared with the fellow eye, suggesting altered immune features in the more symptomatic eye. Changes observed in the fellow eye, including reduced macrophage density at 6 weeks posttreatment and decreased T-cell motility at 12 weeks posttreatment, may reflect a broader immunomodulatory effect. These findings raise the possibility of inter-eye crosstalk, potentially mediated through shared corneal–trigeminal neuroinflammatory pathways [28], although this remains speculative.

Compared with systemic agents (such as gabapentin [29] and pregabalin [30]) and topical treatments (such as autologous serum tears [18] and scleral lenses) for managing COSP, the ETX-4143 device may more specifically modulate corneal sensory nerve signaling via its localized application of cold temperature; however, as a hypothesis, this is speculative. While cenegermin, a recombinant human nerve growth factor (rhNGF) solution, was primarily developed to treat neurotrophic keratitis, defined by corneal nerve damage that leads to a loss of corneal sensation and tissue breakdown [31], it has recently been suggested that this intervention may be useful for treating COSP due to its neuroregenerative properties [32]. A recent study found that an 8-week course of cenegermin, dosed six times daily, healed persistent epithelial defects or corneal ulcers in 84.6% of 26 participants [33]. As not captured specifically in the current study, it would be valuable for future research to fully document participants’ prior COSP treatments to comprehensively define participants’ baseline clinical profiles in the context of their responsiveness to investigational treatments.

As expected, the small sample size of this feasibility study limited our ability to draw definitive conclusions in relation to treatment efficacy or uncommon adverse events related to the investigational device. In addition, use of the fellow eye as an internal comparator (control) rather than a randomized sham control limited causal inference for symptom outcomes. Ocular surface injury, including sensory nerve dysfunction in mice models, has been shown to involve bilateral neuroinflammatory mechanisms [34]. Given this potential for contralateral eye effects, and as also supported by findings for immune cell changes in the fellow eye after treatment in the study eye, a separate sham-treated control group would provide a more robust assessment of treatment efficacy in future studies.

We also acknowledge that use of topical anesthetic eye drops prior to administration of the device intervention, and the prescription of corticosteroid eye drops to address posttreatment adverse events, may contribute to confounding. Nevertheless, while studies have shown that intensive and/or protracted topical anesthetic or corticosteroid treatment can modulate ocular pain responses [35, 36], for the present study these agents were applied short-term, for up to 24 h, thus minimizing potential confounding effects. Future studies could also consider participant stratification on the basis of neuropathic versus nociceptive pain phenotypes, and to include objective measures of tear immunomediators, to provide further insight into the immunomodulatory impact(s) of the device on the ocular surface. Ideally, study outcome assessors would also be masked to the assigned intervention.

Conclusions

The prospective, feasibility paired-eye clinical trial found that ocular surface integrity, including measures of in vivo corneal sensory nerve architecture, is maintained across the study duration following treatment with an investigational ophthalmic cooling device in individuals with COSP, with sustained improvements in eye pain symptoms at 12 weeks posttreatment. These data lend support to the safety of this modality as a potential therapeutic for COSP. Exploratory analyses indicated dynamic changes in corneal immune cell behaviors, characterized by an early increase in T-cell motility followed by attenuation of T-cell and DC activity at 12 weeks posttreatment. These observations raise the possibility of corneal immunomodulatory effects, although the exploratory nature of these findings is acknowledged. Evaluating real-time changes in the morphodynamics of distinct immune cell subsets in living human tissues is a unique capability in the cornea, which has the potential to be leveraged to define immunomodulatory effects in larger randomized clinical trials. Further evaluation of this novel device in larger, sham-controlled studies is warranted to assess its clinical efficacy, durability, and underlying mechanisms.
