TSPO governs bone-lipid homeostasis by redirecting BMSC differentiation via the PI3K/AKT/β-catenin pathway
Authors: Peng Zhang, Hongyu Zheng, Zhao Lin, Minjuan Zhang, Linhai Yang, Zhibo Deng, Chao Song, Hanhao Dai, Yibin Su, Rongsheng Zhang, Guoyu Yu, Jun Luo, Jie Xu, Fenqi Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe imbalance between osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) is a central pathological feature of osteoporosis (OP). The translocator protein (TSPO) is a multifunctional protein, yet its precise role in bone metabolism remains elusive. This study aimed to investigate the role and mechanism of TSPO in OP pathogenesis.MethodsWe integrated bioinformatic analyses of human and mouse OP-related datasets and validated TSPO expression in BMSCs from osteoporotic patients and mouse models. Gain- and loss-of-function experiments in human BMSCs (h-BMSCs) assessed the impact of TSPO on proliferation, senescence, migration, and lineage differentiation. RNA sequencing and mechanistic rescue experiments were employed to identify the involved signaling pathway. The therapeutic effect of Adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV-9)-mediated TSPO silencing was evaluated in ovariectomized (OVX) mice.ResultsTSPO was significantly upregulated in BMSCs from both OP patients and preclinical models. Functionally, TSPO overexpression suppressed h-BMSC proliferation, migration, and osteogenesis while promoting senescence and adipogenesis. Conversely, TSPO knockdown enhanced cellular fitness and osteogenic capacity. Mechanistically, TSPO functioned as a critical upstream regulator of the PI3K/AKT/GSK-3β signaling axis, suppressing the downstream phosphorylation cascade and ultimately inhibiting β-catenin-mediated osteogenic transcription. Crucially, local TSPO silencing in OVX mice effectively improved bone microarchitecture, enhanced bone formation, and reduced marrow adiposity, concomitant with the reactivation of the PI3K/AKT/GSK-3β/β-catenin pathway.ConclusionOur study identifies TSPO as a key pathogenic regulator that impairs osteogenesis by disrupting the PI3K/AKT/β-catenin pathway. Targeting TSPO presents a novel anabolic strategy for osteoporosis, potentially addressing the unmet clinical need for therapies that restore bone formation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-04948-z.Keywords: TSPO, Osteoporosis, BMSCs, PI3K/AKT/β-catenin signaling, Bone marrow adiposity

Background

Osteoporosis (OP) is a prevalent systemic skeletal disorder characterized by diminished bone mass, microarchitectural deterioration, and pathological bone marrow adiposity, leading to increased bone fragility and fracture susceptibility [1, 2]. With the aging of global populations, OP has emerged as a major public health concern worldwide, imposing substantial socioeconomic burdens [3, 4]. The pathophysiology of OP fundamentally stems from an imbalance between bone resorption by osteoclasts and bone formation by osteoblasts, accompanied by excessive fat accumulation in the bone marrow cavity. Although current anti-resorptive therapies can slow bone loss, agents capable of effectively restoring lost bone structure and reversing the fatty marrow microenvironment remain a critical unmet clinical need [5, 6].

The bone marrow microenvironment represents a highly sophisticated and dynamic system, whose homeostasis is maintained through intricate signaling crosstalk among various cellular components, including BMSCs, osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and adipocytes [7, 8]. As common progenitors of osteoblasts and adipocytes, BMSCs play a pivotal role in bone metabolism. The lineage commitment of BMSCs is a tightly regulated process, and an imbalance in their differentiation potential—characterized by suppressed osteogenesis alongside promoted adipogenesis—is now recognized as a central pathological mechanism underlying OP and associated bone marrow adiposity [9, 10]. This differentiation shift is influenced by various pathological stimuli within the aging or dysfunctional bone marrow niche [11].

TSPO is a ubiquitously expressed 18-kDa protein with well-established functions in cholesterol transport, steroid hormone synthesis, and mitochondrial regulation [12–14]. Evidence of elevated TSPO expression in age-related diseases [15] is particularly relevant given that aging constitutes a primary risk factor for OP [16]. Considering this association and TSPO’s broad influence on cellular functions, we sought to investigate its previously undefined role in bone metabolism and OP pathogenesis.

In this study, bioinformatic analysis of multiple osteoporosis-related datasets initially identified TSPO as a consistently upregulated gene. We subsequently validated its elevated expression in BMSCs from both osteoporosis patients and established disease models. Through systematic in vitro and in vivo functional experiments, we established that TSPO acts as a key molecular switch that impairs BMSC proliferation and migration, promotes cellular senescence, and redirects differentiation fate from osteogenesis to adipogenesis. Mechanistically, we demonstrated that TSPO exerts these effects by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT/GSK-3β/β-catenin signaling pathway. Furthermore, we provided proof-of-concept evidence that targeted silencing of TSPO in the mouse bone marrow cavity effectively alleviates osteoporotic phenotypes. These findings collectively position TSPO as a promising therapeutic target for anti-osteoporosis intervention.

Methods

Animals

C57BL/6 female mice were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Fujian Provincial Hospital. The animals were fed a standard diet, housed at 5 mice per cage in a designated room under a 12 h light-dark cycle, and provided with filtered water. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Fujian Provincial Hospital [IACUC-FPH-SL-20250221[0752]] and reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. The mice were anaesthetized by isofurane (792632, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) 2% inhalation during the experimental procedures, and euthanized through CO2 exposure at the end of the study.

Isolation, culture, and identification of primary BMSCs

Human bone marrow blood samples were obtained from patients undergoing elective hip replacement surgery at Fujian Provincial Hospital (Fuzhou University Affiliated Provincial Hospital). All experiments involving human samples were carried out in strict accordance with national guidelines and authorized by the Ethics Committee of Fuzhou University Affiliated Provincial Hospital (K2025-02-169). Informed consent was obtained from all subjects prior to their participation in the study.

Patient inclusion and exclusion criteria were as follows: Patients in the Osteoporosis (OP) group were enrolled based on a clinical diagnosis of primary osteoporosis, confirmed by a pre-operative Bone Mineral Density (BMD) T-score of ≤ − 2.5 at the lumbar spine or femoral neck, in accordance with World Health Organization criteria [17]. Patients in the Non-Osteoporosis (Non-OP) group were included if they were diagnosed with femoral neck fracture due to acute trauma and had a BMD T-score > − 1.0, indicating normal bone density.

Key exclusion criteria for both groups encompassed: (1) secondary osteoporosis caused by malignancies, hyperparathyroidism, chronic renal failure, or long-term use of glucocorticoids or other medications known to affect bone metabolism; (2) presence of other metabolic bone diseases (e.g., Paget’s disease, osteomalacia); (3) severe chronic comorbidities, including hepatic or renal dysfunction (e.g., eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²), uncontrolled diabetes (HbA1c > 7.5%), or autoimmune diseases; (4) a history of previous surgery or radiation therapy on the affected hip; and (5) active local or systemic infection.

Patient demographics, including age, Body Mass Index (BMI), and BMD, are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Based on the above criteria, samples were assigned to the Non-OP or OP group for all subsequent analyses.

Primary human BMSCs (h-BMSCs) were immediately isolated and purified from the human bone marrow blood samples using density-gradient centrifugation. Cells were resuspended in L-DMEM (PM150220, Procell, China) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (16000044, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (1541, Gibco, USA), then seeded into 25 cm² flasks and cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. After 48 h, non-adherent cells were removed by medium replacement. The medium was replaced every 3 days. Upon reaching 80–90% confluence, h-BMSCs were detached using 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA Solution with Phenol Red (C0203, Beyotime, China), reseeded into new flasks (passage 1, P1), and expanded. Cells from passages 3–5 (P3-P5) were used for subsequent experiments.

Primary mouse BMSCs (m-BMSCs) were isolated from C57BL/6 mice by removing the musculature and tendons from the tibiae. Bone marrow was aspirated from the medullary cavity using a syringe and transferred into complete MEM α (12571063, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The resulting cell suspension was seeded into 25 cm² flasks and cultured in the same medium. The medium was replaced every 3 days. Following expansion, m-BMSCs at passages 3–5 were used for subsequent experiments.

The surface marker expression of h-BMSCs was analyzed by flow cytometry. Briefly, cells were digested, and Fc receptors were blocked with a serum-containing buffer. After washing, cells were adjusted to a standardized concentration and aliquoted into flow cytometry tubes. Primary antibodies (diluted in wash buffer) were then incubated with the cells on ice in the dark. Following a final wash and resuspension in buffer, samples were analyzed using a FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). Data were processed with FlowJo software (v10.06; Tree Star, Inc, USA). The antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 2. All surface marker assays were performed in triplicate. Representative flow cytometry results for surface marker identification are presented in Fig. S1.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (C0038, Dojindo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells transfected with various lentiviral constructs were seeded into 96-well plates at 4 × 10³ cells/well. At each time point (days 1–10 post-seeding), 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 1 h. Subsequently, the absorbance of each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase(SA-β-gal)

SA-β-gal staining was performed using the Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (C0602, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were washed once with PBS, then fixed and stained using the reagents provided in the kit.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells and tissues using TRIzol reagent (15596026, Invitrogen, USA), and cDNA was synthesized from the extracted RNA using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (RR047Q, Takara, Japan), following the manufacturer’s protocols. RT-qPCR was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (RR820A, Takara, Japan) on a StepOnePlus™ real-time PCR system. GAPDH was used as an internal control. Gene expression was quantified using the 2 −ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences were listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Western blotting (WB) analysis

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with phosphatase and protease inhibitors (KGP2100, Keygen Biotech, China). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE on 12.5% or 10% (w/v) gels, depending on the target protein molecular weight, and subsequently transferred onto 0.45-µm PVDF membranes (88585, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) via wet transfer. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature. Each membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with the respective primary antibody diluted in TBST containing 5% BSA (or 5% non-fat dry milk, as appropriate). Following three washes with TBST (5 min each), the membranes were incubated with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted in TBST containing 5% non-fat dry milk (or BSA) for 1 h at 37 °C. After final washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with BeyoECL Plus substrate (P0018S, Beyotime, China) for 1–5 min at room temperature. Chemiluminescent signals were then captured and analyzed using an automatic digital imaging system (ChemiDoc XRS+, Bio-Rad, USA). The antibodies used were listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Micro-computed tomography (µCT)

Tibiae were dissected and scanned using a high-resolution µCT scanner (Inveon µCT, Siemens Healthcare, Germany) at a voltage of 46 kV and a current of 75 µA, with an isotropic voxel size of 9.0 μm. Three-dimensional reconstruction and morphometric analysis were performed using the Inveon Research Workplace software (version 4.2, Siemens Healthcare, Germany). The following morphometric parameters were measured: bone mineral density (BMD); bone surface/bone volume (BS/BV); bone surface/total volume (BS/TV); bone volume/total volume (BV/TV); trabecular number (Tb.N); trabecular thickness (Tb.Th); and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp).

Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis

IF staining was performed on fixed cells and paraffin-embedded sections as previously described [18]. After blocking with QuickBlock™ Immunofluorescence Blocking Buffer (P0260, Beyotime, China) for 15 min at room temperature, the samples were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, they were incubated with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ab150084, 1:500; Abcam, UK) and/or Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ab150077, 1:500; Abcam, UK) for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (ab188804; Abcam, UK). Fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (model information, if available).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

After fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, bone specimens were immersed in decalcifying solution (G1107, Servicebio, China) for 1 week and then transferred to 70% ethanol. The samples were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin-embedded bone tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. They were then incubated with secondary antibodies, followed by incubation with a DAB substrate kit (P0202, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and counterstained with hematoxylin to visualize nuclei. Images were captured using a microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

BMSCs differentiation

P3 h-BMSCs derived from Non-OP patients were selected for osteogenic or adipogenic differentiation.

For osteogenic differentiation, upon reaching 70% confluence, the cells were cultured in osteogenic induction medium (HUXMX-90021, OriCell, China), which was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

For adipogenic differentiation, h-BMSCs at 100% confluence were induced using an adipogenic differentiation kit (HUXMX-90031, OriCell, China). The differentiation protocol consisted of alternating culture with Medium A and Medium B, as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Specifically, cells were cultured in Medium A for 3 days, followed by 1 day in Medium B, to induce adipogenic differentiation.

Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining

Following osteogenic induction, ARS staining was performed to detect calcium nodules and mineral deposition. The cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (P1110, Solarbio, China) for 20 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS, the cells were incubated in ARS staining solution (G1038-100ML, Servicebio, China) for 15 min. Subsequently, unbound dye was removed by thorough washing with distilled water (3–5 times). Calcium nodules were visualized and imaged under bright-field illumination with an inverted microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) equipped with a digital camera system.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining analysis

For ALP staining identification, cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and washed again with PBS. Subsequently, they were incubated with alkaline phosphatase staining solution (C3250S, Beyotime, China) for 30 min at room temperature. After a final wash with PBS, the stained cells were visualized under an inverted microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Oil Red O (ORO) staining

ORO staining was conducted to evaluate lipid droplet formation. Briefly, after adipogenic induction, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and then stained with ORO staining solution (G1015, Servicebio, China) for 10 min at room temperature. Lipid droplet formation was observed and imaged under an inverted optical microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Lentiviral transfection

Lentivirus constructs for TSPO-overexpression (OE TSPO), control-overexpression (OE NC), TSPO-shRNA (sh TSPO), and control shRNA (sh NC) were obtained from Genechem Company (Shanghai, China). The sequences for TSPO-targeting shRNA and non-target control shRNA are provided in Supplementary Table 4. P3) h-BMSCs derived from Non-OP patients were selected for lentiviral transfection. Briefly, 5 × 10⁴ cells were plated in 12-well plates. Upon reaching 30–50% confluence, cells were transfected using HitransG Transfection Reagent P (Genechem, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To establish stably expressing cell lines, transfected cells were selected with 4 µg/mL puromycin (ST551-10 mg, Beyotime, China) for 10 days. The efficiency of transfection was assessed by RT-qPCR and WB.

5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) proliferation assay

EdU incorporation assay was performed to assess cell proliferation using a EdU Apollo® kit (C10310-2, RiboBio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, h-BMSCs transfected with various lentiviruses were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells/well. Upon reaching the appropriate confluence, cells were incubated with 100 µL of 10 µM EdU medium for 2 h at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and stained with Apollo® reagent (100 µL) for 30 min at room temperature. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. EdU-positive cells were visualized and imaged under a fluorescence microscope. The percentage of EdU-positive cells (relative to total DAPI-stained nuclei) was calculated to quantify cell proliferation.

Transwell assay

Transwell chambers with a pore size of 8.0-µm (TCS02024, BIOFIL, China) were placed in 24-well plates. Subsequently, 2 × 10⁵ h-BMSCs, starved in serum-free medium for 12 h, were suspended in 200 µL of serum-free medium and added to the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with 500 µL of medium containing 10% serum. After incubation for 24 h at 37 °C and 5% CO₂, the chambers were removed. Cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently wiped off with a cotton swab. The chambers were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.1% (w/v) crystal violet staining solution (C0121, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 10 min. Following three gentle washes with PBS to remove excess stain, the migrated cells on the lower surface of the membrane were visualized, photographed, and counted using an inverted microscope.

RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol™ Reagent (15596026, Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, USA) and further confirmed by RNase-free agarose gel electrophoresis to visualize 18 S/28S rRNA bands. After RNA quality verification, poly(A)+ mRNA was enriched from total RNA using Oligo(dT) magnetic beads. The enriched mRNA was fragmented and reverse-transcribed into cDNA with the NEBNext® Ultra™ II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (#E7530, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), following the strand-specific protocol. Purified double-stranded cDNA underwent end-repair, dA-tailing, and adapter ligation using Illumina TruSeq adapters. The ligation products were purified with AMPure XP Beads (1.0X) and amplified via PCR. The resulting cDNA library was sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. (Guangzhou, China).

For data analysis, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the DESeq2 package in R with thresholds of |log2FC| ≥ 1 and p-value < 0.05. Volcano plots of DEGs were generated from normalized count matrices using the R package ggplot2. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using the Metascape database (http://metascape.org/), covering Gene Ontology (GO) terms for Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), and Molecular Function (MF).

Design of animal experiments

Baseline samples (femora, tibiae) were collected from 5 untreated 12-week-old and 5 untreated 24-month-old male mice, which were sacrificed for tissue harvesting. Forty 12-week-old female mice were randomly divided into two groups using a random number table method: a Sham-operated group (Sham, n = 6) and an ovariectomized group (OVX, n = 18). Four weeks after surgery, the OVX mice (n = 18) were randomly assigned to three subgroups (n = 6 per subgroup): (1) OVX + PBS (Vehicle Control), (2) OVX + AAV9-sh-Scramble, and (3) OVX + AAV9-sh-TSPO.

Two types of AAV9 vectors were constructed by Genechem (Shanghai, China): AAV9-sh-Scramble (AAV9-eGFP-U6-sh-Scramble) and AAV9-sh-TSPO (AAV9-eGFP-U6-sh-TSPO). The sequences of AAV9-sh-TSPO and AAV9-sh-Scramble are listed in Supplementary Table 4. Intra-bone marrow delivery of virus was performed as previously reported [19]. Both AAV9-sh-Scramble and AAV9-sh-TSPO were diluted in sterile 1× PBS to a final titer of 5 × 10² vg/ml for injection. Starting at 4 weeks post-surgery, 5 µL of either AAV9-sh-TSPO or AAV9-sh-Scramble (diluted in PBS, 5 × 10² vg/ml) was injected directly into the tibial medullary cavity (intraosseous injection) twice, with a 2-week interval between injections.

Calcein double-labeling assay

To evaluate dynamic bone formation, mice were administered intraperitoneally with 25 mg/kg calcein (154071-48-4, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) at 8 and 2 days before euthanasia. Following euthanasia, bones were fixed and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 95%, and 100% ethanol), with each step lasting 24–48 h. Subsequently, the undecalcified bones with calcein double labels were infiltrated with and embedded in methyl methacrylate (MMA; M55909-25ML, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany). Longitudinal Sect. (5-µm thick) were cut using a microtome, mounted onto slides, and coverslipped. Calcein double labeling was visualized under a fluorescence microscope. The mineral apposition rate (MAR) was calculated as the distance between the double labels divided by the time interval between administrations (6 days).

Histology assessment

After euthanasia, bones were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 4 °C, decalcified in decalcifying solution (G1107, Servicebio, China) for 1 week at 4 °C, and embedded in paraffin. Longitudinal bone sections of 5-µm thickness were then prepared. H&E staining was performed using an Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining kit (C0105M, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0, GraphPad Software, USA). For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons post hoc test was applied. For comparisons between two groups, the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons involving multiple groups across different time points, a two-way ANOVA with Šidák’s multiple comparisons test was employed. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

TSPO expression is upregulated in h-BMSCs from osteoporotic patients

To identify genes associated with OP, we performed an integrated analysis of multiple publicly available transcriptomic datasets. We first intersected upregulated DEGs from three independent datasets: young versus aged mouse bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs; GSE116925), bone marrow from postmenopausal non-osteoporotic versus osteoporotic patients (GSE230665), and estrogen-treated versus untreated primary fetal mouse metatarsals (GSE1860104). This cross-dataset comparison yielded seven commonly upregulated candidate genes: TSPO, PDK4, DOCK1, PIK3R1, COL6A3, CRISPLD2, and HIF3A (Fig. 1A). To further narrow down candidates with high relevance to the bone marrow microenvironment, we examined their expression patterns in normal human tissues using GEO [20] and GeneCards databases. Among these seven genes, TSPO was uniquely and highly expressed in bone marrow (Fig. S2). Based on this distinct tissue-specific expression profile, its established roles in cellular senescence—a process intimately linked to OP pathogenesis—and the relative scarcity of functional studies on TSPO in the context of OP, we selected TSPO for subsequent experimental validation.

To functionally validate the relevance of TSPO in human OP pathology, we successfully isolated and cultured h-BMSCs from both Non-OP and OP donors. OP-derived h-BMSCs exhibited impaired growth, with a marked increase in fragmented cells and a failure to form typical swirling colonies (Fig. 1B). Consistently, CCK-8 assays showed that the proliferation activity of OP h-BMSCs was significantly decreased at all time points, peaking on day 8 (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, SA-β-gal staining revealed a significantly higher proportion of senescent cells in the OP group (Fig. 1D).

To explore the molecular changes underlying these phenotypic alterations, we assessed the expression of key regulators of cellular stress response. P53 (TP53) and P21 (CDKN1A) are known to be upregulated under pathological conditions to mediate cell cycle arrest and senescence [21–23], while PCNA is a nuclear protein positively associated with proliferation [24]. RT-qPCR analysis showed that OP h-BMSCs had significantly elevated mRNA levels of TSPO, P53, and P21, alongside downregulated expression of PCNA (Fig. 1E). WB and IF analyses further confirmed that TSPO and p53 protein levels were higher in OP h-BMSCs (Fig. 1F, G). Collectively, these findings suggest that TSPO upregulation is closely associated with the impaired growth and accelerated senescence of BMSCs in osteoporosis.

TSPO expression is elevated in mouse models of osteoporosis

We next validated the correlation between TSPO and OP in two established mouse models: OVX mice, a well-established model for inducing osteoporosis [25], and aged (24-month-old) mice, which frequently exhibit an osteoporotic phenotype [26]. µCT, a standard method for assessing bone microstructure [27], revealed a deteriorated trabecular structure in the tibiae of both OVX and aged mice. This was characterized by significantly decreased Trabecular Number (Tb.N), Bone Volume per Tissue Volume (BV/TV), and Trabecular Thickness (Tb.Th), as well as increased Trabecular Separation (Tb.Sp) (Fig. 2A). H&E staining further showed extensive adipocyte infiltration in the bone marrow of these models (Fig. 2A).

We then isolated m-BMSCs for in vitro analysis. RT-qPCR (Fig. 2B) and WB analyses revealed that TSPO expression was upregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels in m-BMSCs from OVX and aged mice (Fig. 2C, D). IHC (Fig. 2E) and IF (Fig. 2F) staining of tibial sections further confirmed elevated TSPO expression in the bone marrow stromal compartment. Collectively, these data demonstrate that TSPO upregulation is a conserved feature of osteoporosis across species and models.

TSPO is downregulated during osteogenic differentiation but upregulated during adipogenic differentiation of h-BMSCs

The core pathological mechanism of OP is the impaired osteogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs [28]. During osteogenic differentiation of h-BMSCs, as evidenced by increased matrix mineralization (ARS staining, Fig. 3A) and ALP activity (Fig. 3B), TSPO expression was progressively downregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3C, D). Notably, TSPO expression was significantly negatively correlated with that of RUNX2 (Fig. 3C, D), a key transcription factor that initiates osteoblast commitment and upregulates downstream osteogenic genes [29].

Another hallmark of OP is bone marrow steatosis, reflecting enhanced adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs [30]. Conversely, during adipogenic differentiation, confirmed by progressive lipid droplet accumulation (ORO staining, Fig. 4A) and upregulation of the master regulator PPARγ [31, 32] (Fig. 4B, C), TSPO expression was gradually increased at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 4B–D). These findings indicate that TSPO expression is inversely associated with osteogenesis and positively associated with adipogenesis in h-BMSCs.

TSPO regulates osteogenic differentiation via the PI3K/AKT/GSK-3β/β-catenin pathway

To elucidate the mechanism by which TSPO regulates BMSC fate, we performed RNA-Seq on control and TSPO-overexpressing (OE TSPO) h-BMSCs after 14 days of osteogenic induction. Principal component analysis revealed a clear transcriptomic separation between the groups (Fig. S4A, B), confirming the robustness of subsequent analyses. We identified 73 upregulated and 232 downregulated DEGs in TSPO-OE cells (Fig. S4C). GO enrichment analysis indicated that these DEGs were primarily involved in developmental processes, biological regulation, and cell differentiation (Fig. 7A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis further highlighted the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway as the most significantly enriched (Fig. 7B).

Based on this bioinformatic prediction and the well-established role of the PI3K/AKT/GSK-3β/β-catenin axis in stem cell fate determination [36, 37], we next investigated whether TSPO regulates this pathway. Western blot analysis showed that TSPO overexpression suppressed the phosphorylation cascade of PI3K, AKT, and GSK-3β, leading to decreased levels of total β-catenin. These suppressive effects were significantly rescued by the PI3K activator 740Y-P, and this rescue was reversed by the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 (Fig. 7C). Critically, this regulatory relationship was confirmed at the transcriptional level: RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that β-catenin mRNA levels mirrored the protein changes, being suppressed by TSPO overexpression, partially restored by 740Y-P, and suppressed again upon LY294002 addition (Fig. 7D).

Conversely, silencing TSPO (sh TSPO) produced the opposite effect, activating the pathway as evidenced by increased phosphorylation of PI3K, AKT, and GSK-3β, along with elevated β-catenin protein levels (Fig. 7E). Again, RT-qPCR analysis of β-catenin mRNA corroborated these findings, showing enhanced expression upon TSPO knockdown, which was then inhibited by LY294002 and subsequently rescued by 740Y-P (Fig. 7F). To directly assess the functional output of this axis, we evaluated β-catenin protein accumulation by immunofluorescence in both gain- and loss-of-function models (Fig. S4D, E). These results recapitulated the expression trends observed by Western blot and RT-qPCR and, importantly, demonstrated that these changes were reliably reversible by the same pharmacological modulators, further solidifying the causal link. Finally, functional rescue experiments confirmed that the pro-osteogenic effects of TSPO knockdown were dependent on this pathway, as they were abolished by LY294002 (Fig. S5).

In summary, we conclude that TSPO acts as a key upstream suppressor of the PI3K/AKT/GSK-3β/β-catenin signaling axis, inhibiting osteogenic differentiation at the level of gene transcription, protein phosphorylation, and functional effector accumulation.

Silencing TSPO alleviates osteoporosis in OVX mice

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of TSPO targeting in vivo, we employed an OVX mouse model that recapitulates key features of human osteoporosis [38, 39]. An AAV9 vector carrying TSPO-specific sh RNA (sh TSPO) was locally injected into the tibial bone marrow cavity (Fig. 8A). No local infection or mortality was observed following AAV9 administration. µCT analysis revealed that TSPO silencing significantly improved bone microarchitecture, as evidenced by increased Tb.N, BV/TV, and Tb.Th, along with decreased Tb.Sp (Fig. 8B, C).

Silencing TSPO enhances bone formation and repair via PI3K/AKT/GSK-3β/β-catenin signaling in vivo

We further assessed bone formation dynamics in OVX mice. Immunostaining for Osteocalcin (OCN) revealed a higher number of osteoblasts on the trabecular surface in the TSPO-silenced group (Fig. 9A). Calcein double-labeling showed a significantly increased MAR in TSPO-silenced mice (Fig. 9B), indicating enhanced bone formation. Western blot analysis of m-BMSCs isolated from these mice confirmed that the bone-protective effects of TSPO silencing were associated with activation of the PI3K/AKT/GSK-3β/β-catenin pathway (Fig. 9C). Together, these in vivo results demonstrate that silencing TSPO counteracts osteoporosis by activating this signaling pathway, thereby promoting osteoblastogenesis and inhibiting bone marrow adipogenesis.

Bone marrow cells associated with osteoporosis include BMSCs, osteoblasts, osteocytes, osteoclasts, and adipocytes. As aging progresses, the content of adipocytes in the bone marrow gradually increases, while the content of osteoblasts decreases, leading to a corresponding reduction in bone volume and ultimately OP. TSPO influences the osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation fate of BMSCs by regulating the PI3K/AKT/GSK 3β/β-catenin signaling pathway within them. Specifically, young BMSCs exhibit low expression of TSPO, high phosphorylation levels of PI3K, AKT, and GSK-3β, and a high content of t-β-catenin, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation. Conversely, aged BMSCs display high expression of TSPO, low phosphorylation levels of PI3K, AKT, and GSK 3β, and a high content of phosphorylated β-catenin, leading to a preference for adipogenic differentiation.

Discussion

The homeostasis of the bone marrow microenvironment is maintained through precise coordination among its cellular components—including BMSCs, osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and adipocytes—via paracrine signaling and direct interactions [40]. This network ensures a critical balance between bone formation and resorption, and its disruption drives pathological conditions such as OP and bone marrow adiposity [41]. BMSCs, as central players in this system, direct bone marrow health through their lineage commitment, a process highly sensitive to pathological cues within the niche [42]. In this context, our study identifies TSPO, a multifunctional protein primarily localized to the mitochondrial outer membrane, as a novel and critical regulator of BMSC fate in OP. For the first time, integrated multi‑omics and functional data systematically reveal that TSPO acts as a dysregulated “molecular switch” in OP and may serve as a potential biomarker by functionally targeting the PI3K/AKT/GSK‑3β/β‑catenin signaling axis (Fig. 10).

Schematic diagram of the mechanism by which bone marrow steatosis related OP and TSPO regulate the osteogenic adipogenic differentiation fate of BMSCs

Using combined bioinformatic and experimental approaches, we consistently observed TSPO upregulation in OP settings. Functional studies established its causal role: TSPO overexpression in human BMSCs recapitulated key OP‑like phenotypes—suppressing proliferation and osteogenesis while promoting senescence and adipogenesis—whereas TSPO knockdown produced therapeutic‑like effects. The central mechanistic finding of our work is that TSPO exerts this regulatory function by modulating the PI3K/AKT/GSK‑3β/β‑catenin signaling axis. Our data demonstrate that TSPO overexpression suppresses the downstream phosphorylation cascade (PI3K, AKT, GSK‑3β), leading to reduced transcriptional expression and protein accumulation of the core osteogenic regulator β‑catenin, as validated by qPCR and quantitative immunofluorescence. Importantly, these molecular alterations corresponded to impaired osteogenic differentiation, and the entire signaling cascade—from phosphoprotein levels to β‑catenin expression and functional osteogenic output—could be sequentially rescued by the PI3K activator 740Y‑P or inhibited by LY294002. This multilayered evidence supports a model in which TSPO contributes to the inhibition of the pro‑osteogenic PI3K/AKT/GSK‑3β/β‑catenin pathway in BMSCs.

A nuanced consideration is that TSPO‑mediated regulation of the PI3K/AKT pathway likely represents an indirect, functional interplay rather than a direct physical binding event. This perspective aligns with existing literature. For instance, in various pathological models such as glioma and myocardial ischemia‑reperfusion injury, TSPO has been reported to influence cellular processes such as apoptosis and autophagy by affecting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, yet no direct protein‑protein interaction between TSPO and either PI3K or AKT has been demonstrated [43]. The cellular biological basis further supports this notion: TSPO is predominantly localized to the mitochondrial outer membrane, where its core functions encompass cholesterol transport, maintenance of mitochondrial membrane potential, and regulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolism—all integral to mitochondrial homeostasis [44]. In contrast, activated PI3K is primarily located at the plasma membrane and endosomal membranes, where it catalyzes membrane phospholipid signaling to generate the second messenger PIP3, which recruits and activates downstream effectors such as AKT [45]. The marked differences in their subcellular localization and core biochemical functions strongly favor the hypothesis that their functional coupling occurs via intermediate signaling molecules or through inter‑organelle communication, such as mitochondria‑plasma membrane crosstalk. Therefore, the potent inhibitory effect of TSPO on the PI3K/AKT pathway observed in our study is more plausibly attributed to upstream cascades triggered by TSPO dysfunction.

Mechanistically, the inhibitory action of TSPO on the PI3K/AKT pathway is likely rooted in its core physiological and pathological functions within the mitochondrion. As a key regulatory protein on the mitochondrial outer membrane, aberrant TSPO expression directly disrupts mitochondrial energy metabolism, redox balance, and the initiation of apoptotic signals [46]. This provides two plausible pathways to explain its indirect mechanism of inhibition. First, the “Energy Deprivation Hypothesis”: TSPO dysfunction can lead to decreased efficiency of the mitochondrial respiratory chain and insufficient Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) synthesis, placing the cell in an “energy crisis” that cannot support PI3K/AKT signal transduction and the anabolic processes it drives. Second, the “Oxidative Stress Hypothesis”: TSPO malfunction readily induces mitochondrial ROS bursts. Excess ROS may directly cause oxidative damage to components of the PI3K/AKT pathway, such as PI3K and AKT, or activate its upstream negative regulators (e.g., PTEN), thereby indirectly suppressing pathway activity [47]. Consequently, TSPO may function as a “mitochondrial stress sensor,” perceiving and integrating states of mitochondrial functional disturbance and transducing this information into inhibitory signals that target key pro‑osteogenic signaling pathways in the cytosol, thereby coordinating cellular energy status with differentiation programs.

Notably, TSPO’s role in guiding BMSCs toward adipogenic differentiation and inhibiting osteogenesis aligns completely with the classical pathological features of bone marrow adiposity in OP [7]. Our in vivo findings unequivocally demonstrated the therapeutic potential of targeting TSPO. Localized silencing of TSPO in OVX mice not only reversed established osteoporotic bone loss—evidenced by restored bone microarchitecture (increased BV/TV and Tb.N), enhanced bone formation (increased MAR), and reduced marrow fat infiltration—but also confirmed activation of the PI3K/AKT/GSK‑3β/β‑catenin pathway in isolated mouse BMSCs.

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of our in vivo approach. Although local injection of AAV9-shTSPO minimized systemic exposure, potential off-target effects in adjacent tissues or low-level dissemination cannot be fully excluded. Moreover, heterogeneous distribution of the viral vector within the bone marrow microenvironment may lead to variations in transduction efficiency [48]. These issues represent notable challenges for the clinical translation of gene-based TSPO targeting. Nevertheless, pharmacological modulation of TSPO presents a promising alternative. Existing TSPO ligands, such as PK11195, FGIN-1-27, protoporphyrin IX, and Ro5-4864, have demonstrated direct effects on bone and marrow adiposity. PK11195 has been used to visualize bone turnover, with its binding levels correlating with bone metabolic activity [49]. In vitro studies on human osteoblasts indicate that TSPO ligands such as FGIN-1-27 and the endogenous ligand protoporphyrin IX can suppress osteoblast metabolism and induce cell death [50]. Furthermore, Ro5-4864 has been shown to reduce glucose uptake and ATP content in human osteoblasts, indicating a significant impact on bone-cell bioenergetics [51]. Of particular relevance to our findings, TSPO ligands have been reported to promote the adipogenic lineage differentiation of human mesenchymal stem cells [52]. This ligand-based strategy may help overcome the technical limitations associated with viral vector delivery.

From a clinical‑translation perspective, current mainstay anti‑osteoporotic drugs such as bisphosphonates primarily inhibit osteoclast activity but cannot effectively restore lost bone structure or correct the fatty marrow microenvironment [53]. In contrast, the TSPO‑targeting strategy proposed in this study represents a novel anabolic approach by promoting osteogenic differentiation of endogenous BMSCs while reducing marrow adiposity. The documented effects of TSPO ligands on osteoblast function and MSC adipogenesis suggest that pharmacological TSPO modulation could complement our genetic findings by similarly influencing the bone‑fat balance. This aligns with emerging therapeutic concepts aimed at rebalancing BMSC lineage commitment [54]. Looking forward, the development of TSPO‑specific small‑molecule inhibitors or improved gene therapies—potentially combined with established preventive measures such as physical exercise and adequate calcium/vitamin D supplementation—could contribute to an integrated management strategy for osteoporosis. It should be emphasized, however, that our study primarily provides mechanistic insights in a preclinical setting. Significant further work is required to optimize delivery strategies, assess long‑term safety, and validate efficacy in human systems before TSPO‑targeted therapies can be advanced toward clinical application.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study identifies TSPO as a critical regulator of bone-fat balance in osteoporosis. We provide evidence that TSPO suppresses PI3K/AKT/GSK−3β/β-catenin signaling, leading to impaired osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. The efficacy of TSPO silencing in a preclinical model highlights its therapeutic potential. Future work should focus on developing TSPO-targeted pharmacological agents and elucidating the full spectrum of its actions in bone metabolism to translate this discovery into a viable anabolic therapy for osteoporosis.
