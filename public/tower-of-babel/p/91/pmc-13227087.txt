Induction Serplulimab and Chemotherapy Followed by Chemoradiotherapy for Bulky Unresectable Stage III NSCLC: A Phase II Study (SUCCEED-01)
Authors: Chengxin Liu, Yang Du, Dan Han, Baosheng Li, Xiaomeng Liu, Bingjie Fan, Hongfu Sun, Chengrui Fu, Longyu Xu, Tianyuan Dai, Wei Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this single‐arm phase II study of patients with non–small cell lung cancer, induction therapy with serplulimab plus chemotherapy led to an objective response rate of 77.1% after chemoradiotherapy and reduced the radiation dose needed.

Introduction

Lung cancer constitutes the leading global cancer burden, with non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for approximately 85% of cases, and 20% to 35% of patients present with locally advanced stage III disease at diagnosis (1, 2). The landmark PACIFIC trial established concurrent chemoradiotherapy (cCRT) followed by consolidative durvalumab as the standard of care (SoC) in unresectable stage III NSCLC, demonstrating significant improvements in both progression-free survival [PFS; median 16.8 vs. 5.6 months; hazard ratio (HR), 0.52] and overall survival (OS; median 47.5 vs. 29.1 months; HR, 0.72; refs. 3, 4). Nonetheless, durable cure rates remain limited, evidenced by 5-year OS rates below 50%, even in cohorts in which more than half (52.9%) of the patients had stage IIIA disease. The GEMSTONE-301 trial confirmed this consolidation immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) strategy in Chinese patients (median PFS: 9 vs. 5.8 months; HR, 0.64; ref. 5), but the magnitude of the PFS benefit was substantially lower than that in the PACIFIC trial. This discrepancy is potentially attributable to the higher proportion of stage IIIB/IIIC disease in the GEMSTONE-301 trial (71.4% vs. 47.1%; refs. 3, 5). Patients with high tumor burden (T3–4 primary lesions and/or with multistation lymph node involvement) face specific challenges, including an increased risk of pneumonitis and inferior locoregional control after cCRT, potentially attributable to extensive radiation volumes and radiation-resistant hypoxic niches within bulky tumors (6, 7). Consequently, significant unmet needs persist in improving survival for unresectable stage III NSCLC, especially among patients with higher tumor burden.

Building upon the success of consolidation immunotherapy, the potential of administering ICIs prior to cCRT as an induction therapy is now being explored to further enhance patient outcomes. Induction immunotherapy may offer dual benefits by reducing preradiation tumor volume, which could enable smaller, safer radiation fields, and priming the tumor microenvironment to enhance the efficacy of subsequent cCRT and consolidation (7). The initial proof of concept for this approach comes from the AFT-16 trial (8), the first prospective study investigating atezolizumab administered both before and after cCRT in patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC. This study demonstrated significant clinical efficacy, with an objective response rate (ORR) of 66.2% and a median PFS of 30 months [95% confidence interval (CI), 15.8–not evaluable (NE)]. Despite these promising results, there remains a lack of prospective studies specifically focusing on induction immunotherapy combined with cCRT in bulky, unresectable stage III NSCLC.

Serplulimab, a humanized anti–PD-1, exhibits superior target affinity and has demonstrated robust clinical efficacy signals across multiple solid tumors, including significant efficacy in advanced NSCLC within the ASTRUM-004 and ASTRUM-002 trials (9). Based on the rationale for induction immunotherapy and the specific challenges posed by bulky disease, we conducted this single-arm, phase II trial to evaluate the feasibility, efficacy, and safety profile of induction serplulimab plus chemotherapy, followed by cCRT and serplulimab consolidation in bulky, unresectable stage III NSCLC.

Materials and Methods

Study design and patients

This was a prospective, single-center, single-arm, Simon two-stage phase II study (ChiCTR2500097379) enrolling patients with bulky, unresectable stage III NSCLC. The study aimed to evaluate the clinical feasibility, efficacy, and safety of induction serplulimab combined with chemotherapy, followed by cCRT and serplulimab consolidation. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong Province Cancer Hospital (SDZLEC2023-223-02), and the study was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent amendments. The trial protocol is available in Supplementary Table S1. All patients provided written informed consent before receiving study treatment.

Eligible patients had to meet the following inclusion criteria: age 18 to 75 years; bulky, unresectable, histologically confirmed stage III NSCLC [per the eighth edition of TNM Classification of Unio Internationale Contra Cancrum/American Joint Committee on Cancer]; Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS) 0 to 1; no prior systemic antitumor therapy; and an expected life expectancy of more than 6 months. A bulky tumor was defined as a primary tumor ≥5 cm in the longest diameter, a tumor with pulmonary atelectasis, or regionally involved lymph nodes ≥2 cm in the shortest diameter (7, 10). Unresectable stage III NSCLC is characterized by radiologic or pathologic evidence fulfilling any of the following criteria: ipsilateral multistation or bulky mediastinal nodal metastases (IIIA: T1–2N2; IIIB: T3–4N2), contralateral or supraclavicular/scalene nodal involvement (IIIB: T1–2N3; IIIC: T3–4N3), or tumor invasion of critical structures such as the heart, aorta, or esophagus (IIIA: T4N0–1; ref. 11). The initial bulky, unresectable status was established during a baseline pretreatment multidisciplinary team (MDT) comprised of a medical oncologist/pulmonary oncologist, thoracic surgeon, radiation oncologist, pathologist, and palliative care specialists at a minimum. Exclusion criteria included mixed histologic lung cancer, known EGFR mutations, anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) or ROS proto-oncogene 1 rearrangements, clinically significant autoimmune conditions, cardiovascular or pulmonary disease, concurrent systemic corticosteroid/immunosuppressant use, or prior thoracic radiotherapy history. The representativeness of the study population is detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Shandong Province Cancer Hospital (SDZLEC2023-223-02) and conducted in accordance with the Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. All patients provided written informed consent.

A total of 49 patients were enrolled in this study. Regarding sample processing, tumor tissue samples were processed immediately for pathologic diagnosis. Peripheral blood samples for lymphocyte analysis were also processed and analyzed fresh immediately after collection, without long-term storage.

Treatment and procedure

Patients received two cycles of induction immunochemotherapy, followed by restaging to assess treatment response. Based on restaging results, patients were assigned to subsequent definitive treatment, either cCRT or surgery, followed by consolidative immunotherapy (Fig. 1A). In the induction phase, patients received two cycles of serplulimab (Henlius Biopharmaceutical, Co. Ltd) 4.5 mg/kg via intravenous infusion on day 1 every 3 weeks, combined with pemetrexed (Shandong New Time Pharmaceutical, Co. Ltd) 500 mg/m2 [non–squamous cell carcinoma (non-SCC)] or nab-paclitaxel (Qilu Pharmaceutical, Co. Ltd.) 260 mg/m2 (SCC) plus cisplatin (Qilu Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd.) 75 mg/m2. Following induction therapy, patients received a mandatory MDT assessment to restage the disease and determine the subsequent phase of treatment. Patients without disease progression but remaining unresectable then proceeded to cCRT, receiving the prescribed dose of 60 Gy in 30 fractions, combined with two cycles of chemotherapy (pemetrexed 500 mg/m2 or nab-paclitaxel 260 mg/m2 plus cisplatin 75 mg/m2). Serplulimab consolidation (4.5 mg/kg, intravenous infusion, every 3 weeks) was initiated within 42 days after cCRT and continued for up to 12 months, unless disease progression or intolerable toxicities occurred. Following induction therapy, patients underwent a new CT simulation to account for anatomic changes, and the radiotherapy plans were entirely reoptimized using the updated imaging data to ensure optimal target coverage and organ-at-risk sparing. Intensity-modulated radiotherapy was delivered using Varian series linear accelerators (Varian Medical Systems). If surgical eligibility was confirmed after restaging after induction therapy, patients underwent resection, followed by adjuvant serplulimab combined with chemotherapy. Resectablity was defined as the feasibility of a curative-intent (R0) resection without pneumonectomy and systematic lymphadenectomy (harvest of ≥6 lymph nodes, including ≥3 from mediastinal and ≥3 from hilar/intrapulmonary stations; refs. 12, 13). These surgical patients were considered a separate exploratory cohort for feasibility analysis and were excluded from the primary efficacy endpoint assessment.

Study endpoints and assessment

The primary endpoints were the ORR after completion of induction immunochemotherapy and cCRT and changes in volume receiving ≥20 Gy of radiation (V20) before and after induction therapy. The secondary endpoints included disease control rate (DCR), duration of response (DoR), PFS, OS, and safety. Tumor volume changes, including gross tumor volume (GTV) and planning targeted volume (PTV), were assessed before and after induction therapy. Dosimetric parameters, such as mean lung dose (MLD), volume receiving ≥5 Gy of radiation (V5), and volume receiving ≥30 Gy of radiation (V30), were also analyzed before and after induction therapy. Tumor response was assessed by investigators every 6 weeks (±7 days) from the initiation of the study treatment until progression, using contrast-enhanced CT according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (RECIST v1.1). The ORR was defined as the proportion of patients achieving a complete response (CR) or partial response (PR), and the DCR was defined as the proportion of patients achieving a PR, CR, or stable disease (SD). The PFS was defined as the time from initiation of induction therapy to radiologically confirmed disease progression per RECIST v1.1 or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. The OS was defined as the time from the first dose of study treatment to death from any cause. The DoR was defined as the time from the first documented response (CR or PR) to disease progression or death from any cause. Adverse events (AE) were assessed according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0. All AEs occurring from the initiation of study treatment until 28 days after the last dose were recorded and followed until resolution, stabilization, loss to follow-up, or death. Additionally, longitudinal explanatory analysis of peripheral blood lymphocyte dynamics was performed in patients receiving radiotherapy, with data collected at four time points: before induction, after induction, and 1 and 3 months after radiotherapy. Peripheral blood lymphocyte analysis was performed using standard clinical flow cytometry assays at Shandong Cancer Hospital.

Statistical methods

The sample size was calculated using Simon’s optimal two-stage design for this phase II trial, with ORR as the primary endpoint. The ORR from historic patients receiving cCRT was estimated at 50% (14). We projected an ORR of 70% with the addition of induction immunochemotherapy, considering a 20% increase as clinically meaningful. With a one-sided α level of 0.05% and 80% power, 15 patients were enrolled and evaluated for stage I. If statistical criteria were met (≥9 CR/PR) in stage I, a further 28 patients would be enrolled in stage II, resulting in a total sample size of 43 patients. To account for an anticipated 15% dropout rate, a total of 49 patients were planned for enrollment. The treatment regimen would be considered sufficiently promising for further investigation if ≥27 CR/PR were observed among all patients. The results of stage I have been reported previously (15).

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2). Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages [n (%)], whereas continuous variables were described using mean ± SD or median (range), as appropriate. The 95% CIs for ORR were calculated using the Clopper–Pearson method. The Kaplan–Meier estimator was used for PFS, OS, and DoR, with median values and 95% CIs reported. Subgroup analysis of PFS stratified by key baseline characteristics was further conducted using univariate Cox proportional hazards model, with HRs and corresponding 95% CIs reported. The median follow-up duration was calculated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. Differences in tumor volume and dosimetric parameters before and after induction therapy were compared using either a paired samples t test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Longitudinal analysis of peripheral blood lymphocyte dynamics was performed using either repeated measures ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis H test, with Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons when the primary test result was significant. The comparison of peripheral blood lymphocytes between responders and nonresponders was performed using either an independent samples t test or the Mann–Whitney U test. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The individual patient clinical data generated in this study are not publicly available due to patient privacy requirements but are available upon reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Results

Baseline characteristics and treatment

A total of 49 eligible patients were enrolled, treated with at least one dose of study treatment, and analyzed (Fig. 1B). The baseline characteristics of study patients are summarized in Table 1. The median age of the cohort was 66 years (range, 43–75), with 33 (67.35%) patients aged 60 years or older. The majority of patients were male (93.88%), had a history of smoking (83.67%), and had an ECOG PS of 1 (91.84%). Most patients exhibited higher disease burden, with 61.22% of stage IIIB and 14.29% of stage IIIC. Thirty-six (73.47%) patients had an SCC.

Efficacy

Among 35 patients who completed cCRT, two patients achieved CR, and 25 achieved PR, resulting in an ORR of 77.14% (27/35; 95% CI, 59.86%–89.58%), which met the prespecified endpoint. The DCR was 91.43% (32/35; 95% CI, 76.94%–98.20%), including five patients with SD (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Subgroup analysis was further conducted across key baseline characteristics. As detailed in the forest plot (Supplementary Fig. S1), no statistically significant difference in treatment effect was observed across most subgroups, including age, smoking history, clinical stage, T stage, N stage, pathologic subtype, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and baseline GTV (all P values > 0.05).

In a sensitivity analysis that excluded five patients who received a supplemental third induction cycle, the ORR and DCR following cCRT were 74.19% and 90.32%, respectively (Supplementary Table S3). The corresponding PFS and OS analyses remained consistent with the primary analysis (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Changes in tumor volumes and pulmonary dosimetry

Significant reductions in target volumes were observed following induction therapy with serplulimab plus chemotherapy in the 35 patients who completed cCRT. Compared with baseline, the GTV decreased by 18.78% (127.85 ± 126.75 to 103.84 ± 108.55 cm3; P < 0.001; Fig. 4A), and the PTV decreased by 15.95% (330.49 ± 230.17 to 277.79 ± 217.04 cm3; P < 0.001; Fig. 4B). These reductions support the utility of two cycles of induction immunochemotherapy in bulky, unresectable stage III NSCLC.

Safety

The safety profile of patients who received at least one dose of study treatment was summarized in Table 3. Thirty-one (63.27%) patients experienced at least one AE, of whom seven (14.29%) patients had grade 3 AEs. The most common AEs were decreased white blood cell count (n = 14, 28.57%), pneumonitis (n = 14, 28.57%), and anemia (n = 12, 24.49%). A total of 23 (46.94%) patients reported an immune-related AE (irAE), with 10.20% (n = 5) experiencing grade 3 irAEs. The most common irAEs were decreased white blood cell count (n = 11, 22.45%), pneumonitis (n = 9, 18.37%), and anemia (n = 9, 18.37%). There were no serious AEs or AEs leading to treatment discontinuation. No patient experienced radiation pneumonitis, and one patient reported grade 2 radiation esophagitis. Additionally, the safety profile was manageable during induction treatment phase or cCRT phase (Supplementary Table S4).

Longitudinal dynamics of peripheral blood lymphocytes

Beyond volumetric and dosimetric improvements, we further investigated immune cell dynamics to explore potential systemic immunologic effects of the treatment regimen. Longitudinal analysis of peripheral blood lymphocytes was performed in 21 patients with complete paired samples across four time points: before induction, after induction, and 1 and 3 months after radiotherapy (Supplementary Fig. S3). Overall lymphocyte counts remained stable after induction therapy (P > 0.05) but showed significant suppression at both 1 month (P < 0.001) and 3 months (P = 0.003) after radiotherapy compared with preinduction levels. Among lymphocyte subsets, total T-lymphocyte levels demonstrated a progressive increase following induction therapy, with levels continuing to increase throughout the subsequent consolidation period after radiotherapy (P < 0.001). NK cells and B cells manifested transient, nonsignificant fluctuations following induction therapy and returned to preinduction levels during consolidation (all P values > 0.05). Neutrophils were significantly reduced after induction (P = 0.004) and 1 month after radiotherapy (P = 0.002), with recovery to near-baseline levels by 3 months after radiotherapy (P = 0.075). Although the NLR decreased significantly after induction (P = 0.009), 1- and 3-month postradiotherapy levels were comparable with baseline (all P values > 0.05).

Comparative analysis between patients who achieved ORR after cCRT and those who did not revealed distinct immune profiles (Supplementary Fig. S4). Although the non-ORR group had higher baseline total T-lymphocyte levels before induction, the ORR group maintained significantly higher levels at all subsequent time points (all P values < 0.05). NK cells were significantly higher in the non-ORR group after induction (P = 0.003) and 3 months after radiotherapy (P = 0.003), despite comparable baseline levels. No significant differences were observed between groups in total lymphocyte counts, B cells, neutrophils, or the NLR.

Discussion

The management of bulky, unresectable stage III NSCLC remains a significant clinical challenge. Although the PACIFIC regimen represents the current SoC, patients with large tumor volumes are at increased risk of treatment-related toxicity, suboptimal tumor response that may compromise radiotherapy delivery, and reduced opportunities for curative-intent therapy (6, 7). To address these limitations, we conducted this patient-centric phase II trial evaluating a treatment strategy of induction serplulimab plus chemotherapy followed by cCRT and serplulimab consolidation. This prospective study demonstrated a post-cCRT ORR of 77.14% and a 12-month PFS rate of 76.15%, alongside manageable toxicity. Notably, the induction phase resulted in significant tumor downsizing, with substantial volumetric reductions in tumor targets, and six patients achieved radical surgery (both were R0 resections). Further investigation into the incorporation of the induction immunochemotherapy approach is eagerly awaited, as it holds the potential to revolutionize the management of bulky, unresectable stage III NSCLC and improve the cure rate in this setting.

Our study demonstrated that two cycles of induction serplulimab–chemotherapy prior to cCRT achieved a favorable tumor response and encouraging survival outcomes (ORR: 77.14%; DCR: 91.43%; 12-month PFS rate: 76.15%; and 12-month OS rate: 90.54%). The reported efficacy outcomes represent promising early signals worthy of clinical attention, exceeding those reported in the AFT-16 trial data, in which four cycles of induction atezolizumab plus chemotherapy followed by cCRT yielded an ORR of 66.2% and a 12-month PFS rate of 68.9% (8). Similarly, the APOLO trial, using three cycles of induction atezolizumab–chemotherapy in unresectable stage III NSCLC, reported a 12-month PFS rate of 68.4% and a 12-month OS rate of 86.8% (16). Notably, our cohort comprised a higher proportion of stage IIIB/IIIC disease (75.51%) compared with the AFT-16 population (stage IIIA/IIIB disease), in which stage IIIB accounted for only 46.8%, making the observed efficacy particularly noteworthy. Additionally, although differences in induction agents and patient populations across studies must be considered, the number of induction cycles seems to be a key factor influencing tumor responses. This notion is supported by recent real-world and prospective studies. At the World Conference on Lung Cancer 2022, Wang and colleagues (17) specifically observed significant lesion shrinkage after the first two cycles of induction PD-1 inhibitor combined with chemotherapy compared with after more cycles (36.9% vs. 2.3%, P = 0.006) in 31 patients with large-volume, unresectable locally advanced NSCLC. Another real-world study reported similar findings, suggesting that more than two cycles of induction immunochemotherapy may not yield significantly additional benefits in tumor response (7). Consistent with our findings, a recent prospective study evaluated the efficacy of two-cycle induction toripalimab–chemotherapy in bulky, unresectable stage III NSCLC, achieving an ORR of 77.8% and a DCR of 96.3% (18). Collectively, these data support the concept that a two-cycle induction window may be sufficient to achieve meaningful tumor reduction while allowing timely transition to radical treatment, thereby minimizing the risk of disease progression.

Building upon the observed tumor response, the induction phase also conferred significant advantages for radiotherapy planning and delivery, a crucial consideration in bulky disease. Tsao and Louie (19) reported a case of large-volume, stage III (T4N3) NSCLC in which persistent failure to achieve adequate PTV coverage while respecting organ-at-risk constraints, despite multiple plan optimizations, highlighted the inherent challenges of bulky locally advanced tumors. Given this therapeutic impasse, multidisciplinary consensus has advocated induction immunochemotherapy (irrespective of PD-L1 status) followed by reassessment to optimize cCRT planning and overcome dosimetric limitations (19, 20). In our cohort, two cycles of immunochemotherapy produced substantial volumetric reductions across all target volumes (GTV and PTV), which translated into clinically meaningful dosimetric sparing of critical structures, particularly the lungs. Specifically, we observed statistically significant reductions in MLD and key volumetric parameters (V5, V20, and V30), those metrics well established as independent risk factors for radiation pneumonitis (21, 22). Consistent with these favorable dosimetric profiles, radiation-related toxicity was rare, with no patient experiencing radiation pneumonitis, and only one patient reported grade 2 radiation esophagitis. Overall, the toxicities were manageable. For patients with large baseline tumor burdens and heightened pulmonary risk, the capacity of induction immunochemotherapy to reduce and refine radiotherapy target volumes while sparing healthy lung tissue represents a major advantage of this approach, potentially improving both the safety profile and the therapeutic ratio of definitive cCRT.

The evolving therapeutic landscape for unresectable stage III NSCLC now extends beyond PACIFIC regimen consolidation. Intensified ICI consolidation represents one emerging strategy, although rational combinations require validation. For example, cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen 4 blockade has been hypothesized to counteract the immunosuppressive effects of irradiation on draining lymph nodes (23). However, in the BTCRC-LUN 16-081 trial, consolidation with nivolumab plus ipilimumab following cCRT in unresectable stage III NSCLC failed to improve survival over nivolumab alone (18-month PFS: 67% vs. 62.3%; median PFS: 25.4 vs. 25.8 months), challenging the anticipated therapeutic efficacy (24). Concurrent ICI with definitive chemoradiotherapy has been proposed to reduce treatment discontinuation during consolidation, as 5% to 30% of patients experience disease progression during cCRT (25). However, overlapping pulmonary toxicities from immunotherapy- or radiotherapy-induced pneumonitis and infectious pneumonia complicate clinical management (26). In contrast, induction immunochemotherapy before cCRT, as implemented in our study, leverages a distinct therapeutic window. This strategy engages an intact immune system for preradiation priming, unimpeded by the cytotoxic effects of concurrent chemotherapy and radiotherapy, as could be the case in an adjuvant approach (27). The approach mitigates radiotherapy planning challenges through rapid tumor downsizing and also leverages unimpaired immunity for early systemic control.

This patient-centric study incorporated MDT reassessment following induction immunochemotherapy to guide personalized treatment decisions. Induction immunochemotherapy demonstrated substantial potential for conversion to surgical resectability, enabling R0 resection in six patients who underwent surgery, with favorable pathologic outcomes (pCR in five and MPR in one). Recently, the TRAILBLAZER trial demonstrated that neoadjuvant SHR-1701 (an anti–PD-L1/TGFβ fusion protein) led to a remarkable surgical rate of 25% in unresectable stage III NSCLC, with all resected patients achieving R0 resection and 26% showing pCR (12). Additionally, a phase II study showed that the resectability rate reached 57.1% following four cycles of induction serplulimab combined with platinum-based doublet chemotherapy in unresectable stage IIIB–IIIC NSCLC (28). This conversion from unresectable to resectable status represents a potentially curative opportunity for a subset of patients traditionally managed with nonsurgical intent. However, the standard definition of resectable NSCLC remains uncertain, and the resectability after induction treatment was ultimately determined by MDT in our study, reflecting the scenario in real-world clinical practice (29, 30). Given the profound advances in neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy for resectable stage III NSCLC, as evidenced by the CheckMate-816 trial (31, 32), it provides compelling rationale for extending this strategy to unresectable disease, necessitating a widely endorsed, uniform definition of resectability to enable robust multicenter trial design and interpretable results.

The longitudinal dynamics of peripheral blood lymphocytes offer preliminary insights into the immunomodulatory effects of this multimodal regimen. Critically, the sustained elevation of T cells, key mediators of antitumor immunity, suggests a potential immune-activating effect of the induction phase that persisted despite subsequent radiotherapy, possibly contributing to the observed efficacy. Responders exhibited progressive T-cell enrichment despite having lower baseline levels compared with nonresponders. This counterintuitive observation suggests that the quality, rather than merely the quantity, of the preexisting T-cell pool is critical. The elevated baseline T-cell levels in nonresponders may paradoxically represent an immune contexture enriched in functionally exhausted or anergic T cells, which, despite their numerical presence, are unable to mount an effective antitumor response and are not sufficiently reprogrammed by the current therapy. This interpretation aligns with the established concept in oncology that a high burden of pretreatment T-cell exhaustion can limit the efficacy of immunotherapy (33). Thus, our data suggest that therapy-driven immunogenicity is most effective in patients with a more “receptive” baseline T-cell repertoire. Conversely, NK cells were enriched in nonresponders after induction and 3 months after radiotherapy, potentially reflecting compensatory innate immune activation in the absence of adaptive antitumor immunity. Additionally, significant neutrophil suppression was observed after induction and 1 month after radiotherapy, correlating with observed hematologic toxicities. However, by 3 months after radiotherapy, neutrophils had recovered to preinduction levels, indicating transient and manageable toxicities. Although these exploratory dynamics of lymphocytes in a limited cohort offer mechanistic hypotheses for the regimen’s immunomodulation, these preliminary findings warrant larger validation studies to map treatment-specific lymphoid evolution patterns definitively. The specific contributions and functional states of key T-cell subsets, such as CD8+ T cells and Tregs, remain to be dissected in future studies incorporating high-dimensional immunophenotyping and functional assays.

Despite the promising efficacy and manageable safety profile, this study has several limitations that necessitate cautious interpretation of those findings. The single-arm design and relatively small sample size limit definitive conclusions about efficacy compared with standard therapy. Although the surgical cohort demonstrated remarkable pathologic responses, these encouraging results must be interpreted cautiously due to the limited sample size and the need for further standardization of the criteria defining resectability after induction. Additionally, the absence of PD-L1 expression data represents another limitation, as it precludes the evaluation of potential correlations between biomarker status and treatment efficacy. The lack of this biomarker analysis restricts our ability to identify patient subgroups that may derive differential benefits from the therapeutic approach. This study provided promising preliminary survival benefits, but long-term studies are necessary to further determine the long-term benefits of this treatment strategy. Additionally, although the patient-centric study design closely mirrors real-world clinical practice and prioritizes patient needs, the findings necessitate validation through larger-scale prospective studies to establish statistical robustness.

Beyond the current limitations, our findings prompt consideration of this regimen’s role in broader contexts. Future studies should investigate its efficacy in specific populations, such as patients with EGFR-mutant or ALK-rearranged disease, and its potential to convert oligometastatic disease into a durable response. To validate this approach, a phase III trial directly comparing this induction strategy against standard cCRT is warranted, ideally incorporating biomarker-guided enrollment. Critical challenges, including optimizing therapy sequencing, managing immunoradiotherapy toxicities, and identifying robust predictive biomarkers, must be addressed in parallel to advance the treatment of stage III NSCLC.

In conclusion, this patient-centric exploratory phase II study demonstrated that adding induction serplulimab–chemotherapy before cCRT is a feasible and promising strategy for managing bulky, unresectable stage III NSCLC. The regimen achieved high response rates, provided significant dosimetric advantages for subsequent radiotherapy by reducing tumor volumes and lung irradiation parameters, and enabled successful downstaging and R0 resection with profound pathologic responses in a subset of patients. Although the limitations highlight the need for larger, randomized phase III trials with long-term follow-up, the current results provide a strong foundation for further investigation of this patient-centric induction strategy aimed at improving outcomes in this challenging disease setting.
