Distribution, pollution status and controlling factors of trace metal(loid)s in Yellow river Estuary and adjacent area
Authors: Weihan Yin, Mengmeng Zhang, Qingyun Yu, Xing Liu, Jing Li, Yingying Liu, Yuhao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study examined the spatial distribution, contamination status and controlling factors of trace metal(loid)s of the surface and bottom water, surface sediments in the Yellow River Estuary and adjacent area. The results indicated that the distribution of metal(loid)s in the surface and bottom water is relatively consistent, and high concentrations detected in the region extending from the Yellow River Estuary to the northwest of the study area. However, the concentrations of all metal(loid)s in the water bodies are below the limits specified for Grade I seawater issued by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment of China. In the surface sediments, high-concentration areas of metal(loid)s are mainly distributed in regions with fine-grained sediments, such as the Yellow River Estuary and the northern part of the study area. The assessment results obtained by using the Geo-accumulation index and Potential ecological hazard index indicate that the pollution of Hg and Cd is serious, besides the geological background, it is significantly affected by releases from human activities. Most metal(loid)s in the water bodies of the study area come from the input of the Yellow River and the impact of near-shore human activities. Similarly, in the surface sediments, most metal(loid)s are derived from the Yellow River input, while Hg, Pb, Cd, and As may also be affected by offshore industrial activities. The spatial distribution of metal(loid)s is controlled by the enrichment of heavy metals in fine-grained sediments under hydrodynamic sorting, as well as sediment components such as TOC content.

Introduction

Trace metal(loid)s have garnered significant concern owing to their inherent toxicity, environmental persistence, and potential for bioaccumulation. These contaminants can circulate and migrate across multiple marine compartments, including seawater, sediments, suspended particles and marine organisms, making them one of the most hazardous categories of environmental pollutants1–3. Trace metal(loid)s enter the ocean via natural processes such as weathering and erosion, as well as from various anthropogenic sources (including industrial, agricultural, and wastewater discharge). The majority of these contaminants are transported to marine environments by river systems, with a large proportion ultimately discharged and deposited in estuarine and coastal regions, where this accumulation poses substantial adverse impacts on aquatic ecosystems4–6.

The estuarine zone, situated at the land–ocean interface, supports a complex and multifunctional ecosystem characterized by dynamic hydrological conditions, substantial sediment fluxes, active material exchange, and intricate interfacial processes7,8. As a key sink for terrestrial pollutants, this zone is also heavily impacted by anthropogenic activities—factors that collectively render it one of the most severely polluted regions in marine environments9–11. Owing to the complexity of influencing factors in estuarine areas, the migration and transport of heavy metal(loid)s at the land-sea interface exhibit high variability and are modulated by multiple drivers4,12,13. In recent decades, the intensification of human activities has posed mounting challenges to coastal communities and ecosystems14,15. Therefore, clarifying the spatial distribution patterns and ecological impacts of trace metal(loid)s in estuaries and the adjacent coastal areas, holds significant scientific value for elucidating the source-sink processes of riverine-transported trace metal(loid)s entering the ocean, and furthermore, this knowledge can provide a critical scientific basis for the remediation of trace metal(loid) pollution and the environmental conservation in these vulnerable regions.

The Yellow River, stretching 5,464 km in total length, originates from the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and empties into the Bohai Sea, is notable for its exceptionally high sediment load16,17. The substantial sediment discharge makes the river a major supplier of terrestrial material to the East China Sea shelf, significantly influencing sedimentation patterns and geochemical processes in the estuary and adjacent coastal areas18. The Yellow River Basin contributes approximately 15% of China’s agricultural output and 9% of its total gross domestic product19. However, this economic and agricultural activity also leads to substantial generation of trace metal(loid)s from domestic, industrial, and agricultural sources, which are subsequently carried by the Yellow River and discharged into the sea20. Furthermore, the Bohai Sea rim is one of China’s most densely populated and economically active regions. Consistent with these factors, numerous studies have highlighted that the Bohai Sea faces a high risk of trace metal(loid)s pollution6,16,21,22.

Given that the Yellow River Estuary and its adjacent region are subject to both substantial material inputs from the Yellow River and intense anthropogenic disturbances, this study aims to characterize the spatial distribution and pollution status of trace metal(loid)s (Cu, Pb, Zn, Cr, Cd, As, and Hg) in the water column and surface sediments of this region, and to elucidate the key factors controlling their behavior. The findings of this study are expected to provide valuable insights into the trace metal(loid) environmental quality of the study area, and offer methodological and data support for future systematic research and protection efforts on regional trace metal(loid) cycling.

Materials and methods

Study area and sampling

The present study focused on the offshore region of the Yellow River Estuary, including the subaqueous delt of the Yellow River and its adjacent areas, with the study scope extending to the central Bohai Sea (Fig. 1). Surface seawater, bottom seawater, and surface sediment samples were collected from 51 stations during a research cruise conducted in May, 2024 (Fig. 1).

Water samples were collected from the surface and bottom layers using Niskin bottles. Immediately after collection, the samples were filtered in the shipboard laboratory through pre-treated fiber filter membranes (47 mm diameter, 0.45 μm pore size), which had been soaked in acid for 12 h and rinsed with ultrapure water. The filtrate was then split into two aliquots for different analyses: one was acidified with high-purity HNO₃ to pH < 2 for the measurement of Cu, Pb, Zn, Cr, and Cd; the other was acidified with high-purity H₂SO₄ to pH < 2 for the analysis of Hg and As. All acidified samples were stored in pre-cleaned glass bottles at approximately 4 °C until laboratory analysis.

Surface sediment samples were collected using a box sampler (Institute of Marine Instrumentation, Shandong Academy of Sciences, China). From each sample, the top 2 cm of sediment was carefully scooped using a plastic spoon and immediately transferred to a polyethylene bag. All the samples were then stored in a refrigerator at approximately 4 °C until subsequent laboratory analysis.

Methods

Grain size analysis

Prior to particle size analysis, the sediment samples were pretreated with 30% H2O2 and 1 mol/L HCl to remove organic matter and carbonate, respectively (Liu et al., 2016). The grain size of the sediments was subsequently measured with a Mastersizer 3000 laser particle size analyzer (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK) at a 1/4 φ interval, with relative errors of replicate measurements being less than 2%. Following measurement, relevant grain size parameters were calculated according to Folk & Ward (1957)23.

Trace metal(loid)s concentration analysis

The concentrations of trace metal(loid)s (Cu, Pb, Zn, Cr, Cd) in both water and sediment samples were determined using inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Agilent 7500), while As and Hg were analyzed with an atomic fluorescence spectrophotometer (GXY-1101 A), following the methodology of Liu et al. (2024)21. All chemical reagents used were of analytical purity and prepared with ultra-pure water.

Water samples were accurately diluted (1:100) using ultrapure water prior to instrumental analysis. This dilution factor effectively reduced the salinity interference to a negligible level while maintaining analyte concentrations within the instrument’s detection range. Quality control was ensured by analyzing certified seawater standard reference materials (SRMs) GBW (E) 080042 and method blanks. The recoveries of the reference materials ranged from 90% to 110%, and the relative standard deviations (RSDs) of replicate measurements were all less than 5%, indicating good accuracy.

For sediment samples, they were first dried and ground to a particle size of 63 μm in the laboratory before analysis. A 0.050-g sub-sample of each sample was extracted and placed into a teflon digestion tank, to which 3 mL HNO3 and HF (1:1) was added, following which the tank was sealed and placed in an oven at 190 ℃ for 48 h. After cooling, the teflon tank was placed on an electric hot plate, 1 mL HNO3 was added, and the solution was evaporated to remove residual HF. Then, 3 mL HNO3 (1:1) was added and the solution was digested at 150 ℃ for 24 h. The solution was then transferred to a clean PET (polyester) bottle and stored at ~ 4℃ until further analysis. During analysis, method blanks and sediment SRMs (GBW07311 and GBW07314) were employed for quality assurance and quality control. The RSDs of replicate measurements were all less than 5%, and the recovery rates of the SRMs were in the range of 97%−103%.

Sea water quality evaluation of trace metal(loid)s

The pollution status of trace metal(loid)s in surface and bottom seawater was evaluated with reference to China’s “Seawater Quality Standard” (GB 3097 − 1997), promulgated by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment of the People’s Republic of China. This standard classifies seawater quality into four grades based on the concentrations of metal(loid)s, with the corresponding standard values for each grade presented in Table 1.

Assessment methods of surface sediment contamination

Among various methods for assessing sediment contamination by trace metal(loid)s, the geo-accumulation index (Igeo) and the potential ecological risk index are widely employed to evaluate contamination levels and potential ecological hazards, respectively24–28.

The Igeo is a widely used parameter to assess the trace metal(loid)s contamination, which originally defined by Müller (1969)29. Statistical analysis

Pearson’s correlation analysis (CA) was performed with a statistical software (SPSS 26.0 for Windows) to identify the relationship among the trace metal(loid)s and to characterize their distributions in the study area.

Results and discussion

Distribution and pollution status of trace metal(loid)s in surface and bottom waters

The concentrations of trace metal(loid)s in the surface water are presented in Table 1. The concentration ranges of the target elements are as follows: Cu from 0.67 to 1.68 µg/L, Pb from 0.21 to 1.05 µg/L, Zn from 4.49 to 13.20 µg/L, Cr from 0.15 to 0.49 µg/L, Cd from 0.04 to 0.14 µg/L, As from 0.52 to 1.22 µg/L, and Hg from 0.013 to 0.049 µg/L. Meanwhile, the average concentrations of Cu, Pb, Zn, Cr, Cd, As, and Hg in the surface water are 1.10 µg/L, 0.50 µg/L, 7.14 µg/L, 0.27 µg/L, 0.07 µg/L, 0.82 µg/L, and 0.032 µg/L, respectively.

Figure 2 illustrates the distribution patterns of trace metal(loid)s concentrations in surface water bodies. Specifically, Cu concentrations are relatively high in the surface seawater of the northern and northeastern regions of Yellow River Estuary, whereas they are notably lower in areas adjacent to the estuary. For Pb, Zn, Cr, and Cd, elevated concentrations are consistently observed in the area extending from the Yellow River Estuary to the northwestern part of the study area, while the eastern portion of the study area exhibits relatively low levels of the metal(loid)s. As for As, sites with high concentrations are primarily distributed in the eastern part of the study area. In contrast, Hg concentrations are relatively high at most monitoring stations, with the exception of the central part of the study area, where levels are distinctly lower.

Overall, the majority of metal(loid)s display analogous spatial patterns, characterized by notably higher concentrations in the northern sector of the study area and the region adjacent to the Yellow River Estuary. Pollution status of trace metal(loid)s in the surface sediment

The content of trace metal(loid)s in sediments is influenced by their natural abundance, anthropogenic inputs, and the retention capacity of the sediments, and their absolute concentrations do not directly reflect the pollution status21,39. Therefore, this study employs the most widely used environmental quality assessment methods to evaluate the contamination status of metal(loid)s in surface sediments11,26,27.

Geo-accumulation index (Igeo) assessment

Based on the geo-accumulation index (Igeo) values of various trace metal(loid)s in the study area presented in Table 3, a comprehensive contamination assessment was conducted. The results indicate that the Igeo values for Cu, Pb, Zn, Cr, and As were consistently below 0 across all sampling sites, suggesting that the sediments throughout the study area are largely uncontaminated by these five elements.

Regarding Cd, the contamination status was spatially variable: 49% of the sampling sites recorded Igeo values below 0 (classified as uncontaminated), while the remaining sites exhibited values ranging between 0 and 1 (maximum of 1). This distribution reflects a transition from uncontaminated to moderately contaminated conditions in localized areas. In contrast, Hg posed a more widespread concern, with Igeo values greater than 0 detected at all sampling sites, signifying varying degrees of contamination across the entire study area. Notably, 75% of these sites exhibited Igeo values between 1 and 2 (maximum value of 1.89), corresponding to a distinct moderately contaminated level.

When ranked by their average Igeo values, the contamination levels of the studied metal(loid)s followed the descending order: Hg > Cd > Pb > As > Zn > Cu > Cr. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that the concentrations of all metal(loid)s in the water bodies were below the Grade I seawater limits issued by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment of the People’s Republic of China, indicating good water quality despite the sedimentary contamination observed for Hg and Cd.

Figure 6 illustrates the spatial distribution characteristics of the Igeo values for trace metal(loid)s in the study area. Notably, high Igeo values of the metal(loid)s are predominantly concentrated in the vicinity of the Yellow River Estuary and the northern part of the study area, which are characterized by finer sediment particles. Particularly, Hg, Cd, Pb, and As exhibit distinctly elevated pollution indices in the sea area near the Yellow River Estuary. This phenomenon has two key implications: first, it confirms the contribution of trace metal(loid)s inputs from the Yellow River to the overall metal(loid)s load in the study area; second, it reflects the strong adsorption capacity of fine-grained sediments for metal(loid) ions, which tends to trap and accumulate these contaminants. In addition, Pb, Zn, and Cr also display relatively high Igeo values at certain sampling sites in the eastern part of the study area. This localized enrichment suggests the potential presence of alternative anthropogenic pollution sources, which may be associated with offshore industrial activities such as oil extraction.

Potential ecological hazard index

The Potential Ecological Hazard Index (Er) provides further insight into the biological toxicity of trace metal(loid)s in the study area. As summarized in Table 3, the assessment reveals a distinct dichotomy in risk levels. Specifically, the Er values for Cu, Pb, Zn, Cr, and As were consistently below 40 across all sampling sites. This categorizes these five elements as posing a “low ecological risk” to the local benthic environment. Notably, Zn and Cr exhibited Er values uniformly less than 1, indicating an “extremely low” potential for ecological harm. Overall, the high Er values of these metal(loid)s are mainly distributed in areas with fine-grained sediments, such as the Yellow River Estuary or the northern part of the study area.

However, the Er values of Cd exceeded 40 at 59% of the sampling sites, indicating a transition from low to moderate ecological risk across a significant portion of the study area. More alarmingly, localized hotspots were identified at two specific stations where Er values surged beyond 80, reaching 84 and 90, respectively. These elevated values classify these locations as posing a moderate-to-heavy ecological risk, suggesting potential adverse effects on local benthic biota. Spatially, these high-risk zones for Cd were predominantly clustered in the vicinity of the Yellow River Estuary. This distribution pattern strongly implies that the elevated Cd levels are primarily driven by terrestrial inputs from the Yellow River, where fine-grained sediments facilitate the accumulation of this highly toxic metal.

It is particularly noteworthy that for Hg, except at 4 stations where the Er values were less than 80, the ecological risk at all other stations exceeded the threshold for a moderate ecological risk. Furthermore, at 30% of the sampling sites, the Er values exceeded 160, indicating that the ecological risk reached a heavy level. The high Er values of Hg are widely distributed in the offshore area of the Yellow River Estuary, as well as in the northern and southeastern parts of the study area.

The comprehensive potential ecological hazard index (EIR) values of these 7 trace metal(loid)s range from 107 to 310, with an average of 204, indicating an overall moderate ecological risk level. Only at 15% of the stations in the study area, the EIR values are less than 150, corresponding to a low ecological risk. Concurrently, there is 1 station where the EIR value exceeds 300, reaching the maximum value of 310, which represents a heavy ecological risk. The EIR values at the remaining stations fall between 150 and 300, indicating a moderate ecological risk.

Since EIR represents the sum of the ecological risks of all trace metal(loid)s, the areas with high-value are still mainly distributed in the region adjacent to the Yellow River Estuary and the northern part of the study area. Among all elements, Hg contributes the most to the EIR, followed by Cd. This suggests that these elements have caused the study area to face relatively high ecological and environmental risks.

Factors influencing the distribution of metal(loid)s contents and pollution status

Trace metal(loid)s in the water and sediments of coastal areas generally originate from riverine transport, atmospheric deposition, and point-source discharges associated with human activities4,8,15. Beyond their sources, their distribution is also influenced by sedimentary environmental factors, including hydrodynamic conditions, physicochemical properties, and so on12,15.

To investigate the factors influencing the distribution of the trace metal(loid)s in the study area, we conducted a correlation analysis of their contents among surface water, bottom water, and surface sediments (Table 4). The results showed that the correlation coefficient (R) of As between surface and bottom water is the highest, reaching 0.82; Zn, Cu, Pb, Hg, and Cr also exhibit a certain degree of correlation, while Cd has a poor correlation. This indicates that the sources and distribution of metal(loid)s in water bodies are highly consistent. In contrast, Cd, due to its strong geochemical mobility, leads to differences in its distribution between surface and bottom water.

High values of dissolved metal(loid)s contents in water are mostly distributed in the northern part of the Yellow River Estuary and nearshore areas around the estuary, while contents are relatively low in offshore areas such as the eastern part of the study area. This indicates that nearshore human activities and input from the Yellow River contribute to the trace metal(loid)s contents in water. However, in the northern and eastern parts of the study area where far from the shore, the pollution of Hg and Cd are relatively serious, which should be related to releases from offshore human industrial activities. The Bozhong Oilfield and Kenli Oilfield are distributed in this region, and it is likely that releases from offshore oil exploitation have affected the contents of As and Hg here. It should be noted that this study is limited by the lack of data regarding water column nutrients and TOC. Future studies should incorporate these parameters to better elucidate the potential biogeochemical transformations of metal(loid)s in this dynamic estuarine environment.

However, the correlation between trace metal(loid)s contents in water and surface sediments is notably poor. Except for Cu, which shows a weak correlation, the other metal(loid)s exhibit almost no correlation, while As presents a negative correlation. This indicates that the sources and controlling mechanisms of trace metal(loid)s in water and sediments are significantly different.

To further analyze the influencing factors of metal(loid)s distribution in surface sediments, we conducted a correlation analysis between metal(loid)s contents, median particle size (Md, φ), and total organic carbon (TOC) content in the sediments (Table 5). The results show that there is a significant positive correlation among the various metals, indicating a high degree of consistency in their sources and geochemical behaviors. In terms of metal distribution and pollution status, trace metal(loid)s exhibit relatively high contents and pollution levels in areas near the outer Yellow River Estuary and the northern part of the study area, with the Yellow River Estuary being more prominent. This suggests that the metal(loid)s in sediments are mostly derived from input from the Yellow River. After the sediments enter the sea, the convergence of freshwater and saltwater within the turbidity zone of the Yellow River Estuary induces the flocculation and deposition of substantial amounts of fine-grained sediments16,40, with trace metal(loid)s being co-deposited in this process. This mechanism gives rise to elevated metal(loid)s concentrations in the sediments—findings that align with both the sedimentary trace metal(loid)s distribution presented in Fig. 5 and the results of their environmental risk assessment presented in Figs. 6 and 7. Such a geochemical pattern underscores the estuary’s role as a natural “filter”: it effectively intercepts and sequesters trace metal(loid)s that are transported by the Yellow River enroute to the sea.

It can also be concluded from Table 5 that there is a relatively obvious positive correlation between metal(loid)s contents and the Md of sediments (with the exception of Cr), indicating that sediment particle size exerts a significant impact on the content and distribution of metals. Meanwhile, the positive correlation between metal(loid)s and TOC content is also relatively significant, which suggests that material components such as TOC in sediments also have an important influence on the distribution of metals. These metal(loid)s, which discharged into the sea from the Yellow River and those maybe from other sources, are transported under the action of tidal currents and coastal currents, and gradually undergo dynamic sorting. Studies by Hu et al. (2024) have shown that the deposition, migration and transformation of metal(loid)s in the Yellow River subaqueous delta are closely related to the composition of sediments, while being less affected by other factors such as the physicochemical properties of water bodies [16,]. In areas traversed by coastal currents (Fig. 1), strong hydrodynamics result in coarser sediments (Fig. 4); in contrast, in the northern part of the study area, weakened hydrodynamics lead to the deposition of fine-grained sediments (Fig. 4). Since fine-grained sediments have a stronger adsorption capacity for trace metal(loid)s11,16, this gives rise to the distribution pattern of metal(loid)s contents and pollution status in the study area.

Since organic matter serves as a crucial carrier for trace metal(loid)s41, the content of TOC in sediments exert a significant influence on the distribution of metal(loid)s. As shown in Table 5, there is a strong positive correlation between the contents of metal elements and TOC. In the study area, the marine primary productivity increases seaward and even exceeds that in the estuarine areas where the water is relatively turbid42. Consequently, the primary productivity in the upper water column leads to the deposition of large amounts of organic matter into the sediments. Meanwhile, as one moves seaward, the dissolved oxygen in the bottom water decreases continuously, which provides a suitable environment for the preservation of organic matter16. Furthermore, since organic matter tends to be present in fine-grained sediments, Table 5 also indicates a significant correlation between TOC and median grain size (Md), therefore, fine-grained sediments offer an ideal environment for the preservation of organic matter, which collectively affects the distribution characteristics and pollution status of trace metal(loid)s.

Conclusions

The distribution patterns of trace metal(loid)s concentrations in the surface and bottom waters of the Yellow River Estuary and adjacent area are basically consistent. Generally, relatively high concentrations are widely observed in the area extending from the Yellow River Estuary to the northwest of the study area, while the eastern part of the study area shows relatively low levels. This indicates that these trace elements originate from the inflow of the Yellow River and are also influenced by human activities in the coastal areas.

Most metal(loid)s in the surface sediments were detected at relatively high concentrations in the northern part of the study area and the regions beyond the Yellow River Estuary-areas predominantly composed of fine-grained sediments. However, relatively high concentrations of Zn and Cr were found at some stations along the eastern edge of the study area, while elevated levels of Cd and As were detected in the southern part of the study area. Metal(loid)s are mainly derived from the input of the Yellow River, while Hg and Cd may also be affected by offshore industrial activities.

According to the seawater quality standard, trace metal(loid)s concentrations were all lower than the values of the Class I seawater quality standard both in the surface and bottom water. However, the results of environmental quality assessment indicated that surface sediments are severely polluted by the Hg and Cd, while other metal(loid)s showed unpolluted in the whole area.

The distribution of trace metal(loid)s in surface sediments is influenced by the following factors: the enrichment of heavy metals in fine-grained sediments under hydrodynamic action, and components in sediments such as TOC content.
