Prevalence of integrase strand transfer inhibitor resistance in people living with HIV and virological failure
Authors: Hung-Chin Tsai, I-Tzu Chen, Hui-Min Chang, Yu-Ting Tseng, Ya-Wei Weng, Yao-Shen Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:This study aims to delineate the resistance profiles of integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs) among patients in southern Taiwan who had experienced antiretroviral therapy (ART) failure. We focused on individuals previously treated with highly active ART (HAART) regimens, providing insights into the implications of INSTI resistance in a broader treatment-experienced population.Methods:Data were collected from patients failing an INSTI-containing regimen in a medical center in southern Taiwan between 2009 and 2022. Virological failure was defined as a plasma viral load >1000 copies/mL. Reverse transcriptase, protease, and integrase coding regions were sequenced at failure. Resistance-associated mutations included in the 2022 International Antiviral Society (IAS)-USA list were used. Drug resistance was analyzed using the HIV Stanford HIVDB 9.4 edition algorithm. Logistic regression analysis was used to analyze the risk factors associated with INSTI failure.Results:A total of 184 patients were enrolled for genotypic drug resistance testing due to virological failure, of whom 104 failed on nonnucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors, 58 on protease inhibitors (PIs), and 21 on INSTIs. Among 21 patients who failed INSTI therapy, 6 failed raltegravir-based treatment, 3 elvitegravir/cobicistat/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (EVG/COBI/FTC/TAF), 2 bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (BIC/FTC/TAF), and 10 abacavir/dolutegravir/lamivudine (ABC/DTG/3TC). Only 10 patients had INSTI drug resistance testing results available, and 40% (4/10) showed INSTI resistance at failure. Among the seven patients who failed on second-generation INSTIs with drug resistance reports available, one harbored E157Q and another with R263K mutations, respectively. Multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that patients with INSTI failure were less likely to have pol resistance (p = 0.007, adjusted odds ratio [OR], 0.176, 95% CI, 0.050-0.618), less previous exposure to NNRTI (p = 0.003, aOR, 0.063, 95% CI, 0.010-0.401), PIs (p = 0.002, aOR, 0.030, 95% CI, 0.003-0.272), and with long duration of HAART (p = 0.018, aOR, 1.02, 95% CI, 1.003-1.037).Conclusion:INSTI resistance was uncommon when used as the first-line single tablet regimen in Taiwan. The results confirmed the robustness of ABC/DTG/3TC and BIC/FTC/TAF regarding integrase resistance in cases of virological failure in routine clinical care.Keywords: Drug resistance, HIV, INSTI failure, Virological failure

1. INTRODUCTION

Integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI)-based antiretroviral therapy (ART) has been proven to be effective therapy against human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection.1 INSTI resistance is low in HIV-1-infected treatment-naïve individuals, and routine Sanger genotypic drug resistance testing is not recommended in current guidelines.2 An epidemiological study on the prevalence of transmitted drug resistance to INSTIs in Taiwan reported a low rate of 1.8% in predominantly men who have sex with men recruited from voluntary counseling and testing programs.3

Raltegravir was the first INSTI used in Taiwan in 2009, initially only as part of a salvage regimen for treatment-experienced patients. In 2012, its use was expanded to some treatment-naïve patients in combined with zidovudine/lamivudine (AZT/3TC), and it also played an important role in the management of patients with previous treatment failure when combined with abacavir/lamivudine (ABC/3TC) or tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/lamivudine (TDF/3TC).4 Other INSTIs formulated as single tablet regimens (STRs) in Taiwan include abacavir/dolutegravir/lamivudine (ABC/DTG/3TC) since 2016, elvitegravir/cobicistat/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (EVG/COBI/FTC/TAF) since 2018, bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (BIC/FTC/TAF) since 2019, and dolutegravir/lamivudine (DTG/3TC) since 2020.3 INSTI-based STRs have been associated with better drug adherence,5 a decreased risk of resistance-associated mutations,6 and a low INSTI resistance rate in treatment-naïve patients. However, the need to perform drug resistance testing in patients with INSTI virological failure remains unclear, and limited clinical data are available on the prevalence of HIV-1 resistance in an area with universal STR ART reimbursement and in patients with virological failure to INSTI-based therapy. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the prevalence and risk factors associated with INSTI resistance in patients failing an INSTI-based therapy.

2. METHODS

2.1. Ethics statement

This study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki, and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital (VGHKS12-CT5-07, VGHKS15-CT5-10, and VGHKS16-CT11-21). In addition, all participants signed informed consent after the study procedure had been carefully explained.

2.2. Study design and participants

This retrospective observational study was conducted at Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital, which is one of the largest medical centers in Kaohsiung City with 1482 in-hospital beds. The hospital treats about 20% of people living with HIV (PLWHIV) in Kaohsiung City and approximately 5% of those living in Taiwan. We enrolled individuals who started highly active ART (HAART) with viral suppression for at least 6 months and then with a viral load of more than 1000 copies/mL from 2009 to 2022. Resistance testing for the INSTI coding region was not performed until 2013. The standard care for PLWHIV in Taiwan includes testing for viral load, CD4+ T cell count, biochemistry, and hematology every 3 months during the first year after the diagnosis of HIV, and every 6 months thereafter for stable patients. HAART is provided to all PLWHIV at no cost; however, pretreatment genotypic drug resistance testing is not reimbursed and is not mandatory. Before June 2012, no ART reimbursement limitation was applied, and the physicians could choose all available ART licensed in Taiwan for free. The available nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) included AZT/3TC, the nonnucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs) were efavirenz and nevirapine, and the protease inhibitors (PIs) were atazanavir, saquinavir, nelfinavir, lopinavir, tipranavir, ritonavir, and darunavir. The only INSTI (raltegravir) was used in ART-experienced patients from 2009. From June 2012 to June 2016, four categories of ART were used depending on the patient’s scenario. The first category consisted of nevirapine or efavirenz with AZT/3TC as the first-line ART; the second category consisted of nevirapine or efavirenz with ABC/3TC; or TDF/3TC or TDF/FTC (emtricitabine, FTC) for patients intolerant to AZT/3TC or concurrent with hepatitis B infection; the third category consisted of AZT/3TC and PIs or raltegravir for patients with treatment failure; and the fourth category consisted of TDF/3TC (FTC), or ABC/3TC with PIs or raltegravir if the patients had virological failure and resistance. After June 2016, three STRs were used universally as the first-line ART: efavirenz/emtricitabine/tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (EFV/FTC/TDF), abacavir/dolutegravir/lamivudine (ABC/DTG/3TC), and emtricitabine/rilpivirine/tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (FTC/RPV/TDF). Elvitegravir/cobicistat/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (EVG/COBI/FTC/TAF) was reimbursed in September 2018, and bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (BIC/FTC/TAF) and rilpivirine/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (RPV/FTC/TAF) were reimbursed in October 2019. Doravirine/lamivudine/tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (DOR/3TC/TDF) and dolutegravir/lamivudine (DTG/3TC) were recommended as the first-line ART in December 2020. During this period, the Taiwanese government adhered to the international HIV care standards. The treat-all policy for PLWHIV was implemented in 2016, and the rapid ART initiation program was initiated in 2018 and is ongoing.

2.3. Genotypic drug resistance testing

The detailed study protocol is described elsewhere.7,8 Briefly, PR/RT (pol gene) resistance was analyzed using a commercial kit ViroSeq HIV-1 Genotyping System version 2.8 (Celera, Alameda, CA), and INSTI (also in the pol gene) resistance was analyzed using in-house population (Sanger) sequencing.7,8 The 2022 International Antiviral Society (IAS)-USA mutation list9 was used to define mutations associated with drug resistance to NRTIs, INSTIs, PIs, and NNRTIs. The levels of resistance and viral subtype were analyzed using the Stanford University HIV Drug Resistance Database (version 9.4, last updated 2022-12-7). Patients with low, intermediate, and high resistance were all classified as having drug resistance.10

2.4. Statistical analysis

Comparisons of median values of continuous variables between two groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. The chi-square test or Fisher exact test was used to compare categorical variables between groups with or without INSTI failure. A logistic regression model was used to analyze the risks associated with INSTI failure, and the factors in univariate analysis with a p value of <0.2 were further entered into a multivariate model. A two-sided p value of <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 12.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

3. RESULTS

A total of 184 patients were enrolled from 2009 to 2022. During this 13-year period, there were changes in the reimbursement for first-line ART in Taiwan, as described in Section 2. Most of the patients were men (97.3%) with an age ranging from 20 to 40 years (74.5%). The most common transmission route for HIV was men who have sex with men (89.2%). The median CD4 level was 263 (interquartile range [IQR], 111-411) cells/μL, and the median plasma viral load (log) was 4.5 (IQR, 3.92-4.82) copies/mL. The concurrent infection rate with hepatitis B was 10.7% (19/178), with hepatitis C 7.8% (14/179), and syphilis 59% (108/183) (Table 1).

The prevalence rate of pol resistance (PR/RT/INSTI region) was 77.7% (143/184). Overall, 89.1% (163/184) of the patients harbored subtype B HIV-1 strains. The resistance rates to NRTIs, NNRTIs, PIs, and INSTIs were 62%, 61%, 7%, and 25%, respectively (Fig. 2). The individual prevalence rates of drug resistance and drug resistance-associated mutations to NRTIs, NNRTIs, PIs, and INSTIs are shown in Figs. 3 and 4. The most common NRTIs exhibiting drug resistance were ABC (62%) and 3TC/FTC (61%); for NNRTIs, they were efavirenz (60%) and nevirapine (61%); and for PIs, atazanavir (7%). Among the 20 patients with available INSTI genotypic drug resistance reports (10 were done at INSTI failure (n = 21) and 10 at failure of NNRTI- or PI-based regimens with INSTI genotypic resistance testing (GRT) requested by the in-charge physician), there was 20% resistance to bictegravir, cabotegravir and dolutegravir, and 25% resistance to elvitegravir and raltegravir. For the drug resistance-associated mutations, the most common NRTI mutations were M184V/I (60.4%) and K65R (8.7%), and the most common NNRTI mutations were K103N (21.7%), Y181C (19%), and G190A (12.5%). The most common INSTI mutations were E138K (10%), R263K (5%), Q148R/H/K (5% each), and G140A/S (5% each). The risk factor associated with INSTI failure in the logistic regression model was less likely to have pol resistance (p = 0.007, aOR, 0.176, 95% CI, 0.050-0.618), less previous exposure to NNRTI (p = 0.003, aOR, 0.063, 95% CI, 0.010-0.401), PIs (p = 0.002, aOR, 0.030, 95% CI, 0.003-0.272), and with long duration of HAART (p = 0.018, aOR, 1.02, 95% CI, 1.003-1.037) (Table 2). The demographic information of the 10 individuals who failed with INSTI-based regimens (a total of 21 patients failed with INSTIs) who had genotypic drug resistance results is shown in Table 3 and Fig. 1. In one 31-year-old man who failed with AZT/3TC and raltegravir, genotypic drug resistance results showed that he had M184V, G140S, and Q148H mutations. He had good virological control with darunavir/cobicistat/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (TAF/FTC/DRV/COB). Another treatment-naïve 33-year-old man who failed with TAF/FTC/EVG/COB had a complex mutation pattern, including A62V, K65R, M184V, G190A, E138K, and Q148R. He achieved viral suppression with TAF/FTC/RPV and DRV/COB. Baseline genotypic drug resistance testing was not done when initiating TAF/FTC/EVG/COB. One 26-year-old man failed with ABC/DTG/3TC and was found to have the R263K mutation. His ART was not changed, and the final follow-up viral load was 64 copies/mL. The details of drug resistance and mutation analysis for specific class failure are shown in Figs. 5 and 6. Of the 58 patients who failed with PIs, none had INSTI drug resistance, and of the 104 patients who failed with NNRTI-based ART, the INSTI drug resistance rate was 33% resistance to first-generation INSTIs (raltegravir and elvitegravir). For the 21 patients with INSTI failure (10 had genotypic drug resistance testing), the prevalence of resistance to bictegravir, dolutegravir, elvitegravir, and raltegravir resistance was 40%.

4. DISCUSSION

In this study, we found that only one of the seven patients who failed second-generation INSTIs developed INSTI resistance (R263K), and this patient achieved viral control without changing their HAART (last visit plasma viral load 64 copies/mL). INSTI resistance was mainly detected in those who failed first-generation INSTIs. Less pol gene resistance (including the PR/RT/integrase region) at failure and less previous exposure to NNRTIs, PIs, and longer duration of HAART were independently associated with INSTI resistance.

Among the 21 patients with INSTI failure, 12 failed to 2nd generation INSTI (10 ABC/DTG/3TC, 2 BIC/FTC/TAF). It is suggested that less acquired drug resistance develops due to the high genetic barrier of 2nd generation INSTI.11 Another explanation for less pol resistance was that patients with INSTI failure were less exposed to low genetic barrier NNRTI before failure to INSTI-based regimen compared to those patients with non-INSTI failure (Table 2). Our previous study showed that a history of previous exposure to NNRTIs increased the risk of virological failure and resistance.12

This is the first study in Taiwan to investigate INSTI failure and resistance in the STR era. Our results are in accordance with other international studies. In a French multicenter study conducted between 2014 and 2017, 674 patients failed INSTI-based ART, and of these patients, 389 (58%) did not have INSTI resistance-associated mutations at failure. In addition, the patients who failed with dolutegravir had significantly fewer INSTI resistance-associated mutations at failure than those who failed with raltegravir or elvitegravir, which is consistent with our findings.13 In Brazil, tenofovir plus lamivudine with dolutegravir (TL + D) has been used as the first-line recommended regimen for PLWHIV since January 2017. In a study of Sanger HIV genotype resistance testing in 113 patients who failed the first-line TL + D after at least 6 months of therapy, the major INSTI resistance-associated mutations were detected in seven patients (6.19%), including four with R263K, one with G118R, one with E138A, and one with G140R.14 In another retrospective study of INSTI resistance among 467 patients who failed a dolutegravir-based regimen, 58 (12.4%) had one or more major INSTI resistance-associated mutation. Despite failure, 89.5% of the patients were still fully susceptible to dolutegravir and bictegravir, and 85.0% to all INSTIs. They concluded that INSTI resistance was low and mainly related to previous first-generation INSTI exposure.15 The results of these studies were consistent, and showed that the distribution of INSTI resistance-associated mutations was different according to the HIV subtype. For example, L74F/I/M and E157Q were more likely to be present in patients with non-HIV subtype B, whereas G140C/H/S and Q148H/K/R were more likely to be present in patients with HIV subtype B. Furthermore, Q148 mutations were more common in subtype B patients with raltegravir failure. Two of our HIV-1 subtype B patients who failed with raltegravir harbored G140S/A, Q148H/K, and E 138K mutations, and another subtype B patient who failed with elvitegravir-based STR had E138K and Q148R mutations. All of them were re-suppressed with salvage regimens.

This study has some limitations. First, we collected blood samples from patients with virological failure at a single medical center. The study period was long at 13 years, and genotypic drug resistance testing for INSTI was not performed initially in everyone who developed resistance and not everyone who failed with an INSTI-based regimen had drug resistance testing. Furthermore, not every patient had baseline GRT for INSTI. However, we previously showed that the prevalence of transmitted drug resistance for INSTI was very low at 1.8% among 369 patients from 2013 to 2021. Therefore, we could only hypothesize a relationship between previous exposure to INSTI drugs and acquired INSTI resistance. Second, this study was retrospective, and the factors related to adherence or bias related to clinicians’ decisions on GRT may have affected the results. Finally, our assessment likely underestimated the prevalence of dolutegravir resistance, considering that resistance to this drug may develop in the integrase coding region and within the HIV-1 3’ polypurine tract (3’ PPT). This latter site is not customarily examined in standard GRT for the INSTI region. The main strength of this study is that the study participants were homogenous and almost all of them were enrolled in the voluntary counseling and testing program without selection bias, and most of them had subtype B.

In conclusion, our results showed that INSTI resistance in patients who failed with a second-generation INSTI-based regimen was uncommon in the modern ART era. Our results confirm the robustness of ABC/DTG/3TC and BIC/FTC/TAF against integrase resistance in cases of virological failure in routine clinical care.
