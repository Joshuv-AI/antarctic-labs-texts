Introduction

Nephrolithiasis (kidney stones or urolithiasis) is associated with significant morbidity, and enormous cost [1–4]. Although many cases of nephrolithiasis arise from environmental risk factors [1, 5, 6], several monogenic disorders (including many solute carrier [SLC]-related disorders) have been associated with an increased risk of kidney stones [1, 6]. SLC family transporters play crucial roles in active transport of many substrates across biological membranes in many organs including the kidneys [7]. Biallelic pathogenic variants in the SLC family 34-member 3 (SLC34A3) gene cause hereditary hypophosphatemic rickets with hypercalciuria (HHRH) [8, 9], while both monoallelic and biallelic pathogenic variants in SLC3A1 have been associated with cystinuria [6, 10].

SLC34A3 encodes the renal type 2c sodium-phosphate cotransporter [6]. The protein product of SLC3A1 is one of the two major transporters for reabsorption of l-Cystine (a dimeric amino acid that is formed when two cysteine molecules are covalently linked by a disulfide bond) and dibasic amino acids (ornithine, lysine and arginine) across the brush border membrane in early proximal tubules [6]. Here, we report the phenotype of a patient with concomitant biallelic and monoallelic pathogenic variants in SLC34A3 and SLC3A1, respectively.

Case Report

We studied a 29-year-old man, who has a personal history of recurrent nephrolithiasis (with over 10 kidney stone episodes requiring lithotripsy and surgical interventions), medullary nephrocalcinosis, pyelonephritis, bilateral septate renal cysts, and chronic kidney disease. The patient’s first kidney stone episode was at age 19. He reported no known personal history of hypophosphatemia, hypotonia, muscle weakness, bone pain, rickets, recurrent fractures, growth delay or hearing loss. His family history appeared noncontributory. He was around 188 cm (74 inches) tall with a normal body habitus without apparent craniofacial dysmorphisms or musculoskeletal anomalies.

Clinical chemistry investigations showed episodes of hypercalciuria with blood calcium concentrations being within reference intervals (RIs; or borderline elevated), ranging from 8.6 to 10.4 mg/dL (with RI being 8.6–10.3 mg/dL) in a context of normal serum albumin levels. The patient’s 1,25-dihydroxy vitamin D [1,25(O)2D] concentration was measured and found to be within normal limits, 39 ng/mL (optimal range is > or = 30 ng/mL). With recurrent episodes of nephrolithiasis, the patient’s renal function declined to 33 mL/min/1.73 m2 (RI being >90 mL/min/1.73 m2). Several metabolic urine analyses showed hypercalciuria (up to 460 mg/day, RI = 159–291 mg/day) and hypocitraturia (as low as 308 mg/day, RI = 492–911 mg/day).

He had normal serum phosphorus concentrations, including 3.1 mg/dL (RI = 2.2–4.5 mg/dL) at age 20, and 2.9 mg/dL at age 29. Numerous metabolic urine analyses showed normal urine phosphorus, with one slightly elevated value (peak = 1.215 g/day; RI = 0.6–1.2 g/day) at age 23. Urine pH values ranged from 6.0 to > 9.0 with RI being 5–8.5. The renal calculi mostly comprised; 50% calcium oxalate monohydrate, and 30% calcium phosphate (hydroxy- and carbonate-apatite) as well as 20% calcium oxalate dihydrate. Based on his clinical presentation, the differential diagnoses for recurrent nephrolithiasis were considered, including hypercalciuria, cystinuria, hyperoxaluria, hypocitraturia, hyperuricosuria, hypervitaminosis D, primary hyperparathyroidism, sarcoidosis, renal tubular acidosis, medullary sponge kidney, high animal protein intake, and high salt diet [2].

The patient elected to pursue a nephrolithiasis gene panel involving sequence analysis and deletion/duplication testing of 40 genes that are recognized to be associated with conditions that manifest with an increased risk of renal stones. Genetic testing showed compound heterozygous pathogenic variants in SLC34A3 [NM_001177316.2, c.448+1G>A and c.575C>T (p.Ser192Leu)] and a heterozygous pathogenic variant in SLC3A1 (gain of exons 5–9). The SLC34A3 c.448+1G>A is a splice donor variant, which can disrupt the splicing process at the 5′ end of the intron during the processing of messenger RNA (mRNA) after transcription, potentially resulting in defective protein production. The SLC34A3 c.448+1G>A fulfilled the very strong evidence level (PVS1) [8], as well as one of the moderate evidence levels (PM3) [11], and another moderate evidence level for pathogenicity (PM2, applied at supporting level) of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) variant classification systems [12]. The SLC34A3 c.575C>T (p.Ser192Leu) fulfilled one of the strong evidence levels (PS3) [13], and two moderate evidence levels (PM1 [14], and PM3), as well as two supporting evidence (PP3, and PP1) for pathogenicity of the ACMG/AMP variant stratification framework [12]. The SLC3A1 gene variant (gain of exons 5–9) has been reported in many individuals affected with cystinuria [10], thus fulfilling the PS2 [6] criteria of the ACMG/AMP variant classification system [12]. The patient declined further testing for urinary excretion of cysteine, ornithine, lysine, and arginine. Surveillance radiographs were offered but the patient deferred the tests due to cost concerns. The patient was treated with potassium citrate (10 mEq three times daily) and hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg hydrochlorothiazide twice daily). He was also placed on serial monitoring of urine phosphorus and serum phosphate concentrations as well as prophylactic treatment with oral phosphate supplements, considering the risk of hypophosphatemia in HHRH. Of note, phosphate supplement was also initiated to help with lowering urinary calcium concentrations [15] with a plan to monitor 24-h urine metabolic profile after phosphate replacement.

Discussion

This is the first reported case of the simultaneous presence of autosomal recessive SLC34A3- and autosomal dominant SLC3A1-related disorders. Although the patient’s overall phenotype appears to be more consistent with SLC34A3-related HHRH as compared to SLC3A1-related cystinuria (Table 1), the patient’s phenotype does not completely overlap with either condition. Patients with HHRH typically present with chronic hypophosphatemia, nephrocalcinosis, renal cysts, rickets, osteomalacia, hypotonia or muscular weakness, limb deformities, recurrent fractures, and short stature, that typically begins in infancy but may also manifest in adulthood [16]. Our observations of compound heterozygous pathogenic variants in SLC34A3 in a patient who presented with recurrent calcium phosphate nephrolithiasis and hypercalciuria without hypophosphatemia or bone disease or short stature, are consistent with previous studies that suggests that some cases of SLC34A3-related disorder (involving the SLC34A3 c.575C>T (p.Ser192Leu) variant) may have a relatively mild phenotype [13]. The relatively mild phenotype observed in patients harboring the SLC34A3 c.575C>T variant is believed to be due, at least in part, to a reduction in function as opposed to a complete defect in the renal type 2c sodium-dependent phosphate cotransporter activity.

Patient’s phenotype as compared to SLC34A3- and SLC3A1-related disorders

Although nephrolithiasis occurs in autosomal dominant SLC3A1-related disorder, the presence of calcium phosphate stones and hypercalciuria suggest that the SLC34A3 pathogenic variants are driving this patient’s nephrolithiasis phenotype [9]. Considering that cystine crystals [17] were not identified on stone analysis and urinary cysteine concentrations were not investigated due to the patient’s preferences, the potential contributions of autosomal dominant SLC3A1-related cystinuria to the patient’s history of recurrent nephrolithiasis remain unclear. Considering the limited clinical and biochemical evidence, it is important to note that the pathogenic SLC3A1 variant may be non-penetrant [18]. It is therefore possible that the SLC3A1 pathogenic variant may represent a secondary finding of uncertain clinical significance in this patient. Our findings suggest that it is important to consider SLC-related disorders in patients with recurrent nephrolithiasis and that phenotypic complexities may occur in patients with comorbid SLC-related disorders. This case adds to the ongoing discussions of the potential utilities of genetic testing in patients with nephrolithiasis [19, 20]. It should be emphasized that the interpretation of genetic test results requires considerable caution and that pathogenic variants of uncertain penetrance must always be evaluated with particular care.
