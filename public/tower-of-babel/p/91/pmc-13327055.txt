Generalized Joint Hypermobility in Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis: Greater Curve Flexibility, Larger Thoracic Kyphosis, but Higher Complication Risk
Authors: Di Liu, Kexin Xu, Xiangjie Yin, Terry Jianguo Zhang, Nan Wu, the Deciphering Disorders Involving Scoliosis and Comorbidities study
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesGeneralized joint hypermobility (GJH) is prototypical clinical feature of hypermobility spectrum disorders, and emerging studies have found a correlation between GJH and structural spinal changes. This study aims to investigate the preoperative radiographic characteristics and clinical outcomes of patients with GJH and adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS).MethodsPatients with AIS were prospectively recruited between July 2023 and August 2024 and divided into GJH (Beighton score ≥ 6) and non‐GJH (Beighton score < 6) groups. The GJH group was matched 1:1 with controls based on age (±1 year), sex, main curve magnitude (±5°), curve type of the Lenke classification, triradiate cartilage status, and Risser stage (±1 grade). Baseline characteristics and surgical data were recorded. Preoperative and immediately postoperative radiographic parameters were measured. The Scoliosis Research Society‐22 revised (SRS‐22r) questionnaire was collected preoperatively and at least 1 year postoperatively. Univariate analysis and multivariable linear regression analysis were used to identify factors associated with main curve flexibility, TK, and T1 tilt angle.ResultsA total of 136 consecutive patients were included, with 29 patients (21.3%) classified into the GJH group. After mating, the GJH showed greater flexibility of the main curve (60.0% ± 27.7% vs. 45.8% ± 22.1%, p = 0.020) and increased thoracic kyphosis (22.9° ± 11.5° vs. 16.7° ± 11.7°, p = 0.031) compared to the control group. Multivariable linear regression analyses revealed that the GJH was correlated with increased spinal flexibility (β = 14.09, p = 0.015) and larger thoracic kyphosis (β = 6.11, p = 0.043). The GJH group demonstrated a relatively higher risk of surgical complications compared to the non‐GJH group (17.2% [5/29] vs. 4.7% [5/107], p = 0.036). However, no significant differences were observed in patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) between the two groups.ConclusionsIn patients with AIS, GJH is associated with increased spinal flexibility and greater thoracic kyphosis and, importantly, a higher risk of surgical complications.Keywords: adolescent idiopathic scoliosis, flexibility, generalized joint hypermobility, kyphosis

Introduction

Generalized joint hypermobility (GJH) is a prevalent condition characterized by an abnormal degree of flexibility in the joints, resulting from excessive extensibility of the surrounding soft tissues [1, 2]. The prevalence of GJH varies significantly, ranging from 2.3% to 35.6% in younger populations, with a gradual decline observed as age increases [3, 4]. GJH exists on a spectrum, ranging from an isolated and asymptomatic condition to being a component of a broader syndrome. As a common clinical manifestation of hypermobility spectrum disorders, GJH is associated with a range of musculoskeletal conditions as well [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. When symptomatic, it may be associated with musculoskeletal pain and functional impairments [2, 11, 12].

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is the most common type of scoliosis, affecting approximately 1%–3% of children in pubescence [13]. AIS is a diagnosis of exclusion, confirmed only after other potential causes of scoliosis have been ruled out. Despite extensive research, the pathogenesis of AIS remains poorly understood, with multiple factors contributing to its onset and progression [14]. Moreover, joint laxity or hypermobility is more prevalent in individuals with scoliosis than those without structural spinal change, and a significant correlation exists between hypermobility and spinal deformities among adolescents, especially in cases with moderate to severe structural spinal changes [8, 15]. In addition, the concurrent presence of spinal deformities and hypermobility in various inherited connective tissue disorders (e.g., Marfan syndrome and hypermobile Ehlers–Danlos syndrome) suggests the possibility of mutual underlying mechanisms between hypermobility and idiopathic scoliosis [9, 10].

This study aimed to: (i) compare preoperative radiographic parameters between patients with AIS with and without GJH; (ii) evaluate differences in postoperative radiographic correction and clinical outcomes between the two groups; and (iii) identify whether GJH is independently associated with key radiographic parameters, including main curve flexibility, thoracic kyphosis (TK), and T1 tilt angle.

Materials and Methods

Participants

Consecutive patients with AIS who underwent surgical correction were prospectively recruited from July 2023 to August 2024 as part of the Deciphering disorders Involving Scoliosis and COmorbidities (DISCO) study (http://www.discostudy.org/). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) age between 10 and 18 years; (ii) a clinical diagnosis of idiopathic scoliosis; and (iii) treatment with posterior spinal fusion surgery. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) previous brace treatment; (ii) history of dance training; (iii) previous spinal surgery; and (iv) presence of spinal infection, tumor, trauma, or intraspinal anomalies. Inherent hypermobile disorders, such as Marfan syndrome, osteogenesis imperfecta, and Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, were excluded from the evaluation through review of medical history, physical examinations, and imaging findings, and genetic evaluations [16]. The detailed selection procedure is shown in Figure 1. This study selected cases and matched controls from within the same cohort. The control group was matched 1:1 with the case group based on age (±1 year), sex, main curve magnitude (±5°), curve type based on the Lenke classification, triradiate cartilage (TRC) status, and Risser stage (±1 grade).

Study selection process and reasons for exclusion.

The Institutional Review Board approved the protocol for this study (No. I‐24PJ0761), and written informed consents were obtained from all participants or legal guardians, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Baseline Variables and Clinical Evaluations

Demographic data, including age, sex, body mass index, and menarcheal status for females, as well as treatment history and experience in dance or acrobatics, were collected.

Surgical variables were recorded, including surgical duration, type of osteotomy, number of fused levels, estimated blood loss (EBL), and postoperative length of hospital stay. All complications were categorized as major or minor complications [17]. Patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) were evaluated using the Scoliosis Research Society‐22 revised (SRS‐22r) questionnaire collected both preoperatively and at a minimum of 1‐year follow‐up.

Radiographic Measurements

Radiographic parameters were evaluated preoperatively and immediately postoperatively using standardized full‐spine standing posteroanterior radiographs to measure coronal, sagittal, and pelvic parameters: (A) coronal parameters: main curve magnitude (°), trunk shift (TS, mm), apical vertebral translation (AVT, mm), apical vertebral rotation (AVR, Nash–Moe classification), T1 tilt angle (°), clavicular angle (°), radiographic shoulder height (RSH, mm), pelvic obliquity (°), and sacral obliquity (°); (B) sagittal parameters: cervical lordosis (CL, C2–7, °), T1 slope (°), upper thoracic kyphosis (UTK, T2–5, °), TK (T5–12, °), thoracolumbar Cobb (TLC, T10–L2, °), upper lumbar lordosis (ULL, L1–4, °), distal lumbar lordosis (DLL, L4–S1, °), lumbar lordosis (LL, L1–S1, °), and sagittal vertebral axis (SVA, mm); and (C) pelvic parameters: pelvic incidence (PI, °), pelvic tilt (PT, °), and sacral slope (SS, °). Additionally, supine bending radiographs were utilized to assess the spinal flexibility of the main curve. The flexibility of the spine was calculated using the following formula: (main Cobb angle − bending Cobb angle)/main Cobb angle × 100%. Spinal flexibility was compared across different curve types by classifying participants into two groups based on the Lenke classification: the main thoracic (MT) group (types 1–4 curves) and the thoracolumbar and lumbar (TL/L) group (types 5 and 6 curves).

All measurements were independently performed by two experienced spine surgeons, with evaluations conducted at a 1‐month interval to ensure interobserver reliability and minimize potential bias.

Evaluation of GJH

The assessment of joint hypermobility was performed utilizing the Beighton scoring system [18]. The evaluation encompassed the following criteria: passive extension of the fifth finger, passive apposition of the thumb to the forearm, active hyperextension of the elbow and knee, and forward flexion of the trunk (Figure 2). All passive movements were performed to the maximum range without inducing pain. A criterion was considered positive as outlined in Table 1. Each positive finding was assigned a score of 1, and the total Beighton score was determined by summing these values, with a possible range of 0 to 9. Recent adaptations of the Beighton score have been proposed to account for variations across different age groups [19]. A Beighton score of ≥ 6 has been established as the threshold for classifying children and pubertal adolescents as having GJH [19, 20].

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize participants' baseline demographics and radiographic parameters. In the unmatched cohort, independent comparisons were performed using the Student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. In the matched cohort, the standardized mean difference (SMD) was calculated for all baseline covariates both before and after matching to assess the degree of imbalance prior to matching and the balance achieved post‐matching [21]. An SMD of less than 10% for a given covariate was interpreted as negligible imbalance, while an SMD between 10% and 34% indicated a small degree of imbalance [22]. Paired comparisons were conducted using the paired Student's t‐test or Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for continuous variables and Bowker's symmetry test for categorical variables. Multivariable linear regression analysis was used to evaluate the effects of age, BMI, Risser sign, income, main curve magnitude, curve type based on the Lenke curve classification, and GJH diagnosis on the flexibility of the main curve, TK, and T1 tilt angle. Intra‐ and interobserver agreement for radiographic measurements was assessed using the Cohen kappa statistic and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). ICC values were categorized as follows: poor (< 0.40), weak (0.40–0.54), moderate (0.55–0.69), good (0.70–0.84), and excellent (0.85–1.00). All statistical analyses were conducted using the SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Post hoc power analyses were conducted using G*Power 3.1 (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). The statistical powers for detecting effect sizes of 0.57, 0.53, and 0.51 were 84.2%, 78.7%, and 75.5%, respectively, for paired comparisons of curve flexibility, TK, and T1 tilt angle, all calculated at a significance level of 0.05.

Results

Study Population

A total of 218 patients were initially evaluated, of whom 136 patients with AIS were finally included in this study (Figure 1). And 29 patients (21.3%) were classified into the GJH group based on a Beighton score of ≥ 6. Intra‐ and interobserver reliability for the GJH evaluation and radiographic measurements was good to excellent (Table 2). Before matching, there was a significant imbalance in several baseline covariates between the two groups (Table 3). After matching, 29 patients with both AIS and GJH were paired with 29 patients with AIS without GJH. Following the matching procedure, the SMD for all covariates was less than 20.0%, indicating only a negligible to small imbalance between the groups (Table 3).

Radiographic Measurements

The preoperative comparisons of radiographic parameters were summarized in Tables 4 and S1. Compared to patients with AIS without GJH, those with both AIS and GJH exhibited significantly greater flexibility of the main curve (60.0% ± 27.7% vs. 45.8% ± 22.1%, p = 0.020), particularly in the MT curve (54.0% ± 23.3% vs. 38.1% ± 19.8%, p = 0.014). Associations Between GJH and Radiographic Differences

Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that younger age (β = −4.70, p = 0.032), smaller main curve Cobb angle (β = −0.81, p = 0.001), Lenke curve classification (β = 4.15, p = 0.015), and the presence of GJH (β = 14.09, p = 0.015) were significantly correlated with increased flexibility of the main curve. Additionally, a diagnosis of GJH was significantly associated with increased TK (β = 6.11, p = 0.043). However, none of the included factors were found to be significantly associated with the difference in the T1 tilt angle (Table 5).

Clinical Outcomes

There were no significant differences in surgical parameters between the two groups (Table 6). However, patients in the GJH group had a higher incidence of complications compared to the control group (17.2% [5/29] vs. 4.7% [5/107], p = 0.036). Preoperative radiographic assessment showed comparable main curve Cobb angles between groups (Table 7). And no significant differences were found in the immediately postoperative main curve Cobb angle (14.3° ± 9.6° vs. 15.4° ± 10.2°, p = 0.564) or correction rate (70.8% ± 14.2% vs. 68.4% ± 15.3%, p = 0.247). Furthermore, there were no significant differences between the GJH and non‐GJH groups in SRS‐22r domain scores at least 1 year postoperatively (p > 0.05) (Table 7).

Discussion

The primary finding of this study was that, among patients with AIS, those with concomitant GJH had greater spinal flexibility and larger TK compared to those without GJH. Furthermore, a relatively higher incidence of surgical complications was observed in the GJH group compared to the non‐GJH group.

Relationship Between GJH and Structural Spinal Deformity

Joint hypermobility refers to the excessive range of motion in the musculotendinous unit. And generally, GJH is not regarded as a specific disease, but rather a common condition or phenomenon related to the musculoskeletal system of an individual [23]. Individuals with GJH exhibit reduced soft tissue restraint around the joints, which increases the mechanical load on spinal motion segments due to an expanded range of motion. This is attributed to facet capsule laxity, intervertebral ligament laxity, and enhanced intervertebral disc motion, potentially leading to earlier onset of degenerative changes [24, 25, 26]. Although some results remain controversial, the majority of previous studies have found that hypermobility was associated with the occurrence and development of low back pain, fatigue, and even disability, which were thought to result from excessive motion at spinal segments [27, 28, 29, 30, 31].

Currently, the relationship between AIS and isolated musculoskeletal hypermobility presents inconsistent findings. In a recent large population‐based study, it was found that generalized hypermobility was correlated with adolescent scoliosis and Scheuermann's kyphosis, particularly in cases of moderate and severe structural spinal changes [8]. And structural spinal changes and joint hypermobility often coexist in some hereditary diseases, all of which involve significant mutations in connective tissue genes and often require specific perioperative managements [9, 10]. These studies demonstrate that concomitant hypermobility may increase the risk of developing spinal deformities and contribute to the progression of underlying curves. Furthermore, early identification and thorough evaluation of joint hypermobility are essential for the exclusionary diagnosis of idiopathic scoliosis and for optimizing perioperative management in patients with connective tissue disorders.

Although Beighton scores showed a positive correlation with trunk rotation, no significant correlation between GJH and curve magnitude, curve type, AVR, or scoliosis length was found [15, 32]. The discrepancy in the association between GJH and the unremarkable radiological findings may be attributed to selection bias and uncontrolled confounding factors. In this study, patients with both AIS and GJH were matched with those without GJH. After case‐matching, individuals with both AIS and GJH exhibited greater spinal flexibility and larger TK compared to those without GJH. These associations were further confirmed through multivariable linear regression analyses. Moreover, the statistical significance of spinal flexibility was observed only in the MT curve. In contrast to the MT curve, the TL/L curve inherently exhibits better flexibility, suggesting that the impact of GJH on TL/L curve flexibility may be less pronounced than on the MT curve. Furthermore, it is important to note that different positions, such as supine, standing, and fulcrum bending positions, have been used to assess spinal flexibility [33, 34, 35]. As a result, the flexibility of the main curve may vary depending on the bending position employed. Future comparisons between studies should therefore be based on consistent radiographic methods.

GJH and Spinal Flexibility

Clinically, the assessment of spinal flexibility helps determine the optimal surgical strategy and predict correction outcomes [36, 37, 38, 39]. In this study, patients with AIS and GJH exhibited greater spinal flexibility compared to those without GJH, highlighting its significance in determining corrective techniques and selecting fusion levels. Moreover, consistent with our findings, Linden et al. [40] reported that patients with greater spinal flexibility were found to be more likely to experience postoperative complications. Accordingly, for patients with both AIS and GJH, greater attention should be given to the potential occurrence of postoperative complications. In addition, patients with mild AIS may undergo intensive scoliosis‐specific physiotherapy based on various approaches [41, 42]. However, individuals with both AIS and GJH often report a deterioration of their condition during physiotherapy, particularly when their hypermobile joints are not managed carefully during manual treatments [43]. Moreover, individuals with GJH are generally more susceptible to soft tissue injuries during physical exercises [44, 45]. These studies underscore the importance of early identification, assessment, and management for patients with GJH to mitigate structural spinal changes.

GJH and TK

In addition to coronal deformities, AIS is frequently associated with abnormal sagittal alignment as well. Patients with AIS, particularly those with a pronounced thoracic curve, often demonstrate a flat spine compared to controls [46]. Furthermore, an increasing number of studies have highlighted that postoperative reduction in TK may lead to adverse changes in radiographic parameters, such as lumbar disc degeneration, cervical decompensation, deterioration of the sagittal vertical axis, and proximal junctional kyphosis, which could result in clinical complications over time [47, 48, 49, 50, 51]. However, a discrepancy exists between spinal kyphosis and rod kyphosis, with considerable variability observed among patients who have undergone surgical correction [52]. These results suggest that individual factors play a critical role in achieving favorable outcomes in either conservative treatment or corrective surgery. In this study, patients with AIS and GJH exhibited larger TK compared to those without GJH. Therefore, it is crucial to take into account the differences in TK when implementing an appropriate intervention and tailored strategy to achieve optimal sagittal alignment and clinical outcomes.

Surgical Complications and Clinical Outcomes

In this study, patients with AIS and concomitant GJH demonstrated a significantly higher overall rate of surgical complications compared with matched controls, although the absolute incidence of individual events remained low. Importantly, the observed complications in the GJH group encompassed both major and minor categories, with implant‐related failure requiring revision surgery representing the most clinically relevant major complication. This finding suggests that hypermobility may predispose patients to mechanical challenges following corrective surgery. Minor complications in the GJH cohort included transient sensory deficits, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, and pleural effusion, none of which resulted in permanent neurological impairment. From a pathophysiological perspective, GJH may be associated with altered tissue structure and biomechanics, which theoretically contribute to implant‐related and wound‐related complications, even when standard surgical parameters are comparable [53]. Although patients with GJH demonstrated a higher rate of surgical complications, no significant differences in PROMs were observed between the two groups at the latest follow‐up. This negative finding is noteworthy and may indicate that the presence of GJH is associated primarily with early postoperative complications, rather than measurable differences in PROMs within the follow‐up period of this study. Alternatively, the current sample size and follow‐up duration may be insufficient to detect subtle or delayed differences in PROMs. Longer‐term studies are therefore needed to further clarify the relationship between GJH, surgical outcomes, and PROMs in patients with AIS.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the potential selection bias exists as only AIS patients who underwent surgical intervention were included. Then, the case–control design precludes the establishment of causality. Although controls were carefully matched with cases, residual confounding from unmeasured or incompletely captured variables, such as baseline functional status, surgical strategy, and rehabilitation‐related factors, remains possible. Therefore, future studies with more rigorous adjustment for potential confounders are needed to validate our findings. Additionally, a relatively small sample size and a short follow‐up duration may not be sufficient to fully assess potential differences in clinical outcomes between the two groups, including implant longevity, junctional pathology, and longitudinal PROMs. Future studies with larger cohorts and extended longitudinal follow‐up will be necessary to enable subgroup analyses and delineate the long‐term clinical implications. Besides, the diagnosis of GJH relied predominantly on the Beighton score, a widely accepted clinical tool, though other diagnostic methods could provide different insights. Notably, the Beighton score does not assess coronal mobility of the spine, and future research should consider developing a more specific tool for evaluating spinal hypermobility in patients with structural spinal changes.

Conclusions

In patients with AIS, the diagnosis of GJH was associated with greater spinal flexibility and increased TK. And patients with GJH tended to have a higher risk of surgical complications than those without GJH. These findings suggest that joint hypermobility may play an important role in the pathophysiology of structural spinal changes, and highlight the need for comprehensive evaluation and personalized interventions for patients with AIS and GJH.
