Cervical complex cystic-solid mass after surgery for papillary thyroid carcinoma: Lymph node metastasis versus thrombosed internal jugular vein aneurysm
Authors: Yang Cao, Yu Xia, Yuxin Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractJugular vein aneurysms are extremely rare, and cases concurrent with papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) and multi-site venous aneurysms are even rarer. This report describes a 59-year-old female with left internal jugular vein aneurysm and left superficial femoral vein aneurysm complicated by thrombosis after total thyroidectomy for PTC. The thrombus within the internal jugular vein was similar to a metastatic lymph node; however, a correct diagnosis was ultimately reached under the physician's examination. We present this case to enhance junior physicians' skills in the differential diagnosis of neck masses and propose a preliminary hypothesis regarding the etiology of venous aneurysms.Keywords: Ultrasound, Venous aneurysm, Thrombosis, Lymph node metastasis, Papillary thyroid carcinoma

Introduction

Venous aneurysm is a rare venous disease defined as a segment of venous dilatation attached to a normal size segment. No clear diameter cutoff exists to define dilatations as aneurysms [1]. Jugular vein aneurysms usually present as a painless neck mass that enlarges during Valsalva maneuvers or straining. Ultrasonography with color Doppler is the best examination for identifying venous aneurysms of the extremities and head and neck [2]. However, in-depth investigations of jugular vein aneurysm are now still lacking. We report a case of a 59-year-old woman with left internal jugular vein aneurysm and thrombosis after total thyroidectomy. At the same time, there was a venous aneurysm located in the patient’s left superficial femoral vein. We analyzed the similarities between thrombi and metastatic lymph nodes and conducted a preliminary exploration into the interrelationship among venous aneurysms occurring at different anatomical sites of the human body.

Case presentation

In August 2022, a 59-year-old female presented to the thyroid surgery department with anterior neck discomfort, with no history of chronic diseases. After admission, the patient underwent thyroid ultrasonography, using an IU 22 or EPIQ 7 (Philips Medical Systems, Bothell, WA) system equipped with a 5-12 MHz linear transducer. The results revealed multiple suspicious malignant thyroid nodules accompanied by structural abnormalities of multiple lymph nodes in the left neck. Subsequently, the patient received ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration biopsy, and the cytological result of the left thyroid lobe nodule was suggestive of PTC. Following a detailed discussion with the patient, a decision was made to perform total thyroidectomy at a scheduled date. Preoperative lower extremity venous ultrasound was also performed and an old recanalized thrombus was detected in the left peroneal vein. The patient then underwent total thyroidectomy and a 2 cm venous aneurysm in the left internal jugular vein was found during the operation. After evaluation by a vascular surgeon, it was determined that the venous aneurysm was small in size and the patient had no obvious clinical symptoms for a long period, thus a conservative treatment strategy was recommended. The postoperative pathological diagnosis of the patient was multifocal papillary thyroid carcinoma. The tumor invaded the capsule on the left side but not on the right side, accompanied by metastatic carcinoma in the left level II, III, IV, and VI lymph nodes. The pathological stage was classified as stage II according to the AJCC (American Joint Committee on Cancer) staging system.

In October 2022, the patient showed good recovery and returned to our hospital for follow-up. Blood tests revealed an elevated D-dimer level (15.4 mg/L) above the normal range. Ultrasound imaging identified a cystic mass (2.5 × 1.4 × 0.9 cm) located superior to the bifurcation of the left common carotid artery, and low-velocity venous flow signals at the communication point with left internal jugular vein were detected, indicating a venous aneurysm located in left internal jugular vein (Fig. 1). Lower extremity venous ultrasound revealed aneurysmal dilation of the left superficial femoral vein with hypoechoic areas on the wall, suggesting a left superficial femoral vein aneurysm with thrombosis. Physical examination showed no lower limb swelling, and the patient reported no discomfort. After evaluation by a vascular surgeon, surgical intervention was deemed not indicated, and the patient was prescribed rivaroxaban 10 mg twice daily. During the follow-up in February 2023, the patient’s serum D-dimer level had significantly decreased (1.15 mg/L). Repeat lower extremity venous ultrasound showed a reduction in thrombus size within the superficial femoral vein aneurysm. The medication was tapered to rivaroxaban 10 mg once daily, and after 3 months of continued treatment, the drug was discontinued.

Ultrasound imaging after surgery identified a cystic mass located superior to the bifurcation (A, B) of the left common carotid artery, the cystic mass was continued with internal jugular vein, and color Doppler showed low-velocity venous flow signals at the communication point (C, D), which supported the diagnosis of venous aneurysm.

In February 2024, a routine follow-up ultrasound examination revealed an oval hypoechoic lesion (2.5 × 1.1 × 0.7 cm) within the venous aneurysm. The lesion exhibited well-defined margins, heterogeneous internal echogenicity, morphologically resembling a metastatic lymph node. Color Doppler demonstrated circumferential blood flow around the lesion, suggesting thrombosis within the venous aneurysm. The ultrasonographic findings indicated that the thrombus was relatively stable, with a low risk of embolic events. Therefore, anticoagulation therapy with rivaroxaban 10 mg once daily was chosen over surgical intervention, and the patient has been maintained on this regimen to date. Subsequent ultrasound examinations confirmed a progressive reduction in thrombus size. The most recent ultrasonographic evaluation (August 2025) confirmed complete resolution of the thrombus (Fig. 2).

Following ultrasound imaging confirmed a progressive reduction in thrombus size (D, E) and the thrombus was absorbed at last (F).

Discussion

Jugular vein aneurysm is a rare venous disease, which was first described by Harris in 1928 [3]. Most of the jugular vein aneurysms are congenital and the specific etiology remains unclear, the others are acquired secondary to age, trauma, degeneration, chronic inflammation, increased venous pressure due to any cardiovascular disease, etc. [4]. Internal jugular vein aneurysms account for approximately 13% of all venous aneurysms, while lower extremity venous aneurysm is the most common subtype of venous aneurysms (77%) [5]. External jugular vein involvement is possible, though external jugular vein aneurysms are significantly less prevalent than internal jugular vein aneurysms [2].

Jugular vein aneurysms are benign and asymptomatic most of the time. Patients often present to the clinic due to a painless neck mass or are diagnosed incidentally during imaging examinations for other unrelated conditions. The common symptom of jugular vein aneurysms is pain, which usually occurs during thrombosis. Jugular vein aneurysms can rarely present with specific clinical symptoms due to the compression of adjacent tissues, for example, dysphagia and Horner’s syndrome [6]. Jugular vein aneurysms could cause massive hemorrhage secondary to traumatic rupture, but the spontaneous rupture has never been reported [7].

The diagnosis of jugular vein aneurysms is not complicated, ultrasonography with color Doppler is considered as a convenient and efficient examination for early screening. On 2-dimensional ultrasound, a jugular vein aneurysm usually appears as a well-defined anechoic cystic mass which could have a communication point with the jugular vein. Color Doppler could detect bidirectional, low-velocity color flow signals at the communication point [8]. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and angiography have a better view of the size and how the venous aneurysm connects to adjacent structures. However, they could not show the hemodynamic information and cost more than ultrasound. Hence, they usually serve as second-line modalities to confirm the diagnosis and evaluate further management [9,10].

Jugular vein aneurysm requires differentiation from various cervical masses, including lymph node, abscess, cyst, Arterio-venous malformation, branchial cleft cyst, etc. [11]. The anatomic relationship between the lesion and adjacent tissues is important for differentiating. It can be observed that jugular vein aneurysms communicate with the jugular vein while others not. Color Doppler demonstrating intraluminal flow in a jugular vein aneurysm helps differentiate it from solid tumors or cysts. In case the ultrasound imaging is atypical and the lesion is difficult to differentiate, contrast-enhanced CT, MRI, or angiography should be applied to avoid the misdiagnosis.

Current therapeutic strategies are controversial. Venous aneurysms in the head and neck have no association with venous thromboembolism [2,12], supporting conservative management in asymptomatic cases [13]. Surgical intervention could be only considered for aneurysms complicated by thrombosis, symptomatic mass effect on adjacent structures, or cosmetic concerns. However, a case of pulmonary embolisms caused by a thrombosed external jugular vein aneurysm was reported, indicating a more active management of jugular vein aneurysm. Operative approaches include proximal/distal jugular vein ligation or aneurysm excision could be considered for both internal and external jugular vein aneurysm. Postoperative outcomes demonstrate minimal severe complications for either procedure [14].

This patient represents a rare case of PTC complicated by an internal jugular vein aneurysm. In our clinical practice, we have encountered instances which junior physicians mistakenly identified an intravascular thrombus or tumor thrombus as a metastatic lymph node. This diagnostic pitfall arises because thrombi or tumor thrombi often present as hypoechoic solid masses with an oval or round shape and heterogeneous internal echogenicity. Chronic thrombi may even develop hyperechoic calcifications. On Doppler ultrasound, these solid components typically show no internal blood flow signal. When a thrombus or tumor thrombus completely fills the vascular lumen, it becomes particularly easy to misjudge its anatomical relationship with the vessel. In PTC patients with known lymph node metastasis, inexperienced examiners are especially prone to such misdiagnosis. Therefore, it is crucial to pay meticulous attention to the anatomical relationship between the lesion and surrounding tissues. The scanning field should be widened carefully to determine the origin of the lesion. Furthermore, proficient use of Doppler ultrasound is essential to delineate the relationship between the lesion and adjacent vasculature.

Since the relatively low incidence of venous aneurysms and their typically non-severe clinical consequences, in-depth investigations of jugular vein aneurysm are now still lacking, and clinicians' understanding of such conditions is often insufficient. For instance, in the case of this patient, preoperative lower extremity deep venous ultrasonography was performed but failed to indicate the presence of a femoral vein aneurysm. Due to limitations in the archival of imaging data, the precise onset time of the patient’s superficial femoral vein aneurysm cannot be confirmed. However, considering that the interval between the 2 ultrasound examinations was merely 2 months, it is more plausible that the superficial femoral vein aneurysm was missed during the preoperative ultrasound scan. To date, no publicly available studies have revealed associations between venous aneurysms occurring in different anatomical sites of the human body. It can be hypothesized that the formation of venous aneurysms may be related to congenital anomalies in the development of the vascular wall. However, given that the etiology of congenital venous aneurysms remains unclear, whether patients with a history of venous aneurysm have a higher risk of developing new venous aneurysms still requires further investigation.

Conclusion

Jugular vein aneurysm is a rare venous disease, while it remains insufficiently recognized in clinical practice. Due to turbulent and stagnant blood flow within the aneurysm, thrombosis is easy to occur. An occlusive thrombus can easily be misdiagnosed as a solid space-occupying lesion, such as a metastatic lymph node. This case involving a patient with concomitant PTC is presented to heighten clinicians’ awareness and help prevent similar diagnostic oversights.
