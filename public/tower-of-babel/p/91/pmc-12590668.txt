The effects of opium consumption on glycemic indices: a systematic review and meta-analysis of human and animal studies
Authors: Fereshte Zanjiri, Yaser Mohammadi, Laya Ravanjo, Seyed Mohammad Riahi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlucose metabolism disorders, such as insulin resistance and diabetes, are major public health challenges influenced by lifestyle factors and substance abuse, including opium. This study aims to clarify the ambiguous effects of opium on glycemic indices through a systematic review and meta-analysis of human and animal studies.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science up to June 2025. Article screening was independently performed by three reviewers in two stages. Data extraction and quality assessment were carried out by independent researchers using the Joanna Briggs Institute scale, and statistical analyses were performed using Stata14.ResultsThis meta-analysis included 22 studies (12 human and 10 animal studies, analyzed separately). The human studies, 84% of which were conducted in Iran, analyzed a total of 6,106 participants (2,312 opium users and 4,794 controls). The results of the meta-analysis generally showed that opium was able to partially, but statistically significant reduction in FBS (SMD = -0.14; 95% CI: -0.28 to -0.01; I² = 58.7%; p = 0.05) and a non-significant reduction in HbA1C (SMD = -0.21; 95% CI: -0.45 to 0.03; I² = 62.7%; p = 0.02). Animal studies consistently demonstrated elevated blood glucose levels following morphine administration.ConclusionsThis meta-analysis suggests that opium consumption may slightly reduce FBS levels, but has no consistent effect on HbA1C, indicating a likely transient impact on glycemic control.The geographical concentration of human studies in Iran is a major limitation, and cohort and prospective studies in diverse populations are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms and long-term effects of opium on glycemic indices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40360-025-00969-1.Keywords: Opium consumption, Addict, Morphine, Fasting blood glucose, HbA1C, Glycemic indices

Introduction

Glucose metabolism disorders, including insulin resistance and diabetes, are among the major public health challenges and are closely associated with other metabolic diseases such as obesity and cardiovascular conditions [1]. It is estimated that by 2030, the global population affected by glucose metabolism disorders, particularly diabetes, will reach 578 million [2]. These disorders not only significantly impact patients’ quality of life but also impose a considerable economic burden on healthcare systems [3]. Various factors, including unhealthy lifestyles, insufficient physical activity, improper diets, and drug use, contribute to the onset and progression of glucose metabolism disorders [4]. Among these, substance abuse is recognized as one of the most significant social and health challenges [5].

Opium, a traditional narcotic, has long been widely consumed in certain societies, particularly in the Middle East and South Asia [6]. Easy access, cultural traditions, and misconceptions about the benefits of opium are the main reasons for its high prevalence in these regions [7]. While opium is known for its sedative and calming effects, its adverse impact on the nervous system can cause severe dependency, psychological, mental, and physical disorders [8]. Additionally, it may affect various metabolic pathways, including those related to glucose metabolism [9]. Therefore, the effects of opium on public health are complex and multifaceted, encompassing both positive and negative influences on different physiological aspects of the body.

This traditional narcotic contains various bioactive compounds, including morphine and non-morphine alkaloids, with its effects attributed to these active biological components [10]. Opium and its derivatives are known to interact with the µ-opioid receptors located in pancreatic β-cells, potentially influencing insulin secretion [11]. Furthermore, chronic opium use may alter the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to increased cortisol levels, which can impair glucose homeostasis [12]. It may also affect hepatic gluconeogenesis and peripheral insulin sensitivity. These mechanisms offer plausible explanations for the inconsistent findings reported in previous studies [13, 14]. Azod and colleagues reported a significant reduction in fasting blood sugar (FBS) and glucose, 2 h post prandial (2hpp) in patients with type 2 diabetes (T2DM) who were addicted to opium compared to the control group [13]. In contrast, Gozashti and colleagues found that opium significantly reduced insulin levels while increasing FBS [14]. Thus, the impact of opium on glucose metabolism pathways remains ambiguous.

Since disturbances in glucose metabolism are directly linked to the onset and progression of diabetes, identifying and managing factors that influence glucose levels are crucial steps in preventing and reducing the burden of metabolic and chronic diseases, including diabetes. The aim of the present study is to investigate the effects of opium on glycemic indices through a systematic review and meta-analysis of human and animal studies.

Methods

Protocol registration

This analysis conducted in line with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [15]. This systematic review and meta-analysis were registered in Birjand University of Medical Science (BUMS) under registration code IR.BUMS.REC.1403.289.

PECOS

To clarify the search question, the PECOS (Population, Exposure, Comparator, Outcome and Study design) was used. Population: general population. Exposure: opium consumption. Comparison: Opium consumers (case group) are compared to non-opium consumers (control group). Outcome: Glycemic indices including FBS and HbA1C. Study designs: cross-sectional and case-control studies.

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted across three databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, up to June 3, 2025. The search strategy was standardized using MeSH terms and relevant keywords. Two independent researchers (F.Z and L.R) performed all searches to identify relevant articles. A third researcher (Y.M) acted as an arbitrator for resolving any disagreements. No language or time restrictions were applied to the studies. Additionally, to enhance the sensitivity of the search, the reference lists of relevant studies were manually reviewed. Details of the search strategy and keywords are provided in Tables 1 and 2, and 3 of the supplementary files.

Study selection

All articles retrieved from the databases were exported to EndNote (version X.21). After removing duplicates, a team of three reviewers (Y.M., L.R. and F.Z.) independently screened the studies in two phases: first based on titles and abstracts, and then by reviewing the full texts for eligibility. To assess inter-rater reliability, Cohen’s kappa (κ) was calculated for a random subset of 30% of the records, yielding a value of 0.82, indicating strong agreement between reviewers. Any disagreements were resolved by a fourth reviewer (S.M.R.). Finally, articles meeting the eligibility criteria were included in the study. Figure 1 illustrates the article selection process.

Flow-chart showing study selection process

Study criteria

Inclusion Criteria: (A) Case-control studies, cross-sectional studies, and retrospective cohort studies. (B) Animal studies. (C) Studies evaluating outcomes related to glycemic indices. Exclusion Criteria: (A) Studies without outcomes related to glycemic indices. (B) Participants using unknown substances. (C) Case reports, case series, commentaries, letters to the editor, published abstracts, unpublished trials, opinion articles, unstructured articles, and theses.

Data extraction

Two researchers (F.Z. and Y.M.) independently extracted data from the included studies, and the extracted data were subsequently reviewed by additional researcher (S.M.R.). The extracted information included the following: general study information (first author, country of study categorized by Human Development Index and WHO region, and publication year), study characteristics (study design—cross-sectional or case-control, duration of the study, mean age and gender distribution of participants), type of opium consumption, inclusion and exclusion criteria, sample size of case and control groups, matching variables used, percentage of diabetic and smoking participants, and the overall quality rating of each study.

Quality assessment

The included articles were assessed by 2 authors (Y.M, Z.F.) independently by using the Joanna Briggs institute scale: (1) appropriate inclusion criteria; (2) description of the study subject and setting; (3) valid measurement for all participants; (4) standard criteria for measuring the condition; (5) identification of confounding factors; (6) use strategies to deal with confounding factors; (7) valid outcome measurement; (8) use of appropriate statistical analysis. Finally, the studies refereed as high risk of bias (less than 6 out of 10 stars) were excluded.

Statistical analysis

Stata version 14.0 (Stata Corp) was used for statistical analyses. SMD (Standardized mean differences) and 95% CI (confidence interval) were used to calculate the pooled effect size of opium consumption on glycemic indices. The random-effect model was applied to estimate pooled SMD. we assessed heterogeneity across studies by Cochran’s Q and, I2. The range of I2 is between 0% and 100%, and in this study, values of 70% or larger were considered heterogenous. Meta-regression and Subgroup analysis were done to exploration of heterogeneity sources. In addition, the Begg’s test were performed to assess the publication bias. in all analyses, the significance level was considered 0.05.

Results

Search results and study characteristics

Tables 1 and 2 show the characteristics of human and animal studies, respectively. After two screening phases, a total of 22 studies (12 human studies and 10 animal studies) met the inclusion criteria and were included in the current meta-analysis (Fig. 1). The human studies were conducted between 2000 and 2021, while the animal studies spanned from 1934 to 2010. Among the human studies, 84% were conducted in Iran (n = 10), with the remaining studies conducted in Belgium and Austria (n = 1 for each). In contrast, the animal studies covered a wide range of geographical regions, including Japan, London, Texas, Tennessee, Hong Kong, Durham, San Francisco, India, and Spain.

Of the human studies, 33% focused on male participants only (n = 4), while 67% included both sexes (n = 8). The animal studies involved various species, including rabbits, cats, dogs, mice, pigs, and goats. In the human studies, the substance under investigation was opium, whereas in animal studies, morphine was the substance studied. The addiction duration in human studies ranged from 0.5 to 8.92 years. In total, data from 6,106 individuals were analyzed, with 2,312 participants in the case group (opium users) and 4,794 in the control group (non-opium users).

In the animal studies, the morphine dosage ranged from 0.5 to 75 mg. Additionally, the average age of opium users ranged from 25 to 64 years. The effects of opium and morphine on glycemic indices were inconsistent across the studies.

Meta-analysis results

The analysis was conducted on human and animals. Most human studies reports that the FBS and HbA1C levels were lower in the opium consumers compared to non-opium consumers. According to the analysis, reducing and increasing trends were observed for FBS and HbA1C level respectively during the years. Most animal studies revealed that blood glucose levels were higher in the animals after morphine consumption.

Human studies

Most human studies reports that the FBS and HbA1C levels were lower in opium consumption than the control group [5, 13, 17–24]. Contrarily, Roger et al. [16] and Gozashti et al. [14] reported that the FBS levels were higher in the case group than control group although the difference was not remarkable. Furthermore, lower levels of HbA1C were reported in the case group in comparing with the control group in 6 studies [5, 13, 17–20]. However, Reece et al. Discussion

Main finding

This comprehensive meta-analysis included 22 studies (12 human and 10 animal studies) that investigated the effects of opium consumption on glycemic indices. The human studies, predominantly conducted in Iran (84%), evaluated opium use among 6,106 individuals (2,312 users and 4,794 controls) between 2000 and 2021, while the animal studies were carried out across various regions globally from 1934 to 2010. The findings from human studies showed inconsistent effects of opium on FBS and HbA1C. Most results indicated a slight reduction in FBS (SMD, -0.14) and HbA1C (SMD, -0.37) among opium users, particularly in male cohorts, with these effects varying across different study designs (e.g., cross-sectional studies). In contrast, animal studies consistently reported an increase in blood glucose levels following morphine administration at doses ranging from 0.5 to 75 mg.

The findings of this study highlight significant patterns in the geographical distribution and demographic characteristics. The geographical distribution of human studies, with 84% concentrated in Iran, reflects a specific epidemiological pattern that may stem from the country’s proximity to Afghanistan, the world’s largest opium producer [44]. Additionally, cultural and traditional beliefs, particularly regarding the therapeutic effects of opium on chronic conditions such as diabetes and hypertension, have significantly influenced its consumption [45]. However, this geographical focus also presents limitations. The lack of sufficient studies from other regions may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Genetic, environmental, and cultural differences among populations can influence metabolic responses to opium [44–46]. Furthermore, legal and ethical constraints surrounding drug-related research in many countries have hindered the geographical expansion of such studies [45, 47].

In this meta-analysis, the observed age range (25–64 years) reflects complex and multifaceted patterns that hold significant epidemiological and physiological importance. Studies have shown that opium consumption at older ages is primarily rooted in traditional and cultural beliefs [7]. Specifically, research conducted in Iran has highlighted the widespread belief in opium’s protective effects against chronic diseases, particularly diabetes and hypertension, as a key factor driving its use among older individuals [45, 48]. Additionally, self-medication for chronic pain relief is another significant motivator for opium consumption in this age group [48]. Studies have also indicated that opium use at older ages can disrupt glucose regulation, increase insulin resistance, and alter lipid metabolism [49].

The results of this meta-analysis indicate a dual pattern in the effects of opium consumption on glycemic indices. The slight reduction in FBS levels observed in some studies, particularly in cross-sectional studies, might be attributed to the short-term effects of opium consumption [45]. Potential mechanisms underlying this observation include the immediate effects of opium on opioid receptors (mu, delta, and kappa) and the activation of downstream pathways such as PI3K/Akt and AMPK, which enhance glucose uptake by peripheral tissues and reduce hepatic glucose production [50]. These short-term effects may lead to a temporary decrease in FBS levels.

However, analysis of HbA1C, a marker of long-term glycemic control, revealed that opium consumption generally failed to significantly reduce three-month blood glucose levels. In some studies, an increase in HbA1C levels was reported in the opium-consuming group [21]. This finding suggests that the short-term effects of opium on blood glucose may not be sustained over time and could gradually give way to more detrimental metabolic outcomes. A possible explanation for this observation might be the development of insulin resistance and increased oxidative stress associated with chronic opium use [45, 51]. Long-term opium use has been associated with the activation of inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and elevated production of inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and IL-6, which may contribute to pancreatic beta-cell dysfunction and impaired glucose regulation [51, 52]. Additionally, the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and a reduction in antioxidant activity, such as SOD and GPX, could negatively affect metabolic pathways involved in glucose homeostasis [38].

The observed gender differences are also noteworthy. The results indicated that the reduction in FBS was more pronounced in studies involving only male participants. This disparity might be due to the interaction between sex hormones and opioid receptors [53]. Specifically, testosterone may exert protective effects against insulin resistance, potentially amplifying the short-term effects of opium [53, 54].

The findings from the animal studies included in this meta-analysis suggest that opium or morphine administration tends to elevate blood glucose levels in animal models. These results are more consistent with the long-term glycemic outcomes observed in human studies (particularly HbA1C), reinforcing the hypothesis that any short-term glucose-lowering effects of opium may not be sustained and could, over time, contribute to adverse metabolic consequences. However, it is important to interpret these findings with caution. Several animal studies used morphine instead of raw opium, and differences in species, dosing regimens, and metabolic responses further limit the direct extrapolation of these results to humans. Moreover, the refined and isolated nature of morphine does not fully represent the complex pharmacological profile of opium as used by humans. Thus, while animal studies provide valuable mechanistic insights, their translational relevance should be considered within these contextual limitations [45, 48, 49].

Strengths and limitations

The strengths and limitations of this meta-analysis can be evaluated from multiple perspectives. Among its strengths, this study represents the first comprehensive meta-analysis to simultaneously examine human and animal studies on the effects of opium on glycemic indices. The substantial sample size, comprising 6,106 participants, enabled robust statistical analyses. The broad time span of the included studies (ranging from 2000 to 2021 for human studies and 1934 to 2010 for animal studies) allowed for an evaluation of temporal patterns. Another notable strength is the simultaneous analysis of short-term (FBS) and long-term (HbA1c) glycemic markers, offering a more holistic assessment of opium’s metabolic effects. Furthermore, the diversity of animal species studied enhances the external validity of the findings across different preclinical models.

However, this meta-analysis also has several important limitations. The geographic concentration of 84% of the human studies in Iran may limit the generalizability of the results to other populations with different genetic, environmental, and cultural backgrounds. Additionally, a critical limitation is the lack of detailed information in most included studies regarding potential confounding variables such as duration and frequency of opium use, route of administration (e.g., oral vs. inhaled), quality and purity of opium, nutritional status, and concomitant use of antidiabetic or other metabolic medications. The presence or absence of underlying health conditions such as diagnosed diabetes, obesity, or chronic liver disease was also poorly documented in many studies. The absence of such data limits our ability to adjust for or stratify based on these important confounders, which could have biased the pooled effect estimates. Moreover, exposure to opium was primarily assessed through self-reporting in most studies, with only a few relying on clinical diagnoses or DSM criteria. Objective verification methods such as urine or blood tests for opioid detection were rarely used, introducing potential recall or social desirability bias. Additionally, most studies did not clearly distinguish between current and former users, nor did they report standardized or quantifiable measures of opium dosage. This heterogeneity in exposure classification reduces the precision and interpretability of the observed associations. Finally, while we included both human and animal studies to provide a broader perspective, the comparability of findings between the two is inherently limited. In animal models, morphine was often used as a proxy for opium, which may not fully replicate the pharmacological complexity of crude opium used by humans. Therefore, caution should be exercised when extrapolating results from animal studies to human health outcomes.

Recommendations for future studies

To further elucidate the relationship between opium consumption and glucose homeostasis, future studies should be designed and conducted with a comprehensive approach and standardized methodology. The primary priority is to establish multicenter clinical trials with international collaboration, enabling the investigation of genetic, environmental, and cultural influences on metabolic responses to opium consumption. Additionally, pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies aimed at determining dose-response relationships and assessing time-dependent effects of opium on glycemic profiles are essential. To identify the molecular pathways and signaling mechanisms involved, it is recommended to evaluate the effects of various compounds present in opium on glycemic indices. Furthermore, prospective cohort studies with long-term follow-up are necessary to assess the chronic outcomes of opium consumption on glucose metabolism and diabetic complications.

Conclusion

This comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis highlights the complex and heterogeneous relationship between opium consumption and glycemic control. The results of 22 studies revealed a dual pattern in the effects of opium on glycemic indices. A slight reduction in FBS levels was observed, while no consistent or significant effect was found on HbA1C, suggesting that any glucose-lowering effect of opium is likely transient and not sustained over time. These findings are influenced by several limitations, including the geographical concentration of studies in Iran, insufficient reporting of key confounding variables, lack of objective verification of exposure, and inconsistencies in the type and dose of opium used.

Given the growing burden of glucose metabolism disorders on public health and the cultural prevalence of opium use in specific regions, future studies should adopt standardized methodologies. Therefore, additional research is needed to better understand the long-term effects of opium consumption on glycemic indices.
