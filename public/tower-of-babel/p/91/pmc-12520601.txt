Optimal Transport based Cross-Domain Integration for Heterogeneous Data
Authors: Yubai Yuan, Yijiao Zhang, Babak Shahbaba, Norbert Fortin, Keiland Cooper, Qing Nie, Annie Qu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDetecting dynamic patterns shared across heterogeneous datasets is a critical yet challenging task in many scientific domains, particularly within the biomedical sciences. Systematic heterogeneity inherent in diverse data sources can significantly hinder the effectiveness of existing machine learning methods in uncovering shared underlying dynamics. Additionally, practical and technical constraints in real-world experimental designs often limit data collection to only a small number of subjects, even when rich, time-dependent measurements are available for each individual. These limited sample sizes further diminish the power to detect common dynamic patterns across subjects. In this article, we propose a novel heterogeneous data integration framework based on optimal transport to extract shared patterns in the conditional mean dynamics of target responses. The key advantage of the proposed method is its ability to enhance discriminative power by reducing heterogeneity unrelated to the signal. This is achieved through the alignment of extracted domain-shared temporal information across multiple datasets from different domains. Our approach is effective regardless of the number of datasets and does not require auxiliary matching information for alignment. Specifically, the method aligns longitudinal data from heterogeneous datasets within a common latent space, capturing shared dynamic patterns while leveraging temporal dependencies within subjects. Theoretically, we establish generalization error bounds for the proposed data integration approach in supervised learning tasks, highlighting a novel tradeoff between data alignment and pattern learning. Additionally, we derive convergence rates for the barycentric projection under Gromov-Wasserstein and fused Gromov-Wasserstein distances. Numerical studies on both simulated data and neuroscience applications demonstrate that the proposed data integration framework substantially improves prediction accuracy by effectively aggregating information across diverse data sources and subjects. Introduction

In large-scale scientific studies, extracting generalizable knowledge from multimodal and heterogeneous data is crucial for achieving more accurate predictions, decisions, and interpretations (Halevy, Rajaraman, and Ordille 2006). These studies often involve collecting vast amounts of data from diverse modalities and subjects, making efficient information aggregation particularly challenging (Boufares and Salem 2012). Overcoming these challenges necessitates the development of advanced data integration strategies.

A significant and often intractable source of data heterogeneity arises from systematic distinctions between data sources in terms of representation and underlying distribution. This leads to misaligned patterns, as the same information may be inconsistently measured and collected across sources. For instance, medical institutions in different regions often use distinct diagnostic processes, complicating efforts to aggregate data from electronic health records or medical tests (Mikkelsen and Aasly 2001). Furthermore, intrinsic heterogeneity in data distributions across sources can obscure common patterns. In neuroscience applications, which are the focus of detailed discussion in this article, the distributions of neuronal activation frequency and intensity can vary significantly across subjects or species, even when responding to the same external stimuli or cognitive tasks. These variations reflect the complex individual differences inherent in neural systems (Akil et al. 2011; Sejnowski, Churchland, and Movshon 2014).

Many domain adaptation methods have been proposed in recent years for integrating complex multi-source datasets (Kouw and Loog 2019; Farahani et al. 2021). Existing techniques can be broadly classified into instance-based and feature-based approaches. Instance-based methods (Huang et al. 2006; Gretton et al. 2008) address data distribution shifts among sources by employing various sample re-weighting strategies. However, these methods are generally not feasible when the covariates collected from each source differ, or the measurement formats are not uniform, as the density ratios of data distributions across datasets are not well-defined. Feature-based adaptation approaches (Gopalan, Li, and Chellappa 2011; Gong et al. 2012) aim to transform the original features into new feature representations that are shared across datasets from different domains, enabling the learning of cross-domain patterns based on the transformed features. Existing methods use a variety of parametric and nonparametric models to learn feature transformation mappings, including factor models (Lindén et al. 2022), Gaussian processes (Ebrahimi et al. 2020), kernel methods (Long et al. 2013), and neural networks (Long et al. 2015). The performance of domain adaptation heavily depends on whether the feature transformation model can accurately capture the mechanisms underlying data heterogeneity in each source. In real-world applications, this assumption is often violated due to the complex and significant within-source heterogeneity. Furthermore, in many fields, such as neuroscience, the number of data sources is often small, and samples collected from the same source are strongly dependent. These issues reduce the effective information extraction for modeling feature transformations and limit the power of over-parameterized models, such as deep learning. Another important line of work utilizes the idea of multi-task representation learning to extract transferable features from heterogeneous datasets (Gebru, Hoffman, and Fei-Fei 2017; Zhang and Chen 2022; Tian, Gu, and Feng 2023).

Optimal transport, as a powerful tool for comparing and learning from data distributions, has been widely used in various machine learning tasks, including supervised learning (Frogner et al. 2015), unsupervised learning (Yan, Wang, and Rigollet 2024), and representation learning (Cherian and Aeron 2020). In this article, we aim to develop a novel and general cross-domain heterogeneous data integration framework, called integrated latent alignment (ILA), building on recent advances in optimal transport (Agueh and Carlier 2011; Courty, Flamary, and Tuia 2014; Titouan et al. 2019) and domain adaptation (Courty et al. 2016, 2017). Specifically, we first align both the geometric structure of domain-specific covariates and the covariateresponse patterns from different datasets. Cross-domain alignment enables filtering out domain-specific heterogeneity and aggregating data from each domain into a shared latent space. Then we can adopt supervised learning methods in the aligned latent space to capture the common conditional mean patterns of response. Unlike existing methods, the proposed method bypasses the data heterogeneity modeling. The key novelty of the proposed method lies in connecting the common patterns in the conditional distribution of responses with the geometric structure of the data distribution and integrating datasets from different sources through geometric alignment.

The new integration design introduces several significant advantages. It offers a principled strategy for integrating data from heterogeneous subjects and increasing statistical power in identifying common patterns. Additionally, the geometric alignment avoids requiring restrictive assumptions about the transformation mappings from heterogeneous domain-specific data to cross-domain features. As a result, the integration is more flexible and robust to intractable data heterogeneity and perturbations between responses and covariates across subjects. Furthermore, the integration remains effective even when the number of data sources is small. Notably, our alignment procedure does not require cross-source data to have uniform variable collections. Thus, the covariates across datasets can differ in terms of measurement, dimension, and domain. Finally, the proposed method can incorporate heterogeneous longitudinal datasets from different domains and extract the common temporal patterns of the target responses. Both the numerical studies on simulations and the neuroscience applications of the proposed data integration method demonstrate substantial improvements in supervised learning task performance compared to methods that use individual datasets alone.

From a theoretical perspective, we provide rigorous guarantees for the proposed data integration approach in supervised learning tasks. Specifically, we derive generalization error bounds for both regression and classification setting by integrating data from multiple sources. We establish a series of convergence rates for the discrepancy between the integrated data distribution and the true data-generating distribution. Our results show that generalization errors depend on both the bias introduced by data integration and the estimation errors of the supervised learning models. These findings apply to a class of widely used nonparametric models. Different to existing theoretical works, our analysis reveals a trade-off between data alignment and pattern learning in the context of data integration. Specifically, we demonstrate that data integration can improve generalization errors in supervised learning tasks if the complexity of regression or classification models exceeds that of integration mappings. Additionally, our theoretical results contribute to a deeper understanding of optimal transport, particularly with respect to the statistical properties of the Gromov-Wasserstein (GW) distance and the fused Gromov-Wasserstein (FGW) distance. First, we establish the existence of Monge maps for the FGW problem under squared distances. Second, we derive convergence rates for the barycentric projection under GW and FGW distances. To the best of our knowledge, these two results are the first in this area of research. Third, we provide an explicit representation of the FGW barycenter under the assumption of Gaussian covariate distributions. These results are nontrivial extensions of well-established findings within the Wasserstein distance framework and pave the way for future studies on optimal transport problems involving GW and FGW.

This article is organized as follows: Section 2 introduces the background of optimal transport and its notations. Section 3 describes the proposed data integration method for supervised learning, along with the corresponding optimization algorithms. Section 4 provides a theoretical analysis of the new integration approach. Section 5 presents simulation studies, while Section 6 illustrates the application of the proposed method to a challenging, yet common, neuroscience problem. The final section offers conclusions, a discussion, and future directions.

Background and Notations

Optimal transport (OT) has been widely applied in various machine learning fields (e.g., domain adaptation and transfer learning), to provide a principled way of aligning distributions while preserving the geometric structures. The classical OT based on the Wasserstein distance requires that data distributions be defined on the same space, which may not be satisfied in many real applications where data could arise from different domains of distributions. To overcome this limitation, the Gromov–Wasserstein distance (Mémoli 2014) and fused Gromov–Wasserstein distance (Vayer et al. 2020) have been introduced to extend the concept of OT to cross-domain distribution alignment. In what follows, we present the relevant concepts and notations of OT, particularly those pertinent to the proposed method in this article.

For a measurable map T:X→Y, the pushforward of a measure μ∈𝒫(X), denoted by T#μ, is defined as T#μ(A)≔μT−1(A) for any Borel set A∈Y. Real Data Application

In this section, we apply the proposed ILA framework to a rodent electrophysiological study, where the neuronal spiking data were collected as rats performed a non-spatial sequence memory task Allen et al. (2014). Briefly, the task involves repeated presentations of a sequence of odors (A,B,C,and D) through the same port, and requires subjects (rats) to determine whether each odor is presented “in sequence” (InSeq; e.g., ABC_) or “out of sequence” (OutSeq; e.g., ABD_) to receive a reward.

Here we use spike data from five rats as they are presented different odors in the sequence memory task. The spike data from the lth rat is reformulated as a three-dimensional data array 𝒜(l)=Aijk,l=1,2,3,4,5 where the i,j,k represent the trials, neurons, and time points for each rat, respectively. The spike data 𝒜(l) consists of binary elements, where Aijk=1 indicates that a specific neuron fired and Aijk=0 otherwise. In each trial, the subjects are presented with one of four odors labeled “A”, “B”, “C”, and “D”. We then extract 4 sec of spike data per trial (2 sec before and after odor onset) in bins of 10 ms, resulting in 400 time points for each trial. In the following, we investigate a subset of the original spike data from time interval between the 250 millisecond and 500 millisecond after the onset of odors. This time interval of the data was shown to exhibit the strongest differentiation of odor information, as well as significant differentiation of neuronal coding for different temporal order and trial outcome information (Shahbaba et al. 2022). In summary, the dimensions corresponding to trials, neurons, and time points of spike data for the five rats are 168×46×25,152×49×25,207×104×25,133×92×25, and 174×79×25, respectively. More detailed background on the electrophysiological study and neural spiking data are provided in Section 1 and 11 of the supplemental materials.

In the following, we first compress the original temporal spike data into a low-dimensional latent space for each rodent by using autoencoder with implementation details in the Section 10 of the supplemental materials. Through the above process, the spike data from each 100ms sub-window are compressed into two-dimensional latent features. We collect the preprocessed spike data from the 16 sub-windows for the sth rodent (s=1,2,3,4,5) as X(s)(1),X(s)(2),…,X(s)(16),Y(s), where X(s)(t)∈ℝnl×2 denotes the latent neural representation of the sth rat at the tth sub-windows (t=1,…,16) and nl is the number of trials from the lth rat. In addition, Y(s)∈{1,2,3,4}ns denotes the odor labels “A”, “B”, “C”, “D” corresponding to each trial of the sth rat. In the following, we first apply the proposed integrated supervised learning method in Section 3.1 to the preprocessed spike data, and compare the results with that of individual learning on the odor classification. Specifically, we sample 20% of the trials from each rat as training data, and the remaining 80% of trials serve as testing data. Then we train a classifier by our integration method and the individual learning method separately, and investigate the corresponding classification accuracies for the two methods on each sub-window. Following (Shahbaba et al. 2022), we use the K-nearest neighbors algorithm (KNN) as our classifier to allow for nonlinear boundaries among the latent neural representations from different odors.

Figure 1 illustrates the classification accuracy of KNN trained at each 100ms sub-windows by the individual learning method (blue line) and the proposed integration learning method (red line), respectively. The results show that the proposed ILA method achieves higher odor classification accuracies for all five rodents over the sequential sub-windows. The improvement from ILA suggests that the neuronal ensembles from the five rodents share a common feature: they exhibit a differentiation of odor information within that time window. More specifically, it shows that our approach can detect the common neuronal coding among the different subjects, and uses the shared pattern to enhance the performance of odor classification. The result shows that the data integration method achieves about 10% higher average classification accuracy compared with the individual learning method. For this specific application, this is a substantial improvement.

This can be explained by the fact that the sequence always starts with odor A, and it is preceded by a running period (so that activity dynamics associated with running extend into the odor period). Therefore, we focus on comparing the coding properties and dynamics observed during odors B–D, in which the behavioral and cognitive demands are matched. Specifically, the activity on odors B, C, and D trials starts at a similar location in the latent space and heads toward the center of their respective clusters (0–250 ms), reaching maximum differentiation in the 250–500 ms period. Toward the end of the odor presentations (750–1250 ms), the activity for each odor then converges back toward the beginning of their trajectory (faster for odor D, than C, than B), before reaching a distinct region of the latent space in the post-trial activity (1500ms; indicated by arrowheads). These results would have been difficult to interpret without this data integration approach, as the neural activity of each subject would otherwise reside in distinct latent spaces, making cross-subject comparisons impossible.

Each dot indicates the location of the neural activity for a 100-ms bin from a given subject, with the color denoting the odor label of the corresponding trial. Discussion

In this article, we propose a novel and general data integration framework based on optimal transport for supervised learning tasks. Our method is effective when the data domains and dimensionalities of each dataset differ, and it remains effective even with a small number of datasets. We provide comprehensive theoretical analysis and performance guarantees for the proposed method. We apply our framework to analyze electrophysiological data from a rodent study investigating non-spatial memory. The method identifies common neuronal coding patterns across subjects during the processing of different odors. This finding aligns with previous research showing that hippocampal neurons represent information critical for task performance. Our results suggest that integrating heterogeneous datasets can enhance the discriminative power of predictive models, fostering advancements in scientific research and related fields.
