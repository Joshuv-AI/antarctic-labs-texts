Long-term loss of TGF-β signaling in smooth muscle cells of aged adult mice disrupts proximal aortic wall structure and homeostasis
Authors: Chloe Y. Lee, Elizabeth F. Johnston, Natalie S. Lim, David A. Dichek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Roles of transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) signaling in smooth muscle cells (SMC) of the adult vasculature remain unclear. In this study, we investigate long-term effects of SMC-specific loss of TGF-β signaling in the aortas of adult mice. Eleven-month-old mice with SMC-specific Cre allele (Acta2-CreERT2) and homozygous floxed type II TGF-β receptor (Tgfbr2) were injected with tamoxifen. Aortas (Acta2-CreERT2 +/0 Tgfbr2 flox/flox; n = 10, 5 males and 5 females) were harvested 16 weeks post-injection and compared with aortas of age-matched control mice (Acta2-CreERT2 0/0 Tgfbr2 flox/flox; n = 10, 5 males and 5 females). There were no grossly observed aortic pathologies or deaths 16 weeks after Tgfbr2 deletion. However, histological examination yet showed significant disruption to aortic wall structure, including aortic dilation, medial thickening, elastolysis, and penetrating aortic ulcers compared to Acta2-CreERT2 0/0 Tgfbr2 flox/flox mice. Furthermore, SMC Tgfbr2 deletion caused macrophage infiltration in the aortic media and collagen accumulation in the tunica adventitia. Long-term loss of TGF-β signaling in aged adult mice does not cause severe aortopathies such as aortic dissection or lethal rupture. However, TGF-β signaling continues to play a critical role in aortic wall homeostasis in adult vasculature. These results caution against proposed therapies that reduce or inhibit TGF-β signaling to prevent or treat aortic diseases in humans.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54906-1.

Introduction

Transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling in vascular smooth muscle cells (SMC) plays a crucial role in aortic development and maintenance. Conversely, elevated TGF-β signaling in adults has been implicated in a spectrum of aortic pathologies. Aortic tissues from patients with Marfan syndrome, a heritable disease caused by a pathologic variant of FBN1, demonstrate signs of elevated TGF-β signaling1–3. Similar findings of increased TGF-β signaling have been found in patients with Loeys-Dietz syndrome, which includes disease caused by heterozygous variants in the genes (TGFBR1 or TGFBR2) that encode the type I or type II TGF-β receptors4. The association of elevated TGF-β signaling with aortopathies has led to a growing interest in therapeutic strategies inhibiting TGF-β signaling to prevent or treat aortic aneurysms5.

Prior studies have demonstrated that the blockade of TGF-β signaling in embryonic or young mice can be severely pathogenic or even lethal. During embryonic development, disruption of SMC-specific TGF-β signaling leads to hemodynamic compromise and embryonic lethality6–9. In young mice from 3 to 13 weeks of age, loss of SMC TGF-β signaling through deletion of Tgfbr1 or Tgfbr2 causes severe aortic pathology, including intramural hemorrhage, ulceration, dissection, and aortic rupture resulting in death10–13. These results have raised concerns regarding therapeutically inhibiting TGF-β signaling in childhood or in younger adults.

The role of TGF-β signaling in SMC of adult vasculature still remains unclear. Li et al. demonstrated an inverse relationship between the age of mice (up to 9 weeks) at the time of SMC-specific Tgfbr2 deletion and the occurrence of aortic dissections11. Our recent study investigating effects of Tgfbr2 deletion in older (11-month-old) mice found aortic hypercontractility and mild medial thickening without any severe pathologies at 4 weeks after deletion14. The absence of severe pathology upon TGF-β signaling ablation in SMC of adult mice would leave open the possibility that TGF-β blockade might be used therapeutically in adult vasculature.

In this study, we further investigate whether SMC-specific TGF- β signaling maintains aortic homeostasis in older mice. We deleted Tgfbr2 in VSMC of 11-month-old mice and examined the aortas at 15 months of age, 16 weeks after deletion. We found substantial increase in medial thickening, dilation, inflammatory cell infiltration, and adventitial collagen accumulation in the aortas of Tgfbr2 deleted mice. These results provide further support for the role of SMC TGF-β signaling in maintaining aortic homeostasis even in adult vasculature and cautions against blockade of TGF-β signaling as a therapeutic strategy for aortic aneurysms in adults.

Methods and materials

Please see the Major Resources Table in the Supplemental Materials.

Mice

Heterozygous Acta2-CreERT2 mice were provided by Daniel Metzger and Pierre Chambon15 and were bred with Tgfbr2flox/flox (exon 4) mice from Per Levéen and Stefan Karlsson16 to generate experimental Acta2-CreERT2 +/0 Tgfbr2 flox/flox mice. Controls were Acta2-CreERT2 0/0 Tgfbr2 flox/flox littermates. Both alleles were maintained in the C57BL/6 J background (> 10 generations). Mice were housed in a specific-pathogen-free facility, fed a standard laboratory diet, and genotyped by PCR of tail DNA (DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit; Qiagen; #69,504); primers are in Table 1. Cre-mediated recombination was stimulated in 11-month-old Acta2-CreERT2 +/0 Tgfbr2 flox/flox mice by 5 consecutive daily intraperitoneal injections of 1 mg of tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich Corp.; #T5648) diluted in autoclaved olive oil (MP Biomedicals; #102,502). These experimental mice are denoted hereafter as TBR2SMΔ (n = 10; 5 males and 5 females). Acta2-CreERT2 0/0 controls received the same injections and are denoted hereafter as TBR2f./f (n = 10, 5 males and 5 females). For finding the Tgfbr2 recombination efficiency, one additional group of Acta2-CreERT2 +/0 Tgfbr2 flox/flox mice were injected with olive oil sham and were denoted as “Sham Control” group (n = 6; 3 males and 3 females). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All experimental protocols animal studies were approved by the Office of Animal Welfare of the University of Washington.

Isolation of aortic medial layer

Mice were anesthetized with intraperitoneal ketamine and xylazine. Following thoracotomy, mice were euthanized by exsanguination and infusion of ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline via cardiac puncture. Aortas were excised, placed on a silicone-bottomed dissection dish, cut open longitudinally, and pinned with the lumen up. Luminal endothelium was removed by blotting with a sterilized cotton swab. The aortic media was peeled from the adventitia with fine-point forceps, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at –80 °C.

DNA extraction and measurement of Tgfbr2 recombination

Genomic DNA was extracted from aortic medias with the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen; #69,504). qPCR was performed on aortic medial DNA using the Luna Universal qPCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs; #M3003) with primers specific for the unrecombined allele of Tgfbr2 (Table 1). The qPCR signal for unrecombined Tgfbr2 was normalized to the qPCR signal for Gapdh in the same extract and quantified using a standard curve constructed by measuring unrecombined Tgfbr2 and Gapdh in serial dilutions of genomic DNA extracted from an TBR2f./f mouse without tamoxifen injections. Genomic DNA of an TBR2SMΔ injected with tamoxifen was used to create the recombined Tgfbr2 allele qPCR standard curve. This standard curve was used to extrapolate relative Tgfbr2 unrecombined allele or recombined allele DNA level of experimental and control mice.

Ultrasound measurements

Aortic ultrasonography was performed in experimental and control mice (n = 13 each) 16 weeks after the last day of tamoxifen injections. Mice were anesthetized with 1%-2% isoflurane and oxygen at 1L/minute. Heart rate and arterial oxygen saturation were monitored with a foot clip sensor and body temperature was monitored with a rectal probe. Anesthetized mice were placed on a 37 °C heated platform in a supine position, and thoracic and abdominal hair removed with a depilatory cream (Nair). Warmed ultrasound scan transmission gel was applied to the ventral surface and the ultrasound probe set at 30 MHz was positioned perpendicular to the platform. After visualization of the proximal aorta, 3–4 ultrasound scan cine loops were collected over three cardiac cycles. Systolic and diastolic diameters of the aortic root and proximal aorta were measured using the B-mode view on the Vevo 2100 ultrasound system. All measurements were made by an observer blinded to genotype and were averaged from the 3 cardiac cycles.

Tissue harvest, processing, and analysis for histology

10 TBR2f./f and 10 TBR2SMΔ (five males and five females each) were anesthetized with ketamine and xylazine. Aortas were perfused with saline then subjected to in situ perfusion-fixation with 10% formalin using a gravity-fixed system. The fixative reservoir was maintained at a height of 1 m above the mice, corresponding to a hydrostatic pressure of approximately 74 mmHg. This pressure was chosen to approximate the physiological mean arterial pressure and was applied identically across all experimental and control cohorts. The aortas were further fixed in 10% formalin overnight at 4 °C, then placed in 70% ethanol at 4 °C for long-term storage. Prior to OCT (Fisher Scientific; 23–730-571) embedding, aortas were placed in 15% sucrose in PBS followed by 30% sucrose in PBS until the aortas sank. The ascending aorta was separated from the rest of the aorta, by making transverse cuts at the cranial end of the aortic root and the cranial border of the brachiocephalic artery take-off and embedded in OCT. The OCT blocks were stored in -80 °C for long-term storage, then later cut into 60- serial 8-μm-thick cross-aortic sections. Each slide was comprised of 5 equidistant aortic sections that were spaced 96 μm apart along the ascending aorta.

Histology, immunohistochemistry, and fluorescence microscopy

Formalin-fixed aortic sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin, Prussian blue, Picrosirius red, Movat pentachrome, and anti-LGALS3 (Mac-2). Stained sections were imaged with a Leica DM4000B microscope and Leica DFC295 digital camera at 100x. Picrosirius red stained sections were imaged under brightfield with circularly polarizing light using an Olympus BX41 microscope fitted with an Olympus SC100 color camera. Picrosirius-polarized images for histology analysis were acquired at 100 × magnification, and stitched together with Fiji v. 2.9.0. DAPI-stained aortic sections were imaged with a Nikon AX R with NSPARC Confocal microscope system at 100x.

Hematoxylin and eosin stained sections were observed under fluorescein filters to visualize the autofluorescence of elastic fibers. We defined an elastic fiber break as an area where two free ends of the elastic fiber were visible along a continuous elastic fiber. Two observers blinded to genotype counted the number of elastic fiber breaks per section in each mouse, with 3–5 aortic sections at different locations along the ascending aorta observed for each mouse and the mean number of elastic fiber breaks calculated per section. Inter-observer and intra-observer agreement were verified with Bland–Altman analysis.

For Prussian blue staining, formalin-fixed frozen aortic sections were dried at room temperature for 1 h. The aortic sections were incubated in a 10% hydrochloric acid (Millipore Sigma; #HX0603) 10% potassium ferrocyanide (Sigma-Aldrich; #P3289) solution for 20 min. Sections were then counterstained with nuclear fast red (Ricca Chemical; #R5463200), dehydrated with ethanol and xylene, and cover-slipped with Permount Mounting Medium (Fisher Scientific; #SP15-500). Prussian blue staining was evaluated as either present or absent by two observers blinded to the genotype of the mice. An aortic specimen known to have past aortic bleeds was used as a positive control.

Formalin-fixed frozen aorta sections from TBR2f./f and TBR2SMΔ mice were air-dried for 1 h at room temperature before staining in picrosirius red staining solution (Abcam; #AB246832) for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were subsequently washed twice in 0.5% acetic acid, dehydrated in three changes of 100% EtOH, then cleared in two changes of 100% xylenes, before cover slipping with Permount Mounting Medium (Fisher Scientific; #SP15-500). Slides were allowed to dry overnight at room temperature before imaging. All image analysis was conducted using Fiji software. Picrosirius-polarized light images were first converted to a RGB stack, then converted to a HSB stack image17. The area of the adventitia was estimated visually, and traced on the brightness slice image to designate the adventitial area as a region of interest (ROI). A histogram representative of collagen quantity was produced from the ROI within the Hue channel image. The sum of the histogram was used to estimate total collagen within the adventitia. Adventitial ROI measurements were completed by two observers blinded to genotypes. Agreement of total collagen quantification between users was evaluated using a Bland–Altman test.

For immunohistochemistry of LGALS3, aortic sections were placed in antigen unmasking solution (Vector Laboratories; #H-3300) for 10 min at 95 °C. After blocking endogenous peroxidase activity, sections were incubated with 1 µg/mL Galectin-3 antibody (Cedarlane; #CL8942AP) or Rat IgG2a Isotype control (Invitrogen; #02–9688), followed by 0.5 µg/mL biotinylated rabbit anti-rat IgG (Vector Laboratories; #BA-4001). Binding of the secondary antibody was detected with Vectastain ABC-HRP kit (Vector Laboratories; #PK-4000) and ImmPACT NovaRED Substrate Kit (Vector Laboratories; #SK-4805). Finally, the aortic sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. The positive cells in both the aortic media and adventitia were quantified across 3–5 sections per mouse, and the resulting counts were averaged to calculate the mean number of macrophages per section.

For counting medial cellularity and cell density, aortic sections stained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich; #F6057) were used. For each mouse, 2–3 sections were analyzed and 5 images per section were taken at evenly spaced intervals around the medial circumference, capturing both the internal and external elastic laminae. Within each image, nuclei were counted in the region defined by the internal and elastic laminae and the image boundaries. The analyzed area was quantified using planimetry in ImageJ. Cell density for each image was calculated as the number of nuclei divided by the corresponding area of the region where nuclei were counted. These values were then averaged to obtain a mean medial cell density for each mouse. To estimate total medial cell number per section, the mean medial cell density was multiplied by the total medial area of each section (determined by planimetry of a nearby Movat pentachrome stained slide).

Aortic planimetry

Movat pentachrome stained aortic sections were used to visualize the aortic media and quantify its dimensions, with 3–5 sections at different locations along the ascending aorta analyzed for each mouse. The internal elastic lamina and external elastic lamina of the aortic media were traced using Image J software to quantify their respective lengths. The aortic medial area was calculated as that between the external and the internal elastic laminae. The medial thickness was calculated as the difference between the radii of external and internal elastic lamina, assuming circular geometry. Planimetry measurements were made by two observers blinded to the genotype. Inter-observer and Intra-observer agreement were verified by Bland–Altman analysis.

Blood pressure measurements

Blood pressure was measured 7 weeks after tamoxifen injection in a designated quiet area using the CODA noninvasive blood pressure acquisition system (Kent Scientific; #CODA-HT2). Mice were guided into restraint holders (Kent Scientific; #HLD-MM), which were adjusted to prevent excessive movement, and placed on a heating pad preheated to 33–35 °C. Mice were acclimated to the heating pad and the holder for 5 min before recording blood pressure. Each recording session consisted of 20 inflation and deflation cycles of the tail cuff (5 warmup cycles and 15 experimental cycles).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis is included in each figure and described in the respective figure legends. The number of independent samples analyzed per condition (n) and P-values are stated in the figures and the results section. Data sets were tested for normality using a Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous data with normal distribution and equal variances were evaluated using a Student’s t test. Normally distributed data with unequal variances were evaluated using a Welch’s t test. All normally distributed data graphs are shown as mean ± SEM. For data sets that did not follow normal distribution, a Mann–Whitney non-parametric analysis was used, and data graphs are presented as median ± interquartile range. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical data between control and experimental groups. All statistical tests were completed with GraphPad Prism (Version 8.3.0).

Variable tamoxifen-independent CreERT2 Tgfbr2 deletion

Spontaneous tamoxifen-independent Cre-activity has been reported in other CreERT2 mouse lines18,19. Tamoxifen independent Cre activity would lead to a gradual deletion of Tgfbr2 over a long period instead of promptly at 11 months. Therefore, we deemed it important to test for unintended recombination. To investigate the extent of tamoxifen independent Cre recombination, we used three groups of 6 mice each for comparison: 1) TBR2SMΔ mice given tamoxifen in olive oil, 2) TBR2SMΔ mice given olive oil sham, and 3) TBR2f./f mice given olive oil sham. Seven weeks after injection, we quantified the amount of recombined Tgfbr2 allele in aortic medial DNA by qPCR. The primers were designed to specifically detect the recombined allele but not the unrecombined allele (Figure IA in Supplemental Figures). We found that the level of detected recombined allele in TBR2SMΔ mice with sham was 22.9% of the recombined allele relative to the TBR2SMΔ mice with tamoxifen (Figure IB-C in Supplemental Figures). The distribution of tamoxifen independent Cre activity appeared to be dimorphic, ranging from 5 to 40% detection of the recombined Tgfbr2 allele. We conclude that spontaneous deletion of Tgfbr2 due to tamoxifen independent Cre activity may be occurring variably in some mice prior to tamoxifen injection at 11 months of age.

Loss of TGFBR2 in aortic SMC does not cause gross aortopathy

Aortic root and ascending aorta from 13 TBR2SMΔ and 13 TBR2f./f mice were observed with ultrasound 16 weeks after tamoxifen injections (Fig. 1D). Aortic root diameter and ascending aorta diameter were not significantly different during either systole or diastole between TBR2SMΔ mice and TBR2f./f (both P = 0.2; Fig. 1E and F). Upon completion of ultrasound observation, aortas were explanted and grossly observed. Neither aortic hemorrhage nor intramural hematomas were detected in TBR2SMΔ mice or TBR2f./f mice.

Loss of TGFBR2 in aortic SMC thickens and dilates the proximal aorta

Formalin-fixed aortas from 10 TBR2SMΔ and 10 TBR2f./f mice were sectioned and stained with Movat pentachrome. We measured the length of the internal and external laminas to determine the effect of Tgfbr2 deletion on aortic dilation. The internal laminal length was 10.8% greater in TBR2SMΔ mice than in TBR2f./f mice (3.34 ± 0.09 mm versus 3.01 ± 0.11 mm; P = 0.03; Fig. 1G). External laminal length increased by 11.8% compared to TBR2f./f mice (3.65 ± 0.09 mm versus 3.26 ± 0.11 mm; P = 0.01; Fig. 1H). The internal and external laminal length measurements were also used to calculate aortic medial area and thickness. Medial area increased by 39.1% in TBR2SMΔ mice, compared with TBR2f./f mice (0.171 ± 0.006 mm2 versus 0.123 ± 0.006 mm2; P = 0.0001; Fig. 1I) and medial thickness was 25.1% greater in the TBR2SMΔ mice (0.049 ± 0.002 mm versus 0.039 ± 0.001 mm; P = 0.0004; Fig. 1K) compared to TBR2f./f mice.

To test whether the aortic ulcerations were correlated to decreased medial cellularity, we counted the total number of medial cells in aortic sections. Medial cell density in the ascending aorta was similar between both groups (3661 nuclei/mm2 versus 3529 nuclei/mm2; P = 0.5; Figure VA in Supplemental Figures), indicating no evidence of reduced cellular density in TBR2SMΔ mice. In contrast, total medial cell number per section was 40.3% increased in TBR2SMΔ aortas compared to controls (630 cells/section versus 449 cells/section; P = 0.003; Figure VB in Supplemental Figures), consistent with medial expansion rather than cell loss.

Elastic fiber breaks were defined as breaks in the elastic fiber where two free ends of an elastic fiber were visible in proximity, excluding penetrating aortic ulcers. A mean of 16 ± 3 elastic fiber breaks was observed for every aortic section in TBR2SMΔ compared to 9 ± 4 elastic fiber breaks per section in TBR2f./f mice. (75.2% increase; P = 0.002; Fig. 2C-D). We also investigated the effect of TGFBR2 deletion on collagen accumulation in the aorta. Picrosirius image analysis using polarized light revealed that adventitial collagen area in the aortas from TBR2SMΔ mice was 190% greater than in aortas from TBR2f./f mice (0.114 mm2 versus 0.039 mm2; P = 0.0001; Fig. 2E-F, Figure IV in Supplemental Figures).

Aortas from 10 TBR2SMΔ and 10 TBR2f./f mice were stained with MAC2 to observe effects of TGFBR2 deletion on accumulation of immune cells. The number of macrophages increased in both the aortic media (17.3 ± 16.4 versus 1.5 ± 1.4 macrophages/section; P = 0.003; Fig. 2G-H) and the adventitia (32.2 ± 24.9 versus 1.5 ± 1.3 macrophages/section; P = 0.006; Fig. 2G-H) of TBR2SMΔ mice relative to TBR2f./f mice.

Blood pressure was not different between mice with and without SMC-specific deletion of TGFBR2

The blood pressure of 13 TBR2SMΔ mice and 13 TBR2f./f mice was measured 7 weeks after tamoxifen injection to investigate whether hypertension contributes to aortic pathologies observed. There was no statistically significant difference in systolic or diastolic blood pressure between the two groups of mice. (P = 0.2 and P = 0.4 respectively; Figure III in Supplemental Figures).

Discussion

We tested the hypothesis that long-term SMC-specific depletion of TGF-β signaling is pathogenic to the adult murine aorta by deleting Tgfbr2 in 11-month-old mice and observing aortic morphology and histology 16 weeks post-deletion. The major findings are: 1) Long-term loss of Tgfbr2 in SMCs causes major structural abnormalities in the aorta, including ulcerations, aortic medial expansion, increased medial cellularity, and elastolysis. 2) Disruptions to the aortic wall are accompanied by macrophage infiltration and adventitial collagen accumulation. 3) Blood pressure did not contribute to the changes in aortic structure. Taken together, our results suggest that TGF-β continues to play a critical role in maintaining aortic homeostasis in aged adult mice.

In a previous study, we analyzed the impact of deleting Tgfbr2 in 11-old month mice 4 weeks after deletion and found evidence of only mild aortopathy14. In contrast, longer-term (16-week) Tgfbr2 deletion in adult mice resulted in more severe aortopathy. Notably, we found penetrating aortic ulcers as well as medial elastolysis, medial and adventitial infiltration of macrophages, and adventitial collagen accumulation that were not previously observed in short-term SMC Tgfbr2 deletion. The increased elastin breaks in the aortic wall are consistent with previous findings that SMC-specific TGF-β signaling not only contributes to elastin deposition in early postnatal period, but that it also continues to play a role in elastic fiber maintenance in the adult aorta10. Increased elastolysis is consistent with presence of macrophages within the medial layer. Furthermore, the presence of penetrating aortic ulcers highlights the role of TGF-β signaling in regulating vascular wall integrity. Our data therefore confirm that TGF-β signaling serves a crucial role of vascular wall maintenance in adult vasculature.

Tgfbr2 deletion for 16 weeks also led to a significant increase in adventitial collagen, which by picrosirius staining consisted primarily of thick fibers. Accumulation of fibrillar collagen in the tunica adventitia can protect the aorta against rupture, but generally compromises its primary (elastic conduit) function, which in turn can adversely affect the hemodynamics in both the aorta and distal resistance beds20. Increased adventitial collagen has been reported similarly in other mouse models with mutations in TGF-β signaling pathway genes21,22. Although this study did not provide a mechanistic explanation for the increased collagen in the TBR2SMΔ aortas, it motivates an exciting avenue for future work. Increased adventitial collagen is common in hypertension due to an initial increase in wall stress23, but there was no increase in blood pressure in TBR2SMΔ mice compared to TBR2f./f mice. Lack of hypertension has also been noted previously in TBR2SMΔ mice13. It is possible, however, that the increased fragmentation of elastic fibers within the media could have increased load bearing in the adventitia, which in turn could have stimulated increased collagen accumulation. Previous work has shown an increase in adventitial IGF1 production in relation to fibroblast activity in young SMC-specific Tgfbr2 knockout mice 4 weeks after tamoxifen injection11. Differences in the extent of adventitial collagen remodeling in young versus old mice with 4-weeks of compromised TGF-β signaling likely relate to the differential half-lives of collagen in young vs. older mice14,23. Our extension of TGF-β disruption to 16 weeks in the adult avoided the half-life limitation. Although much work has focused on the importance of SMCs and the aortic media when examining structural changes that ultimately result in aortic aneurysm and dissection, our current study clearly encourages increased consideration of roles of adventitial collagen remodeling in aortopathy when investigating mechanisms that can promote or prevent progressive detrimental aortic wall remodeling.

Our findings nevertheless provide novel insight into whether TGF-β signaling blockade would be promising as a therapy for aortic aneurysms in adult humans. Habashi et al. showed that inhibiting TGF-β signaling through TGF-β neutralizing antibody or Losartan mitigated aortopathies in a Marfan Syndrome mouse mode24. Because multiple studies investigating effects of TGF-β blockade show that there are severe aortic pathologies in young mice, many suspected that inhibiting TGF-β signaling during development may be detrimental10–12,14. However, this still left the possibility that loss of TGF-β signaling may be therapeutic in older adult mice with fully mature vasculature. Addressing this possibility, our study highlights that, given sufficient periods of deletion, the pathology that arises in aged adult mice from loss of TGF-β can be severe – including penetrating aortic ulcers, medial remodeling with elastic fiber fragmentation, inflammatory cells infiltration, and adventitial collagen accumulation that can compromise aortic function. Although we did not find gross aortopathies that present in young mice10,11, our current study thus further supports the need for caution against inhibiting TGF-β signaling as a therapy for aortic aneurysms, though further research including longer periods of treatment of adult mice with Marfan syndrome or other predisposing pathologic variants is warranted.

Another notable aspect of our study was investigation of tamoxifen-independent CreERT2 activity in Acta2-CreERT2 mouse line. In-vivo CreERT2/loxP mediated gene deletion with cell-type specific promotors is widely used to study biologically significant signaling pathways. Administration of tamoxifen in this system allows researchers to recombine their desired gene at a specific timepoint. Spontaneous Cre recombination in the absence of tamoxifen can lead to premature recombination events, disrupting the timeline of investigations. Previously, Steffensen et. al have reported spontaneous Cre-independent recombination in a mouse model using a different SMC-specific Cre driver (SMMHC- CreERT2)25. In this current study, we show that Acta2-CreERT2 mice exhibit variable (5% to 40%) degrees of tamoxifen-independent Cre activity. Because of spontaneous tamoxifen-independent activity, it is possible that some mice experienced gradual deletion of Tgfbr2 over time instead of sudden loss of TGF-β signaling promptly (within 5 days) at 11 months of age. This evidence of Cre recombination even in the absence of tamoxifen warrants caution for use of similar mouse models using CreERT2/loxP systems and advocates for inclusion of appropriate controls (TAM-untreated mice) for future studies.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, we did not include control mice lacking the CreERT2 allele and, therefore, cannot rule out the possibility of CreERT2 expression contributing to aortic pathologies. However, others have shown that activation of SMC-specific CreERT2 in young mice without Tgfbr2 floxed alleles did not cause any vascular pathology11. Furthermore, although our histological analysis demonstrated a modest increase in internal elastic lamina length in TBR2SMΔ mice, we were unable to detect a corresponding increase in lumen diameter using echocardiography. This likely reflects the limited resolution of echocardiography for detecting subtle structural alterations in the aortic wall. Another explanation for this divergence can be the transition from dynamic in vivo loading to static ex vivo fixation, where standardized perfusion pressure may have unmasked structural remodeling not captured by echocardiography. We did not include a contemporaneous comparison of pathologies between mice with Tgfbr2 deletion from different timepoints. We were underpowered to detect sexual differences in aortic phenotypes and showed all results combining both male and female mice. Lastly, we were unable to directly assess TGFBR2 protein expression in aortic tissue at the time of harvest. Despite testing multiple commercially available antibodies, we were unable to obtain reliable detection of TGFBR2 by western blot. As a result, we relied on genomic evidence of recombination to support sustained target gene disruption. While this confirms deletion at the DNA level, we cannot exclude the possibility of residual or compensatory TGFβ signaling at later stages.

In conclusion, TGF-β signaling in SMCs continues to be essential in adulthood for maintaining aortic homeostasis in the murine aorta. Associated studies must be of appropriate durations, however, given slower turnover of extracellular matrix in adult versus younger mice, which tend to be studied more for practical reasons and the more severe phenotypic presentations. Since adult patients are typically treated over long periods, these results provide further support for caution against blockade of TGF-β signaling as a potential therapy for aortic pathologies.
