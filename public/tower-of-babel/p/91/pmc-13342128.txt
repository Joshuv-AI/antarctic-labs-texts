The Effects of Haze Pollution on County-level Entrepreneurship Activity in China
Authors: Xuan Zhang, Yuexin Li, Zidi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study explores the effects and mechanisms of haze pollution on entrepreneurial activities to provide evidence on the “pollution constraining development” dilemma and guide coordinated environmental and economic governance. Using firm registration data of 2,822 Chinese counties from 2003 to 2022, we examine the impact of haze on overall regional entrepreneurial activity and the proportion of environmental protection entrepreneurship. Results show that haze reduces overall entrepreneurial activity, but increases the proportion of environmental entrepreneurship. The effect on general entrepreneurship follows the environmental Kuznets curve and has a double threshold effect. Mechanism analysis indicates that haze leads to the outflow of human capital and the increase of environmental regulatory costs, which reduces the overall quantity of entrepreneurial activities, but it enhances the government’s support and market demand for green technologies, promoting the development of environmental entrepreneurship. Heterogeneity analysis shows that the effects are more pronounced in the eastern and central regions of China, and in regions with higher industrial agglomeration, weaker developed green finance development or lower information transparency, and that with continuous economic growth, this effect exhibits the characteristic of “being initially pronounced, then insignificant, and subsequently pronounced again”. These results highlight the significance of regional policy design, resource allocation coordination and environmental entrepreneurship promotion support for promoting the sustainable development of the economy and the improvement of the environment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45379-3.

Introduction

Entrepreneurial activity serve as an important engine of regional economic vitality and social progress1, 2. For middle-income countries like China, the expansion of entrepreneurship is an indispensable part of the transition from a low-income country to a middle-income country3. In recent years, the scale and quality of entrepreneurial activities in China have been rapidly increasing. According to the State Administration for Market Regulation, by the end of November 2024, the number of registered business entities in China had risen to 189 million. However, due to factors such as the path dependence of the traditional development model, the imbalance of regional environmental governance and the lag of environmental technology commercialization, environmental pollution and factor misallocation have become important bottlenecks constraining the structure of the entrepreneurial ecosystem4. Among them, haze pollution is not only a major factor restricting the continuous development of entrepreneurship in China, but also a common problem in the world’s pollution control5. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the pollution of the air has reached the point where 99% of the people on the earth live in polluted air, and over 6.7 million people are killed annually by diseases related to air pollution. Against this backdrop, if the problem of insufficient control of haze pollution is not effectively solved, it will impose the “double constraint” consisting of loss of human capital and increase in the cost of environmental compliance on entrepreneurship in heavily polluted areas, and further aggravate the deterioration of the entrepreneurial ecosystem in heavily polluted areas. Notably, although the environmental crisis induced by haze pollution puts some constraints, it also brings structural opportunities6. The urgent need for environmental governance caused by haze pollution promotes the rapid development of traditional industries, and in turn, it creates rich soil and market space for entrepreneurial activities in environmental technology research and pollution control services. Consequently, it gives momentum to entrepreneurship in the environmental protection industry and reshapes the entrepreneurial ecosystem.

The impact of haze pollution on entrepreneurial activities has a distinct dual nature. On the one hand, haze pollution reduces the total number of entrepreneurial activities in the region. First, the health risks caused by haze pollution leads to the outflow of human capital7, highly skilled talents flee to less polluted regions8, lacking the knowledge, technology and other essential resources needed for entrepreneurship. Second, haze pollution leads to strict environmental regulations, which not only increase the direct costs of equipment upgrade, but also increase the implicit costs of efficiency loss9. These increasing costs squeeze the profit space of start-ups and reduce the enthusiasm for entrepreneurship.

On the other hand, haze pollution accelerates the development of the environmental protection industry of entrepreneurship. First, with the intensification of haze pollution and the growing demand for environmental governance, the government introduces policy incentives such as environmental subsidies and tax reductions for environmental protection enterprises to support the operation of environmental protection enterprises10. These measures not only point out the clear direction of environmental upgrading, but also directly reduce the entry barriers of environmental protection start-ups. Second, strict environmental regulations increase firms’ demand for clean technologies11, and deteriorating haze raises public demand for products such as air purifiers12. This growing market demand will provide the environmental protection projects with a stable and abundant profit margin, and also create a number of specialized markets, expanding the entrepreneurial space of the environmental industry.

Based on industrial and commercial enterprise registration data for 2,822 Chinese counties (districts) from 2003 to 2022, this study investigates the impact of atmospheric pollutant PM2.5 on overall regional entrepreneurial activities and on the proportion of entrepreneurship in the environmental protection industry. It further explores the underlying mechanisms of these effects and the heterogeneity across regions. The results show the following. First, haze pollution reduces overall entrepreneurial activity but increases the proportion of entrepreneurship in the environmental protection industry. Second, the impacts of haze pollution on both overall entrepreneurial activity and environmental protection industry entrepreneurship exhibit an inverted U-shaped relationship, consistent with the environmental Kuznets curve. Turning point analysis indicates that the former effect lies mainly in the declining phase, while the latter is situated in the rising phase. In addition, haze pollution exerts a double-threshold effect on overall entrepreneurial activity: as pollution intensifies, its suppressive effect on entrepreneurship follows a pattern of “initial increase, subsequent decline, and then increase again.” Third, mechanism analyses reveal that haze pollution suppresses overall entrepreneurial activity by causing human capital outflow and increasing the costs associated with environmental regulation. Conversely, it fosters entrepreneurship in the environmental protection sector by strengthening governmental support for environmental industries and increasing market demand for green technologies. Fourth, heterogeneity analyses indicate that in eastern and central regions, and in areas characterized by higher industrial agglomeration, weaker green finance development or lower information transparency, both the suppressive effect of haze pollution on overall entrepreneurial activity and its promotive effect on environmental protection entrepreneurship are more pronounced. In addition, with continuous economic growth, this effect exhibits the characteristic of “being initially pronounced, then insignificant, and subsequently pronounced again”.

The potential contributions of this study are as follows.

First, this paper empirically explores the impact of haze pollution on regional entrepreneurial activities and further verifies the environmental Kuznets curve and the threshold effect of haze pollution. Most existing studies focus on the effects of haze pollution on people’s health and the economy. For example, haze pollution has been shown to cause a variety of physical and mental diseases13, cognitive biases14, 15, and lead to significant health expenditures16; It also reduces employees’ work performance1 and the enterprise’s economic benefits17.Within the literature examining haze pollution’s impact on entrepreneurial behavior, scholars have found that haze pollution creates a pessimistic atmosphere, reduces entrepreneurial participation and investment efficiency18, and increases both the financial and psychological costs of entrepreneurship19, thus suppressing regional entrepreneurial vitality4, 19, 20. However, these studies mostly only consider the linear effect of haze pollution on entrepreneurial activities. Different from previous studies focusing on linear effects, this paper further explores the nonlinear effects and threshold effects of haze pollution on entrepreneurial activities. The traditional Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) focuses on the inverted U-shaped relationship between environmental pollution and macroeconomic growth, while this paper innovatively applies EKC to “haze pollution and regional entrepreneurship”. It expands the application scenario of EKC, descending from the macroeconomic level to the micro-entrepreneurship field, and achieves segmentation at the entrepreneurial level. Meanwhile, the paper breaks through the traditional EKC’s simplistic understanding of the inverted U-shape. On the basis of verifying the applicability of EKC, it further identifies the double threshold effect, which more accurately captures the nonlinear relationship between haze pollution and entrepreneurship.

Second, this study explores the impact of haze pollution on entrepreneurship in the environmental protection industry and separately analyzes the specific reasons. While existing studies confirm that air pollution suppresses overall regional entrepreneurial activities4, 20, they have not explored how its effects vary across different types of entrepreneurship. Wei et al. (2024)4 used city-level data from China, showed that air pollution suppressed entrepreneurial activity and identified macro-level mechanisms including talent outflows, declining social capital, higher start-up costs, and tighter financial constraints. Based on county-level data, Zhao et al. (2023)19 documented the inhibitory effect of haze pollution on entrepreneurship, emphasizing heterogeneity in underdeveloped and non–resource-based regions but offered limited discussion of underlying mechanisms. At a more granular level, Guo et al.(2025)20 employed micro-level individual data to examine employer entrepreneurship and household entrepreneurial diversity, finding that haze pollution reduced entrepreneurship through individual channels such as lower risk tolerance, reduced social consumption, and diminished self-efficacy. Building on this literature, this study makes two key contributions. On the one hand, it moves beyond the dominant focus on aggregate entrepreneurship by distinguishing between general entrepreneurship and environmental-industry entrepreneurship, providing new insights into entrepreneurial incentives in green sectors; on the other hand, relative to the macro mechanisms in Wei et al.(2024)4 and the individual mechanisms in Guo et al.(2025)20 this study examines both macro-level mechanisms (human capital and government support) and micro-level mechanisms (environmental regulation costs and market demand for green technologies), thereby enriching the mechanism-based understanding of the relationship between haze and entrepreneurship. For environmental protection companies, haze pollution may actually stimulate their entrepreneurial activities in this field. Although the academic literature has not directly addressed this issue, related studies have shown that haze pollution leads to strict environmental regulations, which in turn promote the green innovation of enterprises21. In addition, the development of green finance provides financing support for environmental protection enterprises22, and the application of artificial intelligence provides new technological tools for such enterprises23, creating a favorable external environment for entrepreneurship in the environmental protection industry. This view is consistent with the Porter Hypothesis. However, the Porter Hypothesis mostly takes individual enterprises as the unit of analysis, while this paper breaks through its research perspective, expanding from the individual enterprise level to the regional entrepreneurship system, and reveals the transmission mechanism through which environmental factors promote entrepreneurial activities. This paper goes beyond the main macro perspective of existing studies and disaggregates entrepreneurial activity to explore the impact of haze pollution on the proportion of entrepreneurship in the environmental protection industry. In this way, the industry-level heterogeneity of the relationship between haze pollution and entrepreneurship is exposed, and the one-dimensional perception of the relationship between haze pollution and entrepreneurship is broken. In addition, this paper also makes distinctions and identifications between the effects of haze pollution on the overall level of entrepreneurial activities and entrepreneurship in the environmental protection industry, thereby supplementing the deficiencies in the existing causal path.

Third, this study uses county-level data to provide empirical evidence for the relationship between haze pollution and entrepreneurial activities. Existing empirical research on the interplay of environmental pollution and entrepreneurship has mainly focused on the national level24, the provincial level in China25, and the urban level4, 20, while county-level analysis remains scarce19. By introducing county-level data, this paper can better reflect the influence of haze pollution on the grassroots entrepreneurial subjects of individual industrial and commercial households and micro and small enterprises. In addition, county-level data can provide a detailed representation of spatial heterogeneity of pollution effects, avoiding the problem of macro-level data masking important regional differences. Accordingly, this study thus enriches the existing literature by expanding the spatial scale of analysis and provides micro-level evidence for the formulation of regionally differentiated entrepreneurship support and environmental governance policies.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the theoretical basis and puts forward the research hypotheses. Section 3 provides a description of the data, empirical model and variables. Section 4 reports the empirical results on the impact of haze pollution on entrepreneurial activities and conducts multiple robustness checks and endogeneity analyses. Section 5 analyzes the underlying reasons and heterogeneity of the effects. Section 6 draws a conclusion and proposes policy suggestions.

Literature review and research hypotheses

Direct impact

The impact of haze pollution on regional entrepreneurial activities is essentially a representation of the systematic transformation of the regional entrepreneurship system under the influence of environmental factors, which alters the deep variables such as human capital allocation, cost structure, market demand and social consensus in the regional entrepreneurship system. These changes are not only manifested in the relocation of the core resources of entrepreneurship, the structure of entrepreneurial costs and returns, but also in the guidance and choice of the direction of industrial entrepreneurship. Specifically, the impact of haze pollution on entrepreneurship is quite prominent in two aspects.

On the one hand, haze pollution reduces the total number of entrepreneurial activities in the region. First, according to the environmental preference theory, haze pollution reduces the livability and quality of the production environment in a region, which triggers health-driven migration of high-end talents26 and thus creates a shortage of core human capital required for entrepreneurial activities. Second, based on the local environmental assessment targets, the region which has more severe haze pollution, has more stringent environmental regulations. The rise in environmental compliance costs causes the marginal cost curve of entrepreneurial activities to shift up, reducing the space for the survival of start-ups27, thus limiting the overall entrepreneurial activities in the region.

On the other hand, haze pollution expands the scope of entrepreneurship within the environmental protection industry. It creates a higher demand for environmental governance, which provides policy incentives for environmental firms through subsidies and tax reductions10, and signals market opportunities through clean technology premiums11. According to the Porter Hypothesis, entrepreneurial opportunities induced by environmental crises attract resources to focus on environmental technology research and development and pollution control services, increasing the proportion of entrepreneurship in the environmental protection sector to achieve structural growth. Based on the above discussion, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1

Haze pollution causes a reduction in overall entrepreneurial activities, but increases the proportion of entrepreneurship in the environmental protection industry.

Impact mechanism

The impact mechanism of haze pollution on overall entrepreneurial activities

Haze pollution leads to the outflow of human resources and the increase of the cost of environmental compliance, thereby reducing the overall level of regional entrepreneurial activities.

Outflow of human capital. Haze pollution is dangerous to both the body and the mind, so talents tend to move to places with less pollution7. It may cause respiratory and cardiovascular diseases28 and psychological problems such as depression and cognitive impairment29. According to Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, health and safety are basic human needs. When haze pollution poses long-term threats to health, high-skilled individuals are more likely to consider environmental quality when deciding whether to relocate26. In addition, entrepreneurs in highly polluted areas have not only to bear the inherent risks of entrepreneurship, but also face additional risks such as health decline and increased medical expenses30, further reducing their willingness to engage in entrepreneurship in these areas. As a result, the outflow of regional human capital caused by haze pollution makes it impossible for entrepreneurship to obtain the necessary knowledge, technology and management resources, thus hindering the development of the entire regional entrepreneurial activities. Second, haze pollution can also cause indirect costs such as logistics delays, employee layoffs and accelerated equipment aging32, which will reduce productivity17 and further increase the hidden costs that enterprises have to bear. The impact mechanism of haze pollution on entrepreneurial activities in the environmental protection industry

Haze pollution can increase the policy incentives for the development of environmental protection enterprises and raise the demand in the market for clean technologies, which can boost the proportion of entrepreneurship in the environmental protection industry.

Enhanced support from the government for environmental protection. The demand for haze alleviation has a direct impact on the strength of the policy signal. First, haze pollution makes the government more concerned about environmental issues. By posing a direct threat to public health, haze pollution raises higher expectations of environmental governance among the people of society. Under the framework where environmental performance is included in the government assessment, local governments, under the impetus of social pressure and the pursuit of reputation, pay attention to and support the development of the environmental protection industry through legislation, standard setting and performance assessment33. Second, governments reduce the entry barriers for environmental projects and encourage enterprises to increase their investment in green technology research and development and application through subsidies, tax incentives and special funds10. Government support not only sends clear policy signals, but also relieves the constraints of the environmental protection industry for entrepreneurship directly. On the one hand, increased public green procurement34 expands the market for environmental technologies, equipment and services, and provides entrepreneurs with clearer profit paths. Government endorsement also enhances investors’ trust in the environmental sector and brings venture capital into green industries35, which offers more opportunities for the development of entrepreneurship. On the other hand, environmental subsidies reduce the initial start-up costs, Tax reduction eases the pressure of cash flow, and the special entrepreneurial fund provides equity financing channels for start-ups, reducing the financial barriers for entrepreneurs in the environmental protection industry10. Research design

Sample selection and data source

This study uses panel data for 2,822 Chinese counties (districts) from 2003 to 2022 to examine the impact of haze pollution on regional entrepreneurial activity. County-level data on the number of registered enterprises were obtained from the industrial and commercial registration database. PM2.5 concentration data were sourced from the high-resolution, high-quality PM2.5 dataset provided by the China Tibet Plateau Science Data Center, measured in µg/m³ 40, 41. Data on temperature inversion at the county level were obtained from NASA’s MERRA-2 satellite dataset. County-level control variables were drawn from the China County Statistical Yearbook and the China Regional Economic Statistical Yearbook. Data on listed companies’ air pollutant fees, environmental protection investments, and other indicators were collected from annual reports, corporate social responsibility reports, and public announcements. Data at the prefecture-level city and provincial levels were obtained from local government work reports, the China City Statistical Yearbook, and the China Statistical Yearbook. Population mobility data were sourced from the 2011–2017 China Migrants Dynamic Survey (CMDS).

Notably, this study takes county-level samples as the unified benchmark geographic unit. All baseline regressions include county fixed effects and use robust standard errors clustered at the county level. Due to limited data availability at the county level, prefecture‑level city or provincial data are employed in robustness tests, mechanism analysis, and heterogeneity analysis. In robustness test, city‑level AQI is added as a control variable, and average PM2.5 concentrations at the city and provincial levels are used. In mechanism analysis, AirTax, EnvirSupport, and EnvirInvest at the city level are measured. Since counties within the same prefecture‑level city face highly similar macro policy and institutional environments, using prefecture‑level city indicators to represent the average conditions of subordinate counties is reasonable and consistent with standard practice in regional economic studies. In heterogeneity analysis, this study only groups county observations by prefecture or provincial characteristics, without including these upper‑level variables in regression models. To avoid estimation bias from inconsistent geographic dimensions, all prefecture‑level and provincial indicators are matched to the county level according to administrative affiliation via official administrative division codes. Specifically, each county is linked to its corresponding prefecture‑level city and province, and the indicator values of higher‑level administrative units are directly assigned to all counties under their jurisdiction.

Variable description

Explained variable: entrepreneurial activity level (EntreActi) and the proportion of entrepreneurship in the environmental protection industry (EntreActi_Envir)

According to Bai et al. (2022)42, entrepreneurial activity reflects the intensity and vitality of regional market-oriented entrepreneurship. Notably, to accurately reflect the relative level of entrepreneurial activity across regions, it is necessary to control for the interference of county scale. To further mitigate the influence of county size, existing literature primarily employs three approaches to measure entrepreneurial activity: the ecological approach, the labor market approach, and the population approach. All three methods standardize the number of newly established enterprises during the sample period to measure entrepreneurial activity. The difference lies in the standardization base: the ecological approach uses the number of existing enterprises in the region, while the labor market approach and the population approach use the number of working-age population and total population in the region, respectively. Following this, this study will measure the relative entrepreneurial activity based on the population approach.

EntreActi is measured as the net increase in private enterprises divided by the total population of the county at the end of the year (per 100 persons). The rationale for using this measure is threefold. First, the enterprise ownership type in the enterprise information of industrial and commercial registration data includes six categories, namely individual industrial and commercial households, institutions, state-owned enterprises, foreign-funded enterprises, private enterprises, and collectively-owned enterprises. Considering that state-owned enterprises and institutions are subject to policy interventions and preferential resource allocation, foreign-funded enterprises are influenced by global strategies and international capital flows, and individual industrial and commercial households as well as collectively-owned enterprises have weak market orientation in their operation models. Therefore, entrepreneurial activity is measured based on private enterprises rather than all firms. Second, rather than focusing solely on the number of newly registered enterprises, this study uses the net growth of private enterprises. The net growth is calculated as the number of newly registered enterprises minus the number of deregistered ones. This measure more accurately reflects the dynamic vitality of the entrepreneurial market. It also helps avoid distortions caused by short-term speculative registrations. Third, to control for regional size, county population is used as the standardization base. Compared with using total enterprise numbers, this approach reduces measurement bias arising from differences in enterprise scale across regions42.

EntreActi_Envir is measured as the ratio of the net increase in private environmental protection enterprises to the net increase in all private enterprises. The core connotation of entrepreneurship in the environmental protection industry in this paper refers to entrepreneurial activities that take environmental protection business as their core business or primary operational activities, rather than all enterprises involved in environmental protection-related businesses. If mixed-operation enterprises—whose core business is non-environmental protection but which engage in a small amount of environmental protection-related businesses—are included in the sample of environmental protection industry entrepreneurship, it will lead to the attribute dilution of EntreActi_Envir, thereby interfering with the core causal identification of the impact of haze pollution on EntreActi_Envir. Therefore, this paper uses the industry codes in industrial and commercial enterprise registration data to distinguish between entrepreneurship in the environmental protection industry and that in non-environmental protection industries. According to the standards of the National Classification of Economic Activities (GB/T 4754 − 2017), this paper defines environmental protection enterprises as those belonging to the industrial category “Water Conservancy, Environment, and Public Facilities Management (N)” or “Electricity, Heat, Gas, and Water Production and Supply (D)”, or the industrial major category “Comprehensive Utilization of Waste Resources (C42)”. The net growth of private environmental protection enterprises is obtained by subtracting the number of private environmental companies that have deregistered from the number of new private environmental companies that have been registered. Compared with the use of the absolute net growth of private environmental protection enterprises, this ratio is free from the interference of the overall scale of entrepreneurship and can more accurately reflect the situation, activities and resource allocation of entrepreneurship in the environmental protection industry.

The temporal trends of EntreActi and EntreActi_Envir are shown in Fig. 1. EntreActi has a significantly increasing trend over time, while EntreActi_Envir remains relatively stable, with a decreasing trend at the beginning and then an increasing trend. This indicates that from 2003 to 2022, in general, the entrepreneurial activities in China developed significantly and stably, but the entrepreneurship in the environmental protection industry fluctuated but remained stable, without the growth trend that can be compared with the overall activity. During this period, Chinese economy focused on the rapid development of industries such as manufacturing and real estate, and resources were concentrated in those industries, which had shorter payback periods and faster returns, attracting more entrepreneurs. Entrepreneurship in the environmental protection industry was restricted by the difficulty of technology research and development, the insufficiency of initial market demand and the imperfection of the policy support system, and the vitality of the industry was limited. Only in the later stage, with the development of ecological civilization initiatives, did environmental entrepreneurship gradually recover. In addition, the different development trends of overall entrepreneurial activity and the proportion of environmental protection entrepreneurship also show the need to break down entrepreneurial activity by industry.

Explanatory variable: haze pollution (Lnpm)

Lnpm is measured as the natural logarithm of the county-level annual average PM2.5 concentration plus one. Haze essentially consists of a complex aerosol system formed by fine particulate matter combined with water vapor and gaseous pollutants, with PM2.5 (particulate matter with a diameter ≤ 2.5 μm) being the most critical pollutant. Due to its extremely small particle size, PM2.5 can remain suspended in the air for extended periods and is not easily settled. In comparison, PM10 settles more quickly, making it less suitable for capturing long-term haze pollution, while gaseous pollutants such as SO₂ and NOx are precursors that must first convert into PM2.5 to participate in haze formation. Therefore, this study uses PM2.5 concentrations to represent county-level haze pollution.

The temporal trend of Lnpm is shown in Fig. 4. The line chart shows the annual average of Lnpm, and the violin plots of each year present the interquartile range and median. Lnpm generally shows a downward trend between 2003 and 2022. Before 2013, the situation of haze pollution was rather serious, but the change was small, and after 2013 PM2.5 concentration shows a downward trend year by year. This change reflects the transformation of the former high-energy-intensive industries during the pre-2013 period that caused severe haze pollution to the later implementation of policies such as the “Ten Measures for Air Pollution Prevention and Control” after 2013, which promoted industrial upgrading, clean energy substitution and pollution governance, and reduced the severity of haze. In terms of regional differences, the degree of haze pollution among counties has become increasingly prominent. This difference is reflected in the characteristics of regional industrial structure, industrial intensive areas have higher pollution control difficulty, and environmentally friendly or rapidly transforming regions have achieved better pollution control results. Uneven environmental investment and enforcement among different regions also increase the disparities.

Southwest and southeast coastal regions have low pollution and are dominated by light industries; The wide land and climate in the northeastern part can disperse pollutants well, so the degree of haze is less.

Mechanism variables

To investigate the mechanisms through which haze pollution affects entrepreneurial activity, this study uses environmental regulation costs (AirTax) and population mobility (Pop_Ratio) as mechanism variables for EntreActi, and government support for environmental protection (EnvirSupport) and market demand for clean technologies (EnvirInvest) as mechanism variables for EntreActi_Envir. Detailed measurements are provided in Table 1.

The population mobility variable is calculated according to the following steps: (1) Data extraction: Only the variables “surveyed province”, “surveyed city”, “surveyed county”, “survey year”, “household ID”, “individual ID”, “registered residence province”, “birth year” and “in-migration year” are retained from the CMDS dataset. (2) Data cleaning: Samples with missing values for “in-migration year” or “birth year” are removed; samples with “in-migration year” earlier than 2003 are removed; and samples where the difference between “in-migration year” and “birth year” is less than 16 are removed (these individuals are below the statutory working age, and their migration is primarily family-related rather than influenced by haze). (3) PM2.5 matching: The three-year rolling average of PM2.5 is calculated for both the origin (registered residence province) and destination (surveyed province) of migration and matched with the CMDS data. (4) Cross-province migration calculation: At the “surveyed county–in-migration year” level, the number of migrants moving from higher to lower pollution areas is calculated (where the PM2.5 of the origin exceeds that of the destination), along with the total in-migrant population. (5) Variable construction: The migration data are matched with the regression dataset by destination county and year to calculate Lnpop and Pop_Ratio.

When measuring government support for the environmental protection industry (EnvirSupport), Concern is quantified as the natural logarithm of the frequency of environmental keywords in the work reports of prefecture-level municipal governments, following the methodology of Wang et al.(2022)43. The environmental keyword lexicon covers four dimensions: ambient air quality, water environment, ecological environment and sustainable development, and general environmental management. The specific keywords under each category are listed as follows: (1) Ambient air quality: haze, haze control, SO₂, CO₂, PM₁₀, PM₂.₅, air, dust, exhaust gas, waste gas, blue sky, VOCs. (2) Water environment: aquatic ecology, chemical oxygen demand, scattered, disorderly and polluting activities, emission, water environment, water security, water quality, clear water, black and odorous water, sewage, COD. (3) Ecological environment and sustainable development: environmental protection, green development, new energy, low carbon, environmental quality, revegetation, ecological environment, ecological protection, ecological damage. (4) General environmental management: pollution, energy consumption, pollution control, emission reduction, pollutant discharge, waste residue, coal-fired-related environmental violations, environmental crimes, environmental cases, environmental penalties, environmental governance.

Control variables

County-level entrepreneurial activity and the share of environmental protection entrepreneurship may be influenced by regional economic development, industrial structure, and other factors. To reduce model bias, these factors are included as control variables. Following Zhao et al. (2023)19 and Wei et al. (2024)4, this study includes economic development (GDP), industrial structure (Indu), fiscal support (Gover), financial environment (Loan), openness to trade (Open), and education level (Edu) as control variables. Detailed measurements are provided in Table 2.

GDP directly reflects the county-level economic aggregate and market potential. The larger the economic scale, the more consumer demand and entrepreneurial opportunities there are. Considering that county-level entrepreneurship is more dependent on overall market capacity, using per capita GDP to measure the correlation between county economic development and entrepreneurship is weaker; Indu reflects the level of industrial upgrading: the higher the proportion of the secondary and tertiary industries, the more refined the industrial division of labor and the higher the technology intensity, providing more scenarios for traditional entrepreneurship. Compared with structural indicators such as single-industry proportion, Indu better reflects the trend of industrial structure upgrading, and its weighted approach more comprehensively reveals the differentiated impacts of industrial structure on entrepreneurship; Gover directly reflects the government’s investment in county-level public services, infrastructure, and entrepreneurship support policies, while expenditures in specific fields such as environmental spending cannot fully cover the overall picture of government support for entrepreneurship; Loan reflects the supply capacity of financial resources at the county level, which can promote entrepreneurship by alleviating enterprises’ financing constraints. However, deposit balance only reflects capital accumulation, and the number of financial institutions cannot reflect actual capital allocation capacity, leading to limitations of these indicators in measuring the county-level financial environment; Open reflects the degree of the county’s integration into the global market. It not only spurs entrepreneurship through overseas market demand but also enhances regional innovation and entrepreneurship through channels such as technology spillovers and management experience; Edu measures the human capital foundation for entrepreneurship. The higher the education level, the stronger the labor skills and innovation awareness, thereby providing high-quality talent supply for entrepreneurial enterprises.

Empirical analysis

Benchmark regression

Columns (1)-(4) in Table 3 present the baseline regression results, examining the effects of haze pollution on EntreActi and EntreActi_Envir. After including control variables, the coefficient of haze pollution on EntreActi is −0.2072, while its coefficient on EntreActi_Envir is 0.0548, both statistically significant at the 1% level. Replace model

To examine whether the effect of haze pollution on entrepreneurial activity exhibits nonlinearity, this paper introduces a quadratic term of haze pollution. The results are presented in Columns (1)-(2) of Table 8. After including the squared term of Lnpm, the coefficients of the linear term are significantly positive, while those of the quadratic term are significantly negative for both EntreActi and EntreActi_Envir. This shows that with the increase of haze pollution, both EntreActi and EntreActi_Envir first increased and then decreased, forming an inverted-U shape consistent with the Environmental Kuznets Curve.

Notably, for EntreActi, the turning point occurs at Lnpm = 2.5034, which is close to the minimum value of Lnpm (2.3061, within the bottom 1%). This implies that the relationship between haze pollution and overall entrepreneurial activity is located predominantly on the downward segment of the curve. In contrast, for EntreActi_Envir, the turning point appears at Lnpm = 4.4359, which is close to the maximum value of Lnpm (4.9920, within the top 10%). This indicates that most of the relationship between haze pollution and environmental-sector entrepreneurship lies on the upward segment of the curve. These findings further confirm the robustness of the baseline model.

The core reason for the emergence of the inverted U-shaped trend lies in the reverse-push effect and destructive effect caused by changes in the intensity of haze pollution. When haze pollution is at a low level, the reverse-push effect dominates. On the one hand, mild haze pollution drives the government to introduce phased environmental subsidy policies, lowering the threshold for entrepreneurship. On the other hand, traditional industries also generate demand for environmental upgrading, spawning opportunities for environmental entrepreneurship. When haze pollution exceeds the turning point, the destructive effect will outweigh the reverse-push effect. Severe haze pollution not only significantly raises enterprises’ production and operation costs as well as labor health costs, but also reduces urban livability, triggering the outflow of core entrepreneurial factors such as high-end talents and capital. In addition, to curb pollution, the government will implement stringent environmental regulations, raise the threshold for market access in high-pollution industries, and restrain entrepreneurial vitality. At this stage, both overall entrepreneurship and environmental entrepreneurship will decline due to the destructive effect of haze pollution.

However, there exists a discrepancy in the turning points of EntreActi and EntreActi_Envir, which stems from the fundamental differences in their core driving factors. Overall entrepreneurship is primarily driven by factor costs and the stability of the market environment. With micro and small enterprises as the main participants, this type of entrepreneurship is highly sensitive to the cost shocks brought by mild pollution and has weak risk resistance capacity. Thus, its turning point is close to the minimum value of haze pollution, meaning that overall entrepreneurship mostly falls within the scope of haze pollution’s destructive effect. In contrast, environmental industry entrepreneurship is core-driven by the demand for pollution control. Mild pollution is insufficient to form large-scale demand, whereas escalating pollution will foster ample market space. Therefore, its turning point is close to the maximum value of haze pollution, indicating that environmental entrepreneurship mostly lies in the scope of pollution’s reverse-push effect.

To verify whether haze pollution exerts a threshold effect on entrepreneurial activity, this paper employs Lnpm as the threshold variable. According to the threshold model tests (Table 9), both the single-threshold and double-threshold effects are statistically significant, whereas the triple-threshold effect is not. This indicates that the intensity of the inhibitory impact of haze pollution on entrepreneurial activity varies significantly across different pollution levels. The regression results for the single- and double-threshold models of haze pollution on EntreActi are reported in Columns (3)-(4) of Table 8. Based on the double-threshold estimation, the effect of haze pollution on EntreActi can be divided into three stages. The inhibitory impact of haze pollution follows a pattern of “first increasing, then decreasing, and increasing again”.

The formation of such phased characteristics stems from the heterogeneous response mechanisms of the entrepreneurial ecosystem to varying levels of haze pollution. In the low-pollution stage, the entrepreneurial environment has not yet developed effective pollution adaptation mechanisms, and most entrepreneurial entities are micro and small enterprises with weak capital strength and insufficient experience in pollution control. These entities are highly sensitive to changes in environmental quality and face relatively high production and pollution treatment costs. Meanwhile, the ambiguity of initial environmental regulatory policies exacerbates the uncertainty of entrepreneurs’ policy expectations, leading to a strengthening of haze pollution’s inhibitory effect on entrepreneurial activities as pollution intensity rises.

In the moderate-pollution stage, entrepreneurial entities gradually develop adaptive governance capabilities through technological transformation and process optimization, which reduces the cost impact caused by per unit of pollution. At the same time, universal environmental protection subsidy policies introduced by local governments effectively offset enterprises’ pollution treatment costs. This stage also generates demand for basic environmental protection supporting services, creating new growth drivers for certain entrepreneurial activities and thus forming a buffer against the inhibitory effect.

In the high-pollution stage, core entrepreneurial factors such as high-end technical talents and innovative capital begin to flow out on a large scale. Severe pollution exceeds the carrying threshold of the entrepreneurial ecosystem, resulting in a sharp decline in urban livability and directly causing the irreversible outflow of high-value-added factors that are crucial for high-quality entrepreneurship. Governments gradually implement stringent environmental regulations, comprehensively tightening market access for high-pollution industries and raising the threshold for entrepreneurship. At this point, the adaptive governance measures developed by entrepreneurial entities in the moderate-pollution stage are no longer able to cope with the destructive impact of severe pollution, leading to a renewed intensification of haze pollution’s inhibitory effect on entrepreneurial activities.

Endogeneity test

There may be a reverse-causality endogeneity issue in the relationship between haze pollution and entrepreneurial activity. On the one hand, haze pollution suppresses entrepreneurial activity while increasing the share of entrepreneurship in environmental protection industries. On the other hand, entrepreneurial activity can in turn influence haze pollution: when new firms are concentrated in energy-intensive traditional manufacturing sectors, pollution may worsen, whereas concentration in environmental industries may help reduce pollution. To mitigate potential endogeneity concerns, this paper employs an instrumental variable approach as well as a regression discontinuity design.

Number of inversion days

This paper employs the natural logarithm of the number of inversion days plus one (Lnday) as an instrumental variable for haze pollution to address endogeneity concerns15. In general, temperature inversions inhibit air circulation in the atmosphere, causing pollutants to accumulate in the lower layers and thereby restricting their transport and dispersion. Inversion weather is a key meteorological condition for the formation and intensification of haze pollution, satisfying the relevance requirement of the instrumental variable. The partial correlation coefficients between Lnday and haze pollution are presented in Table 10. Specifically, the partial correlation coefficients (The raw correlation coefficient incorporates the confounding effects of extraneous variables, whereas the partial correlation coefficient controls for the influence of control variables and reveals the unconfounded linear relationship between Lnday and haze pollution) of Lnday with Lnpm and Lnpm10 are 0.2059 and 0.1703, respectively, and are statistically significant at the 1% level. Furthermore, Lnday is exogenous to entrepreneurial activities: On one hand, the formation of inversion stems from atmospheric thermodynamic laws, a process entirely dominated by meteorological conditions such as latitude, altitude, and diurnal temperature difference. Entrepreneurial activities cannot interfere with the frequency or duration of inversion by altering production behaviors, resource allocation, or policy choices, and changes in the number of inversion days will not adjust with the increase or decrease of entrepreneurial activities, thus possessing an inherent natural exogeneity. On the other hand, the heterogeneity of regional economic and meteorological conditions is an “a priori fixed” natural endowment, which may affect the level differences in inversion (i.e., the number of inversion days varies across regions) rather than the causal relationship between inversion days and entrepreneurial activities. Fluctuations in the number of inversion days remain independent of entrepreneurial activities and will not make adaptive adjustments due to changes in local entrepreneurship.

The Qinling-Huaihe Line is the natural dividing boundary between northern and southern China. In winter, northern cities usually turn on centralized heating, which requires a large amount of coal to be consumed and thus causes serious haze pollution. The mechanism analysis of Lnpm on EntreActi

Haze pollution can reduce county-level entrepreneurial activity through talent outflow and cost effects.

Population outflow. Health risks and decreased well-being caused by haze are major drivers of migration from high-pollution areas to low-pollution areas. Moreover, highly educated and skilled individuals tend to have stronger migration motivations, and their relocation results in the loss of key entrepreneurial resources in the origin regions. Therefore, this study calculates the number of people moving from high-pollution to low-pollution areas (Lnpop) and the proportion of such population (Pop_Ratio) to measure talent inflow at the county level. Further subsample regressions are conducted based on the median of Pop_Ratio. Columns (1)-(4) of Table 12 report the effects of AirTax, confirming H2. The results indicate that the coefficients of haze pollution on Lnpop and Pop_Ratio are − 0.6567 and − 0.0696, respectively, both significant at the 1% level. When a higher proportion of the population moves from high-pollution to low-pollution areas, the effect of haze pollution on EntreActi is more pronounced. This suggests that reduced haze in the destination area attracts migrants from more polluted regions, thereby increasing entrepreneurial activity. The influx of population not only provides labor support but also brings capital, technology, and information. These elements are key entrepreneurial resources. Their arrival directly lowers startup barriers and reduces operational risks. In this way, population inflow acts as a catalyst for entrepreneurship.

(2)Environmental regulation cost. Air emission fees are levied by governments on firms for atmospheric pollutants (e.g., SO₂, particulate matter) according to environmental regulations, and the fee amount is directly determined by the local environmental regulatory intensity. Accordingly, this study uses the average air emission fees of all listed companies at the prefecture level (AirTax) to measure regional environmental regulation costs, with subsample regressions conducted based on the median of AirTax. Columns (5)-(7) of Table 12 report these effects, confirming H3. The results show that the coefficient of haze pollution on AirTax is 0.1844, significant at the 5% level. Haze pollution has a significantly negative effect on EntreActi under both high- and low-regulation cost groups, but the effect is stronger and larger in the high-regulation group. This indicates that haze pollution increases firms’ environmental compliance costs, thereby suppressing entrepreneurial activity. Increased regulatory costs raise entry barriers and compress profit margins, discouraging potential entrepreneurs and reducing the survival capacity of existing firms, which amplifies the inhibitory effect of haze on entrepreneurial activity.

The mechanism analysis of Lnpm on EntreActi_Envir

Haze pollution can stimulate government support for the environmental industry and increase market demand for clean technologies, thereby providing a positive incentive for entrepreneurial activity in the environmental sector.

Policy support. Haze pollution, as a typical negative environmental externality, drives governments to strengthen support for the environmental industry through public health pressures and regulatory targets. Policy support is reflected in two dimensions: the attention given to environmental protection topics in prefecture-level government work reports and the scale of local fiscal expenditure on environmental protection, both of which are essential. Therefore, this study measures government support for the environmental industry (EnvirSupport) as the interaction between prefecture-level environmental concern and provincial-level environmental expenditure, and further conducts subsample regressions based on the median of EnvirSupport. Columns (1)-(3) of Table 13 present the effects of EnvirSupport, confirming H4. The results show that the coefficient of haze pollution on EnvirSupport is 0.0082, significant at the 1% level. In the high-support subsample, the effect of haze pollution on EntreActi_Envir is more pronounced. This indicates that haze pollution increases local government support for the environmental industry, providing policy incentives that benefit environmental entrepreneurship.

Heterogeneity analysis

Geographical location

The impact of haze pollution on entrepreneurial activity varies across counties due to differences in geographic location, climate, population density, and other factors that directly affect haze formation. Following the classification standard of the National Bureau of Statistics, all counties are divided into four regions: Eastern, Central, Western, and Northeastern China, and subgroup regressions are conducted. The results, presented in Table 14, indicate significant regional heterogeneity in the effect of haze pollution on entrepreneurship. In the Eastern and Central regions, haze pollution has a more obvious inhibitory effect on EntreActi and a more pronounced positive effect on EntreActi_Envir, while in the Western and Northeastern regions, the above effects are relatively weak. This is because in the eastern and central parts of China, natural conditions such as topography and climate restrict the dispersion of pollutants; At the same time, with the high population density and complex transportation system, the concentration of haze pollution becomes even more prominent. Furthermore, as leaders in economic development, these regions also face stricter environmental regulations, making entrepreneurial activity more sensitive to the effects of haze pollution.

Economic development stage

The impact of haze pollution on entrepreneurial activities exhibits significantly heterogeneous characteristics across regions with different levels of economic development. Based on the quartiles of per capita GDP (PGDP) of the prefecture-level cities where counties are located, this paper divides the full sample into four groups: the low-level group (PGDP≤Q1), the lower-middle-level group (Q1 < PGDP≤Q2), the upper-middle-level group (Q2 < PGDP≤Q3), and the high-level group (PGDP> Q3), and conducts subgroup regression analysis, with the results presented in Table 15. The findings indicate obvious heterogeneity in the impact of haze pollution on entrepreneurial activities across different economic development stages: the inhibitory effect of haze pollution on EntreActi is significant in the groups below Q1 and above Q2, but insignificant in the Q1-Q2 group. The promoting effect of haze pollution on EntreActi_Envir is significant in the groups below Q1 and above Q3, but insignificant in the Q1-Q3 group. This suggests that with the continuous growth of the economy, the inhibitory effect of haze pollution on overall entrepreneurial activity and its promoting effect on environmental industry entrepreneurship show a trend of increasing first, then decreasing, and then increasing again.

To verify the robustness of this heterogeneity, this paper further adopts the terciles of provincial-level per capita disposable income (PCDI) where the counties are located as the grouping criterion, with the results presented in Table 16. The findings show that the inhibitory effect of haze pollution on EntreActi is statistically significant across all three groups, yet its regression coefficients exhibit a “high, low, high” pattern. In contrast, the promoting effect of haze pollution on entrepreneurship in the environmental industry is consistent with the results in Table 15. This indicates that the heterogeneous characteristics of the impact of haze pollution on entrepreneurial activities across different stages of economic development are robust.

At the stage of low economic growth, entrepreneurial activities are highly dependent on high-pollution and low-value-added traditional industries. Haze pollution directly raises the production and operation costs and labor health costs of enterprises, thereby inhibiting overall entrepreneurship. Meanwhile, local governments introduce universal environmental subsidy policies, which lower the threshold for low-end environmental entrepreneurship and thus drive the growth of environmental industry startups. With the sustained economic development, local governments will relax environmental regulation standards in phases to prioritize economic growth targets, which offsets the negative effects of haze pollution and renders the inhibitory effect on overall entrepreneurship insignificant. At this stage, the environmental industry also faces a supply-demand mismatch characterized by the shrinkage of low-end demand and insufficient high-end supply, leading to the disappearance of the promoting effect. When the economy enters a high-level development stage, entrepreneurial activities gradually shift to technology-intensive sectors, which are highly reliant on the agglomeration of high-end talents. Haze pollution reduces urban livability and hinders the inflow of talents, resulting in a significantly strengthened inhibitory effect on overall entrepreneurship. Meanwhile, mature capital, technology and talent reserves provide comprehensive support for the environmental industry, and environmental entrepreneurship shifts from a policy-subsidy-driven model to a high-end market demand-driven one, thus the promoting effect on environmental industry startups reemerges.

Green finance

Green finance acts as a “lifeline” for entrepreneurial activity, not only by reducing the financing costs of corporate environmental investments and alleviating regulatory pressure but also by decreasing dependence on external clean technologies, thereby effectively mitigating the negative externalities of haze pollution on entrepreneurship. This study measures regional green finance development (GC) using the “Green Credit Interest Subsidy Pilot Policy” issued by local governments from 2017 onwards. In the year a city implements the green credit subsidy policy and in subsequent years, GC = 1; otherwise, GC = 0. Subgroup regression results based on GC are presented in columns (5)-(8) of Table 17. The results show that in regions without green credit, the inhibitory effect of haze pollution on EntreActi and its promoting effect on EntreActi_Envir are more pronounced. This is because, on the one hand, regions that have not implemented the green credit subsidy pilot policy face greater financing constraints, making it difficult for enterprises to bear the costs of pollution control, leading to reduced investment or even market exit. On the other hand, due to limited green funding, enterprises in these regions struggle to independently develop clean technologies to meet environmental regulations, increasing reliance on external clean technologies. This “passive demand” further stimulates environmental entrepreneurship.

Information transparency

Information transparency is an important external support for entrepreneurial activity, as it reduces information asymmetry and stabilizes expectations, providing entrepreneurs with a foundation for risk assessment and fair competition. This study measures regional information transparency (DO) using the public data open platforms that local governments gradually launched from 2012 onwards. In the year a city launches a public data platform and in subsequent years, DO = 1; otherwise, DO = 0. Subgroup regression results based on DO are reported in columns (9)-(12) of Table 17. The results indicate that in regions without public data open platforms, the inhibitory effect of haze pollution on EntreActi and the promoting effect on EntreActi_Envir are more pronounced. This is because, on one hand, the absence of a public data platform implies lower information transparency, leading to insufficient disclosure of pollution emissions, emission reduction standards, and penalties, thereby increasing uncertainty in entrepreneurs’ expectations about the business environment. On the other hand, the suddenness and severity of haze pollution in these regions generate “compensatory demand,” meaning that exposure to pollution triggers a sharp surge in demand for clean technologies, thereby providing a more urgent market space for environmental entrepreneurship.

Conclusions and policy recommendations

Based on enterprise registration data from 2,822 counties (districts) in China during 2003–2022, this study investigates the impact of haze pollution on regional overall entrepreneurial activity and the share of environmental entrepreneurship, and further explores the mechanisms and heterogeneity of these effects. The results indicate the following: First, haze pollution reduces overall entrepreneurial activity while increasing the share of environmental entrepreneurship. Second, the effects of haze pollution on both overall entrepreneurial activity and the share of environmental entrepreneurship exhibit an inverted U-shaped pattern, confirming the environmental Kuznets curve. Point analysis shows that the inhibitory effect on overall entrepreneurial activity mainly occurs in the descending phase, whereas the promoting effect on environmental entrepreneurship mainly occurs in the ascending phase. Moreover, haze pollution exhibits a double-threshold effect on overall entrepreneurial activity: as pollution intensifies, its inhibitory effect on entrepreneurship first increases, then decreases, and finally rises again. Third, mechanism analysis indicates that haze pollution suppresses overall entrepreneurial activity by causing human capital outflow and increasing environmental regulation costs, while simultaneously promoting environmental entrepreneurship by strengthening government support for the environmental industry and increasing market demand for green technologies. Fourth, heterogeneity analysis shows that in the eastern and central regions, as well as in areas with higher industrial agglomeration, weaker green finance development and lower information transparency, the inhibitory effect of haze pollution on overall entrepreneurial activity and its promotive effect on environmental entrepreneurship are more pronounced. In addition, with continuous economic growth, this effect exhibits the characteristic of “being initially pronounced, then insignificant, and subsequently pronounced again”.

Based on the above findings, this study proposes the following policy recommendations:

First, improve the coordination mechanism of environmental governance and entrepreneurial development. In the process of air pollution control and environmental regulation, it is necessary to balance pollution reduction and the vitality of entrepreneurship by dynamically adjusting the intensity of environmental regulations and the focus of entrepreneurship support policies based on the heterogeneous characteristics of different stages of economic development. The government can implement support measures such as tax reduction, R&D subsidy and green credit to reduce the compliance costs of enterprises and avoid excessive crowding-out effect on non-environmental entrepreneurship. Specifically, local governments should take the lead in coordinating resources. Environmental enterprises should give play to their technical advantages. Traditional high-pollution firms should accelerate green transformation. Meanwhile, underdeveloped regions can launch targeted subsidy plans to reduce enterprise costs. These subsidy plans include R&D subsidies for environmental enterprises and transformation subsidies for high-pollution firms. For developed regions, they should focus on talent retention strategies to stabilize the environmental entrepreneurship talent team. At the same time, explore the establishment of a coordination mechanism for pollution control and entrepreneurship support. In addition to improving environmental law enforcement, policies that provide financing facilitation and innovation service platforms for startups should also be introduced to help enterprises seize the opportunities in the green transformation rather than just bear more responsibilities.

Second, implement region-specific green development strategies. Due to the significant differences among different regions in economic development, industrial agglomeration, the construction of green finance and transparency of information, uniform environmental and industrial policies may lead to different effects. Policy tools should be adapted to local conditions accordingly. In regions in the transitional stage of economic growth, it is necessary to dynamically adjust the tightness of environmental regulations, balance the relationship between pollution control and the cultivation of entrepreneurial vitality, and through the combination of phased environmental subsidies and the construction of entrepreneurial incubation platforms, guide traditional high-pollution firms to transform green with policy incentives, encourage environmental enterprises to provide technical support, and help local governments optimize regional entrepreneurial layout. In regions with high industrial agglomeration, efforts should be made to promote green transformation of industrial enterprises and clean technology R&D, so as to weaken haze pollution’s negative impact on entrepreneurship and boost environmental entrepreneurship. In regions where green finance is less developed, efforts should be made to expand the supply of green credit and green bonds, guide financial resources to flow to enterprises and startups that align with green transition goals, and support the growth of new energy, environmental protection, and other industries. In regions where the level of information openness is low, environmental disclosure systems and the transparency of the government should be improved to reduce the uncertainty faced by the market due to information asymmetry and increase entrepreneurs’ confidence in the institutional environment. For developed regions, while maintaining strict environmental regulations, it is necessary to strengthen talent retention, improve talent evaluation and incentive mechanisms, and attract high-end environmental technology and entrepreneurial talents. Differentiated policies that match the regional characteristics can more effectively alleviate the negative impact of haze pollution on entrepreneurial activities and promote the coordinated development of the environmental industry.

Third, develop a market-oriented environmental entrepreneurship ecosystem. In the process of environmental governance, the role of market demand should be fully utilized, and gradually transform policy-driven projects into market-driven incentives, so as to ensure the long-term development of environmental entrepreneurship. The government should improve the support system for the green industry by: (1) promoting the implementation of green procurement policies to increase the proportion of environmentally compliant products and services in the public sector; (2) Developing a unified green certification and standards system to provide clear entry rules for enterprises in the green sector; (3) Expanding the application scenarios of environmental technologies and green products through pilot projects and industrial clusters, creating more market space for green startups. In this process, local governments should optimize market supervision and tailor support policies. These policies need to be differentiated according to regional development levels: for underdeveloped regions, they should focus on targeted subsidies, while for developed regions, talent retention should be the key. Environmental enterprises should focus on technological innovation, as this can effectively enhance their market competitiveness. Traditional high-pollution firms should actively participate in green transformation, and they can rely on policy and subsidy support to promote this process. These measures aim to gradually build an environmental entrepreneurship ecosystem with market demand as the core and policy support as the foundation, so that environmental enterprises can maintain their innovation vitality and sustainable development in the process of environmental improvement.
