AgRP reflects glucocorticoid action: integrated experimental and clinical evidence
Authors: Natsuki Aoyama, Mitsuru Nishiyama, Yasumasa Iwasaki, Shuichi Nakayama, Mizuho Okazaki, Takafumi Taguchi, Masayuki Tsuda, Koshi Hashimoto, Shinya Makino, Shimpei Fujimoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeGlucocorticoids (GCs) are key regulators of energy homeostasis. Clinically, patients with Cushing’s syndrome exhibit obesity, whereas adrenal insufficiency is associated with weight loss. However, circulating biomarkers reflecting GC action have not been established. Agouti-related protein (AgRP), an orexigenic neuropeptide, is upregulated by GC in the rodent hypothalamus. Here, we investigated whether AgRP is a surrogate marker of GC action through in vitro and in vivo experiments as well as a clinical study.MethodsThe GC-dependent transcriptional regulation of AgRP was examined using reporter assays in neuronal BE(2)C cells. In animal experiments, the effects of GC on hypothalamic AgRP mRNA expression in C57BL/6J mice were examined. Circulating AgRP levels were also analyzed in nine patients with adrenal Cushing’s syndrome before and after surgery.ResultsTwo functional glucocorticoid-responsive elements (GREs) were identified in the human AgRP gene promoter, through which GC enhanced AgRP transcriptional activity. In mice, corticosterone (CORT) administration induced hyperphagia and increased hypothalamic AgRP mRNA levels, which positively correlated with plasma CORT levels. In patients with adrenal Cushing’s syndrome, circulating AgRP levels significantly decreased after surgery (118.7 ± 40.3 vs. 37.7 ± 9.5 pg/mL, p < 0.01) and positively correlated with serum cortisol levels (r = 0.79, p < 0.01).ConclusionThese findings demonstrate that GC positively regulates AgRP across molecular, animal, and clinical settings, supporting the hypothesis that circulating AgRP may serve as a surrogate indicator of GC action. Introduction

Glucocorticoids (GCs) are key regulators of energy homeostasis. From a clinical perspective, excess GC causes Cushing’s syndrome, whereas a lack of GC leads to adrenal insufficiency. Cushing’s syndrome has various systemic comorbidities, including central obesity and diabetes mellitus [1, 2], and hyperphagia caused by excess GCs is likely one of the factors contributing to these complications [3, 4]. Because GC levels overlap between healthy individuals and those with Cushing’s syndrome or mild autonomous cortisol secretion (MACS) [1, 2, 5], alternative methods for predicting GC action are desirable. On the other hand, adrenal insufficiency can cause life-threatening conditions, such as appetite loss and emaciation [6, 7]. Measuring morning serum cortisol levels allows for the assessment of adrenal function; however, since stimulation tests are required for a definitive diagnosis of adrenal insufficiency [6, 7], indicators of GC deficiency are also clinically useful.

With the recent rise in the number of obese people, it is imperative to develop effective management strategies for these patients, and remarkable progress has been made in understanding the mechanisms of appetite regulation [8–10]. Hypothalamic proopiomelanocortin-derived α-melanocyte stimulating hormone suppresses appetite through melanocortin 4 receptor (MC4R), whereas hypothalamic Agouti-related protein (AgRP) enhances appetite by acting as an antagonist for MC4R [9, 10]. Although GC is a key regulator of energy homeostasis, the extent to which GC-related abnormalities contribute to the recent increase in obesity remains unclear [11, 12]. In terms of GC-induced hyperphagia, several reports have shown that GC enhances the expression of orexigenic neuropeptides including AgRP and neuropeptide Y (NPY) in the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus (ARC) [13–18]. Furthermore, we and others have shown that GC-induced AgRP expression is more evident than NPY [16–18]. Although GC affects various organs throughout the body, GC-induced hypothalamic AgRP mRNA expression is thought to be a direct action on the central nervous system [18].

No circulating biomarkers have been established as alternative indicators for the effect of GC. AgRP is mechanistically linked to GC action in rodents [15–18], but has not been validated in humans. Thus, to explore whether AgRP is an alternative indicator of GC activity, we investigated the GC-dependent regulation of AgRP using in vitro assays, in vivo mouse models, and clinical samples obtained from patients with adrenal Cushing’s syndrome.

Materials and methods

In vitro experiments

Dexamethasone was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The wild-type glucocorticoid receptor (GR) expression vector was a gift from Prof. Ronald Evans (Salk Institute, La Jolla, CA, USA). The 5’-promoter region of the human AgRP gene (≈ 1.8 kb), with various deletion mutants, was cloned by PCR and incorporated into the luciferase reporter plasmid using standard molecular biological techniques. The human neuroblastoma-derived BE(2)C cells were grown in DMEM/Ham F12 (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. The cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humid atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

In each experiment, the cells were cultured in 24-well plates at 50% confluency. Twenty-four hours after plating, the cells were transfected with the test plasmids (reporter plasmid and GR expression vector) using FuGene 6 transfection reagent (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). When different amounts of expression vector were used in an experiment, the net amount of transfected DNA per well was kept equal using an empty vector to maintain constant transfection conditions. The next day, the medium was replaced with a serum-free medium. In each experiment, 100 nM dexamethasone was added directly to the culture medium, and the cells were incubated for 12–24 h. At the end of the incubation, the culture medium was removed and the cells were harvested to determine promoter activity.

Luciferase assay was performed as previously described [19], and light output was measured for 20 s at room temperature using a luminometer (Berthold Lumat LB9507; Berthold Technologies, Bad Wildbad, Germany).

Electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) were performed as described previously [20]. Recombinant human GR proteins were synthesized from constructs in the pSG5 expression vector using the TNT T7 Quick Coupled Transcription/Translation System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Binding reactions contained 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.6), 50 mM KCl, 12% glycerol, 1 mM dithiothreitol, 1 µg poly(dI-dC)(dI-dC), and 4 µl each of the synthesized nuclear receptors. Double-stranded oligonucleotides (GRE1, 5’-GGCATAGACAGTCCT-3’; GRE2, 5’-GGAACAGTTCGTTCT-3’) were labeled with [α-32P] deoxy-CTP by a fill-in reaction using a Klenow fragment of DNA polymerase. The binding reactions were performed at room temperature for 30 min. For competition experiments, a 200-fold molar excess of cold oligonucleotides (wild type) was included. The protein-DNA complexes were resolved on a 5% polyacrylamide gel in 0.5 × Tris-borate EDTA (45 mM Tris-base, 1 mM EDTA). Dexamethasone was dissolved in 20 mM NaOH as a 1-mM stock solution and diluted to the indicated concentrations in 20 mM Tris buffer (pH 7.5). All EMSAs were repeated at least thrice with similar results, and a representative result is shown.

In vivo experiment with Cushing’s model mouse

Male, 8-week-old, C57BL/6J mice (Japan SLC, Hamamatsu, Japan) were used in this study. All mice were maintained under controlled light conditions (lights on, 0500–1900 h) at 24 °C in a humidity-controlled room and allowed free access to standard chow and drinking water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Committee of the Kochi University.

C57BL/6J mice (n = 16) were randomly divided into two groups and implanted subcutaneously with either a placebo (100% cholesterol, 100 mg) or a 10% CORT pellet (10% corticosterone / 90% cholesterol, 100 mg) under sodium pentobarbital anesthesia, as described previously [21, 22]. The mice and their food consumption were measured every day for 2 weeks, and they were decapitated on day 14 (between 0900 and 1200 h). The average daily food consumption over the three days prior to the decapitation was calculated as the food intake. Trunk blood was collected for the measurement of plasma CORT levels using RIA (MP Biomedicals, Solon, OH, USA), and epididymal fat was obtained for weighing. For the determination of AgRP mRNA, their hypothalami were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and kept at − 80 °C until RNA extraction.

Total RNA was extracted from the hypothalamus using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). RNA samples were treated with DNAse (Invitrogen), and the first strand was synthesized using reverse transcriptase (Omniscript RT Kit; Qiagen, Germany). Each sample was subjected to quantitative real-time PCR analysis of mouse AgRP mRNA using the TaqMan system (ABI PRISM 7000; Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). The primer set for mouse GAPDH was used as an internal control.

Clinical study with adrenal Cushing’s syndrome

Participants in the clinical study were nine patients (all female) with adrenal Cushing’s syndrome (overt Cushing’s syndrome, n = 5; MACS, n = 4) recruited from the Department of Endocrinology at Kochi Medical School Hospital. Adrenal Cushing’s syndrome or MACS was diagnosed by clinical symptoms, increased 24-hour urinary free cortisol, suppressed plasma adrenocorticotrophic hormone (ACTH), a positive 1 mg-dexamethasone test, and a unilateral adrenal tumor [1, 2, 5]. All patients underwent unilateral adrenalectomy for Cushing’s syndrome. The postoperative clinical courses and pathological findings were consistent with those of cortisol-producing adrenocortical adenoma, confirming the diagnosis. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kochi University, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Circulating plasma and serum samples were collected from all nine participants preoperatively and at 3 months postoperatively. Blood samples were collected between 08:00 and 10:00 AM under fasting conditions. All patients required postoperative glucocorticoid replacement, and samples were collected before the administration of hydrocortisone. Serum cortisol and plasma ACTH levels were measured by electrochemiluminescence immunoassays (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). Plasma AgRP levels were determined by ELISA (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA).

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as the mean ± SEM in animal study and the mean ± SD in clinical study. Statistical differences between the control and experimental groups were determined using ANOVA, followed by Fisher’s PLSD test, and correlation analyses were performed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. In the clinical study, cross-sectional associations were examined using multiple regression analysis after adjusting for age, weight, and body mass index (BMI). All calculations were performed using JMP version 18.0 software (SAS institute, Cary, NC. USA). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Identification of GRE in the human AgRP promoter

There are two putative glucocorticoid-responsive elements (GREs) in the 5’-promoter of the human AgRP: GRE1 (from − 491 to -477 bp; +1 indicates the transcription start site) and GRE2 (from − 387 to -373 bp) (Fig. 1A, B). We analyzed the role of GC in the transcriptional activity of AgRP. Using BE(2)C cells, we evaluated the function of the GR using the GR agonist, dexamethasone. We found that dexamethasone (100 nM, 12 h) showed no transcriptional effect, whereas a marked stimulatory effect on AgRP transcription was observed following transfection with GR because BE(2)C cells do not express endogenous GR [23]. The effect of dexamethasone was dependent on the dose of GR expressed, and time-course experiments of up to 24 h showed greater effects on the transcriptional activity (Fig. 1C).

To identify the elements responsible for GC-induced gene transcription, we constructed a series of AgRP promoter deletion mutants. We found that deletion of the promoter to -476 bp markedly diminished GC responsiveness (Fig. 1D), suggesting that GREs are required for GC-mediated enhancement of AgRP transcription and that GRE1 is more essential than GRE2. EMSAs showed that GR homodimer bound to both GRE1 and GRE2 (Fig. 1E), supporting that these are functional GREs in the human AgRP promoter.

Increased hypothalamic AgRP mRNA in Cushing’s model mouse

Administration of CORT for two weeks significantly increased body and fat weights (Fig. 2A, B), and cumulative weight gain (placebo 2.5 ± 0.2, CORT 5.0 ± 0.6 g, p < 0.01), similar to our previous findings in a mouse model of Cushing’s syndrome [21]. Food consumption also increased in the CORT-treated group compared to the placebo group (placebo 4.4 ± 0.1, CORT 5.4 ± 0.1 g/day, p < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). Plasma CORT levels were markedly higher in the CORT-treated group than in the placebo group (placebo 11.1 ± 5.8, CORT 112.9 ± 10.3 ng/mL, p < 0.01) (Fig. 2D). CORT administration significantly increased AgRP mRNA levels in the hypothalamus compared with those in the placebo group (placebo 99.7 ± 7.7, CORT 140.3 ± 9.0 relative levels, p < 0.05) (Fig. 2E).

One of the subjects was overweight (BMI 26.7), while the rest were of normal weight. In both the Cushing’s syndrome and the MACS groups, elevated AgRP levels were observed in conjunction with sustained increases in cortisol levels; given that age, weight, and BMI were comparable between the two groups, these two groups were analyzed together. Three months post-surgery, patients were receiving physiological doses of hydrocortisone (morning: 11.9 ± 3.5 mg, evening: 4.7 ± 0.8 mg, total 16.7 ± 3.5 mg/day) to manage adrenal insufficiency. There was a significant positive correlation between serum cortisol and plasma AgRP levels (r = 0.79, p < 0.01) (Fig. 4C), which persisted after adjusting for age, weight, and BMI (p < 0.01) in the multiple linear regression model. Plasma ACTH levels did not correlate with AgRP levels.

Discussion

This study provides multiple lines of evidence demonstrating the regulation of AgRP by GC. Molecular analysis showed that GC enhanced AgRP gene transcription, which requires GREs. In an animal experiment, administration of GC to mice induced hyperphagia and increased hypothalamic AgRP mRNA levels, which were positively correlated with plasma CORT levels. Plasma AgRP levels significantly decreased postoperatively in patients with adrenal Cushing’s syndrome, and a positive correlation was observed between serum cortisol and plasma AgRP levels. Thus, this study uniquely integrated molecular, animal, and human data, demonstrating the consistent regulation of AgRP by GC.

Our in vitro studies showed that GC induces AgRP transcriptional activity. Although the molecular mechanism underlying GC-induced activation of AgRP neurons has not yet been elucidated, we found two consensus GRE sequences in the human AgRP promoter and GC-enhanced AgRP transcription through these GREs. Lee et al. reported that the GR and brain-specific homeobox factors synergized to directly activate AgRP transcription, and they also reported GC-enhanced AgRP transcription through a GRE-like sequence in the promoter [24]. Although the site of action was not clarified, the identified GRE corresponded to GRE2 (-387 to -373) based on its sequence. Notably, our detailed promoter deletion analysis demonstrated that both GRE1 and GRE2 were functional, and that GRE1 was more essential than GRE2 (Fig. 1D). In molecular studies using rodents, AgRP neuron-specific GR-deleted mice exhibited an obesity-resistant phenotype in response to a high-fat diet [25], and selective GR modulators that suppress GR-induced AgRP transcription represent beneficial metabolic activity in obese mice [26]. These observations support the notion that the GRE is an essential component of the GC-AgRP pathway. We hope that the overall mechanism by which GC activate AgRP neurons will be clarified in future studies.

Our current study showed that CORT administration in mice induced obesity and hyperphagia and increased AgRP mRNA in the hypothalamus. Positive correlations were observed between serum GC concentrations and hypothalamic AgRP mRNA, and between hypothalamic AgRP mRNA and food intake. These results are consistent with those of previous studies linking elevated GC with obesity, hyperphagia, and increased AgRP and NPY mRNA expressions in the ARC [13–18]. Our study and previous reports support that an enhanced GC-AgRP pathway is associated with hyperphagia in Cushing’s syndrome, suggesting that hypothalamic AgRP mRNA may serve as an alternative indicator of orexigenic GC activity.

Few previous clinical studies have analyzed circulating AgRP levels in patients with Cushing’s syndrome. A pilot study suggested elevated AgRP levels in patients with adrenal Cushing’s syndrome [27]. Page-Wilson et al. reported that AgRP levels are useful in the differential diagnosis of ACTH-dependent Cushing’s syndrome [28], that plasma AgRP levels are higher in patients with pituitary Cushing’s disease than in controls, and that they decrease after the resection of ACTH-producing pituitary tumors [29]. This study focused on plasma AgRP levels before and after surgery in patients with adrenal Cushing’s syndrome. Postoperatively, plasma AgRP levels decreased significantly, and a positive correlation was observed between serum cortisol and plasma AgRP levels, consistent with the findings of a previous study on pituitary Cushing’s disease [29]. These results suggest that circulating AgRP levels increase during excess GC; therefore, AgRP is a possible surrogate indicator of GC action [29]. Notably, human AgRP levels exhibit circadian fluctuations [30] and care must be taken regarding the timing of blood collection when using AgRP as an indicator.

Several previous studies have investigated the origin of circulating AgRP. Distribution analysis showed that AgRP RNA was expressed in the hypothalamus and adrenal glands [31], and the basis for considering the hypothalamus as the source of circulating AgRP is as follows: AgRP is released from the hypothalamus, and AgRP levels remained unchanged after bilateral adrenalectomy in rats [32]. In addition, AgRP-like immunoreactivity is evident in the median eminence, which has access to the circulation [33]. On the other hand, a recent study focusing on adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC) demonstrated that AgRP mRNA levels are elevated in ACC tumors and correlate with plasma AgRP concentrations [34]. Although there are no published data on plasma AgRP levels in humans following adrenalectomy, unpublished observational findings state that AgRP persisted in human plasma even after bilateral adrenalectomy in three cases [29]. Therefore, while there is currently clear evidence supporting the hypothalamus as the source of circulating AgRP, the possibility of an adrenal origin cannot be ruled out.

One of the remarkable findings is that reduced AgRP levels are associated with adrenal insufficiency because plasma AgRP levels markedly decreased after surgery for adrenal cortisol-producing tumors, with minimal overlap compared to preoperative states (Fig. 4B). Hypothalamic AgRP mRNA expression is decreased in adrenalectomized rodents [35–37], and plasma AgRP levels rapidly decrease within a week after surgery for pituitary Cushing’s disease [29], supporting the idea that AgRP reduction is associated with adrenal insufficiency and that low AgRP levels may be useful in identifying GC deficiency. Overall, in the current study, hypothalamic or circulating AgRP levels closely mirrored glucocorticoid levels, supporting its potential as a surrogate indicator of GC action.

Although this study primarily demonstrated a close association between GC and AgRP, it did not directly prove that AgRP functions as a mediator of orexigenic GC activity. However, AgRP is an orexigenic neuropeptide, and it is reasonable to assume that GC-induced AgRP in the hypothalamus causes hyperphagia [9, 10, 13–18]. The GC-AgRP pathway is a potential target for the treatment of obesity [25, 26], further supporting the importance of AgRP in mediating GC-induced hyperphagia. In contrast to the link between hyperphagia and the enhanced GC-AgRP pathway, appetite loss observed in patients with adrenal insufficiency may be associated with GC-AgRP inactivation. Recent studies have shown that hypothalamic AgRP plays a pivotal role in maintaining appetite during fasting [38, 39] and that acyl coenzyme A binding protein is a new molecule that mediates GC action [40]. Further studies are needed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms involved, including AgRP, underlying appetite regulation in Cushing’s syndrome and adrenal insufficiency.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small (n = 9). Although the paired analyses of pre- and post-operative samples allowed for the detection of consistent changes associated with GC status, future studies should be conducted with a larger sample size, where overt Cushing’s syndrome and MACS can be separately analyzed. Second, postoperative glucocorticoid replacement may be a significant confounding factor, although hydrocortisone is used at physiological doses. Second, appetite was not assessed in the clinical study. Third, appetite was not assessed in the clinical study. Although changes in the AgRP concentrations corresponding to the GC levels were observed, it was not possible to analyze their effects on appetite. The impact of the GC-AgRP system on the human appetite remains a topic for future research. Fourth, sex differences were not analyzed in the in vivo experiments and clinical studies, and future research involving both sexes is desirable.

In conclusion, our findings provide multiple lines of evidence demonstrating that GC positively regulates AgRP in molecular, animal, and clinical settings, supporting the idea that circulating AgRP may serve as a surrogate indicator of GC action. This integrative approach, from molecular to human data, strengthens the physiological relevance of the GC-AgRP axis. Further studies are warranted to establish clinical applicability.
