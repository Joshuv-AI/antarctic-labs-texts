Effects of hydrated lime and quarry dust dosage and aging on the relaxation modulus of bituminous mastics
Authors: K. L. A. V. Harnadh, A. Padmarekha, S. Karuppasamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bituminous mastics are essential in influencing the stiffness and viscoelastic behaviour of asphalt mixtures, as the binder–filler matrix governs stress transfer and deformation resistance in pavements. Understanding the stress relaxation behaviour of mastics is crucial because it indicates the ability of the material to dissipate stresses induced by traffic loading. This experimental study evaluated the stress relaxation behaviour of bituminous mastics prepared with hydrated lime and quarry dust as fillers at three dosage levels (20%, 30%, and 40%). Stress relaxation experiments were performed at temperatures ranging from 40, 50, and 60 °C under a constant strain level of 10%, considering both unaged and aged conditions (10, 50, 100, and 200 h). The results showed that hydrated lime mastics shown a higher relaxation modulus compared to quarry dust mastics. Under unaged conditions at 40 °C, the peak relaxation modulus rose from 4.46 × 10⁶ Pa for HLM20 to 5.51 × 10⁶ Pa for HLM30, indicating a rise of about 23%, and then decreased to 4.95 × 10⁶ Pa for HLM40. Conversely, quarry dust mastics showed a gradual increase in modulus with filler dosage, rising from 4.71 × 10⁶ Pa for QDM20 to 5.30 × 10⁶ Pa for QDM40 at 40 °C. Aging further increased the modulus of both mastics, with HLM30 reaching 7.86 × 10⁶ Pa after 100 H of aging, indicating a significant stiffening effect. Master curve analysis also showed that the modulus of hydrated lime mastics increased by more than 100% from HLM20 to HLM30 under aged conditions, confirming the improved stiffness and stress resistance at this dosage. Overall, the results indicate that 30% hydrated lime provides the optimum filler dosage, offering a better balance between stiffness and relaxation behaviour compared with quarry dust mastics.

Introduction

Bituminous mastic is a mix substance created by blending bitumen with mineral filler particles that generally pass through a 75 µm sieve. The effectiveness of a bituminous mixture is predominantly determined by the basic characteristics of the bituminous mastic, as this binder-filler matrix regulates stress transfer, deformation resistance, and damage progression under traffic loads and environmental conditions1,2. The features of bituminous mastics are dependent upon the characteristics of both elements, encompassing binder grade, filler type, particle size distribution, surface chemistry, and filler dosage3–8.

Frequency-domain parameters like the dynamic shear modulus and phase angle are derived from oscillatory shear tests, these are commonly used to describe how viscoelastic materials behave at certain loading frequencies. Additional time-domain parameters, such as creep compliance and relaxation modulus derived from creep and stress-relaxation tests, give us an idea of how mastics respond over time when they are constantly being deformed. But traditional frequency-domain parameters like dynamic shear modulus and phase angle show responses that are specific to a certain frequency and may not fully capture long-term stress redistribution in realistic pavement loading situations9–13.

Compared to this, stress relaxation studies provide a more comprehensive understanding of viscoelastic material behaviour by directly characterising stress relaxation while maintaining constant strain. Bituminous mastics have to exhibit a spectrum of relaxation times, indicating the complexity of their microstructure, which comprises bitumen, mineral fillers, and associated physico-chemical interactions14–17. In linear viscoelasticity, fast relaxation intervals are associated with rapid stress relaxation characterised by viscous flow, whereas prolonged relaxation times are related to elastic stress storage and delayed relaxation.

Tschoegl18 discussed that relaxation spectra (temperature regime) give a better picture of how bituminous materials behave rheologically with single-point parameters (Viscosity, Relaxation modulus and Relaxation time). The significance of relaxation behaviour is directly applicable to pavement performance like stiffness reduction with time and better stress dissipation. Research conducted by Airey19, Jing et al.20, and Li et al.21 demonstrated that filler-modified mastics with extended relaxation times show enhanced resistance to permanent deformation, attributed to delayed stress relaxation at elevated service temperatures. On the other hand, at moderate and low temperatures, long relaxation times may slow down stress relaxation during repeated loading or thermal contraction, resulting in stress accumulation and the initiation of cracks. Petersen22 underscored that an optimal equilibrium of relaxation durations is crucial to attain sufficient rutting resistance while preserving fatigue and thermal cracking efficacy. Active fillers, like hydrated lime, tend to make relaxation times longer by creating strong physio-chemical interactions and stiffening the binder matrix. Inert fillers, on the other hand, mostly affect relaxation behaviour through volume-filling effects and particle–particle interactions5,23–25.

As bituminous mastics get older, they become even more difficult to relax because oxidation causes molecular restructuring that makes them stiffer and takes longer time to relax. Short time aging ageing can improve rutting resistance by delaying stress relaxation, but too much ageing can greatly lower relaxation capacity and make the material more likely to get damaged by fatigue. Recent research conducted by Nivitha et al.12, Sivaprakash and Ajithkumar14, and Rai et al.6 has shown that relaxation spectra are especially responsive to the effects of ageing and offer a more precise understanding of performance deterioration compared to conventional rheological parameters. Nonetheless, comprehensive studies of relaxation spectra for filler-modified mastics under extended ageing conditions are still insufficient, and direct comparisons between active and inert fillers within a cohesive relaxation framework are rare.

The motivation for this research stems from the necessity to enhance comprehension of how mineral fillers affect the time-dependent stress dissipation characteristics of bituminous mastics under laboratory simulated aging. Even though hydrated lime and quarry dust are commonly used as filler materials in bituminous concrete, most earlier studies have mainly looked at stiffness-related parameters from frequency domain tests. Not much attention has been paid to relaxation based behaviour, which directly shows how well mastics can relieve stress when they are under constant load. Because pavement materials are always under stress from traffic and the environment, it is important to measure relaxation modulus and relaxation time to find filler combinations that will make them last longer.

To address these gaps in research, this study looks at the relaxation behaviour of bituminous mastics that have been changed by adding two very different mineral fillers, hydrated lime and quarry dust, at different amounts and over time. Stress relaxation tests were performed at high temperatures on both unaged and aged mastics to measure the decay of relaxation modulus, the evolution of relaxation time, and the characteristics of stress relaxation. We used time–temperature superposition principles to create master curves of relaxation modulus that show how viscoelastic materials behave over a long period of time, beyond the time scale of the experiments.

This study is novel due to (i) the use of relaxation spectrum analysis to characterise the time-dependent behaviour of filler-modified bituminous mastics, (ii) the direct comparison of active and inert fillers under the same testing and ageing protocols, and (iii) the determination of optimal filler dosage based on relaxation behaviour rather than stiffness alone. The results yield novel mechanistic understanding of the interrelated impacts of filler type, dosage, and ageing on stress relaxation, establishing a solid basis for performance-driven and durability-focused design of bituminous mixtures subjected to continuous loading conditions.

Materials

Preparation of mastic

VG30 binder graded as per IS:73-2018 (BIS) was used in this study. Binder was procured from Hindustan Petroleum refinery in Mangalore. Hydrated lime and quarry dust were used as fillers. Hydrated lime contains 73.12% calcium oxide (CaO), followed by 11.94% silicon dioxide (SiO₂) and 5.21% aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃). Additionally, it includes 2.61% magnesium oxide (MgO) and 8.72% carbon (C). In contrast, quarry dust is predominantly composed of 64.74% silicon dioxide (SiO₂) and contains significant amounts of 10.44% aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) and 7.58% carbon (C) other elements in quarry dust include 3.81% potassium (K) and 13.26% iron (Fe). The above elemental composition of both the fillers are obtained by performing the Energy Dispersive X-Ray (EDX) test. The specific gravity of hydrated lime is 2.62, while quarry dust has a slightly lower specific gravity of 2.40.

The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of hydrated lime and quarry dust fillers are shown in Fig. 1, captured using SNE—3200 M model and the imaging was conducted at an accelerated voltage of 30 kV with a magnification ranging from 300µ and 30µ. A comparison of the SEM images reveals a significant difference in the texture and the morphology of the two fillers. Hydrated lime exhibited fine, irregular particles with a porous texture, especially at higher magnification (30µ) shown in Fig. 1a and b, suggesting a high surface area that enhances its chemical reactivity. This porosity and surface roughness indicates that hydrated lime can promote better chemical bonding with bitumen5,16,26–28. In contrast Fig. 1c and d quarry dust exhibited larger particles, well-dispersed particles with angular shapes, and its rigid and angular structure contributes to superior mechanical interlocking when blended with bitumen, thereby improving the modulus and structural strength of the mixture. However, due to its relatively smoother surface, quarry dust is less likely to enhance the chemical interaction with bitumen.Fig. 1SEM images of Hydrated Lime and Quarry Dust.

SEM images of Hydrated Lime and Quarry Dust.

Mastic samples were prepared by blending VG30 bitumen with different dosages of fillers. Samples were manually prepared by blending VG30 bitumen with hydrated lime and quarry dust. First, the bitumen was placed in a container and preheated in an oven at 90 °C for 5 min. The container was then transferred to a hot plate. once the bitumen melted, it was stirred continuously while a measured quantity of hydrated lime was gradually added. Mixing was carried out at 160 °C for 15–20 min to ensure uniform blending. Stirring continued until the hydrated lime particles were fully mixed with the binder, producing a homogeneous mixture and preventing particle settlement at the bottom. After several laboratory trials, a constant mixing time of 20 min was standardized for all dosages. The prepared mastic was then transferred to various vessels for testing. The same procedure was repeated for preparing quarry dust treated mastics.

The filler dosages of 20%, 30%, and 40% by weight of binder were selected to prepare the mastics. In the bituminous mixtures, mineral filler is commonly used to improve stiffness, durability, and binder aggregate interaction, and previous studies have shown that the rheological response of mastics is highly sensitive to filler concentration. Earlier investigations on bituminous mastics have frequently adopted filler contents (0.2–20%) to evaluate the effect of filler addition on binder behaviour5,15–17,24. Therefore, the selected dosages were chosen to represent low, intermediate, and relatively high filler dosages, allowing the influence of filler content on the relaxation behaviour of the mastics to be systematically examined.

The unaged samples designated as VG30, HLM20, HLM30, HLM40, QDM20, QDM30, and QDM40. To study the influence of aging, the mastic specimens were aged in a hot-air oven at a constant temperature of 100 °C for 10, 50, 100, and 200 h. The aging temperature was selected to simulate the oxidative aging of the mastic under controlled laboratory conditions. Aging durations of 10 h and 50 h were considered as short-term aging, representing the early stage of mastic oxidation, while 100 h and 200 h were treated as long-term aging conditions. These aging intervals were selected to simulate the long-term aging of mastics that occurs under field conditions.

These intervals were selected to examine how rheological properties evolve under prolonged aging. During the aging, the mastic thickness was maintained at 3–5 mm. The aged specimens were labelled as HLM20-10H, HLM20-50H, HLM20-100H, HLM20-200H, and QDM20-10H, QDM20-50H, QDM20-100H, and QDM20-200H. The same nomenclature pattern was followed for HLM30, HLM40, QDM30, and QDM40 for their respective aging durations. The oven aging protocol was adopted in this work to simulate laboratory aging conditions and capture systematic changes in material behaviour with increasing exposure time.

Test protocol

A Dynamic Shear Rheometer (DSR 102, Anton Paar) was used to test the mastic samples under unaged and aged conditions. All the samples were subjected to the stress relaxation test using a 25 mm plate geometry and a 0.5 mm sample thickness. The tests were performed at 40, 50, and 60 °C. The shear strain linearly increased from 0 to 10% in 0.2 s and then maintained at constant at 10% for 600 s. The linear viscoelastic limit for the stress relaxation test was determined using unaged HLM30 at 40 °C by applying strain levels of 10%, 15%, and 20%. Based on the scaling principle, the relaxation modulus curve obtained for 10% and 15% strain showed overlap within ± 5% indicating linear viscoelastic behaviour. Following the scaling principle, 10% strain were selected for all subsequent stress relaxation experiments. Each sample was tested for at least two trials, and the corresponding repeatability results at 50 °C are presented in Fig. 2. For clarity, 10% error bars were included to assess the variation between repeated trials. The close overlap between the trials indicates that the relaxation modulus measurements were repeatable and showed only minor variation within the acceptable range.Fig. 2Repeatability of stress relaxation test for VG30 at 50 °C.

Repeatability of stress relaxation test for VG30 at 50 °C.

Influence of filler dosage on unaged mastic

However, when the dosage increased from 30 to 40%, a reduction in relaxation modulus was observed. Figure 3b shows that the relaxation modulus of QDM increased consistently with 20, 30 and 40% dosages. Based on the results shown in Fig. 3, the 30% filler dosage was selected for further comparison because HLM30 exhibited higher relaxation modulus among the hydrated lime mastics. This dosage therefore represents the optimum filler content for examining the influence of filler type on the relaxation behavior of mastics. Figure 3a and b shows that HLM30 has the highest modulus compared to QDM30 at both the temperatures.

This behaviour can be attributed to the stronger physico-chemical interaction between hydrated lime and bitumen. Previous rheological investigations showed that hydrated lime increases the relaxation modulus of the mastic up to an optimum dosage of 20%, indicating the formation of a stronger elastic binder–filler network2,6–8,14,22,29,30. Hydrated lime can exhibit both physical and chemical interactions with bitumen, whereas quarry dust mainly contributes through physical interaction. From the previous literature studies results Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis also confirmed the presence of hydroxyl, carbonyl, and aliphatic functional groups resulting from the interaction between hydrated lime and the polar components of bitumen, which enhance binder–filler adhesion1,10,12,16,17,29. These interactions strengthen the binder–filler interface and improve the internal structure of the mastic, enabling HLM30 to retain stress for a longer duration and resulting in a higher relaxation modulus. In contrast, quarry dust contains a high proportion of silica and interacts mainly through weaker physical mechanisms such as particle packing and mechanical interlocking, leading to comparatively faster stress relaxation7,9,28.

While comparing the peak modulus of different mastics, it was observed that the HLM30 modulus increased with the increase in filler dosage, except in the case where hydrated lime was added at more than 30% by weight. The stress relaxation pattern at 50 °C revealed a rapid reduction in relaxation modulus for all the mastics tested. 98–99% of the applied stress relaxed within the first 0.5 s, while the remaining 1–2% of stress relaxation occurred between 0.5 and 10 s. Figure 4a, shows high modulus followed by HLM40, and VG30 has lowest modulus. Figure 4b, showed more viscous response at 60 °C compared to 50 °C. The stress relaxation behavior of VG30, QDM30, and HLM30 mastics (unaged) exhibited three distinct viscoelastic regimes shown in Fig. 4c. The Regime 1 (0–0.5 s) had a high relaxation modulus, indicating the stiffness of the mastic. This elastic response occurs across a shorter span. Regime 2 (0.5–10 s) highlighted the change from elastic to viscoelastic behavior, as shown by a sharp fall in relaxation modulus owning to stress dissipation. In this region, HLM30 and QDM30 has slower modulus loss than VG30, showing that fillers increased structural integrity and delayed stress relaxation. In regime 3 (10–600 s), the modulus decreased due to viscous flow. VG30 exhibited the highest reduction in modulus, indicating higher susceptibility to permanent deformation, whereas HLM30 retained a comparatively higher modulus throughout this period, suggesting resistance to viscous flow and improved long-term stability. Overall, the addition of hydrated lime, and to a lesser extent quarry dust, improved the initial stiffness, delayed viscoelastic relaxation. This regime reflects the time—dependent flow of the bitumen and filler network, and fillers act to retard these processes by reinforcing the internal structure of the mastic Hydrated lime, in particular, strengthens the binder–filler interface through physio-chemical bonding, while quarry dust provides mechanical interlocking and increased stiffness of the mastic structure14,15,29.Fig. 4Compares the variation of relaxation modulus of unaged HLM and QDM at 30% filler dosage.

Influence of filler dosage at aged conditions

Figure 5 shows the relaxation modulus of HLM at three filler dosages (20%, 30%, and 40%) under different aging conditions, such as 50 h and 200 h, along with the corresponding peak modulus values. As seen in Fig. 5a, HLM20 remained largely independent of aging. In contrast, Fig. 5b shows that HLM30 exhibited the highest modulus, with peak modulus following a similar trend across the aging conditions. This behaviour may be attributed to carbonation, where hydrated lime, when exposed to oxygen at higher dosages, undergoes oxidation that leads to the formation of carbonyl compounds6,17,19. This decline can be attributed to prolonged aging, since the reduction of calcium oxide in hydrated lime weakened the mastic and reduced its relaxation modulus and Fig. 5d confirms that the peak modulus of HLM20 at different aging conditions did not significantly influence the mastic2. This indicates that lower dosages of hydrated lime do not have a substantial impact on the bitumen. Relaxation time for unaged and aged mastic

In the stress relaxation test, a constant strain is applied to the material and the decrease in stress with time is recorded. The relaxation time represents the time required for the stress to reduce to a specific fraction of the initial or peak stress value, indicating how quickly the material releases the stored stress. In this study, relaxation times corresponding to 50% and 75% of the peak stress were considered. The 50% relaxation time (HLM50 and QDM50) refers to the time required for the stress to drop to half of the initial peak stress, while the 75% relaxation time (HLM75 and QDM75) represents the time taken for the stress to reduce to 75% of the initial value. These parameters provide useful insight into the stress dissipation characteristics of the mastics. Materials with shorter relaxation times relax stress more rapidly, whereas longer relaxation times indicate slower stress relaxation behaviour12,31. The relaxation times were denoted as HLM20-50, HLM20-75, QDM20-50, and QDM20-75 for the 20% filler dosage, and the same nomenclature was adopted for the remaining dosages. Master curve

A temperature range of 40, 50, and 60 °C was selected for master curve construction, 40 °C taken as the reference temperature (Tref). The relaxation modulus data obtained at different temperatures were superimposed using the time–temperature superposition principle, in which isothermal response measured at temperatures above the Tref was horizontally shifted along the time axis toward longer reduced times until continuity between adjacent datasets was achieved. The shifting process involved the progressive alignment of the higher temperature isotherms with the 40 °C response to obtain a single continuous master curve. The procedure was carried out using RHEA, which applies a pair-wise shifting routine to linear viscoelastic data by minimizing mismatch between neighbouring isotherms during superposition. The resulting overlap of the shifted curves produced a smooth continuous relaxation modulus master curve, indicating that thermo-rheological simplicity was reasonably maintained within the investigated temperature range.

The observed relaxation behaviour also indicates bituminous mixture performance under loading conditions. Filler interaction mechanism based on relaxation behaviour

The higher efficiency of HLM relative to QDM is mostly attributed to the improved binder-filler interaction established inside the mastic network. The current study demonstrated that HLM30 consistently displayed superior peak relaxation modulus, prolonged stress relaxation, and elevated master curve modulus compared to QDM30 in both unaged and aged states, signifying a greater resistance to stress dissipation. This behaviour is directly associated with the physico-chemical properties of hydrated lime, which comprises a high calcium oxide content (70%) and fine porous particles, as corroborated by Scanning Electron Microscopy observations, facilitating enhanced surface interaction with the polar fractions of bitumen2,6,8,29. The FTIR analysis revealed enhanced interactions via hydroxyl and carbonyl functional groups, indicating that hydrated lime alters the binder structure beyond just particle reinforcement. These interactions enhance binder–filler adhesion and establish a stronger internal network, hence delay a modulus degradation during relaxation4,22,23.

On the other hand, quarry dust, which has a mineral composition rich in silica, assists in physical interaction and particle interlocking, offering very limited chemical input to the mastic. Consequently, QDM mastics demonstrated a relatively accelerated modulus decline and diminished peak modulus in both relaxation modulus and master curve analysis7. The aged relaxation modulus obtained in HLM30 indicates that hydrated lime enhances oxidative resistance by stabilising the binder structure, while quarry dust primarily reinforces the system mechanically. Comparable behaviour has been documented in prior research, indicating that hydrated lime improves mastic stiffness via both chemical bonding and the adsorption of polar compounds, whereas silica-based fillers predominantly function through volumetric reinforcement1,2,4,10,16,20,29.

Conclusions

This experimental investigation evaluated the viscoelastic behaviour of hydrated lime (HLM) and quarry dust (QDM) modified bituminous mastics using stress relaxation tests, taking into account the effects of filler dosage, temperature, and aging. The results revealed that relaxation behaviour is strongly influenced by filler type and dosage, and aging conditions.

The base VG30 binder had the lowest relaxation modulus and peak stress, with fast stress dissipation (99.68% within 0.5 s at 40 °C), indicating a viscous response. Among the modified mastics, HLM30 consistently demonstrated the highest relaxation modulus and peak stress, establishing it as the optimal dosage for stiffness increase. Hydrated lime mastics relaxed slowly due to stronger binder–filler interactions, whereas QDM mastics relaxed quicker due to physical interaction. Master curve analysis revealed that HLM30 retained a higher modulus across longer loading durations in both unaged and aged conditions, indicating improved long-term stress resistance as compared to HLM20, HLM40, and all QDM mastics. Under aging, HLM30 retained its higher stiffness and delayed relaxation, indicating improved resistance to oxidation-induced deterioration, whereas, QDM mastics relaxed quicker and were more susceptible to oxidative stiffening. Overall, the data showed that the hydrated lime at an optimum dosage improves the short and long-term viscoelastic performance of bituminous mastics.
