Adrenal suppression secondary to inhaled corticosteroids

Asthma is one of the most common chronic childhood diseases. Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) are the foundation of treatment, and daily low-moderate dose ICS are significantly safer than repeated courses of systemic steroids. However, systemic effects from ICS include adrenal suppression (AS), which is under-recognized and can be life-threatening. Exogenous steroids, including ICS, exert negative feedback on the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis; when doses are supraphysiologic, this suppresses endogenous cortisol production, leading to insufficient cortisol response to stressors with abrupt dose decreases or discontinuation. Clinicians need a high index of suspicion for AS. Signs and symptoms are often nonspecific: decreased linear growth, fatigue, nausea/vomiting, abdominal pain, headache, myalgias/arthralgias, psychiatric symptoms, and emergent hypoglycemia or hypotension (1,2).

A 2-year Canadian pediatric surveillance study reported 46 cases of symptomatic AS secondary to exogenous steroids, 80% associated with ICS (alone or with other steroid forms) (3). The prevalence and severity of AS secondary to ICS are likely underestimated due to variability in measurement methods, definitions, subtlety of symptoms, and comorbid conditions (3). While ICS are safe in most pediatric patients, pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic features, most notably dose, influence AS risk (1,4). High dose ICS is associated with greater rates of AS, highlighting the importance of employing the lowest effective dose and consideration of steroid-sparing therapies, such as long-acting beta agonists, leukotriene receptor antagonists, or biologics. For example, of the symptomatic AS cases with ICS found in the Canadian surveillance study, most used high-dose fluticasone (≥500 mcg/day) for months or longer (3). Other factors increasing AS risk include: concurrent use of other steroid formulations (intranasal, oral, topical), concurrent CYP3A4 inhibitors (antiretroviral and antifungal agents), lower BMI for age, evening ICS administration, ICS particle size, and plasma protein-binding (5).

The Canadian Pediatric Society and Adrenal Suppression Working Group recommend screening for AS in: asymptomatic children receiving high dose ICS therapy for ≥3 months, any dose ICS in conjunction with a CYP3A4 inhibitor, and any children with AS symptoms, regardless of ICS dose (1). Although there are no guidelines, risk factors should raise the index of suspicion for AS and warrant clinical consideration.

Initial screening should consist of a morning serum cortisol (Figure 1). Patients must have a sleep-wake cycle, testing should be done before 8 a.m. to capture the physiologic morning cortisol peak, all ICS held for at least 24 hours and oral steroids held for 48 to 72 hours (4,5). A diagnostic ACTH stimulation test should be performed when morning cortisol is intermediate or low; however, management should be initiated while awaiting diagnostic clarity. While the cut-offs are somewhat arbitrary and local reference ranges must be considered, the literature generally states that a level <100 nmol/L is considered low. In this case, stress dosing should be prescribed (hydrocortisone 30 to 50 mg/m2/day divided TID-QID for moderate illnesses or hydrocortisone 100 mg/m2 IM/IV for severe illness or adrenal crisis) and consideration can be given for maintenance replacement, although practice variations exist across Canada, while awaiting consultation with a Pediatric Endocrinologist. Levels of >350 to 500 nmol/L are generally considered to be reassuring. Levels between 100 and 275 nmol/L are considered intermediate; these patients likely have sufficient maintenance cortisol but can be prescribed stress dose hydrocortisone while awaiting further evaluation. There is uncertainty regarding management of patients with cortisol levels 275 to 350 nmol/L and further research is needed. Serum morning cortisol screening is limited by low sensitivity (approximately 60%); thus Endocrinology discussion is also warranted for clinical suspicion or persistent symptoms despite normal morning cortisol (1,4,5).

Inhaled corticosteroids are generally safe and are critical in asthma management. However, to prevent morbidity and mortality from AS secondary to ICS, it is important to follow age-related maximal dosing recommendations found in the Canadian Thoracic and Pediatric Society guidelines (CTS, CPS; 6,7). Counselling on AS risk, ensuring the correct diagnosis, treating comorbidities (e.g., rhinitis), assessing inhaler technique, inhaler counting and supply, adherence, and trigger exposure should be completed at each visit and before escalating ICS dose. Most pediatric asthma can be managed with low (<200 mcg/day fluticasone equivalent) to moderate (200 to 250 mcg/day ages 1 to 5 years, 201 to 400 mcg/day ages 6 to 11 years) dose ICS; of note, there is variability in classification of ICS doses, and moderate dose outlined in the CTS/CPS guidelines is higher than other asthma guidelines. Pediatric Respirology consult and steroid-sparing asthma therapies should be considered for difficult to control asthma where high-dose (for age) ICS is being considered. Regular follow up should be arranged for all children on ICS to re-evaluate the lowest effective dose for control (1,6).
