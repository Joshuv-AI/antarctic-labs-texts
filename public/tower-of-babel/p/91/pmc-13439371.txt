Effects of asphaltene from residual crude oil on CO₂ foam stability
Authors: Elahe Beheshti, Siavash Riahi, Masoud Riazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
CO2 foam stability in the presence of oil is crucial for CO2 geo-storage in depleted hydrocarbon reservoirs. While previous studies have examined the impact of oil on foam stability, the understanding of how asphaltenes affect foam stability is limited. This study investigates the effects of asphaltene on CO2 foam solutions in synthetic and crude oil samples. Spinning-drop interfacial tension (IFT) measurements showed that asphaltene significantly increased IFT across all oil types. Asphaltene hinders surfactant molecules from reducing IFT and improving surface activity. Foam half-life measurements showed that asphaltene concentrations of 1.6% and 8% decreased average foam stability by 80% and 85%, respectively. Without asphaltene, increasing the surfactant concentration from 0.1% to 1% reduced IFT and increased foam stability. However, in the presence of asphaltene, increasing the surfactant concentration from 0.1% to 0.5% improved foam stability and reduced IFT, whereas increasing it from 0.5% to 1% slightly decreased foam stability and raised IFT. Despite similar asphaltene content, foam stability was higher in crude oil than in synthetic oil, underscoring the influence of oil viscosity and asphaltene stability on foam performance. Image-based analysis further confirms that asphaltene accelerates bubble coalescence and promotes non-uniform bubble growth, leading to a faster decay in bubble number over time. This study sheds light on the application of CO2 foam injection for underground CO2 storage in depleted oil reservoirs, where the presence of oil residue and asphaltene precipitation may hinder CO2 containment.

Introduction

Foams are gas-liquid multiphase mixtures stabilised by surfactants that have attracted considerable attention for various applications. Industries utilise foams in firefighting, textile wet processing, personal care products, mineral flotation, separation processes, enhanced oil recovery (EOR), and CO2 storage. These diverse applications highlight the significance of foams in industrial and environmental contexts.

Foam injection into subsurface geologic porous media is a crucial technique for enhancing CO2 storage by improving fluid flow properties and optimising CO2 mobility. By increasing the viscosity of the injected fluids, foam reduces CO2 mobility and enhances trapping mechanisms within the pore matrix. Additionally, foam plays a vital role in diverting CO2 into unswept reservoir zones, thereby improving sweep efficiency and increasing the reservoir’s overall CO2 storage capacity. However, maintaining foam stability presents significant technical challenges, particularly in the presence of oil, which can destabilise foam by thinning the films and promoting bubble coalescence. This instability diminishes the foam’s effectiveness in blocking high-permeability zones and controlling gas channelling. Several factors, including pore structure and architecture, capillary pressure and wettability, foam viscosity, rock permeability, temperature, and pressure, influence foam stability in porous media. Pore structure properties, such as size, shape, tortuosity, and connectivity, play a significant role in determining foam stability. Despite the differences in foam behaviour between bulk and porous media, bulk foam stability is equally critical for CO2 storage, as the longevity of foam films is essential for preventing premature gas breakthrough or leakage. Therefore, understanding and enhancing foam stability in bulk and porous environments is vital for optimising CO2 injection and ensuring efficient storage in hydrocarbon reservoirs1–8.

Several studies have explored the effects of hydrocarbons on foam stability. Despite extensive research, the behaviour of foam in the presence of hydrocarbons remains complex, leading to inconsistent findings in the literature. While some researchers have noted a destabilising effect of oil on foam, others have reported beneficial effects. For example, Arnaudov et al.9 examined the role of oil additives on foaming and foam stability, highlighting the importance of interfacial properties. They found that dynamic surface tension decreases at low oil concentrations, thereby enhancing the solution’s foamability. However, at higher oil concentrations, the oil negatively affects the foaming ability. Similarly, Zhang10 investigated synthetic oil solutions and found that adding n-dodecane to anionic surfactant systems significantly improved foam stability.

Vikingstad et al.11 conducted several static foam stability experiments to investigate the interactions between foam and oil. They observed that foam could still form in the presence of oil, even at surfactant concentrations below the CMC. Regarding the effect of alkanes, the authors found that the molecular weight of the hydrocarbons influenced foam stability. Specifically, if oil molecules were dissolved in the surfactant micelles, the foam structure could deteriorate. They noted that the foam stability increased in the presence of long-chain hydrocarbons compared to its absence, attributing this to a slower drainage rate in the foam’s liquid films. Furthermore, the impact of asphaltene content in crude oils was explored. They found that the presence of asphaltene slightly enhanced foam stability but did not offer a clear explanation for this effect. They suggested that interactions with foam solutions differ from those with paraffinic hydrocarbons due to the polarity of asphaltene molecules, indicating that the effect cannot be attributed solely to hydrophobic interactions. However, their analysis had a limitation: crude oil contains many other components that may influence foam stability. Thus, attributing variations in foam stability solely to asphaltene content may not accurately reflect its isolated impact.

Sun et al.12 indicated that foam stability would increase in the presence of oil at certain oil concentrations. Pu et al.13 investigated the effects of various parameters—such as oil type and concentration, surfactant type and concentration—on foam stability. Their findings confirmed that oil density and viscosity significantly influence foam stability. They observed that oil could enhance foam stability at certain concentrations. Additionally, they noted that increasing the hydrocarbon chain length and surfactant concentration led to greater foam stability.

However, contrary to these findings, several other studies have highlighted the negative impact of hydrocarbons on foam stability. Lau and O’Brien14 reported on their experiments examining the effects of spreading and non-spreading oils on foam stability. They concluded that spreading oils have a more harmful impact on foam stability than non-spreading oils. Kuhlman15 visualised the effects of crude oil on foam stability and observed that the presence of light hydrocarbons in crude oil increased oil spreading, thereby reducing foam stability. Schramm and Novosad16 noted that lighter crude oils tend to reduce foam stability more significantly than heavier crude oils. Later, Schramm17 suggested that highly oil-sensitive foams could remain stable in porous media as long as the oil content is sufficiently low. Lee et al.18 reported that oil present in surfactant micelles can reduce foam stability by decreasing the foam’s structural energy, resulting in thinner liquid films. The effects of n-hexane on foam stability were investigated, and it was found to reduce both foamability and foam stability in surfactant solutions.

The stability of foam in the presence of hydrocarbons has also been investigated under different conditions. Nikolov et al.20 were among the first to identify the existence of an asymmetrical film at the interface of three phases—oil, gas, and water—in a foam solution. They suggested that this film plays a crucial role in controlling foam stability in the presence of an oil phase. Furthermore, they emphasised that a suitable surfactant for generating stable foam solutions in the presence of oil must meet three key criteria: (a) the surfactant should significantly reduce interfacial tension, resulting in the formation of small oil droplets; (b) it should create a high surface tension gradient to slow the thinning of pseudo-emulsion films; and (c) its concentration must be sufficiently above the CMC to ensure that the micellar structure within the thinning film remains stable.

Lobo and Wasan21 proposed the formation of a compact micellar structure at high surfactant concentrations, resulting in a stratification phenomenon that enhances foam stability. Additionally, the literature indicates that specific additives can significantly affect foam stability. For instance, Schramm17 noted that the stability of colloidal particles in foam solutions could either decrease or increase. One proposed mechanism for this effect is an increase in the bulk fluid viscosity due to colloidal particles. Moreover, the tendency of partially water-wetting particles to adsorb at the interface can enhance the mechanical stability of the lamellae. Based on similar studies, Sheng8 observed that asphaltene could increase the stability of foamy oils. Farzaneh and Sohrabi22 conducted experiments on foam stability in the presence of crude oil and found that increasing surfactant concentrations initially improved foam stability up to a certain level, after which further increases led to a reduction in stability. This suggests the existence of an optimal surfactant concentration at which foam stability is maximised.

In our previous study, we explored the effects of aromatic components on foam stability23. Click or tap here to enter text. Compared with aliphatic components, aromatic components have less harmful effects on foam stability. In addition, we observed that the presence of hydrocarbons increases the surfactant CMC. Alexander et al.24 conducted an experimental study in 2-D Hele-Shaw cells to investigate the effects of surfactant structure and asphaltene aggregates on foam stability. The reduction in foam stability caused by asphaltene depended on the surfactant type. However, they only tested surfactant concentrations equivalent to the CMC and did not conduct experiments specifically designed to examine the effects of asphaltene. Instead, the authors compared different oils with varying asphaltene concentrations. Recently, Chen et al.25 examined the mechanisms by which asphaltene aggregates can act as surface-active agents to generate oil-based foams in the absence of surfactants. They suggested that asphaltene precipitation and aggregation do not alter the solution’s interfacial properties, as indicated by pendant drop IFT measurements. However, the accuracy of pendant drop measurements may not be sufficient for systems with low IFT values, such as those containing surfactants, where IFT can fall below 1 mN/m. Therefore, their analysis may not apply to foams generated by surfactants.

The impact of asphaltene molecules, as one of the most challenging components, exists in oil reservoirs26,27- On foam stability has remained underexplored. In this study, we systematically designed experiments using synthetic and crude oils to investigate the effects of various factors on foam stability. These factors include hydrocarbon type and concentration, surfactant concentration, and the asphaltene content in the hydrocarbon phase. In this study, we explicitly consider the negative impacts of asphaltene on CO2 foam stability, employing asphaltene concentration as an independent variable in the experiments. A correlation was found between IFT measurements and foam stability in the presence of asphaltene. Based on foam stability and IFT measurements, we propose a possible mechanism for asphaltene’s influence on surfactant solutions that complements the findings of Chen et al.25 regarding the behaviour of asphaltene aggregates in systems containing surfactants.

Although previous studies have reported the influence of asphaltene on foam-related systems, the role of asphaltene in surfactant-stabilised aqueous CO₂ foams in the presence of residual oil remains insufficiently resolved. Foam stability arises from the interplay of multiple interconnected mechanisms, including interfacial tension reduction, competitive adsorption of surface-active species, and film drainage behaviour. Therefore, it is essential to isolate the contribution of individual components to accurately evaluate their respective impacts on foam stability and bulk behaviour.

In this study, the asphaltene concentration is treated as an independently varied parameter under otherwise identical chemical conditions, while the surfactant chemistry is held constant. This controlled experimental approach enables a direct correlation between asphaltene loading, ultralow interfacial tension behaviour, and bulk foam stability. This aspect has not been systematically documented in the existing literature. Unlike studies on foamy oils or oil-based foams—where asphaltene may enhance stability by increasing oil-phase viscoelasticity—the present study focuses on aqueous CO₂ foams, in which gas–water interfacial properties and surfactant adsorption govern stability.

Furthermore, differences across prior reports highlight the strong system dependence of asphaltene effects. For example, studies employing pendant-drop tensiometry without surfactants have reported limited asphaltene adsorption at oil–gas interfaces, whereas the present work evaluates surfactant–asphaltene interactions under foam-relevant conditions using spinning-drop tensiometry, which is better suited for ultra-low interfacial tension measurements in systems containing strong surface-active species. In addition, synergistic stabilisation has been observed for oil-based foams formulated with fluorinated surfactants. However, the present study intentionally restricts the formulation to a single, widely used alpha-olefin sulphonate (AOS) surfactant to isolate asphaltene effects without added formulation complexity.

Through this approach, the present work aims to investigate the role of asphaltene in surfactant-stabilised aqueous CO₂ foams, with particular emphasis on its influence on interfacial tension behaviour and bulk foam stability across a range of surfactant and asphaltene concentrations. This study provides formulation-relevant insights for screening CO₂ foam systems in depleted oil reservoirs, while recognising that bulk-scale observations represent a preliminary step towards more comprehensive porous media evaluations. Despite extensive studies on asphaltene–foam interactions, systematic bulk-scale investigations that independently vary asphaltene concentration under otherwise identical chemical conditions to directly correlate interfacial tension behaviour and CO₂ foam stability in the presence of residual oil are lacking. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to establish a quantitative, bulk-scale correlation between independently controlled asphaltene concentration, ultra-low interfacial tension behaviour, and CO₂ foam half-life in surfactant-stabilised aqueous systems containing residual oil. The insights from this study can inform the optimisation of CO2 foam injection into depleted oil reservoirs, where the effectiveness of CO2 geostorage practices may be affected by the presence of asphaltene and its precipitation.

Asphaltene preparation and sample preparation

The primary goal of this study was to investigate the effects of asphaltene on foam stability. The IP143 method was carefully followed to extract asphaltene from crude oil. The method used to extract asphaltene can be found elsewhere28–30.

Various types of foam solutions, including hydrocarbon constituents, were prepared. Simplified synthetic oils were employed to enable systematic control of aromaticity and asphaltene stability while minimising compositional ambiguity. Although such systems do not replicate the full complexity of crude oil reservoirs, they allow isolation of asphaltene-related effects that simultaneous variations in resins, waxes, and other surface-active species may otherwise obscure. The aromaticity of crude oil and the resin-to-asphaltene ratio influence the extent of asphaltene aggregation. Resins can associate with asphaltene molecules, thereby reducing their tendency to aggregate and precipitate, enhancing asphaltene solubility and consequently influencing the foaming and defoaming behaviour of crude oil. The required amount of surfactant, based on the desired weight fraction, was added to DIW and stirred slowly for 10 min to prepare a foam solution. Most often, for applications involving the injection of foam into subsurface porous media, e.g., for enhanced oil recovery and CO2 storage, synthetic sulphonate surfactants (e.g., AOS surfactants) are widely used. This is mainly due to their availability, high compatibility with the hard connate water phase in the reservoir, low adsorption on rock surfaces in porous media, and proper foaming properties. The two types of hydrocarbon solutions were prepared separately. The details of the hydrocarbon solutions are presented in Table 3. Then, the required amount of asphaltene was added to the solutions. The prepared hydrocarbon solution was mixed for 10 min to ensure that the asphaltene was fully dissolved. Asphaltene was weighed and gradually added to the oil phase, then mixed under continuous stirring until no visible particles remained. Homogeneity was verified by visual inspection and by the absence of sedimentation for 1 h before the experiment. Asphaltene is expected to exist primarily as dissolved molecules under the tested conditions, using synthetic solutions, as toluene in the synthetic solution serves as an asphaltene solvent. Finally, the hydrocarbon solution was added to the surfactant solution.

Measurements of interfacial tension

The presence of a surfactant in the solutions considerably reduces the IFT between the different phases. The spinning drop method is an efficient IFT measurement method for accurate measurement of the IFT between oil and water in the presence of surfactants, as it allows for measuring the IFT at very low range values31. Accordingly, in this study, the Kruss spinning drop tensiometer, capable of measuring IFT values as low as 1.0e-5 mN/m, was used. A recirculating heat bath maintained the experiments at a constant temperature. All IFT experiments were conducted at 20 °C. The samples were allowed to equilibrate for at least 90 min before recording the equilibrium IFT values to ensure steady interfacial conditions. For each measurement, the acceptable variations in IFT were set to be less than 5% over 1 h. The calibration was conducted based on a dosing needle with a known outer diameter of 0.78 mm as the reference object. Details of the measurement procedure and mathematical operations used to obtain the IFT are presented in our previous study23.

Foam stability apparatus

The experimental setup used in this study mainly comprises a relatively high-pressure foam vessel connected to a control valve and a pressure transducer. This in-house vessel is specifically designed to measure foam stability at pressures up to 15 bars and temperatures up to 100 °C. The controlling valve is connected to a CO2 gas bottle. To experiment, the foam solution is poured into the vessel. The required amount of hydrocarbon solution will be added to the foam solution. Foam was generated using a gas sparging method, in which CO₂ was injected into the surfactant solution in a graded tube at a controlled flow rate to reach 10 bars, which allowed the foam column to rise to 50 cm (the height of the graded tube). Once the foam height reaches half its initial value, the foam half-life is recorded. The foam half-life was determined by measuring foam height with a graduated tube and by careful visual inspection. The half-life time could then be considered a measure of foam stability. A graphic representation of the foam stability apparatus is shown in Fig. 1.

Foam stability measurements conducted in bulk provide valuable information on formulation sensitivity and interfacial behaviour, but cannot fully represent foam dynamics in porous media. Additional mechanisms, such as lamella division, snap-off, oil entrapment, and flow-induced regeneration, strongly influence foam propagation and longevity in reservoir environments. Consequently, the foam half-life values reported in this study should not be interpreted as direct predictors of foam performance in situ. Instead, they serve as a screening metric to identify unfavourable formulations and guide system selection before more complex porous media or core flood experiments. The present framework will be extended to porous media systems under conditions where CO₂-induced asphaltene precipitation may occur.

Results and discussion

Foam stability in the absence of asphaltene

Impact of oil type and concentration on the stability of foam

The first part of the experiments was dedicated to investigating the influence of different hydrocarbon components on foam stability in the foam solution without asphaltene. A series of experiments was conducted without asphaltene to determine the effects of hydrocarbon type and concentration, and of surfactant concentration. In our previous study, the surfactant’s CMC was 0.01 wt%. Therefore, our initial approach was to investigate the impact of hydrocarbons at surfactant concentrations equal to the CMC. The results showed that, in the presence of any hydrocarbon type, either no foam was generated or, if foam did form, it broke down immediately within 1–5 s, as soon as the foam was generated. These preliminary experiments demonstrated that foam solutions should contain a sufficiently high surfactant concentration to generate foam in the presence of hydrocarbons. Figure 2 presents the results of further experiments conducted to evaluate the impacts of hydrocarbon type and concentration at different surfactant concentrations above the CMC level (0.1, 0.5, and 1 wt%). As shown in Fig. 2a–c, increasing the oil volume in the foam solution decreases the foam stability for both types of synthetic solutions. Another observation is the higher stability of the foam for oil type 1 compared to oil type 2. This suggests that n-heptane would have a greater destructive effect on foam stability than toluene for synthetic oil solutions. In the presence of oil type 2, which has a higher n-heptane content, foam stability is lower across all hydrocarbon volumes and concentrations.

Fig. 2Impact of different oil types and concentrations on foam stability in the absence of asphaltene in oil solution with surfactant concentrations of (a) 0.1 wt%, (b) 0.5 wt%, and (c) 1 wt%.

Impact of different oil types and concentrations on foam stability in the absence of asphaltene in oil solution with surfactant concentrations of (a) 0.1 wt%, (b) 0.5 wt%, and (c) 1 wt%.

In the presence of hydrocarbons, two different types of interfaces are formed: the hydrocarbon-water interface inside the aqueous phase and the water-gas interface. Some of the surfactant molecules are transferred to the hydrocarbon-water interface, facilitating the dispersion of the hydrocarbon phase in water. This decreases the surfactant concentration at the water-gas interface. The surface activity of the gas-water interface is crucial for foam stability32. As the surfactant concentration at this interface decreases, the surface activity and, hence, foam stability are adversely affected. Furthermore, in a solution containing surfactant micelles, oil molecules would adsorb onto surfactant micelles or be adsorbed at the water-gas interface. Although some notions in the literature13,33 suggest that the viscosity of the liquid film would increase in the presence of hydrocarbons, the competition between these conflicting mechanisms, i.e., the reduction of the surface activity and the increase in liquid film viscosity due to the presence of hydrocarbons, would govern the impact of the presence of hydrocarbons on the stability of the foam. In our study, the presence of n-heptane and toluene molecules in the hydrocarbon phase would result in lower foam stability at higher oil concentrations, as their effects on the surface activity of the surfactant solutions are more pronounced. The discrepancy in foam stability between the two types of synthetic hydrocarbons could be attributed to differences in solution viscosity and the components’ micellar solubility. It can be concluded that the viscosity and micellar solubility of the oil components in solutions with similar components would play a role in examining the effects of oil on foam stability. Consequently, type 2 oil, which has a higher n-heptane content, lower viscosity, and higher micellar solubility, has a more detrimental effect on foam stability23.

Impact of surfactant concentration on foam stability

Figure 3a and b present the experimental results, focusing on the impact of surfactant concentration. As these figures suggest, higher surfactant concentrations are associated with greater foam stability. As shown in Fig. 3a, for oil type 1, foam stability increases significantly as the surfactant concentration increases from 0.1 to 0.5 wt%. At 4 vol% hydrocarbon, the foam half-life time doubles from 819 to 1660 s as the surfactant concentration increases from 0.1 to 0.5 wt%. Although a further increase in surfactant concentration from 0.5 to 1 wt% would result in higher foam stability, the extent of the increase in foam stability is not as significant as that observed for the difference between 0.1 and 0.5 wt%. This observation could be made across all cases with different hydrocarbon types and volumes. The impact of hydrocarbon volume is less significant at higher surfactant concentrations.

Fig. 3Impact of surfactant concentration on foam stability in the absence of asphaltene in oil solutions with 1 vol% and 4 vol% for (a) Type 1 oil and (b) Type 2 oil.

Impact of surfactant concentration on foam stability in the absence of asphaltene in oil solutions with 1 vol% and 4 vol% for (a) Type 1 oil and (b) Type 2 oil.

The presence of a surfactant directly influences the solution’s surface activity. When no oil is present in the solution, the surface activity of the surfactant solution reaches its maximum at the CMC. However, in the presence of oil, surfactant molecules compete for potential adsorption sites at the interface between the phases, and the solution’s surface activity decreases. Increasing the surfactant concentration could compensate for the reduction in surface activity caused by oil molecules. Furthermore, it has been observed that stratification would occur more slowly with increasing surfactant concentration. A process leading to higher foam stability. Furthermore, the thinning film existing in the foam system is more stable at higher surfactant concentrations34.

The IFT between three different hydrocarbon phases, namely, toluene, oil type 1, and oil type 2, was measured in line with the foam stability experiments. Figure 4 shows the results of IFT measurements for different hydrocarbon and surfactant solutions at surfactant concentrations of 0.1, 0.5, and 1 wt%.

Fig. 4IFT between hydrocarbons, i.e., toluene, oil type 1, and AOS surfactant solutions with surfactant concentrations of 0.1, 0.5, and 1 wt%.

IFT between hydrocarbons, i.e., toluene, oil type 1, and AOS surfactant solutions with surfactant concentrations of 0.1, 0.5, and 1 wt%.

The findings indicate that raising the surfactant concentration significantly lowers the IFT. For example, when the surfactant concentration increases from 0.1 wt% to 1 wt%, the IFT between toluene and the surfactant solution drops from 0.123 mN/m to 0.006 mN/m. Similar trends are observed for other types of hydrocarbons. It can also be observed that changing the hydrocarbon type slightly changes the IFT. However, changing the surfactant concentration is considerably more important than changing the hydrocarbon type. The IFT results show similar surfactant solution behaviour at different concentrations and in contact with different oil types. Lower IFT values for the solutions correspond to higher foam stability.

Foam stability in asphaltene presence

Since asphaltene molecules can act as surfactants by adsorbing at the gas-liquid interface, the initial focus was on examining the foamability of solutions containing asphaltene rather than conventional surfactants. This approach generates foamy oil, with asphaltene serving as the surface-active agent. However, no foam was produced in the absence of surfactants, even under high-shear conditions and increasing mass flow rates. This observation suggests that an effective surfactant capable of significantly reducing the gas-liquid IFT to very low levels is essential for achieving foamability in these solutions.

Impact of oil type and concentration on the stability of foam

A series of systematic experiments was conducted to understand the effects of asphaltene on foam stability. Different parameters, including hydrocarbon type and concentration, asphaltene concentration, and surfactant concentration, were investigated in these sets of experiments. Figure 5a and b show the impact of hydrocarbon type and concentration on foam stability in the presence of asphaltene for solutions with 0.1 wt% of surfactant. As these figures show, similar behaviour of different hydrocarbon solutions can be observed when asphaltene is present in the foam. For instance, Fig. 4a shows that in the presence of 1.6 wt% asphaltene, increasing the hydrocarbon volume from 1 vol% to 4 vol% decreases the foam half-life time from 257 to 169 s. Similar trends were observed at higher surfactant concentrations (0.5 wt% and 1 wt%): a decrease in foam stability of solutions containing asphaltene due to an increase in hydrocarbon volume in the foam and the n-heptane content of the hydrocarbon phase, as discussed in later sections.

Fig. 5Impact of oil type and concentration on foam stability in the presence of asphaltene with a surfactant concentration of 0.1 wt% for solutions containing (a) 1.6 wt% and (b) 8 wt% asphaltene.

Impact of oil type and concentration on foam stability in the presence of asphaltene with a surfactant concentration of 0.1 wt% for solutions containing (a) 1.6 wt% and (b) 8 wt% asphaltene.

The same mechanisms discussed in Section “Impact of oil type and concentration on the stability of foam ” could be considered as the main reasons for the trends observed in Fig. 5a and b. The solutions’ viscosities and lower surface activity at higher oil volume explain the differences observed in foam stability between the two oil types and hydrocarbon volume in foam solutions. Asphaltene stability in oil type 1 is higher due to its higher toluene content30. As discussed in more detail, when asphaltene molecules are more stable, the foam’s stability increases.

Impact of asphaltene concentration on the stability of foam

Figure 6a and b show the impact of asphaltene concentration on foam stability. As shown in these figures, foam stability decreases substantially when asphaltene is present in the solution. For example, Fig. 6a shows the stability of foam solutions containing 0.1 wt% surfactant and varying volumes of oil type 1 at different concentrations. In the presence of asphaltene, the foam half-life time decreases from 1428 to 257 and 188 s by adding 1.6 and 8 wt% asphaltene, respectively. Asphaltene, even at very low levels, can have significant detrimental effects on foam stability. Similarly, asphaltene is observed at a 4 vol% hydrocarbon concentration. Similar trends could be observed in Fig. 5b for type 2 oil.

Fig. 6Impact of asphaltene concentration on foam stability with 0.1 wt% surfactant concentration for (a) oil type 1 and (b) oil type 2.

Impact of asphaltene concentration on foam stability with 0.1 wt% surfactant concentration for (a) oil type 1 and (b) oil type 2.

This observation could be mainly attributed to the role of asphaltene at the gas-liquid interface. Figure 7 shows a graphical representation of the molecular distribution of different phases in a foam system comprising the CO2 bubble, surfactant solution, and hydrocarbon additives to further clarify the role of asphaltene. Figure 7a illustrates the molecular arrangement of the surfactant when no additives are present. The ability of surfactant molecules to increase surface activity is at its maximum when no additive is present in the solution. Higher adsorption of surfactant monomers at the interface results in higher solution surface activity. This could be one of the reasons for the higher stability of the foam in the absence of hydrocarbon additives. The presence of any hydrocarbon additives, while maintaining the surfactant concentration, would reduce the surface activity of the solution due to the formation of surfactant-hydrocarbon aggregates. In other words, surfactants disperse the asphaltene molecules in the solution. This observation has also been verified by MD simulations in previous studies35. The simplified schematic is shown in Fig. 7b. More surfactant molecules are required to saturate the interface between the CO2 and water phases. As depicted in Fig. 7c, the presence of asphaltene molecules would result in further reduction in the efficiency of the surfactant solutions. Asphaltene molecules can be dissolved in surfactant micelles when they are present in the system. Additionally, since asphaltene molecules are surface-active components, there would be a competition between surfactant molecules and asphaltene molecules to be adsorbed at the solution interface. The presence of CO2 in the system will trigger asphaltene precipitation36,37. The aggregation of asphaltenes and their adsorption at the gas–liquid interface diminishes the surface activity of the liquid solution near the CO₂ bubbles. As observed in earlier studies, the higher the surface activity of the foaming agent solution, the more stable the foam23. Moreover, when surfactant and asphaltene molecules are present in a solution, asphaltene molecules tend to be adsorbed on the surfactant micelles or even attached to the surfactant monomers. The latter phenomenon could also increase the surfactant solution’s CMC. It should also be noted that at higher concentrations, asphaltene molecules are more prone to instability and precipitation from the hydrocarbon solution. Asphaltene precipitation increases the adsorption of asphaltene molecules at the interfaces29. This phenomenon would consequently result in lower foam stability. The proposed interaction between the surfactant and asphaltene at the gas–water interface should be regarded as a hypothesis inferred from correlated experimental trends rather than as a directly observed mechanism. This study does not include dynamic adsorption measurements, interfacial imaging, or precipitation diagnostics under CO₂ injection. Nevertheless, the consistent correspondence between trends in interfacial tension and foam half-life across multiple oil compositions and asphaltene concentrations suggests that competitive interfacial interactions play a controlling role in foam destabilisation under the tested conditions.

This observation aligns with the foam stability measurements, which indicate that asphaltene markedly lowers foam stability.

Impact of surfactant concentration on foam stability

Figure 9a–d show the effects of surfactant concentration on foam stability in asphaltene solutions. Increasing the surfactant concentration from 0.1 to 0.5 wt% substantially increases foam stability. However, further increases in surfactant concentration led to reduced foam stability. Similar results were observed with increasing hydrocarbon volume in the solutions. A similar trend was observed across both asphaltene concentrations (1.6 and 8 wt%) and both oil types. For instance, in the presence of 1.6 wt% asphaltene in a solution containing 1 vol% oil type 1, the foam half-life time first increases from 257 to 1877 s when the surfactant concentration increases from 0.1 to 0.5 wt%. However, a further increase in the surfactant concentration from 0.5 wt% to 1 wt% decreased the half-life time from 1877 to 1526 s.

Impact of surfactant concentration on foam stability in the presence of (a) 1.6 wt% asphaltene and oil type 1, (b) 1.6 wt% asphaltene and oil type 2, (c) 8 wt% asphaltene and oil type 1, and (d) 8 wt% asphaltene and oil type 2.

Variation in the interfacial tension of surfactant solutions in the presence of solid particles and impurities is the main explanation for this interesting observation. In the presence of such impurities, increasing the surfactant concentration decreases the interfacial tension to a minimum, whereas further increases in surfactant concentration cause it to become slightly higher38. Figure 10 shows IFT measurements as a function of surfactant concentration to validate the effect of surfactant concentration on IFT in solutions containing asphaltene. While there is no asphaltene in the synthetic oil samples (Fig. 10a), increasing the surfactant concentration would reduce the IFT. However, the presence of a small amount of asphaltene in the solution (as shown in Fig. 10b and c) would alter the trend in the surfactant concentration impact. Increasing the surfactant concentration from 0.1 wt% to 0.5 wt% in the presence of asphaltene initially reduces the IFT, but increasing the surfactant concentration to 1 wt% slightly increases the IFT.

As discussed above, lowering interfacial tension is a crucial factor in generating stable foam in the presence of surfactants. Accordingly, any increase in the interfacial tension of a foam solution would lower its stability. As shown in Fig. 9, initially, increasing the surfactant concentration results in a decrease in IFT and an increase in solution surface activity, leading to a significant increase in foam stability. However, further increases in surfactant concentration would increase interfacial tension and lower foam stability.

Impacts of crude oil and synthetic hydrocarbon solutions on foam stability

Synthetic solutions provide useful information on interactions, foam instability, and decay mechanisms for solutions containing hydrocarbon components. However, the performance of the foam solution needs to be examined with crude oil. Comparing the results of the experiments with synthetic hydrocarbon solutions and crude oil would provide insight into how foam-decay mechanisms occur in reservoirs. Figure 11 compares the foam stability of synthetic hydrocarbon solutions containing 8 wt% asphaltene with that of crude oil. As shown in Fig. 11, the range of foam stability with crude oil is significantly higher than that with synthetic hydrocarbon solutions, especially for solutions with lower surfactant concentration, despite similar asphaltene content (Fig. 11a). This observation is valid for both oil concentrations. For instance, the foam half-life times of oil types 1 and 2 with 1 vol% surfactant for solutions containing 0.1 wt% surfactant are 188 and 113 s, respectively. The corresponding value for the solutions containing crude oil is considerably higher, standing at 1813 s, which is almost 10 times higher than the value for oil type 1 and 16 times higher than the value for oil type 2. However, Fig. 11b shows that the extent of the differences is much lower for the higher surfactant concentration (0.5 wt%).

Fig. 11Comparison of foam stability with synthetic hydrocarbon solutions with 8 wt% asphaltene and crude oil with (a) 0.1 wt% and (b) 0.5 wt% surfactant concentrations.

Comparison of foam stability with synthetic hydrocarbon solutions with 8 wt% asphaltene and crude oil with (a) 0.1 wt% and (b) 0.5 wt% surfactant concentrations.

The viscosity of crude oil is higher than that of synthetic hydrocarbon solutions. As a result, the liquid film drainage rate would be slower in the presence of crude oil, thereby increasing foam stability with crude oil relative to solutions containing synthetic hydrocarbons. Moreover, as mentioned in previous sections, the stability of the asphaltene molecules and clusters is crucial to foam stability. Only a small amount of toluene is present in the synthetic hydrocarbon solutions used in this study, which could stabilise asphaltene particles in the solution. However, in crude oil, there are many other hydrocarbon components, including aromatic compounds and resins, which help stabilise asphaltene molecules. The results in Fig. 11 suggest that not only the concentration of asphaltene but also its stability state play important roles in the effects of asphaltene molecules on foam stability. Figure 11a and b show that the difference in foam stability between crude oil and synthetic solutions is much smaller at higher surfactant concentrations. This observation again highlights that asphaltene’s greater stability in the hydrocarbon phase can affect foam stability. The effects of other oil components, including resins, on foam stability remain to be investigated. Some components can individually increase or decrease foam stability; they can influence foam stability differently, and sometimes contradictorily, when present in mixtures like crude oil, due to differences in their interactions with other oil components.

Foam bubble image analysis

Detailed image analysis of foam bubble evolution was conducted to further support and strengthen the findings of this study, with particular focus on the influence of asphaltene and other oil components on foam behavior. The results of this section are obtained based on the image analysis of foam bubbles in the presence of 1.6 wt% asphaltene and 1 vol% of oil type 2 with surfactant concentration of 0.5 wt% unless otherwise stated. Figure 12a–f illustrate the temporal evolution of foam bubbles in the presence of 1.6 wt% asphaltene and oil type 2 (1 vol%) during the first 360 s of foam generation. A clear change in bubble size distribution is observed over time within the selected region, indicating the dynamic nature of foam restructuring.

As highlighted by the dashed red circles, liquid-phase migration within the foam lamellae and Plateau borders is evident, accompanied by liquid accumulation in the Plateau borders and the consequent thickening of these regions. This redistribution of liquid contributes to localized destabilization of the foam structure. Initially, the local foam bubbles tend to shrink in size as liquid drainage progresses. In contrast, adjacent bubbles exhibit growth, resulting in increased non-uniformity in bubble size distribution.

Following the coalescence of smaller bubbles, the overall number of bubbles decreases while larger bubbles are formed, further reducing foam stability and accelerating structural decay. Another notable phenomenon observed is the accumulation of the separated oil phase from the aqueous phase within the foam lamellae, as indicated by the blue circles. The amount of accumulated oleic phase within the lamellae increases progressively over time, suggesting enhanced oil entrapment and increasing interference with foam film stability.

Figure 14 compares the temporal evolution of foam bubble number over 7 min for systems with and without asphaltene, using oil type 2 and a surfactant concentration of 0.5 wt%. Here, N denotes the total number of bubbles identified through image-based analysis, and its semi-logarithmic representation reflects the overall foam decay kinetics. The approximately linear behavior of ln (N) with time suggests that foam degradation follows a first-order decay mechanism, where bubble loss is predominantly governed by coalescence and film rupture events.

The slope of the semi-logarithmic curve provides a quantitative measure of the decay rate and, consequently, foam stability. A markedly higher decay constant is observed in the presence of asphaltene (− 0.022) compared to the asphaltene-free system (− 0.0028), indicating a nearly order-of-magnitude increase in foam destabilization rate. This pronounced acceleration in decay can be attributed to the interfacial activity of asphaltene molecules, which are known to adsorb at the gas–liquid interface and modify the rheological and structural properties of the foam films and Plateau borders. Rather than stabilizing the interface, at the investigated concentration and oil content, asphaltenes likely promote interfacial heterogeneity and disrupt the viscoelastic integrity of the surfactant film, thereby facilitating film thinning and rupture.

Furthermore, the consistently lower bubble number observed in the asphaltene-containing system indicates that asphaltene also suppresses initial foamability, likely by competing with surfactant molecules for interfacial adsorption sites and reducing the efficiency of foam film formation during generation. The combined effect of reduced foam generation and accelerated bubble coalescence leads to significantly diminished foam stability. Overall, these results suggest that under the studied conditions, asphaltene acts as a destabilizing agent, enhancing both dynamic coalescence kinetics and interfacial film failure processes, thereby governing the observed reduction in foam lifetime.

Conclusions

This experimental research was conducted to explore the effects of asphaltenic oil on foam stability, a critical factor for the success of CO2 foam injection as a CO2 geo-storage scheme. A series of systematic experiments was conducted to evaluate various key parameters, including surfactant concentration, oil type, and asphaltene concentration. In systems without asphaltene, synthetic oil solutions containing a greater proportion of aliphatic components (represented in this study by n-heptane) exhibited more detrimental effects on foam stability, primarily because of their lower viscosity and higher micellar solubility. The surfactant surface activity is critical to foam stability. In the presence of the oil phase, a surfactant concentration higher than CMC is required to generate stable foam. Asphaltene had a detrimental impact on foam stability. The presence of asphaltene hinders the proper surfactant molecule coverage at the interface. Similar trends were observed in IFT measurements: as the asphaltene concentration increased, the IFT between synthetic oil and surfactant solutions decreased. Furthermore, higher asphaltene content in synthetic solutions resulted in higher IFT values. In the presence of asphaltene, foam stability first increases significantly and then decreases slightly as the surfactant concentration increases. The main cause of this behaviour is a decrease in surfactant effectiveness in reducing interfacial tension, which can be observed at high surfactant concentrations in the presence of surface-active organic additives. IFT measurements confirm this observation: in the presence of asphaltene, increasing the surfactant concentration to a specific level results in lower IFT, and further increases in surfactant concentration increase the IFT. Image-based analysis further supports these findings by revealing accelerated bubble coalescence, enhanced liquid drainage within Plateau borders, and a progressive reduction in bubble number, all of which indicate a faster foam decay rate in the presence of asphaltene.

A key limitation of this study is its exclusive reliance on bulk foam stability measurements, which do not account for flow- and pore-scale effects in reservoir systems. In addition, image analysis indicates that foam degradation follows an approximately exponential decay behavior, suggesting that future modeling efforts should incorporate time-dependent coalescence kinetics. Future work will focus on extending this analysis to porous media experiments and to conditions where CO₂-induced asphaltene precipitation may occur.
