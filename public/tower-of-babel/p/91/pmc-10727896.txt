Multiple Warty Dyskeratomas of the Scalp: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Ken Shiraishi, Koji Sayama
Published: 2023

Dear Editor:

We describe a 54-year-old female who presented with a 1-year history of multiple papules on the scalp. The number of the lesions were gradually increasing. Topical steroid therapy did not improve the lesions. She had no family history of similar skin disorders. Physical examination revealed approximately 30 hyperkeratotic papules on the scalp (Fig. 1A). Dermoscopy revealed well-demarcated, brownish round-shaped lesions with some central white clods around the hair (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Histological examination revealed several large cup-shaped epidermal invaginations filled with keratotic plugs (Fig. 1B). Typical corps ronds were detected in the thickened granular layer (Fig. 2A). The lower portion of each invagination showed suprabasal clefts and villi lined by a single layer of basaloid cells. Many acantholytic dyskeratotic cells were identified above the villi (Fig. 2B). The other biopsy sample revealed a cystic lesion composed of widely dilated follicular infundibulum in the dermis. Corps ronds, suprabasal clefts, villi, and acantholytic dyskeratotic cells were also detected in the follicular epithelium (Supplementary Fig. 1B). A diagnosis of multiple warty dyskeratomas (MWDs) of the scalp was made. The lesions were treated with 10% urea-containing lotion. Some of the papules resolved, and itching was reduced.

Warty dyskeratoma (WD) is a rare benign tumor that typically presents as a solitary, whitish-brown papule or nodule on the scalp, face, or neck in middle-aged or older adults1. MWDs are very rare, and only a few cases have been published1,2,3. Interestingly, most patients were middle-aged or older females, and had no family history of similar skin lesions.

All previously reported cases involved the scalp; however, some patients also had WDs on other body sites such as face, neck, and hands3. The number of lesions varies from 3 to over 1001. They were diagnosed between 1 year and 12 years after development3. The etiology of WD is unknown, but a viral infection, smoking, autoimmunity, chemical carcinogens, and ultraviolet light have been suspected as playing roles1. Kaddu et al.4, confirmed that human papillomavirus plays no role in the etiology of multiple lesions. A follicular infundibular origin has also been proposed1. In our case, widely dilated follicular infundibulum in the dermis had the same histological findings (acantholic dyskeratosis) in the follicular epithelium, which supports the association between WDs and follicular units. However, oral and vulvar lesions that normally lack hair follicles have been described, which could not be explained by these proposals5. Therefore, some investigators suggest that mucosal WD might have a different origin from multiple skin WDs5.

First-line treatment of WD is the surgical excision of lesions. Other treatments, such as tazarotenic acid gel, laser therapy, 5-floxuridine, tretinoin cream, and calamine lotion, have been reported. Systemic isotretinoin (30 mg/day) with topical tretinoin cream was reported to improve hundreds of widespread WDs in one patient2. In our patient, 10% ureacontaining lotion showed some effects, so this might be one treatment that should be tried.

In conclusion, the clinical diagnosis of MWDs is extremely difficult. Histopathological examination should be considered in patients with multiple hyperkeratotic papules on the scalp.
