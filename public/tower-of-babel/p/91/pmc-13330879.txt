Detection of cancer recurrence from Thai-English electronic medical records using sentence embeddings
Authors: Ekapob Sangariyavanich, Wanchana Ponthongmak, Nawanan Theera-Ampornpunt, Nat Tangchitnob, Gareth J McKay, Ammarin Thakkinstian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study developed and validated monolingual and bilingual sentence-bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (SBERT) models for detecting cancer recurrence within Thai-English electronic medical records (EMRs) from Thai cancer hospitals.MethodA multicentre dataset of 32 436 documents from 1250 patients was used for model development. External validation involved an independent dataset of 9244 documents from 384 patients across two Thai cancer hospitals. Performance was benchmarked against a fine-tuned PubMedBERT (MetBERT).ResultsThe development dataset included breast (43.9%), colorectal (12.1%), cervical (28.0%) and head and neck (16.0%) cancers. MetBERT achieved the highest area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) for locoregional versus no recurrence (11.1%) and locoregional versus distant recurrence (91.7%), while monolingual-SBERT excelled at distant versus no recurrence (32.0%). External validation demonstrated MetBERT superiority for locoregional versus no recurrence (9.30%–21.50%). For distant versus no recurrence, bilingual-SBERT performed best with AUPRC 17.55%–24.39%. While MetBERT led in distinguishing locoregional versus distant recurrence (88.30%–94.70%), bilingual-SBERT demonstrated robust external validation performance (AUPRC 85.25%–91.80%).DiscussionLow AUPRC values (9%–32%) reflect the extreme class imbalance in real-world data (~1% recurrence prevalence). Despite this, fine-tuned MetBERT achieved highest performance, while bilingual-SBERT demonstrated superior robustness during external validation. This validates sentence embedding models for handling mixed Thai-English medical records in multilingual clinical environments.ConclusionSentence embedding frameworks provide a practical, generalisable solution for detecting cancer recurrence within multilingual EMRs. Despite text-length constraints, these models are suitable for clinical integration as a screening tool for cancer registry workflows.Keywords: Electronic Health Records, Natural Language Processing

Introduction

Cancer continues to represent a significant global health burden, with 23.6 million new cases and 10 million deaths documented worldwide in 2019.1 Although therapeutic advances have been made, cancer recurrence persists and shortens survival time.

Real-world data (RWD) derived from electronic medical records (EMR), disease registries and claims databases has increasingly gained recognition as an important complementary data source for recurrence detection.2 These RWD sources provide valuable insights into actual clinical practices, treatment utilisation patterns and patient heterogeneity that may not be fully captured in controlled trial settings. Nevertheless, the utilisation of RWD for cancer recurrence identification presents significant methodological challenges due to inherent variability, including data structure, incomplete documentation and unstructured data formats. Manually collecting data on cancer recurrence is a significant burden for registrars, consuming 15% to 50% of their workload.3 Several factors contribute to the completeness and accuracy of this data including inconsistent definitions of recurrence, staffing shortages and varying levels of registrar experience.

To address the burden of workload and time-consuming manual processes, numerous studies4,10 have used natural language processing (NLP) by transforming unstructured texts (eg, medical notes/records, pathology reports, radiology reports) from the EMRs into structured data11 combined with machine learning (ML)4,68 or deep learning (DL)7 9 10 approaches to detect cancer recurrence. Systematic review evidence12 has shown that the contextual word embeddings like bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT)13 could improve performance and overcome traditional ML approach limitations. Among the models reviewed, MetBERT,10 fine-tuned PubMedBERT14 model on MIMIC-III15 dataset, stood out with excellent performance through test and external validation, although more external validations are still required.

Sentence embeddings address this limitation by capturing semantic meaning and contextual relationships, allowing sentences with similar meanings to be grouped together in a vector space. Reimers et al16 introduced sentence BERT (SBERT) by fine-tuning BERT and using Siamese or triplet networks for pairwise sentence comparisons. This transition balances semantic granularity against computational throughput. While BERT provides nuanced token-level interactions, SBERT maps sentences into a vector space, trading explicit cross-token attention for the computational efficiency of cosine similarity. This innovation allowed SBERT to significantly outperform previous sentence embedding methods, for example, InferSent17 and universal sentence encoder.18 In addition, SBERT was also successfully applied in medical contexts, such as COVID-19 severity,19 psychological disorders from interviews and social media data.20 However, sentence representation techniques have not yet been applied to detect cancer recurrence in breast, head and neck, cervical and colorectal cancers. This study aims to address this gap by exploring this application.

Material and methods

Study design and setting

This multicentre retrospective-cohort study included five specialised oncology hospitals (online supplemental figure 1) in Thailand between 1 January 2018, and 31 December 2022: National Cancer Institute (NCI), Lopburi Cancer Hospital (LCH), Chonburi Cancer Hospital (CCH), Surat Thani Cancer Hospital (SCH) and Ubon Ratchathani Cancer Hospital (UBCH). The study was conducted in two phases. First, model development utilised data from three cancer hospitals (NCI, CCH and LCH). Second, external validation phase using datasets from SCH and UBCH. The study obtained ethical approval from all the hospitals involved (EC number: COA 023/2023). The study workflow is shown in online supplemental figure 2.

Data source

Potential patients were identified through two data sources: First, the Thai Cancer Base registry was the primary data source using the international classification of diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) codes for identifying patients (breast [C50.x], colorectal [C18.x-C20.x], head and neck [C01.x-C06.x, C09.x-C13.x, C30.x-C32.x] and cervical [C53.x] cancers). Second, data from the hospital information system were linked to retrieve patient information including diagnosis, treatment, surgical procedure, radiotherapy, chemotherapy by international classification of diseases, 9th revision – clinical modification (ICD-9-CM) and clinical notes. A cohort was constructed based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) adult patients newly diagnosed with one of the pre-specified primary cancers, (2) no distant metastasis at diagnosis and (3) received curative-intent treatment. The patients were excluded if (1) had double primary cancers, (2) had residual or recurrent disease within 3 months of their last cancer treatment and (3) follow-up period less than 6 months (online supplemental figure 3).

Outcome data and ascertainment process

The outcome of interest was cancer recurrence, defined as recurrence occurring more than 3 months after the completion of the last cancer treatment period. Outcome verification details are provided in the online supplemental file 1.

After manual verification, documents (excluding treatment period data) were cross-referenced using the LLM Gemini 1.5 Flash with specific prompts. Disagreements between the LLM labels and the initial human annotations were resolved by the principal investigator (ES).

Data preprocessing

Textual data from outpatient EMRs and scanned documents (via optical character recognition (OCR)) were combined. Thai text was translated to English using the Google Translate API for monolingual datasets. Preprocessing steps included lowercasing, replacing abbreviations, removing punctuation, tokenising numerical values to special tokens (<num>). To fit model input limits (~400 words (~510 tokens)) for both the monolingual and bilingual datasets, document truncation followed a clinical hierarchy prioritising pathology and radiology reports over general clinical notes. This was implemented through fixed word-count allocations focused specifically on text following keywords such as ‘diagnosis’ and ‘impression’, ensuring that important clinical findings from each report were preserved.

The development dataset was partitioned into training, validation and test sets, using a 60:20:20 ratio with patient-level stratification.

Baseline model

We selected MetBERT10 as the highest performing model from our systematic review12 as the baseline model with the specific hyperparameters as reported in the original study.21

Model development

The SBERT model16 was fine-tuned for three binary classification tasks: (1) locoregional recurrence versus no recurrence, (2) distant recurrence versus no recurrence and (3) distant recurrence versus locoregional recurrence. Model development details are provided in Supplementary information.

Monolingual-SBERT development involved three steps: (i) vocabulary expansion and fine-tuning, (ii) fine-tuning SBERT and (iii) classifier training.

Monolingual-SBERT development involved three steps: (i) vocabulary expansion and fine-tuning, (ii) fine-tuning SBERT and (iii) classifier training.

Bilingual-SBERT model used pre-trained multilingual-SBERT LaBSE architecture,22 which supports both Thai and English clinical texts. The bilingual-SBERT model was trained using input length of 512 tokens. Fine-tuning and classifier training followed the same procedures described for the monolingual-SBERT model.

The bilingual-SBERT model was trained using input length of 512 tokens. Fine-tuning and classifier training followed the same procedures described for the monolingual-SBERT model.

Model evaluations

The model evaluations included both discrimination (ie, area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), F1 score, precision and recall) and a calibration Brier score.

Model predictions of false positives (FP) and false negatives (FN) were analysed. Shapley additive explanation (SHAP)23 was used to assess token impact and feature contributions. The error analysis was conducted for both monolingual and bilingual-SBERTs. The prediction threshold was set at the value achieving a recall of 75%–80% on the validation dataset.

External validation

The best SBERT models were externally validated using clinical notes from two independent hospitals SCH and UBCH, with performance compared against the development phase.

Results

Study characteristics

Clinical documents for 1250 (32 436 documents) and 384 patients (5772 documents) (table 1) were included in the development and external validation phases, respectively (online supplemental table 1). Breast cancer was most prevalent in the development dataset (43.92%), followed by cervical cancer (28.00%), head and neck cancer (16.00%) and colorectal cancer (12.08%). Clinical documents were labelled at the document level for locoregional and distant recurrence. This resulted in severe class imbalance, with recurrence-related documents representing only 0.98%–1.59% of documents across both datasets. Most documents were clinical notes, with radiology and pathology reports relatively limited, especially in the external-validation datasets.

Model performance

For locoregional recurrence vs no recurrence, MetBERT achieved the highest AUPRC in all datasets: 11.10% in the test dataset (recall 80.65%), 21.50% in SCH and 9.30% in UBCH (online supplemental table 2). Monolingual-SBERT and bilingual-SBERT models showed comparable performance (p>0.05) across all sets.

For distant versus no recurrence, monolingual-SBERT model performed best in the test dataset (32.00%, 95% CI 24.90 to 41.60) and in the UBCH dataset (17.60%, 95% CI 11.10 to 29.40), significantly outperforming bilingual-SBERT in the test dataset (AUPRC 18.00%, p=0.04) (online supplemental table 3).

For discriminating distant from locoregional recurrence, the MetBERT model achieved the highest AUPRC of 91.70% (recall 75.00%), 88.30% and 94.70% in test, SCH and UBCH datasets, respectively (online supplemental table 4). Bilingual-SBERT showed slightly higher AUPRC than monolingual-SBERT across datasets (eg, 86.44% versus 78.40% in the test dataset), although these differences were not statistically significant (p=0.39).

Subgroup evaluations across cancer types (online supplemental table 5) revealed that MetBERT generally achieved the highest AUPRC for locoregional versus no recurrence, particularly in external validation, despite bilingual-SBERT’s success in breast and colon cancer test sets. In distant versus no recurrence, monolingual-SBERT outperformed MetBERT in most scenarios, including all cancer types in the external validation and colon and head and neck cancers in the test set. Finally, for locoregional versus distant recurrence, MetBERT remained the top performing model for nearly all cancer types. Bilingual-SBERT consistently surpassed monolingual-SBERT, except in head and neck cancer for the test set.

External validation

External validation (SCH and UBCH) showed that MetBERT yielded the highest AUPRC for locoregional versus no recurrence (21.50% and 9.30%, respectively), (online supplemental table 2) and distant versus locoregional recurrence (88.30% and 94.70%, respectively) (online supplemental table 4). For distant versus no recurrence, bilingual-SBERT performed best at 24.39% (SCH) (online supplemental table 3), while monolingual-SBERT led at UBCH (17.60%).

Across both sites, bilingual-SBERT demonstrated comparable AUPRC to monolingual-SBERT across most tasks, except for significant differences in distant versus no recurrence (p value=0.04) and distant versus locoregional recurrence (p value <0.01) in the SCH dataset.

Discussion

Main findings

This study developed and externally validated NLP-based models for identifying cancer recurrence among 1250 patients with four major cancer types (breast, colorectal, cervical and head and neck cancers) using 32 585 EMRs from three specialised cancer hospitals in Thailand, with external validation on 9244 EMRs from 384 patients from two additional independent cancer hospitals. Recurrence rates ranged from 0.98% to 1.06% for locoregional recurrence and 1.13% to 1.64% for distant recurrences.

The fine-tuned MetBERT model demonstrated superior performance across most classification tasks, particularly for distinguishing locoregional from no recurrence (AUPRC: 11.10%, 21.50% and 9.30% in test datasets and SCH and UBCH external validation datasets, respectively) and distant from locoregional recurrence (AUPRC: 91.70%, 88.30% and 94.70%). For identifying distant versus no recurrence, the monolingual-SBERT model performed best in the test data (AUPRC: 32.00%) and in the UBCH validation dataset (AUPRC: 17.60%), while the bilingual-SBERT excelled in the SCH validation dataset (AUPRC: 24.39%). Notably, the bilingual-SBERT model generally outperformed the monolingual-SBERT model, which is attributed to its ability to handle mixed Thai-English medical terminology prevalent in the EMRs. This task-specific performance suggests that different architectures may capture distinct aspects of recurrence patterns within clinical text.

However, these AUPRC values remained relatively low (ranging from 9.30% to 32.00%) for tasks discriminating locoregional recurrence from no recurrence. This might reflect the extreme class imbalance in the dataset, where recurrence events are very rare. Although these results indicate that the models are not yet suitable for autonomous clinical decision-making, their high sensitivity (recall up to 98.36%) suggests potential value as a screening tool within cancer registry workflows. By efficiently filtering cases unlikely to have recurrence, the system could help registrars prioritise high-probability records for review, improving efficiency while maintaining rigorous data integrity.

The superior overall performance of the fine-tuned MetBERT compared with the SBERT models can be attributed to several factors. SBERT’s design inherently presents two primary limitations for this fine-grained clinical task. First, SBERT is primarily optimised for capturing overall semantic similarity between sentences, rather than the fine-grained word relationships and subtle clinical nuances critical for identifying recurrence indicators in complex medical records. Second, its reliance on pooling layers, particularly mean pooling, may result in information loss when condensing document representations. In contrast, fine-tuned MetBERT, specifically trained for cancer classification, allowed the model to learn and capture task-specific patterns and the full context of the EMRs more effectively.

The bilingual model outperformed the monolingual model, particularly when distinguishing distant from locoregional recurrence. Its success stems from processing mixed Thai-English records directly, skipping translation overhead, thereby preserving medical terminology. Furthermore, it captures more semantic information by preventing the fragmentation of English medical terms into meaningless sub-words.

External validation revealed notable variability in performance, particularly for locoregional recurrence detection, with AUPRC of 10.30% at SCH and 5.10% at UBCH. This variance is likely attributable to two key factors. First, cancer type distributions differed markedly between the datasets: the development dataset contained 43.92% breast cancer, while external sites showed more balanced distributions (approximately 25.00% for each cancer type). Second, the inherent heterogeneity in investigation reports and physician documentation styles across different healthcare settings contributed to this variability.

SHAP analysis revealed true-positive predictions used relevant clinical terms such as ‘metastatic’ ‘mass+neck’, while FPs frequently included irrelevant tokens. Misclassifications were mainly caused by duplicated notes, negations and treatment period data.

Comparison with previous studies

Compared with previous studies,7 9 10 24 our models achieved AUROC of 52.60%–93.40% (monolingual datasets) and 65.17%–86.83% (bilingual datasets), which aligned with prior studies (AUROC range: 71.00%–99.00%). However, our F1 scores (3.33%–73.60% monolingual; 4.78%–80.70% bilingual) and AUPRC (5.10%–78.40% monolingual; 4.68%–86.44% bilingual) were notably lower than those previously reported (F1-score, 24.00%–97.00%; AUPRC, 49.00%–94.00%).

This discrepancy is primarily attributed to the extreme class imbalance inherent in our real-world datasets. Unlike many prior studies that used balanced datasets, our evaluation reflects the true clinical prevalence of recurrence, where the positive cases naturally suppress precision-linked metrics such as F1 and AUPRC, despite maintaining high overall AUROC. Furthermore, the use of lengthy, unstructured, Thai-English EMRs presents a significant linguistic challenge. The high noise associated with long-form discharge summaries, combined with the complexities of medical abbreviations and idiosyncratic clinician-specific documentation styles, increases the likelihood of FP. This contrasts with previous studies10 24 which commonly used shorter discharge summaries with significantly higher recurrence proportions.

Consistent with other research,24 our models performed better in predicting distant recurrence than locoregional recurrence, likely because distant metastases follow more consistent metastatic patterns (eg, metastasis to liver, lungs, bone), whereas locoregional recurrence patterns vary widely by cancer site.

Strengths and limitations

This study’s strengths include the use of a multi-institutional dataset covering four cancer types and the evaluation of both monolingual and bilingual architectures. Additionally, the bilingual-SBERT architectures demonstrated that mixed Thai-English text can be processed directly without translation. External validation across two independent hospitals further confirmed the models’ robustness and generalisability. Finally, we demonstrated various adaptable methodologies integrating structured data (ICD-10/9 CM) with advanced NLP techniques like BERT, SBERT and LLMs, providing a framework that other institutions can implement based on their specific resource constraints.

Despite these strengths, the study also has some limitations. First, there is the potential for data inaccuracy stemming from the conversion process. Specifically, potential errors may arise from OCR when processing scanned clinical reports, particularly when stored within imaging files. This technological limitation could lead to incorrect extraction of critical textual data. Second, the ambiguous and subjective language often found in clinical notes poses a challenge to precise data interpretation. Phrases such as ‘suspect’ or ‘cannot be ruled out’ introduce uncertainty, making it difficult to definitively classify clinical events or findings using automated or semi-automated methods. Third, the inherent duplicative nature of EMRs, resulting from physicians frequently copying and pasting previous notes, can introduce information loss. This practice may lead to text truncation methods omitting key, updated clinical information. Fourth, regarding the monolingual dataset, the reliance on the translational process may introduce translation-related artefacts. However, we addressed this by developing a bilingual-SBERT architecture that processes mixed Thai-English EMRs directly. Fifth, two factors may limit the generalisability of our findings. The absence of crucial laboratory data (eg, tumour markers) in the dataset restricts the analysis of comprehensive disease status and treatment response. Furthermore, a significant demographic imbalance (with breast cancer comprising 43.92% of the development dataset) means the findings may not equally represent performance across all cancer types to other patient populations.

Future work

Future research should focus on improving model performance by refining preprocessing and chunking techniques, such as using adapted LLMs for text summarisation25 and applying context-aware methods like late chunking for embedding aggregation.26 Other promising directions include optimising LLM prompts and expanding the dataset to include a broader range of cancer types to enhance the model’s comprehensive understanding.

Conclusion

MetBERT achieved the highest performance, followed by bilingual and monolingual-SBERT models. Although showing slightly lower performance, SBERT models showed robustness during external validation. Truncation of long EMRs remains the main limitation. Nevertheless, sentence-embedding provides a practical and generalisable framework for detecting cancer recurrence from multilingual EMRs in real-world settings.
