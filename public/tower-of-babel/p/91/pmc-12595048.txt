Kinetoplast DNA structure, RNA editing patterns and small respiratory Complex I in Trypanosoma musculi
Authors: Ju-Feng Wang, Ying-Xin Bi, Michael Hammond, Julius Lukeš, De-Hua Lai, Zhao-Rong Lun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Kinetoplast (k) DNA, a specialized form of mitochondrial DNA in trypanosomes, is composed of catenated maxicircles and minicircles, and is essential for the survival of these parasitic protists. In this study, we provide a detailed description of the near-complete minicircle complement of Trypanosoma musculi, a globally distributed, mouse-specific haemoflagellate. We have identified 39 minicircle classes, which can be grouped via their structural organization into five distinct categories. Using RNA-seq, we have reconstructed mitochondrial transcripts of ATP6, RPS12, CYTB, COX2 and COX3, all of which undergo canonical editing that renders them translatable, as well as abnormally edited transcripts of four respiratory Complex I subunits. Additionally, through a combined analysis of the kDNA minicircles, edited mRNAs and small RNAs, we identified 108 guide (g)RNA genes and further categorized their conservation in comparison with Trypanosoma lewisi, an opportunistic human parasite. Moreover, we demonstrate a small respiratory Complex I in T. musculi, highlighting the functional consequences of missing gRNAs. Finally, we propose a model for the evolutionary maintenance of gRNAs, contributing to our understanding of kDNA dynamics in trypanosomes and other kinetoplastid flagellates.

Introduction

Trypanosomes are a diverse group of single-celled eukaryotes, and include significant human and livestock pathogens responsible for severe diseases such as Human African Trypanosomiasis and Chagas disease1. These parasitic protists are characterized by a unique mitochondrial DNA structure known as the kinetoplast (k) DNA, first described over a century ago as a structure adjacent to the flagellar base2,3. Within the genus Trypanosoma, the kDNA consists of two types of relaxed circular DNA molecules, so-called maxicircles and minicircles, mutually catenated into a single network (Jensen and Englund 2012). Maxicircles are usually between 20 and 30 kb long, present in about 20 copies per kDNA, with the same sequence identity. They encode mitochondrial protein-coding genes and mito-ribosomal RNAs, akin to the mitochondrial DNA in other eukaryotes4–7. In contrast, minicircles, numbering around 5,000 copies per kDNA, are much smaller, and exist in various sequence categories, forming the bulk of the densely packed kDNA disk8–11. Each minicircle harbors one or more species-specific conserved sequence regions (CSRs), which typically contain three conserved sequence blocks CSB1-CSB3s, which are critical for the replication of minicircles12.

A notable aspect of maxicircles is the presence of ‘cryptogenes’. A typical kDNA maxicircle encodes 20 genes, comprising two ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) and 18 protein-coding genes8,13–15. Among the protein-coding genes, 12 cryptogenes are present in Trypanosoma brucei8. Cryptogenes have to be decoded into functional transcripts, which require essential RNA editing, involving uridine (U) insertion/deletion at numerous sites16–18. RNA editing in trypanosomes involves several dynamic protein complexes with a range of enzymatic activities19, as well as an RNA component represented by guide (g) RNAs. These 20–60 nucleotide (nt)-long RNA molecules are primarily minicircle-derived and characterized by a unique 5’ anchor region for annealing to the pre-edited mRNA, followed by a central information region complementary to the edited sequence, allowing G:U wobble base-pairing, and a 3’ oligo (U) tail for stabilizing the gRNA-mRNA interaction20.

Recent studies have underscored the variability of kDNA structure and sequence across different trypanosomatid species21,22. While maxicircles have been extensively utilized as a molecular marker for phylogenetic analysis, reflecting the evolutionary history and relationships of trypanosomes, they sometimes fall short in resolving complex taxonomic issues9,13,23,24. In these cases, kDNA minicircles provide additional or complementary information10,25, offering insights into the finer aspects of trypanosome evolution.

Trypanosoma musculi, a mouse-specific species, belongs to the subgenus Herpetosoma, which it shares with T. lewisi, a neglected zoonotic pathogen26. These two trypanosome species infect numerous rodent species, exist as proliferating trypomastigotes and epimastigotes in the bloodstream, are transmitted through flea feces, and show high similarity in their maxicircle sequences13,27,28. Due to its global distribution and ease of cultivation, T. musculi serves as an excellent model for studying host immunity and trypanosome evolution29.

In this study, we present a near-complete assembly and annotation of the T. musculi kDNA minicircle complement and provide insights into its RNA editing mechanisms derived from advanced long-read DNA sequencing and RNA-seq data. We additionally show that irregularities in its editing process have resulted in the impairment of mitochondrial respiratory Complex I, composed of both kDNA- and nucleus-encoded subunits30. Crucially, our findings support the hypothesis that minicircles and the gRNAs they encode are genomic hotspots, reflecting the dynamic evolution of trypanosomes, with interesting implications for the fields of parasitology and molecular biology.

Results

Structure of minicircles

Previously, we have utilized PacBio and Illumina sequencing data (PRJNA792722) to assemble the T. musculi kDNA maxicircle13. Here, we extended this analysis to kDNA minicircles and identified 86 putative minicircle contigs, each containing one or more CSRs with all three CSB motifs (CSB-1 through CSB-3). A total of 39 complete minicircles, ranging in size from 631 to 2557 nts, predicted from these contigs were experimentally confirmed by PCR using outward facing primers (Supplementary Table 1). While the number of minicircle classes is so far the lowest recorded for a trypanosomatid species, the structure of these minicircles is remarkably complex. The T. musculi minicircles were categorized into five distinct types based on size variation and arrangement of the CSRs (Fig. 1A, B; Supplementary Data 1). We identified seven minicircles with a single CSR, whose sizes ranged between 631 and 642 nts (category IA), and 1463 and 1483 nts (category IB), respectively. Next, we have assembled 25 minicircles with two CSRs, the size of which ranged between 1234 and 1299 nts (category II). Finally, the analyzed kDNA was shown to contain a single 1923 nt-long minicircle with three CSRs (category III) and six minicircles with four CSRs, which varied in size between 2505 and 2557 nts (category IV). Based on the position of CSRs, most categories can be divided into one to four units containing a single CSR, each approximately 630 nts in length, except for category IB, which showed a deviation of around 1480 nts (Fig. 1A, B; Supplementary Fig. 1; Supplementary Data 1). The CSRs of T. musculi are highly conserved across all minicircle categories, extending from 27 nts upstream of CSB-1 (AGGGGCGTT) to 17 nts downstream of CSB-3 (GGGGTTGGTGTA), and are highly similar to T. lewisi (Fig. 1C). In addition, CSB-3 is flanked on both sides with a short 8 nt-long symmetrical repeat, which in all 39 mapped minicircles contains only a very few substitutions. Adjacent to the CSR, bent DNA was identified, characterized by periodic repeats of A/T tracts (Fig. 1A). In a 5% PAGE gel, minicircle fragments containing bent DNA migrated more slowly (62.4 - 80.3% in distance) compared to fragments without bent DNA, while all fragments showed normal migration in a 2% agarose gel (Supplementary Fig. 2). These results are consistent with the previously described characteristics of bent DNA31.

To ascertain whether these minicircle categories are specific to the Partinico II strain or distributed across different T. musculi strains, we extended our analysis to the Lincicome, CDC, and ATCC 30,181 strains, and three clones of T. musculi Partinico II. Using PCR with primers designed against the variable regions of the Partinico II minicircles, we successfully amplified and sequenced all minicircle categories across these strains/clones (Fig. 1D; Supplementary Table 1). Additionally, the presence of category IB minicircles in all four strains of T. musculi was confirmed by Southern blot hybridization (Supplementary Fig. 3), testifying to their widespread distribution and conserved structural pattern within the analyzed species.

Evolutionary scenarios explaining observed minicircle pattern

Building upon the structural pattern identified in T. musculi minicircles, we delved into possible evolutionary scenarios explaining their origin. Our approach involved a comprehensive homology analysis of the conserved and variable regions across all minicircle categories. We constructed a phylogenetic tree using all 84 minicircle CSRs, employing maximum likelihood to elucidate their evolutionary relationships. As a result, a complex evolutionary landscape emerged, with the minicircles diverging into five main branches (Fig. 2A), yet this divergence does not correspond with the five minicircle categories. Specifically, CSRs from categories II and IV were distributed among four branches, without a clear clustering relationship among them. Branch 3 includes only one CSR from IA-03, while other CSRs from categories IA, IB and III were distributed among branches 1, 2, and 5. Further analysis revealed that all T. lewisi minicircle CSRs fall into a subclade of branch 1, mixed with three T. musculi CSRs (IA-04a, IB-03a and II-20a) (Supplementary Fig. 4). The examination of variable regions, which encode gRNA genes, revealed a striking pattern of sequence similarity (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Data 1). The variable regions of two category IA minicircles (IA-01 and IA-02) were nearly 100% identical with category II minicircles (II-23 and II-24). A high similarity (>95%) was also observed between variable regions within category II minicircles (II-21 and II-22), as well as between category II (II-14 and II-17) and category IV minicircles (IV-02 and IV-04), which additionally encode the same gRNAs. Furthermore, while most categories II and IV minicircles harbor variable regions with low levels of similarity (70–80%), one of the three variable regions for the only category III minicircle (III-01) showed about 70% similarity with the corresponding region of category II minicircle (II-01). Category IB minicircles represent a notable exception, with their variable regions exhibiting high divergence.

In addition, we identified a G-rich region within category IB, flanked by two variable regions. Such G-rich regions represent a relatively conserved sequence, devoid of the CSBs or similar sequences, located at a position symmetrical to the CSR (Supplementary Fig. 5). The base composition of G-rich regions (especially when cytosines are concerned) shares similarities with the CSRs, distinguishing it from both the variable and gRNA-bearing regions (Supplementary Fig. 5B). This distinctiveness is indicative of a separated evolutionary path for category IB minicircles. Moreover, we noticed potential gene translocation events from minicircles to maxicircles, as IA-03 is virtually identical (>99%) with the insertion in maxicircle-encoded MURF2, and the same applies to IA-04 and an insertion in the maxicircle-located ND5 gene (Fig. 2C).

Reconstruction of mature edited transcripts

The heterogeneity of minicircle sequences is reflective of the size of the gRNA pool, which is essential for RNA editing of maxicircle transcripts. Using T-Aligner, we reconstructed the edited mRNAs of seven maxicircle-encoded cryptogenes of T. musculi, including four subunits of the respiratory chain complexes (COX3, ATP6, CYTB and COX2), one ribosomal protein (RPS12), and two proteins of unknown function (CR3 and CR4) (Table 1; Supplementary Fig. 6A–F, 7). RNA editing patterns of these T. musculi genes are generally similar to those of T. lewisi, its closest relative among the sequenced species, except for CR3 and CR4. As an example, the COX3 subunit of cytochrome c oxidase undergoes extensive editing and shares common features with T. lewisi COX3 (Fig. 3A, B). This gene exhibited high mapping coverage, particularly in the 3’ region, gradually decreasing towards the flanks of both ends, aligning with typical Illumina sequencing characteristics. In terms of its editing, the highest proportion of edited reads was commonly found at the 3’ ends, progressively decreasing in the 5’ direction. This trend was observed in almost all extensively edited (i.e. pan-edited) transcripts of the T. musculi cryptogenes (Supplementary Fig. 6A–F). The post-transcriptional modification of the pan-edited COX3 transcript was predicted to involve 499 and 48 U insertions and deletions, respectively. After validation by reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) and sequencing, the fully edited COX3 transcript was shown to encode a polypeptide consisting of 288 amino acids, sharing 87% and 98% similarity with its T. brucei and T. lewisi homologs, respectively (Table 1). It is worth noting that we did not find any compelling evidence for the existence of AEP-1, which was proposed to be a protein product of alternatively edited COX3 mRNA in T. brucei32. For T. musculi CR3 and CR4, their DNA sequences show high similarity (87% each) to those in T. lewisi, which enables easy recognition of these genes. However, their RNA transcripts diverged after editing. Specifically, their editing was guided by distinct gRNA sets, while T. lewisi CR3 and CR4 require editing to generate stop-codons (Li et al.10). In T. musculi the corresponding transcripts seem to make use of unedited stop codons located downstream (Fig. S7E). Additionally, a detailed analysis revealed unexpected anomalies in four Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase) subunits - ND3, ND7, ND8, and ND9 (Fig. 3C, D; Supplementary Fig. 8), the transcripts of which require extensive editing in other trypanosome species8,10,33. Using T-Aligner, we were unable to identify any translatable mRNA sequences for these genes, with only minimal canonical editing observed at the 5’ and 3’ ends of ND7 and ND8 mRNAs, respectively, leaving substantial parts of these transcripts either unedited or undergoing non-canonical editing (Fig. 3C, D; Supplementary Fig. 8). The same pattern was also observed in the geographically isolated Lincicome strain and markedly differs from editing of the homologous transcripts in the closely related T. lewisi (Supplementary Fig. 9). Indeed, the observed non-canonical editing events in these transcripts cause disruptions of their open reading frames, precluding their translatability. A notable example of this phenomenon is evident in the 5’ region of ND7, where Us are typically inserted at sites 149 and 150, thus creating a translatable open reading frame. However, in T. musculi, these editing events are absent, resulting in the creation of a premature stop codon (Fig. 3E).

To understand the underlying causes for this anomaly, we aligned the edited mRNA sequences of T. lewisi with the minicircle sequences and small RNA transcriptome data of T. musculi. This alignment led to the identification of 38 putative gRNA sequences, covering the most edited regions of the corresponding four mRNAs (Supplementary Table 2), and indicating functionality of these gRNAs. Out of this set, three selected gRNAs can apparently direct editing of ND7 and ND8 (Supplementary Fig. 8). However, the other 35 putative gRNAs do not specify correct editing in the predicted regions of their target mRNAs. Such a disruption of editing results in the failed matches between the anchor of the downstream gRNAs and the pre-edited mRNA. Indeed, the most or near-most 3’ gRNAs for ND3, ND7 and ND8 mRNAs are likely absent, while mutations prevent functionality of gRNA at the 3’ end of ND9 mRNA (Fig. 3F; Supplementary Fig. 8). At the same time, we identified several instances of non-canonical editing events at the 3’ end of ND3, ND7, ND8 and ND9 transcripts (Fig. 3F; Supplementary Fig. 8), which appear to be guided either by gRNAs that also participate in guiding editing of other transcripts, or by gRNAs not previously identified in other trypanosomes.

Further complexity is provided by the capacity of certain gRNAs to anneal to more than a single transcript. Specifically, three gRNAs appear to be involved in editing of ATP6 mRNA, yet they may also participate in decrypting of the CR3 transcript (Supplementary Fig. 10). Furthermore, another set of three gRNAs contributes to the editing of the ATP6, CR4 and/or COX3 transcripts.

gRNA features

In total, we identified a well-known cis-acting gRNA in the 3’ UTR of COX2 mRNA and 108 putative trans-acting gRNAs based on the sequence alignments between assembled minicircles (and maxicircles) and mRNA transcriptome, an analysis further supported by small-RNA transcriptome (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table 2). These putative gRNAs were linked with 38 minicircles, and two maxicircle-derived insertion fragments. No gRNA could be assigned for minicircle IA-01. Interestingly, we noticed several cases when an identical gRNA is encoded by distinct loci, either by two different minicircles or by a minicircle and a maxicircle. For instance, gATP6 (441-481) was identified as potentially derived from both the IA-03 minicircle and an insertion within the maxicircle-encoded MURF2 gene (Supplementary Fig. 11A). Among the 108 putative gRNAs, 70 are likely functional, while the remaining 38 gRNAs associated with the editing of four NADH dehydrogenase subunits appear to produce non-translatable mRNAs. All predicted gRNAs exhibit good complementarity to their corresponding edited mRNAs, with no clear evidence of gRNA redundancy observed. Individual gRNAs exhibit a significant size variation of complementarity to edited mRNA, ranging in length from 30 to 54 nts, with the majority falling between 42 and 52 nts (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Data 1).

Almost all identified gRNAs are oriented in the same direction as the CSBs, as predicted for the closely related T. lewisi (Supplementary Table 2). Specifically, in all minicircle categories the variable regions encode either zero, one or two gRNAs (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table 2). The position of gRNAs ranges from 10 to 350 nts downstream from the CSR, while in a handful of category IB minicircles, they are located further downstream, 200 to 450 nt distant from the G-rich region (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Data 1). II-18 represents a particularly multipurpose category II minicircle, encoding four gRNAs, two of which guide editing of COX3 mRNA, while the other two guide editing of CR3 and CYTB transcripts. These gRNA genes differ in their directionality, as the gRNA, gCOX3(167-211), cognate to COX3 mRNA exhibits reverse orientation compared to the other three genes.

Marked correlations were observed between minicircle small-RNA-seq reads and minicircle depth from PacBio reads (Pearson’s r = 0.47, Spearman’s r = 0.58), as well as between putative gRNA reads and minicircle depth (Pearson’s r = 0.37, Spearman’s r = 0.21). These correlation coefficients indicated that the gRNA abundance only weakly correlates with the minicircle copy number (Supplementary Fig. 12A, B; Supplementary Data 1). Similar counts were noted for the gRNA reads between the set of seven transcripts with standard editing and the four abnormally edited ND mRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 12C; Supplementary Data 1), indicating that gRNA expression is not coupled with the editing status of target mRNA transcripts. Furthermore, the expression of gRNAs does not appear to be linked to the specific minicircle category encoding them, nor their position within the variable region (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Data 1), indicating either a complex regulatory mechanism for gRNA transcription, or a lack of it.

Intriguingly, our study revealed notable shared distribution of gRNAs between the minicircles of T. musculi and T. lewisi, with 79 gRNAs exhibiting very similar distribution patterns in both species (Fig. 4D; Supplementary Table 3). Hence, since the divergence of both trypanosome species the minicircle population remained remarkably stable, although occasional splitting and fusion events have occurred. For example, six gRNAs are located on IV-01 in T. musculi, while in T. lewisi three of them are found on minicircles TLminic25 and TLminic46 each. Similarly, the four CSRs present in a single T. musculi minicircle has been split equally into two minicircles in T. lewisi, although when category IB minicircles are concerned, they carry homologous gRNAs in both species. Moreover, the fragment inserted into ND5 gene in T. musculi shows collinearity with TLminic21 from T. lewisi, which is yet another minicircle featuring two conserved regions. Specifically, 10 minicircles that encode gRNAs cognate for Complex I subunits and/or CR transcripts are present in T. lewisi but absent in T. musculi (Supplementary Table 4). These discrepancies highlight the unusual editing events associated with the kDNA-encoded subunits of Complex I in T. musculi.

Small complex I

Despite mutations in the mitochondrially encoded subunits of T. musculi, its nuclear-encoded core and classical accessory Complex I subunits responsible for NADH oxidation and electron transfer remain intact and are highly conserved when compared to those of T. brucei (Supplementary Table 5). To investigate the effects of mis-editing of Complex I subunits on its structural assembly, we analyzed the activities of respiratory complexes in T. musculi, using clear native gel electrophoresis and in-gel staining. We compared these complexes with those in T. brucei, in which their activities are well characterized34,35. In T. brucei, Complexes I, II, IV, and V are active in the procyclic stage, displaying expected activity (Fig. 5A–C; Supplementary Fig. 13). In T. musculi, complexes II, IV, and V are also clearly active and exhibit similar sizes to those in T. brucei, approximately 720, 760, and 900 kDa, respectively. However, NBT staining in T. musculi visualizes a complex of roughly 900 kDa, which is much smaller than the T. brucei Complex I migrating at approximately 1300 kDa (Fig. 5B). A possible explanation for this result is that incomplete editing of subunits in T. musculi may be responsible for improper or partial assembly of Complex I, which still retains the ability to oxidize NADH in vitro. Discussion

The kDNA and kinetoplast RNA of T. brucei are amongst the best studied organellar genomes and transcriptomes8,37, and the number of trypanosomatids in which these studies are carried out is also steadily increasing2,10,11,21,22. As a result, a rather comprehensive picture is emerging as to the variability in kDNA structure and RNA editing patterns. In this study, we show that the kDNA minicircle complement of T. musculi is probably the most heterogeneous in motif structure identified to date. Moreover, it represents just a second near-complete set of gRNAs, edited mRNAs and minicircles described for a Stercorarian trypanosome.

We have identified in T. musculi 108 gRNAs on 38 minicircles grouped into five structurally different minicircle categories. Comparable structural diversity, though less complex, has been observed in two other Stercorarian species, Trypanosoma rangeli and T. copemani38,39. Moreover, recent deep sequencing of T. lewisi and T. cruzi revealed more complex minicircle structures than originally described40,41. Among these Stercorarian trypanosomes, the minicircle CSR copy number is a key distinguishing feature. T. lewisi has minicircles with either one or two CSRs (mostly two)10, a majority of T. cruzi minicircles contain four CSRs11, T. rangeli and T. copemani have minicircles with two or four CSRs38,39, and T. musculi falls in-between with one to four CSRs, although most of its minicircles carry just two. This variation likely reflects occasional minicircle fusion events. The complex structure of minicircles in this group of trypanosomes may be derived from the common Stercorarian ancestor, as a consequence of multiple fusion and fission events in the minicircle population or may somehow be associated with the characteristic life cycle of these parasites. In contrast, Salivarian trypanosomes, such as T. vivax and T. brucei, as well as Leishmania species retain kDNA networks with a single-CSR minicircle category8,14,42,43.

The transcriptional direction of minicircle-encoded gRNAs varies significantly among trypanosomatid species. In the genus Leishmania, despite limited variations in minicircle size, each minicircle harbors only one CSR, with gRNA transcription proceeding in the direction opposite to that of the CSBs44. In contrast, all gRNAs identified in the T. lewisi minicircles align with the direction of the CSBs10, a trait shared with gRNAs from T. brucei8,37. However, an exception is known from T. musculi, as its gCOX3 (167-211) gRNA encoded by the minicircle II-18 exhibits a reverse orientation. Such opposite orientation also occurs in the case of five overlapping gRNAs responsible for the editing of one COX3 region in T. brucei37, which represents a pattern reminiscent of gRNAs with opposing orientations in Bodo saltans45. This suggests a potential evolutionary compatibility between the kDNA arrangement of obligatory parasitic trypanosomes and the free-living B. saltans, supporting the hypothesis that the pro-kDNA structure found in the latter flagellate may represent an ancestral form of the network-like kDNA structure embraced by all known trypanosomatids9,46.

The comparative analysis of linked gRNAs, in terms of their target transcripts, between T. musculi and T. lewisi (Fig. 4D), allows us to infer possible evolutionary connections among minicircle categories. A notable example is IV-01 of T. musculi, which shows a joint and ordered set of gRNAs when compared to the T. lewisi minicircles TLminic25 and TLminic46, and IV-02 of the former species equally corresponds to TLminic35 and TLminic48 of the latter flagellate. Considering the single CSR found in minicircles of B. saltans and African trypanosomes10, this pattern suggests the ancestral trypanosome’s minicircles may have possessed only one CSR. Therefore, minicircles with more than one CSR may be products of fusion events, exemplified by category II arising from the fusion of two ancient category IA minicircles. Further support for this fusion hypothesis, which is opposed to the “minicircle splitting” scenario, can be derived from the notable homology between a quarter of IV-02 and half of II-17 minicircles.

Moreover, the striking sequence similarity between IA-03 and IA-04 and parts of ND5 and MURF2, respectively, described here for T. musculi, strongly suggests that a fusion may also occur between minicircles and maxicircles, representing an unusual form of gene translocation13. Such fusions appear to be relatively recent evolutionary events, as gRNAs with identical or nearly identical flanking regions were identified. These findings suggest that the disturbed regions are likely non-coding or non-essential. However, for T. musculi category IB and their homologs in T. lewisi category II, no putative indicators of fusion were identified, which may reflect the lack of such events in these minicircles, or their evolution by other mechanisms. It is worth noting that this minicircle category contains a distinct type of G-rich region.

Despite a close relationship between the two compared trypanosomes, there is an intriguing lack of gRNAs specific for the 3’ end editing of the Complex I subunits, and the CR3 and CR4 transcripts in T. musculi, when compared to T. lewisi. The absence of essential gRNAs correlates with anomalies in RNA editing of the Complex I subunits mRNAs, although several gRNAs cognate to the corresponding 5’ regions are still present. This can be attributed to mutations in or losses of certain minicircles, suggesting that there is neither an integrity ‘checkpoint’ for the gRNA sets, nor an occurrence of crosstalk between gRNA transcription and the sophisticated RNA editing machinery. The consequence of such editing abnormalities may be a partial assembly of Complex I, since it migrates slower than expected if it was to contain a complete repertoire of its subunits. Similar incomplete Complex I formations have been observed in mammalian cells or other eukaryotes upon inhibition of mitochondrial translation or knockout of nuclear genes encoding membrane arm subunits47,48. In mammalian cells, mitochondrial ND1-ND6 localize to the membrane arm and are essential for proton pumping activity49, while the above mutants lack the membrane arm and are therefore incapable of proton translocation or electron transfer to the membrane-bound ubiquinone pool, but can still assemble subcomplexes containing flavin mononucleotide (FMN). Nevertheless, multiple studies have shown that subcomplexes containing FMN retain the ability to oxidize NADH and transfer electrons to soluble artificial electron acceptors48,50,51. In other words, subcomplexes containing FMN may show Complex I in-gel activity by NBT staining. Therefore, we propose that abnormal NDs’ editing in T. musculi leads to the formation of an incomplete subcomplex of Complex I, which retains NADH-oxidizing activity in vitro. However, these subcomplexes likely have impaired electron transfer and proton pumping and lack functional activity in vivo.

Indeed, our results are compatible with the dispensability of this complex for T. musculi due to the absence of some gRNAs required for editing of its maxicircle-encoded ND3, ND7, ND8, and ND9 subunits. This is not unprecedented, since a similar situation has been described in the UC strain of Leishmania tarentolae52, Blastocrithidia nonstop22, and Vickermania ingenoplastis21. The lack of Complex I activity may be compensated for by alternative NADH dehydrogenase (NDH2)53, which is present in the genome of T. musculi (GenBank No.: JAWVMP010000136.1), while the apparently active respiratory complexes III and IV are sufficient for proton pumping30,35.

Although T. musculi fails to properly edit mRNAs of all four maxicircle-encoded ND subunits cryptogenes, we have noticed that these transcripts are subject to numerous non-canonical editing events. We speculate that they are caused by misaligned gRNAs that would typically target other transcripts. Such mis-editing is likely attributable to a short match between the pre-edited NDs mRNA and the 5’ anchor of these gRNAs. Indeed, mis-editing by misguiding has been originally described in L. tarentolae54, and later documented also in T. brucei55, Leptomonas pyrrhocoris56,57, and B. nonstop22. Such pairing of gRNAs with the off-target RNAs would eventually result in changed mRNAs and, consequently, peptides, which may have a potential to acquire new functions.

In most kinetoplastid species, such as T. brucei8, T. cruzi58, and L. tarentolae59, gRNA redundancy is a widespread and well-documented feature, whereby the majority of editing sites can be targeted by multiple distinct gRNAs. This redundancy is thought to contribute to the robustness of the RNA editing system, by providing tolerance against the stochastic loss of individual minicircle classes during segregation60. In contrast, most gRNAs in T. musculi appear to be indispensable, lacking functionally equivalent counterparts, which may be a consequence of massive gRNAs loss. Interestingly, in T. lewisi, its closest relative, this phenomenon was not observed10. Moreover, substantial differences in the complexity of minicircles, their relative abundance and gRNA complement have recently been documented even among individual strains of T. brucei, underlining extremely dynamic nature and variation of all these features61.

Moreover, in several cases, we identified gRNAs that appear to match multiple distinct edited mRNA targets (Fig. S10), a rare phenomenon, although it has already been reported from Trypanoplasma borreli and L. pyrrhocoris56,62. This observation points to a potential functional repurposing of gRNAs, possibly as an adaptive strategy to compensate for the reduced minicircle repertoire. Together, these findings suggest that T. musculi may represent an extreme case of editing system simplification, characterized by a trade-off between kinetoplast genome compactness and editing fidelity. This system likely operates near the minimal threshold required for mitochondrial transcript maturation, providing a unique model to explore the evolutionary limits of RNA editing efficiency in kinetoplastids.

It has been proposed that extensive editing (pan-editing) evolved as a mechanism that makes both cryptogene-encoding maxicircles and gRNAs-encoding minicircles indispensable for gene expression. This would prevent the loss of low-copy number minicircles bearing gRNAs non-essential during part of the parasite’s life cycle63. While this mechanism might efficiently protect gRNAs from being lost in phases of the life cycle when targets of their editing are dispensable, they will still be lost when their function is non-essential over a long term. The loss of some but not all gRNAs required for editing of the ND transcripts is likely due to the linked inheritance with gRNAs for RPS12, COX3, and ATP6 that remain indispensable (Supplementary Table 4).

Based on these findings, we propose an advanced scenario for the maintenance of gRNAs and minicircle population heterogeneity in the Stercorarian trypanosome via minicircle fusion (Fig. 6). Most minicircles of Leishmania and the Salivarian trypanosomes have a low coding capacity, with just one or two gRNAs, providing a limited inheritance shield via stage-essential gRNAs. By contrast, the Stercorarian trypanosomes, represented here by T. musculi, evolved minicircles encoding up to six gRNAs each, which likely provide a larger inheritance shield, reflected by high retainment of Complex I gRNAs. In contrast, a recent study on B. nonstop, in which most minicircles carry only a single gRNA, revealed limited retention of Complex I gRNAs and a compressed gRNA repertoire22. Combined, these observations prompt us to postulate that minicircle fusion protects some trypanosomatids from the loss of essential gRNAs. This may be particularly important for species with a complex life cycle and stage-specific essentiality of editing products. Since gRNA distribution is random, some minicircles may exclusively possess non-essential gRNAs, which might then be lost in the course of evolution, similar to what we observe in T. musculi. In some special circumstances, trypanosomes can remain locked in the bloodstream stage, thus becoming resistant to the loss of most kDNA-encoded proteins. It is well documented that a long-term dispensability of these proteins leads to a partial and eventually complete loss of kDNA, as is the case of Trypanosoma brucei f. equiperdum and T. brucei f. evansi, respectively43,64–66. In any case, it is primarily a comparative analysis of kDNA and kinetoplast RNA structure and composition across trypanosome species that provides increasing insight into their intricate evolution.

In conclusion, we provide a detailed description of the near-complete kDNA minicircle genome and transcriptome of T. musculi, enhancing our understanding of minicircle composition, gRNA features, and the evolutionary dynamics of minicircles and gRNAs. Additionally, we have demonstrated that editing abnormalities in four Complex I subunits, which are associated with mutations or losses of minicircles, result in a small respiratory chain complex. Our data further underscores the critical role of kDNA heterogeneity in the cellular function and evolutionary adaptations of trypanosomes.

Materials and methods

Trypanosome cell culture and nucleic acid purification

The Partinico II strain of T. musculi, originally isolated in Sicily in 196267, was obtained from Prof. Philippe Vincendeau (Université de Bordeaux, France). Two additional strains, Lincicome and CDC, along with a clone of the Partinico II strain (ATCC 30,181) were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cultivation was conducted in RPMI-1640 medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment, using a feeder layer of rat embryonic fibroblasts68. For the bloodstream stage of T. brucei AnTat 1.1 strain, cultivation was performed in HMI-9 medium with 10% FBS at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment. The procyclic stages of T. brucei AnTat 1.1 strain and TREU 927 strain were cultured in SDM79 medium with 10% FBS at 27 °C. To collect the cell pellet, trypanosomes were grown to a density of 3 × 106 cells/ml, harvested and centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min. Total DNA was obtained from 1 × 108 cells using phenol-chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation. Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent followed by isopropanol precipitation and ethanol washes.

Kinetoplast DNA was isolated via sucrose gradient ultracentrifugation as described previously69. Approximately 10¹⁰ cells were resuspended in 5 ml lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 5 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) containing 200 μl of 10% SDS and 25 μl of proteinase K (20 mg/ml), followed by lysis at 37 °C for 2 h. The lysate was layered onto 6 ml of 20% sucrose in TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0), then centrifuged at 19,000 rpm for 1 h at 4 °C using a Beckman SW41Ti rotor. The bottom 0.5 ml of the gradient was collected and dialyzed twice against 1 L of TE buffer to remove residual sucrose. Subsequently, n-butanol extraction and phase reduction were performed. The kDNA was then precipitated with isopropanol, pelleted at 12,000 × g for 20 min, washed twice with 75% ethanol, air-dried, and finally resuspended in 30 μl of TE buffer.

RNA sequencing and preprocessing

Three of T. musculi Partinico II total RNAs and one Lincicome strain total RNA were sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq platforms with a 150 nucleotides (nt) paired-end protocol (Annoroad Gene Technology, China). For downstream analysis, the obtained RNA reads underwent a rigorous quality check: Illumina adapters were removed, and low-quality reads were trimmed using fastp version 0.23.4, with the parameters “-q 30 -5 -l 100”70. Subsequently, all processed RNA reads of T. musculi Partinico II strain were combined and pooled into a single file. This file was then utilized for the assembly of kDNA maxicircle transcripts, employing the T-Aligner pipelines62,71.

For sequencing of small RNA, total RNA was extracted from two biological replicates of the T. musculi Partinico II strain. RNA fragments ranging from 40 to 80 nts in length were isolated using a 15% denaturing polyacrylamide gel following electrophoresis. The fragments were eluted, ethanol-precipitated, and treated to remove 5’ phosphate groups, followed by phosphorylation using polynucleotide kinase (M0201S, New England Biolabs, USA). Adapters were then ligated to both the 5’ and 3’ ends of the RNA fragments. The adapter-ligated RNAs were reverse transcribed and amplified by PCR, and the resulting cDNA products, approximately 160-200 nts in length, were purified by gel extraction (DC301, Vazyme International LLC, China). After confirming the quality of the small RNA library, sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform using a 100 nt paired-end strategy.

Minicircle assembly and validation by PCR amplification

PacBio reads of total DNA and Illumina reads of kDNA (BioProject no. PRJNA792722) from the T. musculi Partinico II strain, obtained in previous research13, were de novo assembled using SPAdes 3.15.5, IDBA 1.1.3 and Canu 2.072–74, with maximum and minimal sequence lengths set to 10,000 nts and 400 nts, respectively. The sequences were screened post-assembly for the presence of minicircle conserved region, identified by the 12-mer minicircle CSB-3 (GGGGTTGGTGTA, up to one nucleotide mismatch). To further validate these minicircle-derived sequences, PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing were employed. Primers for this process were manually designed and verified using Primer Premier 5 (Premier Biosoft Interpairs, Canada). All primers are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The PCR reaction was optimized using a high-fidelity DNA polymerase (p505, Vazyme International LLC, China), including an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C, 15 s at 60 °C, and 30 sec at 72 °C, concluding with a final extension of 10 min at 72 °C. The PCR products were then analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis and sequenced using Sanger technology.

For analyzing T. musculi minicircle conserved motifs, multiple sequence alignments were performed with MEGA7 and SnapGene tools75. Conserved sequence regions (CSRs) were analyzed using WebLogo76, and phylogenetic relationships were inferred using RAxML 8.2.12 with 1000 bootstrap replicates77. Minicircle homology was identified via BLAST and visualized using Circos v0.6978, where regions over 400 nts with significant sequence identity were represented as colored ribbons indicating the level of sequence identity.

Putative bent DNA verification

Four minicircle fragments (IB-03, II-04, III-01, and IV-02), representing different minicircle categories, were amplified using specific primers listed in Supplementary Table 1. Following PCR amplification, the products were digested with restriction enzymes specific to each fragment (MboI for IB-03 and II-04; AccI for III-01-1; RsaI for III-01-2; ClaI for IV-02-1; XhoI for IV-02-2) to produce fragments containing either zero or one putative bent DNA. The resulting PCR fragments were analysed using 2% agarose gel and 5% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). Band sizes calculated using Quantity one software version 4.6.2 (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA) were compared to the actual lengths of fragments.

Southern blot analysis

One µg of kDNA digested with restriction endonucleases AccI or RsaI was loaded onto a 1% agarose gel for electrophoresis and subsequently transferred to a membrane overnight via capillary transfer. Post UV cross-linking, the membrane was pre-hybridized at 42 °C for 30 min, followed by overnight hybridization with DIG-labeled probes (DIG-High Prime DNA kit, Roche, USA) at 42 °C with slow agitation. The membrane underwent two washes in 2 × SSC/0.1% SDS at room temperature for 5 min and two more in 0.5 × SSC/0.1% SDS at 65 °C for 15 min. After blocking, it was incubated with Anti-Dig-AP at 37 °C for 30 min, and the DIG signal was detected using CSPD in an alkaline environment.

Editing of maxicircle transcriptome

Edited maxicircle mRNAs of T. musculi Partinico II strain were reconstructed by aligning the trimmed and merged RNA reads to the cryptogene sequences, including 60 nt-long of adjacent sequences, using the T-Aligner tool62,71. For the ATPase subunit 6 (ATP6) and C-rich region 4 (CR4) cryptogenes, T-Aligner parameters were set to “--mf 0.75 --mr 16 --sl 12 --ss10 --mm 1 --xi 16 --j 50000”. For other cryptogenes, the parameters were “--sl 24 --ss12”. Following assembly with T-Aligner, the open reading frames of all mRNAs were accurately adjusted from the start to stop codons. The assembled sequences were aligned to reference proteins from T. brucei, T. cruzi, and T. lewisi using BLAST, to identify the most likely canonically edited transcripts. Moreover, the edited mRNA sequences of ATP6, COX2, COX3, and RPS12 were further confirmed by PCR amplification from cDNA of the T. musculi Partinico II strain, with primers designed using the most probable edited transcript sequences. All primers are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Prediction and identification of gRNA genes

The minicircle sequences were aligned to the best candidate mRNAs for the canonically edited T. musculi and T. lewisi using a Python script (https://github.com/JuFengWang/scripts/ grnafinder.py)79. This alignment utilized the ‘comp’ template matrix, accommodating both the Watson-Crick and non-canonical G-U base pairs to predict gRNA candidates, which were subsequently filtered by four criteria reported previously37. To further support the gRNA annotation and evaluate transcriptional activity, two small-RNA transcriptome datasets from T. musculi Partinico II strain were combined and aligned to predicted gRNA loci using HISAT2 v2.2.1, followed by transcript quantification with StringTie v2.2.180,81. Only those gRNA candidates with clear expression evidence in the small RNA transcriptomes, i.e. sequences showing alignment that included the predicted guide region and poly-T tail, were retained and considered as putative gRNAs.

To assess gRNA homology between T. musculi and T. lewisi, we first aligned their canonically edited mRNAs to define homologous editing regions. gRNAs were then compared based on their mapped coverage of these conserved regions. Putative homologous gRNAs were identified if they directed editing over nearly identical mRNA regions, allowing maximal 3-nt offset. In cases where multiple gRNAs targeted the same region, the gRNAs with the highest inter-species sequence similarity were aligned.

Isolation of mitochondrial vesicles

Mitochondrial vesicles were isolated from T. brucei and T. musculi cells using the hypotonic lysis method described previously82. Approximately 1 × 108 cells were harvested and resuspended in 1.5 ml of NET buffer (0.15 M NaCl; 0.1 M EDTA; 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0). This suspension was then centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting pellet was resuspended in 1.5 ml of DTE buffer (1 mM Tris-HCl; 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.9). The cells were lysed by passing the suspension through a 25 G needle. Subsequently, 180 µl of 60% sucrose was added to the lysate, followed by centrifugation under the same conditions. The obtained pellet was washed twice with 1 ml of STE buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl; 2 mM EDTA; 0.25 mM sucrose, pH 7.9). For solubilization, n-dodecyl-β-d-maltoside (DDM) was added to achieve a DDM/protein ratio of 2 (g/g), and the mixture was incubated on ice for 60 min. Finally, the vesicles were pelleted by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove any insoluble material. The resulting supernatant of mitochondrial lysate was stored at –80 °C for subsequent biochemical analysis of the mitochondrial respiratory chain.

High-resolution clear native electrophoresis (CNE) and in-gel catalytic activity assays

Mitochondrial lysate (50 μg) was prepared with 10% loading dye (0.1% Ponceau-S; 50% glycerol) and applied to a 3–12% clear native gel. The electrophoresis used a cathode buffer (50 mM tricine; 7.5 mM imidazole, pH 7.0; 0.05% deoxycholate; 0.1% DDM), and an anode buffer (25 mM imidazole, pH 7.0)83. The process was conducted for 135 min at 100 V and 4 °C. After electrophoresis, the native gel was subjected to in-gel activity staining for respiratory complexes. Each complex was detected using substrate- and cofactor-specific incubation solutions, which resulted in colorimetric precipitates at the location of active enzyme complexes. For Complex I, the gel was incubated in 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 0.1 mg/ml NADH, and 2.5 mg/ml nitrotetrazolium blue chloride (NBT) for 5 min with slow agitation. Complex I catalyses the oxidation of NADH, which transfers electrons to NBT, reducing it to a blue formazan precipitate. Complex II staining involved overnight incubation in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), 20 mM sodium succinate, 0.2 mM phenazine methasulfate (PMS), and 2.5 mg/ml NBT with slow agitation. Electrons flow from succinate to NBT via PMS, generating a blue formazan precipitate. Complex IV staining was performed overnight with slow agitation in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), 50 μM cytochrome c, and 1 mg/ml 3,3’-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB). Complex IV catalyses the oxidation of cytochrome c, producing hydrogen peroxide which polymerizes DAB into a brown insoluble product, marking enzyme activity. For visualizing Complex V, the gel was incubated overnight in 35 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 270 mM glycine, 19 mM MgSO4, 0.3% [w/v] Pb(NO3)2, and 11 mM ATP, again with slow agitation34. Hydrolysis of ATP by active ATP synthase releases inorganic phosphate, which reacts with Pb2+ to form a white lead phosphate precipitate at the position of the complex. Band sizes were calculated by GelAnalyzer 23.1.1 (http://gelanalyzer.com).

Statistics and reproducibility

RNA-seq and small RNA-seq of the T. musculi bloodstream stage were prepared from three and two independent biological replicates, respectively. Pearson’s r, Spearman’s r, and unpaired two-sided ttests were conducted using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, USA). Ttest results are indicated in the figures as follows: ns: p > 0.05, p ≤ 0.05 (*), p ≤ 0.01 (**), p ≤ 0.001 (***), p ≤ 0.0001 (****). Error bars represent the mean ± standard deviation (SD).
