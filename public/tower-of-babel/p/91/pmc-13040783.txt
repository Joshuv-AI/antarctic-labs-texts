Prevalence of hypertension and its associated factors among public school teachers in Obuasi East Municipality, Ghana
Authors: Maxwell Afranie Appiah, Enyonam Ama Kwawukume, Francis Ansong, Peter Solaga, Samuel Danquah, Joshua Appiah Arthur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension is a major global public health concern, with its burden steadily increasing in developing countries, including Ghana. School teachers are at increased risk due to occupational stress, sedentary behaviour, and lifestyle factors, yet their hypertension status remains understudied. This study investigated the prevalence of hypertension and associated factors among public school teachers in Obuasi East Municipality, Ghana.MethodsThis study employed a cross-sectional design involving 419 teachers from 47 public schools in the municipality, recruited between January 27, 2025, and March 14, 2025. Data from participants were collected using a structured questionnaire adapted from the WHO STEPwise approach to chronic disease risk factor surveillance. Data analysis was performed using Stata version 15.0. Descriptive analysis was conducted to summarise the data in frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with hypertension at a 5% significance level.ResultsThe prevalence of stage 1 and 2 hypertension was 33.89% and 19.81%, respectively. Approximately 24.82% of participants were unaware that they were hypertensive before the study. Older age ≥ 50 years (reference group: < 50 years; aOR = 3.37, 95% CI: 1.17–9.72), alcohol consumption more than once a week (aOR = 9.55, 95% CI: 1.2–76.1), being diabetic (aOR = 9.15, 95% CI: 1.0–80.8), and overweight or obese (aOR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.09–2.84) were significantly associated with hypertension among public school teachers.ConclusionThe prevalence of hypertension among public school teachers in Obuasi East Municipality was high and closer to the national prevalence, with many undiagnosed cases. School-based interventions, including health education, lifestyle modification, and regular screenings targeting modifiable risk factors, specifically alcohol and obesity, are urgently needed. Integrating these initiatives into occupational health policies can foster a supportive environment where teachers prioritise their health and well-being. Introduction

Hypertension is a significant global health concern and a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Globally, hypertension has been steadily increasing, with an estimated 1.28 billion adults aged 30–79 years affected [1]. About 78% of adults with hypertension live in low- and middle-income countries [2]. In Africa, the burden of hypertension is particularly high, with a prevalence of 27% [1]. The situation in Ghana mirrors this trend, with a pooled prevalence of over 30% among the population [3, 4]. Additionally, some studies in the country have reported the prevalence of hypertension ranging from 19% to 48% across rural and urban communities [5–7], making it the most diagnosed outpatient condition.

Hypertension is the main determinant of medical emergencies such as heart disease and kidney failure [8, 9], as well as the leading cause of hospital admissions and deaths in Ghana [10]. Alarmingly, nearly two-thirds of adults in Ghana with hypertension are unaware of their condition, preventing them from taking the necessary steps to manage it [5]. Several factors contribute to the development of hypertension, including eating unhealthy diets (particularly those high in saturated fats and salt and low in fruits and vegetables), excessive smoking and alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, obesity, and stress. Additionally, individuals with a family history of hypertension, as well as those with co-existing diseases like diabetes or kidney disease, are at higher risk [1]. Undiagnosed hypertension can be very problematic since it often presents no symptoms. Thus, regular blood pressure check-ups are essential for diagnosing hypertension, allowing individuals to know their status, receive proper management, and make necessary lifestyle adjustments [11].

Several studies in Ghana have reported on the prevalence of hypertension among various population groups. However, studies focusing specifically on public school teachers in the country are rare, with only one study carried out among female teachers of childbearing age [12]. It is crucial to note that school teachers, as a distinct occupational group, are at heightened risk of developing hypertension due to several factors, including the demands of teaching, the stress associated with managing students, sedentary behaviour, and limited health-seeking behaviours [13]. This study, therefore, assessed the prevalence of hypertension and its associated factors among public school teachers in Obuasi East Municipality, Ghana. Understanding the burden of hypertension and its determinants in this population is essential for developing targeted interventions and policies to improve health outcomes among educators in Ghana.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a school-based cross-sectional study conducted in public schools in Obuasi East Municipality. The municipality is located in the southern part of the Ashanti Region of Ghana. It has a total population of 98,346 and a total of 187 schools (70 Preschools, 76 Primary schools, 37 Junior High Schools and 4 Senior High Schools). Seventy-one (71) schools (20 Preschools, 30 Primary schools, 20 Junior High Schools, and 1 Senior Secondary School) are publicly owned with a total teacher population of 954. The teacher-pupil ratio is approximately 1:14, 1:29, 1:15, and 1:21 for Preschool, Primary school, Junior High School and Senior High School, respectively [14].

Study participants

The study participants were public school teachers within the Obuasi East Municipality. However, the study excluded teachers (1) who were absent on the day of data collection; (2) who were severely ill; (3) who were pregnant; and (4) who did not provide informed consent.

Sampling

A sample size of 419 teachers was estimated using a single population proportion formula, n = z²p(1–p)/d², by considering a 30.3% pooled prevalence of hypertension in Ghana [3], 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, a design effect of 1.2, and a 10% non-response rate. A multistage sampling method was used to select the 419 public school teachers in the study setting. In the first stage, a complete list of all 71 public schools was obtained from the Municipal Education Directorate, from which 47 schools were selected using a simple random sampling without replacement. Each school received a unique identification number, and a computer-generated random list was used to ensure unbiased selection. The number of schools selected comprised 26 primary schools, 18 junior high schools, 3 preschools, and 1 senior high school. In the second stage, each of the 47 schools was treated as a stratum, from which the number of teachers to include in the study was estimated using proportional allocation (where the stratum sample size is proportional to the stratum population size). This estimation was calculated by dividing the total number of teachers in each school by the total number of teachers in the 47 schools, then multiplying by the sample size [15]. Within each stratum, systematic random sampling was used to select teachers. A complete staff list was obtained from each school, and the names of teachers were listed alphabetically. The sampling interval (k) was calculated by dividing the total number of teachers in each school by the number required from that school (i.e., the stratum sample size). A random starting point was selected between 1 and k, and every kth teacher on the list was selected until the required number was reached. All teachers selected at random participated in the study.

Data collection procedure

A screening and survey plan was developed in collaboration with the Municipal Education Directorate to inform each school of its scheduled date. Teaching activities were suspended on the screening day to ensure full participation. Data from participants were collected using a structured questionnaire adapted from the WHO STEPwise approach to chronic disease risk factor surveillance [16]. The questionnaire was written in English language. It included sociodemographic and behavioural characteristics (cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, diet, and physical exercise), a history of raised blood pressure, and anthropometric and blood pressure measurements. Eight trained health professionals conducted the interviews and physical assessments. The questionnaire was piloted to test its validity and reliability. This was done to ensure that the participant understood all the questions clearly, and where necessary, modifications were made accordingly. The pilot study revealed a Cronbach’s alpha (α) value of 0.74, suggesting an acceptable internal consistency of the various measures and their questionnaire items.

Blood pressure measurements were taken on two separate occasions using a digital blood pressure monitor (GreenLife Canada GLC-BIG-M6 Automatic Upper Arm Blood Pressure Monitor). Systolic and Diastolic Blood Pressure measures were taken 15 min apart between each reading. The average of the two measures was taken. The participant’s left arm was used for this measurement. Anthropometric measurements of weight and height were taken using a digital weighing scale (UNICEF seca) and stadiometer tools, respectively, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated. For physical exercise, the study considered any work involving vigorous-intensity activity that causes a large increase in breathing or heart rate (sports, fitness, carrying or lifting heavy loads, running, etc.) undertaken at least 10 min continuously, and the number of days in a week during which such activities were carried out. Blood glucose level was measured using a blood glucose meter (On Call® Plus). All measurement tools were assessed, with model numbers recorded and proper calibration ensured. The data collection was carried out from January 27, 2025, to March 14, 2025.

Data analysis

Data were computed using Microsoft Excel and then imported into Stata version 15.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA) for analysis (Additional file 1). The data were cleaned before statistical analysis was performed; however, there were no missing data requiring exclusion or imputation. Descriptive analysis was conducted to summarise the data in frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation. The prevalence of stage 1 and stage 2 hypertension was described using proportions. Hypertension was defined based on the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology (ACC/AHA) guideline, with a lower threshold of ≥ 130/80 mmHg for stage 1 and ≥ 140/90 mmHg for stage 2. This guideline cutoff was adopted to ensure early detection, prevention, and management of hypertension among participants, which was a key rationale of the study [17]. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify the factors influencing hypertension. Body Mass Index was recategorised into two groups: normal (< 25.0 kg/m²) and overweight/obese (≥ 25.0 kg/m²), and blood glucose level was recategorised into normal/prediabetes and diabetes before being included in the regression analysis. The outcome variable, hypertension, was transformed into a binary dummy variable (yes = 1, no = 0). Hypertensive participants were classified as “yes” and non-hypertensive as “no”. The logistic regression analysis was applied to the “yes” responses for hypertension. The variables with a p-value ≤ 0.05 in the univariate logistic regression analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression analysis. The strength of the association was assessed using the Odds Ratio (OR). Statistical significance was set at p-value ≤ 0.05 (95% Confidence Interval).

Results

Characteristics of participants

The age of the participants ranged from 20 to 60 years, with a mean (standard deviation) age of 37 (± 8.02) years. The majority (50.36%) were within the age group of 30 to 39 years. Most (65.87%) were married and identified as Christian (89.98%). The majority (63.96%) were women. Almost all of them had attended tertiary education. Only a small number reported smoking cigarettes (1.67%) and consuming alcohol (7.88%) in the month before the study. Additionally, 9.09% of those who drank alcohol did so more than twice a week. In a typical week, 16.23% and 5.49% of participants did not consume fruit and green leafy vegetables, respectively, and 24.11% did not engage in vigorous-intensity activity of at least ten minutes continuously. Furthermore, the majority (98.33%) spent eight hours or more at work. A notable proportion of participants were overweight (34.61%) and obese (29.83%), with most of them being women. Moreover, 1.43% of the participants were diabetic (see Table 1).

Prevalence of hypertension

Of the 419 participants, 398 (94.99%) had ever been screened for high blood pressure by a doctor. Among them, only 38 (9.07%) were previously diagnosed with hypertension. Approximately 39.47% (15/38) of those previously diagnosed were not taking antihypertensive drugs, and 15.79% (6/38) relied on traditional remedies. The overall prevalence of hypertension (including both previously and newly diagnosed cases) was 33.89% (39.07% and 30.97% among male and female teachers, respectively) (Table 2). Of all identified hypertensive cases (n = 142), 73.24% (104/142) were unaware of their condition and therefore newly diagnosed. This group accounted for 24.82% of all participants (104/419).

Generally, the majority of hypertensive participants, 74 (52.11%), were aged 30 to 39 years (Fig. 1). Divorced or widowed individuals had a higher hypertension rate (62.50%) compared to those who were single or married. There was no significant variation in the prevalence of hypertension based on religious affiliation. Regarding behavioural characteristics, the rate of hypertension was higher among those who consumed alcohol more than once a week (62.50%) than among those who drank once weekly. Additionally, 42.86% of smokers were hypertensive. Similarly, 36.16% of those who did not engage in vigorous-intensity activity were hypertensive compared to those who did. Moreover, 34.22% of participants who spent more than 8 h per day at work were hypertensive. Hypertension was also more prevalent among diabetic participants (83.33%) than among those with normal blood glucose levels. The mean height and weight of participants were 1.64 (± 0.13) m and 73.29 (± 13.49) kg, respectively. The mean BMI was 27.28 (± 8.35) kg/m², with 24.11 kg/m² among men and 29.07 kg/m² among women. Over a quarter of the participants (38.89%) who were overweight or obese were found to be hypertensive.

Factors associated with hypertension

In the binary logistic regression analysis, participants’ age, marital status, frequency of alcohol consumption per week, BMI, and blood glucose level were found to be significantly associated with hypertension (Table 3). In further multivariable logistic regression analysis, after controlling for potential confounding variables, hypertension was significantly associated with the participants’ age, frequent alcohol consumption per week, BMI, and blood glucose level. The results showed that participants aged ≥ 50 years were 3.37 times more likely to have hypertension compared to those younger than 50 years (95% CI: 1.17–9.72, p = 0.024). Also, those who consumed alcohol more than once a week were 9.55 times more likely to have hypertension compared to those who drank once a week (95% CI: 1.2–76.1, p = 0.033). Similarly, participants with diabetes were 9.15 times more likely to be hypertensive compared to those with normal blood glucose levels (95% CI: 1.0–80.8, p = 0.046). Furthermore, the odds of hypertension were 76% higher among participants who were overweight/obese compared to those with normal weight (95% CI: 1.09–2.84, p = 0.020) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence of hypertension and its associated factors among public school teachers in Obuasi East Municipality, Ashanti Region, Ghana. The results showed that approximately 33.89% of the study participants were hypertensive. The prevalence reported in this study is higher than previous studies in Accra, Ghana and other African settings, including Northwest Ethiopia and Nigeria [12, 18–20], and other settings in South Asia, including Kerala in India and Baghdad [21, 22], but lower than findings from other parts of Asia, particularly Dhaka in Bangladesh, South India, and Kirtipur in Kathmandu [23–25]. The difference in the prevalences could be partly due to using a 130/80 mmHg cutoff according to the new guidelines [17] compared to a 140/90 mmHg cutoff in other studies. Even with the 140/90 mmHg cutoff, the prevalence remained high (19.81%) in this study. Additionally, differences in the study setting and the lifestyle of the study populations may contribute to this discrepancy.

The results also showed that 39.47% of the teachers who were previously diagnosed with hypertension were not adhering to antihypertensive medications. Instead, some relied on traditional remedies to manage their elevated blood pressure. Similar findings have been reported in earlier studies conducted in Ethiopia [26] and Iraq [27]. The non-adherence to antihypertensive medication may be due to concerns about side effects, including sexual dysfunction [28–31], inadequate health education about hypertension and its management within the health system [32] or the stigma associated with young to middle-aged adults taking antihypertensive medications [33]. This highlights the need for improved patient education and follow-up within workplace health programs.

Notably, the findings revealed that 73.24% of hypertensive cases were newly diagnosed, making up 24.82% of the study population. This finding aligns with the estimated prevalence of undiagnosed hypertension in sub-Saharan Africa [34] but it is higher than previous studies in Ghana [5], Ethiopia [26] and Iraq [27]. This suggests that a significant number of the teachers were unknowingly living with hypertension, putting them at risk of severe health complications and even sudden death. The demanding nature of teaching may lead to a common culture of neglecting personal health. This points to missed opportunities for early detection of hypertension in the workplace [13]. Consequently, this finding highlights the need for a widespread community and institutionalised screening program and other relevant public health campaigns targeting the diagnosis of hypertension at early stages.

The findings indicate a significant association between hypertension and age, with a prevalence of 57.58% among participants aged 50 years and above. Specifically, the prevalence of hypertension was approximately three times higher in older adults compared to the younger population, which could be attributed to the decreased elasticity of blood vessels in older adults. This suggests that age-targeted screening initiatives for teachers nearing retirement age may be beneficial. This finding is consistent with reports from previous studies in Ghana and other African countries [11, 18, 20, 26, 35–38].

Additionally, the findings showed a significant association between hypertension and the frequency of alcohol consumption. The likelihood of hypertension was approximately nine times higher among participants who consumed alcohol more than once a week compared to those who drank once a week. This may be due to the effect of alcohol on the cardiovascular system, as regular consumption can lead to blood vessel constriction and disrupt the hormonal balance that regulates blood pressure [39]. This finding reinforces the importance of workplace health promotion programs focusing on reducing alcohol intake. This result corroborates that of previous studies in Ghana [40], southern Ethiopia [26], and India [41].

Similarly, the findings revealed a significant association between hypertension and blood glucose levels. Specifically, participants with diabetes were approximately nine times more likely to be hypertensive compared to those with normal blood glucose levels, aligning with reports from previous studies in Northwest Ethiopia [18], Bangladesh [23], and India [21]. This could be attributed to the accelerated formation of advanced glycosylation end products, which accumulate in vessel wall proteins, causing increased vascular rigidity and resistance [42]. This highlights the need for integrated screening and management of hypertension and diabetes among teachers.

Furthermore, the results indicated a significant association between hypertension and increasing body mass index, with a prevalence of 38.89% among overweight/obese participants and 24.83% among those with normal weight. This finding showed that overweight and obese teachers had a 76% higher likelihood of hypertension compared to those with a normal weight, supporting evidence from previous studies in Ghana [11], Nigeria [20], Bangladesh [23], Iraq [27] and India [21, 24, 41, 43]. This underscores the importance of promoting physical activity and healthy diets within school environments.

Limitations of the study

Although this study is the first to assess the prevalence of hypertension among public school teachers in Obuasi East Municipality and Ashanti Region, it presents several limitations. The study’s focus on public school teachers, to the exclusion of those in private schools, may limit the generalizability of the results to all school teachers in the municipality. Self-reported data on behavioural characteristics may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Additionally, the cross-sectional nature does not allow for the assessment of change over time or the establishment of causality. Future longitudinal and interventional studies could assess the impact of school-based wellness programs on hypertension prevention. Further studies could also consider incorporating a larger and more diverse sample that includes both public and private school teachers for a comprehensive and representative understanding.

Conclusion

The prevalence of hypertension among public school teachers in Obuasi East Municipality was relatively high, with a significant number of cases unaware of their hypertensive status before the study. Factors associated with hypertension among the teachers include older age, frequent alcohol consumption, diabetes, and being overweight/obese. Therefore, the Ministry of Health (MOH) and the Ministry of Education must work together to implement school-based wellness programs that focus on regular health screenings and health education tailored specifically for teachers. Integrating these initiatives into occupational health policies can foster a supportive environment where teachers prioritise their health and well-being. Additionally, the MOH should develop programs that provide resources for managing diabetes and obesity, which will further help mitigate the risk of hypertension in this population. These steps can significantly improve the health outcomes of teachers and create a healthier school environment overall.
