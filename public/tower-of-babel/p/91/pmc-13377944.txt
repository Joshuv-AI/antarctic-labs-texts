Alteration in oral and non‐oral tissues in ligature‐induced periodontitis mice with the Alzheimer's disease risk factor APOE4
Authors: Ae Ri Kim, Yun‐Jung Yoo, Eun‐Jung Bak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe APOE4 gene, particularly the ε4 allele, is linked to susceptibility to Alzheimer's disease, and periodontitis can cause pathological changes in multiple organs, but combined effects of these conditions and the impact of tumor necrosis factor (TNF)‐α regulation on the combined effects remain unclear. We examined the alterations in oral and non‐oral tissues in APOE4‐knockin mice with periodontitis and evaluated the effects of infliximab, a TNF‐α inhibitor. Mice were grouped as control, periodontitis, APOE4‐knockin, APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis, infliximab‐treated periodontitis, and infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis. There were no differences in alveolar bone volume and periodontal inflammatory cells among all periodontitis‐induced groups. The APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group showed a decrease in hippocampal Cornu Ammonis 1 neurons (p < 0.05) and increased renal and hepatic fibrosis (p < 0.05) compared to control group. The infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group had even greater neuronal loss (p < 0.001) and renal fibrosis (p < 0.01). Glial fibrillary acidic protein mRNA expression, a marker of astrocyte activation, was higher in the infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group than in the control group. These suggest that APOE4 with periodontitis worsens neuronal loss and renal and hepatic fibrosis, and TNF‐α inhibition in this coexistence may have harmful effects on both the brain and kidney.Keywords: inflammation, mice, neurodegeneration, transgenic, tumor necrosis factor‐α

INTRODUCTION

Apolipoprotein E (ApoE), encoded by APOE, is synthesized and secreted by various tissues and cells [1, 2, 3]. In rats and humans, ApoE is primarily found in brain and liver and is produced by macrophages and astrocytes, although expression by neurons has also been suggested [2, 4]. ApoE plays an important role in regulating the clearance of lipoproteins from plasma by binding specific cell surface receptors, including the low‐density lipoprotein receptor and heparan sulfate proteoglycan [5, 6, 7]. ApoE may also be involved in amyloid degradation [8], anti‐inflammation [9], anti‐infection [10], and bone metabolism such as increased bone formation and decreased bone resorption [11].

The common APOE isoforms are ApoE2, ApoE3, and ApoE4. The most common allele, APOE3 ε3, is present in approximately 78% of the global population, whereas APOE ε4 is present in about 14%, and the least common allele, APOE ε2, is present in around 8% of the population [12]. Amino acid and structural differences among the ApoE isoforms affect their interactions with the low‐density lipoprotein receptor and heparan sulfate proteoglycan; for example, ApoE2 is associated with type 3 hyperlipoproteinemia due to defects in low‐density lipoprotein receptor binding [13]. ApoE4 is associated with increased risks of cardiovascular disease and Alzheimer's disease [1, 14, 15, 16].

ApoE4‐expressing microglial‐like cells are unable to perform normal microglial functions, suggesting that ApoE4 expression impairs microglial‐mediated phagocytosis, migration, and metabolic activities [17]. Additionally, ApoE4 isoform has a reduced capacity to promote amyloid‐beta (Aβ) proteolysis than ApoE2 or ApoE3 isoforms [8]. Co‐cultures of neurons with ApoE4‐expressing microglia secrete significantly higher levels of tumor necrosis factor (TNF)‐α and have significantly reduced neuronal viability compared with co‐cultures of neurons withApoE2 ‐ or ‐ApoE3 expressing microglia, suggesting that ApoE4 expression may worsen neurodegeneration [18].

In periodontitis, a chronic inflammatory disease caused by the accumulation of various periodontopathogens. Periodontopathogens, their lipopolysaccharides, and inflammatory cytokines produced during periodontal inflammatory responses can spread to other organs through peripheral blood. Transgenic mice expressing amyloid precursor protein that were also infected with the periodontopathogen Porphyromonas gingivalis exhibited higher levels of TNF‐α and interleukin (IL)‐1β in the brain and increased Aβ deposition in the hippocampus and cortex compared to uninfected amyloid precursor protein–transgenic mice [19], suggesting that periodontitis may be involved in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer's disease.

To date, research on the relationship between APOE ε4 and oral diseases has shown that edentulous patients have a higher frequency of carrying the APOE ε4 allele gene than the dentate [20], suggesting the possibility that APOE ε4 may serve as a risk factor for oral disease. However, no in vivo study has yet reported an association between the presence of the APOE ε4 allele and periodontitis progression. As APOE4 ε4 and periodontitis can affect various organs, it is important to evaluate changes not only in periodontal tissue but also in non‐oral tissues when APOE4 ε4 and periodontitis coexist. Given that ApoE4 expression is associated with increased TNF‐α levels [18] and that TNF‐α induced by periodontitis may affect the brain [21], it is necessary to investigate the impact of TNF‐α regulation in the coexistence of APOE ε4 and periodontitis. For this purpose, we evaluated alterations in oral and non‐oral tissues in APOE4‐knockin mice with periodontitis. The effect of TNF‐α inhibition was assessed by administering the TNF‐α inhibitor infliximab to wild‐type mice with periodontitis and APOE4‐knockin mice with periodontitis.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Animals

Male wild‐type C57BL/6 mice and human APOE4‐knockin mice [B6(SJL)‐ApoEtm1.1(APOE*4)Adiuj/J] were purchased from Jackson Laboratory and arrived at the facility at 8‐week‐old. Mice were housed in individually ventilated cages with sterile filter caps, 2–3 per cage, and maintained under specific pathogen‐free conditions at 22°C and 60% humidity with a 12:12 h light:dark cycle. Mice weighed 27 ± 2 g at the start of the experiment and were given access to water and food (PicoLab Rodent Diet 20, LabDiet) ad libitum. The bedding (TAPVEI) was changed twice a week, and environmental enrichment, including diamond twists and nesting materials, was provided. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Yonsei University (Approval Number: 2021‐0166) and were performed according to the Guiding Principles for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Institutes of Health).

Study design

After a 1‐week acclimation period, mice were assigned to six groups using cage‐based randomization via drawing lots. All mice sharing a cage were assigned to the same group to ensure consistency. Mice were divided into 6 groups (Figure 1A): control (n = 8), periodontitis (n = 8), APOE4‐knockin (n = 5), APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis (n = 7), infliximab‐treated with periodontitis (n = 7), and infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis (n = 7). Periodontitis was induced at 9‐week‐old under anesthesia. A mixture of Zoletil 50 (30 mg/kg; Virbac) and Rompun (10 mg/kg; Bayer Korea) at a 1:1 ratio was administered intraperitoneally, after which the mandibular first molar was ligated using dental floss (Oral‐B). Infliximab (5 mg/kg, Janssen Biologics) or saline was administered intraperitoneally once per week from 9 to 24 weeks [22]. After ligation, mice were monitored for pain and signs of distress. All mice were euthanized at 24 weeks using CO2 gas. Body weight was measured weekly. All experimental procedures, including periodontitis induction and treatment, were conducted on the treatment table in the animal laboratory. To minimize bias, all analyses were performed with the observer blinded to the groups. Blinding was accomplished by coding the samples so that the observer could not identify the group assignments during sample collection, data acquisition, and analysis.

During the tissue collection and evaluation process, factors such as tissue damage, section detachment from slides, insufficient plasma volume, and RNA extraction failure led to variations in the number of animals used for each analysis. The number of animals used for each result is indicated in the figure legends and table caption.

Plasma biochemistry

Upon euthanasia, blood was collected from the heart of each mouse into an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tube. Blood was centrifuged at 3000 × g for 20 min to separate plasma. Triglycerides, total cholesterol, and high‐density lipoprotein were measured in plasma using a FUJI DRI‐CHEM 4000i chemistry analyzer (Fuji Film).

Bone assessment

Extracted mandibles and femurs were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (Duksan) for 24 h. The bones were scanned at 70 kV and 139 µA with an exposure time of 1475 ms using a SkyScan 1076 (Bruker Biospin). Three‐dimensional images were reconstructed at 36‐µm‐voxel resolution with a pixel size of 18 µm. For each mandible, 24 two‐dimensional images were collected, and for each femur, 86 two‐dimensional images were collected. For the alveolar bone, the plane where both the mesial and distal roots were clearly visible at the furcation of the first molars was selected. Similarly, for the femur, the plane where the growth plate was clearly visible was chosen. Alveolar bone volume was measured in a region of interest 0.5 mm from the furcation top, between the mesial and distal roots of the first molar. Femoral cortical bone volume and bone mineral density (BMD) were measured in regions of interest 0.7–1.5 mm inferior to the growth plate.

Histological evaluation

At the time of euthanasia, the mandible, brain, right kidney, and liver were extracted. The mandible was fixed in neutral buffered formalin for 24 h, followed by decalcification in 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid solution for 4 weeks. Brain, kidney, and liver were fixed in neutral buffered formalin for 2–3 days. After tissue processing, tissues were embedded in paraffin, cut into 4 µm thickness, and mounted on slides. The slides were deparaffinized, rehydrated with an alcohol series, and stained. Slides of mandibles and brains were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Slides of kidney and liver were stained with Masson's trichrome staining. Stained slides were scanned using a slide scanner (Aperio AT2, Leica Biosystems) and analyzed using Aperio ImageScope software (version 12.3.2.2013).

Inflammatory cell infiltration in periodontal tissue

In hematoxylin and eosin‐stained mandibular slides, inflammatory cells, including mononuclear cells and polymorphonuclear leukocytes, were counted in the region of interest (10,000 µm2) beneath the gingival epithelial cell layer in the distal area of the first molar, and the counts were divided by the region of interest areas.

Neuron number in CA1 region of hippocampus

The evaluation for neuropathology was performed by counting the number of neurons present in the region of interest area (50,000 µm2) of the hippocampal cornu ammonis 1 (CA1) [23]. That is, in the hematoxylin and eosin‐stained brain slides, the number of neurons was counted in the region of interest within hippocampal CA1 region, and the counts were divided by the region of interest areas.

Fibrosis in renal cortex and liver

In Masson's trichrome‐stained kidney and liver slides, the intensity of blue staining in the renal cortex of the right kidney and in the liver parenchyma was quantified and expressed as the percentage of fibrosis.

TNF‐α expression in periodontal tissue and Aβ expression in hippocampus

Immunohistochemical staining was conducted to assess TNF‐α expression in the distal region of the first molar and Aβ expression in the hippocampal CA1 region. Briefly, slides were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol for 15 min at room temperature to block endogenous peroxidase activity. For antigen retrieval, mandible slides were treated with trypsin (Abcam) for 10 min at 37°C, and brain slides were treated with citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) for 20 min at 80–85°C. Slides were incubated with normal horse serum blocking solution (Vector Laboratories) for 1 h at room temperature to block non‐specific reactions. Mandibular slides were incubated with rabbit polyclonal TNF‐α primary antibody (Abcam, 1:250), and brain slides were incubated with mouse monoclonal amyloid 1–42 primary antibody (Biolegend, 1:250) overnight at 4°C. Slides were then incubated with anti‐rabbit or anti‐mouse secondary antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (Vector Laboratories) for 30 min at room temperature. Slides were developed with diaminobenzidine (DAKO) and counterstained with Mayer's hematoxylin (R&D Systems). The quantification of TNF‐α expression in periodontal tissue and Aβ expression in the hippocampal CA1 region was calculated by dividing positive pixels within a region of interest by the total pixels in the region of interest.

Gfap and Tnf mRNA expression in the brain

The mRNA expression levels of the astrocyte activation marker glial fibrillary acidic protein (Gfap) and Tnf in the brain were analyzed by real‐time polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Brain was lysed in Labozol Reagent (Cosmogenetech) to obtain total RNA, and 1 µg total RNA was reverse‐transcribed to synthesize cDNA, according to the manufacturer's protocols. Real‐time PCR was performed using BioFACT A‐Star Taq DNA Polymerase (BioFACT) and a CFX96 Touch Real‐Time PCR Detection System (Bio‐Rad Laboratories). The PCR conditions were as follows: 95°C for 2 min, followed by 40 amplification cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 60°C for 15 s, and 72°C for 25 s. Relative quantification was performed using the ΔCt method. Samples were normalized against the mRNA expression levels of hypoxanthine‐guanine phosphoribosyltransferase1 (Hprt). The primer sequences used for real‐time PCR are shown in Table 1.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM). Using g*power 3.1.9.7 software, a minimum of 30 animals was required for the six‐group comparison (control, periodontitis, APOE4‐knockin, APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis, infliximab‐treated periodontitis, and infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis), based on an effect size of 0.8 for a one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with a power of 80% and an alpha level of 0.05. Statistics were performed using one‐way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests to adjust for multiple comparisons among these groups. A p‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

RESULTS

Effects of APOE4‐knockin and periodontitis on weight and blood parameters, and their responses to TNF‐α inhibition

To evaluate gross findings in response to human APOE4‐knockin and periodontitis induction and their responses to TNF‐α inhibition, body weight gain was calculated by subtracting the body weight immediately before the ligature placement from the body weight at euthanasia (Table 2). Body weight gain was significantly lower in the periodontitis (3.4 ± 1.6 g, ∼47% decrease) and APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis (2.7 ± 1.1 g, ∼58% decrease) groups compared with the control group. Notably, body weight gain in the infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group (1.0 ± 1.9 g, ∼84% decrease vs. control; ∼80% decrease vs. APOE4‐knockin; ∼66% decrease vs. infliximab‐treated periodontitis) was lower than that in the control, APOE4‐knockin, and infliximab‐treated periodontitis groups.

As ApoE is an important factor in human lipid metabolism and cholesterol transport, we measured triglyceride, total cholesterol, and high‐density lipoprotein levels in plasma (Table 2). Triglyceride levels were significantly lower in the periodontitis (108.1 ± 28.9 mg/dL, ∼33% decrease) and APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis (104.9 ± 27.2 mg/dL, ∼35% decrease) groups than in the control group. In the infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group (52.9 ± 16.8 mg/dL), these levels were lower than in all other groups (∼67% decrease vs. control; ∼51% decrease vs. periodontitis; ∼57% decrease vs. APOE4‐knockin; ∼53% decrease vs. infliximab‐treated periodontitis) except the APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group. The total cholesterol level in the APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group (68.3 ± 12.3 mg/dL) was lower than that in the control and periodontitis groups (∼24% decrease vs. control; ∼27% decrease vs. periodontitis). The infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group (64.7 ± 71 mg/dL) was lower than the control, periodontitis, and infliximab‐treated periodontitis groups (∼28% decrease vs. control; ∼31% decrease vs. periodontitis; ∼32% decrease vs. infliximab‐treated periodontitis). The significant differences and degree of decrease in high‐density lipoprotein levels between each group were similar to those in total cholesterol levels. There were no differences in body weight and either blood parameter between the APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis and infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis groups.

Effects of APOE4‐knockin and periodontitis on periodontal tissue, and their responses to TNF‐α inhibition

To investigate the effects of APOE4‐knockin and periodontitis induction on periodontal tissue and their responses to TNF‐α inhibition, we assessed the alveolar bone volume, inflammatory cell infiltration, and TNF‐α expression in the mandibular first molar (Figure 1B–D). All groups with induced periodontitis had significantly lower alveolar bone volume compared to the control group (∼91% decrease vs. periodontitis; ∼87% decrease vs. APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis; ∼88% decrease vs. infliximab‐treated periodontitis group; ∼97% decrease vs. infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis). However, no significant differences were observed between the control and APOE4‐knockin groups (Figure 1B). In the inflammatory cell infiltration, the periodontitis group (1118 ± 456 No/µm2, ∼8.9‐fold increase) and the infliximab‐treated periodontitis group (956 ± 562 No/µm2, ∼7.7‐fold increase) showed significantly greater infiltration compared with the control group (Figure 1C). The periodontitis group displayed significantly higher TNF‐α intensity (20.9 ± 14.6, ∼7.2‐fold increase) compared to the control group (Figure 1D). The infliximab‐treated periodontitis group exhibited lower TNF‐α intensity than the periodontitis group (4.6 ± 3.7, ∼78% decrease), whereas the infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group showed no differences from the APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group.

Effects of APOE4‐knockin and periodontitis on brain, and their responses to TNF‐α inhibition

To evaluate neuronal changes in the brain caused by APOE4‐knockin and periodontitis and the changes resulting from TNF‐α inhibition, we analyzed the number of neurons and Aβ expression in the hippocampal CA1 region, as well as Gfap and Tnf mRNA expression (Figure 2). The number of neurons in the CA1 region was significantly lower in the APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group (1086 ± 46 No/µm2, ∼23.2% decrease) and the infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group (980 ± 137 No/µm2, ∼30.8% decrease) compared with the control group, with the infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group showing a greater difference (p < 0.001) than the APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group (p < 0.05) (Figure 2A). Aβ expression in the CA1 region was significantly higher in the APOE4‐knockin group (20 ± 10, ∼4.6‐fold increase vs. control; ∼3.4‐fold increase vs. periodontitis) than in the control and periodontitis groups (Figure 2B). Gfap mRNA expression levels in the infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group (0.3 ± 0.2) were significantly higher than those in the control group (∼3.0‐fold increase) and the infliximab‐treated periodontitis group (∼3.9‐fold increase) (Figure 2C). However, no significant differences in Tnf mRNA expression were observed among all groups (Figure 2D).

Effects of APOE4‐knockin and periodontitis on femur, kidney, and liver and their responses to TNF‐α inhibition

To assess whether APOE4‐knockin and periodontitis induction affect non‐oral tissues, we histomorphologically evaluated the femur, kidney, and liver (Figure 3). No significant differences in bone volume and BMD were observed in the femoral cortical bone among all groups (Figure 3A,B). Renal fibrosis was significantly higher in the APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group (3.2 ± 0.6, 1.4‐fold increase vs. control; 1.5‐fold increase vs. periodontitis) and the infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group (3.4 ± 0.8, ∼1.5‐fold increase vs. control; ∼1.6‐fold increase vs. periodontitis) compared to the control and periodontitis groups (Figure 3C). The significant differences in hepatic fibrosis between the groups were similar to those observed in renal fibrosis (Figure 3D). Particularly, the renal fibrosis in the infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group showed a greater difference (p < 0.01) compared to the APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group (p < 0.05).

DISCUSSION

Chronic and persistent diseases, such as Alzheimer's disease and periodontitis, can have great impacts on human health due to their extended lifespans. In this study, we investigated alterations in oral and non‐oral tissues by inducing periodontitis in mice knocked in for human APOE4 ε4, a known risk factor for Alzheimer's disease.

Previous reports have indicated that periodontitis is associated with dyslipidemia [24, 25]. In humans, a positive correlation has been identified between periodontitis and dyslipidemia [24]. In an in vivo study, total cholesterol levels increased, and high‐density lipoprotein levels decreased in rats with periodontitis, which showed no weight difference compared to the control rats [25]. However, in this study, the periodontitis, APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis, and infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis groups showed less body weight gain compared to the control group, likely attributable to difficulties in food intake or general discomfort caused by the ligature. Similarly, the triglyceride levels in the periodontitis, APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis, and infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis groups were lower than those in the control group, suggesting that the reduced triglyceride levels in the periodontitis‐induced groups may be related to the lower body weight gain. The infliximab‐treated periodontitis group maintained body weight and triglyceride levels comparable to the control group, implying that infliximab may help mitigate the effects of weight loss in the presence of periodontitis alone. Additionally, the APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group had lower total cholesterol and high‐density lipoprotein levels compared to the periodontitis group. In APOE4‐knockin mice, lower serum total cholesterol and high‐density lipoprotein levels have been reported compared with C57BL/6J mice at 4 and 14 months of age (The 2018 Alzheimer's Association International Conference). Consistent with this finding, in this study, the APOE4‐knockin group tended to exhibit lower total cholesterol and high‐density lipoprotein levels than the control group, although these differences were not statistically significant. Taken together, the lower total cholesterol and high‐density lipoprotein levels in the APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis and infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis groups are inferred to be caused by APOE4‐knockin.

To determine the changes in periodontal tissue in the presence of APOE4‐knockin and periodontitis induction, we assessed alveolar bone volume, inflammatory cell infiltration, and TNF‐α expression in the PDL area. Alveolar bone loss due to periodontitis was very evident in the periodontitis‐induced groups, with no significant difference alveolar bone volume between the control and APOE4‐knockin groups. Higher levels of inflammatory cells were observed in the periodontitis group than in the control group. A significant increase in TNF‐α expression compared with the control group was observed only in the periodontitis group. Taken together, these results suggest that APOE4‐knockin has little effect on periodontal tissue in mice as old as 24 weeks, regardless of the absence or presence of periodontitis. Although a previous study reported no difference in the cementum‐enamel junction to alveolar bone crest distance between ApoE‐knockout and wild‐type mice during a 14‐week periodontitis induction period [26], our study is the first to describe changes in periodontal tissue of APOE4‐knockin in mice with periodontitis.

APOE ε4 is associated with neurodegeneration, including increased amyloid deposition and neuronal loss [27, 28]. As reported in amyloid precursor protein‐/APOE4‐knockin mice, where Aβ levels continuously increased between 13 and 21 months compared to amyloid precursor protein‐/APOE3‐knockin mice [27], in this study, Aβ accumulation was higher in the APOE4‐knockin group than in the control group. It has been reported that neuronal loss increases in patients with APOE ε4 alleles [28]. When evaluating the number of neurons in the CA1 region (comparison of the six groups), we found fewer neurons in the APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group than in the control group. Additionally, Jiang et al. reported that P. gingivalis‐induced periodontitis causes neuronal loss through dysfunction of the brain barrier [29]. In this study, excluding the infliximab‐treated groups, the comparison among the four groups revealed neuronal loss in both the periodontitis group and APOE4‐knockin group, as well as in the APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group, compared to the control group. These results suggest that the coexistence of APOE4‐knockin and periodontitis may aggravate neuronal loss.

To date, limited research has examined changes in organs other than the brain in APOE4‐knockin mice, but we observed changes in the femur, kidney, and liver. No differences in femoral cortical bone volume and BMD were observed across groups. A prior study reported that the bone volumes of the lumbar spine and femur in APOE2‐knockin mice were lower than those in APOE3‐knockin mice, but no differences in the bone volumes of the lumbar spine and femur were observed between APOE4‐knockin mice and APOE3‐knockin mice [30]. In this study, the APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group showed higher levels of renal and hepatic fibrosis than the control and periodontitis groups. In a recent genetic study, APOE ε4 was reported as protective against nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [31], and APOE4‐knockin mice fed a high‐fat or high‐sugar diet for 7 months presented with less hepatic fibrosis than APOE3‐expressing mice [32]. However, a different in vivo study reported no significant difference in the hepatic fibrosis between APOE3‐knockin and APOE4‐knockin mice after being fed a high‐fat diet for 6 months [33]. Although previous studies have shown inconsistent results, our results suggest that the coexistence of APOE4‐knockin and periodontitis may affect other tissues, such as the kidney and liver.

Pro‐inflammatory mediators, such as TNF‐α, may be involved in the correlation between Alzheimer's disease and periodontitis [21]. The TNF‐α inhibitor, infliximab, was administered to the periodontitis group and the APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group. In periodontal tissue, the expression of TNF‐α in the infliximab‐treated periodontitis group was reduced compared to the periodontitis group. However, no significant difference was observed between the APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group and the infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group, suggesting that TNF‐α inhibition by a TNF‐α inhibitor may be observed when periodontitis is present alone. In the brain, neuronal loss was more pronounced in the infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group (p < 0.001) compared to the APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group (p < 0.05) when compared to the control group. Moreover, Gfap mRNA expression was increased only in the infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group compared to the control group. Similarly, in renal fibrosis, the infliximab‐treated APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group (p < 0.01) showed a more pronounced difference compared to the APOE4‐knockin with periodontitis group (p < 0.05) when compared to the control group. Taken together, these results suggest that TNF‐α inhibition in the coexistence of APOE4‐knockin and periodontitis may have detrimental effects on the brain and kidney.

This study has several limitations. First, a longer experimental period may be necessary. Twenty‐four weeks of age is relatively young to observe neuronal changes related to Alzheimer's disease. In addition, when the number of osteoclasts was compared only in the APOE4‐knockin group and the control group, the APOE4‐knockin group had more osteoclasts than the control group, although this difference was not statistically significant (data not shown). Another study has reported that ApoE4 protein has a relatively weaker inhibitory effect on osteoclast differentiation compared to ApoE3 protein in vitro [11] and that the presence of APOE ε4 is associated with an increased long‐term risk of osteoporosis in humans [34], supporting the possibility that APOE ε4 may influence bone resorption in vivo. It is therefore necessary to evaluate the effect of APOE ε4 on alveolar bone volume over a longer period. Second, because our results focused on histomorphological analysis, molecular biological studies will be needed to investigate the function of APOE ε4 related to the correlation between Alzheimer's disease and periodontitis, including the examination of additional pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐6 and IL‐1β. Finally, the observed increase in fibrosis in the kidney and liver associated with the coexistence of APOE4‐knockin and periodontitis in our study is inconsistent with the results of previous studies and requires further validation.

This study represents the first to focus on histomorphological analysis of changes occurring in the presence of APOE ε4 and periodontitis. In the presence of APOE ε4 and periodontitis, neuronal reductions, as well as renal and hepatic fibrosis, were increased, suggesting that their combination leads to adverse effects. Furthermore, when periodontitis coexists with APOEε4, TNF‐α inhibition may have more harmful consequences in organs such as the brain and kidney. Our findings suggest that individuals harboring APOE ε4 should pay close attention to periodontitis prevention and oral health management.
