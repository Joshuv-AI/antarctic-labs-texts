Mechanism of Antinociceptive Effect of Vanillin in Formalin Test
Authors: Valiollah Hajhashemi, Hossein Shahmoradi, Omid Hajihashemi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Backgrounds:Vanillin is the active component of Vanillus planifolia seeds and is widely used as a flavoring agent in food and pharmaceutical industries. It has shown anti-inflammatory and antinociceptive properties. The aim of the present research was to investigate its possible mechanism(s) in formalin test.Methods:Male Swiss mice (25–30 g) were used. Formalin test was used to evaluate the antinociceptive effect. Different groups of mice were pretreated with prazocin (2 mg/kg), yohimbine (5 mg/kg), propranolol (2 mg/kg), cyproheptadine (2 mg/kg), ondansetron (2 mg/kg), naloxone (5 mg/kg), sulpiride (20 mg/kg), arginine (100 mg/kg), L-NAME (20 mg/kg), methylene blue (5 mg/kg), or glibenclamide (10 mg/kg) to evaluate the role of pertinent receptors or pathways on vanillin-induced antinociception.Results:Vanillin showed antinociceptive effect in the second phase of formalin test. Pretreatment with ondansetron, sulpiride, L-NAME, methylene blue, or glibenclamide prevented vanillin antinociceptive effect.Conclusions:Vanillin showed antinociceptive effect in formalin test, and according to the results, the NO/cGMP/KATP pathway and serotonin 5HT3 and dopamine D2 receptors have an important contribution to its antinociceptive effect, but opioid and adrenergic receptors are not involved in this effect.

Introduction

Various herbal medications and their active components have been researched for their potential antinociceptive and anti-inflammatory benefits.[1] One of these substances is vanillin, which is considered as the main component of Vanillus planifolia seeds. Vanillin is a phenolic aldehyde and is widely used as a flavoring agent in food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries. It has anti-inflammatory, anticancer, antitumor, antioxidant, and antineuroinflammatory effects, and based on these properties, it is used for inflammatory diseases and the prevention and treatment of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s,[23] as well as the prevention of liver damage and fibrosis.[4]

The antinociceptive effect of vanillin has been shown in previous studies, but studies on its mechanism are limited and controversial.[56] The findings of one study demonstrated that TRP channels (transient receptor potential channels) mediate the antinociceptive effect of vanillin,[6] while in another study, opioid and alpha-2 adrenergic receptors suggested as the mechanism.[5]

The pain signaling is a very complex process, and various neurotransmitters including serotonin, substance P, dopamine, endogenous opioids, cannabinoids, and nitric oxide signaling participate in the pain pathway.[7]

To the best of our knowledge, the mechanism of its antinociceptive action is not completely understood. Therefore, the aim of the present work was to find out the possible role of some important receptors as well as NO pathway in the antinociceptive activity of vanillin.

Materials and Methods

Drugs

Vanillin was purchased from Merck Chemical Company (Germany). Propranolol and naloxone (Tolid Daru, Iran) and ondansetron (Tehran Chemie Pharmaceutical Co., Iran) were also used. Raha Pharmaceutical company (Isfahan, Iran) provided cyproheptadine as a gift. Other chemicals including prazosin, yohimbine, L-NAME, arginine, methylene blue, glibenclamide, and sulpiride were purchased from Sigma Chemical Co. (St Louis, MO, USA).

Animals

A total of 90 male Swiss mice, aged 6 weeks and weighing between 25 and 30 g, were kept in the animal house of the School of Pharmacy (Isfahan, Iran). The mice were maintained under standard conditions of humidity, temperature, and light/dark cycle. There was free access to food and water. One week prior to starting experiments, animals were transferred to the behavioral laboratory to acclimatize to the environment. All animal studies were conducted in accordance with the standards for the care and use of laboratory animals provided by The National Ethical Committee (Iran) (Ethics code: IR.MUI.AEC.1401.025).

Experimental design

Fifteen groups of male mice (n = 6) received the following drugs:

Group 1 (control): Vehicle (10 mL/kg)

Groups 2–4: Received three different doses of vanillin (12.5, 25, and 50 mg/kg)

Groups 5–7: Pretreatment with prazocin (2 mg/kg), yohimbine (5 mg/kg), or propranolol (2 mg/kg)

Groups 8–9: Pretreatment with cyproheptadine (2 mg/kg) or ondansetron (2 mg/kg)

Group 10: Pretreatment with naloxone (5 mg/kg)

Group 11: Pretreatment with sulpiride (20 mg/kg)

Group 12–15: Pretreatment with arginine (100 mg/kg), L-NAME (20 mg/kg), methylene blue (5 mg/kg), or glibenclamide (10 mg/kg).

All pretreatments were performed 30 minutes prior to vanillin administration (25 mg/kg, i.p.). Formalin was injected into the subplantar space of right hind paw 30 minutes after vanillin. Drug doses were selected based on previous studies.[89] Vanillin and other drugs were administered i.p. at a volume of 10 mL/kg.

Formalin test

Formalin test was performed as described previously.[1011] Briefly, twenty microliter of a diluted solution of formalin (2.5% v/v) was injected into the subplantar space of the right hind paw of each mouse and the time spent for licking in periods of 0–5 and 20–40 min after formalin administration was measured and considered as acute and chronic phases, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Results are expressed as mean ± SEM. The one-way ANOVA and Scheffe post hoc tests were used to analyze the data. The P values less than 0.05 were considered significant. The software programs used for data analyzing and making graphs were SPSS (version 23) and Excel 2020 (IBM, Chicago, Illinois, USA).

Results

Antinociceptive effect of different doses of vanillin

As is seen in Figure 1, vanillin at doses of 12.5, 25, and 50 mg/kg showed significant (P < 0.001 for the all doses of vanillin) antinociceptive effect in the chronic phase of formalin test. The drug failed to suppress pain behavior in the acute phase.

Evaluation of the role of the adrenergic system in vanillin anti-nociceptive effect

When compared to the vanillin alone group, pretreatment with yohimbine, a selective alpha-2 antagonist (5 mg/kg), prazosin, a selective alpha 1 antagonist, and propranolol, a nonselective beta-adrenergic receptor antagonist (2 mg/kg), did not substantially alter the antinociceptive effect of vanillin [Figure 2].

Evaluation of serotonin system on the antinociceptive effect of vanillin

According to Figure 3, vanillin showed a significant (P < 0.001) antinociceptive activity in the chronic phase of formalin test and ondansetrone as a selective 5HT3 antagonist partially reversed the antinociceptive effect of vanillin in this phase.

The effect of pretreatment with naloxone on the antinociceptive effect of vanillin

Naloxone (5 mg/kg) was tested to assess the involvement of opioid receptors. It did not inhibit vanillin’s antinociceptive impact in both phases of the formalin test (data not shown).

Evaluation of the effect of sulpiride on vanillin antinociceptive effect

Sulpiride (20 mg/kg) was utilized as a selective D2 to assess the function of dopamine receptors. The paw licking times in the acute phase for control, vanillin, and sulpiride groups were 34.8 ± 5.4, 28.0 ± 4.8, and 24.3 ± 4.2 s, respectively.

In the chronic phase of the test, the respective times were 92.5 ± 4.2, 22.3 ± 2.8, and 48.3 ± 5.8 s. Pretreatment with sulpiride demonstrated a significant (P < 0.01) reversal of vanillin antinociceptive activity [Figure 4].

The third group was intraperitoneally pretreated sulpiride (20 mg/kg). Data show mean ± SEM of 6 mice per group. ***P < 0.001 compared to control group. ##P < 0.01 compared to vanillin alone group

Effect of the drugs acting on NO/cGMP/KATP pathway on the antinociceptive effect of vanillin

To evaluate the NO system, arginine (100 mg/kg) as a precursor of NO synthesis, L-NAME as a nitric oxide synthase inhibitor (20 mg/kg), methylene blue as an inhibitor of guanylyl cyclase (5 mg/kg), and glibenclamide (10 mg/kg) as an ATP-dependent potassium channel blocker were used. L-NAME (P < 0.05), methylene blue (P < 0.001), and glibenclamide (P < 0.001) prevented vanillin-induced antinociceptive effect [Figure 5].

Discussion

In the present research, vanillin demonstrated antinociceptive effect in the chronic phase of formalin test. Additionally, in this study, the possible mechanisms of the analgesic effect of vanillin were investigated in the formalin test and results indicated that pretreatment of mice with ondansetron, sulpiride, L-NAME, methylene blue, or glibenclamide prevented vanillin-induced antinociception.

In agreement with our results, vanillin showed antinociceptive effect in another model of pain, namely, the acetic acid-induced writhing test. Unlike our findings, vanillin failed to show effect in formalin test. In our study, doses of 12.5–50 mg/kg of vanillin were used and administered intraperitoneally, while in the study conducted by Park et al.,[5] lower doses (1 to 10 mg/kg) were administered orally, and the difference between our results and their results may be explained by the difference in administration route and dosage. In another study, the antinociceptive profile of two doses of vanillin (10 and 100 mg/kg) was evaluated in Wistar rats using hot plate method and it was reported that both doses increased the latency period and showed analgesia and since naloxone-pretreatment reduced the latency period, the authors concluded that the analgesic effect of vanillin is probably mediated through opioid receptors.[12] The difference between our findings and Rathnakar[12] study might be due to different animals, doses of vanillin, and methods. We used formalin test in mice, and the doses of vanillin were 12.5, 25, and 50 mg/kg, while they used doses of 10 and 100 mg/kg in hot plate method in rats.

In the present study, antagonists of receptors of pain pathways were used to investigate the role of different pathways in the analgesic effect of vanillin.

Naloxone was used as an opioid receptor antagonist, and according to the results, naloxone could not reverse the analgesic effect of vanillin. Therefore, it seems that the opioid receptors do not contribute to vanillin analgesic effect.

The involvement of adrenergic receptors in the pain pathway has been previously studied. In addition, clonidine as an alpha-2 adrenergic receptor agonist has shown an analgesic effect.[13] To evaluate the impact of adrenergic receptors in the antinociceptive effect of vanillin, mice were pretreated with prazosin, yohimbine, or propranolol as alpha-1, alpha-2, and nonselective beta receptor antagonist, respectively. By comparing the duration of foot licking in the vanillin group with the groups pretreated with these drugs, it was found that these receptors also have no effect on vanillin antinociception. Contrary to our results, Park et al.[5] reported that yohimbine as a α2-adrenergic receptor blocker inhibited vanillin-induced antinociceptive effect. This discrepancy may be due to differences in doses, route of administration, and animal model used. In the present study, we used i.p. doses of vanillin (12.5, 25, and 50 mg/kg) in formalin test, while they used oral vanillin (from 1 to 10 mg/kg) in the acetic-acid writhing model.[5]

The role of serotonin receptors in pain modulation process has been studied before. It has been proven that the stimulation of the gray area of the brain leads to the release of serotonin (5HT) in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, and then the activation of 5HT2 and 5HT3 receptors leads to the blocking of the spinothalamic pathway and prevents the transmission of pain from the spinal cord to the thalamus.[14] In the present study, cyproheptadine and ondansetron were used. Although cyproheptadine is not a specific antagonist of serotonin receptors, it has a strong antagonistic effect on several subtypes of 5HT2 receptors, including 5HT2A, 5HT2B, and 5HT2C. Ondansetron specifically antagonizes 5HT3 receptors. Of these two antagonists, cyproheptadine could not reverse the antinociceptive activity of vanillin, but ondansetron significantly (P < 0.05) reversed the effect, which confirms the role of 5HT3 receptors as one of the possible mechanisms of the pain reducing effect of vanillin.

The role of dopamine receptors in modulating pain has been well established.[15] Sulpiride as a selective D2 antagonist significantly prevented the antinociceptive effect caused by vanillin in the second phase of the formalin test. Therefore, it is speculated that the analgesic mechanism of this compound may involve the dopaminergic D2 receptors.

Extensive studies have been conducted on NO signaling in the pain process. NO is a direct suppressor of inflammatory pain. On the other hand, pain behavior in the second phase of the formalin test has an inflammatory origin. In the present study, arginine as a precursor of NO enhanced the analgesic activity of vanillin, but this effect was not significant. Our results clearly indicated that the NO/cGMP/KATP pathway plays an important role in vanillin-induced antinociception because pretreatment with L-NAME, methylene blue, or glibenclamide significantly reversed its antinociceptive effect. Methylene blue reduces cGMP concentration by inhibiting guanylyl cyclase, and as expected, these drugs showed preventive effects on the antinociceptive effect of vanillin.[16] The pain signaling process is very complex, and many other neurotransmitters are involved (e.g., GABA, cannabinoids, glutamate, etc.) that have not been studied in the present work and they are considered as limitations of our research.

In conclusion, according to the results, vanillin produced significant antinociceptive effect in the chronic phase of formalin test and the NO/cGMP/KATP pathway as well as serotonin 5HT3 and dopamine D2 receptors showed an important contribution to its antinociceptive effect while opioid and adrenergic receptors did not involve in the effect.
