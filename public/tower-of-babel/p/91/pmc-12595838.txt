Kallikrein–kinin system as a potential target for the treatment of intervertebral disc degeneration
Authors: Tong Lu, Wei Zhu, Fangjun Yang, Kai Zhu, Jiayu Ren, Huanping Zhu, Binglin Ye, Fengqi Sun, Wanqian Zhang, Xiaoming Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is one of the main causes of chronic low back pain (LBP) and related spinal diseases, severely affecting patients’ quality of life and imposing a considerable burden on healthcare systems. Current treatment strategies for IVDD primarily include conservative management and surgical intervention, but the therapeutic outcomes remain limited. Consequently, there is an urgent need to identify novel therapeutic targets. Recent research highlights the kallikrein–kinin system (KKS), a complex polypeptide network, as a key player in the pathological processes of IVDD. This review summarizes the multiple mechanisms by which KKS may contribute to IVDD, focusing on its involvement in mechanical stress, inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, cell proliferation and apoptosis, cellular senescence, extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, angiogenesis, nerve ingrowth, nutrient supply, and genetic factors. Emerging evidence suggests that KKS exerts its functions largely through bradykinin 1 receptor (B1R) and bradykinin 2 receptor (B2R), regulating proinflammatory and oxidative processes, while also playing critical roles in apoptosis, senescence, and neovascularization. Furthermore, KKS is implicated in IVDD progression via modulation of calcium ion channels and key signaling pathways (e.g., NF-κB, MAPK, PI3K/Akt/mTOR). This review also discusses the role of KKS in IVDD-related pain mechanisms, highlighting its potential regulation of nerve growth factor (NGF) and subsequent modulation of pain perception. Collectively, these findings point to KKS as a promising therapeutic target for IVDD. Future studies should delve deeper into the local and systemic expression of KKS in intervertebral discs, develop targeted drugs against B1R and B2R, and evaluate their clinical efficacy and safety in IVDD treatment, thereby providing a theoretical foundation and novel strategies for more effective IVDD interventions.

Introduction

Intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is a major cause of low back pain (LBP) and related spinal disorders, seriously compromising patients’ quality of life and placing a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems [1, 2]. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, LBP was one of the most common conditions in 2020, affecting approximately 619 million people worldwide, a figure projected to rise to 843 million by 2050 [3]. In the United States alone, annual direct or indirect losses attributed to LBP exceed 100 billion USD [4, 5]. In China, LBP likewise ranks among the top causes of disability [6]. Epidemiological data showed that the prevalence of IVDD increased from 24.2% in 2005 to 30.1% in 2017, with more than one-third of patients in the 65–69 age group, regardless of gender [7].

The intervertebral disc (IVD) consists of the central nucleus pulposus (NP), surrounded by the annulus fibrosus (AF) and covered by endplates. It serves critical functions in bearing spinal loads, absorbing shocks, and allowing trunk movements. IVDD is a multifaceted and progressive pathological process: with advancing age, the disc undergoes gradual degeneration characterized by a reduction in extracellular matrix (ECM) components, annular tears, nucleus pulposus dehydration, and diminished disc height. This process often involves enhanced inflammatory responses as well as aberrant vascular and nerve ingrowth, which can contribute to chronic pain and significantly limit patients’ mobility. The underlying mechanisms primarily involve inflammation, oxidative stress, decreased cell proliferation, increased apoptosis, accelerated cellular senescence, and pathological angiogenesis in disc cells, ultimately leading to symptomatic disc degeneration and LBP [8].

Current treatments for IVDD typically include bed rest, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and, in more severe cases, nucleus pulposus excision or spinal fusion. However, such surgical approaches are expensive and mainly alleviate symptoms without reversing the degenerative process. Identifying novel, more effective therapeutic targets is therefore of paramount importance to delay or halt disc degeneration. Recent studies indicate that kininogen 1 (KNG-1), a core component of the kallikrein–kinin system (KKS), is a key mediator of nucleus pulposus cell senescence and is closely associated with inflammation and pain, speculating a broader relationship between KKS and IVDD [9]. Our team’s immunohistochemical analyses have shown that in human IVD tissues, KNG-1, bradykinin (BK), and its bradykinin 2 receptor (B2R) exhibit increased expression with advancing disc degeneration grades (Fig. 1) [10, 11]. Kallikrein–kinin system

The kallikrein–kinin system is a complex network of polypeptides, comprising KNG, kallikrein, BK, and the BK receptors (B1R and B2R). Historically, the earliest kinin-like substance with antihypertensive activity was isolated from urine by Frey and Kraut’s laboratories in Germany [4]. Subsequently, in 1960, Elliott et al. at Roche Laboratory discovered that trypsin-mediated digestion of bovine plasma globulin yields a bioactive substance capable of reducing blood pressure and inducing slow contraction in the guinea pig ileum, which they termed “bradykinin” [5].

In humans, the KNG gene encodes low molecular weight kininogen (LMWK) and high molecular weight kininogen (HMWK), which are predominantly synthesized in the liver but also expressed at lower levels in other tissues such as the brain, heart, lungs, and kidneys. Under the action of tissue or plasma kallikreins, KNG is cleaved to produce BK, a nonapeptide that is rapidly degraded into inactive fragments by endopeptidases (e.g., angiotensin-converting enzyme) [12]. BK exerts its physiological functions largely via two G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), B1R and B2R, promoting dilation of microvessels and small arteries, increasing vascular permeability, lowering blood pressure, and inducing prostaglandin release, thus contributing to redness, swelling, heat, and pain during inflammatory responses (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Key components of the KKS and the process of BK production. Studies have shown that in peripheral tissues subject to trauma or infection, BK promotes inflammatory cell infiltration, edema, and pain through B1R [13]. Under pathological conditions (e.g., cytokine activation, oxidative stress, tissue injury), B1R expression is upregulated via the NF-κB pathway, and B1R activation further drives NF-κB activity, boosting IL-1β and TNF-α levels in a deleterious positive feedback loop that exacerbates inflammatory and chronic pain [14]. Pharmacological studies have shown that B1R antagonists effectively block both acute and chronic inflammatory pain, and B1R upregulation is commonly observed in autoimmune (e.g., asthma, rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis), infectious, and cardiovascular, renal, and intestinal inflammatory diseases. By contrast, B2R is constitutively expressed in healthy tissues and is involved in modulating normal cardiovascular and renal function. It not only mediates most of the physiological effects of BK, but also displays antiproliferative, antihypertrophic, antifibrotic, antithrombotic, and antioxidant properties. Nevertheless, excessive B2R activation can contribute to inflammatory disorders such as angioedema, septic shock, stroke, and hypertension, wherein partial antagonism of B2R can yield therapeutic benefits.

Overall, KKS plays an essential role in the blood complement system, pain modulation, and inflammatory processes, with kinins participating in chronic inflammation via B1R and B2R. Recent work suggests that KKS intimately relates to cellular inflammation, oxidative stress, proliferation, senescence, pain sensitization, and angiogenesis [15]. Our team recently reported the first evidence of B2R expression in nucleus pulposus cells and showed that inflammatory and oxidative stimuli can induce B1R upregulation [10, 13]. Hence, kinins and their receptors B1R/B2R appear to be potential therapeutic targets in the management of lumbar disc degeneration.

Factors causing IVDD

IVDD underlies a variety of musculoskeletal and spinal disorders, including disc herniation, spinal stenosis, spinal instability, radiculopathy, and myelopathy [16]. Major pathological features of IVDD include production of proinflammatory mediators (e.g., tumor necrosis factor-α [TNF-α], interleukin-1β [IL-1β]), progressive loss of ECM, increased cellular senescence and death, phenotypic changes in healthy disc cells, and decreased numbers of active cells [17]. These changes culminate in compromised disc function. The onset of IVDD stems from numerous, interwoven factors—mechanical injury, inflammation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, senescence, and genetic or environmental contributors [18]. Together, these processes degrade the disc’s structural integrity and propel IVDD progression. KKS in cellular pathology

KKS and mechanical stress

Mechanical injury is widely regarded as a principal external cause of IVDD. During daily activities, intervertebral discs endure continuous compressive, shear, and tensile loads. Chronic heavy lifting, poor posture, or repetitive motions can subject the discs to forces that exceed their structural tolerance, precipitating annular tears and nucleus pulposus herniation [16]. Excessive mechanical loads and repetitive micro-injuries (e.g., heavy manual labor, obesity, smoking, improper bending posture, insufficient exercise) can exacerbate cell death and accelerate ECM depletion. For instance, increased proteoglycan breakdown diminishes the disc’s water content and alters its biomechanical properties, contributing to cumulative structural damage and further dehydration of the nucleus pulposus (NP), thereby forcing the annulus fibrosus (AF) to bear greater compressive load from the adjacent vertebrae [17, 19]. These processes ultimately weaken disc stability and elasticity, predisposing it to further degeneration [18].

Recent findings indicate that G protein–coupled receptor 35 (GPR35) is highly expressed in degenerated NP tissues [20]. Mechanical compression can induce intracellular calcium elevation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in NP cells via GPR35, leading to oxidative damage. Excessive mechanical stress can further promote NP cell ROS generation through GPR35 in a detrimental positive feedback loop that exacerbates IVDD [16, 20]. Given that B1R and B2R are also G protein–coupled receptors closely associated with calcium and ROS regulation, inhibiting GPR35 by modulating BK receptors may represent a therapeutic avenue for IVDD.

Mechanical stress is also closely associated with ion channels. Piezo1, a mechanosensitive ion channel, links mechanical stimuli with biological signaling events implicated in IVDD and exhibits significantly increased expression in degenerated NP tissue. Excessive mechanical stress can induce Piezo1 overactivation, causing Ca2⁺ influx that activates ERK1/2 and Drp1, resulting in mitochondrial damage, inflammation, and eventual NP cell apoptosis [21]. Research has shown that BK can mitigate mechanically induced cardiac hypertrophy by inhibiting the Ca2⁺/calcineurin (CaN) pathway, suggesting that targeting KKS to modulate Ca2⁺ handling may have therapeutic potential for IVDD [22].

Transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4) is another key channel that regulates cell permeability and mechanotransduction in chondrocytes [23]. BK receptors sensitize TRPV4 channels and contribute to mechanical hyperalgesia; selective B1R and B2R antagonists can alleviate TRPV4-induced hyperalgesia, mirroring the effects of TRPV4 blockade [23]. Given that nucleus pulposus cells share chondrocyte-like characteristics, targeting TRPV4 channels by intervening in BK receptor signaling may offer novel therapeutic strategies for IVDD.

KKS and cytokines

Cytokines are instrumental in the pathogenesis of IVDD. In the early stages of disc cell injury, the release of proinflammatory factors (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α) triggers an inflammatory cascade and supports chronic inflammation locally. These factors upregulate matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and ADAMTS, thereby exacerbating collagen and proteoglycan degradation in the ECM and ultimately destabilizing disc architecture. Notably, IL-1β and TNF-α are among the most pivotal proinflammatory cytokines, driving a host of inflammatory processes and enhancing ECM catabolism in IVDD.

KKS and inflammation

Interleukin

IL-1β is a typical representative of many inflammatory factors, which can accelerate cell senescence, induce apoptosis and pyroptosis of IVD cells, amplify inflammatory response, stimulate ECM degradation, regulate IVD cell proliferation, aggravate oxidative stress, promote angiogenesis and new nerve innervation. etc., accelerating the progression of IVDD [24]. Mice lacking a natural inhibitor of the IL-1 receptor develop spinal abnormalities similar to those associated with IVDD in humans. Exposure of degenerating disc cells to IL-1β stimulated the production of IL-17, which was significantly increased in more severely degenerated human discs. Exposure of human AF and NP cells to IL-1β significantly increased IL-8 and IL-6 levels and upregulated inflammatory molecules [25]. IL-6 binds to its soluble receptors and in turn promotes IL-1β-induced proteoglycan catabolism, forming a positive feedback loop that further amplifies the inflammatory response in the IVD.

In studying the relationship between kallikrein and interleukin, the research team found that there was a significant positive correlation between plasma KLK1 levels and IL-6 levels in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) [24]. Most of the biological functions of KLK1 are mediated by kinin receptor signaling, which upregulates the expression of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-8. Further studies showed that recombinant KLK1 activated the phosphorylation of p38 and p42/44 MAPK signaling proteins and significantly upregulated the mRNA and protein expressions of IL-6, CCL-2, IL-8, and ICAM-1. In addition, KLK1 was upregulated under high glucose conditions, and receptor blocking by specific antagonists activated the p42/44 and p38 MAPK signaling pathways, attenuating the production of inflammatory factors IL-8 and IL-6 stimulated by KLK1, respectively [26].

B1R antagonism or deficiency has a protective effect against a variety of inflammatory diseases, including diabetic septic shock, acute renal inflammation, organ damage, lethal thrombosis, renal ischemia–reperfusion injury, and cardiovascular and retinal inflammatory diseases [12, 27–31]. KKS is involved in inflammation (IL-1β) and neovascularization in the human and rat retina [31–33], and B1R inhibition reverses diabetes-induced inflammation (IL-1β) in the vessels and retina [28, 34, 35]. In mice, genetic deletion of B1R or administration of its antagonist reduced the expression of proinflammatory mediators and increased anti-inflammatory mediators. In addition, B2R has anti-inflammatory effects. BK can reduce the expression of multiple inflammatory mediators through B2R activation and offset the inflammatory (IL-1β) response in the myocardium and brain [36, 37]. Thus, B2R has a dual role in inflammation, whereas B1R is primarily involved in inflammatory responses, particularly inflammation triggered by cytokines or pathogens. Because B1R is a potent activator of inducible nitric oxide synthase iNOS and NADPH oxidase, it is associated with vascular inflammation, increased vascular permeability, insulin resistance, endothelial dysfunction, and diabetic complications [38].

Studies have shown that BK stimulates the transcription of IL-1β gene in WI-38 cells [39]. It has been shown that when fibroblasts were pretreated with IL-1β and then incubated with BK, the stimulation of PGE2 synthesis was significantly increased [39]. BK is inferred to participate in a positive feedback loop in which BK activates macrophages to release potent inflammatory cytokines; these in turn amplify the responsiveness of BK target tissues [40]. Studies have found that not only does BK affect the production of interleukins, but BK receptors also have a certain impact on the production of interleukins [41]. The possible role of BK in colorectal cancer cell invasion and migration experiments demonstrated that BK increased IL-6 production through the B2R and ERK pathways. Similarly, in the experiments investigating the signaling pathways involved in BK-induced IL-6 production in synovial fibroblasts, by using drug inhibitors or genetic inhibition of the BK receptor, further studies revealed that BK is involved in IL-6 production by activating B2R, leading to the activation of PLC, PKC, IKK and NF-κB, resulting in upregulation of IL-6 expression [42]. Some articles infer that BK-induced IL-8 production, in addition to relying on B2 receptor stimulation, is also partly dependent on the activation of ERK1/2 and p38 MAPK pathways [43].

Interleukin is closely related to IVDD, so further exploring the mechanism of action between KKS and interleukin will help us develop new treatment options and methods for IVDD.

Tumor necrosis factor

TNF-α is a key member of the TNF family and has pleiotropic proinflammatory effects. Injection of TNF-α via ring puncture can enhance pain response in the IVDD model [44]. In the porcine lumbar disc model, exogenous TNF-α injection led to a series of degenerative changes including annular fissures, cell cluster formation, NP matrix loss, angiogenesis, and IL-1β expression [44, 45]. Several studies have confirmed that TNF-α plays an important role in IVDD, such as inducing the production of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-8, IL-6, IL-17) in the intervertebral disc, amplifying local inflammatory responses, promoting ECM decomposition, accelerating cell aging and apoptosis. In addition, excessive TNF-α can cause neuronal apoptosis by inducing caspase signaling, damaging neuronal structure and myelin. BK can inhibit the production of NO and TNF-α in microglia stimulated by LPS through selective agonists of B1R and B2R, which may be related to the inhibition of NF-κB activation [46]. TNF-α can also enhance BK-induced increase in intracellular Ca2⁺ concentration and enhance BK-induced IP accumulation and Ca2⁺ mobilization through the Ras/Raf/MEK/MAPK pathway [47].

Kinins can stimulate macrophages to release TNF and IL-1 through B1R. Studies have shown that the release of TNF in macrophages increased 6-fold after the use of the dual selective agonist des-Arg-bradykinin, suggesting that BK can stimulate the release of TNF in an inflammatory environment through B1R [48]. In addition, TNF-α can enhance B2R expression and synergize with BK to increase IL-8 production in human gingival fibroblasts, indicating that TNF-α may enhance the inflammatory response by affecting BK-induced [Ca2⁺]i release [49]. BK and TNF-α synergistically enhance IL-6 production, and this synergistic effect is mediated through the PLA2 and COX pathways [50]. TNF-α can also upregulate the mRNA and protein expression of B1R and B2R, increase the stimulation of B1R through the p38 and JNK pathways, and enhance the expression of B2R through the NF-κB, p38, and JNK pathways [51]. BK can inhibit TNF-α and protect cell viability through its B2R and downstream MAPK and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways [52].

We intervened rat nucleus pulposus cells with IL-1β and TNF-α and found that the expression of KNG-1, B1R and B2R in nucleus pulposus cells was increased, indicating that KKS is related to the inflammation of nucleus pulposus cells. Although the specific mechanism between them has not been fully explored, the close relationship between them is undeniable, which provides a basis for exploring new treatment paths for IVDD.

KKS and oxidative stress

Oxidative stress is the result of the imbalance between the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the antioxidant system during IVD cell metabolism. With age and the accumulation of external stress, the level of ROS in IVD cells gradually increases, leading to aggravated oxidative damage. ROS not only damages DNA, proteins and lipids, but also induces the expression of proinflammatory factors and MMPs by activating signaling pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK, exacerbating inflammatory responses and ECM degradation. Long-term oxidative stress accelerates cell aging and apoptosis, weakens the structural stability of the intervertebral disc, and promotes the development of IVDD.

A large number of studies have shown that oxidative stress induces apoptosis of nucleus pulposus (NP) cells during IVD degeneration. For example, IL-1β-treated NP cells showed increased NO levels and decreased proteoglycan levels, leading to cell apoptosis; H₂O₂-treated NP cells showed increased ROS levels and increased apoptosis, while the mRNA levels of proteoglycans and type II collagen decreased[53, 54]. The antioxidant glutathione can effectively prevent the harmful effects of H₂O₂ or IL-1β and protect NP cells from apoptosis. By inhibiting the generation of ROS, rat NP cells can be protected from H₂O₂-induced apoptosis, indicating that inhibiting the oxidative stress process may be an effective strategy for the treatment of IVDD.

In our study, we subjected rat nucleus pulposus cells to a gradient intervention of different concentrations of H₂O₂. The results showed that the expression levels of KNG-1, B1R and B2R in nucleus pulposus cells increased in a time- and concentration-dependent manner, indicating that KKS is closely related to oxidative stress in nucleus pulposus cells. Cheng X et al. used a DOX-induced myocardial oxidative stress injury model and found that KNG1 was the most highly expressed core gene. In vitro and in vivo experiments further showed that overexpression of KNG1 exacerbated cardiac oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage, while knocking down KNG1 improved DOX-induced cardiomyocyte injury and exerted a protective effect through Nrf2 (an important antioxidant factor), providing a potential therapeutic target for DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. The study also found that high molecular weight kininogen (HKa) cleaved by KNG increased the production of intracellular ROS [55]. Studies on kallikrein tend to show that it can regulate the expression of oxidants and antioxidant enzymes in cells, reduce the production of intracellular reactive oxygen species, inhibit cellular oxidative stress, and thus play a protective role in the cardiovascular system [56]. The reason why kallikrein produces these effects may be that the increased kallikrein cleaves KNG to produce enough BK to play a role, because its protective effect can be blocked by BK receptor blockers or receptor knockout [57].

In HEK293 cells, B1R activates iNOS through ERK to generate NO, which reacts with superoxide anions to generate peroxynitrite, leading to apoptosis and degeneration of endothelial cells and neurons [58, 59]. B1R is associated with the activation of the iNOS pathway [60]. Peroxynitrite can activate NF-κB and B1R, increase the expression of proinflammatory mediators, and the activation of B1R further amplifies and maintains inflammatory responses and oxidative stress through positive feedback [61]. Therefore, blocking B1R to interrupt the vicious feedback pathway may be a promising treatment method [60].

A large number of studies have demonstrated the function of B2R in resisting oxidative stress and producing cardio-renal protection, which may be achieved through signaling pathways such as NO synthase, guanylate cyclase, cGMP, protein kinase G, mitochondrial KATP channel, reactive oxygen species, kinase C, ERK and Akt. Inhibiting the degradation of BK can reduce the risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in cardiovascular patients [62]. However, some studies suggest that BK activates B2R to stimulate NADPH oxidase, eNOS uncoupling, Ca 2 + pathway, etc., mediating the increase of ROS to produce oxidative stress, which is harmful to the brain, cardiovascular system, kidney and other organs. It can be seen that the role of BK in oxidative stress is complex and controversial.

KKS and cell proliferation, senescence, and death

Excessive apoptosis and cellular senescence are crucial factors in the pathophysiology of IVDD. Prolonged mechanical stress and a harsh microenvironment render disc cells more susceptible to apoptosis and senescence. Senescent cells exhibit a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), continually releasing inflammatory cytokines, proteolytic enzymes, and pro-apoptotic factors that undermine surrounding cells and the ECM [63]. This creates a deleterious microenvironment, depletes the pool of viable disc cells, and exacerbates IVDD, making it even harder to reverse. Additionally, senescence impairs the lamellar network of the annulus fibrosus (AF) and depletes proteoglycans and water from the nucleus pulposus (NP), further advancing disc deterioration.

KKS and apoptosis/proliferation

Excessive apoptosis of nucleus pulposus cells (NPC) is a key factor leading to reduced ECM production and plays a decisive role in the progression of IVDD [64]. Apoptosis is a form of programmed cell death that removes damaged or no longer needed cells without ultimately causing local inflammation [65]. Apoptotic cells often display dramatic morphological changes, including cell shrinkage, plasma membrane blebbing, and nuclear condensation and fragmentation [66]. The initiation of apoptosis depends on a group of cysteine proteases, the caspases, which are activated through both intrinsic and extrinsic pathways [66]. In the process of NP cell degeneration, both intrinsic and extrinsic apoptosis pathways play a key role. The intrinsic pathway promotes the degeneration of NP cells by regulating the levels of Bcl-2, caspase-3, collagen, and proteoglycans. Bcl-2 inhibits apoptosis by controlling the permeability of the mitochondrial membrane, preventing the release of cytochrome c from mitochondria, thereby inhibiting the activity of caspase [67]. The extrinsic pathway initiates apoptosis by activating pro-apoptotic receptors, such as tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNFR1), death receptors (DRs), and Fas on the cell surface [68].

Studies have shown that after overexpression of KNG-1 in glioma cells, the expression of caspase-3 and caspase-9 was significantly increased, the expression of anti-apoptotic proteins was reduced, and cell apoptosis was enhanced [68]. In addition, BK activated Ca2⁺ signaling by upregulating the expression and activity of TMEM16A, inducing Ca2⁺ influx and Ca2⁺ storage release in rat C6 glioma cells, and activating the phosphorylation and Expression of cyclin D1 promotes cell proliferation and migration [69, 70]. Activation of the Akt anti-apoptotic signaling pathway has been shown to effectively protect human NP cells from apoptosis [54].

The BK system is closely related to cell membrane receptors and the Akt pathway. The PI3K/Akt signaling pathway is one of the main pathways for cell proliferation and survival and is regulated by a variety of extracellular signals [71]. Studies have shown that overexpression of KNG-1 inhibits the phosphorylation of p-PI3K and Akt and suppresses cell proliferation [72]. Conversely, KNG1 may be a serum biomarker for proliferative vitreoretinopathy, which plays an important role in a variety of pathological proliferative settings [73]. Inhibition of the activity of PI3K/Akt signaling pathway significantly reduced proteoglycan matrix deposition and decreased proteoglycan gene expression in NP cells [74]. Activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway not only promotes tumor cell proliferation, but also enhances human osteoarthritis chondrocyte survival [75, 76].

Excessive ROS can activate autophagy by inhibiting the PI3K–Akt–mTOR pathway. In an isolated male guinea pig heart experiment, ROS generated by sevoflurane perfusion induced autophagy by activating AMPK and inhibiting the mTOR pathway [77]. Autophagy in the intervertebral disc is mainly mediated by two signaling pathways: mTOR and AMPK. A study showed that miR-654-5p activated the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and inhibited autophagy in NPCs by targeting the ATG7 gene and inhibiting its expression, thereby accelerating ECM degradation [18]. Xiao et al. found that BMSC-Exos promoted NPC autophagy and reduced apoptosis by inhibiting the Akt–mTOR pathway. mTOR inhibits autophagy by inhibiting Unc-51-like autophagy-activating kinase 1, while activation of AMPK promotes the phosphorylation of this kinase and initiates the autophagy cascade [77]. These studies indicate that mTOR, as an inhibitor of autophagy, and AMPK, as an activator of autophagy, can protect NP cells from apoptosis and aging and alleviate IVDD by regulating the level of autophagy [18].

KLK1 plays a role in physiological and pathological processes by activating B1R and B2R. KLK1 cleaves LMWK to generate BK, which activates B2R and then upregulates AMPK, which in turn upregulates mTOR. Activated mTOR inhibits autophagy and reduces SIRT1 activity, accelerating aging. Studies have shown that hKLK1 can improve the erectile function of elderly rats and delay the aging process by inducing autophagy by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway [78]. Previous studies have shown that hKLK1 can protect the heart and kidneys through B2R, reduce ischemic stroke damage, lower blood pressure, and improve symptoms of type 2 diabetes [78]. Liu et al. found that KLK1 protected SH-SY5Y neuronal cells by inducing autophagy under serum starvation or oxygen and glucose deprivation, involving β-catenin downregulation and activation of MEK1/2/ERK1/2 and AMPK/TSC2/mTOR pathways [79].

BK’s B2 receptor can promote autophagy under cerebral ischemic stress by mediating MAPK, ERK and AMPK signaling pathways [80]. In the ROSC rat study, BK post-treatment activated the AMPK/mTOR pathway, inhibited p62 expression, increased the expression of autophagy proteins NBR1 and LC3, and reduced S100β expression and neural injury. After detecting p-AMPK, p-mTOR and caspase-3, it was found that BK post-treatment could also inhibit the expression of caspase-3, indicating that the activation of AMPK/mTOR pathway can prevent ROSC-induced brain injury after CA by promoting autophagy and inhibiting apoptosis damage [80]. In summary, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway plays a core role in the regulation of KLK1 and autophagy. Influencing the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway through KKS can not only improve the proliferation ability of nucleus pulposus cells and regulate autophagy, but also promote ECM production. Delay IVD degeneration process.

KKS and aging

As the body ages and degenerates, IVD cells also undergo significant biological changes, including changes in the density, function, and phenotype of nucleus pulposus cells, which impair the ability to synthesize the correct matrix components and enhance catabolism, leading to IVDD. Moreover, the onset of IVDD in humans begins as early as adolescence, which suggests that we need early intervention to correct the abnormalities of reduced number of viable cells and altered cell phenotype in degenerating IVD, and to delay or reverse IVDD [81].

A reliable marker of cellular senescence is β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) [45]. Early experimental studies have shown that patients with intervertebral disc herniation have more SA-β-gal-positive senescent cells than those without intervertebral disc herniation, and the proportion of SA-β-gal-positive cells in the IVD of aged gerbils increases [82]. The p53 and p16 pathways can activate NP cell senescence, and the p53-p21 and p16 tumor suppressor pathways induce cell senescence by blocking cell cycle progression [83, 84]

In human intervertebral discs and animal models of aging, p16 expression increases with cellular senescence [16]. Knockout of Cdkn2a (encoding p16) alleviates IVDD and reduces age-related IVDD in mice by inhibiting SASP, improving matrix homeostasis, and reducing cell apoptosis [85, 86]. p16 deficiency reduces the SASP, a known initiator of inflammatory cytokine expression and matrix catabolism[85]. However, high expression of p16 in other cell types (such as macrophages) is not directly related to aging, while in articular chondrocytes, p16 is indispensable for the induction and maintenance of aging [87, 88]. p16 expression increases with age in humans and mice, but its conditional deletion does not prevent chondrocyte senescence or the development of osteoarthritis [85]. The increase of senescent cells in the intervertebral disc reduces the self-renewal ability of IVD cells, produces more matrix-degrading enzymes and inflammatory cytokines, worsens the intervertebral disc microenvironment, and promotes the progression of IVDD [18, 45]

[89] reported that treating human endothelial progenitor cells (EPC) with cleaved high molecular weight kininogen (HKa) for 72 h stimulated the phosphorylation of JNK and FOXO4, increased the intracellular H₂O₂ level of EPC cells, and accelerated aging. Other research data show that BK administration reduces the rate of EPC aging and ROS generation, while promoting EPC proliferation and NO secretion, while downregulating the expression of p16, p21, and p53. It is inferred that preventing the loss of B2R expression can prevent and treat oxidative stress-related EPC senescence [90]. Plasma HK is the main component of KKS and is responsible for the binding of the system to the cell surface. KKS is triggered in vivo by tissue destruction or thrombosis, resulting in HK being cleaved into double-chain HK (HKa) by kallikrein. HKa inhibited the clone formation and proliferation ability of EPCs, accelerated EPC senescence, and increased ROS generation and p38 kinase phosphorylation [55]

Lack of B2R exacerbates diabetic complications in Akita diabetic mice, accompanied by premature aging-related phenotypes, including hair loss, skin atrophy, kyphosis, osteoporosis, and testicular atrophy [91]. Studies have shown that exogenous BK inhibits H2O2-induced EPC senescence through the B2R/EGFR/RB and B2R/AKT/RB signaling pathway [92]. BK protects endothelial cells from H2O2-induced senescence via B2R-mediated NO release. Exogenous BK administration significantly inhibited H₂O₂-induced DNA damage and subsequent senescence in H9C2 cells by reducing ROS generation while increasing ROS scavenging dependent on B2 receptor-mediated NO release [93]. Therefore, KKS may be a key and promising therapeutic target for preventing and treating cellular senescence in IDD [94].

KKS in tissue-level changes

KKS and extracellular matrix

In healthy intervertebral discs, nucleus pulposus cells (NP) secrete abundant ECM components, and their synthesis and degradation are balanced. With the development of IVDD, the rate of ECM degradation in NP cells exceeds the rate of synthesis, leading to the occurrence of IVDD [95]. The main components of intervertebral disc ECM are type II collagen (Col II) and proteoglycans. During IVDD, abnormal expression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and type II collagen leads to reduced Col II secretion in NP, disrupting the balance of ECM. Excessive ECM destruction, especially the loss of Col II and proteoglycans, is a key factor driving the occurrence and development of IVDD [95, 96].

Studies have shown that BK at a concentration of 10–100 μg/L can promote ECM secretion. However, other studies have shown that BK inhibits ECM secretion under certain conditions [97]. For example, studies have found that BK inhibits PDGF-induced ECM secretion and ERK phosphorylation by inhibiting PDGF-induced ERK1/2 pathway activation [98]. In the experiment of studying the effect of BK on TGF-β1-induced retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cell proliferation and ECM secretion, it was found that BK inhibited TGF-β1-induced ECM deposition and MMP secretion, and HOE-140 could block this inhibitory effect, indicating that BK inhibited TGF-β1-induced ECM deposition [99]. In addition, BK activated plasminogen activator (PA) and promoted ECM degradation by inhibiting the expression of plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 (PAI-1) gene [98]. Kallikrein (KLK1) also affects ECM under certain conditions. KLK1 and its family members, such as hK4 and KLK13 are abnormally expressed in cancer and promote tumor metastasis. KLK13 is overexpressed in ovarian cancer patients and produces serine protease hK13. Studies have shown that KLK1 can degrade ECM, and hK4 can cleave fibrinogen and type I and type IV collagen, promoting local tumor spread [100]. In-depth study of the relationship between KKS and ECM will help develop new drugs or targets for IVDD treatment.

KKS and neovascularization

Studies have confirmed that neovascularization occurs in degenerated IVDs, and neovascularization has a dual effect on IVDs: on the one hand, it grows along the ruptured endplate and annulus fibrosus, providing nutrients for nucleus pulposus cells and promoting NPC proliferation and ECM synthesis; on the other hand, neovascularization can bring extradiscal substances, destroy the relatively closed immune-privileged environment of nucleus pulposus cells, and promote accelerated IVD degeneration [17].

The contribution of kinin receptors to neovascularization has been widely studied in various models and diseases. In some vascular diseases, drugs are used to inhibit neovascularization (i.e., cancer, neovascular retinopathy, etc.), while in other vascular diseases (e.g., ischemia), treatment aims to stimulate neovascularization. Therefore, both activation and inhibition of kinin receptors are important drug targets for vascular diseases. For example, activation of B1 and/or B2 receptors may be beneficial in neovascularization and angiogenesis in diabetic mice, renal ischemia/reperfusion injury, diabetic nephropathy, and brain and cardiac ischemia [101–107]. B1R deficiency or antagonism was shown to impair neovascularization, whereas B1R agonists enhanced the growth of collateral vessels in ischemic skeletal muscle and accelerated the rate of perfusion recovery in a diabetic mouse hind-limb ischemia model. Several diseases have been ameliorated by inhibition of kinin receptors, and cancer is one of them [108]. In fact, the role of kinin receptors in promoting angiogenesis has been supported by many experimental studies using cancer cells/tissues. For example, B1R activation has been shown to increase IL-4 and VEGF production by human keratinocytes and stimulate endothelial cell migration, thereby promoting neovascularization. Blockade of B1R or B2R reduced tumor vascularization in vivo and significantly inhibited the proliferation and migration of colorectal cancer cells in vitro. In summary, these studies suggest that kinins play a key role in angiogenesis through B1R, B2R, or both [108]. The dual beneficial and detrimental effects of kinin receptors raise questions about the therapeutic value of B1R/B2R agonists or antagonists in various diseases [109]. Therefore, the two-sided nature of kinin receptors needs to be carefully addressed in each pathological setting. The discovery that KNG-1, a component of KKS, is expressed in nucleus pulposus cells has prompted many researchers to address their physiological and pathological roles, especially in the treatment of IVDD diseases.

In addition, both B1R and B2R activation increase vascular permeability. Phipps and Feener [110] proposed that these KKS components penetrate from the systemic circulation with increased vascular permeability and retinal vascular hemorrhage, leading to retinal interstitial and vitreous infiltration and ocular diseases. This may also occur in the intervertebral disc, leading to discopathy.

KKS and nerve ingrowth

Chronic inflammation may increase the infiltration of blood vessels and nerves in the intervertebral disc tissue, causing abnormal pain sensitivity in the intervertebral disc that is originally without blood vessels and nerves, thereby further aggravating the pain symptoms caused by degeneration. Nerve growth factor (NGF) is a neurotrophic factor that plays a key role in pain mechanisms [45]. It is known as a trophic factor for sympathetic and sensory neurons and can promote the survival, development, and growth of neurons. This can cause some neurons to degenerate. NGF is released after tissue inflammation or nerve damage and affects the development of hypersensitivity and persistent pain through multiple signaling pathways [111]. Recent animal model studies have shown that the expression of NGF in the intervertebral disc tissue increases significantly after intervertebral disc injury, and this phenomenon is manifested in both inflammatory and non-inflammatory states, indicating that NGF is not only regulated by intervertebral disc inflammatory factors, but also by anti-inflammatory factors of adjustment [112]. In addition, the study found that under NGF stimulation, the expression levels of Chi3l1, Lcn2 and Mmp3 in rat cells and human tissues increased. Therefore, it can be speculated that NGF may have a potential effect on the turnover activity of the matrix, thereby disrupting the balance between catabolism and anabolism of intervertebral disc cells in an adverse way that aggravates IVDD. Therefore, anti-NGF therapy may help slow down tissue breakdown during disc degeneration and effectively relieve pain [113].

Studies have found that endogenous factors produced by tissue damage enhance the perception of pain by increasing the response of sensory nerve endings to noxious stimuli. For example, BK stimulates phospholipase C (PLC) in primary afferent neurons by activating G protein-coupled receptors (B2R), and nerve growth factor (NGF) stimulates tyrosine kinase (TrkA) receptors. Signaling pathway [114]. Studies have shown that endogenous NGF increases the sensitivity of DRG neurons in AI rats to BK, indicating that NGF-induced plasticity changes in primary afferent neurons may be one of the mechanisms of hyperalgesia [115]. In another experiment, in the medium with BK, NGF-cultured neurons showed significant depolarization, action potential bursts, and Ca2⁺ elevation, while neurons cultured without NGF only showed Slight changes [116]. BK stimulates calcium influx in PC12 cells through receptor-activated non-selective cation channels, and NGF enhances this effect of BK. Further studies found that BK first inhibits M current, activates L-type calcium channels, and then activates Maxi-K channels, limiting cell membrane depolarization and preventing action potential discharge [117]. Under NGF culture conditions, Ca2⁺ influx through L-type calcium channels is reduced, causing BK to depolarize neurons enough to cause action potential firing, resulting in Ca2⁺ entering the cell through N-type calcium channels and a large increase in cytoplasmic Ca2⁺. Furthermore, BK promoted the spontaneous release of NGF in the human urothelial cell line UROtsa [118].

In DRG, B2R has been shown to be strongly upregulated after NGF treatment due to an increase in the number of B2R binding sites and a physiological response to BK [119]. This was confirmed in an experiment where a B2 antagonist was found to inhibit the upregulation of NGF [111]. Induced increases in B1R mRNA levels are associated with neurodegeneration, whereas B2R appears to have neuroprotective effects [111]. Another experimental data showed that B2R plays a crucial role in the neuroprotective activity of NGF. Electrophysiological experiments also confirmed that B2R plays a crucial role in the neuroprotective activity of NGF [120]. The experiment blocked the effect of NGF by HOE140, indicating that the enhancement of LTP by NGF is significantly mediated by B2R. Upregulation of NGF via activation of the B2R is therefore critical for post-exercise mechanical hyperalgesia [121].

Liu et al. [122] activated the AMPK signaling pathway in dorsal root ganglion neurons by intraperitoneal injection of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activator, inhibited mTOR signaling, and alleviated LDH-induced radiculopathy, indicating that inhibiting mTOR signaling can alleviate IVD protrusion caused nerve root pain. In addition, tissue kallikrein upregulates mTOR through B2R-activated AMPK. It is inferred that activating the AMPK signaling pathway through B2R to inhibit mTOR signaling may become a new direction for alleviating IVD.

The latest studies have found that NGF concentrations in IVD are elevated, and anti-NGF therapy has been shown to alleviate pain caused by IVDD [123]. Therefore, subsequent studies can try to use KKS as an entry point to affect NGF through KKS and alleviate the pain of IVDD patients.

KKS and clinical manifestations

KKS and nutrition

The IVD microenvironment is mainly avascular, characterized by high acidity, low glucose, hypoxia, and hyperosmolarity, and is always subject to nutritional restriction, with its ECM homeostasis mainly maintained by a small number of cells [124]. Studies have shown that diabetes is one of the predisposing factors of human IDD, and hyperglycemia causes premature aging of NP cells by increasing ROS levels and NF-κB pathway activation. Studies have confirmed that high blood sugar levels or hyperglycemia accelerate aging and degeneration in disc cell cultures and diabetic rats [125, 126]. Reduced nutrient supply has been shown to negatively affect the IVD’s ability to maintain ECM. Reduced oxygen concentration and pH have been found in degenerative discs. And malnutrition inhibits the IVD’s ability to respond to increased load or injury. Structural damage also accumulates over time, further propagating the degenerative cycle [6].

Obesity is considered to be an important risk factor for degenerative processes. Literature suggests that BMI > 25 kg/m2 is an independent risk factor for the development of imaging evidence of DDD, and obesity at a young age is an important risk factor for increased number of disc degeneration in the future. Another hypothesis states that obesity-related atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease are homologs of spinal atherosclerosis that lead to degenerative cascades. Other studies have shown that obesity is associated with significantly increased levels of systemic IL-6 and proinflammatory cascades, which may lead to inflammation-mediated disc degeneration pathways [19]. Studies have found that hypoxia and stress stimulation can activate the AMPK signaling pathway and phosphorylate Raptor, thereby reducing mTORC1 activity through allosteric inhibition, thereby regulating cellular energy metabolism and promoting cell survival [122].

An important physiological effect of BK is related to glucose transport. Studies have shown that under conditions of normal glucose tolerance and poor glycemic control, exercise-induced plasma BK concentrations increase only in individuals with good glycemic control. BK has been shown to increase insulin in rat and human adipocytes and stimulate glucose uptake. It has been documented that BK can induce browning of white adipose tissue and thermogenesis of brown adipose tissue, and increase the expression of uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1) in adipose tissue [127]. Studies have shown that two different KNG genes, KNG1 and KNG2, are expressed in mouse adipose tissue [127]. KNG2 is only present in mice and has a significant effect on the thermogenic regulation of brown adipose tissue, while KNG1 is only slightly enhanced after thermogenic stimulation. It has been reported that B1R activation promotes the expansion of adipose tissue and leads to metabolic and inflammatory disorders in obesity, indicating that BK can enhance the production of adipose factors, including proinflammatory cytokines, at a certain peptide concentration and in the presence of kininase inhibitors that may degrade the peptide. Therefore, it can be concluded that the effect of BK on energy homeostasis is not only related to the regulation of glucose metabolism, but also to the regulation of lipid metabolism in adipocytes. Studies linking KNG1 polymorphisms to adiponectin levels have shown that KKS has a regulatory effect on adipose factors and show that B1R and B2R also have a regulatory effect on adipose factor levels. Loss of B1R and B2R function or loss of B2R function leads to insulin resistance or decreased insulin levels in mice. Activation of KKS may help reduce the severity of complications of B1R and B2R-mediated diabetes in mice. Studies by Frigolet et al. [128] reported that the B2R/NO pathway improves glucose uptake by activating PKG in adipose tissue. It was confirmed that after inducing glucose uptake in synergy with insulin, the B2R signaling pathway can improve body weight or maintain body weight stability, and can reduce insulin-induced lipid storage, indicating that the B2R/NO pathway is an important regulator of glucose oxidation in insulin-sensitive tissues, thereby achieving B2R signaling-induced glucose fate transformation. Another interesting aspect about B2R signaling and glucose metabolism is that a disturbed metabolic environment (such as obesity, diabetes, insulin resistance, etc.) can lead to a blockage of the B2R/NO pathway in glucose uptake. These changes observed in human patients with type 2 diabetes are similar to those occurring in animal models of type 1 diabetes, and it can be inferred that blood glucose disturbances under basal conditions can weaken B2R signaling [129]. Literature has shown that KLK also activates proteolytic processes related to tumor phenotype and tumorigenesis [130]. In short, BK can enhance glucose uptake in adipocytes, reduce plasma free fatty acid concentrations, reduce hyperinsulinemia, improve glucose tolerance, increase insulin action in target tissues, and enhance glucose transport through exercise (insulin-independent pathway [131]. This can be used as an entry point to improve the IVD microenvironment and become a potential target for alleviating IVDD.

KKS and genetics

Genetics is one of the indispensable factors causing IVDD. Well-known twin studies have shown that about 70% of IVDD patients show genetic variation [18]. This indicates that disc degeneration has familial aggregation. Other genetic polymorphisms that have been found to cause IVDD are polymorphisms of genes encoding vitamin D receptor (VDR) and GDF5 [18]. In a study of Finnish identical twins, VDR was the first gene reported to be associated with IVDD, and alleles of TaqI and FokI polymorphisms were associated with reduced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) signals in thoracic and lumbar discs [132]. Studies have shown that the Tt genotype of the TaqI polymorphism is more associated with multi-segment disc disease, severe disc degeneration, and disc herniation than the TT genotype. The replication of the TaqI polymorphism in three different ethnic groups showed that VDR is the most robust associated gene for IVDD identification [133]. It has been stated that polymorphisms of VDR, COL9, ACAN, MMP3, ASPN, IL1, and IL6 have been validated in more than one ethnic population, indicating that they have the broadest association with disc degeneration, making them logical targets for intervention using gene therapy [134]. It has been stated in the literature that new candidate genes may no longer be limited to ECM molecules, and that clues to candidate genes may come from rare genetic diseases in human chondrodysplasia and mouse models of spinal involvement. For example, it has been reported that mutations in the chondroitin 6-O-sulfotransferase-1 (C6ST-1) gene (CHST3) can lead to severe spinal involvement. Changes in GAG sulfation can lead to changes in disc hydration, making CHST3 a good candidate for IVDD [132]. Genes encoding matrix-degrading enzymes (such as TIMP-1, TIMP-2, MMP-1, MMP-3, MMP-9) are associated with the onset and progression of IVDD. Other studies have shown that specific variants in genes such as MMPs, COL1A1, and COL9A2 increase an individual’s susceptibility to disc degeneration, making them more susceptible to mechanical injury, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses [18].

Experiments have found that BK stimulates the production and secretion rate of TIMP-1 through the autocrine activation of TGF-β1 [135]. In addition, the increase in TGF-β1 and TIMP-1 production caused by BK involves the activation of the MAPK pathway. Tissue kallikrein (TK) is a potent vasoactive peptide called kinin. It is a serine protease that can specifically process low molecular weight kinins [136]. TK has been shown to directly activate the kinin B2 receptor. Experiments on cultured proximal tubular cells showed that TK significantly blocked ANG II-induced ROS production and NADH oxidase activity through the formation of NO, and promoted matrix degradation by inhibiting TIMP-2 and PAI-1 expression and enhancing the activity of MMP-2 [137]. Many studies have determined that BK stimulation induces the release of MMPs in different tissues. B1R is reported to be involved in mediating the expression levels of MMP-2 and MMP-9, and B2R can regulate the secretion of MMP-9 [138, 139]. Studies have shown that KLK and MMP may interact in physiological and pathological conditions mediated by BR signaling, such as TB. For example, KLK12 can regulate the expression of MMP-1 and MMP-9 through the BR/ERK signaling pathway in Mycobacterium bovis infection [140]. In multiple studies, genetic variation in cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β has been associated with susceptibility to IVDD. Some genes have been confirmed to be associated with IVDD through genome-wide association studies (GWAS), transcriptomic approaches, and epigenetic analysis. Notably, GWAS has identified genes such as TNFSF11, FN1, MAPK8, EGFR, GATA3, COL1A2, COL5A2, SPARC, COL3A1, TIMP1, and TGFB1 as being significantly associated with an increased risk of IVDD [18]. Studies have shown that kallikrein reduces I/R-induced TGF-β1 levels and Smad2 phosphorylation through kinin B2 receptor activation. Smad2 activation is the initial and key step in activating TGF-β1 signal transduction. Finally, the experimental results confirmed that NO-mediated kallikrein can inhibit the activation of TGF-β/Smad 2, thereby improving ventricular remodeling [141]. In summary, it is inferred that KKS-mediated regulation of related genes can become a potential idea for the treatment of IVD.

Environmental factors such as smoking, obesity, poor posture, and unhealthy lifestyles such as long-term sitting, by reducing the nutritional supply of the intervertebral disc, aggravating local inflammatory response or increasing mechanical load, are interrelated with genetic susceptibility and affect the progression of degenerative disc disease, which provides new ideas for the clinical management and prevention of IVDD and strengthens our understanding of environmental etiology and genetics [45].

KKS and pain

IVDD is commonly regarded as a leading cause of chronic low back pain, although the correlation is not always absolute. Other spinal structures—such as the disc interior (internal disc disruption, IDD), facet joints, ligaments, and nerve roots—can also generate pain. Notably, disc herniation compressing nerve roots and endplate damage frequently contributes to discogenic pain, with nociceptors in these regions responding to inflammatory and mechanical stimuli [142].

Wahlen et al. identified KNG-1 as a plasma protein highly correlated with pain intensity [143]. Ghafouri B et al. reported that plasma KNG-1 levels were elevated in farmers with musculoskeletal pain [154]. Cleavage of KNG-1 yields BK, a recognized pain mediator that amplifies inflammation and pain perception via nitric oxide (NO) and prostaglandin synthesis. The transient receptor potential ankyrin 1 (TRPA1) ion channel, highly expressed in sensory neurons, is a key mediator in pain models. It is co-expressed with kinin receptors, and its activity may be modulated by G protein-coupled kinin receptors (GPCRs). Upon receptor activation, phospholipase C (PLC) is stimulated to generate intracellular mediators such as protein kinase C (PKC), both of which sensitize TRPA1 channels. In addition, B2R activation increases TRPA1-dependent Ca2 + 2 + influx in sensory neurons, and intracellular sensitization of TRPA1 can mediate the nociceptive actions of kinins. Antagonists of bradykinin (BK) receptors reduce cisplatin-induced mechanical allodynia [144]. One study further demonstrated that antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) targeting BK receptors suppress pain signal transmission in the spinal cord. Beyond chronic inflammation, the excitatory actions of kinins within pain circuits are associated with maladaptive mechanisms—peripheral and central sensitization—that disrupt endogenous pain modulation and thereby alter pain perception. Consistently, administration of BK receptor antagonists (HOE-140 and DALBK) attenuates nocifensive behaviors, supporting the notion that inhibiting BK receptor excitability may offer a therapeutic avenue for neuropathic pain [145].

In a sciatic nerve pain model, oral administration of a B1R antagonist reduced heat hyperalgesia in rats. Additional studies have shown that pharmacological blockade using selective B1R or B2R antagonists markedly alleviates post-fracture pain. Notably, the COX-1/COX-2 inhibitor ketoprofen fails to exert analgesic effects in the absence of BK receptor involvement, and separate evidence indicates that activation of B1R and B2R contributes to tibial fracture–induced pain via downstream COX-1/COX-2 mechanisms [146]. These findings suggest that receptor-mediated pain depends on COX signaling, and that interrupting B1R and B2R pathways may represent an effective strategy for treating post-fracture pain. Clinically, COX-2 inhibitors (e.g., celecoxib) are routinely used to alleviate inflammation and pain in IVDD, and experimental data suggest that BK via B2R can engage NO and COX-2 pathways to enhance prostacyclin production [147].

In addition, transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) constitutes another key nociceptive signaling pathway. It transduces painful stimuli and is hypothesized to contribute to post-fracture pain, although its actions appear to be partially independent of B1R and B2R activation. In a rat model of osteoarthritis, administration of a B2R antagonist achieved approximately a 50% reduction in pain, with an analgesic effect that was more potent and longer-lasting than that of icatibant. The study also reported that a B1R antagonist attenuated hind-limb nociception and accelerated postoperative recovery in the same model. Furthermore, mice subjected to partial meniscectomy or destabilization of the medial meniscus exhibited pain-related behaviors in assays of mechanical and cold sensitivity, analgesimetry, paresis, and forced flexion. Consistent with these phenotypes, the expression of B1R, B2R, and other pain-related genes was upregulated in joint tissues from mice displaying pain-related behaviors. (Tibial post-fracture pain is reduced in kinin receptors deficient mice and blunted by kinin receptor antagonists). Additionally, other studies have reported that increased levels of KNG‑1 and bradykinin (BK) in the synovial fluid of patients with rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis further support the involvement of the KKS in inflammatory pain [148].

Thus, KKS not only partakes in the pathological processes of IVDD but also contributes significantly to pain generation and maintenance (Fig. 5). Summary of key KKS targets and functions

The KKS engages multiple pathological processes in IVDD via kallikreins and the kinin receptors B1R and B2R, with inflammation and oxidative stress at the core. B1R is inducibly upregulated under pathological conditions; its activation markedly promotes inflammation, pain, oxidative stress, and neovascularization, often accelerating disease progression. B2R is broadly expressed under physiological conditions and typically confers cardioprotective, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective effects; however, excessive activation may paradoxically trigger inflammation and oxidative stress. The two receptors thus exhibit context-dependent functional duality—particularly in inflammation, oxidative stress, and angiogenesis—where their actions may overlap or oppose each other depending on disease stage, tissue microenvironment, and agonist concentration.

Corresponding therapeutic strategies may include: administering B1R antagonists during pathological phases characterized by B1R upregulation to suppress inflammation and pain; finely tuning B2R activity (agonism or antagonism) according to disease stage and dose to maximize protection while avoiding overactivation; using KLK inhibitors to indirectly modulate the KKS by reducing bradykinin generation, with a strong emphasis on safety evaluation; and leveraging ACE inhibitors to reduce bradykinin degradation and enhance B2R signaling—which are already established in cardiovascular diseases and may also confer potential benefits in IVDD.

Conclusion

Existing treatment approaches for IVDD are limited in efficacy and fail to effectively halt or reverse disc degeneration at an early stage. Inadequate intervention can cause persistent pain, potential neurological deficits, and even paralysis. Thus, identifying novel therapeutic targets and strategies is critically important. This review examines the multifaceted roles of KKS in IVDD, emphasizing its associations with mechanical stress, inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, cell proliferation and apoptosis, cellular senescence, ECM degradation, angiogenesis, nerve ingrowth, nutrient supply, genetic predispositions, and pain.

A growing body of evidence suggests that through its receptors B1R and B2R, KKS modulates several key processes underpinning IVDD, including inflammation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, senescence, and neovascularization. Although its precise mechanisms warrant further investigation, it is increasingly clear that KKS is integral to multiple IVDD-related pathologies. Future studies should delineate how the KKS is regulated at both local (disc) and systemic levels across distinct stages of IVDD. In parallel, the rational design of B1R- and B2R-selective modulators and rigorous assessment of their efficacy and safety in IVDD could provide clinicians with novel therapeutic options and enable more precise intervention strategies. Critically, there are currently no cohort studies or pilot clinical trials specifically interrogating the KKS in patients with low back pain or IVDD—a pivotal translational gap that warrants urgent, well-designed human studies to bridge preclinical insights and clinical application.
