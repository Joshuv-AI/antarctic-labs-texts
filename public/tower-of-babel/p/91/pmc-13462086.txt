Rapid monitoring of drought and salinity stress responses in wheat via potential Raman-derived biomarkers and traditional biochemical indicators
Authors: Muhammad Ahmed, Berkay Tumkaya, M. Talha Karasoy, Leyla Safarlı, E. Ulas Demircan, Arda Inanc, Feyza Guzelcimen, Bora Akgun, Ayse Sen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abiotic stresses such as drought and salinity significantly constrain the productivity of in vitro-grown wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) by disrupting its biochemical and physiological homeostasis. Rapid, non-destructive, and data-driven diagnostic approaches are therefore essential for the early detection of stress conditions and for supporting sustainable crop management. In this study, Raman spectroscopy (RS) was integrated with conventional biochemical assays to investigate wheat responses under controlled drought and salinity stress treatments. Distinct Raman spectral features associated with pigments, proteins, carbohydrates, and lipids were analyzed alongside biochemical indicators, including proline, chlorophyll, and malondialdehyde levels. Overall, the integration of RS with machine learning provides a rapid, robust, and non-invasive framework for the early detection of drought and salinity stress in wheat. Notably, Raman intensity variations observed at 737, 996, 1051, 1064, and 1518 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document} exhibited consistent spectral trends that closely mirrored changes in conventional biochemical stress markers, confirming that these spectral shifts directly reflect underlying physiological stress responses. To classify stress levels and to identify key Raman-derived biomarkers associated with each stress type, a machine learning approach was implemented, achieving a classification accuracy exceeding 85% in discriminating control, drought-stressed, and salinity-stressed plants. Furthermore, characteristic Raman bands, particularly those associated with C-H and amide vibrational modes, showed strong correlations with established biochemical indicators, underscoring their potential as reliable, non-invasive stress biomarkers. Collectively, these findings provide mechanistic insight into stress-induced structural and biochemical alterations and support the application of RS–machine learning integration for precision agriculture and resilient crop management under changing environmental conditions.

Introduction

Salinity and drought are among the most severe abiotic stress factors limiting global agricultural productivity. These stresses restrict plant water uptake from the soil, thereby inducing osmotic stress that disrupts photosynthesis, nutrient balance, and overall metabolism, ultimately leading to growth inhibition and cell death. Importantly, salinity and drought often co-occur in natural environments, creating synergistic effects that exacerbate crop yield losses and threaten global food security1.

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is one of the world’s most important staple crops, cultivated across more than 200 million hectares with an annual production exceeding 600 million tons2. It contributes approximately 20% of the global caloric and protein intake, underscoring its essential role in human nutrition and food stability3. However, wheat is highly sensitive to both salinity and drought stress, particularly during germination and early vegetative growth stages, when water imbalance and ionic toxicity severely affect morphogenesis and yield formation. This vulnerability underscores the urgent need for efficient stress-monitoring frameworks capable of providing early and accurate assessments of physiological damage and adaptation.

To this end, biochemical biomarkers have long been employed to monitor plant responses to environmental stressors. Under drought or salinity conditions, plants experience excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as superoxide anions (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {O}_{2}^{-}$$\end{document}), hydrogen peroxide (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {H}_{2}\text {O}_{2}$$\end{document}), and hydroxyl radicals (·OH). These reactive molecules cause oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, nucleic acids, and membranes, leading to cellular dysfunction or death4. Nevertheless, at controlled concentrations, ROS also serve as vital signaling molecules that activate defense and acclimation pathways, highlighting the importance of maintaining redox homeostasis for stress tolerance.

Plants possess intricate antioxidant defense systems comprising both enzymatic and non-enzymatic components to maintain this balance. Enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and guaiacol peroxidase (POX) play pivotal roles in detoxifying ROS5. Additionally, non-enzymatic antioxidants including proline, carotenoids, and phenolic compounds act as osmoprotectants and scavengers, reinforcing cellular protection mechanisms6,7. Conventional biochemical assays measuring these parameters, along with malondialdehyde (MDA) and chlorophyll levels, have been widely used as stress biomarkers to evaluate plant physiological and oxidative responses. However, such techniques are often time-consuming, destructive, and limited in sensitivity, highlighting the need for innovative, non-destructive alternatives.

In this context, Raman Spectroscopy (RS) has emerged as a promising analytical technique for plant stress assessment. RS is a non-invasive vibrational spectroscopic method capable of detecting molecular “fingerprints” by probing vibrational modes of biomolecules such as proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids8,9. The technique has recently demonstrated its potential for early, non-destructive detection of abiotic stress, nutrient deficiencies, and crop phenotyping10–12, as well as its integration with extensively studied machine learning algorithms for improved classification and prediction performance13. Despite this, its application in wheat salinity and drought stress research remains limited14,15. Integrating RS with traditional biochemical markers could thus provide a rapid, high-resolution, and sensitive diagnostic approach for plant stress phenotyping and breeding programs.

The present study aims to investigate whether RS-based biomarkers can serve as reliable alternatives or complements to conventional biochemical indicators in evaluating wheat responses to salinity and drought stresses using the experimental scheme shown in Fig. 1. To achieve this, in vitro cultures were established using NaCl to simulate salinity stress and drought stress was simulated using polyethylene glycol (PEG6000), a non-ionic, high-molecular-weight polymer that induces osmotic stress by reducing the water potential of the culture medium without penetrating plant tissues. Conventional biochemical biomarkers including MDA, chlorophylls, proline, and antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, and POX) were quantified spectrophotometrically to assess physiological and oxidative responses. Subsequently, Raman spectra were collected from the same experimental groups to identify spectral features associated with stress-induced biochemical alterations. Results

Effects of salt stress on biochemical parameters

NaCl-induced salinity stress significantly affected the regeneration and growth of wheat plantlets (Supplementary Fig. S1). The degree of growth inhibition intensified with increasing NaCl concentration and exposure duration. After 2 weeks, control plants displayed normal regeneration with vigorous shoot and root development, whereas plantlets grown under 50–100 mM NaCl exhibited a noticeable reduction in shoot height and leaf number. At higher concentrations (150–200 mM NaCl), regeneration capacity declined drastically, with plantlets showing chlorosis, reduced leaf expansion, and stunted roots. By the 4th week, the adverse effects of salinity were more pronounced. Control and low-salinity treatments (łe50 mM) maintained moderate growth and greenness, while 100 mM NaCl caused a marked decline in shoot elongation and leaf integrity. Severe salinity levels (150–200 mM NaCl) nearly halted regeneration, with most plantlets showing leaf necrosis, root suppression, and desiccation symptoms. The overall regeneration percentage dropped below 40% at 150 mM and to nearly 10–15% at 200 mM NaCl, indicating strong ionic and osmotic inhibition of morphogenic potential in wheat under prolonged saline conditions.

Salinity stress induced a concentration and duration dependent decline in all photosynthetic pigments, including chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoids (Supplementary Fig. S2). While a general decreasing trend was observed, not all differences among salinity treatments were statistically significant, particularly at higher concentrations (100–200 mM), where several groups overlapped. For example, chlorophyll a at 150 mM NaCl (week 4) was slightly higher than at 100 mM; however, these treatments shared overlapping statistical groupings and were not significantly different. These findings indicate that, although salinity stress reduces pigment content, responses may plateau under higher stress levels, with variability or partial acclimation contributing to non-linear patterns.

Raman spectroscopy of wheat leaves

Employing RS to analyze the wheat leaves that were exposed to different levels of drought and salinity revealed certain trends in biochemical changes throughout the samples. Certain peaks of interest in the wheat plant that are known to be undergoing changes in their Raman intensity due to various abiotic stresses16,17 are inspected here. The salinity levels which the leaves were subjected to were 50 mM, 100 mM, 150 mM and 200 mM NaCl concentrations, while the drought levels were defined as 20%, 40%, 60% and 80% PEG6000. Each abiotic stress was imposed upon the wheat leaves that were either 2 weeks or 4 weeks old. Discussion

Abiotic stresses such as drought and salinity impose multifaceted constraints on plant growth and productivity, disrupting metabolic, physiological, and structural processes that are fundamental to cellular homeostasis19,20. In the present study, a combination of conventional biochemical assays and Raman spectroscopic profiling was employed to characterize the stress responses of wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) exposed to increasing concentrations of NaCl and PEG6000 under in vitro conditions. The results provide integrative insight into how osmotic and ionic stresses differentially modulate oxidative metabolism, pigment stability, and antioxidant defenses21,22, and how these biochemical changes manifest as distinct Raman spectral signatures that can serve as potential biomarkers for non-destructive stress phenotyping23,24.

Salinity stress

Salinity stress induced by NaCl markedly impaired plant regeneration and physiological performance in a concentration- and duration-dependent manner. Wheat plantlets exposed to moderate (50–100 mM) salinity exhibited reduced shoot elongation and chlorosis, whereas high concentrations (\ge150 mM NaCl) led to severe growth inhibition, necrosis, and root suppression. Such responses are typical manifestations of ionic toxicity coupled with osmotic imbalance, which reduce water uptake and interfere with nutrient assimilation21,25,26.

Photosynthetic pigment degradation was among the earliest and most sensitive indicators of salt-induced injury. Chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoid levels declined significantly with rising salinity, reflecting chloroplast disintegration, pigment oxidation, and inhibited biosynthesis of tetrapyrroles26,27. A ∼70% reduction in total chlorophyll at 200 mM NaCl after four weeks underscores the extent of photooxidative stress. These results corroborate earlier findings that salt-induced accumulation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {Na}^{+}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {Cl}^{-}$$\end{document} disrupts thylakoid integrity and inhibits enzymes such as δ-aminolevulinic acid dehydratase and protochlorophyllide reductase21,28.

Concurrent increases in malondialdehyde (MDA) indicate enhanced lipid peroxidation and membrane injury, a hallmark of oxidative stress under salinity20,29. MDA accumulation paralleled the decline in photosynthetic pigments, suggesting direct ROS-mediated degradation of membrane lipids and chlorophyll-binding proteins21,30. In contrast, proline accumulation was markedly elevated, reflecting an adaptive mechanism for osmotic adjustment, ROS scavenging, and stabilization of cellular structures31,32.

Antioxidant enzymes displayed a biphasic response: SOD, CAT, and POX activities increased significantly at moderate salinity (up to 150 mM NaCl) but declined sharply at 200 mM, indicating induced defense followed by oxidative exhaustion20,29. Such patterns are consistent with the redox homeostasis model, in which ROS initially serve signaling roles that activate antioxidative enzymes, but prolonged accumulation surpasses the scavenging capacity, resulting in oxidative damage30,33. The observed decline in total soluble proteins further supports this interpretation, likely reflecting protein oxidation, aggregation, and metabolic diversion toward stress defense pathways21,27.

Drought stress

Drought stress simulated by PEG6000 induced comparable yet more intense biochemical disturbances than salinity. Increasing PEG6000 concentrations caused significant inhibition of regeneration, reduced shoot and root development, and visible leaf wilting. These phenotypic impairments are attributed to water deficit-induced turgor loss and cellular dehydration, which directly constrain cell expansion and photosynthesis22,34,35.

Chlorophyll degradation followed a concentration dependent decline, with total chlorophyll decreasing by over 70% at 80% PEG6000 after four weeks. The greater loss of chlorophyll b compared to chlorophyll a suggests selective destabilization of light-harvesting complex II (LHCII) and thylakoid photodamage under drought stress27,36. Similarly, carotenoid contents decreased by more than 60%, reflecting weakened photoprotective capacity under excessive ROS accumulation20,37.

The oxidative consequences of drought were evident in the steep increase in MDA and proline levels. MDA concentrations rose more than twofold, confirming enhanced lipid peroxidation and membrane damage under stress conditions20,29. In parallel, proline accumulation increased substantially, consistent with its multifunctional role in osmotic adjustment, membrane stabilization, and redox buffering31,32. The concurrent rise in proline and antioxidant activity suggests a coordinated biochemical defense mechanism aimed at maintaining redox homeostasis and cellular integrity under drought stress30.

Total soluble proteins decreased under drought stress, indicating both protein degradation and reduced synthesis due to metabolic reallocation toward osmoprotective compounds21,27. The activities of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POX, CAT) followed a biphasic pattern similar to that observed under salinity stress, with increases at moderate PEG concentrations (40–60%) followed by declines at higher stress levels (80%)20,29. This biphasic behavior reflects enzyme activation during the acclimation phase and subsequent inactivation under excessive oxidative stress, as reported in drought-stressed cereals22,33.

Collectively, drought elicited more pronounced biochemical and oxidative responses than salinity, consistent with the predominance of osmotic dehydration over ionic toxicity as a primary stress determinant22,34. The elevated proline accumulation and stronger antioxidant enzyme responses under PEG6000 treatments further suggest that osmotic stress is a major driver of oxidative imbalance in wheat20,31. These findings are in agreement with previous studies indicating that water deficit conditions often induce more severe metabolic perturbations than moderate salinity stress due to direct limitations on cellular hydration and turgor maintenance25,35.

Raman spectroscopic insights

RS effectively captured the biochemical alterations underlying the observed physiological responses, providing non-destructive optical biomarkers of both drought and salinity stress. Characteristic peaks corresponding to chlorophyll (737 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document}), carotenoids (1518 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document}), cellulose/polysaccharides (996–1064 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document}), and aliphatic chains ( 1437 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document}) displayed stress-dependent intensity variations and minor wavenumber shifts, reflecting pigment degradation, membrane restructuring, and cell wall remodeling.

Under drought stress, carotenoid-associated peaks (1518 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document}) exhibited strong declines at 60–80% PEG6000, paralleling the biochemical reductions in carotenoids and chlorophylls. This pattern indicates photooxidative pigment degradation driven by enhanced ROS production38. The cellulose-related peaks (996–1064 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document}) initially increased at moderate drought levels, suggesting cell wall stiffening and lignification, but declined at severe dehydration, implying polysaccharide breakdown39.

Similarly, under salinity stress, intensity reductions in chlorophyll and carotenoid bands and subtle shifts near 1518 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document} and 1156 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document} were observed, consistent with ionic-induced disruption of pigment–protein complexes. The cellulose peaks showed moderate enhancement at 100–150 mM NaCl, possibly reflecting cell wall reinforcement during acclimation, followed by decline at 200 mM, corresponding to oxidative damage and enzyme inhibition. These findings align with prior Raman analyses showing that spectral shifts in these regions serve as diagnostic indicators of abiotic stress in cereals24,40.

Machine learning-based classification using SVMs further validated the discriminative potential of Raman features. Although previous studies primarily focus on general chemometric analysis frameworks for Raman spectroscopy using representative plant datasets, our work provides an application-driven investigation by specifically analyzing wheat leaves under both salinity and drought stress conditions, enabling targeted discrimination of abiotic stress responses13. In line with previous studies demonstrating the effectiveness of classical machine learning approaches in spectroscopic plant analysis, our results indicate that SVM provides the most robust classification performance among the tested models13. This consistency suggests that SVM remains particularly well-suited for handling high-dimensional Raman spectral data with limited sample sizes. The observed differences in model performance may be attributed to the high dimensionality and potential non-linear structure of Raman spectral data, where models such as SVM are known to perform robustly, particularly in cases with limited sample sizes. Additionally, SVM-based approaches can better handle complex decision boundaries compared to simpler linear models, which may contribute to their improved classification performance in this study. High prediction accuracies (AUC \ge 0.90) were achieved across most stress levels, with perfect classification (AUC = 1.00) for 80% drought and 150 mM salinity samples. Misclassifications among intermediate drought levels (20–60%) may reflect the biochemical overlap between acclimation and injury phases. These results demonstrate that Raman spectra capture quantitative biochemical gradients, allowing precise discrimination between stress intensities and types. The consistency of these optical and biochemical markers underscores RS’s promise as a rapid, label-free diagnostic tool for early detection of abiotic stress in plants12,41,42.

A direct comparison between Raman spectral profiles and conventional biochemical assays revealed a strong correlation between spectral variations and physiological stress markers. Declines in Raman intensities at 737 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document} (chlorophyll) and 1518 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document} (carotenoids) closely mirrored the biochemical reductions in pigment concentrations, validating the potential of RS to monitor photooxidative pigment degradation non-destructively. Carotenoids exhibit characteristic Raman bands corresponding to C=C stretching vibrations in the polyene chain43. These pigments play a critical role in photoprotection by dissipating excess excitation energy and scavenging reactive oxygen species36,37. In the present study, the decrease in carotenoid-associated Raman intensity under increasing salinity and drought stress reflects reduced photoprotective capacity and increased oxidative damage. This observation is consistent with the biochemical data, which showed a decline in carotenoid content under stress conditions, indicating pigment degradation and disruption of photosynthetic stability. Similarly, the increase in MDA content under both drought and salinity stress corresponded to spectral broadening and intensity reduction in aliphatic and carbonyl-associated regions (1437 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document} and 1650 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document}), reflecting lipid peroxidation and membrane destabilization. Enhanced Raman signals at 996–1064 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document} during moderate stress levels aligned with elevated antioxidant enzyme activities and proline accumulation, indicating cell wall reinforcement and biochemical adaptation prior to stress-induced decline.

These correlations suggest that Raman spectral signatures effectively integrate the molecular events detected by biochemical assays, offering a real-time overview of pigment degradation, oxidative stress, and structural remodeling. While biochemical assays quantify discrete metabolites or enzymes, RS captures aggregate biochemical fingerprints, encompassing pigments, proteins, lipids, and polysaccharides simultaneously. This multi-parametric capability enables early stress detection before visible damage or biochemical endpoint changes occur14,44. Thus, RS not only validates traditional stress biomarkers but also extends their diagnostic reach, bridging the gap between molecular-scale biochemistry and phenotypic expression in stress physiology.

Integrative perspective

The integrative interpretation of biochemical assays and Raman spectral data provides a comprehensive model of wheat stress physiology. Both drought and salinity induced oxidative imbalance, pigment degradation, and enzymatic modulation, but drought triggered more severe osmotic and oxidative effects. Raman spectral trends mirrored these biochemical signatures, confirming that optical signals at 737, 996, 1051, 1064, and 1518 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document} correspond to key biochemical transitions, including pigment oxidation, cell wall remodeling, and lipid peroxidation.

This convergence between biochemical and Raman data establishes a framework for developing Raman-based stress biomarkers capable of distinguishing osmotic versus ionic stress mechanisms. Integrating RS with biochemical validation and machine learning thus offers a robust, non-invasive strategy for high-throughput stress phenotyping and crop resilience assessment. Future work integrating Raman spectral mapping with transcriptomic or metabolomic analyses could further elucidate the molecular pathways governing abiotic stress tolerance in cereals.

Comparative evaluation of the Raman spectral data from the present study alongside those of Kecoğlu et al.40,45 and Şen et al.46,47 reveals several consistent stress-responsive wavenumbers that may serve as candidate biomarkers for abiotic stress in wheat. The spectral regions around 996–1064 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document}, 1515–1518 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document}, and 1650 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document} were commonly identified across all studies and exhibited reproducible changes under both salinity and drought conditions. These peaks correspond respectively to cellulose and polysaccharide vibrations (reflecting cell wall remodeling and osmotic adjustment), carotenoid C=C stretching (indicating photooxidative pigment degradation), and amide I/lipid-associated bands (representing protein and membrane oxidation). The consistent modulation of these peaks under stress conditions suggests that they capture core biochemical processes such as pigment breakdown, lipid peroxidation, and structural adaptation. Although the present work does not yet confirm direct molecular correspondence between these wavenumbers and specific biochemical indicators (e.g., MDA, proline, antioxidants), their repeated appearance across independent studies highlights their potential as reliable Raman-based biomarkers. Future research integrating time-resolved Raman analysis with targeted biochemical quantification will be essential to validate these candidate peaks as robust, non-destructive diagnostic markers for plant stress physiology.

Methods

Plant material and culture conditions

Seeds of the Selimiye wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) cultivar were obtained from the Thrace Agricultural Research Institute, Edirne, Türkiye. The overall experimental workflow is summarized in Supplementary Fig. S1.

The basal culture medium consisted of the mineral salts of Murashige and Skoog48 supplemented with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.1\,\,\hbox {mg}\cdot {\hbox {L}}^{-1}$$\end{document} 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D), 20 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.1\,\,\hbox {g}\cdot {\hbox {L}}^{-1}$$\end{document} sucrose, and 0.8% (w/v) agar. To simulate abiotic stress conditions, varying concentrations of NaCl (0, 50, 100, 150, and 200 mM) were added to induce salinity stress, whereas PEG6000 was incorporated at concentrations of 0, 20%, 40%, 60%, and 80% (w/v) to impose drought stress. PEG6000 is a non-ionic, high-molecular-weight polymer that reduces the water potential of the medium, thereby limiting water availability to plant tissues and mimicking drought-induced osmotic stress.

The appropriate amounts of PEG6000 were gradually dissolved into the culture medium under continuous stirring prior to autoclaving to ensure homogeneity. Due to its large molecular size, PEG6000 does not penetrate plant cell walls, allowing it to impose external osmotic stress without causing direct cytotoxic effects49. This method is widely used in in vitro studies to simulate water deficit conditions in a controlled and reproducible manner.

Seed surface sterilization was carried out by immersing the seeds in 70% ethanol for 5 min, followed by 5% sodium hypochlorite for 20 min, and rinsing three times with sterile distilled water. The sterilized seeds were then imbibed in sterile distilled water for 2 h at 35 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C. Mature embryos were excised aseptically using sterile forceps and scalpels under laminar airflow and cultured on magenta boxes containing the respective media with or without stressors.

Cultures were incubated in a growth chamber under a 16 h photoperiod, at an irradiance of 500 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu {\hbox {mol}}\,\cdot \,\hbox {m}^{-2}\,\cdot \,\hbox {s}^{-1}$$\end{document}, and a constant temperature of 26 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C50,51. Plants were grown under in vitro conditions for 2 and 4 weeks, representing short- and long-term exposure periods. At the end of each period, plant tissues were harvested and stored at -80 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C for subsequent biochemical analyses. Each treatment (NaCl and PEG6000 concentration) consisted of three independent biological replicates (n = 3), with each replicate represented by a separate culture vessel containing multiple plantlets.

Measurement of antioxidant enzyme activities and proline content

For enzymatic assays, 200 mg of frozen leaf tissue was homogenized in an extraction buffer containing 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 1% (w/v) polyvinylpyrrolidone-40 (PVP-40), and 0.1 mM \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {Na}_2$$\end{document}-EDTA. The homogenates were centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 25 min at 4 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C, and the supernatants were used for enzymatic and protein quantification.

Total protein content was determined according to Bradford52 using bovine serum albumin as a standard. Superoxide dismutase (SOD; EC 1.15.1.1) activity was assayed by monitoring the inhibition of nitro blue tetrazolium chloride (NBT) reduction at 560 nm53. One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to inhibit 50% of the NBT photoreduction. Guaiacol peroxidase (POX; EC 1.11.1.7) activity was determined by measuring the increase in absorbance at 470 nm using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {H}_{2}\text {O}_{2}$$\end{document} and guaiacol as substrates. Catalase (CAT; EC 1.11.1.6) activity was quantified by monitoring the decomposition of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {H}_{2}\text {O}_{2}$$\end{document} at 240 nm54.

For proline determination, the ninhydrin-based colorimetric assay was employed following the method of Bates et al.55, with slight modifications. The absorbance of the chromophore was recorded at 520 nm, and the proline concentration was calculated using a standard curve generated with L-proline.

Measurement of chlorophyll and lipid peroxidation (MDA) contents

Absorbance measurements were recorded using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (model: Implen NanoPhotometer C40) at appropriate wavelengths.

Raman spectroscopy experimentation

Raman spectroscopic measurements were performed on multiple individual plantlets from each replicate. Spectra were collected from independent biological samples under each treatment condition to ensure representative and reproducible measurements.

A previously constructed lab-built RS setup was employed for the measurements. A diode laser with 500 mW power and a wavelength of 785 nm was used. The laser beam was guided to the 40X, 0.6 NA ZEISS EC Epiplan objective lens using silver mirrors and a DMLP 805 dichroic mirror (Thorlabs). The beam width was expanded and collimated to fill the back aperture of the objective lens. The laser power at the back aperture of the objective was measured to be around 60 mW. The Raman scattered photons were collected using the same objective lens and directed into the same path but in the opposite direction of the laser input. These Raman scattered photons were then separated from the main line by a dichroic mirror. The separated Raman signal was filtered with Raman edge filters and coupled to a multimode fiber of NA 0.22 and connected to the QE Pro Raman spectrometer (Ocean Insight).

A motorized XY stage was utilized (Standa-8MTF-102LS05) and interconnected with MATLAB to scan the wheat plants. A common area for all samples was defined for the time efficiency of the scans. The samples were adhered on a stainless steel metal slides and raster scanned in an area of approximately 2 × 1 mm (20 × 10 pixels) with 100 μm steps and 3 s integration time for each step. To denoise the spectra, a boxcar width of 1 pixels was used during the scans.

Preprocessing and machine learning analysis of Raman spectra

Preprocessing of the raw data began with wavelength calibration in the 785 nm laser line through Gaussian fitting. Then, the wavelength to Raman shift (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document}) conversion was performed using the fitted value of the laser wavelength. Next, all the spectra were cropped to the 660–1800 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox {cm}}^{-1}$$\end{document} range. Then, the cosmic ray spikes were removed using Whitaker-Hayes algorithm with a 3-point window. Later, the fluorescence background was corrected using iterative asymmetric least squares baseline removal before being subject to noise smoothing with a 3-point boxcar filter. Finally, each spectrum was normalized to unit vector length to enable intensity comparisons between pixels. The processed spectrum was interpolated onto a standardized integer wavenumber grid to ensure that all pixels had identical spectral axes for further analysis.

Prior to analysis, principal component analysis (PCA) followed by OPTICS clustering was applied solely for region selection to identify and exclude metal containing areas; the PCA-transformed data were not used in subsequent analyses.

To ensure our results were not reliant on a single algorithmic architecture, we compared the performance of three distinct machine learning algorithms (a linear model, a non-linear margin-based model, and a tree-based ensemble). After preprocessing the raw data, the output Raman spectra were classified using SVM, Random Forest (100 estimators) and Logistic Regression by implementing the scikit-learn and XGBoost libraries. For both stress types, the models were trained to distinguish between exactly five distinct treatment levels. The Drought models classified: Control, Drought 20, Drought 40, Drought 60, and Drought 80. The Salinity models classified: Control, Salinity 50, Salinity 100, Salinity 150, and Salinity 200.

For all models, we utilized the default settings provided by the scikit-learn library. Specifically, the SVM utilized its default Radial Basis Function (RBF) non-linear kernel with C=1.0 and gamma=‘scale’. For the Random Forest classifier, the number of trees was set to 100, with no maximum depth limit and the Gini impurity criterion used for splitting. For Logistic Regression, the default L2 regularization was applied with a regularization parameter of C=1.0.

Random undersampling was applied exclusively to the training data after the train/test split, ensuring that the test set remained unaffected and preventing any potential data leakage. Drought (Week 2: 4098 samples; Week 4: 4981 samples) and salinity (Week 2: 4564 samples; Week 4: 4572 samples) datasets, each comprising 1140 features across five classes, were split into 70/30 train/test sets; random undersampling was applied to the training data to balance classes to 431, 526, 546, and 613 samples per class, respectively, while the test sets retained the original imbalanced distributions (n = 1230, 1495, 1370, and 1372). Final hyperparameters were not selected via cross-validation; instead, default parameter settings were applied uniformly across all models. All metrics (Precision, Recall, and F1-scores, along with the exact numerical values of overall model accuracies) are provided in Supplementary Tables S2–S7.

The results of SVM can be observed in Fig. 4 while the others are given in the Supplementary Figs. S14 and S15. The samples have undergone random undersampling to have equal class sizes. The dataset was split into two sets with 70 and 30% for training and test, respectively. Performance metrics were confusion matrix and ROC curve with the AUC as shown in Fig. 4. Additionally, the accuracy results can be found in the Supplementary Figs. S12 and S13. The results of the model architecture were validated using a hold-out test set, which is a randomly selected 30% of the original data.

All machine learning models, data handling, and statistical evaluations were implemented in Python (version 3.12.10) utilizing the scikit-learn library for model development, alongside NumPy and pandas for data processing.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in a completely randomized design (CRD) with three independent biological replicates for each treatment. Each biological replicate represented the mean of three technical measurements, and the averaged data were used for all statistical analyses. For biochemical quantifications, leaves were randomly pooled from three seedlings per treatment group to minimize sampling bias. All data were expressed as mean ± standard error. Statistical analyses were performed using OriginPro 202457. For spectrophotometric and biochemical parameters, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied when single-factor comparisons were evaluated. Prior to ANOVA, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were verified using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively, to ensure the validity of the parametric analysis58,59. When significant effects were detected, Tukey’s multiple comparison post hoc test was employed to identify statistical differences among treatment means at a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

All graphical visualizations, error bar plotting, and statistical group annotations were generated in OriginPro 2024 to maintain consistency across datasets. Statistical interpretation followed best practices in quantitative plant physiology and stress biology studies1,60.
