Compatibility and stability of ciprofol with different concentrations of remifentanil or esketamine
Authors: Xi Cheng, Baoxia Fang, Qipeng Liu, Yunjia Ni, Wenya Deng, Xiang Wang, Kun Yang, Fuchao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCombination anesthesia with ciprofol, remifentanil, and esketamine enhances clinical efficacy but poses stability risks due to the emulsion formulation. Given the lack of compatibility data, this study investigated the physicochemical stability of ciprofol mixed with varying concentrations of remifentanil or esketamine.MethodsCiprofol emulsions were mixed with varying concentrations of esketamine or remifentanil at a volume ratio of 1:1. The admixtures were prepared at room temperature (25 ± 1 °C) under ambient light, and samples were collected at predefined time points (0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h) for stability analysis. To assess the stability of the mixtures, drug concentrations (measured by high-performance liquid chromatography), macroscopic and microscopic appearance, pH, mean particle diameter (MPD), polydispersity index (PDI), zeta potential, and osmolality were evaluated.ResultsOver the 8-h period, no visual changes were observed in any mixture, and drug concentrations remained between 96% and 104% of the initial value. The MPD remained stable at 205 ± 7 nm, with a uniform particle size distribution (PDI = 0.1 ± 0.03). Notably, in contrast to the esketamine group, the remifentanil group exhibited significant changes in pH and osmolality (P < 0.05), along with a significantly lower absolute zeta potential. Microscopic analysis further revealed mild droplet aggregation in the remifentanil group.ConclusionsCiprofol exhibits chemical stability and acceptable physical compatibility with esketamine or remifentanil at the tested concentrations for up to 8 h. However, given the mild physical instability (e.g., droplet aggregation) observed in ciprofol-remifentanil mixtures, prolonged storage is not recommended, because it may affect their stability.

Background

Total intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA) is the exclusive intravenous administration of anaesthetic agents to induce general anaesthesia [1]. Physical or chemical incompatibility between intravenous anaesthetics is a prevalent cause of medical errors, potentially resulting in therapeutic inefficacy or life-threatening complications. Drug compatibility must be systematically validated through rigorous scientific investigations to increase the safety of Y-site intravenous coadministration. Ciprofol, a novel γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptor agonist, has emerged as a promising anaesthetic agent. Structurally, ciprofol is an analogue of propofol (2,6-diisopropylphenol) in which the isopropyl groups at the 2- and 6-positions of the phenolic ring are replaced by cyclopropyl substituents. This modification introduces a chiral centre that enhances stereochemical interactions, resulting in a 4- to 5-fold increase in receptor binding affinity compared with that of propofol [2, 3]. Characterized by rapid induction, swift recovery, and minimal respiratory depression, ciprofol has emerged as a promising agent with expanding clinical applications in anaesthesia management and critical care settings [4]. However, in clinical practice, because anaesthetic drugs need to be infused through a single intravenous access (Y-site connector), if the drugs are chemically or physically unstable, incompatibility may occur. However, single-agent anaesthesia regimens often struggle to simultaneously meet the analgesic and sedative requirements in complex surgical procedures or for specific patient populations [5].

Remifentanil a potent short-acting µ-opioid receptor agonist, and esketamine, an N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist with analgesic and dissociative properties, synergistically complement ciprofol through multitarget mechanisms. However, as an intravenous emulsion formulation, ciprofol exhibits intrinsic thermodynamic instability, creating risks of phase separation phenomena such as flocculation, particle aggregation, and active pharmaceutical ingredient precipitation [6, 7]. When they are combined with remifentanil (with hydrophilic properties) and esketamine (requiring strict pH control), ciprofol emulsions are at risk of physicochemical destabilization through mechanisms such as pH fluctuation, ionic strength imbalance, and formulation excipient conflicts. Intravenous emulsion droplets exceeding 5 μm in diameter thereby pose pulmonary embolism risks through alveolar capillary occlusion [8, 9]. Current pharmacopeial standards stipulate that the mean particle diameter (MPD) of parenteral emulsions should not exceed 500 nm and volumetric quantification requires fewer than 0.05% of lipid globules to exceed the 5 μm threshold in the dispersed phase.

Currently, data regarding the compatibility and stability of the novel emulsion ciprofol with opioids or ketamine are lacking. To simulate clinical administration, this study systematically investigated the physicochemical stability of ciprofol mixed with varying concentrations of remifentanil or esketamine over an 8-h period. Parameters including drug concentration (determined by HPLC), macroscopic and microscopic appearance, pH, MPD, polydispersity index(PDI), zeta potential, and osmolality were evaluated to provide a scientific basis for safe clinical use. This investigation was conducted exclusively in vitro and did not involve human or animal subjects.

Methods

Drugs and reagents

A ciprofol reference standard was obtained from Liaoning Haisco Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (lot number: 1115122005; purity: 99.5%). Remifentanil reference standards were provided by the National Institutes for Food and Drug Control of the People’s Republic of China (lot number: 171260–202002; purity: 99.9%). An esketamine reference standard was obtained from Jiangsu Enhua Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China (purity: 99.6%). Ciprofol injection (2.5 mg/mL, for IV use; specification: 20 mL: 50 mg; lot number: 20230764; manufacturer: Liaoning Haisco Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Liaoning, China), esketamine hydrochloride injectable solution (25 mg/mL, for IV use; specification: 2 mL: 50 mg; lot number: 20233336; manufacturer: Jiangsu Hengrui Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China), remifentanil hydrochloride injectable solution (1 mg/mL, for IV use; specification: 1 mL: 1 mg; lot number: 20233314; manufacturer: Jiangsu Enhua Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China), 0.9% sodium chloride injection (9 mg/mL, for IV use; specification: 250 mL: 2.25 g; lot number: 23112145; manufacturer: Dalian Otsuka Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Dalian, China), potassium dihydrogen phosphate (obtained from Tianjin Bodi Technology Development Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China), and HPLC-grade acetonitrile (obtained from Tianjin Kemiou Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China) were used in this study. All the experiments were conducted at the Clinical Pharmacy Laboratory of Sinopharm Dongfeng General Hospital, Shiyan City, China, from October 2024 to December 2024.

Solution preparation

Pure drug solutions

Individual stock solutions were prepared at concentrations of 1.25 mg/mL for ciprofol, 0.1 mg/mL for remifentanil, and 0.15 mg/mL for esketamine. A 50% acetonitrile solution (v/v) was selected as the solvent to disrupt the ciprofol lipid emulsion structure, ensuring complete drug dissolution and solution homogeneity. All stock solutions were freshly prepared daily prior to analysis.

Drug–drug mixed solution

In accordance with clinical protocols, esketamine and remifentanil solutions were diluted with 0.9% sodium chloride to achieve the following targeted concentrations: esketamine at 5 mg/mL and 1 mg/mL, and remifentanil at 20, 50, and 100 µg/mL. These diluted solutions were then mixed with ciprofol emulsion at a volume ratio of 1:1 (v/v) to prepare Samples 1–2 (esketamine group) and Samples 3–5 (remifentanil group), respectively. Each sample set was prepared in triplicate under aseptic conditions, transferred to sterile syringes, and stored under indoor natural light at 25 ± 1 °C for subsequent analysis.

Negative control solution

A 0.9% sodium chloride solution without the target components to be tested was used as a blank control in the experiment to exclude interference from other substances in the test results.

High-performance liquid chromatography

Instruments and chromatographic conditions

The analysis was performed using an Agilent 1260 High Performance Liquid Chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA) equipped with a quaternary pump, autosampler, column thermostat, and variable wavelength detector (G1314C), controlled by OpenLab CDS 2.8 software. This HPLC method was employed to quantify the drug concentrations in the mixtures and assess their chemical stability. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Shim-pack GIST C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm, 5 μm) maintained at 30 °C. The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile (A) and 0.05 mol/L potassium dihydrogen phosphate (B) at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min, with detection at 215 nm and 271 nm. For the ciprofol and esketamine mixture (Condition 1), the gradient programme was: 0–3 min at 25% A; 3–5 min linear increase to 75% A; 5–12 min held at 75% A; 12–14 min returned to 25% A; and 14–24 min re-equilibration (injection volume: 20 µL). For the ciprofol and remifentanil mixture (Condition 2), the gradient was: 0–3 min at 35% A; 3–5 min linear increase to 70% A; 5–14 min held at 70% A; 14–16 min returned to 35% A; and 16–26 min re-equilibration (injection volume: 10 µL).

Validation of the HPLC methodology

Method validation included a system adaptability test, linearity, precision, stability, and recovery to ensure that the analytical results were reliable.

System adaptability test

Aliquots of the reference, mixed, and negative control solutions were analysed using the previously described chromatographic conditions. Method specificity and system suitability were evaluated by assessing parameters including retention time, theoretical plate count, and resolution between the target analytes.

Linearity

Calibration standards were prepared by diluting the stock solutions with 50% (v/v) acetonitrile to obtain the following concentration ranges: 0.0125–0.1 mg/mL (ciprofol) and 0.0125–0.15 mg/mL (esketamine) for Condition 1; and 0.25–1.25 mg/mL (ciprofol) and 0.01–0.1 mg/mL (remifentanil) for Condition 2. All solutions were analysed in triplicate under the established chromatographic conditions. Calibration curves were constructed by plotting peak areas (Y) against drug concentrations (X), and regression equations with coefficients of determination (R2) were calculated using linear least-squares analysis.

Precision

Precision was evaluated using reference solutions prepared in 50% acetonitrile at varying concentrations within the calibration range: ciprofol (0.025, 0.050, 0.100, 0.250, 0.500, and 1.000 mg/mL), esketamine (0.050, 0.100, and 0.150 mg/mL), and remifentanil (0.025, 0.050, and 0.100 mg/mL). These solutions were analysed on the same day to assess intra-day precision and over three consecutive days for inter-day precision. The RSD of the peak areas was calculated for each condition.

Stability

To evaluate solution stability, four sample solutions were prepared and diluted with 50% acetonitrile. The diluted samples were stored at room temperature and analysed at 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h using the established chromatographic method. The RSD of the peak areas was calculated to assess chemical stability over the 8-h period.

Recovery

In the recovery test, the reference solution was added to the ciprofol–esketamine mixtures and ciprofol–remifentanil mixtures with known contents (high, medium, and low concentration levels of 50%, 100%, and 150%, respectively). After thorough mixing, volume adjustment, and dilution with a 50% (v/v) acetonitrile–water solution, the solutions were injected for analysis under the established chromatographic conditions. The recovery rate of each drug was calculated as follows: the known amount was subtracted from the measured amount, and the result was then divided by the added amount. Moreover, the RSD was determined.

Investigation of compatibility and stability

Studies on drug mixtures included assessments of drug concentration, appearance, pH value, osmolality, MPD, PDI, zeta potential, and microscopic morphology, with the goal of comprehensively evaluating their physicochemical properties and stability during the observation period.

Drug concentration analysis

At 25 ± 1 °C, 1-mL aliquots were sampled in triplicate at 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h. Samples were diluted with 50% acetonitrile, sonicated to ensure complete dissolution, and filtered through a 0.22-µm membrane filter. The filtrates were analysed by HPLC to determine the concentrations of ciprofol, esketamine, and remifentanil. Results were expressed as the percentage of the initial concentration (0 h). Chemical stability was defined as maintaining 90–110% of the initial drug concentration over the 8-h period [10, 11].

Macroscopic appearance and pH value

Under ambient conditions (25 ± 1 °C), Samples 1–5 were sampled at 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h. These mixtures were visually inspected in Nessler cylinders against a black background to detect precipitation, phase separation, or colour changes. Visual compatibility was defined as the absence of any precipitation, phase separation, or colour variation [12]. The pH of the five mixtures were measured using a PHS-3 C pH meter (Shanghai Yidian Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., China). Physical incompatibility was determined if the pH deviation from baseline exceeded ± 1.0 units [13].

Osmolality

At each time point, sample aliquots (250 µL) were analysed using a Model 2020 Osmometer (Advanced, USA) based on the freezing point depression method [14]. Significant fluctuations in osmolality were considered indicative of physical incompatibility.

Zeta potential, mean particle diameter, and polydispersity index

Zeta potential, MPD, and PDI were measured at 25 ± 1 °C using a Nano ZS90 Analyzer (Malvern Panalytical Ltd, UK) after the instrument was equilibrated for 1 h. For zeta potential analysis, samples were diluted 1:5 (v/v) with purified water, whereas for MPD and PDI measurements, 5 µL of each sample was dispersed in 1 mL of purified water.

Microscopic changes

Under room temperature conditions (25 ± 1 °C), a 5 µL aliquot of each drug mixture was placed on a glass slide and covered with a coverslip to minimize air bubbles. At specific time points, droplet morphology, size, and distribution were examined using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica DMI6000B; Leica Microsystems, Germany). The images were used to evaluate the uniformity of droplet size and shape, as well as any signs of aggregation or stratification.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons were analysed using Student’s t-test or analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0 software.

Results

HPLC

HPLC analysis showed retention times of 13.0 and 5.1 min for ciprofol and esketamine, respectively, and 15.7 and 4.2 min for ciprofol and remifentanil, respectively. Method specificity was confirmed by the absence of interfering peaks in the negative control (Fig. 1). The calibration curves exhibited excellent linearity. For Condition 1, the regression equations were Y=57363x + 12.352 (R²=0.9998) for ciprofol and Y=54693x + 43.272 (R²=0.9997) for esketamine. For Condition 2, the equations were Y=6335x-31.831 (R²=0.9998) for ciprofol and Y=12919x-0.9805 (R²=0.9998) for remifentanil. Precision was confirmed with intra- and inter-day RSDs of peak areas below 2.0%. Solution stability was verified over 8 h, with RSDs ranging from 0.8% to 1.6%. Method accuracy was demonstrated by average spike recoveries of 99.6% (ciprofol) and 102.5% (esketamine), and 99.4% (ciprofol) and 100.3% (remifentanil) in their respective mixtures. Finally, chemical stability studies (Table 1) indicated that the concentration of each drug remained within 96–104% of the initial value throughout the observation period.

Macroscopic appearance and pH value

All samples remained white and homogeneous for over 8 h at 25 ± 1 °C, showing no signs of phase separation, precipitation, or stratification. In the esketamine group, Samples 1 (5 mg/mL) and 2 (1 mg/mL) exhibited stable pH levels relative to baseline (0 h). However, intergroup comparison revealed that Sample 1 had a significantly lower pH than Sample 2 (P < 0.0001). For the remifentanil group, pH stability varied across concentrations. Sample 3 (20 µg/mL) showed a significant increase in pH from baseline (5.39 ± 0.05) at 6 and 8 h (P < 0.01), whereas Sample 4 (50 µg/mL) exhibited a decrease at 2, 4, and 6 h compared to baseline (4.26 ± 0.04; P < 0.015). In contrast, Sample 5 (100 µg/mL) remained stable throughout the observation period. Notably, a significant concentration-dependent decrease in pH was observed as remifentanil concentration increased (P < 0.0001).

Osmolality measurements

Osmolality was monitored over 8 h at 25 ± 1 °C. In the esketamine group, the mean osmolality was 322 ± 1.0 mOsm/kg for Sample 1 (5 mg/mL) and 296 ± 1.4 mOsm/kg for Sample 2 (1 mg/mL). Neither sample exhibited significant changes relative to the 0 h baseline. However, consistent with the higher drug concentration, Sample 1 maintained significantly higher osmolality than Sample 2 (P < 0.0001).

For the remifentanil group, mean values were 302 ± 8.4, 305 ± 7.3, and 301 ± 2.1 mOsm/kg for Samples 3, 4, and 5, respectively. Sample 3 (20 µg/mL) showed a significant increase from baseline (294 ± 0.9 mOsm/kg) at 4, 6, and 8 h (P < 0.0001). Similarly, Sample 4 (50 µg/mL) exhibited elevated levels at 4, 6, and 8 h compared to its baseline (298 ± 0.8 mOsm/kg; P < 0.005). In contrast, Sample 5 (100 µg/mL) remained stable throughout the observation period. Notably, intergroup analysis indicated that Sample 5 maintained significantly lower osmolality than Samples 3 and 4 at the 6–8 h time points (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2).

Zeta potential, MPD and PDI

Zeta potential analysis indicated a general decrease over time. For the esketamine group (Samples 1 and 2), mean zeta potentials at 8 h were 27.1 ± 2.7 mV and 32.7 ± 3.3 mV, respectively. Both samples exhibited a significant reduction compared to baseline (0 h; P < 0.0001). Intergroup comparison revealed that Sample 1 (higher concentration) had a significantly lower zeta potential than Sample 2 (P < 0.0001).

In the remifentanil group, mean zeta potentials were 17.0 ± 1.9, 16.6 ± 1.3, and 11.3 ± 1.1 mV for Samples 3, 4, and 5, respectively. Sample 3 (20 µg/mL) showed a significant decrease in zeta potential at 6 h (15.6 ± 0.6 mV; P < 0.001) and 8 h (14.7 ± 0.6 mV; P < 0.0001) relative to baseline (18.5 ± 0.7 mV). Sample 4 (50 µg/mL) remained stable throughout the study. Conversely, Sample 5 (100 µg/mL) exhibited a significant reduction at all time points compared to baseline (P < 0.0001). Intergroup analysis indicated that Sample 5 displayed significantly lower zeta potential than Samples 3 and 4 (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3).

Regarding particle size, no significant changes in MPD or PDI were observed over 8 h for any group, nor were there significant intergroup differences. Mean MPD values were 197.3 ± 3.9 nm and 198.2 ± 2.6 nm for the esketamine group, and roughly 210 nm for the remifentanil group (ranging from 209.4 to 210.7 nm). Microscopic changes

Discussion

This study investigated the physicochemical compatibility of ciprofol with remifentanil or esketamine at different concentrations. A reliable HPLC method was established to determine the three drugs in the mixed emulsion and verify their stability. The chromatographic conditions employed the same stationary phase and mobile phase (with different component proportions). This consistency enabled us to perform simultaneous detection on the same day during the stability study, which simplified the research process and increased efficiency. HPLC chromatograms revealed well-separated peaks with good specificity. We investigated the physicochemical stability of ciprofol combined with different concentrations of esketamine and remifentanil, including conventional clinical practices and label-recommended dosage scenarios to meet actual medication needs.

According to the HPLC results, no drug content degradation was observed within 8 h, with contents maintained within 96–104%. These findings are consistent with those of Emily Henkel et al. [15], who systematically investigated the content stability of remifentanil–propofol mixtures under different pH conditions (pH 4.0–8.0) over a 0–24 h monitoring period and reported that the amide bond of remifentanil is prone to hydrolysis in neutral to alkaline environments (pH ≥ 7.0). The drug degradation rate exceeded 20% within 24 h, with degradation products posing potential toxicity risks. In contrast, when the pH of the mixture was maintained within the weakly acidic range of 4.5–5.5, the degradation rate of remifentanil was significantly reduced (< 5/24 h), and the short-term content stability markedly improved. Given the acidic pH of the ciprofol mixtures in our study, remifentanil hydrolysis was effectively minimised.

All five samples in this study were acidic, with significant differences in their pH values among the different concentration groups: the esketamine group exhibited a pH range of 5.5–6.1 without significant time-dependent changes. In contrast, compared with the esketamine group, the medium- and high-concentration remifentanil groups (Samples 4 and 5) had a lower pH range (3.6–4.2), whereas the low- and medium-concentration remifentanil groups (Samples 3 and 4) exhibited significant time-dependent pH fluctuations. Notably, despite the acidic nature of all samples, commercially available ciprofol emulsions are typically formulated as weakly alkaline (pH 8) to achieve optimal antiaggregation stability. This formulation ensures the complete ionization of emulsifiers (phosphatidylcholine and free fatty acids) and maintains a high absolute value of the zeta potential through electrostatic repulsion [16, 17]. The acidic nature of the remifentanil solution may promote droplet aggregation when it is mixed with ciprofol emulsion because of the limited buffering capacity of the latter. However, in clinical intravenous Y-site infusion scenarios, the degree of mixing between the two solutions (lipophilic ciprofol emulsion and aqueous remifentanil solution) may be partial or dynamic, potentially resulting in phase separation where the two components remain in separate lipophilic and aqueous phases. This incomplete mixing might reduce the risk of immediate droplet aggregation compared with thorough in vitro mixing. Nevertheless, even partial mixing could locally alter the microenvironment (e.g., pH fluctuations at the interface of the two phases), which may gradually induce physicochemical changes in the emulsion, thus warranting careful evaluation of their coadministration.

The zeta potential of the esketamine group gradually but significantly decreased, with an approximate difference of 5 mV between the high- and low-concentration groups, but the zeta potential of the esketamine group remained above 25 mV overall. In the remifentanil group, the zeta potential of the high- and medium-concentration subgroups (Samples 5 and 4) markedly decreased over time, particularly in the high-concentration subgroup (Sample 5), which decreased to approximately 10 mV at 8 h. Collectively, the zeta potential of the remifentanil group was much lower than that of the esketamine group, indicating a higher risk of aggregation instability [18, 19]. Furthermore, microscopic observations revealed a more concentrated distribution of emulsion droplets compared with the esketamine group; however, this potential change did not affect the particle size distribution, and no changes in dispersibility were observed over 8 h. The acidic pH observed in the mixed samples may partially reduce the ionization efficiency of the emulsifiers, which may explain the decrease in the absolute value of the zeta potential in both the esketamine and remifentanil groups over the 8-h period.

Another long-term stability study [20] provides valuable insights into time-dependent variations in the physical stability of emulsions. Dynamic light scattering was used to evaluate the zeta potential and particle size distribution as key stability parameters, and five commercially available propofol formulations were combined with sufentanil at concentration ratios of 0%, 2%, 4%, and 9% (v/v) under storage conditions of 4 °C, 25 °C, and 37 °C. The findings revealed that within 24 h after mixing, the absolute value of the zeta potential decreased in four out of the five formulations, indicating decreased electrostatic repulsion between the emulsion droplets. Nevertheless, no significant alteration in droplet size was detected, with the PDI remaining at approximately 0.1 (good dispersibility) and the absolute value of the zeta potential remaining above 25 mV, suggesting a stable emulsion system. However, starting on Day 8, the high-concentration sufentanil group (9%) exhibited a marked decrease in the zeta potential, accompanied by a progressive increase in the average particle size. By Day 14, the particle size exceeded 600 nm, and the PDI increased beyond 0.35. The concurrent increase in particle size and decrease in zeta potential support the physical stability mechanism of emulsion droplet aggregation resulting from diminished electrostatic repulsion. This study highlights that even without observable changes in particle size within the short term (e.g., within 8 h), prolonged coadministration with high-concentration fentanyl analogues may still present risks to infusion safety because of the increased potential for microaggregation.

The ciprofol formulation used in this study was composed of soybean oil, sodium hydroxide (used for pH adjustment), medium-chain triglycerides, and oleic acid/sodium oleate. The carboxyl groups of oleic acid/sodium oleate become ionised under alkaline conditions and revert to a non-ionised state when the pH decreases. Because the emulsifying properties of sodium oleate are superior to those of non-ionised oleic acid, these shifts in ionisation state may induce changes in the physical properties of the formulation. Previous studies have reported that variations in the degree of oleic acid ionisation can alter the nanostructure of dispersed systems [21], and this phenomenon may gradually contribute to an increase in droplet size.

In terms of clinical safety, Variations in physicochemical parameters have distinct clinical significance. Existing studies have confirmed that particles larger than 5 μm in intravenous emulsions may occlude pulmonary capillary beds, causing severe complications such as pulmonary embolism and microcirculatory disorders [22, 23]. In the present study, although the average particle size of all samples remained below 500 nm within 8 h, the low zeta potential and the observed tendency of emulsion droplet aggregation (as indicated by microscopic evaluation) in the remifentanil group warrant vigilance regarding the potential risk of increasing particle size during long-term coadministration. Furthermore, emulsion instability induced by pH fluctuations—via their effect on the ionization state of emulsifiers (e.g., the carboxyl ionization equilibrium of oleic acid/sodium oleate)—may result in the formation of local acidic microenvironments because of insufficient mixing at the clinical Y-site infusion interface, accelerating emulsion droplet aggregation. Therefore, in clinical practice, prolonged infusion or storage of ciprofol-remifentanil admixtures is not recommended, and improved visual monitoring during infusion (e.g., regular observation of solution clarity, avoidance of prolonged standing) should be implemented. In contrast, the more stable zeta potential and pH characteristics of the esketamine group strongly support the safety of its clinical coadministration.

Moreover, the remifentanil group exhibited significantly greater fluctuations in osmolality compared to the esketamine group, suggesting reduced physical stability. Microscopic analysis corroborated this, revealing droplet aggregation (an early sign of destabilisation) in the remifentanil mixtures. However, given that the mean particle diameter remained below 500 nm—within safe limits for intravenous infusion—these admixtures were considered clinically acceptable. Overall, no gross physical incompatibility was observed over the 8-h period, supporting the feasibility of short-term co-administration.

This study focused exclusively on physicochemical compatibility. However, potential interactions between the emulsion and infusion materials (e.g., sorption to PVC lines or containers) remain unassessed. Additionally, variations in excipients and manufacturing processes across different pharmaceutical brands may influence stability. Future investigations should therefore extend to evaluating drug-material interactions and validating consistency across diverse formulations.

Conclusions

Ciprofol exhibited physicochemical stability for 8 h when mixed with remifentanil (20–100 µg/mL) or esketamine (1–5 mg/mL). However, given the instability risks observed in the remifentanil group, prolonged pre-infusion storage is discouraged, although Y-site administration appears clinically feasible. In contrast, the esketamine–ciprofol combination demonstrated superior compatibility suitable for clinical use. These conclusions are specific to the formulations and concentrations tested.
