Revisiting the rhythm: A systematic review and meta-analysis of long-term arrhythmia outcomes after extracardiac versus intracardiac Fontan completion
Authors: Dicky Fakhri, Yohana Reena Bunyamin, Jennifer Jesslina Gunawan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStudies on the arrhythmia incidence in the Fontan population, especially comparing intracardiac (IC) and extracardiac (EC) Fontan, are limited, with inconsistent findings. This meta-analysis aims to compare arrhythmia risk in EC and IC Fontan patients. Eligible studies comparing arrhythmia outcomes following different Fontan procedures, including EC conduit (ECC), lateral tunnel (LT), and classic Fontan, were identified through a systematic search of the PubMed, Cochrane Library, EMBASE, and Google Scholar databases from 2016 to 2025. Random-effects meta-analysis was performed to pool odds ratios (ORs). A subgroup analysis based on follow-up duration and a restricted analysis comparing ECC versus LT were conducted to explore heterogeneity. Twelve publications with 6280 patients were included in the final analysis. In our systematic review and meta-analysis, the EC Fontan was associated with significantly lower odds of arrhythmia compared to the IC Fontan (OR = 0.38, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.27–0.52, P < 0.001), with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 74%). In a restricted analysis comparing only ECC with LT, the association remained significant (OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.39–0.69; P < 0.001). EC Fontan is linked to a lower observed arrhythmia burden compared to IC Fontan. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as the observed effect may partly reflect the higher arrhythmia burden in classic Fontan, as well as differences in surgical era and follow-up duration. Given the persistent risk of arrhythmia in all Fontan patients, individualized management and long-term surveillance are essential.

INTRODUCTION

The Fontan operation is a palliative procedure performed in patients with single-ventricular circulation. It was first introduced by Francis Fontan in 1971 and has since evolved from the classic Fontan to total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC), such as intra-atrial lateral tunnel (LT) and extracardiac conduit (ECC). The classic atriopulmonary (AP) Fontan separates the pulmonary and systemic circulations by creating the AP connection, bridging the superior vena cava to the right pulmonary artery (PA) and the right atrium (RA) to the left PA. Another variant of the classic Fontan establishes an anastomosis between the RA and the right ventricle (RV).[123]

Despite its success in increasing the life expectancy of patients with single-ventricular circulation, the Fontan procedure is prone to complications. One of the most clinically significant complications is arrhythmia. It is estimated that 15%–60% of patients with Fontan circulation develop arrhythmia, with increasing risk as they age. Patients with arrhythmia has increased risk of morbidity and mortality associated with acute hemodynamic compromise, heart failure, thromboembolism, and sudden cardiac death.[4]

In 1988, de Leval et al. developed the TCPC as a modification from the classic Fontan in an attempt to increase efficiency and minimize the occurrence of arrhythmia. TCPC initially used an LT to create a pathway through the atrium, enabling the inferior vena cava to flow into the PAs. However, this technique requires suturing within the atrium, raising concerns regarding the risk of short- and long-term arrhythmia.[5] In 1990, Marcelletti et al. developed the ECC Fontan as an effort to reduce supraventricular arrhythmia.[6] The ECC Fontan decreases the risk of arrhythmia by avoiding atrial incision and sutures, manipulating the area near the sinus nodes and its blood supply. It also mitigates the likelihood of ventricular dysfunction due to ischemia and prolonged surgery.[7]

Several studies have analyzed long-term outcomes in Fontan procedures (AP, LT, and ECC), demonstrating arrhythmia as a major cause of morbidity and mortality in Fontan survivors. However, these studies mostly compare LT and ECC techniques, offering only a partial view of the overall arrhythmic burden across the full spectrum of Fontan procedures. As a result, current evidence still lacks a comprehensive assessment of arrhythmia outcomes among all surgical types, particularly over long-term follow-up periods.[8] Therefore, this meta-analysis and systematic review aim to fill this gap by systematically comparing arrhythmia risk in extracardiac (EC) and intracardiac (IC) Fontan patients.

METHODS

Search strategy

We conducted a systematic search of the PubMed, Cochrane Library, EMBASE, and Google Scholar databases from 2016 to 2025 to identify studies comparing arrhythmia outcomes following different Fontan procedures, including ECC, LT, and classic Fontan. The protocol has been registered in Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with registration number CRD420261292480.

The search strategy incorporated both Medical Subject Headings and free-text keywords related to “Fontan procedure,” “Arrhythmias, Cardiac,” and “Univentricular Heart.” Boolean operators and truncations were applied to broaden and optimize the search. To ensure literature saturation, we manually screened the reference lists of included studies and relevant review articles. Duplicates were removed prior to screening.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies included observational cohort or case–control studies reporting arrhythmia outcomes among patients who underwent Fontan surgery. The inclusion criteria were as follows: reported arrhythmia as an outcome post Fontan procedure, regardless of whether arrhythmia was defined as a primary outcome or reported as part of secondary outcomes or patient characteristics, and included at least one IC Fontan (LT and/or classic Fontan, either AP connections or RA-RV connections) and EC Fontan.

Due to reported variability in Fontan types and arrhythmia outcomes across studies, patients who underwent LT Fontan and classic Fontan were combined into a single IC group, whereas patients who underwent Fontan using ECC were categorized as the EC group. We excluded case report studies, reviews, and nonfull-text articles. Pregnant women, nonhuman data, and animals were also excluded from this study. Only studies published in English were considered.

Data extraction and statistical analysis

Data extracted included study design, year of publication, country, sample size, age at operation, Fontan type, and the number of patients developing arrhythmia. This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines.[9] Arrhythmia events between Fontan types were calculated using a random-effects model due to anticipated inter-study heterogeneity. All quantitative analyses were performed using the Mantel–Haenszel model to pool odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A restricted analysis excluding AP Fontan was performed to assess the robustness of the findings by limiting comparisons to EC versus LT.

Subgroup analysis based on follow-up duration was performed to explore the potential impact of varying follow-up periods on the pooled estimates. Studies were stratified into two groups using a 9-year cutoff, selected based on the median of reported follow-up durations across included studies. Subgroup analysis based on age group and surgical era was not performed due to inconsistent data and heterogeneous reporting across included studies, as well as differences in operational definitions. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic. The thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75% were interpreted as low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. A sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding the study with the most extreme effect estimate to evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimates and the influence of individual studies on the overall results.

The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS)[10] was employed to determine the quality of nonrandomized controlled trial studies, such as cohort and case–control studies. There were three indicators to assess: selection, comparability, and outcome of the studies. Publication bias was assessed using visual inspection of funnel plots, Begg’s rank correlation test (Kendall’s tau),[11] and Egger’s regression test.[12] Statistical significance was set as P < 0.05 with 95% CI.

RESULTS

Study selection

Twelve studies[31314151617181920212223] met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis. All of the studies had a NOS score ranging 6–9, indicating a moderate-to-high methodological quality. A total of 2705 records were identified through database searching and screened by title. The other 736 were screened by abstracts, and 57 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 18 were excluded due to reporting a single Fontan type and 27 for incomplete arrhythmia data [Figure 1].

Underlying congenital heart diseases (CHDs) varied among the included studies. The most frequently reported conditions were tricuspid atresia (TA), hypoplasia left heart syndrome (HLHS), and double-inlet LV (DILV). Other single-ventricle defects were occasionally mentioned but lacked complete data reporting. Thus, these three underlying conditions were summarized in this study. From 8 studies which reported the underlying diagnosis, HLHS (65.5%) was found to be the most common condition, followed by DILV (25%) and TA (12.5%).[313141518192122]

The absolute number of arrhythmia events varied substantially between Fontan types and across cohorts. Table 2 presents descriptive arrhythmia counts for each study; however, due to substantial variation in cohort size and follow-up duration, comparative interpretation was based on pooled ORs rather than absolute incidence. Studies with larger LT or classic Fontan populations tended to report higher raw arrhythmia events, whereas ECC population generally reported fewer events, reflecting differences in population size rather than direct risk comparison.

Pooled analysis comparing the EC group with the IC (LT + classic Fontan) group demonstrated a significantly lower odds of arrhythmia in the EC group (OR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.27–0.52, P < 0.001) with moderate-to-high heterogeneity (I2 = 74%). The forest plot [Figure 2] showed that most studies[31315181920212223] demonstrated an OR below 1.0 and the 95% CI not crossing unity, indicating a statistically significant reduction in arrhythmia risk with ECC, while a smaller number of studies[141617] exhibited wider 95% CI overlapping 1.0. Despite the effect size variation across studies, the overall direction of effect consistently favored the EC approach. In a restricted analysis comparing only ECC with LT [Figure 3], the overall findings remained consistent with ECC linked to lower odds of arrhythmia (OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.39–0.69, P < 0.001) with 62% of heterogeneity.

Subgroup analysis based on follow-up duration was conducted in 10 included studies and demonstrated consistent findings across both the groups. In studies with a follow-up period ≤ 9 years, the EC group was linked with significantly lower odds of arrhythmia compared to the IC group (OR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.25–0.68, P < 0.001), with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 72%). Similarly, in studies with a follow-up period >9 years, the EC group remained associated with reduced odds of arrhythmia (OR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.15–0.66, P < 0.001), despite higher heterogeneity (I2 = 84%). The overall pooled estimates across subgroups remained significant (OR 0.36, 95% CI: 0.24–0.55, P < 0.001, I2 = 78%). No significant difference was observed between subgroups (P = 0.57) [Figure 4].

Arrhythmia subtypes

Following evaluation of overall arrhythmia incidence, we further examined subtype-specific arrhythmia and their distribution between tachyarrhythmia and bradyarrhythmia. Atrial arrhythmias (AAs) – including atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter (AFL), intra-atrial re-entrant tachycardia (IART), focal AA, and supraventricular tachycardia – were the most commonly reported subtype across the included studies,[1416171819202223] reflecting differences in reporting emphasis rather than true incidence. Ventricular arrhythmias (nonsustained ventricular tachycardia [VT], sustained VT) were the second most reported arrhythmia,[1314161822] followed by bradyarrhythmias (atrioventricular [AV] block, sinus node dysfunction [SND]).[14151922]

Several studies[14151618] had reported lower arrhythmia subtypes in EC patients compared with IC. However, not all studies provided subtype data according to Fontan type, and patients could be classified in multiple arrhythmia subtypes if more than one arrhythmia was observed. In addition, arrhythmia definitions and surveillance intensity varied across studies. Several studies defined arrhythmia based on electrocardiographic criteria, while others reported clinically significant events requiring intervention, such as antiarrhythmic therapy, cardioversion, or pacemaker implantation. In multiple studies, arrhythmia was reported as part of composite outcomes or secondary findings without detailed specification. Differences in surveillance intensity and follow-up practices may also influence arrhythmia detection rates. This variability in reporting and definition limited the direct comparability of reported arrhythmia estimates.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

Sensitivity analysis to evaluate the reliability of the study was conducted by excluding a study with the most extreme effect estimate (OR = 0.09, 95% CI: 0.04–0.19).[21] Exclusion of this study did not substantially change overall estimates and resulted in a reduction of heterogeneity (I2 = 63%).[313141516171819202223] This may indicate that no single study disproportionately influenced the pooled effect [Supplementary Figure 1].

All of the included studies were analyzed for publication bias.[31314151617181920212223] The funnel plots visually showed mild asymmetrical distributions [Supplementary Figure 2]. However, Egger’s (P = 0.253) and Kendall’s tau (P = 0.545) tests did not show statistically significant publication bias. The Rosenthal Fail-Safe N value of 547 (P < 0.001) suggests that a substantial number of additional studies would be required to overrun the observed effect.

DISCUSSION

This meta-analysis demonstrated that the EC Fontan was associated with significantly lower odds of arrhythmia compared with IC modifications (OR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.27–0.52, P < 0.001).[31314151617181920212223] This finding is consistent with prior observational studies reporting lower arrhythmia burden following EC Fontan compared with LT or classic Fontan procedures. As illustrated in the forest plot [Figure 2], most included studies showed effect estimates favoring the EC approach, supporting a consistent reduction in arrhythmia risk across diverse cohorts.[5]

In a restricted analysis comparing only ECC with LT Fontan, the association remained significant (OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.39–0.69; P < 0.001), with a reduction in heterogeneity (I2 = 62%). This reduction suggests that variability within IC techniques, particularly the inclusion of classic Fontan, may contribute to the heterogeneity observed in the primary analysis. Subgroup analysis based on follow-up duration, including both shorter (≤9 years) and longer (>9 years) follow-up, showed no significant subgroup differences (P = 0.57), indicating that follow-up duration alone does not significantly modify the relationship between Fontan type and arrhythmia risk.

Despite these findings, follow-up duration represents an important confounding factor in the observed association between Fontan type and arrhythmia risk. Although comparisons within individual studies were conducted over similar follow-up periods, differences in follow-up duration across studies may result in unequal cumulative exposure to arrhythmia risk at the meta-analysis level. Studies with longer follow-up duration allow greater time for late-onset arrhythmias to develop.

The substantial heterogeneity observed across studies likely reflects variability in study characteristics, including variations in patient populations, surgical era, and outcome reporting. Differences in baseline risk, study design, and reporting approaches may contribute to the observed variability in effect estimates. These factors collectively limit direct comparability between studies and should be considered when interpreting the pooled results.

Earlier-generation techniques were associated with less optimized hemodynamics and greater atrial dilation, whereas more contemporary approaches benefit from advances in surgical techniques and postoperative care. Chen et al. reported that Fontan procedure outcome improved over time – from 2005 to 2023 – affected by advancements in surgical techniques and postoperative care (such as aggressive approach and structured follow-up to manage the probability of Fontan failure).[24] However, the surgical era of Fontan procedures was inconsistently reported across the included studies, limiting the ability to account for potential era-related effects. Therefore, the observed reduction in arrhythmia risk with EC Fontan may be partly confounded by temporal improvements rather than the technique alone.

In this analysis, both classic and LT Fontan procedures were grouped under the IC category. Although largely replaced by TCPC, the classic Fontan remains clinically relevant in specific settings, particularly in cohorts influenced by the surgical era, patient characteristics, and resource availability. In addition, complex anatomical conditions such as heterotaxy syndrome, elevated pulmonary vascular resistance, or significant AV valve regurgitation may influence surgical decision-making. These differences in patient selection and procedural context may contribute to variability in baseline arrhythmia risk across studies.[25]

Nevertheless, including the classic Fontan procedure in the IC group warrants cautious interpretation, as these patients are known to carry a substantially higher risk of arrhythmias. Consequently, the observed protective effect of the EC group in this meta-analysis may be partially influenced by the disproportionately higher arrhythmia risk inherent to classic Fontan cases. In spite of this limitation, incorporating classic Fontan cohorts allows a more comprehensive representation of the real-world Fontan population and reflects the full spectrum of surgical strategies encountered in long-term follow-up studies.[1316202123]

Although the funnel plot showed mild asymmetry, statistical tests did not indicate significant publication bias. The observed asymmetry may be related to heterogeneity and small-study effects rather than true publication bias.

Variability in arrhythmia definitions across studies may influence the interpretation of the pooled results. Differences in outcome definitions and reporting approaches may affect the measured event rates and the magnitude of effect estimates. However, the overall direction of effect remained consistent across studies, suggesting that the main findings are relatively robust despite this limitation.

From a mechanistic perspective, the reduced arrhythmia risk observed in the EC Fontan was largely attributed to the placement of the external conduit, which was positioned outside of the heart and directly reduced IC disturbances. By avoiding atrial incisions and extensive suture lines, the EC approach reduces the risk of sinus node injury, intra-atrial scarring, and re-entrant circuit formation that predispose tachyarrhythmia.[5]

Conversely, the LT and classic AP connections involve direct atrial suturing or anastomosis, creating potential arrhythmogenic substrates that may persist permanently. Supporting evidence, Di Mambro et al. reported that despite the lower overall risk, ECC still had a significant risk of arrhythmia events in patients, reflecting the underlying “Fontan paradox” hemodynamic – chronically elevated venous pressure with restricted cardiac output – which may lead to late-onset arrhythmia.[26]

Despite significant improvements in surgical techniques and perioperative management, arrhythmia was still the most common long-term complications following Fontan circulation. The absence of subpulmonary pump in single ventricle anatomy caused higher central venous pressure with reduced cardiac output. This pathological condition created a stressful electrophysiology environment that promoted conduction abnormalities, atrial dilation, and remodeling.[127]

The pathophysiological mechanism underlying arrhythmia events in Fontan circulation remained multifactorial. In the early postoperative period, atrial tachyarrhythmias typically rose from surgical scars and atrial enlargement that caused multiple macro re-entry circuits, particularly in IC Fontan modification. Over time, atrial dilatation and chronic venous hypertension promoted progressive electrical remodeling that led to late-onset IART and AFL, as a result of atrial myopathy.[14]

Among arrhythmia subtypes, IART is the most frequently reported, particularly in long-term follow-up. Classic Fontan carries the highest risk, followed by LT and EC. IART imposes a significant clinical burden, often showing limited response to antiarrhythmic therapy and high recurrence rates even after catheter ablation, reflecting ongoing hemodynamic stress and disease progression in Fontan circulation. A similar pattern is seen with other types of AA.[1414]

In contrast, ventricular arrhythmias (VAs) were less common but may affect prognostic significance. VA was a major contributor to sudden cardiac death and failing Fontan. Chronic high venous pressure also played a role in ventricular strain and dilation, leading to fibrosis and remodeling which caused electromechanical dyssynchrony.[113] Additionally, patients with prior RA-PA circulation who underwent reoperation, such as ventriculotomy or conduit placement, may develop scar tissue that promoted the formation of arrhythmogenic substrates and the subsequent malignant VAs.[11316]

Bradyarrhythmia arises from both previous CHD pathophysiology (e.g., sinus node abnormality) and procedure-related injury. Fontan procedure is a predisposition to conduction disturbances.[28] AV block is the most frequently reported subtype, while other forms such as SND and junctional rhythm are less consistently described. Long-term follow-up shows that these conditions remain clinically relevant, often requiring permanent pacing. However, the unique Fontan anatomy may complicate lead placement and long-term management.[1314151922]

Arrhythmia incidence was affected by several variables, such as fenestration, ventricle morphology, and CHD diagnosis. Fenestration reduced the rise in venous pressure post Fontan procedure and improved early outcomes after surgery.[28] Previous articles had reported lower arrhythmia events and reduced PA pressure.[2930] Across all included studies, the most reported underlying CHD in the Fontan population is TA, HLHS, and DILV with various ventricular dominancy. These may influence postoperative hemodynamics and contribute to heterogeneity in arrhythmia incidence and subtype distribution among populations.[1314151819]

Clinical implications

The findings of this meta-analysis suggest that EC Fontan is associated with a lower long-term arrhythmia burden compared with IC Fontan. In clinical practice, this may support consideration of EC technique when anatomical and patient-related factors permit, especially in individuals at higher risk for arrhythmia. However, with substantial heterogeneity among studies and the inclusion of different Fontan eras and patient characteristics, Fontan type selection should remain individualized. Irrespective of the modification of Fontan techniques, long-term arrhythmia surveillance is still essential, as arrhythmia risk persists across all patients with Fontan circulation. These findings may assist clinicians in risk stratification and tailoring long-term follow-up strategies.

Limitation

There are several limitations in this study. All included studies were observational, which limits causal inference. Differences in study characteristics, including patient populations, surgical era, follow-up duration, and outcome reporting, may introduce potential confounding and limit comparability across studies. In addition, the transformation into dichotomous outcomes in some studies, as well as the combination of LT and classic Fontan into a single IC group, may influence the pooled estimates.

CONCLUSION

In our systematic review and meta-analysis, it was found that the EC Fontan was associated with a lower observed risk of arrhythmia compared to the IC Fontan. This may be related to the reduced risk of sinus node injury, intra-atrial scarring, and re-entrant circuit formation that predispose to tachyarrhythmia. However, this association should be interpreted with caution, as the observed effect size may partly reflect the higher arrhythmia burden in classic Fontan, as well as differences in surgical era and follow-up duration across studies.

Arrhythmia in Fontan circulation was influenced by multiple procedural and patient-related factors; therefore, the selection of Fontan surgery methods should be determined on a case-by-case basis while considering the patient’s characteristics, anatomy, hemodynamic status, as well as long-term goals and the operator’s surgical expertise. Regardless of Fontan type, continued long-term arrhythmia surveillance remains essential.
