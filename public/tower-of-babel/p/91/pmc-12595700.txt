Exploring the associations between food security and multidimensional well-being under economic uncertainty: a cross-sectional study in Türkiye
Authors: Mehmet Haydaroğlu, Tuba Nur Yıldız Kopuz, Fatma Esra Güneş
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFood insecurity, as a reflection of both material deprivation and the broader social and psychological challenges related to accessing food, has emerged as a core indicator of sustainable development. This cross-sectional study examines the link between food security status and multidimensional well-being in Türkiye, a country facing high inflation and income inequality.MethodAn online survey was conducted in December 2024, involving 526 participants. Food security was assessed by the Food Insecurity Experience Scale, and the Flourish Index was used to assess multidimensional well-being across five domains. Perceptions of inflation and income inequality were assessed using single-item questions, alongside sociodemographic information.ResultsFood insecurity was significantly associated with the perceived inflation impact (d = − 0.37, p = .009) and income inequality (d = − 0.23, p < .001). The moderately or severely food insecure group reported a lower Flourish Index (d = 0.56, p < .001), particularly in life satisfaction (d = 0.53, p < .001) compared to food secure or mildly food insecure group. Hierarchical linear regression analysis identified monthly income status and influence of religious belief as significant positive predictors of the Flourish Index (p < .001), while food insecurity showed a significant negative association even after adjusting for these factors (β = − 0.16, p < .001). Cluster analysis revealed four distinct profiles based on food security, income status, and religious influence. Significant differences were observed in the Flourish Index and all its domains across the clusters (p < .001). The largest effect sizes were found in life satisfaction (η² = 0.17), happiness (η² = 0.137), and the overall Flourish Index score (η² = 0.127) indicating variation in well-being. Participants in the “Financially Fragile & Low Religious” group reported the lowest scores across most domains, whereas those in the “Financially Secure & Highly Religious” group exhibited the highest levels of flourishing.ConclusionFood insecurity was associated with reduced flourishing across multiple domains under economic uncertainty, and well-being outcomes differed across groups defined by financial conditions and religious engagement. This suggests that policy and intervention strategies targeting food insecurity should adopt a multidimensional perspective on well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25152-3.

Background

Traditional poverty assessments have primarily relied on income-based indicators, such as absolute income thresholds and consumption levels, which focus on determining whether individuals can afford a minimum need [1]. However, this approach may fail to capture the multidimensional nature of poverty [2]. Therefore, multidimensional poverty methodologies have gained importance which reflect the lived experiences of individuals in poverty [3]. In this broader context, nutrition represents a fundamental dimension through which poverty is both experienced and assessed. The inability to meet dietary needs in situations of poverty results in health inequalities and is associated with a range of adverse outcomes including physical and mental health, well-being, and daily functioning [4]. As indicators of poverty have evolved into a multidimensional structure, the assessment of meeting dietary needs has also developed beyond the concept of hunger. In this context, the food security indicator has gained prominence as a more comprehensive perspective [5]. As a development indicator, food insecurity remains a persistent and deepening global issue driven by a convergence of interconnected factors including conflict, climate extremes, economic downturns, the unaffordability of healthy diets, unhealthy food environments, and entrenched social inequalities. According to the 2024 State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World report, 2.33 billion people, nearly 30% of the global population, experience moderate or severe food insecurity, highlighting the urgency of addressing food-related vulnerabilities [6].

Food security is defined as a situation in which all people have physical, social, and economic access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food to meet their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life at all times [7]. Initially, food security was approached as a matter of ensuring sufficient food availability at the national level primarily focusing on meeting energy requirements. While food may be sufficiently available, this does not automatically mean that it is accessible to everyone, nor does calorie sufficiency ensure a healthy and well-balanced diet. Consequently, the concept of food security evolved beyond a focus on the availability of food, now encompassing access, stability, and utilization at individual and household levels [8]. Current food security assessment methods can be conducted at both macro and micro levels across the four dimensions, and micro-level indicators primarily focus on the lived experiences and consequences of food insecurity [9]. These dimensions—availability, access, utilization, and stability—aim to capture the diverse experiences and consequences faced by individuals and households regarding food. And has been proposed conceptualizing food insecurity as a multidimensional indicator of deprivation, moving beyond income-based measures to encompass social and psychological dimensions of eating [10]. Given this multidimensional nature, the consequences of food insecurity are also diverse. Nutritional inadequacy can lead to undernutrition, impaired growth, and higher vulnerability to chronic and infectious diseases [11]. Beyond physical health, food insecurity significantly affects mental well-being, increasing the risk of anxiety, depression, and psychological distress due to uncertainty about accessing food. It is linked to feelings of hopelessness, low self-worth, and reduced life satisfaction. Cognitive function can decline, sleep may be disrupted, and social well-being is compromised as individuals face isolation and stigmatization. In severe cases, chronic food insecurity can even contribute to suicidal thoughts, reflecting its profound impact on emotional and psychological health [12, 13].

In a stable food security environment, food prices are generally expected to remain stable as well. However, inflation can create a risk environment for food insecurity, depending on the rate, duration, and severity of price increases [14]. At the microeconomic level, both rising food prices and declining purchasing power directly affect consumers’ access to food [15]. In the context of economic uncertainty, increasing financial stress significantly reduces household confidence in both current economic conditions and future expectations [16]. Overall, food security is influenced not only by physical access to food but also by anxiety resulting from unpredictability. One of the most notable recent examples is the 2007/2008 global food price crisis, which pushed over 100 million people into hunger and malnutrition within a single year [17]. Between 2005 and 2019, Türkiye’s Consumer Price Index (CPI) recorded its highest annual increase of 20.3%. However, inflation surged to 36.08% in 2020, 64.27% in 2022, and 64.77% in 2023. Although a decline was reported for 2024, CPI still stood at 44.23% [18]. In addition, Türkiye continues to struggle with income inequality. In 2024, the Gini coefficient in Türkiye was reported at 0.413 [19], indicating high inequality. According to Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) data from 2022, Türkiye ranked third among 31 countries following Costa Rica (0.472) and Chile (0.448) [20]. Such macroeconomic instability can undermine not only material conditions but also collective well-being, fostering widespread financial insecurity [21].

In this context of high inflation and income inequality, this study aims to examine the relationship between food security status and flourishing, defined as a holistic and multidimensional construct of well-being that integrates both positive feelings and effective functioning across key life domains. Specifically, it addresses the following hypotheses:

Methods

Participants and procedures

The study was planned as a cross-sectional design, and data were collected in December 2024 via the online platform Google Forms. Given the study’s primary aim of examining the effect of food security on the Flourish Index while controlling for covariates, sample size estimation was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.7 [22] for a linear multiple regression model, targeting 528 participants to achieve sufficient power (f² = 0.02, α = 0.05, 1 – β = 0.90). The study employed a convenience sampling strategy, with participants recruited through online platforms including social media networks and messaging applications. Responses were collected anonymously. Participants were eligible to take part in the study if they were aged 18 years or older, resided in Türkiye, and provided informed consent. The dataset was screened for exclusion criteria, including missing data, inconsistent or illogical responses, and incomplete entries that prevented the accurate assessment of food security status or flourishing score. Missing data were handled using listwise deletion, and only complete cases were retained for analysis.

Measures

Demographic information was collected through a set of questions including age, sex, education level, marital status, accommodation, and employment status. Participants’ perception of religious influence was assessed using the question, “To what extent does your religious belief influence your daily life and decisions?”, adapted from Borges et al. [23]. Response options were presented on a 6-point scale ranging from “no religious belief” to 5 “very strong influence.”

Participants’ perception of their monthly income status was assessed using a question from the Türkiye Nutrition and Health Survey [24], “Which of the following best describes your income status?” Response options were presented on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from “not enough to live” to “enough to live comfortably.”

Given the ongoing economic instability in Türkiye, two single-item measures were used to assess individuals’ perceptions of inflationary pressure and income inequality. To assess the perceived impact of inflation, a single-item measure was included, adapted from a previous study in Türkiye that showed pronounced differences across food security levels [25]. Participants were asked: “To what extent do you think inflation in Türkiye has negatively affected your household’s or your own livelihood?” Responses were recorded on a Likert scale ranging from 0 (“No negative impact”) to 10 (“Extremely negative impact”). The second item explored perceived income inequality by asking: “Do you think income distribution in Türkiye is fair?” Responses were recorded on a Likert scale ranging from 0 (“Not fair at all”) to 10 (“Completely fair”). The item was inspired by the economic values module of the World Values Survey Wave 7 [26]. All primary constructs in this study including food insecurity and flourishing were assessed using validated instruments with established reliability and conceptual clarity.

The Flourish Index, developed by VanderWeele et al. [27], is a multidimensional measure designed to assess human flourishing, conceptualized as complete human well-being that extends beyond happiness or life satisfaction to encompass multiple domains of life. The index comprises five foundational domains: happiness and life satisfaction, mental and physical health, meaning and purpose, character and virtue, and close social relationships. Each domain includes two items, rated on a scale from 0 to 10, resulting in a total of 10 items in the standard “Flourish” version. Although a “Secure Flourish” version with additional financial stability items exists, this study employed the standard 10-item version. The Turkish version of the scale was validated by Alkan [28], confirming its five-domain structure with satisfactory internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.82). The index was selected due to its multidimensional structure that captures diverse aspects of human flourishing, making it particularly relevant in the context of the multidimensional nature of food security. Although a composite Flourish Index score was calculated, domain-level analyses were prioritized to provide a clearer understanding of each dimension. This decision was guided by the multidimensional nature of the flourishing construct and by recent literature emphasizing variability in how flourishing is defined and measured [29].

The Food Insecurity Experience Scale (FIES), developed by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), is regularly used to collect data at the individual or household level, which is then reported at national and global levels as a development indicator. The scale captures not only physical availability and access to food but also the lived experience of uncertainty and concern about obtaining food [6, 30]. This self-reported, experience-based tool assesses individuals’ experiences during episodes of food insecurity through eight items arranged in order of increasing severity. Based on the severity of affirmative responses, participants were categorized into three levels: food secure or mildly food insecure (FSMI), moderately food insecure, and severely food insecure. The FSMI group includes individuals who may worry about their ability to access food or compromise on food quality or variety, but do not face actual reductions in quantity. The moderately food insecure group includes individuals who reduce the quantity and/or frequency of food consumption due to lack of resources. Severe food insecurity is characterized by having to skip a meal and, at its most extreme, spending an entire day without eating due to lack of food [31]. In the initial classification, 337 participants were identified as FSMI. In line with global reporting practices, participants categorized as moderately (n = 96) or severely food insecure (n = 93) were combined into a single group: moderately or severely food insecure (MSFI).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 23.0). Group differences by food security status were examined using independent samples t-tests, one-way ANOVA, or chi-square tests depending on the variable type. The normality of continuous variables was assessed via skewness and kurtosis, with values between − 1 and + 1 considered acceptable for parametric analyses. Hierarchical multiple regression models evaluated the association between food security status and the Flourish Index. Covariates were added or removed based on statistical significance. Multicollinearity was evaluated using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), and all values were within acceptable limits. Flourishing scores across clusters were first examined using one-way ANOVA to test for overall group differences. As variance homogeneity was violated, post-hoc comparisons were conducted using the Games–Howell test, which accounts for unequal variances and sample sizes while controlling Type I error. Effect sizes were reported using Cohen’s d for continuous variables and eta-squared (η²) for ANOVA. Significance was set at p <.05 [32, 33].

Cluster analysis used a two-step approach. First, hierarchical clustering using Ward’s method with Squared Euclidean Distance was performed to explore the data structure. Examination of the dendrogram and agglomeration coefficients indicated either a three- or four-cluster solution. To further evaluate model adequacy, a Two-Step Cluster analysis was conducted, which yielded average silhouette measures of 0.20 for the three-cluster solution and 0.30 for the four-cluster solution. In addition, cluster sizes and K-Means stability indicators were compared. Taken together, these criteria, along with the interpretability of group profiles, favored the four-cluster model, which was therefore retained. In the second step, K-Means clustering was used to refine the solution. The algorithm was set to a maximum of 10 iterations, with convergence achieved after the third. The minimum distance between initial cluster centers was 2.236, indicating sufficient separation at initialization. Distances between final cluster centers further confirmed the distinctiveness of groups, with the largest distance observed between Cluster 1 and Cluster 4 (2.474) and the smallest between Cluster 2 and Cluster 3 (1.947). Finally, the stability and validity of the four-cluster solution were supported by ANOVA tests on the clustering variables, demonstrating significant differences across clusters [34].

Results

A total of 562 individuals initially accessed the online survey. After data screening, 36 cases were excluded for the following reasons: did not provide consent to proceed (n = 2), incomplete responses (n = 6), unrealistic response patterns (n = 2), insufficient FIES responses for food security classification (n = 15), and not meeting inclusion criteria (n = 11). The final analytical sample included 526 participants. Among all participants, 64.1% (n = 337) were classified as food secure to mildly insecure, while 35.9% (n = 189) were moderately or severely food insecure. Participants in the MSFI group were significantly younger (d = 0.21, p =.019), with a higher proportion of young adults (18–25 years) (48.7%) (w = 0.17, p <.001). Income perception was strongly associated with food security. A substantial proportion of MSFI participants (69.3%) reported that their income was either “just enough to live” (45.5%) or “not enough” (23.8%). In contrast, this combined figure was 34.4% in the FSMI group (w = 0.36, p <.001). Perceptions of economic uncertainty also differed between groups. The MSFI group reported a greater perceived negative impact of inflation (8.4 ± 2.2) than those in the FSMI group (7.5 ± 2.6) (d = –0.37, p =.009). In addition, perceptions of income inequality were also higher among participants in the MSFI group (8.7 ± 1.8) compared to those in the FSMI group (8.3 ± 2.2) (d = –0.23, p <.001). Table 2 presents the associations between flourishing domains and socioeconomic factors. The Flourish Index was significantly associated with age group (p =.009), marital status (p <.001), religious influence (p <.001), accommodation status (p =.027), and monthly income status (p <.001). No significant correlation was found between chronological age (r =.068), yet young adults reported the lowest Flourish Index (p =.009). Married participants reported significantly higher scores in meaning (7.0 ± 2.2 vs. 6.1 ± 2.1), mental health (6.4 ± 2.4 vs. 5.6 ± 2.3), and satisfaction with relationships (7.6 ± 2.2 vs. 6.8 ± 2.6) compared to single participants (p <.001), with these domains showing the largest differences. The perceived impact of religious belief on daily life was significantly positively associated with all domains except physical health (p =.638), with participants reporting high religious influence consistently having the highest scores. Monthly income perception was strongly associated with the Flourish Index and its domains. As monthly income insufficiency worsened, scores in all flourishing domains and the overall index decreased, except for character (p =.065), particularly in life satisfaction (p <.001), happiness (p <.001), and mental health (p <.001). Self-reported experiences of inflation-related financial strain and income inequality were both negatively associated with the Flourish Index (p <.05). However, the correlations were weak, with the strongest associations observed in the life satisfaction domain for inflation (r =.203) and income inequality (r =.264).

Table 4 presents the hierarchical multiple linear regression results examining the association between food security status and the Flourish Index. Model 1 assessed the direct effect of food security, revealing a significant negative association with the Flourish Index (B = − 0.86, β = − 0.261, p <.001), explaining 7% of the variance (R² = 0.07). Model 2 introduced age group, marital status, income perception, and religious influence as control variables, selected based on their significant associations with the Flourish Index and several domains (Table 2). While the effect of food security remained significant (B = − 0.48, β = − 0.15, p =.001), the explained variance increased by 10% (ΔR² = 0.10) reaching a total of 17% (R² = 0.17). Notably, income perception (B = 0.40, β = 0.24, p <.001) and religious influence (B = 0.34, β = 0.17, p <.001) were stronger predictors, while age (p =.782) and marital status (p =.103) were not significant. Model 3, which retained only significant variables from Model 2, food security remained significantly negatively associated with the Flourish Index (B = − 0.53, β = − 0.16, p <.001), even after adjusting for income perception and religious influence. Income perception (B = 0.39, β = 0.24, p <.001) and religious influence (B = 0.40, β = 0.19, p <.001) both showed significant positive associations with the Flourish Index. The final model explained 16% of the variance (R² = 0.16).

Discussions

This study examined the relationship between food security and multidimensional well-being in Türkiye’s ongoing economic challenges. Individuals experiencing moderate or severe food insecurity reported significantly lower flourishing scores particularly in life satisfaction, happiness, and sense of purpose. Food insecurity was also significantly associated with perceived inflation impact and income inequality. Hierarchical regression analysis showed that food insecurity remained significantly negatively associated with flourishing even after adjusting for income perception and religious influence both of which emerged as significant positive correlations. Furthermore, cluster analysis identified four distinct profiles defined by food security, income perception, and religious influence. These profiles demonstrated notable variations in flourishing, reflecting differences shaped by financial conditions and religious engagement.

Approximately one-third of participants in this study experienced moderate or severe food insecurity. According to FAO et al. [6], the global prevalence of moderate or severe food insecurity in 2023 was 28.9%. Türkiye is classified by the World Bank [35] as an upper-middle-income country, with a gross national income (GNI) per capita falling between $4,516 and $14,005. In this income group, the average prevalence of moderate or severe food insecurity between 2021 and 2023 was reported as 12.9% [6], considerably lower than the 35.9% observed in the present study. Despite the absence of country-specific data for Türkiye in the report, the 35.9% prevalence observed in this study exceeds both global and income group averages. Notably, these figures are based on the FIES, the same instrument employed in the present study. Recent cross-sectional studies in Türkiye also highlight high levels of food insecurity. Haydaroğlu and Bilgiç [36] reported that 41.7%, Celik et al. [37] found 22.4%, and Kılınç et al. [38] reported 26.5% were moderately or severely food insecure. The prevalence may be partly attributable to Türkiye’s sustained period of high inflation and the resulting decline in purchasing power alongside increasing living costs. Similarly, a study among minimum-wage workers indicated that 47.9% of participants experienced food insecurity [39], underscoring the heightened vulnerability of economically fragile groups. For example, the rise in global inflation from 4.8% in 2007 to 8.9% in 2008 [40] pushed over 100 million people into hunger and malnutrition within a year [17]. Türkiye’s prolonged inflationary environment may represent a similarly disruptive context, contributing to rising food insecurity. This is supported by recent findings from Türkiye indicating that the adverse effects of food inflation are particularly pronounced among food-insecure groups [25].

In addition to high inflation, Turkey also faces a persistent problem of income inequality. This study revealed that perceived effects of inflation and income inequality were negatively correlated with flourishing, especially in life satisfaction and happiness. However, food security status showed a considerably stronger association with flourishing. Evidence by Schneider [41] suggests that income inequality can reduce people’s sense of social standing and lower their well-being, while also increasing the importance of social status for life satisfaction. Perceptions of inequality are influenced by cultural values. In more hierarchical and conservative societies like Türkiye, their impact on well-being may depend less on abstract ideas of fairness and more on everyday economic struggles such as difficulties meeting basic needs [42]. In this context, financial perceptions may be more closely linked to food insecurity than to overall flourishing. This aligns with Oshio and Urakawa’s [43] argument that inequality perceptions and their consequences for well-being are shaped not only by objective income levels, but also by individuals’ relative position and vulnerability such as being food insecure.

Notably, nearly half of those experiencing moderate or severe food insecurity were aged between 18 and 25, indicating a heightened vulnerability among young adults. Consistent with the current findings, previous studies have also demonstrated that young adults are at greater risk of food insecurity compared to older age groups [44, 45]. Young adults often face heightened financial vulnerability due to a combination of structural and transitional factors. Prolonged educational trajectories can delay entry into stable employment. In addition, limited access to institutional support systems, lack of accumulated resources, and increased exposure to income volatility further contribute to economic insecurity [46, 47]. This is consistent with recent studies among Turkish university students. Celik et al. [37] found that 35.5% of students from a single university experienced food insecurity, which was strongly associated with income sufficiency and living arrangements, while Esin and Ayyıldız [48] reported a prevalence of 68.2% in a large multi-university sample. In line with these vulnerabilities, younger individuals reported significantly lower scores in the overall Flourish Index, as well as in mental health, sense of meaning and purpose in life, and satisfaction with personal relationships. These patterns raise the question of whether younger adults are more likely to experience vulnerability across both psychosocial domains. This interpretation is supported by previous studies showing that food insecurity in young adults is strongly linked to mental health difficulties including depression, anxiety, and feelings of isolation [49, 50]. Taken together, these findings highlight that food insecurity among young adults is not only a financial issue but is closely linked with psychological and emotional well-being, making them particularly vulnerable.

The findings of this study support the view that food insecurity should not be regarded solely as a physical health concern, but rather as a condition with extensive psychosocial implications. Likewise, Pourmotabbed et al. [51] also demonstrated that food insecurity significantly increases the risk of depression and stress, highlighting its psychosocial consequences. The study discussed several mechanisms that may underlie this association including food-related uncertainty, feelings of shame and perceived failure, social exclusion or stigma, and even potential biological effects due to inadequate micronutrient intake. This study found that individuals experiencing food insecurity reported significantly lower Flourish Index. In the unadjusted model, food insecurity alone explained 7% of the variance in the Flourish Index. After including age, marital status, income perception, and religious influence, the explained variance rose to 17%, while the effect of food insecurity was reduced but remained significant, confirming its role as a negative predictor. Although both age and marital status were significantly associated with Flourish Index, these associations did not remain significant in the fully adjusted regression model. Their effects may be mediated or confounded by other variables in the model particularly income perception and religious influence. Steptoe et al. [52] demonstrated that the negative relationship between age and well-being is not always direct. Instead, this association may be partially explained by age-related declines in income and health status. This aligns with the current study’s interpretation that demographic factors like age may influence well-being through more immediate factors, such as income perception and religious beliefs. Similarly, the impact of marital status on flourishing may depend on household dynamics and financial factors. For instance, Chen et al. [53] demonstrated that life satisfaction in dual-earner couples is shaped by not only total household income but also the relative income contribution of each partner. Wu [54] further emphasized that gendered expectations and income equality within marriage can influence subjective well-being. Overall, while age and marital status were associated with flourishing, their explanatory power may be overshadowed by income perception and religious influence.

In this study, perceived income sufficiency and religious influence emerged as significant correlates of flourishing. Religiosity has been widely associated with positive well-being outcomes [55]. Participants who reported a higher degree of religious influence in their daily lives also scored significantly higher in overall index and across nearly all subdomains. Consistent with these findings, Aydogdu et al. [56] found that higher religious salience, importance of God, and mosque attendance were positively linked to happiness and life satisfaction in Türkiye. While food insecurity remained significantly associated with the Flourish Index, religious engagement stood out as a key psychosocial factor. Prior studies have shown that religiosity can enhance coping abilities through spiritual frameworks such as gratitude [57], promote prosocial behaviours such as altruism and helping [58], and reduce materialistic values by decreasing perceived resource scarcity [59]. In this study, individuals reporting higher religious influence were more likely to belong to the food-secure group. However, Purzycki et al. [60] showed that the link between food insecurity and religiosity is highly variable across societies, with some contexts showing a positive association while in others the expected relationship does not appear. Moreover, the association between food security and perceived religious influence in this study was not statistically significant (p =.07), and no empirical evidence was found to suggest that religiosity is directly associated with food security. Thus, causal interpretations should be made with caution. The cluster analysis showed that economically vulnerable groups differed in flourishing based on religious influence, with those reporting higher religious influence consistently scoring higher across several domains. This may suggest that religiosity might buffer the negative effects of deprivation. Further, as Gulliford et al. [61] noted, gratitude and religious expressions may sometimes serve socially strategic purposes, such as gaining approval or meeting expectations. Therefore, while religiosity appears to have psychological benefits, these findings should be interpreted with caution because self-reported data may be influenced by social influences.

This study underscores the importance of examining food insecurity not only as a matter of access to food but also as a psychosocial condition linked to broader aspects of well-being. Through both regression and cluster analyses, the study demonstrates that food insecurity remains a significant negative correlate of flourishing even after accounting for key socioeconomic factors. Conducted in the context of economic uncertainty in Türkiye, the study offers context-specific evidence in a high-inflation setting. The combined use of regression and cluster analyses allows both the independent effects of food insecurity and the broader patterns of vulnerability to be identified. This provides a more comprehensive understanding of its psychosocial implications. While this study offers valuable insights, several limitations should be acknowledged.

Several methodological considerations should be noted when interpreting the findings of this study, as they may affect the generalizability of the results. First, the cross-sectional design captures only a snapshot of the relationship between food insecurity and flourishing. Although associations were observed, causality cannot be inferred, and the possibility of reverse causation cannot be excluded. Second, the study relied on convenience sampling through online recruitment via social media and messaging applications. While this approach enabled access to a diverse group of respondents, it represents a non-probability sampling method that may introduce self-selection bias. In addition, individuals with limited or no internet access may have been excluded, which could influence the demographic profile of the sample and potentially bias the observed distribution of food security status. Third, a total of 36 cases were excluded due to incomplete or inconsistent responses, particularly related to the FIES items that included “don’t know” or “prefer not to answer” options. While this approach ensured valid classification of food security status, it may introduce some selection bias, as individuals who declined to answer may differ systematically from those who completed the items. In addition, religious influence was assessed with a single-item measure, which has not been validated as a multi-item construct. This may limit reliability and fail to capture the multidimensional aspects of religiosity. Therefore, findings related to religiosity should be interpreted with caution.

Future studies could employ longitudinal designs to capture how changes in food security status influence flourishing over time, especially in the context of changing economic conditions. In addition, quasi-experimental approaches would be valuable for establishing temporal ordering and causal pathways that cannot be addressed with cross-sectional data. Qualitative approaches, such as in-depth interviews or focus groups, could provide a deeper understanding of how individuals experience and cope with food insecurity, revealing psychosocial impacts that may not be captured through surveys. Given the observed role of religiosity, future research should employ validated, multi-dimensional measures of religious beliefs and practices to provide a more reliable assessment. Such approaches would allow for a deeper understanding of how different dimensions of religiosity may influence well-being among individuals experiencing food insecurity.
