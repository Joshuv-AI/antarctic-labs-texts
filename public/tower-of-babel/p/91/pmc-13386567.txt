Refractory Mycobacterium fortuitum skin and soft tissue infection following dog bite in a pediatric patient: a case report
Authors: Zhao Mei, Ming Xia, Yueqing Tan, Tieqi Zheng, Guilan Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMycobacterium fortuitum (M. fortuitum), a rapidly growing nontuberculous mycobacterium (NTM), is an emerging pathogen in trauma-associated skin and soft tissue infections (SSTIs). Pediatric NTM infections are rare and often misdiagnosed due to nonspecific clinical manifestations mimicking those of pyogenic bacterial infections. We report the first pediatric case of refractory M. fortuitum SSTIs following a dog bite in an 8-year-old child, highlighting diagnostic and management challenges.Case presentationAn 8-year-old child presented with SSTIs on the anteromedial right thigh following a dog bite, which remained refractory to a 2-week course of empiric β-lactam therapy. Histopathology revealed nodular granulomatous inflammation with epithelioid cells and purulent necrosis, and acid-fast bacilli were identified via Ziehl-Neelsen staining. Targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) rapidly identified M. fortuitum within 48 h, with confirmation by automated liquid culture (7-day growth) and targeted PCR. A multidisciplinary approach integrating broth microdilution-guided combination therapy (amikacin 15 mg/kg IV q48h, linezolid 10 mg/kg PO q12h, and doxycycline 3 mg/kg PO q12h) with weekly or biweekly surgical debridement achieved complete resolution of the cutaneous lesions. No relapse was noted during 6 months of follow-up, with regular clinical and laboratory evaluations.ConclusionsThis case underscores three critical findings: (1) NTM infections warrant inclusion in the differential diagnosis of animal bite wounds refractory to β-lactams; (2) tNGS facilitates rapid rare pathogen identification, enabling target-directed therapy within 48 h; (3) The multidisciplinary team is imperative to coordinate combined antimicrobial and surgical interventions. Background

Animal bites are a significant global health issue, accounting for 5% of trauma-related emergency visits, with dog bites representing 80–90% of cases [1, 2]. Pediatric populations are particularly affected. In the United States, dog bites cause 337,000 emergency visits and incur $2 billion in annual healthcare costs [3]. In China, 40 million people suffer from animal-related injuries yearly (2009 Ministry of Health data) [4]. Although only 5–25% of dog bite wounds develop infections [5], complications such as rabies and the burden of post-exposure prophylaxis, along with antibiotic-resistant skin and soft tissue infections (SSTIs), place a substantial strain on healthcare systems. Dog bite wounds are intrinsically prone to polymicrobial infections, classically involving canine oral flora, human skin commensals, and environmental pathogens [2, 5]. In delayed presentations, fastidious pathogens, including nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) may emerge as opportunistic agents. Mycobacterium fortuitum (M. fortuitum), a rapidly growing NTM intrinsically resistant to β-lactams and variably susceptible to macrolides, exemplifies diagnostic challenges through three mechanisms: clinical mimicry of pyogenic bacterial infections, culture positivity in only 40%–60% of clinically confirmed cases, and limitations of routine microbiologic screening [6]. Effective management requires susceptibility-guided combination therapy for prolonged durations [7]. We present the first pediatric case of refractory M. fortuitum SSTIs following a dog bite to illustrate the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. This case underscores the imperative for early suspicion of NTM in refractory, culture-negative SSTIs and highlights the essential role of multidisciplinary collaboration in managing such complex infections. We present this case in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist.

Case presentation

An otherwise healthy 8-year-old girl (height 140 cm, weight 34 kg) was admitted with a non-healing right thigh wound complicated by sinus tract formation and continuous exudate, persisting for 2 months after a domestic dog bite. Initial treatment at the referring hospital included tetanus immunoglobulin, rabies vaccine, wound irrigation followed by suturing, and prophylactic intravenous antibiotics (penicillin, clindamycin; dosages undocumented). However, the wound failed to heal. Ultrasound and MRI revealed a subcutaneous abscess in the anteromedial right thigh (maximal transverse diameter 3 × 2 cm), with MRI demonstrating surrounding soft tissue edema and ultrasound showing ipsilateral inguinal lymphadenopathy (short-axis diameter 1.0 cm). Wound cultures showed no pathogenic bacterial growth.

On Hospital Day (HD) 1, physical examination revealed two residual dog bite wounds located on the anterior-medial aspect of the right thigh. These wounds were characterized by purulent discharge from a central sinus tract, erythema measuring 4 × 3 cm, and tenderness in the right inguinal region. Laboratory tests showed white blood cell count (WBC) 7.8 × 10⁹/L and neutrophil percentage (NEUT%) 65%. On HD 2, she underwent debridement, inguinal lymphadenectomy, and placement of negative-pressure wound therapy (NPWT) in the operative room. Intraoperatively, the depth of necrosis was observed to extend to the subcutaneous fat but did not involve the underlying fascial plane. Post-operatively, she developed intermittent fever and was treated with amoxicillin-clavulanate (30 mg/kg intravenous [IV] q8h). The clinical course is shown in Fig. 1. Histopathology examination of the surgical specimens revealed epithelioid cell granulomatous inflammation with purulent necrosis (Fig. 2). Acid-fast staining was positive, showing 3–9 bacilli per 100 fields at ×1000 magnification under oil immersion. However, serum tuberculosis antibody detection using the colloidal gold method was negative. One week later, during the second debridement in the same sterile operative setting, the intraoperative specimen tested negative for Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) DNA.

On HD 16, during the third debridement, targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) identified M. fortuitum (sequence count 14,155; relative abundance 97.04%, see Methods for tNGS in the Supplemental Materials) within 48 h of sampling. Following consultation with a clinical pharmacist, a treatment regimen was initiated, including clarithromycin (7.5 mg/kg per os [PO] q12h), linezolid (10 mg/kg PO q12h), and amikacin (15 mg/kg IV q48h). However, wound healing was suboptimal. Two weeks later, tissue from re-debridement was cultured for 7 days, and M. fortuitum was confirmed by automated liquid culture and PCR, with antimicrobial susceptibility results obtained (Table 1). The clinical pharmacist recommended replacing clarithromycin with doxycycline (3 mg/kg PO q12h).

On HD 37, during the fifth debridement, less necrotic tissue was noted, but acid-fast staining remained positive. Cefmetazole (30 mg/kg IV q12h) was added. Doxycycline was discontinued after a 21-day course due to elevated liver enzymes (alanine aminotransferase [ALT] 80.0 U/L and aspartate aminotransferase [AST] 55.9 U/L). On HD 51, the sixth debridement revealed abundant granulation tissue with normalized liver enzymes. Amikacin was administered continuously from HD 17 to HD 63 for a total inpatient duration of 47 days. Otoacoustic emission (OAE) testing was performed weekly throughout this course for ototoxicity monitoring, with all serial results within normal limits. Therapeutic success was defined by three criteria: no signs of infection (erythema, purulence, pain), three consecutive negative acid-fast stainings for wound exudate, and extensive healthy granulation tissue covering most of the wound bed.

The patient was discharged after 63 days of inpatient management. Oral linezolid was administered continuously from HD 17 to 46, resumed from HD 51 to 63, and continued for an additional 3 months post-discharge (total treatment duration ~ 4.3 months). Complete wound healing was achieved without reported discomfort or recurrence. Serial clinical assessments and laboratory monitoring consistently showed the absence of pain, erythema, or purulent discharge, while liver function parameters remained within normal ranges throughout the follow-up.

Discussion

This case provides molecular evidence supporting a possible association between dog bites and M. fortuitum infection, offering insights into a potential zoonotic transmission pathway of NTM. As environmental commensals, NTM are primarily transmitted through soil or water exposure, with previous consensus suggesting a lack of evidence for animal-to-human transmission [8]. Although domestic animals can harbor NTM and develop self-infections [9, 10], human infections resulting from animal bites remain exceedingly rare, with only five documented cases of canine-associated NTM infections in the literature [11–15]. Notably, microbiological testing of the biting dog’s oral cavity was not performed in this case, precluding definitive confirmation of the pathogen’s origin in the animal’s microbiota. However, this report raises the possibility that oral NTM colonization in canines may represent a potential novel transmission risk. The underlying mechanisms involve traumatic inoculation leading to biofilm formation, dysbiosis of the animal’s salivary microbiome promoting proliferation of opportunistic pathogens, and immunodeficiency-related opportunistic infections [5]. Dogs may act as potential zoonotic vectors, underscoring the necessity for clinical reevaluation of infection risks associated with animal-related trauma.

The clinical manifestations of NTM skin infections are relatively insidious, posing significant diagnostic challenges. With an incubation period of 2–4 weeks, the pathological presentation involves granulomatous inflammation combined with suppurative changes, which overlaps histologically with tuberculosis infection. Traditional diagnostic methods for NTM—such as acid-fast staining (with low sensitivity), prolonged culture cycles (4–6 weeks), and phenotypic differentiation difficulties [7, 8]—highlight the limitations of conventional diagnostic systems. In this case, tNGS achieved accurate pathogen identification within 48 h of sample receipt. As a target-specific method, target gene sequencing analyzes characteristic genes of pre-defined microorganisms, enabling targeted analysis of intended pathogens but potentially missing rare ones due to its predefined scope. Here, the high abundance (97.04%) of target gene sequences and absence of background contamination confirmed M. fortuitum as the pathogen, highlighting its value for timely targeted therapy in detecting known pathogens [16, 17]. We consider tNGS as a valuable adjunctive diagnostic tool for wounds without clinical improvement by day 7 following standard care of animal bites, or cases refractory to empirical antimicrobial therapy [2, 18, 19].

The treatment of NTM skin infections requires individualized regimens integrating antimicrobial susceptibility testing, tissue penetration profiles, and patient-specific factors [7]. M. fortuitum generally exhibits resistance to first-line antituberculosis drugs, with amikacin demonstrating the strongest in vitro antibacterial activity, while susceptibility varies for ciprofloxacin, linezolid, imipenem, doxycycline, and clarithromycin [20–22]. For patients, priority is given to oral agents with high bioavailability and effective cutaneous tissue concentrations, while balancing safety considerations: avoiding fluoroquinolones due to skeletal toxicity risk (e.g., tendinopathy) and restricting carbapenems to preserve intestinal microbiota equilibrium. The initial empirical therapy included clarithromycin, linezolid, and amikacin. Clarithromycin was chosen for its high bioavailability, favorable cutaneous tissue penetration, and activity against rapidly growing mycobacteria (RGM), including M. fortuitum [21, 23]. Subsequent susceptibility testing, however, revealed clarithromycin resistance. Given limited laboratory capacity, we did not test for specific resistance genes like erm(39), but literature suggests that erm(39)-mediated methylation of 23 S rRNA is a primary mechanism of resistance in M. fortuitum [23]. Based on these findings, clarithromycin was replaced with doxycycline. Additionally, due to the unavailability of cefoxitin, cefmetazole was chosen as an alternative. Cefmetazole, with its broad-spectrum activity and similar antimicrobial profile, effectively covers primary pathogens and demonstrates in vitro activity against M. fortuitum [22]. This ensures comprehensive coverage of both NTM and primary pathogens (including Staphylococcus spp., Streptococcus spp., and anaerobes), addressing the polymicrobial nature of dog bite wounds. A pediatric-specific risk control system was implemented, including low-dose intermittent amikacin administration [24, 25] combined with OAE monitoring to mitigate cumulative dose-related ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity, alongside doxycycline therapy limited to ≤ 21 days [26]. The dynamic therapeutic strategy featured an intensive intravenous combination phase lasting 6 weeks, followed by an oral step-down consolidation phase for 12 weeks [7, 27]. Additionally, six staged surgical debridements were performed (weekly acute-phase/biweekly chronic-phase) to control microbial burden, particularly addressing biofilm formation and polymicrobial colonization in refractory bite wounds. This multimodal approach ultimately led to complete wound healing after over six months of intervention.

In the absence of tNGS, traditional methods—acid-fast staining, granulomatous histopathology, and prolonged cultures—remain viable for NTM identification in β-lactam-refractory animal bite wounds (> 2–4 weeks). With limited surgical support, operative room debridement and prolonged courses of accessible antimicrobials (amikacin combined with oral doxycycline or linezolid) can support healing, given that thorough debridement of NTM-associated fistulous tracts requires the controlled conditions of an operative room.

Conclusions

This case highlights the risk of NTM infections from dog bites and underscores the critical role of tNGS in rapid diagnosis. Effective management requires a combination of susceptibility-guided antimicrobial therapy and surgical intervention, with careful monitoring for adverse effects. Given the limitations of current guidelines and the single-case nature of this report, further multicentric studies are needed to refine treatment protocols. Clinicians should consider zoonotic NTM transmission in refractory SSTI and enhance awareness in bite wound management.
