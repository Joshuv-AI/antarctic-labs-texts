Procurement in German office based ambulatory physician practices: system specific challenges and evidence based management strategies
Authors: Matthias B. Stope
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Procurement is an important managerial function in healthcare, influencing supply continuity, process stability, and quality assurance. While hospital procurement has been studied extensively, evidence on procurement structures in German ambulatory care remains limited and conceptually fragmented. In particular, office based physician practices, group practices, medical care centers, outpatient hospital departments, and physician networks are often discussed under the same umbrella term, although these organizational forms differ substantially in governance, scale, and purchasing capacity.This narrative, concept driven review focuses specifically on office based ambulatory physician practices in Germany, with particular attention to small group practices in urban primary care settings. The available literature indicates that direct peer reviewed evidence quantifying procurement specific cost shares in this sector remains scarce. International physician practice economics further suggests that procurement related savings alone are unlikely to generate large absolute efficiency gains across all ambulatory settings. Procurement should therefore be understood not only as a cost factor, but also as a managerial field affecting supply reliability, workflow continuity, coordination, and resilience.Compared with hospitals, office based ambulatory practices are characterized by smaller purchasing volumes, limited economies of scale, fragmented supplier relationships, and restricted administrative capacity. Instruments such as ABC VED analyses, Kanban based replenishment, periodic stock review, and structured supplier arrangements may support procurement management in these settings. The proposed model should be interpreted as a practice oriented framework rather than as an intervention with proven large scale cost effects.

Introduction

In business administration, procurement is defined as the process of supplying an organization with materials, services, capital, and labor. It comprises planning, supplier selection, ordering, receipt, and storage, with the aim of ensuring adequate quality, timely availability, and economic efficiency [1]. Procurement is therefore a strategically relevant function influencing value creation and competitiveness [2].

Procurement includes both strategic and operational components. Strategic procurement concerns long term decisions such as supplier selection and supply chain design, whereas operational procurement addresses short term activities including demand planning, order execution, and inventory management [3]. Supplier evaluation typically follows structured multi criteria approaches that integrate cost, quality, delivery performance, flexibility, and risk [4]. Commonly applied methods include multi criteria decision making and the analytic hierarchy process [5, 6].

In healthcare, procurement contributes to balancing supply and demand, buffering fluctuations, and stabilizing operational processes [7, 8]. With increasing system complexity, procurement is also linked to resilience and supply chain stability [2, 9, 10].

Hospitals operate with large purchasing volumes, central logistics structures, and formal contracting systems [11, 12]. In contrast, office based ambulatory physician practices are smaller, more fragmented, and less formalized. These structural differences limit the transferability of hospital procurement models to ambulatory settings.

Particularities of medical procurement

Procurement in medical settings must balance economic efficiency with quality requirements, patient safety, and supply security [10, 11, 13]. Purchasing decisions therefore integrate clinical, technical, and organizational criteria.

Structured tools such as ABC VED analyses allow prioritization of materials according to economic value and clinical relevance [14]. Resilience oriented strategies, including safety stocks and supplier diversification, have been shown to improve supply stability during disruptions [15]. However, most evidence originates from hospital settings and broader healthcare supply chains rather than small ambulatory practices [12].

Ambulatory healthcare in Germany

Ambulatory care in Germany is a heterogeneous sector comprising office based physician practices, group practices, medical care centers, psychotherapeutic services, and outpatient hospital services [16–19]. These structures differ substantially in governance, scale, and administrative capacity, and are therefore not directly comparable in procurement terms.

This review focuses on office based physician practices and considers other structures only as contextual comparators [20, 21]. Office based physicians represent the primary entry point to healthcare in Germany [17, 20]. At the same time, the separation between ambulatory and inpatient care continues to influence organization and resource allocation [16].

Compared with hospitals, ambulatory practices operate with lower purchasing volumes, decentralized storage, and limited management capacity. Procurement must therefore be adapted to small scale organizational conditions.

Structural and economic challenges

Office based practices face limited bargaining power, restricted storage capacity, fragmented supplier structures, and constrained administrative resources. Procurement tasks often compete directly with clinical duties.

The available evidence does not support the assumption that procurement constitutes a major cost lever in ambulatory practices. Direct data on procurement specific cost shares in German practices remain scarce. Existing studies focus primarily on efficiency and cost functions rather than detailed cost structures [22–24]. International evidence suggests that cost structures vary by specialty and that procurement related savings alone are limited in absolute terms [25, 26].

Scale economies exist but are moderate. Empirical studies show that efficiency gains plateau at relatively small practice sizes and are not continuously increasing [25–27]. German data similarly indicate heterogeneous performance without strong scale driven effects [22–24].

Procurement should therefore be interpreted within a broader management context, including workflow stability, coordination, and resilience. Structured practice management plays a key role in handling administrative complexity [28]. Digitalization barriers further constrain procurement optimization, particularly in smaller practices [29].

Procurement systems in ambulatory practices

Practical procurement strategies in small practices rely on simple and robust approaches. Regular stock monitoring and predefined reorder points improve supply reliability. Evidence from the COVID 19 pandemic indicates that stockpiling and preparedness increase perceived supply security [30].

ABC VED classification supports prioritization of materials [14]. Kanban based replenishment systems and two bin approaches reduce ordering complexity for frequently used items [31], while periodic review is suitable for low turnover materials [32].

Framework agreements can improve standardization and reduce transaction effort, although their direct price impact may be limited in small practices [11, 13]. Cooperative purchasing models may offer advantages through pooled demand and coordination. Such models are well established internationally but insufficiently studied in German ambulatory care [33, 34].

Urban context

Urban location affects procurement through supplier density, delivery times, and competition. Short delivery routes can facilitate flexible replenishment [20, 35]. However, competition, fragmented care structures, and fluctuating patient volumes complicate planning [21, 35, 36].

Procurement efficiency in urban settings depends less on geography alone and more on structured management, transparent stock control, and coordination mechanisms.

Optimized procurement model

An optimized procurement model for a small urban group practice should focus on proportional and manageable solutions.

ABC VED classification enables prioritization of critical materials [14]. Kanban systems and two bin logic simplify replenishment [31], while periodic review reduces administrative burden [32].

Risk management includes safety stocks and secondary suppliers [15, 30]. Framework agreements improve predictability and coordination [11, 13].

Structured practice management is essential. Indicators such as stock coverage, stockout frequency, delivery reliability, and procurement related workload provide relevant performance insights [28, 37].

Cooperation between practices may create incremental benefits, but should not be assumed to generate large efficiency gains [25, 27, 33, 34].

Methodological considerations

This narrative review integrates evidence from health economics, health services research, and supply chain management. It does not follow a formal systematic review design.

Evidence on procurement in ambulatory practices is limited and indirect. Most studies address efficiency, organization, or coordination rather than procurement specifically [22, 28, 29]. Data on procurement related cost shares remain scarce.

Many management tools discussed are derived from hospital or industrial contexts [11, 12, 14, 15, 31, 33]. Their application to ambulatory practices should be interpreted as theoretical transfer.

The analysis focuses on office based physician practices. Evidence for larger ambulatory structures is less comparable.

Outlook

Procurement in ambulatory physician practices is a bounded but relevant managerial field. Its primary value lies in ensuring supply continuity, reducing coordination burdens, and supporting organizational resilience rather than generating large cost savings.

Future research should quantify procurement related costs, assess specialty specific differences, and evaluate the impact of cooperative purchasing and digital systems in ambulatory care.
