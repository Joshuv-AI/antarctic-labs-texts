Who can hold their breath? understanding individual patient’s needs and screening for deep inspiration breath hold (DIBH) suitability in rural cancer care: a retrospective evaluation
Authors: Kathleene Dower, Haryana M Dhillon, Kate Mueller, Moira O’Connor, Julan Amalaseelan, Georgia KB Halkett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Deep Inspiration Breath Hold (DIBH) is currently the accepted practice for supine left-sided BC patients undergoing radiation therapy (RT) [1]. By increasing the distance between the heart and chest wall, DIBH lowers the risk of radiation-induced cardiotoxicity and pulmonary side-effects [2]. Despite its dosimetric advantages, not all patients tolerate DIBH. The technique is non-intuitive [3] and requires patients to repeatedly sustain deep inhalations, demanding a high degree of self-efficacy, cooperation, and physical capacity [4]. Barriers to DIBH include respiratory issues [5], musculoskeletal or positioning difficulties [6], psychological distress [4], and systemic side-effects of chemotherapy [7].

Responsibility over their own treatment outcomes can exacerbate anxiety in BC patients [4], who commonly exhibit clinical levels of anxiety [8]. This anxiety can impact adversely on a patient’s ability to perform DIBH satisfactorily [4]. This inability may result in higher levels of anxiety, leading to the abandonment of DIBH-RT in favour of free breathing (FB) RT [5], rendering patients at higher risk of heart and lung damage [9], [10]. While individual studies have described coaching [5] or supportive interventions to improve DIBH tolerance [4], there is limited guidance available on structured approaches to predict or manage intolerance due to other factors. The lack of standardisation is particularly challenging in rural centres, where resources and access to allied health services are often limited [11].

At NCCI, Lismore, rural New South Wales, Australia, patients have historically attempted DIBH during simulation, with coaching provided by radiation therapists. This approach often prolonged simulation sessions [12], potentially increasing anxiety in those unable to tolerate DIBH and placing additional demands on resources [4]. Frequent DIBH intolerance prompted the development and implementation of a structured screening program designed to identify potential barriers earlier, enable targeted interventions, and streamline patient triage.

The aims of this study were to: i) determine the reasons patients were either unsuited or limited in their capacity for DIBH; ii) develop a screening program to assess DIBH suitability for prospective patients; and, iii) guide interventions to assist BC patients offered DIBH to maximise their capacity.

Methods

Design

A retrospective evaluation of patients offered DIBH between January 2020 and December 2024 at NCCI was conducted. Approval was obtained from the Northern New South Wales Local Health District (NNSWLHD) Research Office under a non-Human Research Ethics Committee pathway for low-risk projects (reference QA563).

Participants

Participants were patients with breast cancer, irrespective of laterality, who were offered DIBH-RT within the NCCI and identified through electronic medical record (eMR) review.

Data Collection and analysis

Patient factors for DIBH intolerance were extracted from the eMR by the lead author (KD) for all patients offered DIBH from 2020 to 2024. Variables extracted included age at time of treatment, chemotherapy treatment, patient self-report of acute physical issues, claustrophobia, or anxiety, and reasons for either the limitation or intolerance of DIBH. Prior to analysis, the data was deidentified and stored in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet before exporting to NVivo 15. Initial coding of the factors impacting or limiting DIBH was discussed until agreement was reached between three authors (KFD, GKH, HMD). Content analysis, specifically with manifest analysis [13], was used to identify and categorise reasons patients were either unable to tolerate DIBH or factors impacting their DIBH capacity. Four overarching categories were identified:1.Respiratory-related issues (e.g., minimal lung volume change compared to FB, unstable trace, inability to achieve or sustain a breath hold).2.Physical and pain-related limitations (e.g., musculoskeletal or positioning difficulties, chronic pain).3.Psychological barriers (e.g., anxiety, claustrophobia as self-reported by patients).4.Chemotherapy-related side effects (e.g., fatigue and other systemic toxicities) resulting in compromised lung function.

Screening program development

From 2023 onwards, a radiation therapist developed and led screening program for prospective DIBH patients was introduced. From September 2023 − December 2024, 52 patients were screened for DIBH-RT following their initial radiation oncologist consultation. Patients not screened (n = 28) included those undecided about proceeding with RT, those who did not wait for the session, where the patient’s CT simulation immediately followed their radiation oncologist appointment, where patients were offered the prone technique, or instances where a screening radiation therapist was unavailable. Patients were assessed in a mock treatment position (supine, head supported, arms elevated, knees bent) on a cushioned treatment couch to replicate the treatment setup (Fig. 1). For further comfort, support the patient’s knees with a pillow, if needed. Radiation therapists used a structured checklist, developed from our review of DIBH intolerance factors, to identify potential barriers to DIBH tolerance and to recommend supportive interventions (Table 1). During CT simulation and treatment delivery, surface-guided radiation therapy (Catalyst™, C-RAD, Sweden) was used for DIBH monitoring. Patients received real-time visual feedback of their breathing trace, supplemented by verbal coaching from radiation therapists.

Following screening, the radiation therapist assessed the patient’s DIBH versus FB status and provided further recommendations and coaching as required. The screening session was designed to complement the radiation oncologist consultation by enabling radiation therapist-led, technique-specific functional assessment of DIBH performance and positioning tolerance, which is difficult to assess within standard consultation workflows. The screening session took less than 10 min. From a human resources perspective, this represented 0.02 full-time equivalent radiation therapist time for a maximum of 8 sessions over 2 days.

Factors impacting DIBH tolerance

Several factors limiting an individual’s ability to achieve and/or sustain DIBH were identified including respiratory-related issues, physical and pain limitations, psychological concerns, and chemotherapy related side effects. DIBH was not tolerated in 75 (35%) patients, whilst 45 (21%) patients had issues that limited their DIBH capacity but did not prevent them from completing DIBH-RT.

DIBH failure

Respiratory-related limitations (n = 42; 20%) were the most common reason for DIBH failure. Physical and pain-related factors (n = 16; 7%) also negated DIBH tolerance and psychological issues (n = 12; 6%) and chemotherapy side effects (n = 5; 2%) affected smaller, subsets. Patients classified as having “no benefit compared to FB” were identified based on functional assessment rather than CT-derived volumetric or dosimetric analysis. This category included patients who demonstrated minimal observable inspiratory change during attempted DIBH relative to FB that was not clinically usable or reproducible for treatment delivery. Also included were patients who were unable to achieve a stable and reproducible breath hold using surface-guided motion monitoring. In these cases, DIBH was considered unlikely to provide practical or reproducible benefit for treatment delivery. DIBH limitations

Among patients who demonstrated DIBH capacity, pain related limitations were the most common (n = 31; 14%), followed by anxiety (n = 6, 3%), breathing instability (n = 4; 2%) and unstable traces (n = 4; 2%). While these factors limited performance, the technique was not abandoned. Interventions to facilitate patient tolerance of DIBH included analgesia, physiotherapy referral, and supportive equipment.

Other challenges requiring management

One patient had limited English skills but was supported by a translator at her radiation oncologist consultation and further RT appointments. Another patient had adverse effects from the strobe lights used for motion management and required verbal instructions for DIBH due to closing her eyes. Both successfully completed their DIBH treatment.

Screening program

Patient Screening and OutcomesOf the 52 patients screened, 32 (62%) were suitable for DIBH-RT and successfully completed treatment using this technique. Of these, 11 patients required additional interventions, including supportive measures, physiotherapy referral, analgesia, and/or psychological support to maximise DIBH tolerance and completed DIBH treatment.

DIBH intolerance was identified in 29% of the patients (n = 18) during screening. Among those unable to undertake DIBH, 11 (73%) exhibited breathing-related difficulties, including minimal observable inspiratory change relative to FB or unstable breath hold traces, resulting in breath holds that were not clinically usable or reproducible for treatment delivery. Despite coaching during the screening session, reassessment conducted with Sentinel technology in the patient’s simulation session confirmed DIBH intolerance. Two patients were considered suitable in screening but later deemed unsuitable at CT; these patients were sitting while screened. Two patients experienced chemotherapy-related complications that, despite coaching, rendered them unsuitable for DIBH. One patient, who struggled with coughing and heightened self-awareness of her breathing, concluded she was unable to sustain the required breath holds for treatment.

After refinement and regular use of the screening sessions, the duration of each session was typically less than 10 min in routine clinical practice.

Discussion

This retrospective evaluation documented causes of DIBH intolerance and limitations observed in a rural RT department, and the implementation of a screening tool to assess patients for DIBH and identify issues with DIBH.

Respiratory-related issues were noted as the primary reasons patients were unable to undertake DIBH. Specifically, patients unable to participate in DIBH had unstable traces or minimal volume difference of their deep inspiration compared to FB. These findings align with prior reports documenting the technical demands of maintaining consistent inspiratory depth and stability during DIBH [5]. To overcome these challenges, studies have described advanced, mechanically-induced DIBH [14] or respiratory coaching abdominal inspiration, both with promising results [5], [15] for breath stability and DIBH optimisation over FB.

Within our resource constrained rural department, mechanically-induced DIBH [14] would place excessive demands on resourcing. Coaching sessions, however, were deemed resource neutral as they shifted timing of radiation therapist resources (i.e. from patient’s simulation to screening session). Previous studies have shown that coaching sessions at least one week prior to treatment planning appointments increase patient adherence to DIBH [4], [15]. In the literature, coaching was reported as a positive means to meet the endpoint of performance of DIBH for the treatment planning and subsequent treatment session [4]. Additionally, coached patients reported lower levels of anxiety, deeper and longer inspiration breath holds, [4], [15] and received lower pulmonary and cardiac doses [16] than non-coached patients. While our department embedded coaching and training of DIBH into our screening program, some patients who received coaching were ultimately managed with FB following breathing issues at simulation.

Physical and pain-related barriers were commonly reported reasons for intolerance of DIBH, particularly shoulder, neck, and spinal discomfort exacerbated by positioning during treatment. As screening was conducted in the treatment position, some physical or pain-related limitations reflected intolerance of the position itself, which was at times exacerbated by breath hold attempts. Distinguishing between positioning-related limitations and DIBH-specific intolerance is clinically important, as positioning issues may be amenable to supportive measures, whereas true DIBH intolerance may require alternative treatment approaches.

While some issues were addressed with physiotherapy or supportive devices, others were chronic or worsened with repeated breath hold attempts, resulting in patients being unable to complete DIBH. While there is a dearth of literature that musculoskeletal comorbidities can reduce DIBH suitability or that DIBH exacerbates musculoskeletal tension, this study shows providing early access to supportive interventions, including ancillary equipment, physiotherapy referrals or analgesia, may increase DIBH success.

Psychological factors, particularly anxiety, contributed to DIBH intolerance and, despite preparatory coaching, the treatment technique defaulted to FB for numerous patients. While routine anxiety screening and integration of supportive care pathways are important, distinguishing treatment-specific anxiety from generalised anxiety is clinically necessary to guide appropriate intervention, as highlighted by Mayr et al. In their study, Mayr et al [4] reported breathwork training and cognitive behavioural training increased the DIBH capacity of every participant. This aligns with the rise of breathwork coaching to reduce anxiety [17] and improve DIBH tolerance.

Chemotherapy-related toxicities were recorded in a small but important subset of patients unable to complete DIBH. Patients commonly reported fatigue, reduced lung capacity, and systemic effects that prevented them from sustaining adequate breath holds. This supports literature on chemotherapy where fatigue, breathlessness and cognitive decline are noted [7].

Historically, our radiation oncologists recommended DIBH for all supine left-sided BC patients based on anticipated dosimetric benefit. Consequently, a broader, less pre-selected cohort proceeded to CT simulation, likely contributing to higher intolerance rates compared with centres that pre-screened and optimised analgesia. The screening program evaluated in this study was developed in response to these observations, aiming to identify barriers earlier and enable targeted intervention prior to CT.

A key outcome was successful implementation of a structured DIBH screening pathway that triaged patients as suitable without intervention, suitable with intervention, or unsuitable. Screening reduced prolonged or unsuccessful CT simulation appointments by identifying limitations in advance and initiating early coaching or referral, thereby optimising radiation therapist time. Patients identified as DIBH-capable required minimal coaching at CT, while those requiring additional support benefitted from early referral to multidisciplinary interventions. Our recommendations to maximise the effectiveness of the screening program and resourcing are included in Table 4. Although the screening represented an additional pre-simulation interaction, sessions were brief (<10 min) and resulted in a net reduction in workflow disruption. Importantly, the screening program did not aim to increase appointment volume, but to shift assessment earlier in the pathway, resulting in fewer prolonged or unsuccessful CT simulation sessions and clearer, earlier treatment decisions. In our rural setting, many patients travel substantial distances (at times > 100 km) to attend appointments; avoiding additional pre-simulation visits reduces both patient burden and departmental resource demands. Informal feedback from staff on the screening program indicated improved staff awareness of referral pathways and reinforcement of patient-centred care with psychologically safe communication while minimising patient anxiety when DIBH was not feasible. Also, the screening program was perceived by staff to reduce patient distress and departmental resource burden through earlier triage and clearer pre-simulation decision-making. Communication skills training for radiation therapists to assist them in reducing anxiety and distress can assist with providing psychologically safe communication with patients [18]. Clear supportive communication and shared decision-making ensure patients unsuitable for DIBH remain supported and confident in FB treatment.

Breathwork training, when successfully implemented, is known to impact positively on other diseases such as Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease [19], asthma [20], and psychological concerns [21] and may assist with the breath fitness of future patients to meet DIBH capability. Although our coaching benefitted some, optimising breathwork training in collaboration with exercise physiologists may enhance DIBH capacity of future patients.

To further reduce the resourcing burden, more research to expand the information delivery options for RT and DIBH is needed. Using the visual, auditory, and kinesiology learning framework through apps or other multimedia formats is one approach to optimise adult learning, which may be applicable to this setting [22], [23]. Multimedia formats of education also provide an opportunity to reduce the resource burden whilst meeting the educational needs of DIBH patients [6].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. A comprehensive retrospective evaluation was conducted using electronic medical records encompassing a five-year period. No patients with BC offered DIBH were excluded, reducing the risk of selection or sampling bias. DIBH tolerance before and after the implementation of a screening program was assessed and reported.

A limitation of this study was reliance on information documented in the electronic medical record, the completeness and consistency of which may have varied over time and between clinicians. Reasons for DIBH intolerance, including breathing instability or minimal observable inspiratory change relative to FB were based on contemporaneous clinical documentation. These observations, which were considered not clinically usable or reproducible, were not independently verified or re-measured, and did not include independent confirmation of internal lung expansion, introducing the potential for misclassification. The causes of DIBH intolerance may also have been multifactorial and not fully captured in clinical notes. For example, anxiety related to DIBH performance may have contributed to breathing instability but was not explored as an underlying factor in patients categorised as having respiratory limitations. Quantitative measures of breath-hold duration and reproducibility were not prospectively recorded, thus, could not be analysed as an outcome measure.

Education about DIBH, patient comprehension, and the rigour of coaching and training were not examined in this study, despite evidence that structured education and coaching can reduce anxiety and improve DIBH performance [8], [15]. This study was conducted at a single rural radiation therapy site with relatively small numbers, which may limit generalisability to centres with different patient populations, workflows, or resources. Only one patient required interpreter support; in settings with larger culturally and linguistically diverse populations, additional resourcing may be necessary to optimise DIBH participation.

Conclusion

Understanding the factors impacting on DIBH patients is important both for the supportive care of our patients and reducing resource use in departments. Interventions to improve DIBH capacity can only be introduced when these factors are identified and understood.

Our findings support routine adoption of structured screening for DIBH suitability. By determining the causes of DIBH intolerance and aligning interventions to the barriers faced, departments can maximise patient capacity for DIBH, conserve resources, and deliver patient-centred care with psychological safety. The screening program may be particularly valuable in rural departments, where reducing unnecessary patient travel and avoidable appointments is an important benefit. Future research should evaluate interventions to improve the ability of patients to participate in DIBH − our next study involves virtual reality education for DIBH preparation.
