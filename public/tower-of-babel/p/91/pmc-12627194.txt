The effects of various site preparation treatments on the control of Alnus incana and forest regeneration in boreal forests of Western Quebec, Canada
Authors: Jonathan-Kusa Kimbukusu, Nelson Thiffault, Annie DesRochers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur study, conducted in the mixed boreal forest of western Quebec, Canada, assessed the effectiveness of various site preparation treatments to promote black spruce (Picea mariana (Mill.) BSP) regeneration on wet sites invaded by speckled alder (Alnus incana subsp. rugosa (du roi) R.T. Clausen) following harvest. In 2019, four treatments were established: scalping, mulching, soil inversion, and an untreated control. Black spruce seedlings were planted in 2020. Measurements taken in 2021, at the end of the second growing season, revealed that the treatments doubled the growth and size of seedlings compared to the control group. While differences were small, seedlings in mulched plots showed significantly greater height growth and annual shoot elongation compared to those in inverted plots. The treatments also reduced competing species cover, while promoting the presence of grasses. Environmental conditions and soil properties did not vary significantly between treatments and the control group, except for pH and potassium in some cases. These results suggest that all three site preparation treatments tested are effective in establishing black spruce regeneration on wet sites invaded by speckled alder. Long-term monitoring is needed to assess the economic impact of treatments and better understand the role of alder in nitrogen availability on treated sites.Keywords: Mechanical site preparation, Scalping, Mulching, Inversion, Silviculture, Competition

Introduction

Boreal forests play a critical role in global carbon storage, biodiversity conservation, and timber production (Bradshaw and Warkentin 2015; Gauthier et al. 2015; Martin et al. 2023). However, their regeneration following natural disturbances and forest management remains a major challenge, particularly on wet sites where environmental constraints limit seedling establishment and growth (Lafleur et al. 2016). In eastern Canada, black spruce (Picea mariana (Mill.) BSP) regeneration on wet sites is primarily hindered by rapid moss growth and high water tables, with additional competition from shrubs such as speckled alder (Alnus incana subsp. rugosa (Du Roi) R.T. Clausen) further reducing seedling establishment and survival (Camill et al. 2010). While black spruce is naturally resilient (Groot 1995; Viereck and Johnston 1990), its regeneration on excessively wet soils is often compromised by high seedling mortality.

Speckled alder regenerates vigorously on wet soils through sprouting and suckering, particularly following cutting or mechanical disturbance (Jobidon 1995; Johnston 1968). This species forms a symbiotic association with Frankia, a nitrogen-fixing bacteria that enhances soil nitrogen availability (Brown and Hansen 1954), contributing to the nutrient supply in nutrient-poor boreal soils (Fortin et al. 1983; Roy et al. 2007). Additionally, alder’s dense foliage produces abundant litter, improving soil physicochemical properties and benefiting black spruce, which has a shallow root system (Fortin et al. 1983). In wooded peatlands, alder can also lower the water table (Jutras et al. 2006). However, alder encroachment is stimulated by increased light levels and water availability following harvesting, often occurring at the expense of conifer regeneration (Brown and Hansen 1954; Healy and Gill 1974). As a result, black spruce regeneration in managed mixed boreal forests may be compromised by alder competition (Jobidon 1995). To ensure the successful re-establishment of black spruce in harvested forests, effective site preparation treatments are needed to limit alder expansion and promote conifer growth.

Several site preparation methods have been tested in boreal mixedwood stands, but challenges remain (Lieffers et al. 2003). Scalping involves removing the organic layer to expose mineral soil, creating planting microsites (Prévost 1992; Thiffault 2016). By eliminating competition from the forest floor, it improves soil temperature, as mineral soils warm up more quickly than those covered by insulating organic layers (von der Gönna 1992). Warmer soils can accelerate root growth in conifer seedlings (Vapaavuori et al. 1992), which is particularly beneficial in cold environments. However, scalping also removes a significant portion of soil organic matter, potentially reducing nutrient availability (Prévost and Thiffault 2013). Additionally, in clay-rich soils, large temperature fluctuations due to exposure may increase the risk of frost heaving, leading to seedling mortality (Goulet 2000). While scalping is often combined with mounding on sloped terrain to regulate soil moisture (von der Gönna 1992), its effectiveness on wet sites remains unclear, requiring further assessment of its impact on soil properties and black spruce growth.

Inversion is another site preparation technique that involves flipping the organic layer and covering it with loosened mineral soil, forming a stable planting microsite (Örlander et al. 1998; Löf et al. 2012). This method improves soil conditions by increasing temperature and aeration, which accelerates root development and nutrient mineralization (Hallsby and Örlander 2004). In contrast to traditional mounding, inversion avoids excessive surface disturbance while still improving moisture retention and drainage (Hallsby and Örlander 2004). It has shown promising results in enhancing conifer seedling growth, outperforming disc trenching and mounding after several growing seasons in Scandinavia (Örlander et al. 1998; Hallsby and Örlander 2004). Additionally, deeper soil warming in spring (Salonius 1983; von der Gönna 1992) may reduce susceptibility to late frosts and drought stress, further benefiting black spruce establishment. However, a major limitation of inversion is its inability to control competing vegetation in the long term, as buried seeds in the soil seed bank often lead to rapid regrowth of competing species (Löf et al. 2012; Prévost and Thiffault 2013). Its potential role in improving nutrient cycling through buried organic matter decomposition remains to be fully understood, particularly in nitrogen-limited boreal forests (Buitrago et al. 2015).

Mulching involves mechanically shredding post-harvest plant material into a fine layer of mulch that remains on the soil surface (Stefani et al. 2023). In the short term, this method effectively suppresses competing vegetation by physically covering the soil and reducing light availability for germinating weeds (Chauvel and Martinez 2013). Mulch also helps retain soil moisture by reducing evaporation and buffering temperature fluctuations (Lanphear and Spangler 1996), although it can lower soil temperature (Mallik and Kravchenko 2016). While mulching has been shown to enhance early seedling growth and reduce competition from invasive species in other forest ecosystems (e.g., Mallik and Kravchenko 2016), its specific effects on black spruce regeneration and speckled alder suppression in boreal conditions remain unknown. The long-term impact of mulching on nutrient dynamics and soil microbial activity also warrants further investigation, particularly in relation to nitrogen availability and organic matter decomposition in wet boreal sites.

Mechanical site preparation plays a critical role in improving environmental conditions and nutrient availability for reforestation (Sikström et al. 2020; Thiffault et al. 2025). However, conifer establishment on wet boreal sites remains a challenge, and the effectiveness of scalping and inversion in such conditions is poorly documented. Moreover, the role of mulching in controlling alder competition and influencing soil properties is unknown. Our objective was thus to evaluate the early effects of scalping, inversion, and mulching on black spruce growth in wet, alder-dominated sites in northwestern Quebec, Canada. We hypothesized that these treatments would reduce competing vegetation and create favorable conditions for black spruce early growth and foliar nutrition. Specifically, we predicted that scalping and inversion would improve soil temperature and that all site preparation treatments would improve soil moisture conditions, promoting nutrient mobilization and seedling establishment. We also expected that early growth of black spruce would be inversely proportional to the cover of competing vegetation, while foliar nitrogen concentrations would be higher in untreated plots with abundant alder due to its nitrogen-fixing capacity.

Materials and methods

Study site

Our project leverages sites invaded by speckled alder following harvesting in the Lake Duparquet Teaching and Research Forest (FERLD). The study is located in the western mixed boreal forest of Quebec, Canada, characterized by a cold and dry continental climate, and within the balsam fir (Abies balsamea (L.) Mill.) − white birch (Betula papyrifera Marsh.) bioclimatic domain (Saucier et al. 2009). The study area comprises two distinct sectors: an eastern sector where clear-cutting was conducted between 2008 and 2009, and a western sector where clear-cutting occurred between 1945 and 1972 (Bescond 2002). Our research was conducted within a stand divided into four blocks exhibiting similar characteristics. The average annual temperature in the study area was 1.7 °C, with average annual precipitation of 985 mm between 1981 and 2010 (Environment Canada 2019). The landscape is characterized by a lowland relief and is covered with extensive wetlands (Bergeron et al. 1983). On the surface, predominantly glaciolacustrine clay deposits are found, with mesic to subhydric drainage, as well as wet organic deposits associated with the sites (Bergeron et al. 1983).

Experimental design and sampling

The study area comprises two sectors with contrasting management histories. The eastern sector was clear-cut in 2010, treated with mulching, and replanted with black spruce in 2011. By the time treatments were installed in fall 2019, the site supported a dense cover of speckled alder, a closed shrub layer that limited light, and only scattered spruce seedlings. In contrast, the western sector had not been harvested or replanted since the last cut in 1972 and, at treatment installation, was dominated by mature alder with very limited natural conifer regeneration.

At the end of autumn 2019, we delineated four blocks with one located in the eastern sector and three located in the western sector. Each block measured 50 m × 100 m and was divided into four main plots, each of which randomly received one of four mechanical site preparation treatments: scalping, mulching, inversion, and an untreated control (Fig. 1). The mulching treatment could not be conducted in one of the replicate blocks for operational reasons, thus resulting in an incomplete block design. In total, 15 experimental units (EUs) were established.

Description of the mechanical site preparation treatments and field data collection in the experimental units of a silviculture experiment conducted in northwestern Quebec, Canada

We used a 14.5-ton hydraulic excavator to create microsites in the scalping and inversion treatments. Scalping involved removing the organic matter (OM) down to the mineral soil to form patches about 15 cm deep. In the inversion treatment, the OM layer and mineral soil was excavated to a depth of 1 m, upturned and replaced in its original hole. In total, we created the equivalent of 200 patches/ha for scalping and inversion. Each patch measured about 5 m × 3 m (15 m2) and was spaced 3 m from the nearest patch. We used a roller chopper (Denis Cimag DAH100c head) to perform vegetation mulching and avoiding affecting the OM. All organic debris were left on the soil surface.

In early summer 2020, we planted all blocks with large-sized black spruce seedlings grown in 25-cavity containers of 310 cm3 each from a local seed source. Six black spruce seedlings were planted in each of the scalping and inversion treatment patches, at a density equivalent to 1200 seedlings/ha, and no seedlings were planted outside of the patches. In the control and mulching plots where the surface area was treated in full, seedlings were regularly spaced and planted at a density of 2500 seedlings/ha (Fig. 1).

Field data collection

In early summer 2021, we set up five circular plots of 100 m2 (radius of 5.64 m) per EU for a total of 75 plots (Fig. 1). Between June 1 st and September 28th, 2021, we measured soil temperature and moisture at a 10 cm depth from the soil surface using 30 weather micro-stations (WatchDog 1000 Series) evenly distributed in two plots for each experimental unit. Monthly average moisture and temperature were calculated based on the hourly recordings.

At the end of the growing season (2021), we measured height (cm), ground-level diameter (mm), and annual shoot length (mm) of all planted seedlings inside the 100 m2 circular plots. Measurements were taken on a total of 390 living black spruce seedlings in the inversion and scalping treatments, and on 248 living seedlings in the control and mulching treatments. We established 1 m2 micro-plots (radius of 0.56 m) in each plot for visual estimation of competing vegetation cover by functional groups of interest (alder, trees, shrubs, ferns, grasses) and for soil sampling. We visually assessed vegetation cover by classes (0%; 0–1%; 1.1–5.1%; 5.1–10%; 11–20%; 21–30%; 31–40%; 41–50%; 51–60%; 61–75%; and > 75%) and averaged observations from two observers (Vanha-Majamaa et al. 2000). In the inversion and scalping treatment plots, we established six micro-plots, two located in each of the disturbed patches and spaced 1 m apart, and two on undisturbed soil for soil sampling only (Fig. 1). In control and mulching treatment plots, we established five micro-plots, with four at the cardinal points and one in the centre. In total, 115 soil samples (≥ 200 g) were taken at a depth of 0–10 cm to determine total N, total C, P, K, Ca, Mg concentrations, and pH. Soil samples collected from outside the patches of inversion and scalping, but located within the same plot, were combined to form a single composite sample for each plot (Fig. 1). In contrast, soil samples taken from inside the micro-plots of the mulching and control treatments, also located within the plots of the same experimental unit, were grouped. In total, 75 samples were obtained from the treatments included in our study (1 sample per plot × 5 plots per experimental unit × 15 experimental units), while the other 40 samples came from the undisturbed areas, that is, undisturbed soils located outside the inversion and scalping patches (1 sample per plot × 5 plots per experimental unit × 4 blocks). In the fall of 2021, we also collected one composite current-year foliar samples from all living planted seedlings in each micro-plot.

Using a LAI-2200 plant canopy analyzer (LiCOR Inc., U.S.A.), we measured incident light intensity at 1 m above ground level in each EU. In the mulching and control treatment plots, we measured light at the center of the micro-plots, while in the scalping and inversion treatment plots, we measured light at the center of each patch within the micro-plots. We also took concomitant full sunlight measurements in an open area near each block, above any vegetation cover, if present. Percent incident light was calculated as the ratio of light measured in treatment plots compared to available full sunlight.

Laboratory analyses

Foliar and soil samples were brought to the laboratory and kept refrigerated at 2 °C for two weeks. We used a 1:3 ratio (soil/distilled water) to determine the pH of all soil samples using a pH meter (Thermo Scientific ORION STAR A 211, Indonesia). The remaining soil samples were weighed and dried in an oven at 70 °C for 48 h. Each soil sample was manually ground using a mortar and sieved to 2 mm before analysis. Foliar samples were weighed, dried at 65 °C for 48 h, reweighed to confirm the absence of moisture, and ground in a Cyclone mill with a 1 mm mesh (UDY Corp., Fort Collins, CO). Total N and total C concentrations were analyzed by dry combustion (Vario MAX cube; Elementar, Langenselbold, Germany). Extractable P, K, Ca, and Mg were extracted in a Mehlich-III solution and measured by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (Optima 4300 DV; Perkin-Elmer, Norwalk, CT).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.1.1 (R Development Core Team 2021). Analyses of site preparation treatment effects on seedling height, diameter, annual shoot length, pH, concentrations of foliar and soil nutrients, as well as soil moisture, soil temperature, light, and competing vegetation cover were subjected to linear mixed-effects model analyses using the lme function from the lme4 library (Bates et al. 2015). Block (replicates) was considered as a random effect; site preparation treatments were considered as fixed effects.

Model assumptions were tested by examining the homogeneity and normality of variance using graphical approaches. The model assumptions were met without data transformation. A threshold of α = 0.05 was used to identify significant effects. Comparisons between treatments were conducted using mean comparison tests with Tukey’s test (Multcomp). The emmeans package was used as a post hoc method to perform pairwise comparisons when a significant effect was found for a variable (Lenth et al. 2018).

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to visualize the relationships between height, diameter, annual shoot growth, foliar nutrients, soil nutrients, light, temperature, moisture, and soil pH in each site preparation treatments. PCA was conducted using the PCA function from the FactoMineR library with correlation matrices (Lê et al. 2008).

Results

Height, diameter, and annual shoot elongation

We observed that all site preparation treatments increased black spruce height growth relative to the control. Mulching produced slightly greater total height and annual shoot elongation than scalping and inversion (differences of ~ 1–2 cm in height and ~ 1 cm in shoot elongation per year), but these differences were modest. For ground level diameter, all three treatments resulted in significantly larger values than the control, with no significant differences among the treatments (Fig. 2).

Competing vegetation

Mechanical site preparation treatments significantly reduced the cover of alder, trees, shrubs, and ferns (Fig. 3). Grass cover was important in all site preparation treatments, and increased above the control in the scalping and inversion treatments. Tree species percent cover was greater in the control and mulching treatments compared to the scalping and inversion treatments, which showed significantly lower values. Compared to seedlings growing in the mechanical site preparation treatments, the environment of seedlings in the control treatment was characterized by a significantly higher cover of competing species groups (Fig. 3).

Nutrient concentrations in soil and needles

Two years after treatment application, significant differences in soil chemical properties were observed among treatments. Overall, the site preparation methods did not result in differences in soil N, C, and P, but led to significant differences in soil K, Ca, Mg, and pH (Table 1). Soil K concentration significantly increased in the plots subjected to inversion and in the off-plots of the scalping treatment compared to the control, while in the scalping, mulching, and off-plots of the inversion treatment, K values were intermediate. Additionally, the inversion treatment resulted in a significant increase in soil Ca compared to scalping, while scalping, mulching, off-plots of the inversion treatment, and control plots showed intermediate values. The inversion treatment also significantly increased soil Mg compared to the control, while scalping plots (in and off-plots), mulching, and off-plots of the inversion treatment had intermediate Mg values. The inversion method increased soil pH compared to the off-plots of the scalping treatment, mulching plots, and control, while in the scalping plots and off-plots of inversion, soil pH was intermediate and slightly lower compared to inversion. Statistical analyses indicated that scalping and inversion treatments significantly influenced soil chemical properties in a manner similar to that of off-plots.

Relationships between soil and foliar variables and the height, diameter, and annual shoot growth of black spruce

Overall, the PCA explained 34% of the variance in the data along a fertility gradient (Fig. 4). We observed a strong positive relationship between height and diameter of the seedlings with soil pH and carbon in the inversion, scalping, and mulching treatments. Unlike scalping and inversion, seedling growth was not associated with light availability in the mulching treatment. Height and diameter of the seedlings were not associated with soil temperature. Soil moisture was positively associated with N mobilization and annual shoot growth of seedlings across all treatments. We found that foliar concentrations of N, P, Ca, and Mg of the seedlings were strongly associated with the mulching treatment compared to scalping, inversion, and control treatments. We found that environmental conditions of the control treatment were less varied and did not favor the development of seedlings in height and diameter. The overlap of ellipses of the scalping, mulching, and inversion treatments, compared to the control on both axes indicates similarity in conditions among treatments. Among the four treatments, the ellipse of inversion was larger and more spread out than those of the scalping, mulching, and control treatments.

Discussion

Our results show that mechanical site preparation significantly increased the height, annual shoot length, and diameter of the planted seedlings compared to those planted in untreated (control) plots. However, our findings revealed that seedlings planted in the mulching treatment grew taller than those planted in inversion.

The improved height growth in the mulching treatment can be attributed to more effective control of ground-level grass vegetation compared to scalping and inversion. Indeed, the layer of organic matter left on the ground during mulching seems to have acted as a barrier, delaying the rapid regeneration of trees, shrubs, alders, ferns, and especially grasses competing for resources during the summer. Due to their dense rhizome network, grasses exert strong competition for nutrients in young coniferous stands, to the detriment of black spruce growth (Landhaüsser and Lieffers 1998; Chomel et al. 2020); their dense swards can also reduce early-season light at seedling height and, when dominated by Calamagrostis spp., increase the risk of snow press on seedlings. Therefore, as the early years of black spruce seedling planting are crucial for their establishment, low grass coverage in mulching plots would have allowed the shallow roots of black spruce to better absorb available nutrients. Another possible explanation is that soil warming, and higher water availability allowed for better soil nutrient mobilization and seedling development. The higher temperature in the root zone probably increased nutrient availability, sugars (Barba et al. 2015), and hormones, including cytokinins, which stimulate vegetative bud break (Sachs and Thimann 1967; Bronson et al. 2009). Such stimulation may, however, advance bud break and, in some years or microsites, increase vulnerability to late spring frost (Marquis et al. 2021).

The results obtained for soil and needles are apparently inconsistent. Soil sample chemical analysis revealed no differences between scalping, mulching, inversion, and control treatments regarding soil N concentrations, while needle N concentration was higher in the mulching treatment than in the inversion treatment and similar to scalping and control treatments. This suggests a positive effect of mulching on N nutrition for young black spruce seedlings planted boreal forest soils. Through their superficial dispersion, black spruce roots likely rapidly absorbed N from the decomposition of alder organic matter and other plants left aboveground. High temperatures and high humidity also favored decomposition, optimizing nitrogen availability for root uptake.

The inversion treatment resulted in a significant change in edaphic conditions: it warmed the soil, reduced its humidity and induced a very acid pH. While these conditions favor the decomposition of organic matter and contribute to the nutrition of black spruce (von der Gönna 1992), they also appear to be a limiting factor for optimal seedling growth. Indeed, the acid pH in the inversion treatment restricts nutrient uptake by roots, particularly when organic matter is buried to a depth of around 1 m, making these nutrients less accessible. This limitation is reflected in the weak correlation between seedling height and soil nitrogen concentration in the principal component analysis (Fig. 4). Furthermore, all treatments improved seedling diameter relative to the control, with similar magnitudes across treatments and irrespective of light availability. This likely reflects effective suppression of competing vegetation (trees, shrubs, alder, and ferns), to which diameter is particularly sensitive (Jobidon 2000; Thiffault et al. 2014). Alternatively, it may indicate that residual competition was broadly similar, and not strongly limiting, across the three treatments, consistent with the view that diameter growth is more responsive to competition than height (Lanner 1985).

While scalping is recognized as potentially reducing soil nutrient availability (Prévost and Thiffault 2013), we found a significant improvement in soil chemical properties following scalping, as well as in all treated plots and scalping treatment non-plot areas. Despite scalping and other treatments applied not resulting in differences in soil N, C, and P, these treatments had significant effects on K, Ca, Mg availability, as well as soil pH. We also observed that scalping and inversion treatments significantly influenced soil chemical properties in the same way as their non-plot areas. One explanation for these results would be the increase in soil water in the scalping treatment; snowmelt may have transported nutrients to scalping microsites, thereby improving soil fertility and pH, which benefited planted seedlings. Indeed, we observed a close relationship between soil moisture and N availability in the scalped zone (Fig. 4). With the inversion method, soil acidity was significantly reduced compared to mulching treatments, control, and scalping non-plot areas. Additionally, inversion significantly increased soil K, Ca, and Mg compared to the control, while Ca concentration in inversion was higher than in scalping. These results would be related to significant soil warming. Upturning the surface organic layer likely created better conditions for root growth, facilitated nutrient mobilization, and improved soil physicochemical properties, explaining the wide ellipse of the inversion treatment in the principal component analysis (Fig. 4). Upturning mineral soil over organic soil in the same location favors faster microsite warming in summer, as well as deeper warming, making nutrients available (Salonius 1983; von der Gönna 1992).

The increased N concentration in needles in the mulching treatment may be due to dilution related to potential differences in seedling biomass in the different site preparation treatments (Imo and Timmer 1998). A greater amount of N was absorbed by seedlings producing longer annual shoots, as shown by the relationship between soil N and annual shoot length of black spruce seedlings planted in mulching and scalping treatments (Fig. 4). Another possible explanation is the low grass coverage in the mulching treatment compared to inversion and scalping. Seedlings planted in the mulching treatment were spared from strong competition for nutrients caused by grass rhizomes and benefited from quickly absorbing N mobilized in the soil by organic debris and living alder individuals. Although inversion showed a significant increase in soil K, Ca, and Mg compared to the control, and high Ca concentration compared to scalping, foliar concentration analysis showed no significant difference in needles concentrations. Such a result would be related to mass flow. This is the primary process by which plants absorb K, Ca, and Mg (McGonigle and Grant 2015), so low absorption may reflect low water flow through seedling stems. This is consistent with the lower water content observed in the inversion treatment. Additionally, the wetter and warmer soil conditions in the mulching treatment would have contributed to greater N absorption by black spruce seedlings.

Overall, our results show that mechanical site preparation has the potential to restore productivity of forest sites with significant alder encroachment. Longer-term monitoring is needed, though, to determine if interactions between alder and replanted black spruce are sustained. The growth differences between treatments were small and may not hold in the future, but they may give seedlings a small advantage to survive the early phase of establishment under these harsh growing conditions. Although all three treatments improved regeneration outcomes, we were unable to formally assess cost-effectiveness, as the experimental context does not provide reliable operational per-hectare estimates. Future research should combine operational trials with time-and-motion studies and post-treatment survival assessments to calculate both cost per hectare and cost per successfully established seedling across treatments. Based on expert knowledge and cost estimates calculated Canadian technical sources (e.g., Cormier and Paterson 1997; Thiffault et al. 2020), it is likely that scalping and mulching are the most cost-effective options on a per-hectare basis (approximately 1,200 CAN$ ha−1), compared with inversion (approximately 1,500 CAN$ ha−1). When accounting for planting density and cost per established seedling, mulching appears to offer the most cost-effective approach overall.
