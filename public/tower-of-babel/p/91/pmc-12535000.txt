Understanding sex-related differences among Asian axial spondyloarthritis patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Prayash Paudel, Asutosh Sah
Published: 2025

Introduction

Axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) is a chronic inflammatory, immune-mediated disease that primarily affects the spine and sacroiliac joints and has significant implications for physical function, mobility, and quality of life [1],. AxSpA was historically considered a male disease, primarily because early epidemiological research reported male-to-female prevalence ratios of up to 10:1 [2]. However, more current numbers show that non-radiographic AxSpA (nr-axSpA) emerges on average at roughly the same rate in women as in men but that r-axSpA, also ankylosing spondylitis, is more common in males.[1, 3]

Despite these changing epidemiological patterns, sex differences persist in disease presentation, delay in diagnosis, treatment response, and overall disease burden. [4–6] Compared with male patients, female patients with axSpA have longer diagnostic delays, higher disease activity scores, more extensive disease, and poorer responses to biologic therapy. [1–3, 7] These variations are more pronounced in Asian populations, in which genetic causes (e.g., lower HLA-B27 positivity in females), cultural disparities, and the accessibility of health care may even contribute to late diagnosis and suboptimal disease control.[2, 7, 8].

Given these sex-specific distinctions, there is a growing need to reassess diagnostic criteria, treatment methods, and healthcare strategies for axSpA among women, particularly in Asian contexts, for which evidence remains minimal and underexamined. [6, 9] This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to comprehensively examine sex differences in clinical presentation, diagnostic delay, therapeutic response, and health-related quality of life among Asian axSpA patients, bridging critical gaps in the present research.[1, 2]

Patient knowledge of axSpA as a condition in both men and women has increased over the past two decades, but certain sex-specific knowledge gaps remain. [5, 6] Women with axSpA also frequently present with atypical symptoms such as greater peripheral joint involvement, widespread musculoskeletal pain, and greater fatigue; therefore, these patients are often misdiagnosed with fibromyalgia. [1, 5, 8] Thus, women patients experience a longer diagnostic delay than men do, and recent meta-analyses place this estimate at 8.8 years in women versus 6.5 years in men.[9]

In Asian populations, there are additional challenges presented by sociocultural beliefs and healthcare barriers. Asian women with axSpA present late to medical services, possibly due to a lower perception of the severity of back pain, family responsibilities, and sex biases in healthcare systems. [7, 9] Underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis could also be caused by a lack of awareness among primary care physicians about the presentation of axSpA in women.[6]

With these parameters in mind, this systematic review aims to assess sex differences in axSpA patients among Asians in general, with a particular focus on age of onset, age at diagnosis, disease duration, diagnostic delay, clinical presentation, and extra-articular manifestations.

Method

Study protocol

The study protocol, with well-defined methodology and inclusion criteria, was registered on PROSPERO with registration number CRD42025637163. The review is carried out in accordance to PRISMA Statement 2020. The PRISMA 2020 checklist and PRISMA 2020 for Abstracts Checklist are included in S3_Appendix and S4_Appendix respectively.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) involved male and female patients diagnosed with axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) in an Asian population, (2) reported sex-specific data on clinical presentation, diagnostic delay, treatment response, or health-related quality of life, and (3) were observational or interventional studies published in peer-reviewed journals.

Exclusion criteria included (1) studies that did not differentiate outcomes by sex, (2) case reports, reviews, editorials, or conference abstracts without full-text availability, and (3) studies with insufficient or unclear data on relevant outcomes. For synthesis, studies were grouped based on reported outcomes, including demographic characteristics, disease burden, extra-articular manifestations, and treatment responses. Where applicable, meta-analysis was performed using standardized effect measures to compare differences between male and female axSpA patients.

Search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the principles of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses. The PubMed, Embase and Google Scholar databases were searched up to the 30th January 2025. Studies were also obtained from supplementary sources, manual searches and other repositories. Cross-references from the published articles were manually searched to retrieve additional literature.

To create an extensive search strategy that encompassed all fields in the records as well as Medical Subject Headings (MeSH words) for broadening the search in an advanced PubMed search, the predefined phrases were identified. For the PubMed database, medical subject headings (MeSH terms) and relevant keywords were systematically combined via Boolean operators (AND, OR) to identify records. Details of the preliminary search strategy are provided in the S1_Appendix. Similarly, for Embase, Emtree terms and keywords were combined with Boolean operators to ensure the comprehensive retrieval of relevant studies. For Google Scholar, the keywords “axial spondyloarthritis”, “sex differences”, and individual Asian country names were used to identify relevant studies for this meta-analysis.

Selection of studies

The literature search was performed by PP. The included studies were exported to Google Sheets in compatible format. Duplicate articles were screened manually. Duplicates were then recorded and removed. After removing duplicates, two independent authors, PP and AS, screened the title and abstract of every article that remained. Full-text articles were obtained for the relevant studies satisfying the inclusion criteria. The data were extracted by the two authors, PP and AS, independently. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus among the authors. In this review, sex/sex was determined via self-report, with participants providing their own identification.

Consideration of sex in study design

For systematic approaches to the use of sex information in study design, data analysis, outcome reporting and research interpretation, Sex and Sex Equity in Research (SAGER) guidelines was followed. The SAGER checklist is attached as S5_Appendix.

Only studies with sex-disaggregated data on axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) were included to compare male and female patients. Sex was determined through self-report, as no studies used genetic testing. Single-sex studies were excluded unless they provided relevant comparisons. This review did not impose sex-specific interventions like contraception requirements or pregnancy-related exclusions, as it focused on real-world observational and interventional studies. Potential biases from sex-specific study designs were considered in the risk-of-bias evaluation.

Data extraction

The following data were extracted from the study: author, number of male and female patients, participant age, age at disease onset, age at diagnosis, disease duration, and delay in diagnosis. Additional clinical characteristics included HLA-B27 positivity, axial manifestations, peripheral arthritis, uveitis, enthesitis, dactylitis, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and psoriasis. Disease activity and functional impairment were assessed via the BASDAI, BASFI, BASMI, and ASDAS-CRP scores was evaluated. Laboratory parameters such as C-reactive protein (CRP) levels and the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) were recorded, along with radiological progression measured by the BASRI and ASDAS-ESR. The data were stratified by sex to facilitate comparative analysis.

Quality assessment of the studies

The risk of bias for each identified study was assessed independently by the authors via the ROBINS-I Version 2 tool, which assesses seven domains of bias: confounding, participant selection, and outcome measurement. The tool compares the results of a study to those of a hypothetical “target trial” and categorizes the bias as low, moderate, serious, or critical. Details of each identified study are provided in the S2_Appendix.

Statistical analysis

The data collected from the Google Sheets were exported, and analysis was performed via R and Jamovi 2.3.28. Continuous outcomes, including age of onset, age at diagnosis, disease duration, diagnostic delay, BASDAI, BASFI, BASMI, BASRI, ASDAS-ESR, ASDAS-CRP, ESR, and CRP, were analyzed via R, with the primary effect measure being the mean difference (MD) or standardized mean difference (SMD). For dichotomous outcomes, such as HLA-B27 positivity, extra-articular manifestations, and articular involvement, the proportion and log odds ratio (OR) were calculated from the raw proportions. Heterogeneity was assessed visually via forest plots and statistically via the I2 statistic and Cochrane’s Q test. A heterogeneity test was considered statistically significant when p ≤ 0.05. In such cases, a random-effects model was applied; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was used for analysis. Pooled estimates are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Publication bias was assessed visually through funnel plots and statistically using Egger’s regression test for outcomes where 10 or more studies contributed. Prespecified sensitivity analyses evaluated robustness by: (i) repeating each meta-analysis after excluding studies judged at serious or critical risk of bias; (ii) pooling studies separately by diagnostic definition (ASAS-only and modified New York-only); and (iii) performing leave-one-out (influence) analyses for each pooled outcome. We considered a change-in-estimate > 20% or a loss of statistical significance as evidence of non-robustness; otherwise, results were deemed robust.

Data availability statement

The data that supports the findings of this study are available in the supplementary material of this article -S6_Appendix.

Results

Study selection

The search of PubMed, Embase, and Google Scholar identified a total of 2350 studies. After removing duplicates, 1583 studies remained. Following a review of abstracts, 1,269 studies were excluded as they did not meet the criteria. Additionally, 263 studies were removed due to unavailable full texts. The remaining 51 studies were assessed in full, leading to the exclusion of 21 owing to irrelevant data. Ultimately, 30 studies were included in the review. The PRISMA flow diagram is in Fig. 1.

Preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) diagram detailing the study identification and selection process

Several studies initially appeared to meet the inclusion criteria but were excluded upon full-text review due to reasons such as lack of sex-stratified data, insufficient reporting of key outcomes, or study design limitations (e.g., case reports, reviews, or conference abstracts)

Study characteristics

All 30 studies ultimately selected for the review were published in English. The included studies involved a total of 31,523 male and 10,815 female participants. Across these studies, data were collected on various demographic and clinical parameters, including age at presentation, age at onset, age at diagnosis, disease duration, and delay in diagnosis. Additionally, studies have assessed the prevalence of HLA-B27 positivity and the distribution of axial manifestations, peripheral arthritis, and extra-articular manifestations such as uveitis, enthesitis, dactylitis, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and psoriasis. Disease activity, severity, and progression were evaluated via multiple indices, including the BASDAI, BASFI, BASMI, ASDAS-CRP, ASDAS-ESR, and radiological progression (BASRI). Other inflammatory markers, such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), were also recorded. The timing of the outcome assessment varied across studies. Risk of bias in studies

We used the ROBINS-I V2 tool to assess the risk of bias for each of the included studies. Overall, the majority of studies were judged to have concerns regarding the risk of bias across various domains, with no studies classified as having a critical risk of bias. A detailed breakdown of the risk-of-bias assessment for each included study across the seven domains, along with the reasoning, is provided in the S2_Appendix. Several key outcomes in this meta-analysis were influenced by missing results and reporting biases, leading to varying degrees of risk. Diagnostic delay was assessed in 16 studies, but inconsistencies in measurement (self-reported vs. clinician-confirmed delays) and selective reporting in retrospective cohorts resulted in extreme heterogeneity (I2 = 96.1%), introducing a moderate risk of bias. Disease activity (BASDAI) was reported in 9 studies, although missing sex-stratified results and reliance on self-reported measures contributed to high heterogeneity (I2 = 73.6%) and moderate bias risk. Radiographic progression (BASRI) was reported in a single study (Almousa et al. 2022), with several studies not stratifying results by sex or using non-validated indices, leading to a high risk of bias due to selective reporting. HLA-B27 positivity data were available in 14 studies, although missing subgroup data and potential underreporting in female cohorts resulted in a moderate risk of bias. Treatment adherence was reported in only three studies, with missing sex-specific data in five others, making it impossible to pool the results and thus carrying a high risk of bias.

Overall, variability in measurement, selective reporting, and missing subgroup data introduced moderate to high risk of bias across several syntheses. Diagnostic delay and HLA-B27 positivity were notably affected by inconsistent reporting, whereas radiographic progression and treatment adherence syntheses relied on limited studies, reducing generalizability. These gaps highlight the need for standardized reporting and sex-stratified analyses in future axial spondyloarthritis research.

Sex-stratified reporting was incomplete across several outcomes. As concrete examples, BASDAI was available from only 9 studies, HLA-B27 positivity from 14, uveitis from 18, and treatment response/adherence from just 3; radiographic progression (BASRI) came from a single study. Beyond these, sex-stratified data were inconsistently reported for BASFI, BASMI, ASDAS-ESR/CRP, ESR, CRP, peripheral arthritis, enthesitis, dactylitis, psoriasis, and inflammatory bowel disease. Accordingly, each meta-analysis reflects only the subset of studies that provided sex-specific results, which can introduce selective-reporting bias and limit generalizability if contributing studies differ systematically from those that did not stratify by sex.

Result synthesis

Study characteristics

A total of 30 studies investigating sex differences in Asian patients with axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) were included. Most studies exhibited a moderate risk of bias as assessed by the ROBINS-I tool, although some raised serious concerns regarding confounding and participant selection. Variability in sample sizes, study designs, and diagnostic criteria contributed to heterogeneity across outcomes.

Disease onset, diagnosis and duration

Males demonstrated a significantly earlier age of onset (pooled mean difference [MD]: −3.48 years; 95% CI: −4.78 to −2.17; p = 0.000141) and younger age at diagnosis (MD: −3.57 years; 95% CI: −5.25 to −1.89; p = 0.002) than females did, with high (I2 = 67.0%, τ2 = 2.51) and moderate (I2 = 58.8%, τ2 = 1.99) heterogeneity, respectively. Diagnostic delay did not differ significantly between genders (MD: −0.11 years; 95% CI: −1.51, −1.30; p = 0.87), although extreme heterogeneity (I2 = 96.1%) was observed, likely due to variations in diagnostic practices and atypical symptom presentation in females. Disease duration was longer in males (MD: 1.92 years; 95% CI: 0.48–3.35; p = 0.0136; I2 = 74.7%). Clinical presentation

Clinical features revealed sex disparities: the incidence of enthesitis was lower in males (25.6% vs. 32.6%; logOR = −0.349, p < 0.001; I2 = 98.73% in males, 96.68% in females), as was the incidence of peripheral arthritis (33.2% vs. 40.1%; logOR = −0.278, p = 0.045; I2 = 97.47% in males, 92.63% in females). Males also presented reduced risks for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD: 2.5% vs. 2.96%; logOR = −0.133, p = 0.029) and psoriasis (4.2% vs. 4.6%; logOR = −0.0968, p = 0.036). The incidence of uveitis was 16.6% in males (95% CI: 1.26–20.6; I2 = 97.16%), whereas it was 19.2% in females (95% CI: 12.4–25.9; I2 = 91.1%), with no significant sex association (logOR = −0.201, p = 0.238). The incidence of dactylitis was comparable between sexes (males: 3.68%, 95% CI: 2.4–4.9; females: 3.95%, 95% CI: 0.3–7.6), with no significant association (logOR = −0.569, p = 0.087). HLA-B27 positivity was markedly greater in males (79.5% vs. 69.2%; logOR = 0.526, p < 0.001; I2 = 35.54%), with moderate heterogeneity attributed to regional genetic variability. Disease activity, severity and progression

Females presented increased disease activity, as reflected by elevated BASDAI scores (MD: −0.57; 95% CI: −0.86 to −0.27; p = 0.00211; I2 = 73.6%) (Fig. 6) and inflammatory marker levels (ESR: MD: −3.99 mm/h; p = 0.0476; I2 = 77.0%) as in Fig. 7. BASMI scores (Fig. 8) showed a nonsignificant trend toward higher values in males (MD: 0.81; 95% CI: −0.07 to 1.70; p = 0.0605; I2 = 60.8%, τ2 = 0.1354). BASFI scores (Fig. 9) were not significantly different between males and females (MD: −0.16; 95% CI: −0.50 to 0.19; p = 0.322; I2 = 67.1%). The ASDAS-ESR (Fig. 10) was lower in males (MD: −0.40; 95% CI: −0.62 to 0.18; p = 0.000436; I2 = 21.1%), whereas the ASDAS-CRP (Fig. 11) was not significantly different (MD: −0.16; 95% CI: −0.53 to 0.22; p = 0.217; I2 = 21.1%). CRP levels as shown in Figure 12 were marginally higher in females (MD: 4.82 mg/dL; 95% CI: −0.27 to 9.91; p = 0.0604; I2 = 94.6%). A study of Almousa et al. 2023 [10] reported that males had greater radiographic progression; BASRI-s and sacroiliitis grades were significantly higher in men (BASRI-s 7.2 ± 1.9 vs. 4.6 ± 1.9, with a predominance of grade 3–4 sacroiliitis), highlighting a sex-specific propensity for structural damage in axSpA.

Treatment response

Based on a synthesis of twelve studies, notable gender differences in the treatment and management of ankylosing spondylitis (AS) emerge, underscoring the need for tailored therapeutic approaches. While several studies [19, 31, 34], report no significant overall difference in the use of foundational treatments like NSAIDs and biologic agents (specifically TNF blockers) between men and women, a consistent pattern suggests that male patients often receive more intensive treatment regimens. This is potentially driven by objective markers of higher disease severity; A study by Qian et al. 2017 [31] noted that men had elevated CRP and ESR levels and were more likely to undergo surgical interventions such as hip replacements. Similarly, two studies [32, 36] found a higher usage of anti-TNF-α therapies and sulfasalazine in males, indicating a tendency towards more aggressive treatment strategies for men, possibly due to more severe radiographic progression [24] and a higher burden of comorbidities like hypertension and hyperuricemia [32].

Conversely, female patients, despite often presenting with similar or even higher subjective disease activity scores, frequently experience worse patient-reported outcomes and a distinct clinical profile that may be inadequately addressed by standard treatments. Two studies [19, 35], found that women had significantly worse health status, functionality, and quality of life, reporting more fatigue, emotional distress, and functional limitations even when objective disease activity measures were comparable to men’s. This discrepancy highlights a critical gap in care; as the study by Chen et al. 2020 [19] suggests, there is a pressing need for treatment plans that incorporate enhanced supportive, rehabilitative, and psychosocial interventions for women. Furthermore, diagnostic delays are a significant issue for female patients [10], and their clinical presentation often differs, featuring a higher prevalence of peripheral arthritis, enthesitis, uveitis [23], and fibromyalgia-like symptoms [10], which can influence treatment choice. For instance, a study [23] observed that women were less likely to be prescribed anti-TNF agents but used more NSAIDs, potentially reflecting a different treatment focus.

The collective evidence strongly advocates for a gender-informed treatment paradigm in AS. As the studies [10, 29] suggest, optimal management must consider not only biological disease activity but also psychosocial context—such as the higher prevalence of depression and anxiety in women [29]—and differences in comorbidity profiles. The findings indicate that while men may require more intensive anti-inflammatory and surgical interventions to manage objective disease severity, women often need a more holistic strategy that addresses their greater subjective disease burden, functional impairments, and mental health to ultimately improve their quality of life.

Subgroup analysis

In the subgroup analysis, we performed two primary analyses to explore the variability in our results. The first analysis was region-specific, dividing the studies into East Asia and West Asia. We did not include other regions of Asia due to insufficient data and the lack of consistent reporting across studies from those areas. The second analysis focused on diagnostic criteria, comparing the ASAS and Modified New York criteria. Other diagnostic criteria were not included in the analysis as they were infrequently used across the included studies, which limited the ability to draw meaningful comparisons. These subgroup analyses helped provide a more nuanced understanding of the results based on geographical and diagnostic variations.

The subgroup analysis reveals substantial variation in pooled estimates across regions and diagnostic criteria. East Asia generally shows stronger and more consistent results, with significant effects in parameters like age of onset, age at diagnosis, disease duration, and BASDAI (p < 0.001), despite some moderate heterogeneity. West Asia, in contrast, exhibits weaker results or no significance (e.g., age of onset, ESR, CRP) and higher heterogeneity in some cases. Diagnostic criteria also influence results, with Modified New York criteria yielding stronger estimates compared to ASAS. These findings highlight the importance of considering regional and diagnostic variations in clinical studies.

In the subgroup analysis, standardized disease-specific measures such as BASDAI and BASFI demonstrated substantially lower heterogeneity across studies. In contrast, unstandardized laboratory parameters (e.g., ESR, CRP) and demographic variables (e.g., age at onset, age at diagnosis, disease duration, diagnostic delay) continued to exhibit notable heterogeneity. This suggests that the use of standardized scoring systems reduces between-study variability, whereas unstandardized or context-dependent measures contribute more to heterogeneity. Such differences may reflect variability in laboratory reference ranges, measurement protocols, and population characteristics, which are less controlled compared to validated disease indices. Sensitivity analyses

The sensitivity analysis by leave-one-out meta-analysis reveals that the pooled estimates for most clinical parameters, including age of onset, disease duration, and biomarkers like BASFI and BASMI, remain stable across different sensitivity tests, suggesting robust findings. However, parameters such as ESR and CRP exhibit wider ranges and higher heterogeneity, indicating that these results are more sensitive to the inclusion or exclusion of specific studies. Despite this, the confidence intervals for original and sensitivity estimates overlap for most parameters, reinforcing the reliability of the analysis. The findings suggest that while most results are consistent, parameters with high heterogeneity warrant further scrutiny to confirm their stability. Reporting biases

Most meta-analyses demonstrated a low risk of bias due to missing evidence, ensuring robust findings. Minor concerns exist in specific outcomes, but overall, the evidence is comprehensive and reliable.

Discussion

Our analysis highlights significant sex disparities in axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) within Asian populations, particularly concerning age of onset, diagnostic delay, disease burden, extra-articular manifestations, and therapeutic response. These findings underscore the necessity for sex-sensitive clinical strategies.

One of the key findings of this study is the difference in the age of onset and diagnosis between male and female patients. Our results indicate that while men tend to develop symptoms at an earlier age, women experience significantly longer diagnostic delays. Our analysis suggested that the prolonged diagnostic delay in female patients is multifactorial. Compared with men, women exhibit lower HLA-B27 positivity rates, reducing the degree of clinical suspicion for axSpA. Additionally, they more frequently present with peripheral symptoms rather than classical axial involvement, which can overlap with other rheumatologic conditions. This pattern is consistent with European data [1], which also attributes longer diagnostic delays in women (8.2 years vs. 6.1 years for men) to physician biases, differences in clinical presentation, and lower HLA-B27 positivity. Misdiagnosis with fibromyalgia is also common, further delaying access to appropriate treatment. [2, 40] Zhao et al. 2021 [41] reported a global mean diagnostic delay of 6.7 years, with female patients experiencing even longer delays in certain regions. The IMAS study [42] provides a broader global context, revealing significant regional variation. While Asia had the shortest mean diagnostic delay (4.2 years), delays were much longer in other regions like South Africa (10.8 years), closely mirroring the prolonged delays experienced by women in our study and in Europe. A Study [43] has shown that peripheral arthritis and enthesitis can contribute to delayed diagnosis owing to their overlap with other rheumatic conditions. Furthermore, despite improvements in classification criteria, sex bias in clinical recognition remains a key challenge, particularly for nonradiographic axSpA patients [44].

With respect to disease severity, our analysis confirmed that male patients exhibited greater radiographic progression, whereas female patients reported greater disease activity and peripheral joint involvement. The strong association between HLA-B27 and axSpA is evident in our findings, with male patients displaying higher HLA-B27 positivity and more extensive structural damage. [45, 46]Disease duration also plays a role in disease burden, as a longer disease duration correlates with worsening functional impairment and increased radiographic progression, reinforcing the importance of early intervention.[47]

Our study further revealed that female patients tend to have higher disease activity scores, including BASDAI, ASDAS-ESR, and ASDAS-CRP scores, despite showing less radiographic progression on the basis of the BASRI. This finding of higher subjective disease activity in women is strongly supported by the European study [1], which also reported women had higher BASDAI scores, including higher levels of fatigue, pain, and stiffness. In contrast, male patients exhibit greater spinal mobility impairment, as reflected in BASMI scores, and worse functional impairment, as measured by the BASFI. A key finding was the disconnect between subjective experience and objective measures: women reported greater pain and fatigue (higher BASDAI) despite often having lower inflammatory markers. This pattern, consistent with prior research [44, 48], indicates that the disease burden in women may be driven more by central sensitization and non-inflammatory pathways, whereas in men, it correlates more directly with objective measures of inflammation. [44, 48]. Furthermore, comorbidities such as osteoporosis and cardiovascular disease are more prevalent in female patients than in male patients, highlighting additional disease burdens [49]. These differences may be linked to sex-specific immune responses, as suggested by Vergne-Salle et al. 2022 [50].

Our analysis revealed that extra-articular manifestations (EAMs) in axSpA patients follow a sex-specific pattern, with uveitis and psoriasis more frequently affecting women, whereas IBD, dactylitis, and enthesitis are more common in men. The increased prevalence of uveitis in women contributes to a greater healthcare burden, necessitating more frequent medical interventions [48]. Similarly, men exhibit a greater prevalence of dactylitis and enthesitis, which are associated with more severe disease courses. These regional patterns can be further understood in a global context. The IMAS study [42] found that the prevalence of EAMs like uveitis, psoriasis, and IBD varied significantly by region (e.g., uveitis highest in North America (30.8%), IBD highest in South Africa (24.1%)), suggesting that both sex and geographic/ethnic factors contribute to the expression of EAMs. These findings align with study showing that inflammatory pathways linking axSpA to IBD result in increased systemic inflammatory markers in affected patients [46]

Despite the strengths of this study, certain limitations must be acknowledged. A key limitation is incomplete sex-stratified reporting across outcomes. While age of onset, age at diagnosis, and diagnostic delay were commonly reported by sex, other measures—including BASDAI, BASFI, BASMI, ASDAS-ESR/CRP, inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP), HLA-B27, uveitis, treatment response/adherence, peripheral arthritis, enthesitis, dactylitis, psoriasis, and inflammatory bowel disease—were not uniformly disaggregated. Consequently, pooled estimates for these endpoints rely on a subset of eligible studies, with attendant risks of selective-reporting bias and reduced external validity. Future studies should adopt standardized sex-specific reporting to enable more reliable comparative syntheses.

The heterogeneity in diagnostic criteria, study methodologies, and comorbidity reporting presents challenges in synthesizing uniform conclusions. Geographic bias, with most studies originating from East Asia, limits the generalizability of findings to other Asian populations with differing genetic and environmental backgrounds. The retrospective study design introduced potential recall bias, particularly in self-reported outcomes such as diagnostic delay and pain severity. Furthermore, the underrepresentation of women in clinical trials reduces the external validity of treatment efficacy data [40]. Variability in HLA-B27 testing methodologies and radiographic scoring systems further complicates comparisons across studies [51].

Adjustment for a broad range of potential confounders—spanning genetic background (HLA-B27 and subtypes), disease phenotype (nr-axSpA vs r-axSpA), comorbidities (fibromyalgia, osteoporosis, metabolic and psychiatric disorders), lifestyle factors (smoking, BMI, physical activity, diet), treatment-related variables (access to imaging and biologics, prior therapies, adherence), and sociocultural determinants (health-seeking behaviour, gender roles, socioeconomic status, and physician awareness)—could further explain the observed male–female differences. However, inconsistent and incomplete reporting of these covariates across primary studies precluded reliable meta-regression; instead, we relied on prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses, which confirmed the robustness of the findings. Future studies with individual-participant data or harmonized multinational registries are needed to disentangle these biological, behavioural, and sociocultural effects more precisely.

Clinically, these findings advocate for sex-sensitive diagnostic protocols that incorporate atypical symptoms such as peripheral arthritis and fatigue in women while prioritizing early use of advanced imaging in HLA-B27-negative patients. Treatment strategies should emphasize the potential benefits of IL-17 inhibitors for women and incorporate routine screening for psychosocial comorbidities [48]. Furthermore, our review underscores a critical gap in the literature: the inconsistent sex-stratified reporting of outcomes. To address this, policymakers and journal editors should mandate the routine presentation of sex-disaggregated data in all clinical trials and cohort studies of axSpA [43]. This is essential to build a more reliable evidence base for sex-specific care. [43]Future research should focus on multinational Asian registries to capture ethnic and geographic diversity, investigate hormonal–immune interactions such as estrogen’s modulation of IL-17, and validate objective biomarkers to reduce reliance on subjective disease assessments [8]. Addressing these disparities will facilitate earlier diagnosis, improve treatment efficacy, and enhance the overall quality of life for female axSpA patients.

The evidence synthesized in this review has several limitations. First, significant heterogeneity (e.g., I2 > 60% for diagnostic delay, BASDAI) arose from variability in study designs, diagnostic criteria (e.g., ASAS vs. modified protocols), and inconsistent reporting of comorbidities (e.g., fibromyalgia, depression) and lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking, physical activity), which confounded pooled estimates. Geographic bias was evident, with most studies originating from East Asia (e.g., China, Japan), limiting generalizability to South Asian and Southeast Asian populations where genetic, cultural, and environmental factors may differ. Retrospective designs dominated the included studies, introducing recall bias, particularly in self-reported outcomes such as diagnostic delay and pain severity. Additionally, underrepresentation of women in clinical trials of biologics reduced the external validity of treatment efficacy data, whereas variability in HLA-B27 testing methodologies and radiographic scoring systems (e.g., BASRI) further weakened comparability across studies.

This review’s processes were constrained by database and language restrictions, as searches were limited to PubMed, Embase, and Google Scholar, excluding regional databases (e.g., CNKI, CiNii) and non-English literature, potentially omitting relevant studies. The reliance on the ROBINS-I tool for risk of bias assessment, while methodologically rigorous, may have overestimated bias in observational studies owing to its hypothetical “target trial” comparator. Exclusion of gray literature, such as conference abstracts and unpublished datasets, risked overlooking emerging trends or null findings. Furthermore, the meta-analysis could not adjust for publication bias in outcomes with limited studies (e.g., BASRI data from a single study) and the inability to perform subgroup analyses by axSpA subtypes (nr-axSpA vs. r-axSpA) owing to insufficient data reducing granularity in interpreting sex-specific disparities.

Clinically, these findings advocate for sex-sensitive diagnostic protocols that integrate atypical symptoms (e.g., peripheral arthritis, fatigue) in women and earlier use of advanced imaging in HLA-B27-negative patients. Treatment strategies should prioritize IL-17 inhibitors for women, given their potentially sex-agnostic efficacy, while routinely screening for psychosocial comorbidities. Policymakers must address structural inequities by subsidizing MRI access in resource-limited settings and mandating sex-stratified reporting in clinical trials. Future research should establish multinational Asian registries to capture ethnic and geographic diversity, investigate hormonal‒immune interactions (e.g., estrogen modulation of IL-17), and validate objective biomarkers (e.g., proteomic profiles) to reduce reliance on subjective measures. Funding agencies should prioritize mechanistic studies on sex-specific pathobiology and longitudinal cohorts to clarify causal relationships between sociocultural factors and disease outcomes, ultimately bridging equity gaps in axSpA care.

Registration and protocol

The study protocol, detailing our methodology and inclusion criteria, was registered on PROSPERO under registration number CRD42025637163. The complete protocol is publicly accessible through the PROSPERO database.

We have made several amendments to our protocol to improve clarity and scope. First, we replaced “sex” with “sex” to emphasize biological differences rather than social constructs. Additionally, we broadened our study area from Southeast Asia to encompass all of Asia, which allows for a more comprehensive analysis across diverse populations. Finally, due to insufficient data on treatment response, this component was excluded from our analysis to maintain the integrity and robustness of our findings.
