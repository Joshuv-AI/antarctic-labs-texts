Age‐Related Changes in the Amygdala From In Utero to Early Childhood: Association With Social and Cognitive Outcomes
Authors: Megan E. Mueller, Emily S. Nichols, Sarah Al‐Saoud, Barb de Vrijer, Charles A. McKenzie, Roy Eagleson, Sandrine de Ribaupierre, Emma G. Duerden
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe amygdala is a key element of the limbic system. It is involved in emotional processing controlling responses to social, affective, or motivational stimuli, facilitating interaction with the surrounding environment. Given its importance in typical development, understanding early normative growth patterns in the amygdala can provide insights into the origins of emotional and social behaviors. This study examined age‐related differences in amygdala volume from the fetal to early childhood period across six cohorts from in utero to the first 4 years of life, and explored the association with social, cognitive and communication outcomes. Amygdala volumes were analyzed in 471 participants ranging from 27 to 195 weeks postmenstrual age (PMA). The results of the mixed model analyses indicated that volume significantly increased in size with age (t(534) = 7.92, p < 0.001), with the left amygdala demonstrating stronger age‐related changes (p < 0.001) and with males showing steeper age‐related changes compared to females (p = 0.012). In a subset of participants with developmental outcome data (n = 30), significant associations were observed for the left amygdala, with larger volumes predicting receptive communication (B = −0.02, p = 0.017), including an interaction with sex (B = 0.03, p = 0.028). Additional effects were found for social–emotional (B = −0.03, p < 0.001) and cognitive outcomes (B = −0.02, p < 0.001), with interactions by sex and age (all p < 0.05), whereas no significant findings were detected for the right amygdala. These results characterize age‐related changes in amygdala volumes early in development and highlight the role of sex and laterality, while underscoring its importance in social communication during early development.

Introduction

The amygdala is a key element of the limbic system and is involved in emotional and affective processing (Rolls 2015; LeDoux 2012, 2003), including emotional attention, learning, and regulation (Ochsner et al. 2012; Adolphs and Spezio 2006; Phillips et al. 2003). In typically developing children, the limbic system controls responses to stimuli related to social, emotional or motivational salience (Sokolowski and Corbin 2012), playing a key role in facilitating adaptation of individuals to their surrounding environments (Stefanacci and Amaral 2002). Dysfunction of the amygdala can lead to disordered emotional regulation, and overactivity has been found in anxiety disorders, panic disorders, and social phobias (Šimić et al. 2021; Madonna et al. 2019). Additionally, alterations in size have been associated with neurodevelopmental disorders characterized by impaired social interaction, such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD) (Seguin et al. 2021; Schumann et al. 2009). Given how crucial the amygdala is in healthy development and wellbeing, characterizing its developmental trajectory in a typically developing sample can provide an important benchmark to which atypical development can be compared.

Previous studies of amygdala development have varied in the age range investigated and methodology used. Postmortem research in embryos has found that the structure of the amygdala is identifiable at eight weeks, and cytoarchitectonic differentiation starts by 12 weeks post‐conception (O'Rahilly and Fabiola 2006; Nikolić and Kostović 1986). In the postmortem fetus, cytoarchitecture closely resembles that of the mature amygdala by 8 months gestation (Setzer and Ulfig 1999; Ulfig et al. 1999; Eleftheriou and Humphrey 1972). Using MRI methods in infants and children, research has found exponential growth (Seguin et al. 2021; Holland et al. 2014; Uematsu et al. 2012), with volume changes across childhood (Goddings et al. 2014; Brenhouse and Andersen 2011; Nardelli et al. 2011; Mosconi et al. 2009), peaking between 9 and 11 years of age (Uematsu et al. 2012). In girls, this peak occurs approximately 1.5 years earlier than in boys (Uematsu et al. 2012). In school‐aged children, the right amygdala develops at a faster rate than the left (Russell et al. 2021), and this is consistent with research that has found larger right volumes in both children (Giedd et al. 1996) and adults (Guadalupe et al. 2017).

Limited research has examined amygdala development and social development in typically developing children; however this relationship has been thoroughly studied in atypical youth populations who experience challenges with social and communication skills, such as those born preterm and children diagnosed with ASD. Preterm born children demonstrate atypical development of the amygdala (Fernández de Gamarra‐Oca et al. 2024; de Almeida et al. 2021; Nolvi et al. 2021; Chau et al. 2019; Rogers et al. 2017; Cismaru et al. 2016; Scheinost et al. 2016), in whom volumetric differences throughout childhood are predictive of emotional regulation abilities and social challenges (Pereira Camejo et al. 2024; Mueller et al. 2022). Children with ASD have enlarged amygdala volumes in toddlerhood and early school age (Avino et al. 2018; Schumann et al. 2009), and larger amygdala volumes are associated with both social and communication impairments in children (Seguin et al. 2021; Lucibello et al. 2019; Kim et al. 2010; Schumann et al. 2009; Munson et al. 2006).

Although amygdala development during childhood has been well characterized, few studies have investigated the late fetal, neonatal, and early childhood periods, likely due to the challenges of imaging very young populations. To address this gap, we examined age‐related differences in amygdala volume using 521 MRI scans from 471 fetuses, infants, and children ranging from 26.9 to 195.3 weeks postmenstrual age. Developmental follow‐up data were available for a subset of participants (n = 30), enabling us to explore associations between amygdala volume and social, cognitive, and communication outcomes in this subsample.

APrON

The data included in the APrON cohort consisted of toddler datasets from the Alberta Pregnancy Outcomes and Nutrition Study (APrON; obtained from https://osf.io/axz5r/) (Lebel et al. 2016). All anatomical volumes were collected within the APrON Study (Kaplan et al. 2014). Inclusion criteria were the following: no diagnoses of neurological, genetic, and neurodevelopmental (e.g., ADHD, ASD) disorders and infants born ≥ 36 weeks' gestation. Exclusion criteria included women who did not speak English and women who did not plan to reside in the region for the duration of data collection.

The APrON cohort was composed of 15 cross‐sectional and longitudinal datasets from 11 children (8 female, with 1 infant scanned 4 times), ranging from 142.6 to 195.3 weeks postmenstrual age (1.9–2.9 years postnatal age) (M = 182.62, SD = 13.95). The study was approved by the corresponding institutional ethics board and caregivers provided written informed consent.

CMIND

This cohort included infant and toddler datasets collected through the CMIND study (obtained through the National Institute of Mental Health Data Archive [NDA], dataset identifier: NDARCOL0002329). Inclusion criteria were the following: no family history (in first‐degree relatives) of neurological or psychiatric disease, body mass index between the 5th and 95th percentile for age and gender, and a typical neurological exam. Exclusion criteria included chronic illness, gestation less than 37 weeks or greater than 42 weeks, birth weight less than the 10th percentile, history of head trauma including a loss of consciousness, special education, orthodontic braces or other metallic implants and standard MRI contraindications (Sroka et al. 2015).

The CMIND cohort was composed of 30 datasets (18 female) ranging in age from 44.3 to 170.4 weeks postmenstrual age (M = 112.7, SD = 37.9). All participants in the CMIND cohort were full‐term (≥ 37 weeks' gestation), consistent with the dataset's inclusion criteria; therefore, no preterm infants were included in the behavioral follow‐up analyses. The study was approved by the corresponding institutional ethics board and caregivers provided written informed consent.

Developing Human Connectome Project (dCHP)

Neonatal datasets from the dHCP (obtained from http://www.developingconnectome.org/) (Cordero‐Grande et al. 2018; Hughes et al. 2017) were also included in the study. Inclusion criteria were the following: gestational age between 23‐ and 44‐weeks, estimated from the mother's last menstrual period and confirmed where possible by early ultrasound scanning and singleton pregnancy. Exclusion criteria included mothers or infants with contraindications to MR imaging (including implanted metallic devices such as orthopedic devices or noncompatible clips to close patent ductus arteriosus), preterm infants who are too unwell to tolerate the scanning period, despite full intensive neonatal care, language difficulties preventing proper communication about the trial and the consent process, congenital anomalies, concomitant substance use, significant infection (e.g., TORCH), preterm premature rupture of membranes, history of cardiovascular disease (i.e., abnormal heart rhythms, congenital heart disease), and growth restriction.

The dHCP cohort was composed of 348 infants (168 females, 2 scanned twice) ranging from 28 to 44 weeks postmenstrual age (M = 40.27, SD = 2.68). The study was approved by the corresponding institutional ethics board, and caregivers provided written informed consent.

Fetal Cohort #1

Fetal Cohort #1 included cross‐sectional data collected at Western University. Pregnant women in their third trimester between October 2018 and March 2020 participated in a study of healthy fetal development. Inclusion criteria were the following: singleton pregnancy and maternal age ≥ 18 years. Exclusion criteria included contraindication to safely undergoing non‐contrast MRI, suspected congenital anomalies, weight/body habitus that would prevent a successful MRI and concomitant substance use.

The cohort was composed of nine pregnant women with fetuses (5 female) ranging from 35.0 to 38.9 weeks postmenstrual age (M = 36.77, SD = 1.21). The study was approved by the Western University Research Ethics Board and caregivers provided written informed consent.

Fetal Cohort #2

Fetal cohort #2 included longitudinal data collected at Western University. Pregnant women in their third trimester of pregnancy between October 2020 and October 2023 participated in the study. Inclusion criteria were the following: singleton pregnancy, maternal age ≥ 18 years, and typical growth. Exclusion criteria included contraindication to safely undergoing noncontrast MRI, suspected congenital anomalies, concomitant substance use, weight/body habitus that would prevent a successful MRI, significant infection (e.g., TORCH), preterm premature rupture of membranes, history of cardiovascular disease (i.e., abnormal heart rhythms, congenital heart disease), and growth restriction.

The cohort was composed of 50 pregnant women with fetuses (29 female, 2 unknown) ranging from 26.9 to 39.3 weeks postmenstrual age. For some participants, a second scan during pregnancy occurred at least 2 weeks later, at 28.9–39.1 weeks postmenstrual age. Longitudinal data were available for 13 of the participants with a final age range of 26.9–39.3 weeks postmenstrual age (M = 32.71, SD = 3.32). The study was approved by the Western University Research Ethics Board and caregivers provided written informed consent.

Infant Cohort

The infant cohort included cross‐sectional and longitudinal data collected from Western University. Participants were recruited from the community and directly from Fetal Cohort #2 between August 2021 and September 2023. Inclusion criteria were the following: singleton pregnancy, maternal age ≥ 18 years, infant age ≤ 12 months, and normal growth. Exclusion criteria included contraindication to safely undergoing noncontrast MRI, congenital anomalies, concomitant substance use, weight/body habitus that would prevent a successful MRI‐significant infection (e.g., TORCH), preterm premature rupture of membranes, history of cardiovascular disease (i.e., abnormal heart rhythms, congenital heart disease), and growth restriction.

The cohort was composed of cross‐sectional and longitudinal data from 21 infants (11 female, 4 scanned twice) ranging from 45 to 90 weeks postmenstrual age (M = 15.56, SD = 13.09). Eighteen of the infants also participated in the fetal study, and, therefore, prenatal–postnatal longitudinal data were available. The study was approved by the Western University Research Ethics Board and caregivers provided written informed consent.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging

APrON

Child T1‐weighted anatomical images (repetition time [TR] = 8.23 ms, echo time [TE] = 3.76 ms, matrix size = 512 × 512) were collected. The images were acquired at the Alberta Children's Hospital on a GE MR750w 3T scanner, using a 32‐channel head coil. Scanning occurred either under natural sleep or while children were awake and watching a movie. To prepare their children for scanning, parents were given the option to practice in the MRI simulator and/or to take home a package of information on MRI, including noise recordings (Reynolds et al. 2019).

CMIND

Infant and child T1‐weighted anatomical images (TR = 2800, TE = 3.7 ms, 192 × 180 matrix, slice thickness = 1 mm) were collected. The images were acquired on a 3T Siemens Tim Trio scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) through the C‐MIND project (Dean et al. 2014).

dHCP

Neonatal T1‐weighted anatomical images (TR = 4.8 s, TE = 8.7 ms, 0.8 × 0.8 mm resolution, slice thickness = 1.6 mm) were collected. The images were acquired at the Evelina Newborn Imaging Centre on a Philips Achieva 3T scanner, using a 32‐channel neonatal head coil (Hughes et al. 2017). Anatomical volumes were collected as part of the dHCP (Makropoulos et al. 2018). Turbo spin echo (TSE) sequences were used with two stacks per weighting (sagittal and axial). An inversion recovery TSE sequence was also utilized. The resulting images were motion corrected as described in Cordero‐Grande et al. (2018). Super‐resolution reconstruction was performed, following the methods of Kuklisova‐Murgasova et al. (2012), which produced 3D volumes resampled to 0.5 mm isotropic resolution. The images were also corrected for bias field inhomogeneities.

Fetal Cohort #1

A minimum of three single shot fast spin echo (SSFSE) image stacks (TR > 1000 ms, TE = 80 ms, field of view [FOV] = 38–44 mm, 0.74 × 0.74 [coronal and sagittal] or 0.86 × 0.86 [axial] matrix, slice thickness = 5 mm, 19–25 slices) were collected for each participant, with one in each x, y, and z plane. The resulting images were corrected for motion and bias field inhomogeneities. Super‐resolution reconstruction was performed using NiftyMIC (Ebner et al. 2020), resulting in 3D volumes resampled to 1 mm isotropic resolution. The T2‐weighted anatomical images were acquired at the Translational Imaging Research Facility (Robarts Research Institute, Western University, London, Canada; n = 5) using either a 3T GE Discovery scanner (Milwaukee, Wisconsin, USA) with a 32‐channel torso coil or at the London Health Sciences Center (London, Canada; n = 6) using a 1.5 T GE scanner with a GEM posterior and anterior array coil.

Fetal Cohort #2

A minimum of 3 SSFSE image stacks (TR > 1000 ms, TE = 80 ms, FOV = 38–44 mm, 0.74 × 0.74 [coronal and sagittal] or 0.86 × 0.86 [axial] matrix, slice thickness = 5 mm, 19–25 slices) were collected for each participant, with one in each x, y, and z plane. The resulting images were corrected for motion and bias field inhomogeneities. Super‐resolution reconstruction was performed using NiftyMIC (Ebner et al. 2020), resulting in 3D volumes resampled to 1 mm isotropic resolution. The T2‐weighted anatomical images were acquired at the Translational Imaging Research Facility (Robarts Research Institute, Western University, London, Canada; n = 5) on a 3T GE Discovery scanner (Milwaukee, Wisconsin, USA), using a 32‐channel torso coil.

Infant Cohort

Infant T1‐weighted images (TR = 2300 ms, TE = 6.1 ms, 192 × 192 matrix, slice thickness = 1 mm) were collected. The images were acquired at the Centre for Functional and Metabolic Mapping (Robarts Research Institute, Western University, London, Canada) on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner (Erlangen, Germany) using a 32‐channel head coil (Gilbert et al. 2023).

Volumetric Quantification

Fetal: Semiautomated Segmentation of the Amygdala

Image processing of the fetal data was performed using an in‐house pipeline combining NiftyMIC (Ebner et al. 2018), ANTs version 2.3.5 (Avants et al. 2011), AFNI version 24.1.15 (Cox and Hyde 1997; Cox 1996), and FSL version 6.0.7.11 (Jenkinson et al. 2012; Smith et al. 2004). The pipeline, including the validation with manual segmentation, is described by Wang et al. (2022). In brief, ANTs was used to perform a nonlinear registration with three dimensions to a 36‐week gestational age fetal brain template (Gholipour et al. 2017). The atlas has 126 predefined labels, including the left and right amygdala. Using the inverse of the native‐to‐template transformation matrix, for each participant, the labeled atlas was transformed into native space. Following this transformation, region of interest (ROI) masks were generated for the left and right amygdala using AFNI's 3dCalc. Using FSL's fslstats, volume information was extracted for each ROI.

Neonatal/Infant/Toddler: Automated Segmentation of the Amygdala

For the neonatal, infant, and toddler data, all cortical reconstruction and volumetric segmentations were completed using Infant FreeSurfer version infant‐dev‐4a14499 (Zöllei et al. 2020; de Macedo Rodrigues et al. 2015; Ghosh et al. 2010). Infant FreeSurfer is an automated segmentation tool and surface extraction pipeline for infant T1‐weighted neuroimaging data. It is documented and freely available for download online (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/fswiki/infantFS). The specific details of these procedures are described in prior publications (Reuter et al. 2012; Reuter et al. 2010; Han et al. 2006; Jovicich et al. 2006; Fischl et al. 2004; Ségonne et al. 2004; Fischl et al. 2002; Fischl and Dale 2000; Fischl et al. 2001; Dale et al. 1999; Fischl, Sereno, and Dale 1999; Fischl, Sereno, Tootell, and Dale 1999). In summary, the processing includes eight steps: motion correction (Reuter et al. 2010), removal of nonbrain tissue using a hybrid watershed/surface deformation procedure (Ségonne et al. 2004), automated Talairach transformation, segmentation of the subcortical white matter and deep gray matter volumetric structures (including hippocampus, amygdala, caudate, putamen, ventricles) (Fischl et al. 2004, 2002), intensity normalization (Sled et al. 1998), tessellation of the gray matter white matter boundary, automated topology correction (Ségonne et al. 2007; Fischl et al. 2001), and surface deformation following intensity gradients to optimally place the gray/white and gray/cerebrospinal fluid borders at the location where the greatest shift in intensity defines the transition to the other tissue classes (Fischl and Dale 2000; Dale et al. 1999).

Age in months is specified when running Infant Freesurfer. The segmentation atlas was built from 4 participants who were closest in age. In the current study, individual ages were specified for the Infant, CMIND, and APrON cohorts, and for participants who are newborns, such as those in the dHCP cohort, all five newborns in the database were used to create a template instead.

Data Quality Assurance

The CMIND, APrON and dHCP cohorts include open access data that has been heavily curated and has been previously analyzed for quality and found to be adequate (Makropoulos et al. 2018; Thieba et al. 2018; Sroka et al. 2015). Fetal cohorts who underwent MRI scanning at Western University had multiple anatomical images taken in each plane to ensure the best quality image for processing. A researcher on the study (E.S.N.) completed visual inspections of the anatomical images and selected the clearest images. Following the segmentation of the amygdala in all cohorts, two researchers blind to subject criteria (M.E.M. and S.A.‐S.) inspected the segmentations to ensure adequate quality. Segmentation accuracy was assessed using manual segmentation protocols (de Macedo Rodrigues et al. 2015), which were used in the initial training of infant freesurfer (Zöllei et al. 2020). Six participants required manual correction, again performed according to previously published manual segmentation protocols.

Developmental Outcomes

In the CMIND cohort, infant and toddlers' neurodevelopmental abilities were assessed using the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, third edition (BSID‐III). This is a standardized assessment that yields Cognitive, Language, Motor, Social Emotional and General Adaptive composite scores that are compared to a normative age‐matched sample with a mean score of 100 and a standard deviation of 15, for infants, toddlers and children aged 1–42 months. The Cognitive Composite Score includes subtests that measure raw and scaled cognitive performance. The Language Composite Score assesses receptive and expressive communication through raw and scaled subtests. The Motor Composite Score evaluates fine and gross motor skills, also raw and scaled subtests. The Social–Emotional Composite Score determines social and emotional development with raw and scaled scores. The general adaptive composite score encompasses a range of subtests such as communication, community use, functional pre‐academics, home living, health and safety, leisure, self‐care, self‐direction, social, and motor, each providing raw and scaled scores to reflect the child's abilities. Raw scores represent the number of correct responses or observed behaviors, while scaled scores normalize these results for comparison across different age groups. Scaled scores, derived from individual subtests (e.g., expressive and receptive language, fine, and gross motor), provide domain‐specific measures of development that can identify relative strengths and weaknesses. Here, the scaled subtest scores were used for analysis, rather than composite scores.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed in R version 4.4.0 (R Core Team 2024), and all data and code (in the form of R Markdown .html files) for replicating the analysis and generating figures are available at https://osf.io/qnrx4/. To address our aim of characterizing amygdala size in relation to age, sex, and laterality, volumes were submitted to linear mixed effects analysis. Amygdala volumes were treated as the dependent variable, and sex × age and hemisphere × age interaction terms were added. A second‐order polynomial term was included for age in both interactions to assess whether the fit followed a quadratic shape. Cohort was included as a regressor to account for between‐site or scanner variance, including between the different cohorts recruited at Western University. Participant was included as a random intercept to account for repeated measures of volume across hemisphere, and for shared variance in participants with multiple time points. TCV was not included as a regressor due to its high correlation with age (r = 0.9, p < 0.001); however, a second, supplementary set of analyses was conducted including TCV to ensure that its inclusion did not alter the pattern of results.

To address whether amygdala volumes were related to socioemotional outcomes in the CMIND (n = 30, 17 females) cohort, separate generalized linear models with an identity link function were applied to the socioemotional, cognitive and communication outcomes data. The BSID‐III subtest scores for socioemotional abilities, cognitive, and communication (receptive and expressive language) abilities, were the dependent variables and the left and right amygdala volumes were the independent variables, adjusting for age and sex. p‐Values were corrected for multiple comparisons using a false discovery rate (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995), with the effects of left and right amygdala volumes being corrected separately. To determine the robustness of fit given the small sample size, leave‐one‐out cross‐validation was run to determine the magnitude of error in our models.

Social–Emotional and Communication Outcomes

In the CMIND cohort, the composites for communication (M = 97.9, SD = 13.1), cognition (M = 107.67, SD = 12.7) and social–emotional processing (M = 112.27, SD = 13.4) on the BSID‐III were in the typical ranges. In the social–emotional domain, none of the children scored below 85, which would be indicative of atypical development. However, 1 (3%) of the children scored below 85 in the cognitive domain, and 4 (13%) children scored below 85 in the communication domain. When examining the outcome data, social–emotional outcomes showed a main effect of left amygdala volume (B = –0.03, p < 0.001), along with significant interactions between left amygdala volume and sex (B = 0.04, p = 0.027) and between left amygdala volume and age (B < 0.01, p < 0.001). For expressive communication, there was no main effect of left amygdala volume, but both a significant interaction between left amygdala volume and sex (B = –0.04, p = 0.005) and a three‐way interaction with sex and age (B < 0.01, p = 0.002) emerged. Receptive communication was predicted by left amygdala volume (B = –0.02, p = 0.017), with an additional significant interaction with sex (B = 0.03, p = 0.028, Figure 3). Finally, cognitive outcomes showed a main effect of left amygdala volume (B = –0.02, p < 0.001), as well as interactions with sex (B = 0.03, p = 0.027) and age (B < 0.01, p < 0.001). Leave‐one‐out cross‐validation of our models showed root mean square errors of 3.49 for expressive communication, 6.83 for social–emotional, 2.88 for cognitive, and 3.31 for receptive communication.

Estimated marginal mean predictive plots of receptive communication developmental outcomes in relation to amygdala volumes and sex in the CMIND cohort assessed from the newborn period up until toddlerhood. Expressive communication scores are shown on the y axis, in relation to left amygdala volumes for males (red) and females (blue, x axis). The shaded areas represent 95% confidence intervals.

To examine whether the reported associations between amygdala volumes and developmental outcomes were driven by global brain size, supplemental models were conducted including TCV as a covariate (https://osf.io/qnrx4/). TCV was a significant predictor of social–emotional (p = 0.0002), receptive communication (p = 0.035), and cognitive outcomes (p < 0.001), but not expressive communication (p = 0.57). However, the inclusion of TCV did not alter the results concerning the significant interactions between left amygdala volume and sex, as well as the three‐way interaction between left amygdala, sex, and age on expressive communication (p = 0.0006); all remained significant when TCV was added to the models.

Sensitivity Analyses

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted two sensitivity analyses: one removing the preterm‐born infants, and one removing the fetal data. By running each sensitivity analysis separately, we were able to determine whether the effect of including preterm‐born infants differs from the effect of including healthy third trimester fetuses. The results remained largely unchanged, and the changes we observed were similar in both analyses.

After removing the fetuses, there was a significant main effect of the linear age term (t(434) = 6.76, p < 0.001), a significant main effect of sex (t(401) = 4.31, p < 0.001), and a significant main effect of hemisphere (t(510) = −12.92, p < 0.001). The main effect of the quadratic age term was no longer significant (t(554) = −1.76, p = 0.079), but there remained a significant interaction between the linear age term and sex (t(403) = 2.08, p = 0.038) and between the quadratic age term and sex (t(501) = −2.33, p = 0.020). Finally, the relationship between hemisphere and the linear age term was no longer significant, although it was trending towards significance (t(415) = −1.65, p = 0.052).

After removing the preterm‐born infants, there was a significant main effect of the linear age term (t(400) = 5.74, p < 0.001), a significant main effect of sex (t(383) = 3.43, p < 0.001), and a significant main effect of hemisphere (t(392) = −13.21, p < 0.001). The main effect of the quadratic age term was no longer significant (t(536) = −0.69, p = 0.493), but there remained a significant interaction between the linear age term and sex (t(371) = 2.21, p = 0.028) and between the quadratic age term and sex (t(362) = −2.39, p = 0.017). Finally, the relationship between hemisphere and the linear age term was no longer significant (t(392) = −1.77, p = 0.078).

These sensitivity analyses are available on the OSF repository, here: https://osf.io/qnrx4/.

Discussion

In the current study, we examined age‐related changes in amygdala volume from the third trimester of pregnancy to 4 years of age and identified both sex and laterality differences. Males had larger amygdala volumes, and across both sexes, the left amygdala showed steeper age‐related increases. Moreover, later communication, social–emotional functioning and cognitive functioning were found to be sensitive to left amygdala volumes, age and sex.

The structure of the brain rapidly changes throughout infancy into toddlerhood, enhancing vulnerability to environmental factors that impact normative growth trajectories. Examination of these trajectories allows for a more thorough understanding of the variation in development and subsequent neurodevelopmental outcomes (Bethlehem et al. 2022; Howell et al. 2019). Consistent with previous findings (Uematsu et al. 2012; Østby et al. 2009), amygdala volumes were positively associated with age. The current study builds on previous findings by suggesting that there is both a linear and quadratic component to the growth trajectory of the amygdala. In addition, a rapid increase in volume in both the fetus and in the first year of life was observed across sexes, which extends previous findings of Uematsu et al. (2012) and Matsuzawa et al. (2001), who identified a rapid period of growth in childhood.

Biological sex was a significant predictor of amygdala volumes, whereby males had larger amygdala volumes compared to females. Findings are consistent with previous research which suggested that males have larger amygdala volumes in infancy (Uematsu et al. 2012) and childhood (Koolschijn and Crone 2013). In addition, the current study found that the male amygdala has a steeper age‐related increase, but tapers off earlier than that of the female amygdala. In contrast, Uematsu et al. (2012) identified that in adolescence, the growth rate of the amygdala in females reached a peak earlier than in males, concluding that the longer growth period of the amygdala contributes to the larger amygdala volumes seen in males. The contrasting results of the current study suggest that sex differences may differ depending on the developmental period under investigation.

The laterality differences identified in the current study differ from those previously found in children (Ocklenburg et al. 2022; Giedd et al. 1996). Previous research in this area did not examine volumes in the fetal brain or in infancy, which may explain the hemispheric differences throughout development. Taken together, it is possible that the left amygdala grows faster throughout fetal development and infancy, while later in childhood, the right amygdala overtakes the left. This hypothesis is supported by the previous findings of Russel et al. (Russell et al. 2021) which suggest that the growth trajectory of the left amygdala tapers off earlier than the right amygdala. In addition, Weems (2017) hypothesized that the right amygdala is more susceptible to environmental stress, which may explain its accelerated growth in populations of children who experience adversity (Cismaru et al. 2016). Although there is limited research on this in children, there is suggestion in the literature that lateralization differences of amygdala volumes are related to psychopathology in adults, such as depression and other internalizing disorders. This may be related to the role of the amygdala on affect processing, emotional regulation, and so forth (Zavorotnyy et al. 2018). In children specifically, there is research that suggests that children with ASD have larger right amygdala volumes (Kovacevic et al. 2023). Although there is not a direct link identified here between right amygdala volumes and maladaptive outcomes, given what is known about social difficulties in children with ASD, it is suggestive that the right amygdala could play a unique role in the development of these skills. As the current study consists of cohorts of typically developing children who have not experienced extreme stress and adversity, this may explain why the same trajectories of volumetric changes were not observed.

Our results also showed that left amygdala volumes, in combination with age and sex, were predictive of communication, cognitive and social–emotional outcomes in a population of typically developing children. These findings align with previous research in preschool children showing that increased amygdala size in infancy is associated with poorer language abilities in later childhood. Children with larger right amygdalae at 6 months had lower expressive and receptive language scores at ages 2–4 (Ortiz‐Mantilla et al. 2010). These results suggest that early amygdala development may play a significant role in social language processing, potentially due to the amygdala's involvement in processing emotional information, which is crucial for effective communication. Evidence from preterm‐born children and those with ASD has also reported similar associations between amygdala volumes and social communicative outcomes (Seguin et al. 2021; Lucibello et al. 2019; Kim et al. 2010; Schumann et al. 2009; Munson et al. 2006). Larger amygdala volumes have been associated with greater social and communicative impairments (Seguin et al. 2021; Lucibello et al. 2019; Kim et al. 2010; Schumann et al. 2009; Munson et al. 2006). However, with children born preterm, the opposite correlation is seen such that larger amygdala volumes are associated with more positive social cognitive outcomes longitudinally (Mueller et al. 2022). Further research is needed to explore the mechanisms underlying this relationship and to determine how variations in amygdala development impact language and social outcomes.

While this study has strengths of a large, cross‐cohort sample size, some limitations remain. Although utilizing data from multiple cohorts is a strength, it can also introduce additional variance, despite using cohort as a regressor. A key challenge in fetal and early postnatal neuroimaging is maintaining high image quality despite motion artifacts and variability in tissue contrast across development. Thus, one limitation of the present study is the potential variability in segmentation precision between the fetal and postnatal datasets, as different methods were used for each developmental stage. Given the differences in acquisition protocols between fetal and postnatal and the segmentation methods, we applied a quality control pipeline, including visual and manual inspection by trained raters and preprocessing techniques tailored to each imaging sequence. Inhomogeneity correction was implemented to reduce intensity variations and improve segmentation reliability. Future studies may benefit from incorporating automated quality metrics, such as motion scores or SNR‐based evaluations, to complement manual assessments and further standardize image selection criteria. In addition, the cohorts had differing sample sizes and outcome variables available for analysis. As a result, we were only able to analyze developmental outcomes in one cohort, which was small and results should be interpreted as preliminary. Finally, although some longitudinal data were collected in the study, most of the data are cross‐sectional, limiting our ability to make conclusions about growth trajectories.

Conclusion

To conclude, we found that sex, laterality, and age predicted the volumes of the amygdala in a typically developing sample, providing a more thorough understanding of its development in the fetus and throughout infancy. Examining the development of the amygdala throughout a time of steep age‐related increases and development provides valuable information that can be used to study sensitive periods in development. Additionally, identifying the impact of the development of the left amygdala on later communication, cognitive and social outcomes in toddlerhood provides further evidence that biological differences may be predictive of later prosocial or adverse outcomes, even in typically developing populations. Future longitudinal studies should continue to study growth trajectories of the amygdala in relation to later outcomes as further identification of biomarkers that may indicate later risk would be beneficial for early intervention and support.
