Comparison of Intraoral Scanning and Cone-Beam Computed Tomography for Implant Position in Edentulous Patterns☆
Authors: Bin Guo, Leizi Ma, Xing Wu, Zhongming Shi, Linhong Wang, Wentao Zhang, Fan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the accuracy of intraoral scanning (IOS) for postoperative implant position evaluation in comparison with cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) across different partially edentulous patterns.Materials and MethodsWe retrospectively analysed 172 patients who underwent implant placement between January 2020 and January 2025. Patients were classified into six clinical subgroups according to edentulous configuration. Nonfree-end patterns were defined as tooth-bounded edentulous spaces with remaining teeth on both sides, whereas free-end patterns lacked a distal tooth-supported reference. IOS- and CBCT-derived postoperative implant positions were compared using shoulder and apical deviations, buccolingual, mesiodistal, and vertical components, and angular deviations.ResultsA total of 172 patients with 278 implants were included. The within-group analysis indicated no statistically significant difference between IOS and CBCT measurements in the single tooth and nonfree-end subgroup (Group A). Conversely, the free-end subgroups (Groups B, C, and F) exhibited greater deviations, particularly the unilateral multiple-tooth free-end subgroup (Group C), which demonstrated a significantly different buccal-lingual angular deviation (P < .05). Between-group comparisons confirmed greater discrepancies in free-end than in nonfree-end patterns. Vertical and apical deviations contributed most prominently to subgroup differentiation. The findings from linear discriminant analysis and leave-one-out cross-validation indicated that there was overlap in the deviation performance across edentulous patterns. Nevertheless, vertical displacement, particularly apical deviation, emerged as the most significant indicator for differentiating the characteristic deviations associated with each group.ConclusionIOS showed better concordance with CBCT in nonfree-end edentulous patterns than in free-end patterns. However, greater variability in free-end cases, particularly in apical and angular measurements, indicates that IOS-based postoperative assessment should be interpreted according to the clinical edentulous pattern. These findings suggest a promising but indication-dependent clinical application of IOS for postoperative implant position assessment.

Introduction

In oral rehabilitation, precise three-dimensional positioning of dental implants is fundamental to long-term functional and aesthetic success.1,2 Accordingly, clinicians routinely use cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) to analyse site anatomy and plan prosthetically driven implant positions.3, 4, 5 Nevertheless, plan-to-placement discrepancies persist across freehand, static, dynamic, and robotic techniques and can jeopardize vital structures or osseointegration when excessive.6,7

To verify whether the implant matches the preoperative plan in both position and angulation, clinicians commonly obtain postoperative panoramic, periapical, or CBCT images.8 However, by imaging principle, 2D radiographs are single-projection views and therefore cannot capture buccolingual (BL) displacement, which is particularly problematic in the thin-walled anterior region.9 In addition, 2D images do not reliably depict the true three-dimensional relationship between the implant and critical structures (eg, the inferior alveolar nerve, maxillary sinus floor), increasing the risk of misinterpretation or missed complications.10 Consequently, in certain cases, additional postoperative 3D imaging may be warranted. However, this modality is associated with non-negligible ionizing radiation exposure.11 Effective doses for dental CBCT are highly variable, ranging approximately from 3 to 1073 μSv, with specific values largely determined by imaging parameters and field of view; these doses are consistently and markedly higher than those observed for panoramic radiography.12, 13, 14, 15 To address these safety concerns, the ALADAIP framework (As Low as Diagnostically Acceptable being Indication-oriented and Patient-specific) has been proposed to promote patient-specific dose optimization rather than routine CBCT application.16

With the advent of digital dentistry, intraoral scanners have gained widespread adoption for intraoral imaging owing to their ease of operation, high accuracy, and absence of ionizing radiation.17,18 Postoperatively, an intraoral scan body can be secured to the implant, and the intraoral scanners can be used to acquire an intraoral image.19 This dataset can then be registered to the preoperative CBCT, enabling the reconstruction of the postoperative implant position within the CBCT framework. Given the continual improvement in the precision of both intraoral scanners and registration software, intraoral scanning may emerge as a viable alternative to CBCT for assessing postoperative implant placement accuracy.20,21

Although prior studies have compared intraoral scanning (IOS) and CBCT for assessing implant placement accuracy, two gaps remain. First, many investigations were in vitro on model specimens,22, 23, 24, 25 bypassing intraoral confounders (eg, saliva, metallic restorations, tongue and cheek mobility) that degrade IOS feature capture and thus measurement and registration stability. Second, prior prospective clinical studies and case series often presented aggregate errors without systematic subgroup analysis.20,26 Although one recent clinical study differentiated single- and dual-implant scenarios,19 outcomes have not been systematically analysed according to clinically meaningful partially edentulous configurations, particularly bounded vs free-end situations, unilateral vs bilateral defects, and span extent. Yet in clinical practice, these edentulous patterns may substantially influence both IOS data quality and registration robustness, suggesting that accuracy may be context-dependent. In long-span partially edentulous cases, for instance, the extended distance between implants may exacerbate stitching errors, as even minor misalignments in overlapping scans can accumulate along the dental arch.27,28 As a result, previous findings have limited applicability in guiding complex clinical scenarios. Furthermore, conventional accuracy assessments often report only absolute deviation values, neglecting the directional component of error.19,20

In the present study, we introduce a definition of directional deviation that assigns positive values to mesial, buccal, and apical (deeper) deviations, while attributing negative values to distal, lingual, and coronal (shallower) deviations. This framework facilitates the quantification of error vectors and enhances clinical interpretability.29 The aim of this study was to assess whether IOS-based reconstruction of postoperative implant positions yields measurements that are comparable to those obtained from CBCT across various partially edentulous configurations. This work provides a foundation for the broader adoption of IOS in clinical and research contexts. Given the high precision, widespread availability, and established role of CBCT as the reference method in numerous scientific studies, it was designated as the reference method for comparative evaluation in the present analysis.20,26,30 The null hypothesis of this study is that there is no significant relationship between the accuracy of IOS-based postoperative implant measurement and the clinical edentulous pattern, as assessed by comparison with CBCT.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study retrospectively analysed patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria and underwent implant surgery at Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital between January 2020 and January 2025. The study protocol and the use of clinical case data were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval No. OT2025098).

Patient selection

Patients were screened according to the following inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria:

(1) Availability of preoperative CBCT scans (Planmeca ProMax, Planmeca Oy); (2) postoperative CBCT scans acquired after implant placement (Planmeca ProMax, Planmeca Oy); (3) postoperative intraoral scans obtained using Trios 3 (3Shape). (4) Age ≥18 years; (5) smoking fewer than 20 cigarettes per day; (6) no history of head and neck radiotherapy or bisphosphonate use; (7) implant sites with either insufficient available bone height or width requiring bone augmentation procedures, or close proximity to critical anatomical structures (eg, the maxillary sinus or inferior alveolar nerve canal).

Exclusion criteria:

(1) Patients with fewer than four residual teeth were excluded because cusp-based landmark registration would not be feasible; (2) patients with four or more residual teeth but fewer than four eligible reference teeth for registration were also excluded. Eligible reference teeth were defined as natural teeth with intact crown morphology and clearly identifiable cusp landmarks on CBCT images. Teeth with coronal restorations, particularly extensive or metallic restorations, were not considered eligible reference teeth if the cusp landmarks could not be reliably identified.31 (3) Patients with severe motion artefacts affecting image registration or measurement accuracy were excluded. Severe motion artefacts were defined as blurring, ghosting, double contours, or discontinuity of the dentition, alveolar bone, or implant margins on CBCT images.32,33 Cases were excluded when these artefacts impaired reliable identification of cusp-based landmarks, implant contours, or adjacent critical anatomical structures required for registration and measurement. Artefact assessment was performed independently by two calibrated examiners according to predefined criteria after standardized training; the examiners were blinded to group allocation and imaging modality, and any disagreement was resolved by consensus, with adjudication by a senior examiner when necessary.

According to the inclusion criteria, a total of 273 participants were initially recruited. Among them, 59 were excluded due to having fewer than four remaining teeth, 24 were excluded because of extensive intraoral restorations, and 18 were excluded owing to severe motion artefacts. Ultimately, 172 participants were included in the study.

Classification of edentulous patterns

After case selection, patients were classified into six clinical subgroups according to the number and location of missing teeth, encompassing bounded and free-end configurations. Group A was defined as a single-tooth nonfree-end edentulous pattern; Group B, a single-tooth free-end edentulous pattern; Group C, a unilateral multiple-tooth free-end edentulous pattern; Group D, a multiple-tooth nonfree-end edentulous pattern; Group E, a bilateral nonfree-end edentulous pattern; and Group F, a bilateral free-end edentulous pattern. Implant planning

Preoperative imaging was performed using CBCT (Planmeca ProMax, Planmeca Oy). All scans were acquired at 80 kV and 6.0 mA over 15 seconds (voxel size: 0.15 mm; greyscale depth: 15 bits; focal spot size: 0.5 mm; field of view: 12 × 9 cm).

During preoperative assessment, implant planning was performed according to prosthetically driven principles using Planmeca Romexis 3.5.1R, with consideration of the available bone volume, the occlusal relationship, the condition of the opposing dentition, and the soft tissue profile.34, 35, 36 Implant positions were determined using digital tooth set-up, with attention to BL contour, mesiodistal (MD) safety distances, and vertical position, in order to define the planned implant coordinates in three dimensions.37,38 Specifically, whenever feasible, a minimum MD distance of approximately 1.5 mm from adjacent natural teeth, 3.0 mm between adjacent implants, and about 2.0 mm from the inferior alveolar canal in posterior mandibular sites was respected during planning.39 In posterior maxillary sites, the relationship to the maxillary sinus floor was also evaluated (Figure 2). Depending on the clinical situation, implant placement was performed either freehand or with the aid of a surgical guide. Acquisition and measurement of postoperative implant positions using CBCT

We obtained CBCT only for indication-driven, high-risk scenarios, when 2D radiographs or IOS verification were inadequate to confirm implant position or exclude complications, consistent with ALADAIP. Typical indications included proximity to the inferior alveolar canal, sinus elevation or grafted sites, and suspected cortical perforation. We also included certain complex cases of free-end edentulism, such as when restricted mouth opening or malpositioned adjacent teeth hindered visual orientation and compromised the precision of implant placement. Imaging findings directly informed immediate clinical decisions (eg, need for secondary grafting or repositioning). A small or medium FOV matched to the indication and a dose-optimized protocol were used (12 × 9 cm; 80 kV; 6.0 mA; 15 s; voxel 0.15 mm; 15-bit; focal spot 0.5 mm).

Both the preoperative CBCT data, which contained the virtual implant planning, and the postoperative CBCT data, which reflected the actual implant positions, were subsequently imported into a dedicated measurement software (CycadX 1.1.0.20241107, Hangzhou Jianjia Robot Company).

`We used a rigid, two-stage hybrid registration. The rationale for choosing a rigid transformation approach, rather than a nonrigid or deformable method, is based on two main reasons. First, the anatomical reference structures (jawbone and dentition) are inherently rigid. Second, a nonrigid algorithm would locally warp the images, which could inadvertently mask the actual surgical errors we aimed to measure. Therefore, a rigid transformation was mandatory to preserve the true geometric relationships and avoid artificial deformation.40 After light preprocessing and jaw segmentation, coarse alignment was achieved by multiresolution, intensity-based registration on the target jaw (maxilla or mandible, depending on the implant) using an ROI that excluded implant-related artefacts. Fine alignment combined volumetric bone registration with surface-based registration guided by four manually identified cusp tips – one anterior and one posterior per side on the target jaw; restored or severely worn cusps were avoided.

The postoperative implant position was obtained by fitting the manufacturer’s CAD model to the high-density segmentation. The final rigid transform mapped postoperative data into the preoperative planning frame, from which shoulder or apex linear and angular deviations were computed and decomposed into BL, MD, and vertical component. Acquisition and measurement of postoperative implant positions using IOS

During the prosthetic phase, postoperative intraoral scans were obtained 2 to 3 weeks after the healing abutment was placed, using Trios 3 (3Shape) and cylindrical titanium scan bodies (Segma) compatible with the corresponding implant systems used in this study. Depending on the clinical situation, second-stage surgery was generally performed about 4 months after implant placement in cases without bone augmentation and 6 to 9 months after surgery in cases requiring bone augmentation. The scan bodies were tightened to 15 N·cm using a torque wrench before scanning, and all IOS procedures were performed by the same experienced operator according to a standardized clinical workflow for scan body seating and intraoral scanning. After scanning, the scan bodies were replaced with healing abutments. The postoperative IOS and the virtually planned implant positions were imported into the measurement software (CycadX 1.1.0.20241107, Hangzhou Jianjia Robot Company).

Four cusp tips were selected bilaterally (one anterior and one posterior per side) as landmarks. Candidates were chosen on intact natural cusps and visually verified to avoid worn or restored tips. A robust landmark-based rigid solution (RANSAC/Procrustes) provided the initial transform, which was minimally refined using small local surface patches around the same cusps to compensate for landmark picking noise, without altering the global frame. Once the IOS coordinate system was registered and locked to the CBCT reference frame, scan-body CAD models were locally fitted to the scan data to recover the implant positions.41 Planned and postoperative positions were then compared in the CBCT planning frame; deviations were reported at the shoulder or apex and decomposed into BL, MD, and vertical components. Quality checks included inverse-consistency and visual overlays. The present workflow was broadly consistent with the digital registration framework reported by Han et al,19 in that postoperative implant position was reconstructed from postoperative IOS data using a scan body–based approach and subsequently compared with the preoperative plan. In the present study, the IOS dataset was first aligned to the CBCT planning reference using cusp-based landmarks, after which scan-body CAD fitting was performed within the established CBCT coordinate system. Thus, the difference from the protocol of Han et al mainly lies in the specific registration sequence rather than in the overall methodological concept (Figure 4).Fig. 4Registration between preoperative CBCT and postoperative IOS scans. Analysis of the implant position

To decompose the overall three-dimensional deviation of the implant position into clinically meaningful two-dimensional planar deviations, this study adopted the implant position orthogonal projection (IPOP) method, as validated by Verhamme et al.29 The core principle of this method is to reconstruct a curve representing the dental arch morphology on a digitized model of the arch by marking four anatomical reference points (eg, the midpoint of the incisal edge, the cusp tip of the canine, and the central fossa of the first molar).

For each implant site, the system generates two mutually orthogonal virtual planes based on this dental arch curve: a tangential plane at the implant site, representing the BL/buccopalatal dimension, and a perpendicular plane representing the MD dimension. By projecting the actual three-dimensional implant position onto these orthogonal planes, the IPOP method decomposes the complex spatial deviation into two-dimensional deviations in the MD and BL/buccopalatal planes, and further quantifies depth discrepancies within each plane (Figure 5). This method was applied to all measurements and analyses in this study for both CBCT (reference standard) and IOS data. All measurements were performed by a calibrated primary examiner, blinded to subgroup and modality, under a standard operating procedure. To evaluate inter-rater reliability, a random 30% subsample of implants (n = 94) was independently remeasured by a second calibrated examiner. Agreement between examiners was quantified using a two-way random-effects, absolute-agreement, single-measurement ICC [ICC(2,1)] with 95% confidence intervals.43 Analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 27.0 (IBM Corp.). Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), and statistical significance was set at P < .05. Because multiple implants were nested within the same patient, inferential analyses were performed using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) with patient ID included as a random effect. Inter- and intrarater reliability were assessed using ICC (2,1) with 95% confidence intervals; intrarater testing involved repeated landmark selection, image registration, and deviation measurement by the same examiner.

Bland–Altman analysis was used to assess agreement between IOS and CBCT and to quantify the magnitude and range of deviations for the eight two-dimensional directional metrics and the three-dimensional composite measurements. Mean differences, standard deviations, and 95% limits of agreement were calculated for each metric and subgroup. Proportional bias and heteroscedasticity were further examined using linear regression.44,45

Within-group analyses were performed to determine whether systematic differences between IOS and CBCT were present within each clinical subgroup. For these analyses, each deviation metric was modelled using LMMs with group, registration method, and their interaction as fixed effects, and patient ID as a random effect. P values for within-group IOS-CBCT comparisons were derived from pairwise contrasts of estimated marginal means within each subgroup, with Tukey adjustment applied for multiple comparisons.

Between-group analyses were performed to assess whether the magnitude of IOS-CBCT deviations differed across edentulous patterns. For these comparisons, precomputed IOS-CBCT deviations were modelled as the dependent variable with group as a fixed effect. Residual normality was checked using Q–Q plots and the Shapiro–Wilk test, followed by type III ANOVA and Tukey-adjusted post hoc tests when appropriate.

To explore whether different edentulous patterns were associated with distinct multivariate deviation profiles, linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was performed. The six clinical edentulous patterns (Groups A-F) served as the grouping variable. Eleven deviation indices were entered as z-standardized predictors, including linear deviations at the implant shoulder and apex (3D global, BL, MD, and vertical) and angular deviations (3D global, BL, and MD). Before modelling, extreme observations were trimmed within each subgroup in the original predictor space using squared Mahalanobis distance with a 99% chi-square threshold. Model performance was assessed by leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) and summarized using overall accuracy and the cross-validated confusion matrix. For visualization, LD1-LD2 score plots with 95% normal-theory confidence ellipses and coloured centroids with black outlines were generated.

To assess the adequacy of our sample size, a posthoc power analysis was conducted using the G*Power software package (version 3.1.9.7) for the primary between-group comparison (one-way ANOVA). Based on a significance level (α) of 0.05, six clinical subgroups, a total sample size of 278 implants, and the empirically derived effect size f = 0.1706, the achieved statistical power (1 − β) was 0.807. This result indicates that the sample size was sufficient to detect the observed between-group differences.

The present study was in compliance with STROBE reporting guidelines.

Results

Results are presented hierarchically: first assessing IOS-CBCT agreement within each pattern, then comparing patterns to determine if free-end cases are more challenging, finally identifying which anatomical parameters drive between-group differences.

Patient population and edentulous pattern distribution

Among these patients, 78 were male, and 94 were female, with ages ranging from 23 to 82 years. Discussion

Studies on the relationship between specific edentulous patterns and the directional accuracy of IOS-based implant assessment are still limited. The impact of various partially edentulous patterns on the assessment of implant positioning through IOS remains unclear. Six clinical subgroups were identified based on the number and distribution of missing teeth, which include both nonfree-end and free-end patterns. In the present study, IOS showed closer agreement with CBCT in nonfree-end cases (Groups A, D, and E), whereas larger and more variable discrepancies were observed in free-end spans (Groups B, C, and F), particularly in unilateral multiple-tooth free-end situations. Across groups, vertical errors contributed most prominently to subgroup differences, especially at the implant apex, followed by the shoulder. LDA further showed that the first two discriminant functions explained a cumulative 72.6% of the between-group variance (LD1 = 49.2%, LD2 = 23.4%), whereas the overall LOOCV accuracy was only 25.2% (95% CI 20.1%-30.9%), indicating only partial separation among groups. Taken together, these findings indicate that the accuracy of IOS-based postoperative implant assessment is strongly influenced by the edentulous patterns, and the null hypothesis of this study is therefore partially rejected. These findings can be contextualized by considering previous evidence on IOS scan-body accuracy, the availability of tooth-supported reference structures, and the directional nature of implant-position deviations.

The present findings are consistent with previous clinical observations showing that IOS accuracy decreases when scan bodies are located farther from stable tooth-supported references. Nagata et al46 reported that scan body accuracy decreased with increasing distance from the distal abutment tooth, while Abdelrahman et al22 similarly documented reduced accuracy in free-end situations as the edentulous span increased. These findings support our observation that free-end situations, especially unilateral multiple-tooth free-end cases, exhibited larger IOS-CBCT discrepancies and broader variability than nonfree-end cases.

In addition to these pairwise and literature-based comparisons, the multivariate LDA results further supported this interpretation. In the LDA score plot, the nonfree-end subgroups (Groups A, D, and E) clustered closely, indicating similar multivariate deviation patterns and greater overlap than the free-end subgroups (Figure 7). This finding suggests that nonfree-end patterns provide more stable conditions for digital measurement consistency. In contrast, the wider dispersion of free-end groups in discriminant space is consistent with the view that missing distal support compromises measurement stability.

Mechanistically, these pattern-dependent differences may be explained by the availability and distribution of stable reference structures during IOS acquisition. IOS-based 3D model generation relies on extensive image stitching, a process that requires stable anatomical landmarks, or ‘anchors’,47,48 to ensure accurate alignment.49 In free-end edentulous situations, the absence of a distal reference ‘anchor’ may destabilize this scanning process. As the scanner moves away from the last abutment tooth, it must rely increasingly on feature-poor soft tissue to estimate the spatial position of the scan body. This mechanism may explain why, as observed by Nagata et al,46 scan bodies located farther from the distal abutment show lower accuracy.

The difference between unilateral and bilateral free-end situations may also be related to scan initiation and reference distribution. In most intraoral scanning protocols, scanning is recommended to start from the dentate end because dental anatomy provides an ideal initial recognition area for the scanner.50,51 In bilateral free-end edentulism, however, the starting point may be less stable regardless of which side is scanned first, because distal reference support is absent on both sides. This may partly explain the distinction observed between Groups B and F in the LDA plot.

Conversely, the close clustering of Groups A, D, and E may be explained by the presence of natural teeth on both sides of the edentulous space. These abutment teeth provide stable bilateral landmarks that help inhibit the propagation of cumulative stitching and registration errors.52,53 This stable reference framework may minimize the influence of variations in the number or position of missing teeth within tooth-bounded situations.

Across deviation dimensions, we observed a consistent trend in which BL deviations exceeded MD deviations. A similar tendency was reported by Abdelrahman et al22 and may be related to the typical IOS acquisition strategy, such as the zigzag scanning technique.54 During scanning, the occlusal surfaces, which are rich in geometric features, may provide a relatively stable MD ‘data backbone’. In contrast, buccal and lingual data streams are less geometrically distinctive and must be spatially aligned with this backbone, which may introduce additional error. Accurate capture of lingual surface data is further challenged by tongue interference, restricted scanner movement, and unfavourable posterior angulation.55 Saliva-induced artefacts may further generate noise and pseudosurfaces in the point cloud, thereby amplifying BL errors.56

The apparent discrepancy between our findings and those of Mangano et al57 may be attributable to methodological differences. Mangano et al57 reported that an increased number of missing teeth reduced scan body accuracy in nonfree-end situations, whereas the present study found minimal differences among the nonfree-end groups (Groups A, D, and E). This difference may be explained by the fact that Mangano et al57 calculated the overall root mean square deviation of the scan body and surrounding mucosa, whereas our IPOP-based approach decomposed implant-position discrepancies into directional components projected onto clinically relevant planes. Overall root mean square values may be more sensitive to the extent of the edentulous area, whereas directional analysis, which isolates specific BL, MD, and vertical deviation vectors, may be less affected by span length in tooth-supported scenarios.42 Nevertheless, definitive conclusions regarding this discrepancy require further investigation using standardized measurement protocols.

These differences also highlight the importance of using a measurement framework that can distinguish not only the magnitude but also the direction of implant-position discrepancies. The use of the standardized IPOP approach enhanced the clinical interpretability of these findings. The IPOP method decomposes complex three-dimensional global deviations into more clinically interpretable BL and MD components.29 To ensure methodological rigour and reliability, a standardized semiautomatic registration protocol was used. This workflow demonstrated excellent reproducibility, as evidenced by the high ICCs shown in Table 2, Table 3.

The methodological contribution of the present study lies not in proposing a new registration algorithm, but in applying a clinically oriented, pattern-based framework to IOS-CBCT agreement analysis. Previous studies have commonly reported global deviations or aggregate accuracy values, whereas the present study stratified patients according to clinically meaningful edentulous configurations and decomposed implant-position discrepancies into BL, MD, vertical, apical, shoulder-level, and angular components using the IPOP framework.19,20,26,29 This approach allowed us to identify not only whether IOS and CBCT differed, but also where and in which direction the discrepancy was most likely to occur.

Beyond mean directional differences, the Bland–Altman analysis provided important information on individual-level agreement between IOS and CBCT. Based on the IPOP methodology,29 we used mean deviation <0.25 mm for two-dimensional linear directional metrics and <1° for angular metrics as interpretive benchmarks for mean measurement agreement, while recognizing that clinical acceptability may vary according to anatomical and procedural context. In selected nonfree-end groups, the mean directional biases were small and, in several metrics, close to these predefined benchmarks. However, the Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated that small mean bias does not necessarily indicate interchangeability between IOS and CBCT at the individual measurement level.

The 95% limits of agreement were relatively wide for several parameters, particularly for apical and angular measurements and in free-end edentulous patterns. For example, although the mean BL shoulder deviation in Group A was only 0.069 mm, its 95% limits of agreement ranged from −0.83 to 0.97 mm, indicating that individual cases could deviate by nearly 1 mm despite a small average bias. In free-end scenarios, the variability was more pronounced; for example, the upper limit of agreement for MD apical deviation reached approximately 2.25 mm. Given that implant planning commonly relies on safety margins of approximately 2 mm around critical anatomical structures, discrepancies approaching this magnitude may have clinical relevance when implants are close to the inferior alveolar canal, the maxillary sinus floor, or a thin buccal cortical plate. Although this safety margin refers to a clinical three-dimensional anatomical distance rather than to the two-dimensional IPOP benchmark, directional discrepancies approaching this magnitude may still complicate interpretation of the overall three-dimensional implant position in anatomically constrained sites.39,58,59

Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence of pattern-dependent agreement rather than universal interchangeability between IOS and CBCT. IOS may serve as a radiation-free postoperative assessment option in selected low-risk, tooth-supported nonfree-end scenarios, provided that clinical findings do not otherwise require three-dimensional imaging. In contrast, CBCT remains appropriate in indication-driven high-risk situations, especially when implants are close to critical anatomical structures, when cortical perforation is suspected, when sinus elevation or grafted sites require three-dimensional verification, or when free-end anatomy may compromise IOS registration stability. This interpretation is consistent with indication-based radiographic selection criteria and patient-specific dose optimization principles.9,16 More broadly, Chen et al60 reinforce the importance of postoperative CBCT-based registration and multidimensional deviation assessment for validating digital surgical accuracy.

Several factors may explain the relatively wide limits of agreement. IOS-based reconstruction is susceptible to stitching error, especially in free-end or long-span edentulous situations where distal tooth-supported reference structures are absent. In addition, scan-body visibility, soft-tissue interference, saliva-induced artefacts, and the distance from the scan body to the nearest stable reference tooth may affect IOS data acquisition and registration, thereby increasing individual-level variability. Small acquisition or fitting errors may also be amplified at the implant apex and in angular measurements, which may explain why apical and angular parameters showed greater variability than shoulder-level measurements. In line with Kim et al,24 auxiliary reference markers may also be considered when IOS is used in free-end scenarios, as added anchorage may help mitigate the apex-positioning challenges identified in this analysis.

Nevertheless, several methodological considerations should be addressed. First, we intentionally utilized a unified computational platform (CycadX) to process both IOS and CBCT measurements. While this design choice characterizes our findings as a study of comparative concordance rather than absolute physical accuracy, it serves an important methodological purpose: by eliminating the confounding noise and systematic errors inherent in cross-platform data transfer and heterogeneous registration algorithms,19 we were able to isolate and quantify – with high fidelity – the specific impact of anatomical edentulous patterns on the consistency of the digital workflow.

The relatively large residual variance observed in the mixed-effects models deserves further consideration. In the present analysis, patient ID was included as a random effect to account for the clustering of multiple implants within the same individual. However, the residual variance exceeded the patient-level variance across all parameters, indicating that the main source of unexplained variability was not between-patient heterogeneity, but rather within-patient, implant-specific, or measurement-specific variation. This finding is clinically plausible because implants within the same patient may differ in arch location, anterior or posterior position, proximity to remaining teeth, scan-body visibility, local soft-tissue interference, implant angulation, and the quality of available reference landmarks.

Several technical factors may also have contributed to this residual variability. IOS-based reconstruction is susceptible to stitching error, particularly in free-end or long-span edentulous situations where distal tooth-supported reference structures are absent.22,46 CBCT-based assessment may also be affected by segmentation uncertainty, metal artefacts, and threshold-dependent implant contour identification.11,13,14 In addition, both IOS-CBCT and CBCT-CBCT workflows rely on rigid registration, and small inaccuracies in landmark identification, surface matching, or scan-body CAD fitting may be amplified at the implant apex or in angular measurements.19,29,40 The influence of edentulous span length may be particularly important, because the current subgroup classification captured broad clinical patterns but did not quantify continuous variables such as the distance from the scan body to the nearest reference tooth or the total scan path length. Alternative model specifications, including additional fixed effects or implant-level hierarchical random effects, were considered conceptually but were not adopted because several relevant implant-level and scan-level variables were not consistently available in this retrospective dataset. In addition, adding multiple parameters could have increased the risk of model instability or overfitting.

This study has several limitations. First, postoperative CBCT was acquired only in indication-driven cases rather than as a routine postoperative examination. Therefore, the study sample predominantly comprised complex or high-risk clinical scenarios, which may have influenced the observed bias patterns and may limit the generalizability of the findings to lower-risk routine implant cases. Second, this study used a single IOS system and a unified software platform to maintain methodological consistency and reduce hardware- or software-related heterogeneity. Consequently, the observed pattern-dependent discrepancies should therefore be interpreted within this specific hardware and software context. Future multicentre studies involving different IOS systems, CBCT units, scan bodies, and registration platforms are needed to validate the generalizability of these findings. Third, although the overall sample size was sufficient for the primary between-group comparison, the retrospective dataset did not consistently capture several potentially relevant implant-level and scan-level variables, including precise edentulous span length, scan-body-to-reference-tooth distance, local landmark quality, scan path characteristics, soft-tissue interference, and scan-body visibility. This limited our ability to evaluate more complex hierarchical models with additional fixed effects or implant-level random effects without increasing the risk of model instability or overfitting. Future prospective studies with larger and more granular datasets should systematically collect these variables to better explain the residual variability observed in IOS-CBCT agreement.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this retrospective study, the results indicated that IOS exhibited a higher degree of concordance with CBCT in the assessment of postoperative implant position, demonstrating greater accuracy in nonfree-end cases. Conversely, its accuracy was comparatively diminished in free-end cases. Nonetheless, these findings indicate a promising clinical application of IOS for the assessment of postoperative implant position and deserve further investigation.
