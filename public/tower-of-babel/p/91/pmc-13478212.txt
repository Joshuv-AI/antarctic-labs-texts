Proteome-scale quantification of the interactions driving condensate formation of intrinsically disordered proteins
Authors: Rasmus K. Norrild, Sören von Bülow, Baptiste Zanchet, Einar Halldórsson, Kresten Lindorff-Larsen, Joseph M. Rogers, Alexander K. Buell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cellular organization in the form of biomolecular condensates is a fundamental regulatory mechanism across all forms of life. Formation of condensates relies on multivalent interactions that are often weak and transient, making them challenging to study experimentally. We have developed Condensate Partitioning by mRNA-Display (CPmD) to measure these interactions from the partition free energies of peptides and nucleic acids into reconstituted condensates. CPmD increases experimental throughput by several orders of magnitude, and we apply it to reveal the interactions driving condensate formation of intrinsically disordered proteins. We show that the partition free energies of about one hundred thousand peptides derived from the disordered proteome into a model condensate directly reflect their intrinsic propensity to form condensates. We reveal that amino acid content, linked to hydrophobicity, is the primary determinant of phase behavior. Additionally, CPmD uniquely resolves subtle sequence-dependent contributions that can encode specificity. CPmD thus provides a powerful tool to decipher how weak interactions between protein and RNA regulate biological function through condensate formation.

Introduction

The understanding of biomolecular organization within cells at the mesoscale has been revolutionized by the concept of biomolecular condensates1. At the nano-scale, stoichiometric assembly of molecular complexes, such as the ribosome or proteasome, relies on pairwise high-affinity binding between components. The association of biomolecules at the meso-scale (100–1000 nm) is instead based on multivalent and often weak binding interactions2,3. Such interactions can lead to percolation coupled to phase separation into dynamic and viscoelastic condensates4. Condensates have emergent material properties that lead to altered diffusivity of molecules5, and their solvent-like properties confer selective partitioning of small molecules and proteins6,7. Altered material properties of condensates or erroneous partitioning can be the basis of many diseases, such as cancer8, neuro-degeneration9, and autism10.

The interactions leading to emergent condensate properties are transient and therefore often difficult to directly observe using traditional structural biology methods, such as X-ray diffraction, cryo electron microscopy, or nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)7. The capacity of intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) of proteins to promote condensate formation has been particularly challenging to study due to their highly dynamic nature11–13. For these proteins, mutagenesis-based approaches have proven powerful and have revealed that valence and patterning of aromatic amino acids are major contributors to phase separation of prion-like low complexity domains14. A more refined understanding requires unbiased screening, but mutation studies are currently limited by the need to purify each single sequence variant in a significant quantity needed for quantitative assays of phase separation15–17. Display-based screening methodologies have yielded throughput capable of unbiased data on, e.g., the energetic landscape of protein folding18,19 and mapping of binding interfaces by deep mutational scanning of nonproteogenic amino acids20. Recently, a cellular assay called CondenSeq showed how large-scale experiments give unique insight into the protein sequence determinants of nuclear condensates21. However, the complicated cellular environment makes it challenging to disentangle all relevant effects, as proteins interact with other cellular components and condensates.

In this study, we introduce Condensate Partitioning by mRNA-Display (CPmD) to directly measure the partitioning free energies of RNA and peptide molecules into reconstituted condensates by massively parallel DNA sequencing. Inspired by a central idea of protein science, i.e., that partitioning of amino acids into nonaqueous solvents informs about their energetic contributions to protein folding22, we use peptide partitioning into reconstituted condensates as a scalable proxy measurement23. We show that the minimal and covalent linkage of phenotype (peptide) and genotype (mRNA) through puromycin24,25 allows us to measure partitioning of proteome-scale libraries of peptide clients. We applied CPmD to the conserved RNA helicase DDX4, whose N-terminal IDR (DDX4N1) drives condensate formation26,27 to form essential germ granules that contain factors critical for germ cell determination28–31. By measuring tiling libraries, unbiased mutagenesis libraries, and a library containing peptides covering all experimentally verified IDRs, we show that partitioning of peptides into DDX4N1 condensates directly and quantitatively maps onto their intrinsic ability to form condensates. Furthermore, we show that the general ‘molecular grammar’ of IDR condensate formation is governed by additive amino acid-specific partition free energies and showcase CPmD’s ability to resolve sequence-specific effects that can encode condensate specificity.

Results and discussion

Peptide partitioning explains DDX4N1 condensate formation

We first set out to use CPmD to map the interactions that govern condensate formation of DDX4N1 itself. We designed libraries with peptides of different lengths (14–50 aa) tiled through DDX4N1 starting at every second position, and one higher-resolution tiling using only peptides with 16 residues, but starting from every single position. Such tiling approaches have proven to be powerful in mapping important functional regions of IDRs32. We transcribed the libraries into mRNA constructs with and without displayed peptides (Supplementary Fig. 1). The displayed and non-displayed mRNA libraries were then allowed to partition into reconstituted condensates of DDX4N1 in independent experiments. The condensates were formed by performing a fivefold rapid dilution of protein and NaCl with salt-free buffer that initiates phase separation by moving the system into the two-phase regime of the phase diagram. By separating the dilute and condensed phases through centrifugation and sequencing the content of the dilute phase before and after induction of phase separation, we obtained partition free energies for all library members (Supplementary Fig. 2). The typical concentration of purified mRNA-display libraries (1–100 nM) was three to five orders of magnitude lower compared to the 120 μM DDX4N1 used. Even with strongly partitioned libraries with an average partition free energy of more than 20 kJ/mol, corresponding to several thousand-fold concentration increase, the library concentration inside the condensates was only tens to hundreds of μM (Fig. 1d) whereas, DDX4N1 has a concentration of tens of mM inside the condensates (Supplementary Fig. 4). Any interactions between library members or perturbations to the overall phase separation of DDX4N1 by the addition of the library were therefore negligible.

The partition free energies of the peptides were approximately 10% of the contribution of the mRNA. The tenfold difference in partition free energy matched the relative molecular weights of peptide and mRNA, because nucleotides are, on average three times heavier than amino acids and three nucleotides encode one amino acid. Despite this dominant energetic contribution from the mRNA, the peptide effects were resolved with high reproducibility (Fig. 1e). The 16 amino acid DDX4N1 tiles partitioned with −1 to −2 kJ/mol free energy, representing only approximately twofold enrichment in the condensed phase (Fig. 1f). However, the length of the peptide and the average partition free energy showed a linear relationship (Fig. 1g,h). Extrapolating to the full domain length (236 amino acids) yielded −13.2 ± 0.16 kJ/mol, closely matching the energy based on measurements of the DDX4N1 domain’s dilute and dense phase concentrations alone (−14.6 ± 0.16 kJ/mol) (Fig. 1h, and Supplementary Fig. 5). Furthermore, we randomly selected peptide tiles of varying lengths that together make up the entire DDX4N1 domain. The sum of the partition free energies of these tiles successfully explained the condensate formation of the complete domain within experimental error (Fig. 1i). These results confirmed that CPmD allows for drawing quantitative conclusions about the condensate formation of the parent protein domain.

Specific regions of DDX4N1 drive condensation

A moving mean of the 16 amino acid tiles, where all partition energies of overlapping peptides are averaged along the sequence, revealed which regions of DDX4N1 drive condensate formation. The resulting profile showed a periodicity of ~40 amino acids, indicating a block copolymer-like separation of regions driving partitioning and thus phase separation (Fig. 1f, and Supplementary Fig. 5). Profiles derived from longer peptide tiles exhibited a stronger overall partitioning (Fig. 1g), consistent with the linear scaling of the partitioning. The resolution decreased with increasing length, due to longer peptides masking local effects (Supplementary Fig. 5).

A central region of the IDR from position 132 to 166 demonstrated particularly strong partitioning. In support of CPmD’s relevance to condensate formation, the second natural isoform of DDX4 (UniProt ID: Q9NQI0-2), which lacks the exon encoding this region, does not form condensates under relevant conditions34 (Supplementary Fig. 6). The center of the exon is strongly positively charged (Fig. 1f), and previous studies partly attributed condensate formation of DDX4N1 to electrostatic interactions between charged patches12,26,35,36. Furthermore, given the predicted net charge of DDX4N1 of −3 at pH = 6.5, positively charged peptides were expected to partition more strongly into condensates. Indeed, predicted peptide charge showed a negative correlation with partition free energy (−0.13 kJ/mol per positive charge). Surprisingly, this correlation was driven primarily by arginine (−0.28 kJ/mol per residue) rather than lysine (−0.04 kJ/mol per residue, Supplementary Fig. 7), likely because the guanidine group of arginine has delocalized electrons and forms stacking interactions with other arginines and aromatic amino acids37. In line with this, the regions of the profile most strongly partitioned showed enrichment in aromatic amino acids, with an average effect of −0.44 kJ/mol per aromatic residue (Fig. 1j). Although highly negatively charged mRNA could potentially mask charge effects on partitioning, we found very good agreement between CPmD and measured partition free energies of solid-phase synthesized peptides using confocal microscopy and Capillary flow experiments (Capflex)38 (Fig. 1k, and Supplementary Fig. 4). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CPmD provides a picture at great detail of how regions rich in aromatic residues and arginine drive condensate formation.

Unbiased mutagenesis probes grammar of phase separation

We next asked what specific features of the protein sequence facilitated partitioning and, by extension, condensate formation. To determine whether interactions are driven by specific sequence motifs39, we measured the partitioning of peptides with scrambled amino acid sequences for each tile in the wild-type (WT) sequence (Fig. 2a). In general, these scrambled peptides partitioned similarly to the WT peptides, indicating that amino acid composition rather than specific motifs primarily determines partitioning. However, the central region of DDX4N1 showed differences between scrambled and WT peptides. We found no statistically significant correlation with previously reported features associated with condensate formation40, such as helix propensity or the distribution of charged and hydrophobic residues. The only significant factor that influenced the impact of scrambling was the strength of the partitioning of the WT peptide, whether it was particularly strong or weak (Supplementary Fig. 7). To further explore this sequence dependence, we created a combinatorial mutant library of a 40-residue-long peptide fragment (residues 100–139) in the center of DDX4N1. The scale of our CPmD experiment enabled unbiased mutagenesis, yielding reliable data on 28,613 peptides (20,886 without stop codon) containing an average of 10.7 substitutions per peptide (27% residues mutated) (Fig. 2b, and Supplementary Fig. 8). Using Global Multi-Mutant Analysis (GMMA)41, we disentangled the effects of all single substitutions in the peptide (represented as changes in partition free energy: ΔΔGpart) (Supplementary Fig. 9), revealing a resolvable degree of sequence-specific substitution effects (Fig. 2b). The main contributing factor was overall composition of the peptides, but when incorporating sequence position information, our predictions closely matched the experimental data within the limits of measurement noise (Fig. 2c). Other condensate forming IDRs partition to DDX4N1 condensates

In the absence of sequence motifs driving specific assembly, different chemical grammars have been proposed to govern the coexistence without mixing of diverse cellular condensates7,27. We therefore investigated which IDRs of other proteins could partition into DDX4N1 condensates, and whether their amino acid composition differed from that governing DDX4N1 peptide partitioning. Capitalizing on the unique scale of CPmD, we measured a tiling library of 59,657 peptides that cover the disordered proteome of multiple organisms in the manually curated database of intrinsically disordered proteins (DisProt)42.

Although this library partitioned significantly less than the DDX4N1 peptides on average, the dependence on amino acid composition remained similar, with increased hydrophobic amino acid content favoring partitioning (Fig. 2d, and Supplementary Fig. 7). Building on the demonstrated linearity of partitioning with respect to length for DDX4N1 (Fig. 1h), we estimated partition free energies for all 3546 original full-length IDRs through extrapolation (Fig. 2e, f). The results showed that 17% of IDRs would be more than tenfold enriched (ΔGpart < −5.6 kJ/mol) in DDX4N1 condensates. This subset showed enrichment in gene ontology (GO) terms related to DNA/RNA-biology and biomolecular condensates (Fig. 2g). We observed only a weak correlation with proteins listed in the CD-CODE database of biomolecular condensates43, primarily driven by proteins present in multiple condensates (Supplementary Fig. 7), consistent with recent observations44. This suggests that partitioning into DDX4N1 condensates relates either to the IDR being a condensate scaffold itself or to its recruitment to various condensates with low specificity.

33 human proteins in the library showed more than 50-fold enrichment in DDX4N1 condensates (ΔGpart < −7.8 kJ/mol). 12 of these IDRs have been shown to form condensates in their purified forms and had diverse compositions (gene symbols: AFF4, AR, DDX3X, EIF4B, EWSR1, FMR1, FUS, HNRNPA1, HNRNPA2B1, MAPT, MBP, and POLR2A (RNA polymerase II C-terminal repeats) (literature references are listed in the Supplementary Information for all genes in this paragraph)). Another 9 IDRs were from proteins known to participate in condensate formation. The remaining 12 IDRs were from proteins that have no previously described condensate behavior (gene symbols: AHCTF1, AHR, CDKN2A, DDX23, FGA, IBSP, MCM2, RBM7, RICTOR, SECISBP2, STAG1, and TP53BP2) (Fig. 2g). We also included a high-resolution sublibrary that contained 1-skip tiling, including scrambled peptides, of known condensate-forming IDRs. These generally showed partitioning amplitudes similar to DDX4N1 itself, indicating strong partitioning. However, these protein domains exhibited greater differences between WT and scrambled tiles compared to DDX4N1 (Fig. 2h, i), possibly due to transiently ordered segments influencing partitioning that are absent in DDX4N1. Notably, CPmD effectively captures this sequence specificity, enabling further study of how transient structure could encode condensate partitioning-specificity across diverse IDRs.

CPmD reveals a core set of interactions driving condensate formation

Peptide fragments of DDX4N1 directly report on condensate formation of the full-length IDR, because they experience the same set of interactions in the condensate as the full-length sequence. However, peptides from other IDRs will experience different interactions in DDX4N1 condensates than during homotypic condensate formation. The partitioning of other condensate-forming IDRs to DDX4N1 condensates led us to investigate whether this behavior could provide general information on the formation of IDR condensates.

First, we examined this question using minimal perturbations caused by point mutations of DDX4N1 from our mutation library, by introducing these mutations into purified full-length variants of the DDX4N1 domain. We quantified their homotypic phase separation using Taylor Dispersion-Induced Phase Separation (TDIPS)45, which disperses salt ions and protein molecules differently in a microfluidic capillary, enabling robust and efficient exploration of phase separation behavior (Supplementary Fig. 6). Mutations showed stronger effects in TDIPS than in CPmD, indicating that homotypic condensate formation was more strongly affected by mutations than partitioning into condensates formed by the WT sequence. We attributed this difference to the fact that substitutions simultaneously alter both the peptide’s intrinsic partition propensity and the host condensate’s general ability to recruit peptides.

In order to bridge the difference between the two scenarios, we developed a theoretical framework that combined these separate effects (Fig. 3a, and Supplementary Fig. 10). We argue that the general ability of condensates to recruit peptides should be related to the concentration of interaction-prone amino acids within them. We then explored more broadly how the interactions that drive a peptide to partition into DDX4N1 condensates could represent the core set of interactions that govern IDR phase separation. We modeled the partitioning data on all peptides from the DisProt library with a linear model using amino acid residue-specific partition energies, similar to the composition model for the mutation library (Supplementary Methods). However, here we included pairwise terms to catch specific effects when two amino acid residues are next to each other. This fit yielded very similar amino acid residue-specific parameters to those obtained from the mutation library (Fig. 3c, d, and Supplementary Fig. 7). With the mean-field correction described above, our model aligned remarkably well with published experimental data for mutation effects on phase separation of the unrelated hnRNPA1 low-complexity domain measured at 20 °C16 (Pearson’s = 0.93, Fig. 3e, Supplementary Methods) and predicted interaction hotspots in CAPRIN1-LCD that matched NMR measurements46 (Fig. 3f).

Using this established quantitative link between partitioning into DDX4N1 condensates and de novo condensate formation, we converted our CPmD data on partitioning to represent homotypic condensate formation for all full-length IDRs in the DisProt library (Fig. 3g). Many of the strongly partitioned proteins mentioned above are also predicted to homotypically form condensates. Notably, these data accurately predict condensate formation of prion-like LCDs, such as those from FUS, HNRNPA1, HNRNPA2, and TARDBP (TDP43), despite their amino acid composition differing significantly from that of DDX4N1. Our partitioning model also predicted partitioning to DDX4N1 condensates for IDRs absent from the DisProt library, revealing that several proteins expected to colocalize with DDX4N1 in germ granules contained IDRs predicted to cause strong partitioning (Fig. 3h).

To explore the generalizability of the composition model, we predicted the homotypic phase separation propensity of the entire human IDRome47, and compared it with predictions from PSPred, a model trained from coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations40,48 (Fig. 3i). We found a strong correlation with our composition-based model (Pearson’s = 0.72), while estimating a threefold higher driving force for condensate formation compared to PSPred. PSPred is based on the CALVADOS force field, designed to simulate the structural ensembles of IDRs. The central parameters describing the “stickiness” of the coarse-grained amino acids (one bead per amino acid) were optimized using experimental data of IDR structural ensembles, with approximately 100 previously reported hydrophobicity scales serving as Bayesian priors48. We found that the amino acid-specific partition free energies of the composition model correlated well with the “stickiness” parameters, shown as an integrated form of the pair potential AHpairs40 (Fig. 3j). This remarkable correlation underscores that the experimentally derived amino acid-specific transfer free energies in our partitioning model represent a fundamental physicochemical hydrophobicity scale, based on dynamic interactions between amino acids49–51.

To test the agreement between CALVADOS simulations and our CPmD measurements, we simulated tiled peptide partitioning into excess full-length DDX4N1 (Fig. 3k, and Supplementary Fig. 9). The individual peptides showed decent correlation (Pearson’s r = 0.65, Supplementary Fig. 9), which improved when taking the moving mean along DDX4N1 (Fig. 3l). We also simulated the partitioning of multi-mutant peptides of DDX4N1, which showed good correlation, especially on single amino acid-effects extracted using GMMA above (Fig. 3m, n, and Supplementary Fig. 9). The agreement between CPmD measurements and CALVADOS further validates the ability of CPmD to decipher the dynamic interaction network governing biomolecular condensate formation.

In this study, we introduce CPmD, a high-throughput approach that enables measuring data reporting on the molecular interactions driving the formation of biomolecular condensates at scale. These data can, in turn, provide insights into relationships between amino acid and nucleotide sequence and phase separation propensity. Uniquely, we show that CPmD provides direct free energies measured at an experimental throughput that is several orders of magnitude higher than previously available. This capability allowed us to measure the interactions responsible for condensate formation of IDRs at the proteome scale.

Our CPmD data show that amino acid composition, rather than specific sequence motifs, primarily determines the partitioning behavior of IDRs in biomolecular condensates. This compositional code elucidates how IDRs, despite lacking well-defined structures, can encode complex biological functions through collective physicochemical properties. Furthermore, CPmD accurately measures additional interactions that are sequence-specific and have the potential to encode preferential partitioning to a given condensate.

We find a remarkable correlation between our experimentally determined average amino acid-specific partition free energies and the “stickiness” parameters of the state-of-the-art coarse-grained force field for IDR simulations, CALVADOS. This agreement underscores that our approach captures the dominant fundamental interactions that govern IDR behavior. Inside a protein condensate, amino acids experience an averaged environment of all other amino acids in the sequence, providing a more natural environment than hydrophobic solvents used for traditional hydrophobicity scales. This explains why our partitioning experiments with a single condensate type yield insights with surprising generalizability across the disordered proteome.

The mean-field model that we present extends beyond DDX4N1, quantitatively explaining the phase separation of unrelated IDRs. Surprisingly, this is possible without data on the role of peptide charge, which was likely masked by the mRNA’s strong negative charge in CPmD. Protein net charge influences complex coacervation52, and close-to-neutral proteins are generally less soluble, which also translates into a greater tendency to undergo homotypic phase separation16,48. Although charge patterning can affect homotypic phase separation26,40,53,54, the charges in naturally occurring IDRs are generally well mixed54,55. The predictive success of our charge-deficient mean-field model strongly supports the importance of generic hydrophobicity for biomolecular condensate formation. Furthermore, we find that substitution effects are largely additive for condensate formation, which parallels the principles observed in two-state protein folding56,57. Analogously, our mean-field model thus elucidates a global constraint that might govern the evolution of IDR sequences involved in condensate formation. The need to maintain the phase separation potential in the face of random mutations would retain a constant chemistry rather than sequence, which is common in IDRs7,47,58.

DNA synthesis and sequencing are currently bottlenecks for evaluating longer IDRs or full proteins, which is, in principle, possible with CPmD. In this work, we utilized more readily available experiments with peptides and protein fragments to showcase the efficiency of CPmD and the fundamental insights that these experiments can provide. As DNA synthesis and sequencing costs continue to decrease, CPmD will enable experiments of even greater scale and complexity. This approach opens possibilities for investigating reconstituted biomolecular condensates at near-cellular complexity, including folded protein domains. CPmD can also discover peptides capable of specific enrichment in condensates to enable detection or drug delivery. As condensates are increasingly recognized as central organizers of cellular biochemistry, with implications in neurodegeneration, cancer, and viral infection, CPmD provides a quantitative foundation for understanding the often weak and transient molecular interactions that encode crucial biochemical roles of condensates in health and disease.

Methods

DDX4N1 CtoA expression and purification

A mutant of the N-terminal domain of Dead-box helicase 4 (DDX4N1) in which all cysteine residues were mutated to alanines (CtoA) was used in all experiments. This mutant simplified handling of the protein and removed the potential for disulphide bridge formation and has been shown to have little or no effect on phase separation 45. Purification was performed through bacterial recombinant expression of DDX4N1 CtoA fused to a de novo-designed thioredoxin-tag, eMM941. Briefly, eMM9 is thought to facilitate protein expression through its high expression level and its high thermodynamic stability. The stability confers resistance to both heat and high denaturant concentrations, useful for purification strategies used for intrinsically disordered proteins. A pET29(a) plasmid was constructed using Golden gate cloning containing an open reading frame coding for C-terminally His-tagged eMM9, followed by codon-optimized DDX4N1 CtoA linked by a 10-residue GS-linker and a TEV-cleavage site (ENLYFQ/G). The plasmid was used to transform E. coli strain BL21 (DE3) (New England Biolabs), and a clone was selected on kanamycin LB-agarose plates and Sanger-sequenced to confirm the correct sequence. 10 ml overnight culture was prepared in LB-medium supplied with 50 mg/l kanamycin and was used to inoculate 1l AB-LB (LB-medium with 5 g/l NaCl supplemented with 40 mM sodium/potassium phosphate (K/NaPi) buffer, pH 7.0, 15 mM ammonium sulphate, 50 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM CaCl2, 3 nM FeCl3, 50 mg/l kanamycin). The culture was grown at 37 °C to an OD600 of 1.6 and induced with 1 mM IPTG before incubating overnight at 20 °C. The next day, the cells were harvested by centrifugation at 7000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the pellet was frozen and stored at −20 °C. Next, 30 ml lysis buffer (50 mM NaPi buffer, pH 6.5 with 500 mM NaCl) was used to resuspend the cell pellet, and 8 μl Benzonase (purity > 90%, Merck-Millipore) was added. Cells were lysed by 30s/30s sonication pulses for a total time of 30 min on ice. Insoluble debris was then removed by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was then heat-treated by incubation at 80 °C for 15 min. Heat-induced protein aggregates were cleared by centrifugation at 4500 × g for 30 min at 4 °C and passed through a 0.22 μm filter. 1 M imidazole was added to a final concentration of 10 mM followed by quick mixing. The solution was then loaded onto 5 ml pre-equilibrated Ni-NTA resin (ThermoFisher Scientific) on a gravity flow column and washed with 10 column volumes of a buffer containing 50 mM NaPi, pH 6.5, 500 mM NaCl, and 20 mM imidazole, then 10 column volumes denaturing buffer containing 50 mM NaPi, pH 6.5, 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, and 3M GdnHCl. Finally, the denaturing salt was washed out with 10 column volumes of the non-denaturing wash buffer. A protease solution (9.5 ml lysis buffer with 2 mM TCEP and 500 μl His-tagged TEV protease (10KU, Genscript or in-house purified)) was then added to release DDX4N1 CtoA into the solution during overnight incubation at room temperature with gentle mixing. TEV protease stays bound, and the eluted solution was concentrated using a 10 kDa centrifugal filter to 2 ml and SEC purified on a HiLoad Superdex 75 16/600 (GE Healthcare, USA) column equipped to an ÄKTA Pure system in a cooled cabinet at 8 °C. Pooled fractions were concentrated to 500–600 μM, and aliquots were flash frozen in liquid nitrogen before storing at −80 °C. The protein purity was checked at different stages of purification using Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate-Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and the protocol yielded ~14 mg protein per litre culture.

Plate based purification of mutated protein variants

Single point mutations were chosen based on the resolved ΔΔG-values obtained from the DDX4N1 mutation library. Deletion mutants were designed to match the partition profile of the DDX4N1 tiling library. Primers for generating mutants via Golden Gate cloning were ordered from IDT (Germany) at 100 μM concentration TE buffer in a 96-well plate. The primers targeted 30 different substitutions and deletions of DDX4N1. They were then diluted to 1 μM in MilliQ to use for PCR amplification of the pET29a plasmid encoding eMM9-tagged DDX4N1 CtoA. 20 μL PCR reaction was set up using 1× Phusion master mix (F531L, ThermoFisher Scientific), 0.04 ng/μL plasmid, and 50 pM primers. The PCR reaction was done in an Opentrons pipetting robot equipped with a thermal cycler (Opentrons, USA). 30 cycles were run with initial denaturation for 30 s at 98 °C, final extension for 10 min at 72 °C, and cycling through: 5 s at 98 °C and 240 s at 72 °C. The PCR reaction was assessed on 1% agar gel, and the successfully produced PCR products were pooled together, 3.7 μL of 27 products, totaling 100 μL. The pooled PCR product was cleaned with QIAquick PCR clean-up kit (Qiagen, Netherlands). The product was then ligated via Golden Gate Assembly in an overnight ligation reaction at 37 °C with the following final concentrations: 1000U T4 DNA ligase (M0202, NEB, USA), 1× T4 DNA ligase buffer, 30U Bsal-HFv2 (R3733, NEB, USA), 192 ng PCR product. The reaction was then inactivated by heating to 60 °C for 5 min. 1 μL of Dpn1 (FD1703, Thermo, USA) was added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C, and then inactivated at 80 °C for 5 min. 50 μL of BL21 (DE3) cells (NEB, USA) were transformed with the product, and colonies formed were transferred to a 96-well agar kanamycin plate to be sequenced by Eurofins Genomics (Germany) and to a deep well plate for making glycerol stocks from overnight cultures.

The sequencing resulted in 23 unique sequences of mutants that were then used for expression together with DDX4N1 CtoA as a control. This was done in two 24-deep-well plates (2 × 2 ml of each variant). 20 μL of overnight cultures made from the glycerol stocks were transferred to 2 mL of Studier ZYM-5052 medium (Teknova, USA) with kanamycin. The cultures grew in an Eppendorf ThermoMixer shaking at 800 RPM at 25 °C for 24 h. Cells were harvested in 2 mL tubes by centrifugation at 7000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the 2 mL from the other plate was added and centrifuged again. 500 μL wash buffer (20 mM NaPi, 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM Imidazole, pH = 6.5) was added to resuspended cells. The lysis was then performed by heating to 80 °C for 20 min, followed by centrifugation for 20 min at 20,000 × g to remove debris and aggregates. The supernatant was purified using 200 μL NTA-beads (#88222, Thermo, USA) in a filter plate using a vacuum manifold. After washing the beads, 500 μL of the supernatant was loaded onto the plate, allowed to sit for 10 min before vacuum was applied, and then washed 3× with 200 μL wash buffer + 3M GdnHCl. Then, several more wash steps were performed with the wash buffer without GdnHCl. Finally, 100 μL of TEV cleavage solution (CombiTEVp 11 μM (in-house purified) in wash buffer supplemented with 0.5 μM TCEP) was loaded and incubated overnight. The protein was then eluted using three additional 100 μL washes with wash buffer and concentrated with 30 kDa filters (UFC503096, Merck Millipore, USA) to 30 μL. The produced proteins were quality checked using SDS-PAGE (Supplementary Fig. 6), and their hydrodynamic radius was measured using FIDA.

Microscopy

Confocal microscopy of mRNA-display library

1 nM mRNA-display libraries containing a FITC dye were mixed with 120 μM DDX4N1 at 100 mM NaCl in NaPi buffer pH 6.5 with 0.05% Tween-20. Brightfield and confocal micrographs were recorded using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope (Germany) using an 63×/1.40 oil immersion objective at the DTU bio-imaging core facility. Confocal images were obtained with a 16-bit depth. An excitation wavelength of 488 nm and emission channel of 500–600 nm was used. The gain was set to 1121V for the fluorescent channel, and the sensitivity was set to 85% to detect the faint signal from the mRNA-display libraries. Images were recorded on rectangular glass coverslips 24 × 50 mm (Menzel Gläser), which were plasma cleaned for 1 min using the PDC-002-CE plasma cleaner from Harrick Plasma (USA). Plasma cleaned PDMS gaskets with 3 μL circular wells were attached to the coverslips and used for imaging.

Confocal microscopy of fluorescent peptides

In addition to the Capflex measurements, the partition coefficients of the fluorescent peptides at 3 different concentrations were measured using fluorescence confocal microscopy. Samples were prepared as for the Fida 1 Capflex experiments except that the peptide stock solutions were diluted to 700, 600 and 500 nM and a DDX4N1 stock solution of 350 μM was used. The total volume after mixing was 5 μL, while keeping the ratios the same as in the Fida 1 experiment (70 μM DDX4N1, 560/480/400 nM peptides final). A Z-stack (10 images, 0.3 μm step) was recorded for each sample as described above in the Microscopy section, with a few changes: the laser intensity was set to 0.5% to minimize bleaching, and a gain set to 795 V. The laser intensity and gain were the same for all the samples except P78 (10%, 774 V) and P62 (0.5%, 740 V). The settings were kept the same within a dilution series. For each sample, the partition coefficients were calculated by subtracting the median pixel value outside the condensates from inside the condensates in a single slice. The fluorescence was uniform across one image; the median value was used to remove noise. The condensates were segmented using a custom Python script. Briefly, the images were pre-processed by applying Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE), followed by non-local means denoising and unsharp masking. This was necessary to ensure proper masking of the samples that had large differences in noise levels. The condensates were masked using distance transform watershed with Otsu thresholding. The smallest condensates were filtered out (<150 px2) for the calculations. The mask was applied to the raw images, and the raw pixel values were used for the calculations. The partition coefficient of each peptide was consistent across dilutions and slices. Only one image per Z-stack was used for the calculations. The partition coefficient of each peptide is the average value across the dilution series.

mRNA-display

DDX4N1 tiling library from oligo pool (FragLib)

To investigate which parts of the DDX4N1 sequence participated most strongly in the condensation, we designed a tiling library consisting of many overlapping fragments of the protein domain. The library was designed with fragments that ranged from 14 amino acids in length and increased by two to 50 amino acids (l = 14,16,18... 50). For each length, the overlapping fragments were placed to start from every other amino acid, so that for 14 amino acid length fragments, each amino acid in DDX4N1 sufficiently far from the N and C-terminus was present in 7 different fragments. The library was synthesised by Twist Bioscience (USA) with 5′ and 3′ regions designed for PCR amplification. This PCR step is recommended by the supplier, but also allows the attachment of the remaining 5′ and 3′ regions needed for mRNA-display59. The library was solubilised to 2.5 ng/μL in TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH = 8) and 1 μL was added for a total of 100 μL PCR solution (0.5 μM Fivep_flank, 0.5 μM Threep_flank_HA, 1X Phusion HF buffer, 0.2 mM dNTPs, and 0.02 U Phusion (NEB M0530, USA)). 14 cycles were run with initial denaturation for 30 s at 98 °C, final extension for 60 s at 72 °C, and cycling through: 10 s at 98 °C, 30 s at 56 °C, and 40 s at 72 °C.

DDX4N1 mutation library from doped oligo nucleotides (MutantLib)

Doped oligos are oligo nucleotides synthesised with error, by adding a small amount of each of the other three nucleotides to each vial for synthesis. The resulting synthesised products will be highly diverse, with almost all molecules being unique, given a sufficiently high mutagenesis rate. We designed two such oligos, Pep6_lib_fw and Pep6_lib_rev, to generate a randomly mutated library of a central region in DDX4N1 positions [100:139]. The two oligos were designed with constant regions to anneal to Fivep_flank and Threep_flank_HA, respectively, for PCR amplification. Additionally, the two oligos overlapped with 15 base pairs at their 3′-ends allowing them to anneal and be extended to a double-stranded product using the Klenow fragment polymerase (NEB M0210, USA). The extension reaction was performed in 100 μL with 1 μM of each oligo in NEBuffer 2 (50 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, pH 7.9). The oligos were annealed in the buffer but without dNTPs and polymerase by heating the solution to 94 °C for 2 min and then ramping down the temperature at 0.2 °C/s down to 50 °C. The solution was kept for 2 min and then ramped down to 37 °C at the same speed. Then 2.5 μL 10 mM dNTPs and 1 μL Klenow fragment were added, and the reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. To limit the number of variants in the library, the 10 nM double-stranded product from the Klenow extension was diluted tenfold (5 μL in 45 μL 0.05% triton X-100) 6 times to a theoretical maximum amount of molecules in the solution in 1 μL of ~600,000. A PCR reaction similar to the one described above for the tiling library was performed using GC buffer instead of HF buffer, which was run for 33 cycles to recover the library.

DisProt library (DisProtLib)

A 59,657-member tiling library of the Disprot database (release 2022_12) containing 16-residue-long peptides with fourfold overlap was ordered from Agilent (G7270A, USA). The library was solubilised in 40 μL TE buffer, yielding ~1 μM concentration. It was then diluted 1000-fold into a 100 μL PCR solution as described above, and split in 2 × 50 μL during PCR amplification. 13 cycles were run with initial denaturation for 30 s at 98 °C, final extension for 60 s at 72 °C, and cycling through: 5 s at 98 °C, 15 s at 66 °C, and 30 s at 72 °C. The two samples were combined after the PCR reaction.

Transcription

Before transcription, the template DNA was cleaned using phenol-chloroform extraction. Briefly, 3M NaCl was added to the solution to a final concentration of 300 mM. For a 100 μL solution, 100 μL (same volume) phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol (PCI) at a 25:24:1 ratio, pH = 6.5 was then added before vortexing and centrifuging at 10,000 × g for 4 min. The top aqueous phase was then transferred to a new tube, and cleaned by adding 100 μL chloroform-isoamyl alcohol (CI) at 24:1 ratio. Again, the solution was vortexed and centrifuged to separate the two phases. Finally, the aqueous phase was then transferred to a new tube and precipitated by adding ethanol to a final 70% (v/v). The pellet was washed with fresh 25 μL of 70% ethanol, dried for a couple of minutes, and then re-solubilised in 10 μL RNAse-free water, for a theoretical maximum of 5 μM (assuming complete PCR and no loss in PCI/CI extraction). Transcription was performed using the Ribomax kit (Promega, USA) in a 50 μL reaction: 10 μL 5X buffer, 15 μL 100 mM rNTPs, 2.5 μL DNA template, 0.5 μL 1 mM DDT, 0.3 μL RNAsin Plus (Promega, USA), 5 μL Enzyme mix, and 16.7 μL RNAse-free water. The reaction was incubated at 37 °C overnight (~16 h). To remove template DNA, 5.7 μL DNAse buffer (0.4M Tris pH 8.0, 0.1M MgSO4, 0.01M CaCl2) and 1 μL DNAse 1 (Thermo EN0525, USA) were added, and the solution was incubated for a maximum of 30 min at 37 °C. To stop the reaction, EDTA pH = 8 was added to a final concentration of 50 mM and NaCl was added to a final concentration of 300 mM. The RNA was then purified using PCI/CI as described above using a PCI solution with pH = 4.5. Instead of precipitation, the aqueous phase after CI treatment was transferred to a NAP-5 column (Cytiva, USA) equilibrated with 10 mL RNAse-free water. If adding a 70 μL sample, 430 μL of RNAse-free water was then added. Finally, 500 μL RNAse-free water was added to elute the mRNA. 0.3 μL RNAsin was added to the tube before storing at −80 °C.

Puromycin ligation: Y-ligation

Ligation to the puromycin-containing linker was done in 50 μL reaction containing 7.5 μM FPur-Linker and 5 μM mRNA. A 25 μL solution with 2× concentration of the two (15 and 10 μM, respectively) was heated to 95 °C and slowly cooled to room temperature. Then, 5 μL 10× T4 RNA ligase buffer and 5 μL T4 ssRNA ligase 1 (NEB, USA) were added and the reaction was incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. 3 μL of the reaction was loaded onto a PAGE gel containing TBE (Tris-Borate-EDTA), 8M Urea, 20% (v/v) formamide, and 8% acrylamide 19:1 to evaluate ligation. EDTA was added to the remainder of the reaction to a final concentration of 50 mM and NaCl to 300 mM, and the solution was purified using PCI/CI as described above using a pH 6.5 PCI solution. For the DisProt library, additional measures were taken to purify the ligated product from unligated mRNA. 2 μL 10% acetic acid was added to decrease the pH of the solution, together with 3 μL formamide to a final 5% to inhibit RNA-RNA interactions. Using a pH 6.5 PCI solution, ligated mRNA was found to be located between the lower phenol and upper aqueous phase. The aqueous phase was removed, and the remainder was washed with 300 mM NaCl, which was then removed again after centrifugation. The purified product was released using 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, with 300 mM NaCl by vortexing. The aqueous phase was recovered after centrifugation and cleaned using CI as described above. The aqueous phase was then precipitated in 70% ethanol, washed with 70% ethanol, and solubilised in 7.5 μL RNAse-free water. This solution also contained un-ligated mRNA; however, this was removed in the HA-tag purification step.

Puromycin ligation: splint ligation and biotin purification

For the second CPmD measurement of the DDX4N1 tiling library, we developed a better way to isolate the puromycin-ligated mRNA before translation using a splint-templated reaction. For 50 μL SplintR ligation reaction, 1.5 μM mRNA stock was mixed with 1.8 μM FPur-Linker and 2.0 μM biotin-splint and annealed at 95 °C for 3 min and slowly cooled to room temperature. The mix was cooled to 20 °C and mixed with 1X SplintR ligase buffer (NEB), 1.25 U/μL SplintR ligase (NEB), and 0.3 μL RNasin. The reaction was incubated at 20 °C for 80 min. To stop the reaction, 0.5 μL 0.5 M EDTA, pH 8.0, was added to 50 μL reaction. At room temperature, 48 μL SplintR ligase reaction was mixed with 48 μL 2X blocking buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 2 M NaCl, 0.1 % (v/v) Tween20, 0.2% (w/v) acetylated BSA). Also at room temperature, 100 μL Dynabeads M-280 Streptavidin (Thermo Scientific) were washed 3 times with 1X washing buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 1M NaCl, 0.05 % (v/v) Tween20, 0.1 % (w/v) acetylated BSA). For each washing step, the beads were pelleted using a magnet, and the clear supernatant was removed. The beads were resuspended in fresh washing buffer and transferred to a new tube. For binding of the biotin to the streptavidin, the pelleted beads were resuspended in 96 μL blocked sample at 35 °C. The suspension was incubated at 35 °C for 15 min and resuspended multiple times to avoid sedimentation. At 35 °C, the beads were washed 2 times with 50 μL 1X washing buffer and 1 time with 50 μL RNase-free ddH2O. For elution, the pelleted beads were resuspended in 20 μL RNase-free ddH2O for 2.5-fold concentration at room temperature and transferred to a new tube. The puromycin-ligated mRNA was heat-eluted from the biotin-splint by incubation at 80 °C for 10 min. The purified, puromycin-ligated mRNA was transferred to a new tube, frozen in liquid nitrogen and saved at −80 °C.

Translation

Translation of the ligated mRNA was performed using the PureExpress ΔRF1,2,3 kit (NEB, USA). A 25 μL reaction was performed using 10 μL solution A, 7.5 μL solution B, 5 μL template, and 2.5 μL RNAse-free water. The solution was incubated for 1 h at 37 °C for translation to proceed. 3.25 μL 100 mM Mg(OAc)2 pH = 8, were added to the solution to improve the efficiency of the puromycin reaction during 30 min incubation at 37 °C. Finally, 8.25 μL 100 mM EDTA pH = 8 was added to release the ribosomes during 30 min incubation at 37 °C. The translation solution was then mixed 1:1 with 2X blocking buffer (100 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.6, 400 mM NaCl, 0.1% (v/v) Tween-20, 0.1 g/L yeast RNA (Thermo, USA), 0.2 g/L acetylated BSA (Thermo, USA), and 5 μM GS3an_R36) and cooled on ice. 40 μL Pierce Anti-HA beads (Thermo, USA) were washed 3 times in ice-cold wash buffer (50 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.6, 200 mM NaCl, 0.05% (v/v) Tween-20, 1 μM GS3an_R36) using magnetic separation. The blocked translation mixture was used to resuspend the pelleted beads and incubated for 1 h at 4 °C with rotation to allow binding. The supernatant was then removed after magnetic separation, and two rounds of washing were swiftly performed using ice-cold washing buffer. A final wash was performed with elution buffer (20 mM NaPi, 500 mM NaCl, 0.05% (v/v) Tween-20, and trace amounts of RNAsin (Promega, USA)), before eluting the bound molecules by adding 20 μL elution buffer and incubating the beads at 80 °C for 10 min. The solution was aliquoted, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Quantification was performed using qPCR or fluorescence from the fluorescein on the puromycin linker, yielding a typical concentration of ~20 nM.

Partitioning experiments

An aliquot of the library to be screened for partitioning was diluted tenfold with low salt buffer (20 mM NaPi and 0.05% (v/v) Tween-20) such that this solution could induce condensate formation of DDX4N1 CtoA. In parallel, the mRNA used to create the library was diluted to the same concentration, usually ~2 nM, to be used as a control. Both of these solutions were cooled on ice and then added to two different PCR tubes, one containing 1.5 μL 600 μM DDX4N1 in elution buffer and one with 1.5 μL elution buffer without protein, resulting in four tubes in total. All experiments were at least performed in duplicates, except the first CPmD experiments of FragLib and MutantLib. All tubes were incubated for 1 min at room temperature and then centrifuged for 10 min at 10,000 × g in a pre-equilibrated centrifuge at 20 °C. After centrifugation, 1 μL of the supernatant from each tube was immediately added to the reverse transcription solution (1x SS-IV buffer, 0.25 mM dNTPs, 0.75 μM GS3an_R36, 1.5 units of SS-IV reverse transcriptase (Thermo, USA), and trace amount of RNAsin (Promega, USA)). The reaction was left to proceed at 50 °C for 1 h to ensure completion. The resulting solution was stored at −20 °C.

Sequencing

Library preparation for sequencing

Attachment of Illumina adapter sequences was done through two consecutive PCR reactions. But first, the concentration of the template in each of the reverse transcription reactions was estimated using qPCR, such that over-cycling could be avoided. The first PCR reaction was performed in 50 μL using 0.15 μM of Rd1T7g10M_F71 and an13Rd2_R49, 1X HF buffer, 0.2 mM dNTPs, 0.5 μL reverse transcription solution, and 0.5 μL Phusion polymerase (NEB, USA). A varying amount of cycles (usually 10-20) was run based on the number needed to get 0.05 μM double-stranded product. The PCR program included an initial denaturation for 30 s at 98 °C, final extension for 60 s at 72 °C, and cycling through: 10 s at 98 °C, 30 s at 52 °C, and 40 s at 72 °C. For NovaSeq analysis, phasing version of the two primers with added random nucleotides to frame-shift the reads were used, such that all PCR products had the same length, e.g., 2 extra bases at one end and 6 extra bases at the other for a total of 8. For the second reaction, 0.15 μM of Rd2N(701)P7_R52 and P5S(502)Rd1_F57 were used in a similar PCR reaction using 2.5 μL of the former PCR reaction as a template in a 50 μL reaction. Some sequencing runs used primers annealing closer to the peptide coding region (NGS1_F_RBS and NGS1_R_GS_HA) to optimize read length. Each sample was assigned a unique combination of multiplexing barcodes in the two primers. The first samples of the DDX4N1 tiling library, FragLib, were cleaned with QIAquick PCR clean-up kit (Qiagen, Netherlands) and sequenced using MiSeq v3 chip for 2 × 300 bps read at the DTU Multi Assay Core. Samples for MutLib and DisProtLib were cleaned using Mag-Bind TotalPure NGS (Omega Bio-tek, USA) and sequenced using an SP Flow-cell or 2 × 150 bps on NovaSeq at the Kennedy Center, Rigshospitalet, Copenhagen. The second CPmD experiment on FragLib was sequenced by Genewiz Azenta (USA) using Illumina NovaSeq X.

Processing of data

All sequencing reads were processed using a custom-made pipeline (available in published code): Briefly, NGMerge60 was used to merge paired-end reads using “-d” flag for dovetailed reads. Counting of repeated sequences was done using custom code written in C++. For most libraries, only the expected sequences were counted in the sequencing fastq-files. For the mutation library, we did not have a priori knowledge of the library sequences, so all variants inside the mutated region of the peptides were counted. As the random mutations in the library are identical to sequencing errors, the only way to remove sequencing noise is to require a minimum number of reads. This was implemented by requiring all variants to have at least hundred reads and be present in all samples.

Analysis and modeling

Calculation of partition free energy

The model to calculate partition free energies from sequencing and qPCR data rests on a few assumptions that are expanded on in the supplementary materials but briefly mentioned here. (1) The relative abundance of sequencing reads is directly proportional to the concentration of each variant in the library. (2) The concentration in the condensate phase is exactly the molecules removed from the dilute phase divided by the volume fraction of the condensed phase. To avoid contamination between phases, we chose to measure only the concentrations of library members in the dilute phase (Fig. 1c) and infer the concentrations in the condensed phase using information on the total concentrations in the library and the dense phase volume fraction. (3) The system is at equilibrium, and the decrease in the concentration of a molecule in the dilute phase is directly due to its partition constant and, thus, the free energy of partitioning. (4) The free energy of partitioning is a sum of the contributions from mRNA, linker, and peptide. Derivations of the formulae used to calculate the partition energy can be found in the Supplementary Information.

RNA structure prediction

RNAfold61 was used to predict minimum free energy secondary structures and their energies for all mRNAs in the DDX4N1 tiling library using the default parameters. For the mutation library, the structure of the mRNA for the unmutated “WT” species was calculated using default parameters and the “-p” option to also calculate the partition function.

Fitting of linear models

Weighted ordinary least squares regressions were used to fit models of sequence-dependent partitioning of both peptides and RNA. Sequences were encoded either using their amino acid or nucleic acid composition in 20-member vectors or using one-hot encoding. These were all fitted in Python using linear algebra in Numpy. When needed, L2 regularisation (also called Tikhonov or Ridge regression) was used, and regularisation strength was tuned using fivefold cross-validation. Potential over-fitting was evaluated on 10% held out data in all cases. Model performance and regularisation parameters are all reported in Supplementary Table 6.

GO-term enrichment analysis

To obtain the extrapolated partition free energy of all IDRs in the Disprot library, the average partition energy of the tiling peptides within one IDR was calculated. This value was then divided by 16 (the number of amino acids in the peptides) and multiplied by the length of the IDR. To obtain a single value for proteins with multiple IDRs, the one with the highest affinity for the DDX4N1 condensates was chosen. GO-term enrichment was performed using GOATOOLS62 in Python, using genes from species with more than 100 entries in the DisProt database. TaxIDs: 9606 (Human), 10090 (Mouse), 10116 (Rat), 4932 (Yeast), 3702 (mouse-ear cress), 7227 (fruit fly), and 6239 (Caenorhabditis elegans). Enrichment p-values were calculated using Fisher's exact t-test, and correction for multiple testing was done using the Benjamini and Hochberg procedure (Corrected false discovery rate (FDR)).

Coarse-grained molecular dynamic simulations

We performed coarse-grained molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with openMM 8.063 using the CALVADOS 2 force field, with force field parameters described elsewhere48,64. Briefly described, the CALVADOS 2 force field represents IDRs using one bead per residue, with neighboring amino acid residues bonded via a harmonic potential. Salt-screened electrostatic interactions are represented via a Debye–Hückel potential. Non-electrostatic short-ranged interactions are described by a modified Lennard-Jones potential with residue type-specific sizes and stickiness values λ.

Each simulation was performed at constant molecule number, volume and temperature (Langevin integrator, T = 293 K, friction coefficient = 0.01 ps−1) at 175 mM ionic strength and pH 6.5 in a tetragonal, periodic simulation box with edge lengths 20 nm × 20 nm × 300 nm. For each simulation, the first 600 ns were discarded as equilibration. For WT tiling simulations, WT fragment tiles of 16 residue length were generated along the DDX4N1 sequence, beginning from every residue (res. 1–16, 2–17, etc). We simulated a slab of 200 molecules of DDX4N1 together with one copy of all WT tiles in the same simulation box (Supplementary Fig. 22). The WT tiling simulations were run in triplicate. The partitioning free energy ΔGpart for a given fragment tile was calculated using the ratio of the average number of molecules in the dilute phase of the simulation box and the average number of molecules in the DDX4N1 condensate (dense phase) across the trajectory. The boundaries between dense and dilute phases were determined from the DDX4N1 concentration profile as described previously64. Reported ΔGpart values are averages across the three independent simulation runs (average SEM(ΔG) = 0.30 kJ mol−1).

To estimate single substitution effects on fragment partitioning into DDX4N1, all possible single substitution variants of a peptide corresponding to DDX4N1 residues 100–139 were generated. Five copies of each of the 20 fragments corresponding to the WT and all single substitutions at a given residue position were simulated together with a slab of 200 DDX4N1 molecules. Each setup was simulated in four replicates, resulting in 4 × 40 = 160 simulations. Reported ΔΔG values are calculated as the mean partitioning ΔG of all 5 × 4 copies of a given variant fragment minus the mean partitioning ΔG of all 5 × 4 × 20 control WT fragments in the simulations (average SEM(ΔG) = 0.13 kJ mol−1).

We simulated a subset of 200 of the multi-mutant fragments for DDX4N1 residues 100–139 that only contained a defined subset of substitutions in batches of 20 different fragments together with a slab of 200 DDX4N1 molecules, as above in four replicates (average SEM(ΔG) = 0.19 kJ mol−1). GMMA analysis was performed on these simulated data using the linear models described above similarly to how they were used for CPmD data.
