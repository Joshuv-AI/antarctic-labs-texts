Liver Ewing sarcoma: A case report
Authors: Sarah Lembarki, Fatma ez zahrae El malki, Nazik Allali, Latifa Chat, Siham EL haddad, Zineb Isfaoun, Amina Kili, Maria El kababri, Laila Hesissen, M Ouchen, Mounir Kisra, S Sefiani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEwing’s sarcoma (ES) is a rare malignant small round-cell tumor that usually arises in bone and only exceptionally originates in visceral organs. We report the case of a 7-year-old boy with primary hepatic ES, the youngest case described to date. Imaging revealed a large, solid hepatic mass with heterogeneous enhancement and extensive local vascular involvement, associated with pulmonary and skeletal metastases. Histology and immunohistochemistry (CD99 +, INI-1 +, NKX2.2 +, Ki-67 ≈ 60%) confirmed the diagnosis. This is the first Moroccan and the seventh case of primary hepatic ES reported in the literature, emphasizing the need to consider this entity in the differential diagnosis of pediatric liver tumors.Keywords: Ewing sarcoma, Liver, Foetal

Introduction

Ewing sarcoma (ES) is a rare, aggressive small round-cell tumor of neural crest origin that predominantly affects children and young adults [1]. The Ewing sarcoma family of tumors (ESFT) includes Ewing sarcoma of bone, peripheral primitive neuroectodermal tumor (pPNET), Askin tumor (a malignant small cell tumor of the chest wall), and extraosseous Ewing sarcoma (EES) [2]. While ES most commonly arises in bone, accounting for 10%-15% of primary bone tumors, it can also infrequently originate in soft tissues [3]. Rare manifestations have been documented in various visceral organs, including the lungs, kidneys, uterus, and gastrointestinal tract [4]. Primary involvement of the liver is exceptionally rare, with only 6 cases reported in the literature to date [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. We present a case of a 7-year-old child with primary ES of the liver and provide a review of the literature on extraosseous ES.

Case presentation

A 7-year-old child presented with a 3-month history of abdominal pain and intermittent fever, associated with pallor and diffuse arthralgia. The patient also reported significant weight loss and loss of appetite. In the 4 days preceding admission, a large epigastric mass approximately 12 cm in diameter was noted. Physical examination revealed a painless abdominal mass in the epigastrium without signs of local inflammation.

Routine laboratory investigations on admission, including serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and bilirubin levels, were within normal ranges. However, serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was markedly elevated at 359.3 U/L (normal range: 120-250 U/L). Serological tests for hepatitis A, B, and C viruses, cytomegalovirus (CMV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), and HIV were negative.

Abdominal ultrasound revealed a large, heterogeneously hypoechoic lesion in the left lobe of the liver (Fig. 1). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan identified a voluminous, oval, solid mass with regular margins in segments II, III, and IV, measuring 113×80×100 mm (Fig. 2). The mass demonstrated heterogeneous enhancement on contrast administration and was associated with perilesional perfusion abnormalities. It exhibited extensive local invasion, extending to the abdominal wall, stomach, and pancreas, with involvement of major vascular structures. It also invaded the portacaval space, obscuring the identification of the portal trunk, the splenomesenteric trunk, and the superior mesenteric vein. The mass showed intimal contact (< 90°) with the suprarenal abdominal aorta, displaced the celiac trunk, and encompassed the common hepatic artery over more than 180° for approximately 15 mm. It was in contact with the right gastric artery and the inferior vena cava (IVC) without signs of thrombosis, while compressing the common bile duct and causing upstream biliary dilation. Additionally, the mass was associated with multiple hepatic nodules, some exhibiting a cystic center.

Based on the clinical and radiological findings, we made a presumed diagnosis of hepatoblastoma, sarcoma or lymphoma.

Ultrasound-guided liver biopsy was performed. Histology revealed small, round tumor cells with low mitotic activity. Immunohistochemistry showed diffuse positivity for CD99, nuclear positivity for INI-1 and NKX2.2, heterogeneous positivity for synaptophysin, and a high proliferative index with Ki-67 expression in approximately 60% of cells (Fig. 6). The combined morphological and immunohistochemical profile was consistent with a diagnosis of primary Ewing's sarcoma of the liver.

Discussion

Ewing sarcoma (ES) is a highly aggressive malignancy belonging to the small round blue cell sarcoma family. Approximately 80% of pediatric ES cases originate in bone with < 20% arising in soft tissues; primary visceral involvement is exceptionally rare [11]. The kidney is the most commonly involved visceral organ site. The liver is an uncommon site for primary ES, with only a few cases reported in the literature.

A review of the PubMed database confirms the scarcity of this entity, revealing only 6 cases of primary hepatic ES reported to date (Table 1).

All reported cases occurred in patients under the age of 30 [5,8]. Our patient, at 7 years old, represents the youngest reported case of primary hepatic ES to date.

Clinical presentation is nonspecific, typically including abdominal pain, fever, weight loss, or a palpable abdominal mass [8]. Laboratory tests are often unremarkable except for elevated LDH [8], reflecting high tumor burden, as was the case in our patient. The mean size at the time of diagnosis it usually exceeds 10 cm [8,10].

Due to the rarity of this entity, little information on the imaging features is available.

The imaging characteristics of primary hepatic ES are variable and non-specific. As illustrated in the literature, these tumors can manifest as either solid or multiloculated cystic lesions. For instance, Ozaki et al. [5] and Ates et al. [9] reported cases presenting as multiloculated cystic masses, initially suggestive of benign entities like cystadenoma. In contrast, our case and others, such as those by McGrann et al. and Lu et al. presented as large, solid, heterogeneously enhancing masses with areas of necrosis or hemorrhage [[6], [7], [8],10]. On magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), described in 2 previous reports, these tumors typically appear hypointense on T1-weighted images and hyperintense on T2-weighted images, with heterogeneous enhancement and features suggestive of hemorrhage or necrosis [6,8].

Diagnosis requires histological, immunohistochemical, and genetic study. On histology, the tumor is composed of small blue round cells [12]. In the immunohistochemical study, there is usually a diffuse positivity for CD99 and PAS, but these are not specific markers [8]. The genetic study allows reaching the final diagnosis because the translocation t (11, 22) (q24; q12) is specific to Ewing sarcoma, resulting in the production of the EWS/Fli1 gene fusion, which is present in >85% of cases [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]].

The management of primary Ewing sarcoma of the liver is similar to the multimodal approach used for Ewing sarcomas in other locations, centered on neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with surgical resection when feasible [13]. Radiotherapy is indicated for inoperable forms and metastases. For localized forms, this strategy offers the best chance of survival. However, the prognosis for patients with Ewing sarcoma depends on multiple prognostic factors, including age, the presence of metastases at diagnosis, tumor size and location, and the histological response to chemotherapy [14]. Our patient presented with synchronous bone and lung metastases and initiated systemic chemotherapy. Hepatic surgery to resect the primary tumor will be performed after neoadjuvant chemotherapy. The indication for radiotherapy and autologous stem cell transplantation will be discussed based on the PET scan assessment of the response to chemotherapy.

This case adds to the limited literature by documenting the youngest patient reported with primary hepatic ES, presenting with both hepatic and extrahepatic spread at diagnosis. It highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by this rare entity and emphasizes the importance of considering ES in the differential diagnosis of large hepatic masses in children.

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge and as per published literature, this is the seventh case of Ewing’s sarcoma occurring in liver as primary and first case to be present in a young boy of 7-year age. It should be considered as a differential diagnosis in young child presenting with liver mass lesion for prompt and early treatment of patient.
