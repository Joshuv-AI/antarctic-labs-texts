M2-exo promote orthodontic bone remodeling via the MeCP2-TCF20-HDAC1 axis
Authors: Mingzhu Chen, Yanjie Li, Zhenjin Yang, Mengting Chen, Junxiong Li, Guangyu Bao, Liya Ma, Jiangtian Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOrthodontic bone remodeling is a complex process involving a dynamic balance between osteogenesis and osteoclast genesis. Although M2 macrophage-derived exosomes (M2-exos) have emerged as key regulators of bone metabolism through immunomodulation and paracrine signaling, their specific mechanisms in orthodontic tooth movement (OTM) remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate the role of the epigenetic MeCP2-TCF20 complex in M2-exo-mediated orthodontic bone remodeling.MethodsA rat OTM model was established to evaluate bone remodeling dynamics using micro-CT, histomorphometry (H&E and TRAP staining), and immunohistochemistry. Periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) were isolated and treated with M2-exos to assess osteogenic differentiation through alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and alizarin red S (ARS) staining, qPCR, and Western blot. Molecular mechanisms were explored via RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS), and functional validation experiments (siRNA knockdown and overexpression).ResultsOrthodontic tension force upregulate M2 polarization and osteoblast differentiation, in contrast to compressive force which triggered M1 polarization and osteoclastogenesis, with concomitant modulation of MeCP2 expression in mechanically stressed periodontal tissues. M2-exos significantly promoted the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs. Mechanistic studies revealed that M2-exos activated the MeCP2-TCF20 complex, which directly suppressed HDAC1 expression, thereby activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway.ConclusionThese findings highlight the critical role of the M2-exo/MeCP2-TCF20 axis in orchestrating orthodontic bone remodeling through epigenetic regulation. This study provides novel insights into the therapeutic potential of M2-exos for enhancing orthodontic treatment efficiency.

Introduction

Orthodontic treatment achieves three-dimensional tooth repositioning and functional occlusion reconstruction through mechanically induced alveolar bone remodeling [1–4]. This physiological process, termed orthodontic tooth movement (OTM), relies on the spatiotemporal equilibrium between bone resorption on the compression side and bone formation on the tension side [5–8]. Clinical evidence indicates that disrupted coupling of osteoclastic/osteogenic activities leads to prolonged treatment duration and complications such as root resorption, ultimately compromising therapeutic outcomes [9, 10]. Mechanistically, osteocytes transduce mechanical stimuli via paracrine signaling pathways, with periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) serving as pivotal orchestrators of microenvironmental homeostasis [11, 12].

As a unique mesenchymal stromal cell population within periodontal tissues, PDLSCs exhibit remarkable self-renewal capacity and multilineage differentiation potential [13, 14]. Beyond their intrinsic regenerative properties, PDLSCs regulate tissue homeostasis through extracellular vesicle-mediated intercellular communication. Notably, a bidirectional regulatory loop exists between macrophage polarization states and PDLSC functionality: PDLSCs drive macrophage M2 polarization via immunomodulatory factors, while M2 macrophage-derived exosomes (M2-exos) reciprocally enhance PDLSC osteogenesis through delivery of effector molecules such as miR-21a-5p [15–18]. Although M2-exos have demonstrated pro-regenerative effects in bone defect repair, their epigenetic regulatory mechanisms in orthodontic remodeling, particularly under mechanical loading conditions, remain unexplored.

Emerging evidence highlights the centrality of epigenetic networks including DNA methylation, histone modification, and non-coding RNA regulation in bone metabolism [19–22]. Our previous work identified methyl-CpG-binding protein 2 (MeCP2) as a critical mediator of M2-exos enhanced osteogenesis in PDLSCs [23]. Intriguingly, MeCP2 forms a functional complex with transcriptional coactivator transcription factor 20 (TCF20), a configuration originally characterized in synaptic plasticity regulation [24]. Preliminary data suggest MeCP2 modulates osteogenic differentiation through SFRP4/Wnt pathway interactions, though its role in mechanotransduction-driven bone remodeling requires systematic investigation [25, 26]. Furthermore, histone deacetylase HDAC1, a putative MeCP2 interactor, exerts inhibitory effects on osteogenic gene networks, yet the tripartite regulatory axis connecting these molecules remains elusive [27–29].

Based on these insights, it is hypothesized that epigenetic effectors are delivered by M2-exos, leading to activation of the MeCP2-TCF20 complex, subsequent suppression of HDAC1 activity, and enhancement of Wnt/β-catenin mediated osteogenesis, thereby accelerating orthodontic bone remodeling. To validate this hypothesis, an integrated approach combining rat OTM models, multi-omics analyses, and functional validation of molecular interactions was employed. The present study is designed to decode the epigenetic program governing M2-exos regulated PDLSCs differentiation, with a novel therapeutic axis targeting the MeCP2-TCF20 complex through the HDAC1/Wnt/β-catenin pathway being established, aiming to optimize orthodontic treatment efficiency while minimizing post-therapeutic relapse.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and ethical standards

All animal experiments in this study were approved and conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Experiment Ethics Review Committee of Kunming Medical University (Approval No. KMMU20230702). The protocol strictly complied with ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. Twenty-five female Sprague–Dawley rats (8 weeks old, 200–250 g, SPF grade, Kunming Medical University) were randomized using a computer-generated sequence into five experimental groups (n = 5/group) corresponding to observation time points(0, 5, 7, 14 and 21° days). A standardized orthodontic force system was established using medical-grade nickel-titanium closed-coil springs (50 g continuous force, Shinye, China) anchored between maxillary first molars and incisors (Fig. 1A). All surgical procedures were performed under isoflurane anesthesia (3% induction, 1.5% maintenance). Rats were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg). Death was confirmed by the absence of corneal reflexes and cessation of breathing for > 1 min. This protocol complies with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for Euthanasia (2020) and was performed by trained personnel. Microcomputed tomography analysis

The maxillae of rats were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio #G1101, China) at 4 °C for 72 h. Subsequently, micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) imaging was conducted using the SkyScan 1176 scanner (Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany). Bone-related metrics were evaluated using Data Viewer (version 1.5.6) and CTAn (version 1.18) software. Bone mineral density (BMD) was determined by referencing two hydroxyapatite (Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2) models with BMD values of 0.250 g/cm3 and 0.750 g/cm3, respectively. The region of interest (ROI) in the femoral trabeculae was defined as a horizontal section positioned 1.00 mm from the distal growth plate, extending proximally (83 slices). Parameters such as trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular spacing (Tb.Sp) and BMD were measured and quantified. To ensure reliability, all analyses were independently performed by two researchers.

Histological analysis

Tissue samples were fixed in neutral-buffered 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio #G1101, China) for 48 h, followed by decalcification in 10% EDTA (pH 7.4, Servicebio #G1105, China) with weekly solution replacement, and finally embedded in medical-grade paraffin (Leica, Germany). Serial sagittal sections (5 μm) were prepared for the following analyses: H&E staining (H&E Kit, Servicebio #G1005, China) to assess general histology, and TRAP staining (TRAP Assay Kit, Servicebio #G1058, China) to quantify multinucleated osteoclasts. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0, Servicebio #G1202, China) at 95 °C for 20 min. Sections were incubated with validated primary antibodies (iNOS: 1:500, Proteintech #22226-1-AP; Arg-1: 1:800, Proteintech #16001-1-AP; RUNX2: 1:1000, Proteintech #20700-1-AP; MeCP2: 1:600, Proteintech #10861-1-AP, China) at 4 °C overnight, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (ZSGB-BIO #PV-9000, China) and DAB chromogen development (ZSGB-BIO #ZLI-9017, China). Three non-consecutive fields per sample were imaged using a Nikon Eclipse E100 microscope (Japan) and analyzed with Image-Pro Plus 6.0 (Media Cybernetics).

Cell culture and processing

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Hospital of Stomatology, Kunming Medical University (Project No. KYKQ2023MEC024). All patients provided written informed consent. Periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) were isolated from premolars or impacted third molars requiring orthodontic extraction. Under sterile conditions, periodontal ligament tissue from the apical third of the roots was minced and cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium (Gibco #11320033, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco #10099141, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S, Gibco #15140122, USA). The characterization of PDLSCs was performed using flow cytometry to analyze surface markers (CD29+/CD44+/CD90+/CD105+, CD34−/CD45−) (BD Pharmingen, USA), and differentiation capabilities were verified through colony-forming assays, osteogenic differentiation using Alizarin Red staining, and adipogenic differentiation using Oil Red O staining. Additionally, immunofluorescence was conducted to detect markers such as Nestin (Proteintech #19483-1-AP, China) and Vimentin (Proteintech #10366-1-AP, China).

For macrophage polarization, THP-1 cells, obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (China, TCHu 57), were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco #C11875500BT, USA) with 10% FBS and 50 nm β-Mercaptoethanol (Solarbio #M8210-100, China) to maintain the monocyte state. M0 polarization was induced by treating the cells with 100 ng/mL Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (Solarbio # P6741, China) for 24 h. For M1 polarization, the cells were stimulated with 100 ng/mL Lipopolysaccharides (LPS) (Solarbio #L8880, China) for 48 h. M2 polarization was achieved by stimulating the cells with 20 ng/mL IL-4 (Solarbio #P00021, China) and 20 ng/mL IL-13 (Solarbio #P00131, China) for 48 h. To confirm the polarization status, surface markers (CD14 Biolegend #301807/CD11b Biolegend #301305/CD86 Biolegend #374205/CD206 Biolegend #321105, China) were analyzed using flow cytometry, and immunofluorescence was conducted to detect markers such as IL-1β (Proteintech #16806-1-AP, China) and CD206 (Proteintech #18704-1-AP, China).

M2-exos separation and identification

Exosomes from M2 macrophage-conditioned media were isolated by differential ultracentrifugation: initial clarification at 300×g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris, followed by the elimination of large vesicles at 2000×g for 10 min and apoptotic bodies at 10,000×g for 30 min. The final enrichment of exosomes was achieved through ultracentrifugation at 120,000×g for 70 min at 4 °C, with the resulting pellets resuspended in sterile PBS for further applications. Comprehensive characterization of the isolated exosomes included nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), which quantified the vesicle size and concentration using a Nano Sight NS300 system. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed the cup-shaped morphology of the exosomes following negative staining with 2% phosphotungstic acid. Zeta potential measurement revealed a surface charge, assessed using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS. Surface marker profiling via nano-flow cytometry identified CD63⁺/CD81⁺ subpopulations, while Western blot analysis demonstrated the enrichment of exosomal markers such as HSP70, TSG101, CD9, CD63, and CD81(Abcam #ab275018, USA), alongside the exclusion of the endoplasmic reticulum contaminant Calnexin.

Molecular mechanism analysis

siRNA knockdown: Validated targeting MeCP2(5′-GCUACGAUGCCUAGAACUATT-3′) and TCF20(5′-CGGACUAUCUGUACGAUAUTT-3′) siRNA was designed and transfected into PDLSCs using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen #L3000015, USA) (50 nM). After 48 h, gene knockdown efficiency was validated by qPCR and Western blot (WB).

Lentiviral overexpression: The MeCP2 coding sequence (NCBI Accession: NM_004992.4) was cloned into the pLVX vector and packaged into lentivirus (titer: 1 × 10⁸ TU/mL) in HEK293T cells. PDLSCs were infected with lentivirus at an MOI of 20, supplemented with 5 μg/mL polybrene (Beyotime #C0351, China) to enhance infection efficiency. Stable transfected cell lines were selected using 2 μg/mL puromycin (Beyotime #ST551, China) for 72 h, followed by qPCR and WB to confirm MeCP2 overexpression efficiency.

Osteogenic differentiation assessment: Cells underwent ALP staining using BCIP/NBT substrate (Beyotime #C3206, China) after 7 days of osteogenic induction (α-MEM, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid, 100 nM dexamethasone) (HyCyte #BMHX-D101, China). Calcium deposition was visualized by 2% Alizarin Red S staining (ARS, Servicebio #G1038, China) at day 14 post-induction.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR): Total RNA isolated with TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA) was reverse transcribed using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara). SYBR Green-based amplification (TB Green Premix, Takara) quantified MeCP2 and TCF20 mRNA levels, normalized to GAPDH.

Western blotting: Proteins extracted with RIPA buffer (Beyotime #P0013B, China) were resolved by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes probed with antibodies against MeCP2 (Invitrogen #PA1-887, USA), TCF20 (CST #95359, USA), HDAC1 (Proteintech #10197-1-AP, China), Col-1(Proteintech #67288-1-Ig, China), RUNX2(CST #12556, USA), ALP(Proteintech #11187-1-AP, China), BMP2(Proteintech #66383-1-Ig, China), OCN(Proteintech #20277-1-AP, China)and Wnt3a(Proteintech #26744-1-AP, China), β-catenin (Proteintech #51067-2-AP, China), GAPDH(Proteintech #60004-1-Ig, China) as a loading control. Signals were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, Biosharp #BL523B, China) and quantified via Image Lab (Bio-Rad).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP): PDLSC lysates incubated with anti-MeCP2 antibody (4 °C, 12 h) were captured using Protein A/G agarose (Beyotime #P2195S, China). Co-precipitated TCF20 was detected by immunoblotting.

Molecular Docking Analysis: This study utilized molecular docking techniques to investigate the interaction mode between MeCP2 and TCF20. Firstly, the molecular structures of MeCP2 and TCF20 were retrieved from the RCSB PDB database. The three-dimensional structure of TCF20 was derived from the AlphaFold 3 prediction model. Visualization was performed by removing water molecules and non-protein ligands using PyMOL.

RNA sequencing & bioinformatics: Strand-specific libraries (NEBNext Ultra II) were sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (150 bp PE). DESeq2 identified differentially expressed genes (|log2FC|> 1, FDR < 0.05), with KEGG/GO enrichment analyzed via clusterProfiler.

Interaction proteomics: MeCP2-bound complexes were trypsin-digested and analyzed by IP-MS (Q Exactive HF-X, Thermo). STRING-db (v11.5) reconstructed interaction networks (confidence score > 0.7).

Statistical analysis

Data from three independent biological replicates were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Student’s t-test (two-group) or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc (multi-group); p < 0.05 deemed significant. Fiji (ImageJ2) quantified staining areas and colocalization coefficients.

Results

Orthodontic forces dominate alveolar bone remodeling by dynamically regulating macrophage polarization

Orthodontic forces dynamically regulated alveolar bone remodeling through macrophage polarization, as demonstrated in our reproducible rat orthodontic tooth movement model with distinct spatiotemporal characteristics (Fig. 1A). Quantitative assessments revealed progressive mesial displacement of the first molar (Fig. 1B) accompanied by significant intermolar space expansion (Fig. 1C). Under continuous 50-g orthodontic loading, micro-CT reconstructions confirmed force-dependent dynamic responses in tooth movement distance and alveolar remodeling (Fig. 1D). Time-graded microstructural reconstruction was observed in root furcation regions through sagittal and coronal plane analyses (Fig. 1E, F), with bone trabecular number (Tb.N) exhibiting transient reduction (5–7 days), nadir at day 14, and partial compensatory recovery at day 21 (*p < 0.05, Fig. 1G). Concurrently, trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) showed sustained decline (**p < 0.01, Fig. 1H), while progressively increasing trabecular separation (Tb.Sp; ***p < 0.001, Fig. 1I) and persistently reduced bone mineral density (BMD) despite local recovery at day 21 (*p < 0.05, Fig. 1J) collectively indicated mechanically induced adverse remodeling outcomes.

Histopathological and molecular signatures of mechano-immune crosstalk

Histopathological and molecular analyses revealed mechano-immune crosstalk signatures, with H&E staining showing inflammatory cell infiltration on the compression side at days 14–21 and resorption lacunae at day 21 (Fig. 2A, red arrows). TRAP staining confirmed predominant osteoclast accumulation peaking on days 7/14 at compression sites (Fig. 2B), where localized bone resorption occurred, contrasting with minimal osteoclast presence at tension sites. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated spatial macrophage polarization: iNOS⁺ (M1) macrophages selectively accumulated in compression zones (Fig. 2C), whereas Arg-1⁺ (M2) polarization dominated tension areas with sustained high expression (Fig. 2D). Concurrently, RUNX2 expression showed early transient suppression (days 5–7) at both sites followed by significant rebound at day 14 (*p < 0.05, Fig. 2E), suggesting its contextual regulatory roles in compression-side bone repair and tension-side remodeling. MeCP2 exhibited site-specific dynamics persistent suppression on compression side, early transient downregulation then significant upregulation (days 14/21) on tension side (**p < 0.01, Fig. 2F) indicating its potential mechano-responsive regulation. MeCP2-TCF20 complex regulates osteogenesis via HDAC1/Wnt/β-catenin axis

MeCP2 and TCF20 form a complex that regulates neural development [24, 30]. Protein interaction prediction (Fig. 6A) revealed that MeCP2 interacts with TCF20. Immunofluorescence assays demonstrated that both MECP2 (Fig. 6B) and TCF20 (Fig. 6C) are expressed in PDLSCs. Western blot analysis (Fig. 6D and 6E) showed a positive correlation in their expression levels. Molecular docking (Fig. 6F) identified multiple binding sites between the two proteins. Furthermore, immunofluorescence co-localization (Fig. 6G) and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP, Fig. 6H) experiments confirmed the physical interaction between MeCP2 and TCF20 in PDLSCs. Compared with the control group, the formation of the MeCP2-TCF20 complex was significantly enhanced in the OE-MeCP2 and M2-exo groups, while it was markedly reduced in the si-MeCP2 group, confirming a positive correlation between MeCP2 expression levels and complex formation, and indicating that M2-exo effectively promotes the assembly of this complex. Discussion

Orthodontic bone remodeling represents a sophisticated biological process orchestrated by the dynamic interplay between mechanical stimuli and immune networks [34, 35]. This complex cellular symphony involves multifaceted interactions among PDLSCs, macrophages, and adjacent tissue components [36], mediated through diverse communication modalities including direct cell contact, paracrine signaling, extracellular vesicles (EVs), and non-coding RNAs [37–39].Alveolar bone, as the most actively remodeled bone tissue in the human body, exhibits a bidirectional remodeling pattern under orthodontic force, characterized by bone formation in tension side and bone resorption in compression side [40, 41].

Macrophages, as mechanosensitive immune cells, undergo significant proliferation and polarization in response to orthodontic forces to participate in tissue remodeling. Mechanical stress modulates cellular inflammatory responses and immune functions via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including the NOD-like receptor family [42]. The Wnt signaling pathway serves as a pivotal mediator of mechano-immune crosstalk and plays an essential role in mechanically induced bone remodeling [43]. In compression side, M1 polarization predominates. M1 macrophages act as central regulators of the osteoimmune microenvironment [44], exhibiting dual functionality: they initiate repair processes by secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α) [45, 46] but may also contribute to pathological bone destruction under prolonged activation [47–49]. Orthodontic compressive forces activate the NF-κB pathway [50], induce macrophage autophagy [51], and promote M1 polarization, thereby enhancing osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption [52, 53]. Conversely, in tension side, mechanical stimulation preferentially induces M2 polarization. M2 macrophages exhibit anti-inflammatory and pro-repair properties, secreting growth factors (PDGF-BB) [54, 55] and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [56] to facilitate tissue repair and bone formation. Tension forces suppress M1 polarization through epigenetic modifications [52], nitric oxide (NO) secretion [57, 58], and autophagy activation [59, 60], while promoting M2 polarization to coordinate alveolar bone remodeling [61, 62]. Thus, orthodontic bone remodeling represents a synergistic interplay between mechanical stimulation and immune regulatory networks [6, 63].

Orthodontic tooth movement fundamentally depends on the precise spatiotemporal regulation of bone resorption and formation by mechanical signals. Research over the past thirty years has established three primary theoretical frameworks governing this process: (1) The RANKL/OPG pathway mediates pressure-side bone absorption through communication between osteoclast precursors and osteoblasts [64, 65]; (2) Direct mechanosensation via YAP/TAZ nuclear translocation or Piezo channel activation drives tension-side osteogenic differentiation [63, 66]; (3) An inflammatory cytokine network model highlights the opposing regulation by pro-resorptive cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α) and pro-formative cytokines (TGF-β, IL-10) in balancing remodeling [67, 68]. However, these classical frameworks are constrained by a core paradox: the RANKL/OPG axis fails to identify a bone formation molecular switch, YAP/Piezo mechanisms overlook immune cell involvement, and diffusible cytokines lack the precision for millimeter-scale spatial targeting. This central challenge revolves around understanding how mechanical stimuli are transduced into biological signals that elicit site-specific cellular responses.

This study demonstrates that mechanically induced immune polarization gradients serve as a spatial decoding mechanism. Within the periodontal microenvironment, pressure-side monocytes polarize to an M1 phenotype within 48 h, activating osteoclast precursors. Conversely, tension-side macrophages exhibit sustained polarization toward an immunomodulatory M2 phenotype. These M2-exo mediated suppression of specific miRNAs releases inhibition of the epigenetic regulator MeCP2, which exhibits a functional role in bone remodeling distinct from its established role in the nervous system. While in Rett syndrome MeCP2 represses target gene transcription via CpG island methylation leading to synaptic deficits [69], and impaired MeCP2-TCF20 complex formation contributes to neurodevelopmental disorders [24], under mechanical force, M2-derived exosomes (M2-exos) deliver MeCP2 to periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs), facilitating the formation of a MeCP2-TCF20 complex. This complex significantly reduces HDAC1 activity through an epigenetic derepression mechanism, alleviating the HDAC1-mediated blockade of β-catenin nuclear translocation and consequently activating the canonical Wnt pathway to promote bone formation. Three lines of experimental evidence support this mechanism: 1. siRNA-mediated TCF20 silencing increases HDAC1 protein expression. 2. Pharmacological HDAC1 inhibition using trichostatin A (TSA) markedly elevates osteogenic marker expression (RUNX2, ALP, BMP, Col1). 3. Conversely, putative HDAC1 activation by theophylline significantly suppresses expression of these osteogenic markers.

Based on this mechanism, we propose a novel therapeutic paradigm exploiting the bidirectional modulation of the immune exosome-epigenetic axis to overcome limitations of existing treatments. Unlike the systemic bone metabolism interference caused by TNF-α inhibitors (e.g., Y2641) [70] or RANKL antagonists (e.g., denosumab) [71], local M2-exos delivery offers targeted activation of the tension-side MeCP2-Wnt pathway. Significantly, the capacity for bidirectional HDAC1 regulation (TSA promoting bone formation; theophylline inhibiting bone formation) provides a potential strategy for dynamically controlling tooth movement velocity.

This study has several limitations: (1) The precise DNA binding sites and pattern of MeCP2 under mechanical stimulation require further definition. (2) Collagen density barriers and biofluid dynamics within periodontal tissue may reduce the efficiency of M2-exos delivery. (3) Achieving precise rate control necessitates optimized spatiotemporal coordination of exosome dosing and HDAC1 modulator application.

Despite these limitations, the identified mechanism provides a foundation for investigating neuro-osseous crosstalk. Critically, we establish that mechanical force-induced M2-exos transfer MeCP2 to locally enhance the formation of the MeCP2-TCF20 complex in PDLSCs, thereby activating osteogenic differentiation and accelerating tooth movement in mice. Key questions for future investigation include: 1. Whether exosomes derived from pressure-side M1 macrophages can induce the formation of the MeCP2-TCF20 complex in M0 macrophages. 2. The role of the HDAC1-Wnt axis in facilitating osteoclast differentiation.

Conclusion

Orthodontic bone remodeling is a complex biological process that involves the dynamic interplay of mechanical signals, immune networks, and epigenetic regulation. Research indicates that mechanical stimuli regulate the polarization state of macrophages, promoting a dynamic balance between M1 and M2 macrophages, which in turn affects the bidirectional orthodontic remodeling of bone through bone resorption and formation. In the compression side, bone resorption is primarily initiated by inflammatory responses mediated by M1 macrophages, while in the tension side, bone formation is promoted by growth factors secreted by M2 macrophages. M2-exo play a crucial role in orthodontic bone remodeling, which enhance the osteogenic differentiation capacity of PDLSCs by carrying specific molecular information, thereby facilitating the process of orthodontic bone remodeling. MeCP2, as a key epigenetic regulatory factor, plays an important role in orthodontic bone remodeling, with its expression levels exhibiting dynamic changes in both pressure and tension zones, thereby regulating the osteogenic differentiation capacity of PDLSCs. M2-exo can significantly enhance the expression of MeCP2, promoting the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs. The MeCP2-TCF20 complex maintains the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway by inhibiting the activity of HDAC1, further regulating the bone remodeling process. These findings reveal a mechanical-immune-epigenetic regulatory network, providing new insights into the dynamic balance of orthodontic bone remodeling. Additionally, they lay the foundation for developing therapeutic strategies targeting HDAC1 inhibition and stabilizing the MeCP2-TCF20 complex.
