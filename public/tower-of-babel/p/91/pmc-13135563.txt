Clinical Outcomes of a Tech-enabled Perinatal Collaborative Care Program to Treat Depression and Anxiety
Authors: Katherine Steele, Martha Zimmermann, Tiffany A Moore Simas, Nancy Byatt, Roshni Koli, Melissa Sherman, Jessica Gaulton, Clare McNutt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate a tech-enabled collaborative care program in an obstetrics and gynecology (OB/GYN) practice for the treatment of perinatal depression and anxiety symptoms.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort chart review from the electronic medical records of N = 185 patients enrolled in a pilot perinatal collaborative care program delivered by a behavioral health company, Family Well Health, in partnership with a large OB/GYN practice in Massachusetes (USA). FamilyWell’s collaborative care model (CoCM) provides telehealth sessions with a certified perinatal behavioral health coach or a licensed therapist, medication management, and implementation assistance to enhance depression screening, assessment, and treatment of perinatal mood and anxiety disorders (PMADs). English-speaking adults aged 18 years who screened positive for depression (Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale [EPDS] score 10) by their OB/GYN clinician were referred to the FamilyWell program. Enrolled patients received weekly or biweekly telehealth sessions with a certified coach or licensed therapist and completed monthly electronic symptom assessments for anxiety (Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale [GAD-7]) and depression (Patient Health Questionnaire 9-item scale [PHQ-9]). Assessment scores were monitored by a behavioral care manager. We evaluated three clinical outcomes including change in GAD-7 and PHQ-9 scores, treatment response (50% or greater decrease in GAD-7 or PHQ-9 scores), and symptom remission (follow-up PHQ-9 or GAD-7 score < 5).ResultsOver 80% of referred patients enrolled in CoCM and more than 70% completed their intake session within one week. At baseline, 86% had a positive GAD-7 or PHQ-9 score (GAD-7 or PHQ-9 10 indicating symptoms of moderate, moderate-severe, or severe depression or anxiety). Patients received services from a certified coach (77%) or therapist (23%). PHQ-9 scores decreased by 1.3 points per month (95% CI [− 1.5, − 1.2], p < .001) and GAD-7 scores decreased by 1.3 points per month (95% CI [− 1.5, − 1.2], p < .001). Forty-seven percent of patients (n = 33) demonstrated depression treatment response and 52% (n = 45) demonstrated anxiety treatment response. In addition, 30% of patients (n = 26) experienced anxiety symptom remission in a mean time of 3.2 months and 31% (n = 22) experienced depression symptom remission in a mean time of 3.5 months. Patients received a mean of 8 sessions with a therapist and 9 sessions with a coach before experiencing symptom remission.ConclusionThese data suggest that implementing a tech-enabled, coach-driven perinatal collaborative care program is associated with decreases in depression and anxiety symptoms. Symptom remission occurred within eight sessions with a therapist or nine sessions with a coach. Unlike other psychiatric collaborative care programs described in the literature, this program uniquely addresses the behavioral health workforce shortages by integrating certified coaches as a primary intervention and utilizing telehealth- and text-based support to enable scale. Our results suggest that this integrated behavioral health approach can be implemented outside the research setting.Keywords: Collaborative care model, Perinatal depression, Perinatal anxiety, OB/GYN, Tech-enabled interventions

Introduction

Mental health conditions and substance use disorders (SUD) are the leading cause of pregnancy-related deaths in the United States, accounting for 23% of perinatal fatalities and contributing to preventable maternal mortality (Clarke et al., 2023). These effects are disproportionate in under-resourced communities, with 63% of pregnancy-related mental health deaths occurring in the Medicaid population (Clarke et al., 2023; Trost et al., 2021). One study examining mental health treatment utilization found that 80% of pregnant or postpartum individuals lacked access to mental health treatment (Dagher et al., 2021). If left untreated, mental health conditions can lead to harmful outcomes for both women and infants such as preeclampsia, preterm birth, low birthweight, and sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS), among others (Howard & Khalifeh, 2020). Barriers to mental health care that contribute to high morbidity rates globally include community-level stigma, environmental challenges such as poverty, lack of geographic access, and insufficient investment in mental health infrastructure (Byatt et al., 2012).

The Collaborative Care Model (CoCM) is an evidence-based, data-driven, and patient-centered approach for integrated behavioral health treatment that has been shown to improve clinical outcomes, lower costs, and reduce health disparities (Archer et al., 2012). Included in the five pillars of CoCM is measurement-based care (MBC), which incorporates validated screening assessments such as the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) for depression and the General Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) to guide clinical decision-making (Scott & Lewis, 2015). Currently, CoCM is an evidence-based integrated behavioral health model, with over 100 randomized control trials demonstrating its efficacy in improving the quality of care in diverse settings, diagnoses, and populations (Menear et al., 2022). Compared to usual care, CoCM has been found to improve the detection, treatment, and relapse prevention of depression and anxiety in primary care, pediatrics, and obstetric settings and is more cost-effective than usual care (Asarnow et al., 2005; Glied et al., 2010; Grote et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2017; Moriarty et al., 2020)..Recent studies also report the model’s effectiveness and scalability in the primary care setting, demonstrating the successful implementation of a CoCM approach outside controlled research settings. One study of CoCM implementation in 135 primary care clinics across nine states found wide variability in clinical outcomes, however, suggesting that the level of implementation support is also an important factor that influences the effectiveness and scalability of CoCM (Unützer et al., 2020). Therefore, ensuring robust implementation support in OB/GYN settings is crucial to achieving the scale required to address the unique needs of perinatal patients.

OB/GYN clinicians play a critical role in the identification, assessment, and treatment of depression and anxiety during pregnancy and the first postpartum year due to their partnership with patients and the frequency of clinical contact (Taouk et al., 2018). Importantly, patients are nearly four times more likely to follow up with mental health treatment offered at their OB/GYN clinic compared with being referred elsewhere (Melville et al., 2014; Poleshuck & Woods, 2014). Furthermore, during pregnancy and postpartum, CoCM has been shown to increase screening rates for perinatal depression (Miller et al., 2021) and decrease depression symptoms by more than 50% in 12 months compared to usual OB/GYN care (Grote et al., 2015). CoCM has also been shown to increase depression remission, reduce PTSD severity, and increase treatment adherence among pregnant or postpartum individuals from groups that have been socially and economically marginalized (Grote et al., 2015). While randomized controlled trials have supported the effectiveness of CoCM in the academic OB/GYN setting (Moore Simas et al., 2018), it remains unclear whether these results are generalizable beyond controlled research environments. This study explores the implementation of a novel tech-enabled, coach-driven perinatal CoCM program delivered by FamilyWell Health, a start-up company, in collaboration with a large OB/GYN practice in Massachusetes, USA. Specifically, we conducted a real-world implementation evaluation and cohort case study to investigate the extent to which depression and anxiety symptoms improve following the implementation of FamilyWell Health’s perinatal CoCM program in this non-research setting.

Methods

We included data from 185 pregnant or postpartum adults who received FamilyWell’s perinatal CoCM treatment through an OB/GYN practice and who had depression and anxiety outcomes tracked in the Care Management Tracking System (University of Washington AIMS Center, 2019) over eight months (July 2023–March 2024). For this pilot, FamilyWell partnered with a suburban private practice in a hospital system without established perinatal behavioral health providers. The OB/GYN practice contracted with FamilyWell Health to provide implementation support (training, technical assistance, and external facilitation) and deliver perinatal CoCM services to patients. Insurance was billed for CoCM services provided using Collaborative Care Model codes. FamilyWell’s Behavioral Care Manager used the Care Management Tracking System to record patient contacts and track Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9; Kroenke et al., 2001) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 scale (GAD-7; Spitzer et al., 2006) scores over time. Our analyses included all English-speaking patients 18 years of age or older who were enrolled in FamilyWell’s CoCM program and had at least two documented PHQ-9 or GAD-7 scores—one at intake and one or more within the following eight months. All data were deidentified and obtained with permission from FamilyWell Health and the participating practice. The institutional review board at the University of Texas at Austin, USA approved the study.

Collaborative Care Intervention

FamilyWell Health is a tech-enabled mental health company that partners with OB/GYN practices to embed a comprehensive perinatal mental health program directly into clinics. FamilyWell’s framework leverages the evidence-based psychiatric collaborative care model (CoCM) widely implemented in primary care. Their program includes hybrid in-person and tele-mental health services delivered by a care team of psychiatric consultants, licensed therapists, and certified coaches who engage with patients at multiple touch points (see Fig. 1). Any patient receiving care from a partnering OB/GYN practice with mental health concerns, covered by either a Commercial or Medicaid insurance plan, is eligible for services.

FamilyWell CoCM clinical team structure. Behavioral health team members (highlighted in orange) include the Behavioral Care Manager, Psychiatric Provider, Licensed Therapist, and Certified Perinatal Behavioral Health Coaches

FamilyWell’s program enhances the traditional CoCM model in two important ways. First, it incorporates a HIPAA-compliant digital platform that uses SMS-based communication and virtual sessions to support scalable delivery of care. Second, the program integrates Certified Perinatal Behavioral Health Coaches. Coaches were recruited from individuals with lived experience of childbirth, parenting, or perinatal mood disorders and at least 2 years of professional experience supporting perinatal families, most commonly as practicing birth workers (e.g., doulas, lactation consultants, nurses, and midwives). Coach certification included 15 hours of training on perinatal behavioral health and cognitive behavioral coaching and 10 hours of live mentoring. Coaches received ongoing monthly supervision and were embedded within a multidisciplinary care team that included a Behavioral Care Manager, Psychiatric Provider (NP or MD), and a Licensed Therapist. Coach-participant interactions consisted of structured, skills-based coaching sessions focused on psychoeducation, goal setting, and supportive strategies delivered via telehealth, with clear protocols for escalation to licensed clinical care when higher-risk needs were identified.

Program Recruitment and Enrollment

Clinicians at the partnering OB/GYN practice screened for perinatal depression using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) during the first and third trimesters of pregnancy, and again at the six-week postpartum visit. English-speaking patients with an EPDS score 10 were referred to the FamilyWell program for evaluation and treatment. Enrollment required that patients have access to a mobile device with SMS capabilities or a device that supports Zoom for virtual provider sessions. Patients actively enrolled in intensive mental health treatment (e.g., intensive outpatient programs, inpatient psychiatric care) or those presenting with suicidal ideation or psychosis at the time of referral were excluded and referred to a higher level of care. Upon confirming eligibility, the Behavioral Care Manager contacted patients via FamilyWell’s HIPAA-secure digital platform (phone or text) to offer program enrollment and obtain verbal and written consent for behavioral health and medical treatment.

Program Intake and Treatment

Upon enrollment, patients were introduced to their FamilyWell care team and completed an intake session with a licensed therapist specializing in perinatal mental health. During intake, the therapist screened patients for bipolar disorder and other perinatal mood disorders, reviewed the screening results, and conducted follow-up assessments as necessary to make a diagnosis. Based on specific criteria including diagnosis, case complexity, and severity of symptoms, the therapist decided on a care pathway.

Following intake, patients received weekly or biweekly telehealth sessions with a certified coach or a licensed therapist. Patients with higher acuity symptoms were paired with a therapist and those with lower acuity were paired with a certified coach. Patients completed, at a minimum, monthly PHQ-9 and GAD-7 screenings via the Care Management Tracking System. Additional individualized screenings were administered based on the patient’s history, symptoms, and diagnosis. PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores were closely monitored by the patient’s care team to track progress and guide clinical decision making.

All patients were considered for discussion at regular multi-disciplinary case review meetings during which the OB/GYN clinician and FamilyWell behavioral health team discussed adjustments to patient treatment plans when escalation or de-escalation in care was indicated. When psychoactive medication such as an antidepressant was initiated, the prescriber was typically the OB/GYN clinician. For medication outside the OB/GYN clinician’s scope of practice, particularly for patients with severe or complex symptoms, one of FamilyWell’s licensed psychiatric providers, either a Medical Doctor (MD) or Nurse Practitioner (NP), assumed responsibility as the prescriber. If a clinical concern for self-harm arose or screening indicated a higher risk of suicidal ideation (SI), the team would determine the appropriate next steps and referrals. While none of the patients in this cohort developed active SI, if an imminent safety concern had occurred, FamilyWell’s suicide escalation protocol would have been activated, and the team would have worked closely with the OB/GYN clinician to transfer the patient to the appropriate local crisis unit, inpatient unit, or emergency department.

When PHQ-9 or GAD-7 scores suggested a patient met clinical criteria for symptom remission, patients transitioned to the maintenance or relapse prevention phase of the program. During this phase, patients typically received monthly coaching sessions and screenings. Any patients displaying a relapse in symptoms re-entered the active phase and were discussed in case review with the OB/GYN clinicians and FamilyWell’s behavioral health team.

Measures and Outcomes

Using data from validated tools available in the Care Management Tracking System, we examined patient characteristics and three commonly used clinical outcome metrics based on PHQ-9 and GAD-7 symptom scores completed at program intake and eight months of follow-up assessments. We assessed clinical outcome data for patients with positive symptom assessment scores at baseline, indicating moderate, moderate-severe, or severe symptoms of depression (PHQ-9 10; Kroenke et al., 2001) or anxiety (GAD-7 10; Spitzer et al., 2006). Among patients with symptom scores 10, indicating at least moderate symptom severity, we also evaluated treatment response and remission outcomes. Treatment response was defined as a 50% decrease in a PHQ-9 or GAD-7 symptom score at any point during the program, a threshold that has been widely used as an indicator of clinically meaningful improvement in randomized trials of depression and anxiety treatment (Bandelow, 2006; Coley et al., 2020; Nierenberg & DeCecco, 2001). Depression remission was defined as a PHQ-9 symptom score < 5 on any assessment following baseline (Kroenke et al., 2001), and anxiety remission was defined as a GAD-7 symptom score < 5 on any assessment following baseline (Spitzer et al., 2006). These thresholds reflect validated interpretive conventions and facilitate comparability with prior clinical trials and collaborative care evaluations (Grote et al., 2015; Melville et al., 2014; Miller et al., 2021).

Statistical Analyses

We used linear mixed-effect modeling to examine within-person change in monthly follow-up GAD-7 and PHQ-9 symptom scores. We included time in months as a fixed effect and patient as a random effect to test the null hypothesis that there would be no change in GAD-7 or PHQ-9 scores over time. We conducted all analyses in RStudio using the nlme v. 2.1–142 package (Pinheiro et al., 2021). We summarized patient-level depression and anxiety outcomes as mean (M) and standard deviation (SD).

Results

Enrollment and Patient Characteristics

Through universal screening practices by the OB/GYN clinicians, 280 patients were referred to FamilyWell’s CoCM program. Of those referred, 90.7% (n = 254) were eligible, 81.0% (n = 205) enrolled, and 73.1% (n = 185) completed their initial intake session (Table 1). Patients referred during pregnancy were a mean of 21 weeks gestational age (GA) and those referred postpartum were a mean of eight weeks postpartum. Because the pilot duration was eight months, full program completion (defined as 12 months postpartum) could not be assessed. However, interim engagement was evaluated using available follow-up data and retention through the latest available assessment point (month seven check-in).

Symptom Change and Treatment Response

Mixed-effects models assessing the main effect of time on clinical outcomes (N = 185) revealed that symptom assessment scores decreased over time, an estimated 0.84 points per month for PHQ-9 symptom scores (95% CI[− 0.9, − 0.8], p < .001) and 0.94 points per month for GAD-7 symptom scores (95% CI[− 1.0, − 0.9], p < .001). Among patients with positive baseline symptom scores for depression (PHQ-9 scores 10; n = 70) or anxiety (GAD-7 scores 10; n = 86), PHQ-9 symptom scores decreased by 1.3 points monthly (95% CI[− 1.5, − 1.2], p < .001) and GAD-7 symptom scores decreased by 1.3 points monthly (95% CI[− 1.5, − 1.2], p < .001). Furthermore, 47% demonstrated anxiety treatment response ( 50% decrease in GAD-7 score at any time point following baseline; see Table 3).

Symptom Remission

Among patients with positive baseline scores for depression or anxiety, 31% experienced depression symptom remission (PHQ-9 score < 5 at any time point following baseline), and 30% experienced anxiety symptom remission (GAD-7 score < 5 at any time point following baseline) at least once during the pilot (see Table 3). Patients experienced depression symptom remission in a mean time of 3.5 months (SD = 1.4) and anxiety symptom remission in a mean time of 3.2 months (SD = 1.4). It took a mean length of eight sessions with a therapist and nine sessions with a coach to experience remission.

Discussion

Research trials have demonstrated that CoCM improves clinical outcomes, reduces costs, and mitigates health disparities in perinatal populations (Grote et al., 2015, 2017; Huang et al., 2017; Moore Simas et al., 2018). To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to show that implementing a tech-enabled, coach-driven perinatal CoCM program improves clinical outcomes for pregnant and postpartum individuals in an OB/GYN practice, outside of a clinical trial setting. The results indicate that embedding a perinatal CoCM program directly within an OB/GYN practice is associated with improved clinical outcomes for pregnant and postpartum patients with moderate to severe depression and anxiety. We observed significant reductions in GAD-7 and PHQ-9 symptom scores, along with high rates of treatment response and symptom remission within 3.5 months of program enrollment. Additionally, there were strong rates of referral enrollment and intake, with over 80% of patients enrolled and more than 70% completing their intake session within one week. It is important to note that these data were not collected as part of a research trial but were retrospectively extracted from FamilyWell’s Care Management Tracking System, reflecting real-world outcomes and impact.

Although this study lacked a control group, engagement and treatment initiation rates appear favorable compared with prior reports from the literature ranging from 12% to 98% for integrated care interventions (Moore Simas et al., 2018). Treatment initiation following referral for perinatal depression has been reported as low as 15% in usual care without targeted interventions (Meltzer-Brody, 2016) whereas systems-level collaborative care interventions have demonstrated higher initiation rates of 52% and 43% in the PRISM trial (Byatt et al., 2024). Observational treatment cascade analyses have also found remission rates as low as 4.8% during pregnancy and 3.2% postpartum (Meltzer-Brody, 2016). While cross-study comparisons should be interpreted with caution due to methodological differences, these benchmarks suggest that outcomes observed in this pilot fall within or above ranges previously reported in integrated or usual-care settings.

In 2023, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) published new clinical guidelines recommending enhanced screening for PMADs. The postpartum period represents a time of sustained vulnerability for adverse maternal mental health outcomes. Maternal health conditions, including suicide and overdose, are among the leading causes of death for women in the first year following pregnancy (Trost et al., 2024), underscoring the need for affordable, high-quality, and timely access to care beyond the immediate postnatal window. Studies show that 62% of new mothers experience significant depressive symptoms and fewer than 20% access care (Dagher et al., 2021). ACOG now recommends screening for depression and anxiety at the first OB/GYN visit, later in pregnancy, and at every postpartum visit. ACOG also recommends that OB/GYN clinicians screen for bipolar disorder before prescribing antidepressants due to an increased risk of postpartum psychosis in patients with unrecognized bipolar disorder (American College of Obstetricians & Gynecologists, 2023). However, enhanced screening guidelines are of little utility without timely access to behavioral health care providers who can assess and treat patients whose needs exceed the training and capacity of the OB/GYN clinic.

For this pilot, we partnered with an OB/GYN clinic with limited system access to perinatal behavioral health services. Currently, one of the greatest barriers preventing access to care is the mental health provider shortage (Byatt et al., 2012). When OB/GYN clinicians identify a high-risk patient through screening and do not have direct access to embedded perinatal behavioral health services, they typically refer patients to a therapist and other behavioral health providers in the community. However, many therapists and counselors only accept cash payments or have long waitlists (Byatt et al., 2012), making access to timely care unaffordable and unattainable for most patients. Embedding perinatal CoCM in the OB/GYN clinic provided a streamlined pathway for early intervention to treat PMADs and helped ensure that patients received timely access to care (in this study, within one week of referral).

To expand and scale perinatal CoCM programs beyond the academic research setting, it is critically important to address the mental health provider shortage. To meet the growing demand for perinatal mental health support, FamilyWell added certified coaches with specific perinatal training to the traditional psychiatric CoCM care team model to address this shortage. The data from this pilot support that coaches can provide effective care for the perinatal population with PMADs. Additionally, FamilyWell’s model leverages telehealth, texting, and intelligent algorithms embedded in its proprietary digital platform to support scalable operations, including the ability to recruit and staff roles across a nationwide geographic pool.

Although patients experienced significant symptom improvement and treatment response within the first several months of the program, individual factors such as engagement and retention likely influence outcomes (Yang et al., 2024). Observed improvements in patient-reported symptoms are therefore descriptive and should be interpreted within the limitations of a single-arm cohort design. Notably, a small subset of enrollees (n = 20) did not complete the initial intake, and their outcomes were not captured. Future work could examine how longitudinal patterns of patient engagement with the program relate to clinical outcomes.

While patients in this pilot experienced symptom remission at least once during the first eight-months of the program, longer term sustainability remains unknown. One prior study evaluating outcomes from another collaborative care intervention for perinatal depression observed sustained symptom improvements up to 18 months post-baseline, suggesting potential for durable benefits (e.g., Grote et al., 2015). Future work could explore sustainability by tracking re-admission to the program as a proxy for relapse, as well as continued engagement with program components, to better understand which factors support lasting symptom remission and whether the intervention is equally effective across patients from diverse geographic settings. FamilyWell’s program is designed to be inclusive of both commercially insured and publicly insured patient populations through a billing model utilizing collaborative care billing codes, reducing barriers to care for higher-risk groups such as those insured by Medicaid. In the studied OB/GYN practice, 30% of participants received Medicaid and 70% were privately insured. However, a limitation of the pilot is the lack of data on race, ethnicity, and income, which constrains the generalizability of the results to certain populations that may be at higher risk for developing perinatal mood and anxiety disorders. The studied practice represented a metropolitan suburb and may not be generalizable to more urban or rural areas. Additionally, participants were required to be English-speaking and have access to a mobile device with SMS and Zoom capabilities. In practice, all participants reported having a phone, and while some were limited to Wi-Fi, they were still able to engage with the intervention. Public insurance programs in the state Insurance Programs In Massachusetts also provide subsidized cell phones for individuals above 135% of the poverty line, which likely mitigates some access barriers. Nonetheless, it is possible that individuals with the greatest digital or language access challenges were underrepresented.

Historically, the mental health system has struggled to meet the needs of racially and ethnically diverse populations, contributing to disparities in service utilization and outcomes (Heun-Johnson et al., 2021). Individuals with limited English proficiency, for instance, experience lower utilization rates and face more barriers to accessing care (Al Shamsi et al., 2020). The findings from this study reflect FamilyWell’s program delivery exclusively to English-speaking birthing people and may not capture the experiences of non-English speaking populations who encounter compounded structural barriers to mental health services. Future research should explicitly examine whether this model addresses health equity across linguistically and culturally diverse groups. In addition, cost is a critical determinant of access and scalability for perinatal mental health interventions. While this pilot was not designed to evaluate program costs, future studies should assess cost and cost-effectiveness of reimbursable CoCM services embedded in OB/GYN practices, relative to standard care and alternative care models to better inform equitable and sustainable implementation. Despite these limitations, this study demonstrates significant improvements in depression and anxiety symptoms among patients enrolled in a perinatal CoCM program implemented within an OB/GYN clinic through partnership with an external mental health provider. These findings offer insight into how a tech-enabled, coach-driven model may be scaled beyond clinical trials to reach pregnant and postpartum adults receiving care in a real-world OB/GYN clinic. In the absence of easily accessible and timely perinatal mental health services, this evaluation reflects conditions in which the practical alternative to intervention is often delayed care or no care at all. Additionally, partnering with an external mental health provider may help address workforce and capacity constraints by enabling OB-GYN practices to offer perinatal mental health care without establishing in-house behavioral health programs. Future research is needed to further evaluate and refine this tech-enabled, coach-driven approach to perinatal CoCM and to assess its generalizability across diverse healthcare settings and patient populations, particularly those in under-resourced communities.
