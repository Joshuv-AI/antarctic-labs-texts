Are Social Media Platforms an Effective Forum for Engaging Early‐Onset Cancer Survivors? Insights From X (Formerly Twitter)
Authors: Abby Dawson, Savio George Barreto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRates of early‐onset pancreatic cancer (EOPC) and early‐onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC) are rising globally. Although research and clinical guidelines have sought to address the psychosocial and supportive care needs of people with colorectal and pancreatic cancers, these efforts have focused largely on older adults, meaning the experiences of individuals with EOPC and EOCRC may be poorly understood. Social media offers a source of real‐time, first‐hand narratives that may help address this gap. This study analyzed posts on X (formerly Twitter) to explore the lived experiences and needs of people with EOPC and EOCRC to inform more age‐appropriate, person‐centered care.MethodsPosts containing keywords relating to EOPC and EOCRC, shared between 2023 and 2025, were extracted. Of 7441 posts retrieved, approximately 40% were categorized automatically using predefined keywords, with the remainder manually coded. Automated coding demonstrated substantial agreement with manual coding (Cohen's κ = 0.717).ResultsMost posts related to “academic and clinical” content (66.1%) or “medical advice and discussion” (13.5%). Only 11 posts (0.2%) described patient experiences, all relating to EOCRC, with none reflecting lived experiences of EOPC. These posts highlighted rising EOCRC incidence, late‐stage diagnoses, advocacy for screening, survivorship themes, and peer support.ConclusionX posts by people with early‐onset cancers were scarce, whereas clinician‐ and researcher‐ posts dominated. This imbalance on X suggests a one‐directional, paternalistic model of healthcare communication on this platform that contrasts with person‐centered principles. Future research should explore alternative platforms and digital strategies to better engage younger cancer populations and support more collaborative communication.Keywords: communication, quality, survivorship

Introduction

Early‐onset pancreatic cancer (EOPC) and early‐onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC) refer to malignancies arising from the pancreas, colon, and/or rectum that are diagnosed in individuals under the age of 50 [1]. Both cancers have shown a gradual but concerning rise over the past 30 years [2, 3]. The global incidence of EOPC increased from 0.64 per 100,000 people in 1990 to 0.94 per 100,000 in 2019 [2], and EOCRC incidence has risen from 3.5 to 5.7 per 100,000 people during the same period [3]. Furthermore, between 2013 and 2017, Australia had the highest age‐standardized incidence of EOCRC among 50 countries included in the World Health Organization International Agency for Research on Cancer's “Cancer Incidence in Five Continents Plus” database [4]. The rising incidence and high mortality trends of EOPC and EOCRC underscore the urgent need for targeted approaches to care for this younger population. The National Pancreatic Cancer Roadmap [5], an initiative by the Australian Government to improve the outcomes and survival of Australians diagnosed with pancreatic cancer, and the Cancer Council Australia's clinical practice guidelines, endorsed by the National Health and Medical Research Council [6], for the management of people living with colorectal cancer, are largely based on studies of individuals over the age of 50, and therefore may not accurately reflect the experiences and needs of those with EOPC and EOCRC.

In recent years, the experiences of individuals living with EOCRC have been increasingly explored through qualitative research, offering deeper insight into their unique challenges and perspectives [7, 8]; however, no such studies have been published regarding the experience or needs of individuals living with EOPC. The broader literature does contain studies exploring the supportive care needs of individuals with pancreatic cancer across all age groups; however, extrapolating these findings to those living with EOPC may overlook age‐specific challenges. This can be seen when comparing studies that explored the needs of people with EOCRC to those of individuals with colorectal cancer across all age groups. The needs which are unique to people living with EOCRC are prompt investigation and diagnosis of symptoms, fertility and sexual health support, support to prevent feelings of isolation, a need for peer connection and advocacy, and social media engagement [7, 8].

Social media has proven to be a real‐time source of first‐hand data, which can be used to better understand the patient experience in the healthcare context [9]. Because these platforms capture naturally occurring, spontaneous narratives, content analysis is well‐suited to systematically interpret the large volume of unstructured data, by identifying recurring categories or themes of content [9]. Given the current lack of published data on the needs of people living with EOPC and EOCRC, social media may offer a valuable and underutilized resource for capturing experiences in this population. X is a platform ideal for examining discourse around these early‐onset cancers, given its user demographics [10] and established role in health‐related communication. The age profile of X [11] users overlaps with populations affected by early‐onset cancers [10], while the platform enables rapid, public sharing of research, advice, and personal experiences.

This retrospective, observational study sought to analyze posts on X [11] to explore the experiences and needs of individuals living with EOPC and EOCRC, in order to guide more age‐specific care to this patient group. By also analyzing posts related to EOCRC, they can be compared to existing published literature to contextualize and deepen the insights gained from X posts about EOPC.

Methods

Data Collection and Coding Framework

Data were extracted from X [11] using the TrackMyHashtag [12] software. All identified posts were retrieved via a global search of X and analyzed without stratification by geographic location. X posts containing the phrases “early‐onset pancreatic cancer” or “young onset pancreatic cancer” or “early‐onset colorectal cancer” or “young‐onset colorectal cancer” posted between January 1, 2023, and January 1, 2025 were extracted. This time period was chosen to capture a contemporary and recent picture of the social media landscape following the acquisition of Twitter (now X), reflecting current platform structures and user engagement. Content categories for the content analysis were predefined based on a pilot manual content analysis in which 300 unique posts were analyzed and allocated into relevant categories. Automated Content Analysis

All extracted posts were analyzed using an automated content analysis approach. The coding framework was developed based on recurring keywords identified within each content category during the initial manual pilot analysis. These keywords were incorporated into a Python [13] script (run via Anaconda [14]), which automatically assigned posts to their respective categories. Posts containing keywords from multiple categories were classified based on the priority order outlined in Table 1.

Manual Coding and Validation of Automated Content Analysis

To assess the validity of the automated coding, up to 100 unique posts from each category (or all posts if fewer than 100 were available) were manually reviewed and coded for comparison. Cohen's κ was used to quantify the level of agreement between the manual and automated coding. Furthermore, all posts within the “patient experience” category were manually coded during the validity check. Any discrepancies between the automated and manual classifications were corrected to match the manual coding, as this is considered the gold standard in content analysis, and the patient experience category was the primary focus of the investigation. Posts that did not include any predefined keywords were assigned to an “uncategorized” category. The remaining uncategorized X posts were manually analyzed by a single investigator.

Exploring the Needs of Individuals Living With Early‐Onset Colorectal and Pancreatic Cancers

To explore the lived experiences and unique needs of people living with EOPC and EOCRC, posts from the patient experience category were identified and analyzed from the broader dataset of X posts. The content of these posts was reviewed to ascertain recurring themes.

Results

A total of 7441 X posts were extracted. Using the automated content analysis, approximately 2982 posts were successfully categorized, while the remaining 4459 posts were assigned to the “uncategorized” category. The automated coding was validated by manually coding 100 unique posts from each category (or all unique posts if fewer than 100 were available) and comparing the results using Cohen's κ coefficient. Cohen's κ coefficient for the level of agreement between the automated code and the manual coder was 0.717, indicating substantial agreement.

The remaining 4459 posts classified as uncategorized were subsequently analyzed manually using the same predefined categories and definitions applied in the automated content analysis. These manually coded posts were then combined with those already classified by the automated approach to generate the final category totals. The most frequent post category was academic and clinical, accounting for approximately 66% of the total sample. The second most common category, medical advice and discussion, comprised around 13.5% of posts. In contrast, posts classified as patient experience represented only 0.2% of the dataset, with the remaining 20.2% of posts categorized as either carer perspective, grief, awareness, and advocacy, fundraising, media promotion and discussion, and posts included in error. A total of 11 posts detailing experiences of people living with EOPC and EOCRC were identified within the dataset. Seven were original posts, and the remaining four were re‐posts of two of these originals. Although there is no validated method for determining whether X accounts are human or automated, many accounts contributing patient experience posts displayed features associated with human‐operated users, including personalized biographies, irregular posting patterns, and engagement in replies and discussions, rather than purely repetitive automated posting. Many posts acknowledged the increasing rates of EOCRC, with some expressing frustration that the issue has not previously received sufficient public attention. A recurring focus across the posts was raising awareness of EOCRC, often in connection with Colorectal Cancer Awareness Month. Two posts included links to educational resources, blog posts, or updated screening guidelines. One post speculated that “the jab,” likely referring to a vaccine, may be contributing to the rising incidence of EOCRC. Four posts mentioned the stage at which the authors were diagnosed: one at Stage 2, one at Stage 3, one at Stage 4, and one described as “late‐stage.” Advocacy for early detection and screening was a common theme, with one post explicitly urging readers to “get a colonoscopy.” Several posts referenced treatment experiences, including surgery and chemotherapy, while one expressed appreciation for an EOCRC peer group at their treatment center. Themes of hope and survivorship were also prominent, with some posts celebrating years of being cancer‐free and offering encouragement to others. Discussion

This study aimed to extract and qualitatively analyze data from the social media platform X [11] to better understand and provide recommendations addressing the unique needs of individuals living with EOPC and EOCRC. X posts discussing EOPC and EOCRC from January 1, 2023 to January 1, 2025 were extracted using keywords. A total of 7441 posts were extracted from X [11], and a content analysis was performed. Only 11 posts (0.2%) were categorized as patient experiences, while the majority focused on academic and clinical content (66.1%) and medical advice and discussion (13.5%).

Content analysis proved to be an effective method of quantifying the types of content on X, whilst also providing more in‐depth and contextual analysis of these types of content. However, the research process also highlighted ongoing challenges in applying content analysis to social media contexts. The brevity of posts, absence of user demographic information, and reliance on a manual coder's single subjective interpretation of the vast and context‐dependent lexicon of the internet introduce a risk of misclassification and limit the interpretive accuracy and generalizability of this method.

Using keywords identified during pilot analyses, a Python [13] script was developed to automatically assign X posts to predefined content categories. Of the 7441 X posts, 2982 (approximately 40%) were categorized based on these keywords, indicating that a substantial proportion of posts did not contain any of the defined terms. To capture a larger proportion of posts in future automated analyses, additional keywords could be incorporated into the code; however, this may compromise the specificity of category assignment and, hence, the precision of the algorithm. In this study, specificity and precision were prioritized over recall and sensitivity due to the anticipated large volume of data detailing the experience of EOPC and EOCRC. Inter‐coder reliability between the automated and manual coding approaches was assessed using Cohen's κ coefficient. The calculated value of 0.71 indicated substantial agreement between the two coding techniques [15, 16].

Analysis of posts detailing experiences of young‐onset cancer survivors revealed experiences exclusively from individuals with EOCRC, with no posts from survivors of EOPC. These posts contained similar sentiments to those identified in the existing literature, highlighting the EOCRC survivor's needs for reducing isolation, fostering peer connection and advocacy, and engaging in online support groups [7, 8]. Other needs reported in the literature for this population, such as timely diagnosis, support with career, relationships, family planning, fertility, and sexual health [7, 8], were not expressed in this data set, and the absence of EOPC experiences reflects existing gaps in the literature.

The limited number of X posts relating to the experiences of people with EOPC and EOCRC identified in this study suggests that survivors of these cancers may not be actively engaging with X [11] as a platform to share their personal experiences. The reasons for this are likely multifactorial. Based on the findings of this study, most posts related to EOPC and EOCRC originate from academics and clinicians. People living with EOPC and EOCRC may therefore use X [11] primarily as passive observers rather than active contributors, possibly deterred by the dominance of professional and academic‐focused content. Individuals may also feel uncomfortable or embarrassed sharing such personal experiences online. However, current literature suggests that young people often use social media to discuss personal issues [17, 18, 19], although the public nature of X may limit this type of discourse. Furthermore, a study analyzing X [11] and Facebook [20] in public health messaging found Facebook posts by political figures and experts received higher engagement, while X more frequently featured infographics, surveillance data, and hyperlinks [21]. Posts on policy discussions generated greater engagement on X [21]. The relatively low engagement on X from those with EOPC and EOCRC likely reflects its professional focus and limited capacity for personal storytelling compared with more multimedia‐driven platforms.

This study has revealed an important communication gap between early‐onset cancer survivors and public health education efforts. This predominantly one‐directional, paternalistic style of communication does not align with the needs of young‐onset cancer survivors described in the existing literature, which emphasize collaboration, peer connection, person‐centered communication, and shared decision making [7, 8, 22, 23]. Addressing this discrepancy is essential to ensure that public health messaging and support initiatives more effectively meet the needs of this unique group. It is important to consider whether social media is sufficiently developed to support the collaboration between healthcare professionals and people living with early‐onset cancer. Based on the results reported in this study, social media, and more specifically X [11], may not yet be advanced enough to support this discourse. Additionally, we were unable to comment on the medical accuracy of the X posts in this data set; future research could employ systematic fact‐checking of posts to better understand the accuracy and reliability of health‐related information on social media.

We acknowledge that this study has limitations. Manual qualitative analysis of all X posts was beyond the scope of this study. However, further examination of non‐patient experience posts would enable assessment of alignment between informational content and lived experience. This represents an important area for future research and may clarify how well online academic discourse reflects patient priorities. Manual content analysis is inherently susceptible to coder bias; however, this risk can be mitigated by involving multiple coders and assessing inter‐coder reliability, which was unable to be completed in this analysis due to resource limitations. Despite the relatively large data set, the smaller number of posts directly relating to experiences of people with early‐onset cancer limits the generalizability of the results. As the dataset included only posts written in English, the findings are representative only of English‐speaking people, and whilst the use of social media as a data source enables the capture of real‐time, first‐hand experiences, it inherently limits representation to individuals who are active on these platforms. The search strategy was limited to early‐onset‐cancer‐specific terms to maximize specificity. However, relevant content not explicitly labelled as early‐onset may not have been captured, and the findings may represent a conservative estimate of early‐onset–related engagement on X. Due to logistical and practical constraints, this study analyzed only one social media platform, X [11]; therefore, the findings may not fully represent the broader social media landscape. The lack of posts relating to the experience of individuals with EOPC and EOCRC may be unique to X [11] as a social media platform, and hence, more research in the area is required to further understand the relationship this group has with public health education through social media. As tools such as AI continue to advance, researchers will have greater capacity to study image‐ and video‐based content, opening up future research opportunities to analyze additional social media platforms such as TikTok [24], Instagram [25], or YouTube [26].

As rates of EOPC and EOCRC continue to rise both internationally and locally, it is critical to understand and address the unique needs of this younger cohort to ensure the delivery of high‐quality, person‐centered care. Our results revealed a significant communication gap between those living with early‐onset cancer and public health education efforts on social media. To bridge this gap, healthcare and public health organizations should prioritize the development of digital communication models that promote real‐time, two‐way engagement with young‐onset cancer survivors, ensuring that social media evolves as a genuine tool for collaboration, education, and support. Future research should incorporate additional social media platforms, particularly those fostering interactive, community‐based dialogue, and refine automated analysis techniques using multi‐coder validation frameworks.
