Effectiveness of hard court slide braking technique and influencing factors in elite college tennis players
Authors: Yutian Ji, Tianhao Gao, Wentao Zhang, Ruichao Zhang, Yifan Wang, Liya Xu, Xinyu Wang, Xu Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of hard court slide braking technique of elite college tennis players and to analyze the factors that influence the use of hard court slide braking technique.MethodsA total of 100 elite tennis players (58 males and 42 females) participated in tests involving hard court hit-to-return and Forcedecks Dual Force Plates System-based physical function. Independent samples t-tests, paired samples t-tests and binary logistic regression were employed in data analysis.ResultsThe use of slide braking technique on hard courts, compared to traditional methods, led to a 10 % reduction in time for short-distance shots and a 7 % reduction for long-distance shots (p < 0.001). Athletes skilled in the hard court slide braking technique completed the movement faster when they employed traditional braking technique than those who are unskilled in the hard court slide braking technique (p < 0.001). Key factors that significantly influenced the use of slide braking technique included years of training, left hip abduction, 10-m sprint speed, braking leg reactive strength index (RSI), ground reaction force on the braking leg and recovery balance time of the braking leg (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThe findings offer new perspectives into tennis-specific physical training, assisting more tennis players in enhancing their athletic skills effectively.Keywords: Tennis, Braking technique, Influencing factors, Performance, Effectiveness

Introduction

As modern tennis techniques continue to evolve, athletes in professional competitions typically perform over 1000 directional changes, with increased emphasis on movement and striking near the baseline.1,2 Consequently, exceptional mobility has become a critical factor for success in professional tennis today.3 Despite extensive research on striking techniques, there remains a comparative lack of systematic research on movement techniques in tennis.4 Studies suggest that tennis movement involves a continuous cycle of acceleration, braking, and re-acceleration,1 and yet physical training programs often focus on acceleration rather than effective deceleration and braking.5

Sliding is a traditional tennis movement technique, frequently used by players to decelerate before striking the ball or after completing a stroke.6 This technique is commonly observed on clay courts, where the surface offers less friction and the ball bounces higher, requiring players to cover more ground. Sliding is primarily employed to decelerate after high-speed movement and to control body direction.7 Ferrauti8 and colleagues tested 12 professional male tennis players on clay courts and found that controlled sliding technique could reduce plantar pressure and allow for quicker deceleration and direction changes.

Playing on hard courts imposes greater time pressure compared to playing on clay courts.9 Research indicates that in men's matches at the Australian Open, players experience time pressure on 44.7 % of rally shots.10 Furthermore, rapid and precise movement on hard courts is a crucial skill for elite players.11 Recent studies of motion capture of 18 professional tennis players emphasize the need for quick braking and striking under high-speed conditions on hard courts.12 Yang Xi et al.13 using accelerometer and GPS data, analyzed the running patterns of four male tennis players in a simulated match, identifying differences in agility, anticipatory skills, and speed transitions. With evolving playing styles, top professionals like Novak Djokovic have increasingly adopted sliding braking technique on hard courts, a practice historically more common on clay courts.14,15 However, it remains uncertain whether the sliding technique on hard courts provides similar deceleration and directional change benefits as it does on clay courts. Pieper et al.6 suggested that, compared to traditional braking methods—such as using small steps or lateral crossover steps after a sprint1 —hard court sliding requires the players to abduct their hips, touch the ground with the braking leg on the same side as the striking hand, adjust posture according to the ball's position, and brake after sliding to a certain degree, while they are rotating their hips to strike.7 Nonetheless, this technique requires substantial physical fitness, and not all players can effectively adopt it.16 Identifying the physical fitness indicators that strongly correlate with hard court sliding technique, determining relevant metrics, and developing specialized training protocols are critical challenges for coaches and researchers to address.

Numerous scholars have investigated the braking capacity of elite athletes, the component of mobility,1,3 which is influenced by a combination of factors.17,18 Developing this capacity requires specialized training and substantial time investment.19 Explosiveness and flexibility are fundamental metrics for assessing braking capacity differences among athletes,20 with balance also playing a critical role in rapid movement and brake.4,21 Furthermore, kinematic experiments by Brandon et al.12 involving male and female professional tennis players have determined that gender and body structure significantly impact athletes' movement patterns and brake selection. Based on these findings, the study seeks to analyze the correlation between various body functions and the hard court slide braking technique. It is hypothesized that the use of the hard court slide braking technique will be influenced by years of training, gender differences, lower extremity explosiveness, flexibility, and balance. As the slide braking technique on clay courts has been shown to be effective in aiding athletes to decelerate and change direction,7,8 the study also seeks to determine whether this technique can help tennis players efficiently strike the ball while in motion. It is also hypothesized that athletes utilizing the hard court slide braking technique for hitting and returning will achieve a higher return rate than those employing the traditional braking technique for hitting and returning. This study may be the first study to examine the hard court slide brake as a key technique. Upon dissecting this technique step by step, the study seeks to provide a cushion for athletes experiencing time pressures on the hard court, aiding them in braking and striking the ball more effectively at high speeds of movement. The study is beneficial for improving athletic performance and competitiveness among tennis players in hard court matches, and it is expected to inspire new ideas for future skill development and personalized physical training in tennis.

Materials and methods

Participants

A total of 100 collegiate tennis athletes were recruited, including 58 males and 42 females. The participants had an average age of 21.4 years (±3.4), a height of 175.8 cm (±8.04), and a weight of 69.3 kg (±11.4). The required sample size was determined a priori using G∗Power 3.1. Based on previous study,22 we assumed an odds ratio (OR) of 2.0 for the primary exposure variable, with a baseline event rate of 30 % in the control group. A two-sided logistic regression analysis with α = 0.05 and power (1−β) = 0.8 required a minimum of 92 participants. To account for potential loss to follow-up, we enrolled 100 participants. All participants are recognized national-level college tennis players (All are professional athletes who meet the technical standards set by the Chinese Tennis Association, and all have either won one of the top two places in national-level tournaments or one of the top two places in provincial-level tournaments), who voluntarily consented to participate in the study. They possess professional sports training experience, with an average training duration of 9.8 years (±3.8). None of the participants had experienced sports injuries, cardiovascular issues, or metabolic diseases in the past six months. All athletes were informed about the purposes of this study, signed an informed consent prior to the actual data collection and were made aware of their right to withdraw from the study at any time. This study received ethical approval from the local university's Ethics Committee (Ethics Approval No. 2024059).

Experimental design

Data collection took place between October 2024 and November 2024. A technical manual was developed for the project to outline testing standards, and technical training was provided for the experimental team and personnel before the commencement of testing. Participants were examined in the local university's biomechanics laboratory facilities and tennis courts. Participants were instructed to maintain a regular diet and sleep schedule and to refrain from moderate to high-intensity activities for three days prior to testing. The tennis players were asked to wear professional tennis shoes. Before the tests, the athletes' height, weight, and years of tennis training were recorded. The body height and mass of all participants were measured to the nearest 0.1 cm and 0.1 kg using a stadiometer (SECA 217, Hamburg, Germany) and a digital scale (Omron HN288, Healthcare BV, Hoofddorp, Netherlands), respectively. The functional tests and the braking tests were completed over two days and were always administered in the same order. Participants conducted the functional test on the first day and, following adequate rest, the braking test on the second day. The study tested the athletes using the lower extremity functional test specifications23 and the change of direction test specifications.24 Due to the complex nature of hard court sliding braking technique,16 this investigation focuses exclusively on these techniques employed by high-level tennis players on their forehand side (dominant hand).

Functional tests

Six specific physical performance tests, based on the structure and fundamentals of the sliding braking technique, were utilized to evaluate the participants' physical functions. A standardized 10-min warm-up, consisting of various light running exercises and dynamic stretches, was implemented before participants completed the actual test battery. As shown in Fig. 1., the functional tests were performed sequentially in a prescribed order, with an advanced hip joint range of motion test, followed by the braking leg functional test based on the Forcedecks dual force plate system (ForceDecks, FDLite V.2, VALD, Brisbane, Australia), and a sprint test. Participants were asked to take a 15-min break between each test to ensure adequate rest.

Functional testing schematic.

Hip joint range of motion (ROM) test

Manual goniometer (1° resolution) was used to assess the flexibility of the participants' hip joints, encompassing abduction, internal rotation, and external rotation. The stationary arm of the goniometer was aligned with the longitudinal axis of the hinge, ensuring that the movable arm was aligned with both the core and the longitudinal axis of the moving joint. The fixed and movable arms were adjusted to maintain a level line of sight with the goniometer. Clapper et al.25 reported excellent inter-rater reliability for hip mobility measurements using a goniometer, with ICC(2,1) ranging from 0.80 to 0.95. Participants were positioned in a standardized posture according to the International Society of Biomechanics (ISB) recommendations. Before recording, the accuracy was double-checked, and the measurements were documented. Each participant was given three attempts per leg for every test. The test was conducted using a crossover design, where participants were randomly assigned to two groups. The first group was tested on the right leg followed by the left, while the second group was tested on the left leg followed by the right. This ensured that both sides of the body were alternated throughout the entire testing protocol. Participants were given 60 s of rest between the attempts per test, and 3 min of rest between tests.

Braking leg functional test

The braking leg function of participants was tested sequentially in a prescribed order using the Forcedecks dual force plate system (ForceDecks, FDLite V.2, VALD, Brisbane, Australia). The test items, in order, are single leg range of stability test, single leg land and hold test, single leg hop test and single leg jump test. The braking leg, also referred to as the supporting or active leg, is the primary limb an athlete employs in the final stage of the sliding process to actively push against the ground, support body weight, and achieve deceleration and stabilization. Its core role is to convert the kinetic energy of sliding into braking force by contracting leg muscles and utilizing the friction resistance of the foot, aiding the athlete in quickly stabilizing their body posture and preparing for subsequent strikes or movements.1,14 Based on the requirements of sliding brake technology and previous studies,26 the primary lower limb segment involved in the slide braking process for tennis players is the braking leg on the same side as the racket.16 Since the study focused solely on an exploration of tennis players utilizing the hard ground slide braking stroke technique for forehand strokes, the researchers primarily assessed the bounce and functionality of the braking leg used by participants during the tennis forehand side slide braking stroke. Additionally, since all participants in this study were right-handed tennis players with a habit of holding their rackets in their right hand, we tested the participants' right leg (the braking leg). The bilateral lower extremities were not tested simultaneously. Each participant was given three attempts for every test. Participants were given 60 s of rest between the attempts per test, and 3 min of rest between tests. Collings et al.27 reported that the reliability of ForceDecks was similar to that of laboratory force plates, with 530 out of 674 (79 %) showing good or excellent validity and 611 out of 773 (79 %) exhibiting good or excellent reliability (ICC >0.75), including the four tests involved in our study (ICC >0.87).

Single leg range of stability test: Participants were instructed to take a starting position on the board and measure the individual's weight. They were then directed to stand on one leg, using the braking leg, and maintain the starting position for 2–3 s before beginning the test. Arms were to be crossed to prevent arm swinging, and participants were to remain completely still, lift their chests, look forward, and tilt their center of gravity as far as possible in each direction: forward, backward, left, and right.27 The independent sensor output function of ForceDecks is utilized to identify and monitor center of pressure (CoP), and the ForceDecks app calculates the participant's maximum balance range for the braking leg. Signal processing was performed using a dual-pass, fourth-order Butterworth low-pass filter.

Single leg land and hold test: The researcher marked the jumping line with chalk at a distance of 50 cm towards the non-braking leg side of the force measuring platform. Participants were instructed to position their lower limbs close to the outside of the jumping line, keeping their bodies parallel to the force measuring platform. They were then to stomp and jump sideways, with hands folded over their hips to prevent arm swinging. Simultaneously, they were to gently land on the force measuring platform with the braking leg and remain completely still for 2–3 s, ensuring their bodies remained parallel to the platform throughout the entire process.27 The test was designed to measure the peak landing force during braking upon landing and the time required to regain equilibrium post-landing. Signal processing was performed using a dual-pass, fourth-order Butterworth low-pass filter.

Single leg hop test: Participants were instructed to take the starting position on the board, measure their individual weight, and maintain this position for 2–3 s before and between each repetition of the test. They were to stand on one leg, using the braking leg, with arms crossed to prevent arm swinging, remaining completely still. They were to lift their chest, look forward, and bend at the waist; then, they were to jump up on their toes in rapid succession to achieve the desired number of jumps, keeping their knee straight as safely as possible.23 The test measured the braking leg reactive strength index (RSI). Signal processing was performed using a dual-pass, fourth-order Butterworth low-pass filter.

Single leg jump test: Participants were instructed to take the starting position on the board, measure their weight, and then hold that position for 2–3 s before the test. They were told to stand on one leg, using the braking leg to maintain complete stillness, lift their chest with arms crossed to prevent arm swinging, look forward, and bend at the waist. After that, they were to jump up and land softly.28 The test measured the peak force of braking leg at jump. Signal processing was performed using a dual-pass, fourth-order Butterworth low-pass filter.

Speed assessment

The Brower Timing System (Test Center TCi System, Brower, America) was used to measure the participants' 10-m sprint time. A measuring tape marked the 10-m distance, and photoelectric gates were placed at both ends to establish the start and finish lines. A chalk line marked the start. When ready, participants sprinted through the photoelectric gates, and the time taken to complete the 10-m sprint was recorded. Each participant was given three attempts and 60 s of rest between the attempts. The test-retest reliability of the 10-m sprint, utilizing infrared timing gates (Brower Timing Systems), was found to be excellent (ICC = 0.88, 95 % CI: 0.82–0.93) in previous studies.29,30

Braking test

Hard court slide braking confirmation test

According to researchers who have studied the slide braking technique in tennis, players utilizing the hard court slide braking technique for a forehand typically adopt an open stance and use the braking leg on the same side as the racket to slide a short distance across the tennis court's surface before completing the stroke.7 We standardized on athletes capable of completing a slide braking displacement of 45 cm or more on the court and striking the ball with a forehand. As shown in Fig. 2., to ensure the hitting position remained consistent, we positioned the starting line on the midpoint side of the baseline and placed a target ball rack on the forehand side of the tennis court, 20 cm from the sideline and 80 cm from the baseline. Place an effective Brake Start Line (BL) at the baseline, 20 cm from the sideline (directly behind the point of impact), and an effective Slide Start Line (SL) 45 cm from the BL, on the inside measurement of the tennis court. Designate the area between SL and BL as the effective sliding zone and the area beyond BL as the effective braking zone. After getting ready in front of the start line, participants were asked to sprint toward the tennis forehand sideline and hit the forehand at the point of impact using a hard court slide braking technique. Participants were required to use the braking leg to land and start sliding before the slide start line, and then completed the brake as well as hit the ball behind the brake start line. A video recording device was placed 1 m directly behind the BL to assist the 2 evaluators in evaluating the technical maneuvers by videotaping each of the participant's sliding distance. A standardized 10-min warm-up, consisting of various light running exercises and dynamic stretches, was conducted before participants completed the actual test battery. Each participant was given three attempts and 60 s of rest between the attempts. Participants who were able to perform 2 or more standard technical maneuvers would be categorized as being able to use the hard court slide braking technique (Group A). Participants who failed to complete the slide displacement in the test on 2 or more occasions or who displaced less than 45 cm were categorized in the group that could not use the hard ground slide braking technique (Group B). After participants completed this test, they were asked to start the short-distance hitting and return test after a 5-min break. Table 1 shows the descriptive statistics of this test.

Short-distance court hitting and return test

The short-distance hitting and return test requires participants to follow the same requirements as the slide braking confirmation test. As shown in Fig. 3. A photoelectric gate was installed at the midpoint (Point A) of the baseline, serving as both the start and finish line. A chalk-delineated starting line was placed outside the photoelectric gate, where participants positioned themselves. When ready, they made their own start and sprinted to the target ball rack, positioned 20 cm from the singles sideline and 80 cm from the baseline on their dominant hand side. Group B participants, who were not proficient in the hard court slide braking technique, used the traditional braking technique. Participants were asked to complete braking in the effective braking area. After hitting the ball, they swiftly returned to their original position and crossed the photoelectric gate at the midpoint of the baseline. Each participant was given two attempts and 60 s of rest between the attempts. Group A participants, proficient in the hard court slide braking technique, completed the test using both slide and traditional braking technique. After striking the ball, they quickly returned to their original position and crossed the photoelectric gate at the midpoint of the baseline. To prevent the testing of Group A participants from being confounded by order effects, we employed a crossover design method for the test. Participants were randomly assigned to two groups. One group was instructed to perform the first test using the traditional braking technique and the second test using the hard court slide-braking technique. The other group was asked to perform the first test with the hard court slide-braking technique and the second test with the traditional braking technique, ensuring an alternation between both techniques throughout the entire testing protocol. Participants were asked to take a full 10-min break after completing this test and then perform the long-distance hitting and return test.

Short-distance court hitting and return and long-distance court hitting and return test.

Long-distance court hitting and return test

The long-distance hitting and return test also requires participants to follow the same requirements as the slide braking confirmation test. As shown in Fig. 2. This test used the standard tennis court baseline of 8.23 m as a reference point. Photoelectric gates were set up at the intersection of the singles sideline and baseline on the non-racquet hand side (Point C) and at the midpoint (Point A) of the baseline to serve as starting and finishing lines. A chalk-delineated starting line was drawn outside the photoelectric gate. Participants positioned themselves beyond this line and, when ready, made their start, sprinting to the target ball rack. Group B participants, who were not proficient in the hard court slide braking technique, used the traditional braking technique. Participants were asked to complete braking in the effective braking area. After hitting the ball, they swiftly returned to their original position and crossed the photoelectric gate at the midpoint of the baseline. Each participant was given two attempts and 60 s of rest between the attempts. To prevent the testing of Group A participants from being confounded by order effects, we employed a crossover design method for the test. Participants were randomly assigned to two groups. One group was instructed to perform the first test using the traditional braking technique and the second test using the hard court slide-braking technique. The other group was asked to perform the first test with the hard court slide-braking technique and the second test with the traditional braking technique, ensuring an alternation between both techniques throughout the entire testing protocol.

Kinetic data analysis

ForceDecks have been independently tested to ensure validity and reliability, and an extensive validation study by Collings et al.27 demonstrated that the device provides accurate and consistent measurements across a wide range of tests and conditions. ForceDecks feature industry-standard 1000 Hz sampling rates, wireless connectivity and use the same powerful, intuitive ForceDecks app. The data were processed using Matlab (R2024a). A dual-pass, fourth-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 20 Hz was utilized to eliminate high-frequency noise while preserving the effective mechanical signal. Additionally, ForceDecks can precisely identify and track center of pressure (CoP)movements due to their capability to measure the output of each sensor individually.27

Statistical analysis methods

Measured data are presented as either mean ± standard deviation or median. IBM SPSS Statistics 27 was used for statistical analyses. The Anderson-Darling test was conducted to assess the normality of the data. The variability of each functional outcome measure was assessed by calculating the coefficient of variation (CV). Values of CV below 10 % were deemed acceptable.31,32 Independent Samples T-tests were used to compare the overall performance between participants who could and could not use the hard court slide braking technique. Depending on the distribution of the data, either Paired t-tests (for normally distributed data) or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (for non-normally distributed data) were employed to compare the ball-hitting and returning time between the hard court slide braking technique and the traditional braking technique across different movements and return distances. Cohen's d effect size was calculated to determine the magnitude of the effect, where 0.2 < d < 0.5 indicates a small effect, 0.5 < d < 0.8 indicates a medium effect, and d > 0.8 indicates a large effect. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to investigate the factors influencing the use of the hard court slide braking technique among athletes. The ability to use the hard court slide braking technique was set as the binary dependent variable, while gender, height, weight, years of training, hip abduction, internal and external rotation, 10-m sprint speed, braking leg jump peak force, landing peak force, post-landing balance duration, and maximum ankle balance range were considered as independent variables. Multicollinearity among the independent variables was initially assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Comparison of hard court slide braking and traditional braking technique

On average, participants in Group A returned to their starting position after short-distance shots in 2.03 s (±0.17) using the hard court slide braking technique, compared to 2.26 s (±0.20) with traditional braking technique. During the sprint phase of long-distance shots, they took 0.94 s (±0.13) using the hard court slide braking technique and 1.03 s (±0.09) with traditional braking technique. For the entire long-distance hits and returns, the time was 1.87 s (±0.16) and 2.03 s (±0.19), respectively. The hard court slide braking technique significantly reduced the time required for short-distance hits and returns by 10 % (p < 0.001) and for long-distance hits and returns by 7 % (p < 0.001). The differences in time for short-distance hits and returns, sprint phases, and long-distance hits and returns between the hard court slide braking and traditional braking groups were statistically significant (p < 0.001), with effect sizes exceeding 0.8 (Table 4; Fig. 4.).

Comparison of return speed using traditional braking technique

Overall, participants proficient in the hard court slide braking technique exhibited a faster return speed using traditional braking technique compared to those not proficient in sliding braking. Specifically, Group A and Group B recorded an average return time of 2.27 s (±0.19) and 2.68 s (±0.32), respectively, after short-distance shots using traditional braking technique. During the sprint phase of long-distance shots, Group A and Group B averaged 1.03 s (±0.09) and 1.13 s (±0.12), respectively. For the entire long-distance hits and returns, Group A recorded an average time of 2.03 s (±0.19), while Group B averaged 2.26 s (±0.21). Compared to Group B, Group A reduced the time required for short-distance hits and returns by 15 % (p < 0.001) and for long-distance hits and returns by 10 % (p < 0.001). The differences in return time for short-distance hits and returns, sprint phases, and long-distance hits and returns between Group A and Group B using traditional braking technique were statistically significant (p < 0.001), with effect sizes greater than 0.8 (Table 5; Fig. 4.).

Analysis of factors influencing the use of hard court slide braking technique

Before conducting the regression analysis, the variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated for the independent variables to assess multicollinearity. The analysis indicated no multicollinearity issues among the variables: height, weight, years of training, left and right hip abduction, left and right hip internal and external rotation, 10-m sprint time, braking leg reactive strength index (RSI), peak force during braking leg jumps, landing force on the braking leg, recovery balance time of the braking leg, and the maximum lateral and longitudinal balance range of the braking leg.

The binary dependent variable for this analysis was whether athletes could use the hard court slide braking technique, with the aforementioned factors serving as independent variables. Binary logistic regression analysis was carried out to identify the factors significantly influencing the adoption of the hard court slide braking technique among collegiate tennis players. The model results (Table 6, Fig. 5.) show that years of training (p = 0.005), left hip abduction (p = 0.043), 10-m sprint time (p = 0.024), braking leg reactive strength index (RSI) (p = 0.041), landing force on the braking leg (p = 0.030), and recovery balance time of the braking leg (p = 0.014) significantly affect the use of the hard court slide braking technique in this cohort.

Discussion

The analysis of participants' performance using various braking methods during ball striking and returning indicated that the hard court slide braking technique helped to reduce the time for both short and long distances compared to the traditional method. Moreover, participants skilled in the hard court slide braking technique could complete the movement much more quickly when they employed traditional braking technique for ball striking and returning than those who were unskilled in the hard court slide braking technique. The hard court slide braking technique demonstrated a distinct advantage in the ball striking and returning tests, proving significantly effective in reducing the time required for these actions. Factors influencing the adoption of the hard court slide braking technique include years of training, left hip abduction, 10-m sprint time, braking leg reactive strength index (RSI), landing force on the braking leg, and recovery balance time of the braking leg.

Analysis of the effectiveness of the hard court slide braking technique

Arriving on time or early at the ball impact point is essential for successful striking.5,33 In modern professional tennis, often playing on hard courts, athletes need to perform multiple high-speed, high-intensity movements in a short amount of time.11 This performance directly relates to the magnitude and speed of ground reaction forces,8 making explosive acceleration and braking maneuverability crucial factors in an athlete's striking performance.13 Compared to traditional braking methods, the hard court slide braking technique significantly reduces the time needed to return to position after striking on hard courts. Sébastien et al.16 conducted an experiment with 18 elite tennis players using special shoes designed for sliding on hard courts, which allowed for quicker adjustments in body posture after striking. Further research comparing sliding braking technique to traditional braking technique while using special athletic shoes showed more pronounced muscle activity during the sliding process.14 However, this study, different from those that require special equipment and laboratory methods, is the first to examine and discuss the hard court slide braking technique as a regular tennis skill. By treating the hard court slide braking technique as a routine skill, this research overcomes the limitations of having to use special soles for the experiment and experiments are conducted in a more practical context, thus enhancing the evaluation of its value in actual matches and further confirming that the hard court slide braking technique helps players quickly reposition themselves after striking.

Research indicates that optimal mobility combines the attributes of speed and efficiency.4 This study corroborates the efficacy of the hard court slide braking technique in conserving time during movements over both long and short distances. Test segment analysis reveals that participants employing the hard court slide braking technique save time in both the sprint and return phases. This suggests that utilizing this technique in tennis rallies meets the requirements for faster sprinting, quicker deceleration, and more rapid directional changes, thereby optimizing speed and movement efficiency. It also alleviates the time pressure in hard court rally rounds,9,34 allowing athletes to execute more movements within a shorter time frame. Thus, the hard court slide braking technique, characterized by its speed and efficiency, proves to be an effective method for braking and direction changing.

Despite its advantages, the use of the hard court slide braking technique should be judicious. As with clay court slide braking, effective hard court slide braking generally requires extensive lateral or diagonal movements.8,35 Machar Reid12 conducted a biomechanical analysis of the braking process before striking in elite tennis players, and found that higher sprint speeds prior to striking can constrain the lower body during braking. This limitation hampers the transfer of energy to the extremities, which is crucial for maximizing racket head speed. Therefore, slide braking-based striking is more suitable for scenarios involving extensive movement or when quick anticipation of ball striking is needed in defensive rallies, rather than in offensive plays. In such situations, the primary objective is to gain additional time under pressure, and when the aim is to achieve the fastest possible speed of racket head, other traditional movement techniques are recommended. Furthermore, in co-ed competitions, such as mixed doubles, while the hard court slide braking technique effectively aids female athletes in enhancing their mobility, it remains challenging for them to gain an advantage over male athletes. Naturally, this is an area that subsequent research needs to explore further.

Further research indicates that athletes proficient in the slide braking technique require significantly less time to return to position after striking when using traditional braking methods compared to those unskilled in the hard court slide braking technique. This suggests that athletes capable of employing the hard court slide braking technique possess superior acceleration and deceleration capabilities compared to those who cannot.

Analysis of factors influencing the use of hard court slide braking technique

This study found that athletes proficient in the hard court slide braking technique took less time to perform traditional braking maneuvers than those unable to use the hard court slide braking technique; this difference was statistically significant. However, the variance was less pronounced when directly comparing hard court slide braking to traditional technique, suggesting that athletes skilled in hard court slide braking may possess superior acceleration and deceleration capabilities. Kovacs et al.1 argue that braking ability is a trainable skill, and athletes who can decelerate more quickly tend to exhibit better body control, making their movements more efficient. Consequently, this raises the question: does the ability to use the hard court slide braking technique enhance body control and provide other benefits, thereby facilitating its use and improving traditional braking performance? This study aims to further explore this possibility. After conducting a binary logistic regression analysis of the data about body morphology, mobility, and strength, it is found that factors such as years of training, left hip abduction, 10-m sprint speed, braking leg reactive strength index, landing force on the braking leg, and recovery balance time of the braking leg influence the adoption of the hard court slide braking technique among athletes. These indicators reflect various physical attributes previously identified as essential for mastering this complex skill, which demands significant physical capability.16

Tennis is a sport that heavily relies on techniques, where the complexity and stability of technical movements are essential for success.36, 37, 38 Athletes are committed to continuously refining and optimizing their movements to maintain consistent performance.19,39 The foundation for stable and refined technical movements is built on a combination of strength, speed, flexibility, and coordinated stability over time and space.4,11,18,19 Therefore, the duration of training experience is a crucial factor that enables athletes to enhance their technical proficiency. Model-based research has demonstrated that the length of training significantly affects the use of the hard court slide braking technique among athletes, further supporting the theories discussed earlier.

Joint mobility is vital for the effective execution of technical movements and serves as a key indicator of an athlete's technical proficiency.40 In assessing lower limb hip joint mobility among subjects, only left hip abduction made a significant difference. This may be attributed to the fact that when athletes sprint and brake at high speeds, the left gluteal area is more extensively engaged,8 playing a crucial role in both propulsion and stabilization at high velocities.41 Consequently, the capacity for left hip abduction can support and enhance the hard court slide braking process.

The 10-m sprint test is considered a golden standard for measuring speed and explosive power.42 Kovacs et al.11 argue that strength and explosiveness are fundamental for rapid movements on the tennis court. Pavailler et al.14 observed in their experiments that lower limb muscle activity, particularly in the gluteus maximum, is significantly intense when players use slide braking technique to strike the ball on hard courts. In this study, the 10-m sprint speed, indicator of lower limb explosiveness and strength,43 was significant in the regression model. The findings deepen Pavailler's conclusions to a certain extent, suggesting that strength and explosiveness not only facilitate rapid court movements but also contribute to the development of more efficient techniques.4

During matches on hard courts, athletes experience increased plantar pressure.8,44 Pavailler14 hypothesized that athletes utilizing slide braking technique might encounter less plantar pressure and joint force on hard courts. The significance of the braking leg's landing force in this study confirms his hypothesis, indicating that athletes proficient in hard court slide braking technique exhibit lower landing forces, thereby reducing plantar pressure and joint stress.

Time required for balance recovery can reflect an individual's balance capability,22 which is critical for skill acquisition and effective movement.45 It also plays a significant role in influencing athletes' use of slide braking technique. Athletes proficient in hard court slide braking can regain balance and maintain relative stability on their braking leg more quickly after lateral jumps and landings. This is also why athletes with superior movement technique have a better support base; they can more effectively control their torso stability and posture under both dynamic and static conditions.4

More interestingly, based on the regression analysis, the effect of Reaction Strength Index (RSI) on the use of slide braking technique was reversed, which may be due to the fact that RSI is more responsive to the athlete's ability to jump and change direction quickly, whereas the hard court slide braking technique tests the athlete's ability to move laterally as well as centrifugal braking and holding at high speeds.16 The specific reasons for this may need to be analyzed in depth by further research. The study has also found that there is no significant gender difference in the use of hard court slide braking technique. This demonstrates the complexity and sophistication required of the athlete's bodily functions by this technique. This also reinforces the comprehensive and universal nature of this technique. Potential changes in the skill of using this technology due to gender differences could also be a direction further explored in subsequent research.

This study evaluated a total of 17 indicators, with 6 showing significant results. These outcomes align with the diverse physical fitness capabilities required by athletes, ensuring that high-level tennis players can adeptly utilize slide braking technique on hard courts and further refine their traditional braking technique. However, from the perspective of technical training and development, it is still difficult to help professional coaches to visually instruct athletes in targeted slide braking technique training on hard court.46 Based on the experimental results derived from the study, we preferred technical instruction and development through a combination with specialized physical training.4 Perhaps on the basis of emphasizing the striking technique, gradually increasing the training of hip mobility, especially hip abduction, balance training and acceleration training can more quickly help athletes to obtain the necessary physical requirements, and lay a better foundation for learning the hard ground sliding brake striking technique.

Research limitations and future Prospects

Due to individual circumstances, most athletes included in this study were not accustomed to initiating hard court slide braking shots on their backhand side. Consequently, the tests for hard court slide braking shots on the backhand side could not be completed. The study was limited to experiments and assessments solely for hard court slide braking shots on the forehand side, without exploring the use of hard court slide braking technique in other scenarios. Another limitation of this study is that it did not include beginners or young players; all participants were experienced tennis players. Therefore, further research is needed to determine if the study's results would also benefit young players and beginners. After the study identified the main influencing factors of the hard court sliding brake technique to a certain extent, subsequent research could employ motion capture systems under laboratory conditions to comprehensively analyze the entire process of hard court slide braking shots. Such studies would further investigate the key factors that significantly impact the use of this technique, thereby deepening research in this field.

Conclusion

The tests conducted on subjects using different braking methods for ball striking and returning revealed that the hard court slide braking technique exhibited a distinct advantage and is significantly effective in reducing the time required for both actions. The main factors influencing the use of the hard court slide braking technique include years of training, left hip abduction, 10-m sprint time, reactive strength index of the braking leg, landing force on the braking leg, and recovery balance time of the braking leg. The findings from this study offer new perspectives and insights into tennis-specific physical training, assisting more tennis players in mastering and enhancing their athletic skills effectively.
