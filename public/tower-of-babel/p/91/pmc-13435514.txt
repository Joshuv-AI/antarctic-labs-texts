Professional identity in psychiatric nursing: a qualitative study of nurses’ perceptions and influencing factors
Authors: Nasrin Rezaee, Leila Mohammadifard, Sally Pezaro, Marjan Mardani-Hamooleh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProfessional identity in nursing enhances understanding of the profession, acceptance of professional roles, and self-awareness, enhancing patient care overall. Here, we aimed to explore the professional identity of nurses within psychiatric environments.MethodsWe employed a qualitative descriptive design. Individual and semi-structured interviews were conducted with nurses (n = 28) working in psychiatric wards. Data were analyzed using conventional content analysis.ResultsData analysis identified professional identity as a central construct, primarily expressed through ethical excellence, and shaped by internal (professional interaction) and external (cultural threats, organizational and social support) influences.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that professional identity is a foundational characteristic of psychiatric nursing, grounded in ethical excellence and shaped by professional and sociocultural influences. Professional identity emerged as a morally grounded and socially negotiated construct, continuously shaped by dynamic interactions within clinical and societal contexts. These findings offer a strategic framework for nursing policymakers to strengthen professional resilience within psychiatric care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04831-5.

Introduction

The cultivation of a robust and valued professional identity is a prerequisite for nurses to navigate the complex transition into their professional roles. This identity formation is a dynamic process, catalyzed as practitioners move beyond the acquisition of theoretical knowledge and begin to internalize professional values through immersive clinical practice [1]. Rather than a static milestone, this transition represents the foundational integration of self-concept with the clinical competencies and ethical standards required for autonomous practice. Indeed, the cultivation of a professional identity is increasingly codified as an explicit learning objective within nursing curricula because it is fundamentally rooted in the perceived social and clinical value attributed to the profession by both students and the broader healthcare system [2].

In nursing, professional identity enables the recognition of professional roles and behaviors and plays a critical role in ensuring workplace dignity [3]. A robust professional identity serves as a foundational element of nursing practice because it is sustained by a synergistic relationship between clinical competence and a profound sense of belonging to the professional community [4]. These contributing factors ensure that practitioners can internalize the core values of the discipline while demonstrating the technical proficiency required for high-quality care [5]. Essentially, professional identity acts as a primary catalyst for the advancement of nursing as a distinct discipline. It provides a formal framework that reflects a practitioner’s clinical competence and specialized skill set [6]. This identity serves as a critical interpretive guide for nurses during their interactions with patients and ensures that care is delivered within a clearly defined nursing philosophy [7]. Maintaining a professional identity that is distinct from others is essential for clinical clarity and allows nurses to articulate their unique contribution to the healthcare team. This ultimately promotes long-term retention in the profession and encourages sustained employment within the wider healthcare system [6].

Key characteristics of professional identity for nurses include proficiency in providing care, competence across clinical fields, and adherence to professional standards [8]. Within psychiatric nursing, professional identity represents a particularly complex and nuanced concept that remains challenging to define. This identity not only fosters a commitment to professional roles but also reinforces the distinct philosophical and clinical foundations of psychiatric practice [9]. The primary objective of psychiatric nursing is to promote holistic health, enhance quality of life, and facilitate patient rehabilitation [10]. Clinical experiences in psychiatric wards allow nurses to acquire specialized cognitive skills that contribute directly to the development of their professional identity [11]. Furthermore, because mental health systems place significant emphasis on the importance of professional identity [12], the rising prevalence of psychiatric disorders motivates nurses to strengthen their attitudes and motivation. This drive to meet complex and specialized patient needs plays a crucial role in the ongoing evolution of their professional identity [13] and thus warrants deeper study.

Wider literature indicates that a robust professional identity within nursing enhances a deeper understanding of the discipline while fostering the acceptance of professional roles and increasing self-awareness during patient care [14]. In psychiatric settings specifically, identity is shaped by the quality of nursing care, effective nurse-physician collaboration, favorable social and occupational status, and high levels of psychological capital [15]. Conversely, exposure to workplace violence acts as a significant deterrent that adversely affects the stability of a nurse’s professional identity [14–16]. However, these identities remain vulnerable within clinical environments. Factors such as excessive workloads, a lack of interprofessional collaboration, low self-esteem, and job insecurity pose substantial barriers to forming and maintaining a resilient professional self-concept. Furthermore, the experience of being a second victim or possessing limited clinical experience can further erode this foundation. These threats to professional identity can ultimately impair the ability of nurses to establish effective communication with patients in psychiatric wards, which potentially degrades clinical interactions and leads to increased patient dissatisfaction with nursing procedures [17–22].

Although professional identity among nurses in psychiatric wards has garnered some scholarly attention, the necessary paradigm shift within clinical practice remains largely incomplete. It is therefore essential to establish clear professional expectations for nurses in these specialized settings while providing dedicated time and opportunities for them to actively cultivate their professional self-concept [23]. Consequently, qualitative explorations into the professional identity of psychiatric nurses are vital to uncover their deep-seated beliefs, values, and workplace attitudes. Such approaches allow for a nuanced understanding of how these practitioners perceive themselves within their specific clinical roles. Given the relative scarcity of qualitative evidence regarding this phenomenon in Iran, we sought to explore how psychiatric nurses construct and understand their professional identity and the factors that shape it.

Methods

Study design, setting, and participants

A descriptive qualitative design was employed to explore nurses’ perceptions of professional identity in psychiatric wards. This design, using a naturalistic approach, is particularly appropriate when existing knowledge about a concept is limited, allowing for an in-depth exploration of the concept based on participants’ experiences and perceptions [24, 25].

The study was conducted in two public, educational–therapeutic psychiatric hospitals affiliated with Zahedan University of Medical Sciences in southeastern Iran, located in Zahedan. Participants were recruited through purposive sampling. Participants who met the inclusion criteria were nurses in current employment in psychiatric wards with prior experience of providing care to patients with mental illnesses (≥ 5 years). Nurses who were unwilling to participate or were on unpaid leave or academic assignments were excluded. In total, 28 nurses chosen to participate, with maximum variation sampling employed to ensure diversity in age, sex, work experience, educational level, and marital status (Table 1). This study adhered to standardized qualitative research reporting guidelines [26].

Data collection

Following institutional approval to access the study sites, head nurses coordinated the introduction of eligible participants to the researcher. Data were collected through semi-structured, face-to-face interviews conducted by the first author, who holds a PhD in nursing, has received formal training in qualitative research methods, and possesses 10 years of clinical experience in psychiatric nursing. Interviews were conducted during the first three months of 2025 and lasted between 40 and 60 min. Each participant was interviewed once, resulting in a total of 28 interviews. Data collection continued until data saturation was achieved, i.e., when no new information emerged [27]. All interviews were conducted and analyzed in Persian. However, the analysis was subsequently translated into English prior to finalization.

Prior to data collection, the researcher coordinated specific times and locations with each participant to ensure a conducive environment for the interview to take place. All interviews took place in designated rooms within the clinical wards while participants were off duty to prevent professional interruptions. The semi-structured interview guide was informed by the researcher’s extensive clinical experience in psychiatric nursing. Primary inquiries included questions such as: “As a nurse, how would you describe professional identity?” and “What factors do you think influence your professional identity in psychiatric wards?” To ensure depth and clarity, the researcher employed strategic probing techniques and follow-up questions to encourage further elaboration. These prompts included requests for the participants to expand on specific points, clarify their intended meanings, and provide illustrative examples or personal experiences related to the phenomenon under study.

Data analysis

Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently. Conventional content analysis was employed to analyze the data [28]. Each interview was audio-recorded, transcribed word by word, and compiled into a single text for analysis. Through repeated review of the transcripts, meaning units were identified, which were then coded. The extracted codes were grouped into subcategories based on similarities and differences, and the subcategories were subsequently aggregated into overarching categories. During the final stage of analysis, distinctions were made between categories representing the core construct of professional identity and those representing influencing factors, allowing the development of a conceptual model illustrating their relationships.

Rigor

Rigor was ensured using the criteria of credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability [29]. To establish credibility, the research team reviewed the data collection and analysis process. In this regard, the study employed several established strategies: generation of rich and comprehensive descriptions, rigorous member checking, and systematic reflexivity. The clinical experiences of three authors in the team (NR, MMH, and LM), who possess rich experience nursing for individuals with mental illness within the Iranian cultural context, informed both the data collection and analysis processes. The inclusion of a third author, SP, a midwife with extensive qualitative research proficiency but an outsider to the specific clinical and cultural context, provided necessary methodological consistency and critical reflexivity to challenge potential insider bias during data analysis and interpretation. The findings were also subjected to peer review and reflection with five participants. Reflexivity was maintained throughout the analysis process. The research team remained aware of how their values and interpretive commitments could influence data interpretation. Ongoing critical reflection and team discussions facilitated the assessment of assumptions and confirmed that the results were grounded in the nurses’ perceptions. Knowledge about professional identity as a social construct was ultimately co-created with psychiatric nurses.

Dependability was ensured through consultation with an external observer holding a doctoral degree in nursing, who was familiar with both the clinical environment and qualitative research, and agreement was reached regarding the results. In addition, an audit trail was maintained. This was documented using a clear coding schema that identified the results emerging from the analyses. For confirmability, all research activities were documented, and a detailed report of the research process was prepared. The research team maintained a reflexive journal from the outset of the analysis process. The interview data were analyzed using a comparative approach to identify categories and sub-categories within the data. All data and findings were reviewed with the research team to minimize the influence of researcher bias. To ensure transferability, the participants were selected purposively with attention to demographic diversity. Furthermore, transferability was supported by providing a thick description of the concept, thereby allowing others to assess the relevance of the findings across different settings. This approach allows the findings to be more readily applied to similar populations of psychiatric nurses.

Results

Data analysis identified two categories encompassing the confluence of internal cohesion and cultural pressures, alongside value and support frameworks. The confluence of internal cohesion and cultural pressures included the subcategories of professional interaction and cultural threats to the profession. The category of value and support frameworks comprised ethical excellence, as well as organizational and social support (Table 2). While Table 2 presents categories and subcategories descriptively, further analysis revealed a hierarchical relationship in which ethical excellence represents the core expression of professional identity, and the remaining subcategories function as influencing factors.

The categories and their subcategories

Importantly, analysis revealed that these categories do not represent equivalent conceptual aspects of the phenomenon. The confluence of internal cohesion and cultural pressures

This category reflects contextual and relational factors that influence the formation of professional identity. Participants described how both supportive intra-professional relationships and broader cultural perceptions shape how psychiatric nurses understand and evaluate themselves professionally. These influences operated in both positive and negative directions, either strengthening or undermining professional identity.

Professional interaction

This subcategory represents an internal professional factor shaping identity formation, particularly through collaboration, mentorship, and team-based practice. Constructive interactions among nurses, especially between experienced staff and newcomers, played a critical role in facilitating adaptation to the psychiatric environment and fostering a sense of professional belonging.

As one participant explained:

New nurses who enter psychiatric wards experience a lot of stress because they are unfamiliar with the ward and its responsibilities. They are confronted with patients who may be aggressive, and facing psychotic patients is very difficult for them. These experiences may even conflict with their professional identity. I try to collaborate effectively with them and provide orientation within my capacity so they can adapt more quickly to the new conditions and environment and eventually become interested in working in psychiatric wards (Nurse 5)

Participants also highlighted that multidisciplinary collaboration reinforced their competence and professional value:

When a patient is newly admitted without insight and has prominent delusions and hallucinations, I cannot manage alone as a nurse. The psychiatrist must initiate antipsychotic treatment, and later the psychologist becomes involved through psychotherapy. At the same time, I provide psychological nursing care. If I try to manage the situation alone, nothing progresses, and I experience a sense of reduced competence, which, in turn, undermines my professional identity (Nurse 10)

These findings indicate that effective professional interaction reinforces identity through validation of competence and shared professional values.

Cultural threats to the profession

This subcategory reflects an external sociocultural factor that challenges and undermines professional identity. Participants described how negative societal perceptions of psychiatric nursing contributed to feelings of stigma, reduced professional prestige, and identity ambiguity.

In Iran, not only mental illness but also working in psychiatric wards carries stigma. Our relatives label us when they find out we work in psychiatry and mockingly say, ‘You take care of crazy people—one day you’ll lose your mind too.’ This means nursing in psychiatric wards has not received a positive social evaluation. This inaccurate cultural image and belief must be corrected, because it can create ambiguity in our professional identity (Nurse 2)

The intensity of this stigma was captured through the metaphor of “cultural assassination”:

I can honestly say that due to working in psychiatric wards, we have been targeted by countless cultural attacks from friends and acquaintances. We are stigmatized nurses, as if we have been culturally assassinated by society. These culturally rooted adversities conflict with our professional identity, and for our professional identity to grow, we must survive this cultural assassination (Nurse 27)

Thus, cultural threats act as destabilizing forces that undermine identity formation and continuity.

Value and support frameworks

This category encompasses structural, ethical, and developmental influences that shape professional identity. Participants described how identity evolves through the interaction between internalised values and external systems of support and recognition.

Ethical excellence

This subcategory represents the core expression of professional identity, reflecting how psychiatric nurses define themselves through deeply internalised ethical values, professional commitment, and moral responsibility toward patients.

Participants consistently framed their identity in terms of ethical practice:

I try to consider ethical values such as passion for service, fundamental belief in the profession, professional conscience, commitment, and avoidance of negligence as key factors in developing professional identity in nursing. From the day I decided to work in psychiatric wards, I accepted the ethical values of this field, because I believe that a professional identity deeply grounded in these values has a profound impact on patient care (Nurse 22)

From the day I decided to work in psychiatric wards, I accepted the ethical values of this field, because I believe that a professional identity deeply grounded in these values has a profound impact on patient care (Nurse 22)

Ethical conduct was also closely linked to professional pride:

A psychiatric nurse who accepts the patient as a human being must genuinely care about people, never tire of listening, show compassion in care, and avoid judgment… By doing so, the nurse emphasizes the ethical dimension of professional identity and develops a sense of professional pride (Nurse 15)

A psychiatric nurse who accepts the patient as a human being must genuinely care about people, never tire of listening, show compassion in care, and avoid judgment… By doing so, the nurse emphasizes the ethical dimension of professional identity and develops a sense of professional pride (Nurse 15)

These findings indicate that professional identity is fundamentally grounded in ethical excellence, which serves as its primary defining characteristic rather than merely an influencing factor.

Organizational and social support

This subcategory reflects structural and societal factors that reinforce and validate professional identity. Participants emphasised the importance of continuing education, institutional support, and public recognition in strengthening their sense of professional self.

I like to progress in my work, day by day. For example, the various courses that hospital managers organize for us on mental disorders and modern care approaches… keep us up to date… This up-to-date knowledge enables nurses to provide more scientifically grounded care and enhances their professional identity (Nurse 26)

Social recognition also played a crucial reinforcing role:

When the patient’s family… saw that we were constantly at the bedside and had performed a successful resuscitation, they thanked us a lot and acknowledged our efforts… the positive impact our work can have on public perceptions enhances our professional identity (Nurse 12)

Synthesis of professional identity

Thus, organizational and social support function as external validation mechanisms that strengthen and sustain professional identity. Synthesising findings across all categories, professional identity emerged as a morally grounded, relational, and socially negotiated sense of professional self. This identity was most clearly expressed through ethical excellence, reflecting nurses’ internalised values, commitment, and sense of responsibility.

At the same time, professional identity was not static; it was continuously shaped, reinforced, or challenged by internal professional dynamics (professional interaction) and external forces (cultural threats and organizational and social support). Thus, professional identity emerged as both an internal value-based construct and a dynamic outcome of ongoing engagement with professional and societal contexts.

Discussion

This study advances understanding of professional identity in psychiatric nursing by distinguishing between the core construct of identity and the factors that shape it. Our results provide a more comprehensive conceptualisation of professional identity as both a value-based construct and a contextually shaped process.

In this study, we investigated the professional identity of nurses within psychiatric environments, revealing that effective intra-professional collaboration is a primary driver in the creation of constructive and dynamic clinical relationships. These connections are fundamental to fostering a professional identity. Participants specifically highlighted the necessity of structured intra-professional support during the transition of novice nurses into psychiatric settings. This focus on early-career practitioners is vital because evidence from England suggests that nurses entering psychiatric wards encounter significant stressors, including acute anxiety and feelings of insecurity [30]. These challenges, alongside the adoption of unfamiliar roles and responsibilities, represent a substantial threat to the stability of their professional identity. Furthermore, the narratives in this study indicate that a robust team-based approach, underpinned by effective communication with non-nursing professionals, is essential for shaping a positive professional self-concept. This emphasis on collective action aligns with existing research in Iran that identifies teamwork as a cornerstone of high-quality care provision [31–33]. Similarly, findings from Brazil suggest that interdisciplinary care experiences in psychiatric wards not only enhance professional identity but also elevate the overall quality of clinical performance [34]. Corroborative findings such as these suggest a need for strengthening teamwork and team-based approaches in the healthcare setting. As psychiatric nurses increasingly define their professional identity through their roles as care coordinators within multidisciplinary teams, it is evident that identity construction transcends membership in a single professional group [35]. These practitioners develop a multidimensional identity rooted in diverse professional interactions, deriving their clinical motivation from collaborative spaces that exist beyond traditional disciplinary boundaries [5]. Thus, it will be important to consider the duality of roles in healthcare and how these may interact.

Unfortunately, the prevalence of negative societal attitudes regarding mental illness poses a substantial threat to the professional identity of the psychiatric nursing workforce. Consequently, practitioners in these settings often find themselves marginalized by social stigma [36, 37]. The participants’ use of the “cultural assassination” metaphor to describe these stigma-laden attacks illustrates the profound impact of distorted societal perceptions concerning mental disorders and psychiatric care. Rectifying this public image is therefore essential for the robust enhancement of professional identity. Consistent with these findings, other studies in Iran have confirmed that both psychiatric disorders and the act of providing care for affected individuals are deeply associated with social stigma [38–41]. It can therefore be argued that the framework of professional identity for psychiatric nurses is inextricably linked to the deeper layers of societal culture. Cultivating a positive cultural representation of nursing within the public sphere may therefore be of critical importance, because mitigating experienced stigma is essential for the consolidation of a resilient professional identity. The present study reveals that professional identity, as a core component of a nurse’s broader social identity, is profoundly influenced by societal recognition of professional competencies and capabilities. Public awareness regarding nursing performance is critical. Research conducted in Saudi Arabia has demonstrated that a lack of public knowledge concerning nursing roles negatively impacts professional self-concept [42]. Indeed, the social status of nurses is dictated by public perceptions, which play a decisive role in shaping how practitioners perceive themselves and the intrinsic value of their discipline [43, 44]. Our own experiences similarly demonstrate that specific sociocultural factors surrounding the nursing profession actively construct societal attitudes toward nursing identity. The findings of this study indicate that cultural perceptions of nursing in Iran are not universally positive and are heavily context dependent. Within this framework, the social value of nursing is culturally constructed and remains vulnerable to prevailing local beliefs. As this study demonstrates that the professional identity of psychiatric nurses is highly contingent upon the sociocultural landscape, it is essential to systematically examine and enhance the cultural image of nursing to foster professional growth. Furthermore, the development of culturally sensitive educational programs designed to destigmatize mental disorders and psychiatric care is vital. These initiatives should aim to increase public awareness of the specialized nature of psychiatric nursing and provide a foundation for a stable and resilient professional identity. Such cultural and educational interventions could be effectively delivered through mass media platforms with the direct participation of psychiatric nursing specialists to ensure the accurate representation of the field.

In this study, we identify ethical excellence, manifested through a steadfast adherence to professional and ethical values, as a primary determinant for the development of a positive professional identity. By internalizing these values, participants were able to provide humanistic, compassionate, and respectful care to patients and their families. This finding is consistent with existing literature in Iran which suggests that nursing care is fundamentally grounded in humanity and respect [45–49]. Consequently, the integration of ethical principles into clinical practice may constitute a central pillar of professional identity formation [50]. Professional identity is therefore inherently intertwined with professional commitment, altruism, and humanity, suggesting that the nursing self-concept is profoundly ethics-based [6]. Furthermore, organizational support serves as a critical external validator of professional identity. When management provides encouragement and formal recognition of professional and scientific competencies, they contribute to the overarching trajectory of professional development. This support ultimately enhances identity by incentivizing the expansion of nursing knowledge and skills. A similar correlation was identified in an Australian study where recognition within psychiatric work environments was found to lead directly to the development of a positive professional identity [51]. Research in this field demonstrates that psychiatric nurses must command a sophisticated range of knowledge and clinical skills to effectively promote mental health [52]. By advancing these competencies, nurses simultaneously improve their emotional resilience and fortify their professional identity [53–55]. According to researcher interpretation, the professional identity of psychiatric nurses evolves within a synergistic context where the pursuit of high-quality care is driven by continuous and simultaneous intellectual and clinical growth. This supports the growth of more clinical academics in the workforce to enhance care. The participating nurses identified social support as a primary determinant of professional development. This observation aligns with research from Denmark which demonstrates that the evolution of the nursing self-concept is heavily contingent upon broader social factors [7]. It is evident that professional identity construction is a dynamic and multifaceted process shaped by the unique social contexts in which it is embedded [56]. The findings demonstrate that professional identity is not a single-dimensional construct but a dynamic and multifaceted phenomenon. Ethical excellence represents its central defining feature, while professional interaction, cultural context, and organizational and social support operate as interconnected influences that shape its development. This model highlights that professional identity is simultaneously internally grounded in ethical values and externally shaped by professional and sociocultural environments.

This study has several limitations. As is characteristic of qualitative inquiry, the findings are fundamentally context-dependent and are intended to provide depth rather than broad generalizability to diverse clinical settings. In addition, since the present study was conducted in psychiatric hospitals affiliated with a single university, the findings should be generalized cautiously to all psychiatric hospitals across Iran. Furthermore, because this investigation focused exclusively on the professional identity of nurses within psychiatric wards, the findings represent a specific disciplinary perspective. Future research should therefore examine the construction of professional identity across various other clinical environments to facilitate a robust comparative analysis of the nursing self-concept. Additionally, given that the current findings highlight the critical role of a team-based approach in fostering identity, future qualitative studies should explore the professional identity of other mental health team members, such as psychologists and psychiatrists. Such an expanded research agenda would provide a more holistic understanding of how interprofessional dynamics shape the collective and individual identities of those providing mental healthcare.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that professional identity in psychiatric nursing is primarily grounded in ethical excellence, while being continuously shaped by internal professional dynamics and external sociocultural and organizational factors. These internal constructs are essential for the delivery of humane and ethically grounded patient care. This identity functions as a multifaceted sociocultural construct that evolves through continuous professional practice and relational engagement. However, the findings also highlight a critical vulnerability where the identity of the psychiatric nurse remains compromised by distorted public perceptions. Consequently, there is an urgent need to refine the societal image of the profession and secure a more prominent social status for these practitioners. Transforming societal perceptions of psychiatric nursing is therefore imperative. To project a more accurate and positive image, nursing authorities and policymakers must actively reform the professional standing of nursing within the public sphere. These findings provide a strategic foundation for nursing leaders to develop targeted interventions that strengthen the sociocultural dimensions of professional identity and ensure the long term resilience of the psychiatric nursing workforce. To the best of our knowledge, this study is novel as it is the first to explore the professional identity of nurses working in psychiatric settings in Iran. The findings of this study add to the body of psychiatric nursing knowledge regarding professional identity and reveal that it is crucial for nurses to deliver a good quality of care.
