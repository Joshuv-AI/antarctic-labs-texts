Real-world safety and effectiveness of lenvatinib in unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma in Korea: post-marketing study
Authors: Wonseok Kang, Yoon Jun Kim, Seung Up Kim, Yeon Seok Seo, Jin-Wook Kim, Ji Hoon Kim, Soo Young Park, Yang-Hyun Baek, Kang Mo Kim, Hae Lim Lee, Ki Tae Yoon, Hyeyeong Kim, Jae Youn Cheong, Jae Seok Hwang, Ju Hyun Kim, Kwang Min Kim, Pil Soo Sung, Jieun Kim, Do Young Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aim: This post-marketing surveillance study evaluated the safety and effectiveness of lenvatinib as first-line treatment for unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma in Korea.Materials & methods: Adverse drug reactions (ADRs) and other safety and effectiveness end points were assessed in patients who initiated lenvatinib according to the approved label in republic of Korea.Results: Among 658 lenvatinib-treated patients, ADRs were reported in 57.8%; ADRs grade ≥3 in 13.5%. The most common grade ≥3 ADRs were asthenia (1.2%) and hepatic encephalopathy (1.2%). Physician-reported tumor responses (n = 511) were complete (1.0%) or partial (12.9%) response and stable (45.2%) or progressive disease (40.9%); objective response rates were higher with longer lenvatinib treatment duration (p < 0.001).Conclusion: Lenvatinib was generally well tolerated and effective in real-world clinical practice in Korea.Clinical trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05225207

Introduction

According to 2020 estimates, worldwide, liver cancer, of which hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common form [1], was the seventh most common cancer and the third leading cause of cancer mortality [2]. The distribution of HCC differs by factors such as race/ethnicity and sex [1]. The disease risk is two- to threefold higher for males than females [1], and in Korea, where HCC ranks as the fifth most common cancer, it is the second leading cause of cancer deaths among men [3]. Risk factors for HCC include chronic hepatitis B (HBV) and C virus (HCV) infection, excessive alcohol consumption and metabolic liver disease, particularly nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [1]. Although early-stage HCC can be treated curatively, such as with local ablation or surgical resection [4], for advanced or unresectable HCC (uHCC), the prognosis is poor; the estimated median survival time is 6 to 8 months [5].

Sorafenib remained the first-line treatment for uHCC for a decade following its introduction in 2007, as various new targeted therapies failed to demonstrate superiority or noninferiority in clinical trials. Since 2017, the development of systemic therapies for uHCC has expanded rapidly and several other oral tyrosine kinase inhibitors ([TKIs], among which lenvatinib was the first, have been approved as first- (lenvatinib) or second-line (regorafenib, and cabozantinib) therapies for uHCC. Additionally, immunotherapies such as the immune checkpoint inhibitors nivolumab and pembrolizumab, and the fully human immunoglobulin G1 monoclonal antibody, ramucirumab, have been approved as second-line treatments for uHCC, each with unique safety profiles [5,6].

Lenvatinib is an orally active, multi-targeted TKI of the vascular endothelial growth factor receptors (VEGFRs) 1–3, fibroblast growth factor receptors (FGFRs) 1–4, platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR)α, the tyrosine kinase receptor, rearranged during transfection (RET) and stem cell factor receptor, c-KIT [7]. These receptors are considered to be drivers for the development of numerous cancers [7]. Following the approval of lenvatinib for the treatment of patients with differentiated thyroid cancer and renal cell carcinoma, and the reasonable safety and efficacy profile shown in a Phase II study in patients with advanced HCC [8], lenvatinib was assessed in a global Phase III trial (REFLECT) for non-inferiority compared with sorafenib as a first-line treatment for uHCC [9].

In the randomized, open-label REFLECT trial in patients with uHCC, lenvatinib (n = 478) was non-inferior to sorafenib (n = 476) for the primary efficacy end point of overall survival (OS), 13.6 vs 12.3 months, hazard ratio [HR] 0.92 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.79–1.06) [9]. Additionally, compared with sorafenib, progression-free survival (7.4 vs 3.7 months), median time to progression (8.9 vs 3.7 months) and the objective response rate (ORR; 24.1% vs 9.2%) were significantly improved with lenvatinib (p < 0.0001 for all). Based on these favorable results, in 2018 lenvatinib was approved in several countries, including the USA, Japan and the Republic of Korea, as first-line treatment for uHCC [10–12]. To minimize early dose reduction or interruption, the recommended lenvatinib dosages for patients with HCC are 8 mg/day in those with a body weight <60 kg and 12 mg once daily in patients with a body weight of ≥60 kg [13].

Similar to other multi-targeted TKIs, lenvatinib is associated with clinically important adverse events (AEs) [14]. In the REFLECT trial, the most common AEs of any grade with lenvatinib were hypertension, diarrhea, decreased appetite, decreased weight, fatigue, palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia syndrome, proteinuria and dysphonia [9]. Although the safety and efficacy of lenvatinib in uHCC have been demonstrated in Phase II and III studies, which included patients in Korea [8,9], clinical trial patient characteristics may not reflect the range seen in clinical practice.

This post-marketing surveillance (PMS) study assessed the safety and effectiveness of lenvatinib as first-line treatment for uHCC in Korea in routine clinical practice. The objectives of this real-world study were to identify the occurrence and incidence of serious adverse events (SAEs) and serious adverse drug reactions (SADRs), ADRs and non-serious ADRs and other safety and effectiveness data, including factors considered to affect the safety and effectiveness of lenvatinib in the post-marketing setting. The study was conducted as part of a risk-management plan, as required by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) of the Republic of Korea.

Methods

Study design

This study was planned as a prospective, multicenter, observational, PMS study of lenvatinib (Lenvima®; Eisai Korea Inc., Korea) in patients with uHCC in Korea, in compliance with the latest Standard for Re-examination of New Drugs (ClinicalTrials.gov Trial Registration ID: NCT05225207). The study protocol was reviewed by the MFDS, and the study was conducted from the day the protocol was approved up until 28 August 2022. Among institutions administering lenvatinib, those which agreed to participate entered into a contract with the sponsor and participated in the study. The study was planned to be conducted at 10 or more institutions in Korea. The protocol and case report forms (CRFs) were reviewed by each institutional review board according to the requirements of the respective study centers. Study investigators conducted the study according to the protocol and related regulations and obtained written consent from all patients regarding the use and disclosure of personal medical data. This study conformed to the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Pharmaceutical Affairs Law and ministerial ordinance in the latest Standard for Re-examination of New Drugs in Korea.

Patient selection

Patients aged >18 years who initiated lenvatinib as first-line therapy for uHCC, according to the approved label for Korea, who did not meet any contraindication criteria for lenvatinib, and who provided written consent to participate in the study, were enrolled. In line with MFDS guidelines, the planned number of study participants was at least 600. Key exclusion criteria were hypersensitivity to active pharmaceutical ingredients or excipients of lenvatinib, and pregnancy or breastfeeding.

Patients were observed for a maximum of 12 months from the date of first lenvatinib administration following consent until the date of the last visit (the observation period). After completion of 12 months of observation, there was a follow-up period of 30 days from the date of last study drug administration; patients for whom the drug was discontinued during the study period were followed up in the same manner. Patients who had been administered lenvatinib at least once and had provided safety data through visits thereafter were included in all assessments (the safety population). Patients unable to visit the study center were followed up by phone, letter, e-mail or other means. AEs reported by the investigators during this follow-up period were included in the study results, with reasons provided for drop-outs who were lost to follow-up. The effectiveness population included all patients in the safety population for whom efficacy data were available.

Data collection

Patient demographics and clinical history collected included sex, date of birth, pregnancy/breastfeeding status, inpatient/outpatient status, diagnosis, initial diagnosis date, weight and height. Clinical characteristics extracted from patients' medical records included allergy history, hepatic/renal impairment, other medical history/concomitant disease, cancer treatment history before lenvatinib administration, lenvatinib administration record (prior/initial administration status, dose and doses/day, administration period and reasons for drug discontinuation, dose modifications), concomitant medication and laboratory test results and vital signs. Internal consistency checks were conducted to ensure the consistency and completeness of data, and where necessary, monitoring of the study centers was conducted.

Safety assessments

All AEs which occurred during the surveillance period were recorded, regardless of any causal relationship with the study medication and were coded by System Organ Class (SOC) and Preferred Term (PT) according to the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) Version 25.0. AEs were defined as any untoward and unintended signs (e.g., anomalies in laboratory test results) or symptoms/responses occurring during administration/use of lenvatinib, which did not necessarily have a causal relationship with the drug.

Investigators assessed the seriousness and causal relationships of all AEs reported in the CRFs. Causality of the AEs was categorized according to the World Health Organization-Uppsala Monitoring Center (WHO-UMC) criteria as certain, probable/likely, possible, unlikely, conditional/unclassified, unassessable/unclassifiable or not applicable [15]. In accordance with the protocol approved by the Korea Health Authority, AEs for which a causal relationship with lenvatinib could not be ruled out were defined as ADRs. AEs with unknown causality to lenvatinib were also considered ADRs. SAEs, which were required to be reported within 24 hours, regardless of causal relationship with lenvatinib, included those resulting in death, considered life-threatening, requiring hospitalization or prolonged hospitalization, resulting in persistent or significant disability or incapacity, resulting in birth defect or congenital anomaly, or other medically significant events.

The severity of an AE was graded according to the latest version of the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 4.0. Items not in the CTCAE were classified as grades 1 to 5 based on the following assessment criteria:Grade 1: mild AE, not requiring treatment; anomalies in imaging results/test result without symptom;Grade 2: moderate AE, requiring minimal treatment/non-invasive treatment;Grade 3: severe AE, with clear symptoms requiring hospitalization or invasive treatment/interventional radiology/transfusion/therapeutic endoscopy/surgery, etc.;Grade 4: life threatening or incapacitating AE, acute life-threatening metabolic/cardiovascular complications, etc.; requiring intensive treatment or emergency measures (such as interventional radiology/therapeutic endoscopy/surgery, etc.;Grade 5: death related to the AE.

Incidence rates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of AEs, ADRs and SAEs occurring in the safety population were assessed. AE incidence rates and 95% CIs are presented for patient background factors (demographic analysis, medical history/concomitant disease, presence of hypertension before study drug administration, cancer treatment history before study drug administration, and lenvatinib administration records, concomitant medication), while incidence rates and 95% CIs of AEs, ADRs, unexpected AEs and SAEs in special patient groups (pediatric, geriatric, pregnant, renal impairment, hepatic impairment and others) are presented using statistical tests such as chi-square test, Fisher's exact test and logistic regression.

Effectiveness

Tumor response was evaluated using imaging data, including contrast-enhanced dynamic computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and overall tumor response (complete response [CR], partial response [PR], stable disease [SD] or progressive disease [PD]) was classified by the attending physician according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) v1.1 and/or modified RECIST (mRECIST) criteria [16] according to each investigator's discretion. To qualify any incomplete data, a discussion with the investigators who evaluated tumor response using both criteria was conducted to ascertain the most valid data. Therefore, our effectiveness data show the best overall response. In Korea, the frequency of assessments of tumor response is typically every 2 or 3 months. This may vary for each patient depending on their disease progression or AEs during lenvatinib treatment. In this study, assessments were generally conducted within 30 or 45 days.

To investigate factors thought to affect effectiveness, CR and PR rates and 95% CIs are presented by background factors including demographic analysis, medical history/concomitant disease, presence of hypertension before study drug administration, cancer treatment history before study drug administration, lenvatinib administration records and concomitant medication. The relationship between the respective factors and effectiveness were analyzed using statistical methods such as chi-square test, Fisher's exact test and logistic regression.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics for all safety and effectiveness parameters, including the safety and effectiveness results for each background factor. Categorical data were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, while continuous data were analyzed using t-test or Wilcoxon rank sum test. Incidence rates and 95% Cls were determined for all AEs reported during the PMS period.

Results

Patient characteristics

In total, 700 CRFs were collected from 33 investigators at 29 institutions throughout Korea. Patients were enrolled at these institutions between 28 May 2019 and 31 January 2022. Among them, 42 were excluded from the safety analysis for reasons including: violated the inclusion/exclusion criteria (n = 15); violated the dosage (n = 13); lost to safety follow-up (n = 8), had not taken lenvatinib capsules 4 mg or 10 mg (n = 5), or other reason (n = 1) (Figure 1). Overall, 658 patients were included in the safety analysis, of whom 511 were included in the effectiveness analysis. Tumor response was evaluated by RECIST v1.1 or mRECIST criteria in the real-world setting.

Prior to the study, none of the safety population had received chemotherapy, while 64.1% had received non-chemotherapy treatment, and 42.1% had a history of treatment for hypertension.

Special populations

There were no pediatric patients; all patients were aged ≥19 years. Elderly subjects aged ≥65 years accounted for almost half of the study population, 47.0% (309/658). Renal impairment was present in 5.9% (39/658) and hepatic impairment in 74.3% (489/658) of all patients.

Safety

During the surveillance period, 1567 AEs were reported in 486 (73.9%) of the 658 patients in the safety population (Table 3). The most common AEs by PT were hypertension (14.9%, 100 events), abdominal pain and diarrhea, which were each reported in 11.1% (84 and 78 events, respectively) of patients, and palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia syndrome, 10.3% (69 events). Of all AEs, 908 reported in 380 patients (57.8%) were ADRs, as the causal relationship with lenvatinib treatment could not be ruled out. Adverse drug reactions

Among the 908 ADRs reported in 380/658 patients (57.8%), the most frequently reported were hypertension (14.4%), diarrhea (10.5%), palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia syndrome (10.0%) and decreased appetite (7.6%). ADRs of severity grade ≥3 comprised 108 ADRs reported in 89 patients (13.5%). These were asthenia (1.2%), hepatic encephalopathy (1.2%), diarrhea (1.1%), hypertension (0.9%), ascites (0.6%), muscular weakness (0.6%) and proteinuria (0.6%).

Serious adverse events & serious adverse drug reactions

The incidences of all SAEs and SADRs that occurred in the 658 patients in the safety population are shown in Supplementary Table S1. Overall, 249 SAEs were reported in 174 patients (26.4%), the most common were asthenia (2.6%, n = 17), abdominal pain (2.3%, n = 15), death (1.7%, n = 11), hepatic encephalopathy (1.7%, n = 11) and disease progression (1.1%, n = 7). SADRs occurred in 52 patients (7.9%); the most common were hepatic encephalopathy (0.9%, n = 6), asthenia (0.8%, n = 5), diarrhea (0.6%, n = 4), muscular weakness (0.6%, n = 4), ascites (0.5%, n = 3) and death (0.2%, n = 1).

For the 1567 AEs reported in 486 patients, no change in lenvatinib dose was required for 928 events; however, dose reduction was required for 155 events, dose interruption for 87 and treatment discontinuation for 218 events. The most frequent AEs that led to treatment discontinuation (with an event rate of >5) were asthenia (21), abdominal pain (10), hepatic encephalopathy (8), nausea (8), ascites (7), vomiting (6), diarrhea (6) and proteinuria (6).

Factors considered to affect safety

The AE incidence rate by background factors (demographic data [gender, age, inpatient/outpatient status], medical history [HCC disease duration, allergy history, hypertension], other medical history and concomitant disease, prior and concomitant medication [chemotherapy treatment history before lenvatinib treatment, hypertension treatment, other concomitant medication], lenvatinib administration status [total treatment duration, average daily dose]) and in special populations (elderly, renal impairment, hepatic impairment) were analyzed in relation to safety. In the analysis of AE incidence rate among the 658 patients in the safety population, factors with statistically significant differences in AE incidence rate were the presence of other medical history and concomitant disease (p < 0.001), history of non-chemotherapy treatment prior to lenvatinib treatment (p = 0.008), hypertension treatment (p < 0.001), other concomitant medication (p < 0.001), average daily dose of lenvatinib (p < 0.001), presence of renal impairment (p = 0.020) and presence of hepatic impairment (p = 0.001).

In logistic regression analysis, three factors were associated with the AE incidence rate (Table 4): history of non-chemotherapy treatment prior to lenvatinib treatment (p = 0.016); hypertension treatment (p < 0.001) and the average daily dose of lenvatinib (12 mg/day compared with <8 mg/day, p < 0.001; 8 mg/day – <10 mg/day, p = 0.003).

Among the study analysis population, 145 patients were not evaluable for tumor response and two patients were not evaluated by either mRECIST or RECIST 1.1; therefore, 511 patients were included in the effectiveness population for tumor assessment. Additionally, effectiveness analysis by special populations (elderly, renal impairment and hepatic impairment) showed no statistically significant differences in ORRs. Among 231 elderly patients (45%) aged ≥65 years, the ORR was 13.9% and, for the 55% of patients aged <65 years was 13.8% (p = 0.994). Renal impairment was present in 28 patients (5.5%), for whom the ORR was 25% (n = 7) compared with an ORR of 13.2% in those without renal impairment (p = 0.090). Among the 384 patients with hepatic impairment (74.9%), the ORR was 14.3% compared with 12.4% in patients without hepatic impairment (p = 0.585).

Discussion

In this prospective PMS study in patients with uHCC in Korea, the safety (n = 658) and effectiveness (n = 511) of lenvatinib were demonstrated in a real-world clinical practice setting. A total of 145 patients were not evaluable for tumor response. Among these patients, 74 discontinued lenvatinib treatment due to AEs, and 35 were lost to follow-up. Additionally, nine patients discontinued lenvatinib treatment based on physician's judgement, and four patients could not be evaluated due to death.

The safety profile of lenvatinib was consistent with that observed in the pivotal Phase III REFLECT trial in patients with uHCC [9] and in studies in patients with advanced HCC [Ikeda et al. 2017] and other advanced solid tumors [17]. Additionally, the results are in agreement with the safety and effectiveness of lenvatinib in relatively smaller, observational, retrospective, real-world studies in patients with uHCC conducted in Korea [18,19].

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population, including mean age 64 years, 85% male, 47% elderly, 74.3% with hepatic impairment and 42% with hypertension, suggest that this PMS study captured a cross-sectional population with uHCC, similar to those in previous real-world studies in Korea [18,19].

Most patients in this study experienced AEs (73.9%) and relatively fewer had ADRs (57.8%). By AE severity, AEs of grade ≥3 were reported in 32.5% of patients and an ADR of grade ≥3 in 13.5% (89 patients). Notably, in the randomized, Phase III REFLECT study, among 476 lenvatinib-treated uHCC patients, treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) occurred in 99% of patients, 94% experienced treatment-related AEs and, while TEAEs of grade ≥3 were reported in 75% of patients, those considered treatment-related accounted for 57% [9].

The most common ADRs in this PMS study were hypertension (14.4%), diarrhea (10.5%), palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia syndrome (10.0%) and decreased appetite (7.6%), while ADRs of grade >3 were palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia syndrome (0.3%), death (0.2%), hepatic encephalopathy (0.2%), acute kidney injury (0.2%), hepatorenal syndrome (0.2%), spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (0.2%) and liver abscess (0.2%). The incidences of ADRs were higher in a PMS study in patients with uHCC in Japan, including decreased appetite (23.9%), hypertension (21.3%), proteinuria (18.3%), palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia (16.5%) and diarrhea (13.9%) [20]. Similarly, in the REFLECT trial, the most common TEAEs of any grade were hypertension (42%), diarrhea (39%), decreased appetite (34%), decreased weight (31%), fatigue (30%), palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia syndrome (27%), proteinuria (25%) and the most commonly reported TEAEs of grade ≥3 were hypertension (23%), decreased weight (8%), proteinuria (6%) and increased blood bilirubin (7%) [9]. Relative to the incidences of ADRs reported in the REFLECT trial, the incidences in this PMS study are lower, although the AE profile is similar. This was also seen in other retrospective real-world studies in Korea [18,19]. In the study of lenvatinib in 92 patients with advanced HCC by Cheon et al., the most frequent AEs of grade 3–4 were hyperbilirubinemia (8.7%), aspartate aminotransferase elevation (6.5%), diarrhea (5.4%), fatigue (4.3%) and hypertension (4.3%) [18]. Discrepancies may be a consequence of the differences between clinical study conditions and real-world settings, including factors such as patient characteristics, treatment duration and follow-up. For example, this PMS study was conducted in Korea and the median duration of study treatment was 3.1 months with 1 year follow-up, while the REFLECT trial included patients from 20 countries across the Asia-Pacific, European and North American regions, median treatment duration was 5.7 months and follow-up was 27.7 months [9].

In the current study, SAEs and SADRs occurred in 26.4% and 7.9% of lenvatinib-treated patients, respectively. All of the SAEs occurred at a frequency of ≤2.6%, while the incidences of SADRs were: hepatic encephalopathy (0.9%), asthenia (0.8%), diarrhea (0.6%), muscular weakness (0.6%), ascites (0.5%) and death (0.2%). According to the investigator's assessment, the one death associated with treatment was in a patient who had palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia syndrome and who also had staphylococcal bacteremia. In the REFLECT trial, serious TEAEs and serious treatment-related TEAEs occurred in 43% and 18% of lenvatinib-treated patients and treatment-related fatal AEs occurred in 2% (n = 11) of lenvatinib-treated patients, including hepatic failure (n = 3), cerebral hemorrhage (n = 3) and respiratory failure (n = 2) [9].

Logistic regression analysis showed that three factors were significantly associated with the incidence of AEs in this study, nonchemotherapy treatment history, hypertension treatment and the average daily dose of lenvatinib. Hypertension is a known comorbidity in patients with HCC [21] and it is recognized as the most frequent AE associated with lenvatinib, regardless of the severity [14]. In this PMS study, hypertension was an ADR in 14.6% of patients. Hypertension has previously been classified as an important identified risk and lenvatinib should be administered with caution in patients with hypertension, as per the product label [13]. Additionally, the average daily dose of lenvatinib and nonchemotherapy treatment history were deemed not clinically significant. Importantly, expert guidance provides strategies for the management of lenvatinib-associated AEs to ensure uninterrupted treatment and maximum survival benefit [14]. In clinical practice, hypertension is the most common AE associated with lenvatinib treatment; hence, blood pressure should be controlled prior to lenvatinib treatment and monitored during treatment to minimize lenvatinib dose modification and maximize survival benefit [14].

The effectiveness of lenvatinib based on mRECIST criteria was demonstrated in 161 assessable patients. CR, PR and ORR were 1.9%, 14.9% and 16.8%, respectively. In comparison, in the REFLECT trial, CR, PR and ORR by investigator review according to mRECIST were 1, 23 and 24%, respectively [9], while in a hospital-based retrospective study in 111 uHCC patients in Korea, CR, PR and ORR based on mRECIST were 0.9, 18.0 and 18.9%, respectively [19]. Considering that the REFLECT trial was conducted under controlled conditions with unrestricted follow-up duration, whereas this PMS study involved real-world usage with a maximum follow-up of 1 year, and considering the nature of chemotherapy where treatment discontinuation occurs with disease progression, it was observed that the tendency of ORR increased as the administration period of the study medication increased (p < 0.001). This is consistent with the findings in clinical trials, confirming that the effectiveness of the study medication is significant in real-world usage. Notably, effectiveness analysis by special populations (elderly, renal impairment and hepatic impairment) showed no statistically significant differences in ORRs.

There are several limitations associated with this multicenter PMS study, including the role of the study investigators in enrolling patients and data collection, which may have resulted in inconsistencies between study centers. Additionally, there can be inconsistency in the real-world setting regarding the imaging for mRECIST and RECIST 1.1, and the data collection period was limited to 12 months. Nevertheless, the study included a large number of patients at multiple centers, and the safety and effectiveness results provide evidence of the outcomes of lenvatinib in the real-world clinical setting in Korea and are in agreement with recent studies in routine clinical practice in Japan [20] and the USA [22].

Conclusion

In conclusion, this PMS study in the real-world clinical setting in Korea supports the data from the REFLECT clinical trial. Lenvatinib has a manageable tolerability profile and provides clinically meaningful effectiveness as first-line treatment for patients with uHCC.
