Time of day of CAR T-cell infusion and outcomes in large B-cell lymphoma∗

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Earlier infusion time of CAR T-cell therapy was independently associated with improved PFS in large B-cell lymphoma.•These findings support the prospective evaluation of circadian-informed CAR T-cell delivery.

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy has transformed care for relapsed or refractory large B-cell lymphoma (LBCL), yet outcomes and toxicities remain heterogeneous, motivating the search for factors that influence efficacy and safety. Circadian biology, which governs the endogenous circadian timing system, regulates multiple layers of antitumor immunity.1,2 Rhythmic cues shape leukocyte trafficking,3 activation states,4, 5, 6 and the tumor microenvironment,7, 8, 9 providing a mechanistic basis to consider time of day in adoptive cellular therapy. Preclinical work indicates that antitumor T-cell immunity oscillates across the circadian cycle, reflected by phase-specific differences in CD8+ tumor infiltration and function. In murine models, aligning treatment with these windows improves tumor control. Furthermore, adoptive T-cell therapies, including CAR T-cell therapy, also display timing-dependent performance in vivo.7 Clinical observations from immune checkpoint inhibitors support this concept,10,11 with meta-analyses reporting better outcomes associated with earlier time-of-day administration,12 although causality has yet to be established. Although preclinical and early exploratory observations hint at the circadian modulation of CAR T-cell function,7,13 its impact in clinical practice remains undefined. Therefore, we conducted a multicenter retrospective study to evaluate whether within-day infusion timing is associated with outcomes following CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy for LBCL.

Study design

We included adults with primary or transformed LBCL treated with CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy (axicabtagene ciloleucel [axi-cel], tisagenlecleucel [tisa-cel], or lisocabtagene maraleucel [liso-cel]) across 7 international centers between 2017 and 2025. The primary exposure was the time of CAR T-cell infusion, abstracted from electronic health records and verified manually. Time of infusion was modeled as both a continuous and dichotomous variable (early [before 12:00 noon, hereafter “<12:00 noon”] vs late [at or after 12:00 noon, hereafter “≥12:00 noon”]). The continuous analysis treated infusion time as hours from 9:00 am, reflecting the observed range of administration (9:00 am to 6:45 pm). The primary end points were progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the time from infusion to relapse, progression, or death, and overall survival (OS), defined as the time from infusion to death from any cause. Additional outcomes and detailed definitions are provided in the supplemental Methods (available on the Blood website).

Correlative analyses

Inflammatory biomarkers (ferritin, C-reactive protein [CRP], and interleukin-6 [IL-6]) were measured at baseline and postinfusion using institutional clinical assays. Lymphocyte subsets (CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+)14 were quantified by flow cytometry at serial time points. CAR T-cell expansion was assessed using recombinant human CD19-immunoglobulin–based flow cytometry on day +7 after infusion.15

Statistical analysis

Associations between time of infusion and primary outcomes of PFS and OS were analyzed using multivariable Cox proportional hazards modeling. Center was treated as a stratification factor because it allows for the baseline hazard function to vary by center, thereby better accounting for intrinsic center-specific differences. Multivariable linear regression models were generated examining the relationship between time of infusion and CAR T-cell expansion. All statistical analyses were performed with R version 4.4.0. Institutional review boards at all sites approved the study, which was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results and discussion

A total of 1052 adult patients from 7 sites were included. The median age was 66 years (interquartile range [IQR], 57-73), and 57.0% received axi-cel, with slightly more use of tisa-cel compared with liso-cel (25.3% vs 17.7%, respectively; supplemental Table 1). Most patients (62.0%) received at least 2 treatment lines before apheresis. Median follow-up from CAR T-cell infusion was 21.6 months (IQR, 8.8-42.1).

The median infusion time was 11:48 am (IQR, 11:06 am to 12:45 pm; range, 9:00 am to 6:45 pm). Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, Karnofsky performance scale score, lymphoma subtype, and prior therapy lines, were generally balanced between early (<12:00 noon) and late (≥12:00 noon) infusion groups (Table 1). Notable differences included a higher prevalence of elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level before lymphodepletion (P = .001) and less frequent use of bridging therapy (P < .001) in the late group compared with the early group. Axi-cel was administered to 54.9% of the patients in the early group and 59.6% in the late group (P = .202).

One-year PFS was 51.4% (95% confidence interval [CI], 42.8-61.7) for early infusions vs 35.2% (95% CI, 25.5-48.6) for late infusions (Figure 1A). In multivariable Cox regression, each hour later in infusion time was associated with a higher risk of a PFS event (hazard ratio per hour, 1.11; 95% CI, 1.03-1.20; P = .004; Figure 1C; supplemental Table 2). The association remained robust across models incorporating measures of tumor burden and systemic inflammation (supplemental Table 2). Exploratory spline analyses demonstrated an increasing hazard of PFS and OS with later infusion times (supplemental Figure 2A-B), and PFS analyses by quartiles of infusion time yielded concordant results (supplemental Figure 2C). Sensitivity analyses further supported this relationship, with subgroup analyses stratified by CAR T-cell product, geographic region, and treatment line showing a consistent direction of effect (supplemental Table 3).

One-year OS was 54.3% (95% CI, 45.8-64.3) vs 55.8% (95% CI, 45.4-68.5) for early and late infusions, respectively (Figure 1B). In multivariable analysis, the adjusted per-hour hazard ratio for death was 1.04 (95% CI, 0.95-1.13; P = .414); no consistent association was observed in alternative models (Figure 1C; supplemental Table 2).

In competing risk analyses, early infusion was associated with a lower cumulative incidence of relapse (P = .027) but showed no difference in nonrelapse mortality (P = .251; Figure 1D-E). Median time to relapse or progression after CAR T-cell therapy was 18.0 months (IQR, 2.6 to not reached) for early infusions and 8.4 months (IQR, 2.0 to not reached) for late infusions. Earlier infusion was also associated with higher best overall response and complete response rates within 100 days (P = .010 for both, Figure 1F). The rates of grade ≥2 cytokine release syndrome, immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome, and immune effector cell-associated hematotoxicity were comparable between the infusion groups (supplemental Table 4; supplemental Figure 1A-D).

Systemic inflammation and diminished CAR T-cell expansion are recognized drivers of poor CAR T-cell outcomes16, 17, 18 and were therefore evaluated in relation to infusion timing. Baseline and peak postinfusion ferritin levels were significantly higher in the late-infusion group, and peak IL-6 was likewise elevated (Figure 2A-C; supplemental Table 5). In multivariable models additionally adjusting for baseline ferritin, CRP, or the InflaMix inflammatory signature,17 the association between infusion time and PFS remained significant (supplemental Table 2; Figure 1C). When examining day 7 CAR T-cell expansion, each hour later in infusion time was associated with a decrease of 0.12 (95% CI, ˗0.23 to 0.00; P = .05) in log-transformed CAR T-cell counts (Figure 2D). In terms of endogenous T-cell counts, no consistent differences were observed in CD3+, CD4+, or CD8+ counts at day 30 or 100 between early and late infusions across products (supplemental Figure 3). All these findings suggest that later infusion coincides with a more inflammatory systemic milieu and attenuated CAR T-cell expansion, potentially contributing to inferior efficacy.

In this large, multicenter international retrospective study, later time of day of CAR T-cell infusion was associated with inferior PFS in patients with LBCL, along with significantly worse overall response and complete response rates, suggesting that time of day may affect depth of response to CAR T-cell treatment. The lack of OS benefit despite a robust PFS benefit may reflect improvement in the management of relapsed LBCL, including the increasing availability of effective salvage options.19 Patients infused later in the day also had higher baseline and peak levels of ferritin, LDH, and IL-6, consistent with a more inflammatory milieu at the time of treatment. Prior studies have shown that heightened systemic inflammation before CAR T-cell infusion is detrimental and associated with inferior disease control.17,18,20,21 Our findings suggest that later infusion times may intersect with this inflammatory state to negatively influence outcomes. Interestingly, we did not identify differences in toxicity profile, including cytokine release syndrome, immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome, and immune effector cell-associated hematotoxicity, which suggest that timing-related inflammatory variation influences efficacy rather than acute toxicity. However, we did find that in a subset of patients with available data on CAR T-cell expansion, later time of infusion was robustly associated with diminished CAR T-cell expansion, potentially providing yet another explanation for worse PFS outcomes. These observations align with preclinical data demonstrating circadian regulation of immune cell trafficking, activation, and antitumor function.3,7,22 In animal models, adoptive T-cell therapies, including CAR T cells, are most effective when delivered during favorable circadian phases, leading to improved tumor infiltration and tumor control.7 All these data provide both biological plausibility and clinical evidence that circadian timing may shape CAR T-cell efficacy.

Several limitations warrant emphasis. This was a retrospective analysis subject to residual confounding, including differences in disease burden, treatment sequencing, and institutional workflows that may influence scheduling. Patients receiving late infusions more frequently had elevated LDH and higher inflammatory marker levels, which could partially mediate the observed associations. Furthermore, peak inflammation is likely correlated with baseline inflammation, which presents a potential source of confounding. Delays in infusion timing may also be driven by patient-related factors such as acute clinical issues, imaging or transfusion needs, or other interventions, which could not be fully accounted for in this analysis. Extremely late infusions were rare, limiting the precision of estimates beyond midafternoon. Furthermore, clock time does not account for individual circadian phase, which can be altered by illness, hospitalization, or prior therapy.23 Importantly, causality cannot be inferred from these findings, and it remains unclear whether optimizing infusion timing ultimately improves clinical outcomes. Even though we performed multiple sensitivity analyses and rigorous multivariable adjustments, all of which reinforce the robustness of the association between later infusion time and inferior PFS, these limitations nevertheless underscore the need for prospective validation.

In summary, these data support the concept of chronotherapy, aligning treatment delivery with endogenous biological rhythms, as a strategy to enhance CAR T-cell efficacy. Optimizing infusion timing could represent a low-cost, readily implementable intervention, but several key questions remain: whether time-of-day effects persist in randomized trials, how lymphodepletion regimens interact with circadian immune modulation, and whether pharmacological targeting of circadian regulators (eg, BMAL1, REV-ERBs, and RORγ) can further improve efficacy.24,25
