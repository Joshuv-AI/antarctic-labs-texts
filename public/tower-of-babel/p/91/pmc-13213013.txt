Pregnancy Outcomes in Women With Valvular Heart Disease: Insights From 30 Years of Single-Center Experience
Authors: Heayoung Shin, Sung-Ji Park, Soo-young Oh, Jihee Son, Jinyoung Lee, Seung Hyeok Bang, Jihoon Kim, Eun Kyoung Kim, Sang-Chol Lee, Seung Woo Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundValvular heart disease (VHD) during pregnancy is associated with significant maternal and neonatal risks. While large-scale cohort studies have documented these risks in western populations, evidence in Korean women remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate maternal and neonatal outcomes by VHD severity and identify predictors of adverse events to inform risk assessment.MethodsThis retrospective study included 138 pregnant women with VHD who delivered at Samsung Medical Center between December 1994 and June 2023. Patients were stratified by VHD severity. The primary outcomes were adverse maternal outcomes, defined as maternal death or pregnancy-related complications requiring hospitalization, and adverse neonatal events, defined as neonatal intensive care unit admission or fetal death.ResultsMitral regurgitation was the most common lesion (47.8%). The incidence of adverse maternal outcomes increased with VHD severity (P = 0.007), mainly driven by heart failure hospitalizations. In age-adjusted Cox models, moderate (hazard ratio [HR], 3.6) and severe VHD (HR, 9.8) were associated with higher maternal risk. Independent predictors of adverse maternal outcomes in multivariable analysis included New York Heart Association (NYHA) ≥ 2 in the first trimester (odds ratio [OR], 3.27; P = 0.028), prior hospitalization for acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF) before pregnancy (OR, 6.59; P = 0.002), and moderate (OR, 6.27; P = 0.019) or severe VHD (OR, 7.36; P = 0.025). For neonatal outcomes, NYHA ≥ 2 (OR, 3.75; P = 0.026) and ADHF hospitalization during pregnancy (OR, 3.87; P = 0.029) were significant predictors.ConclusionVHD severity was associated with maternal heart failure hospitalization, but functional status and prior decompensation were equally important. These findings highlight the importance of pre-pregnancy evaluation and structured perinatal care. Multidisciplinary management may reduce complications, even in high-risk patients.Keywords: Valvular Heart Disease, Mitral Regurgitation, Heart Failure, Pregnancy

INTRODUCTION

Valvular heart disease (VHD) during pregnancy presents significant challenges to maternal and neonatal health. The prevalence of VHD varies globally, affecting approximately 1–2% of reproductive-age women. According to the European Registry Of Pregnancy And Cardiac disease (ROPAC), VHD represents 25% of all cardiac disease during pregnancy.1,2,3 VHD prevalence and outcomes differ across regions due to variations in healthcare systems, disease etiology, and genetic predisposition. While extensive research from western countries has provided critical insights, data from Korean populations remain limited, underscoring the need for region-specific data to inform global clinical practice. During pregnancy, blood volume, heart rate and cardiac output increase, leading to both volume and pressure overload.4 The added circulatory load from the uteroplacental circulation increases the hemodynamic burden and raises the risk of cardiac decompensation in women with pre-existing VHD, particularly in those with impaired left ventricular (LV) function or severe valvular stenosis. Previously asymptomatic patients may develop heart failure or arrhythmias and are at increased risk of maternal mortality during pregnancy or delivery.3,5 The ROPAC study reported arrhythmias in 2% of pregnant women with heart disease and a maternal mortality rate of 0.6%.2 Heart failure rates were particularly high, ranging from 23.1% to 48.1% in mitral stenosis and from 9% to 44% in aortic stenosis.3,6 Furthermore, 13% of pregnant women with moderate or severe regurgitative valve disease experienced cardiac complications, which were associated with adverse neonatal outcomes including intrauterine growth restriction, premature birth, and neonatal death.1,7

The American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines emphasize preconception counselling and recommend considering valve intervention before pregnancy based on disease severity.1 Although numerous studies have examined VHD-related pregnancy outcomes, most available data are derived from western populations. Despite well-established international guidelines from major societies such as the ACC/AHA and the European Society of Cardiology, population-specific recommendations are lacking in Korea. As a result, clinical practice in Korea largely relies on western standards, highlighting the urgent need for region-specific evidence to guide care.

This 30-year, single-center analysis aimed to evaluate maternal and neonatal outcomes in pregnant women with VHD in Korea, stratified by disease severity, and to identify key predictors of adverse events.

METHODS

Study population and data collection

This retrospective single-center cohort study included pregnant women diagnosed with VHD who delivered at Samsung Medical Center between December 1994 and June 2023. Eligible patients had a VHD diagnosed before or during pregnancy, underwent echocardiography before delivery and had complete maternal and neonatal outcome data available. Data were collected from electronic medical records, including baseline clinical presentations, echocardiographic parameters, treatment interventions, and pregnancy outcomes. Exclusion criteria included minimal VHD, defined as clinically insignificant valvular lesions, as well as diagnosis of VHD after delivery, postpartum cardiomyopathy, prior heart transplantation, refusal to participate, or incomplete echocardiographic data. A detailed patient selection process and study flow chart are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Definitions

Baseline characteristics included comorbidities, surgical history, anticoagulation status and pre-pregnancy arrhythmia. Functional status was defined by New York Heart Association (NYHA) class in the first trimester. VHD severity was determined from the earliest echocardiographic evaluation during pregnancy, or the most recent pre-pregnancy study if unavailable, and was categorized as mild, moderate, or severe based on current guidelines. For patients with prosthetic valves, severity was assessed based on prosthetic valve function parameters.1

Hospitalization for acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF) prior to pregnancy was defined as hospitalization requiring intravenous diuretics or inotropic support with objective evidence of cardiac dysfunction, accompanied by dyspnea, peripheral edema or fatigue and supportive findings such as pulmonary congestion on chest radiography, elevated N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide or echocardiographic signs of dysfunction.

Heart failure hospitalization during pregnancy was defined as any admission during pregnancy or within 14 days postpartum for acute heart failure requiring intravenous therapy.

Thromboembolism was defined as any embolic event including systemic arterial events such as ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack, as well as venous thromboembolic events including pulmonary embolism.

Maternal death was defined, according to AHA criteria, as death during pregnancy or within 42 days postpartum due to pregnancy-related causes excluding accidental events.8

Neonatal outcomes were defined as preterm birth (< 37 weeks),9 miscarriage (< 20 weeks or < 500 g),10 stillbirth (≥ 20 weeks), low birth weight (< 2,500 g),11,12 small for gestational age (SGA) (< 10th percentile for gestational age [GA]),13,14 and a 5-minute Apgar score < 7.15,16,17

Multidisciplinary care pathway and surveillance

Pregnant patients with VHD were co-managed in a standardized cardio-obstetric pathway by cardiology and maternal–fetal medicine with anesthesia and neonatology input. Visit cadence and surveillance were risk-stratified: mild/NYHA I—each trimester; moderate or NYHA II—every 4–6 weeks then every 2–4 weeks ≥ 28 weeks; severe or NYHA ≥ II—every 2–4 weeks, then weekly/biweekly ≥ 32 weeks. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed at baseline, mid-gestation and late third trimester with additional studies for new or worsening symptoms. Fetal assessment included routine anatomy scan, serial growth and Dopplers, and non-stress testing from 32 weeks in high-risk cases. Delivery planning was finalized at 32–34 weeks in a multidisciplinary conference, and high-risk women (those with severe stenosis, NYHA ≥ II, recent ADHF, or mechanical valves) were electively admitted at 36–37 weeks for optimization. Full operational details are provided in Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Table 1.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were adverse maternal and neonatal events. Adverse maternal outcomes were defined as a composite of maternal death or pregnancy-related complications requiring hospitalization, including heart failure, thromboembolism or uncontrolled arrhythmia occurring during pregnancy or within 14 days postpartum. Adverse neonatal outcomes were defined as a composite of neonatal death at any GA or the need for admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). Secondary outcomes included maternal intensive care unit (ICU) admission during pregnancy, a 5-minute Apgar score below 7 and SGA.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Group comparisons were conducted using analysis of variance, Kruskal–Wallis, χ2, or Fisher’s exact tests. To identify predictors of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, logistic regression analysis was used. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. The cumulative incidence of primary outcomes during pregnancy was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was also performed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for adverse maternal outcomes according to VHD severity, with adjustment for maternal age as a potential confounder, considering its known association with pregnancy outcomes, including neonatal complications.18

To evaluate temporal trends, we categorized calendar time into three eras (1994–2003, 2004–2013, 2014–2023) and compared outcome rates using χ2 tests for trend and multivariable logistic regression including era and VHD severity; sensitivity models further adjusted for age, NYHA class, and pre-pregnancy ADHF.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Samsung Medical Center and the requirement for informed consent was waived (IRB SMC 2025-03-136).

RESULTS

Baseline clinical and echocardiographic characteristics

Among 276 pregnant women with VHD, 138 met the inclusion criteria, including 46 (33.3%) with mild VHD, 67 (48.6%) with moderate VHD and 25 (18.1%) with severe VHD. The median age was 32 years (IQR, 29–35). The cohort included 58.7% multigravida women, of whom 18 had a history of miscarriages only, without any prior deliveries.

The most common valvular lesion was mitral regurgitation (47.8%), followed by mitral stenosis (16.7%), aortic stenosis (9.4%), aortic regurgitation (8.7%), tricuspid regurgitation (8.7%), pulmonary regurgitation (6.5%) and pulmonary stenosis (2.2%). Of the 25 patients with severe VHD, mitral regurgitation was most common, followed by pulmonary regurgitation, aortic regurgitation, and mitral stenosis, aortic stenosis and tricuspid regurgitation and pulmonary stenosis (Supplementary Table 2).

Normal sinus rhythm was present in 89.9% of patients, while 7.2% had atrial fibrillation (Supplementary Table 3). A history of hospitalization for ADHF before pregnancy was observed in 13% of patients, without significant differences across VHD severity groups (mild 10.9%, moderate 11.9%, severe 20.0%, P = 0.514). Previous valve repair or replacement had been performed in 31.9% of patients, including mechanical valve replacement in 5.8% and bioprosthetic valve replacement in 5.1%.

The median GA at echocardiography was 21.6 weeks (IQR, 5.5–32.3). The median LV ejection fraction was 60% (IQR, 56–63) with a significant overall difference among groups (P = 0.012). Post hoc analysis revealed a significant difference in LV ejection fraction only between the mild and moderate VHD groups (P = 0.005), while no significant differences were observed among the other groups. NYHA class ≥ 2 during the first trimester was more prevalent in the severe VHD group (76%) compared to the moderate (29.8%) and mild (15.2%) groups (P < 0.001). There were no significant intergroup differences in the frequency of cardiology visits, obstetric ultrasound evaluations or follow-up echocardiography during pregnancy (all P > 0.05, Table 1).

Maternal outcomes

Adverse maternal outcomes were significantly more frequent with increasing VHD severity (P = 0.008), with the highest incidence observed in patients with severe VHD (36%). This difference was primarily attributable to hospitalizations for worsening heart failure (P = 0.016). These hospitalizations tended to occur in the late third trimester, with a median GA of 37.5 weeks (IQR, 33.1–39.5). Most events occurred during antepartum period, particularly in the severe VHD group (Supplementary Fig. 2). The median GA at delivery was 38.7 weeks (IQR, 37.7–39.8, range 17.1–41.3) across groups without significant differences. The need for ICU management during pregnancy or early postpartum period was significantly higher in the severe VHD group (24.0%) than in the mild (2.2%) and moderate (3.0%) groups (P < 0.001).

Neonatal outcomes

Adverse neonatal outcomes occurred in 14.5% of cases, increasing with VHD severity (mild 6.5%, moderate 14.9%, and severe 28.0%; P = 0.049). Preterm birth before 37 weeks occurred in 13% overall without significant group differences (P = 0.254), while preterm birth before 34 weeks (5.8%) and before 28 weeks (1.4%) were confined to the severe group. NICU admission was required in 11.3% of cases and tended to be more frequent with increasing severity (P = 0.096). Fetal death occurred in 3.6%, highest in the severe group (8.0%). The prevalence of SGA infants was also greatest in the severe group (26.1%). A 5-minute Apgar score below 7 was most frequently observed in the severe VHD group (4.3%), although group differences were not statistically significant (P = 0.738) (Table 2).

Temporal trends over 30 years

We evaluated adverse maternal outcomes across three eras (1994–2003, 2004–2013, 2014–2023), stratified by VHD severity. Although the distribution of severity appeared to vary over time, the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.055). The overall rate of adverse maternal outcomes increased significantly over time—from 6.8% to 20.8% and 29.4% (P = 0.047)—with the highest incidence among severe VHD in 2014–2023 (62.5%). Maternal age rose across eras (medians 29.0, 32.5, 35.0 years). Among severe VHD, both maternal age and pre-pregnancy ADHF hospitalization varied significantly over time (P = 0.030 and P = 0.026, respectively; Supplementary Table 4).

In multivariable logistic regression adjusted for VHD severity, both the second and third eras were independently associated with higher odds of adverse maternal outcomes versus the first era (odds ratio [OR], 4.67; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.33–22.19; P = 0.027; and OR, 7.00; 95% CI, 1.84–35.05; P = 0.008, respectively). Although the point estimate was higher in the third versus second era, this difference was not statistically significant (OR, 1.50; 95% CI, 0.54–4.19; P = 0.437; Supplementary Table 5).

Outcomes in severe VHD cases

Among the patients with severe VHD during pregnancy, eight had stenotic lesions and 17 had regurgitant lesions. Severe mitral stenosis cases (n = 4) were frequently complicated by maternal decompensation, with three patients requiring hospitalization and ICU care. Most underwent valve replacement after delivery, and adverse neonatal outcomes included one intrauterine fetal death and one SGA infant. Patients with severe aortic stenosis (n = 3) generally tolerated pregnancy well, although one required valve replacement postpartum. One patient with severe pulmonary stenosis was hospitalized for heart failure but delivered electively at term, and the neonate required NICU management. Among the 17 patients with severe regurgitant lesions, five were hospitalized for heart failure, three of whom required ICU care. One experienced miscarriage, and several neonates required NICU care. Collectively, these findings suggest that severe stenotic lesions, particularly mitral stenosis and prosthetic valves, were associated with higher maternal morbidity, while regurgitant lesions were more often linked to adverse neonatal outcomes.

Predictors of adverse outcomes

The cumulative incidence of adverse maternal outcomes differed across severity groups (P = 0.007), driven by the difference between mild and severe VHD, while mild–moderate and moderate–severe comparisons were not significant.

In multivariate Cox regression adjusted for age, both moderate and severe VHD were independently associated with increased maternal risk compared with mild VHD (moderate: HR, 3.60; 95% CI, 1.03–12.53; P = 0.044; severe: HR, 9.79; 95% CI, 2.57–37.35; P < 0.001), and severe VHD also carried higher risk than moderate VHD (HR, 2.72; 95% CI, 1.16–6.38; P = 0.021; Fig. 1). In multivariable logistic regression analysis, each one-year increase in maternal age was associated with higher risk (OR, 1.19; 95% CI, 1.06–1.37; P = 0.005). NYHA class ≥ 2 in the first trimester (OR, 3.27; 95% CI, 1.15–9.70; P = 0.028) and prior hospitalization for ADHF (OR, 6.59; 95% CI, 2.00–23.40; P = 0.002) were also independent predictors. DISCUSSION

This study provides novel insights into the association between VHD severity and pregnancy outcomes in a Korean population, addressing a critical gap in research focused on Korean cohorts.

Large international registries such as ROPAC and Cardiac Disease in Pregnancy Study (CARPREG) II have established lesion-specific risks during pregnancy.3,16,19 However, their populations are predominantly western and drawn from heterogeneous practice environments, limiting direct translation to single-system care. Previous Korean studies have been limited by small sample sizes, short follow-up, and lack of functional assessment.20 In contrast, our 30-year, single-center cardio-obstetric cohort provides standardized surveillance and delivery planning. Within this setting, maternal adverse events increased stepwise with anatomic severity, but functional status and heart failure hospitalization whether before or during pregnancy were stronger predictors of both maternal and neonatal outcomes. This hierarchy of predictors extends prior registry findings by showing in Korean patients that maternal hemodynamics primarily drive neonatal risk, while anatomical severity mainly predicts maternal heart-failure hospitalization.

Our data also refine the temporal profile of risk by demonstrating late-third-trimester clustering of heart-failure admissions (median 37.5 weeks), aligning pathophysiologically with peak volume/pressure load, and occurring without maternal mortality under a standardized multidisciplinary pathway. Finally, we quantify delivery-related correlates—emergency delivery and GA—as potential drivers of neonatal compromise, whereas anatomic severity alone was not. Collectively, these findings build on ROPAC/CARPREG by i) clarifying the relative prognostic weight of functional versus anatomic markers in a Korean care system, ii) delineating timing of decompensation within a consistent management pathway, and iii) translating these signals into actionable risk stratification (pre-pregnancy counselling and third-trimester intensification) that can be adopted in similar multidisciplinary settings.

For everyday care, these results support a two-axis risk model: use anatomic severity to anticipate maternal decompensation risk and use functional and hemodynamic markers such as NYHA class or a prior history of ADHF hospitalization to anticipate both maternal and neonatal risk and guide surveillance intensity and delivery timing. This clarifies the application of guideline principles in cardio-obstetric practice and may be particularly relevant for systems with coordinated multidisciplinary care.

The strong association between VHD severity and adverse maternal outcomes reflects the limited cardiac reserve in these patients, which impairs their ability to accommodate the hemodynamic demands of pregnancy. In this study, heart failure hospitalizations were concentrated in the late third trimester, sustained volume overload rather than acute decompensation was the main driver of adverse events.3,21,22

The incidence of cardiovascular complications increased with severity, as reflected by higher ICU needs in the severe group, although emergency cesarean section rates were similar across groups. Arrhythmias and thromboembolic events were rare, but anticoagulation management in mechanical prosthetic valves remained a critical issue, underscoring the importance of individualized strategies in this subgroup.

Functional parameters were as important as anatomical severity in predicting outcomes. NYHA class ≥ 2 in the first trimester and prior ADHF hospitalization were strong predictors of maternal complications, supporting the prognostic value of combining anatomical and functional assessments, consistent with previous large cohorts.2,19 This reinforces current guideline principles advocating symptom-based intervention and highlights the utility of comprehensive preconception counselling and intensive perinatal surveillance. The absence of maternal mortality even in this high-risk cohort further supports the effectiveness of such a structured approach.

Over three decades, adverse maternal events in severe VHD patients increased despite a stable proportion of severe cases. This trend likely reflects older maternal age and greater functional compromise in recent years, coupled with a greater willingness to manage high-risk pregnancies within a multidisciplinary system. These findings emphasize that anatomical severity alone cannot explain rising risks, and that functional vulnerability is increasingly relevant in contemporary practice.

Adverse neonatal outcomes including fetal death or NICU admission were more closely associated with maternal functional status, such as NYHA class or heart failure hospitalizations during pregnancy, than with anatomical VHD severity. With increasing VHD severity, there was a trend toward higher rates of SGA infants and need for NICU management, suggesting that impaired uteroplacental perfusion plays a central role in fetal growth restriction. Prior studies support this link between maternal cardiac dysfunction and placental perfusion deficits.5,23 These findings emphasize the importance of pre-pregnancy optimization and intensive maternal–fetal surveillance, including continuous monitoring of placental function and fetal growth.

Compared with national data from the Korean Statistical Office, our cohort showed a relatively lower rate of preterm birth even among severe VHD cases, likely reflecting the benefits of intensive maternal–fetal surveillance and multidisciplinary management.24 Rather than anatomic severity itself, maternal hemodynamic status emerged as the key determinant of neonatal outcomes, emphasizing the need for pre-pregnancy optimization and close hemodynamic monitoring to improve neonatal prognosis. Further research is warranted to establish evidence-based protocols for delivery timing and perinatal management in this population.

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size, especially after stratification by severity, may have limited statistical power to detect differences. Second, as a retrospective single-center study, there is potential for selection bias with an overrepresentation of severe cases, which may limit generalizability. Third, the absence of long-term follow-up data precluded assessment of disease progression or the cumulative impact of repeated pregnancies. Fourth, variations in the timing of echocardiographic examinations during pregnancy may have introduced measurement bias, as GA–related hemodynamic changes could affect severity assessment. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable clinical insights as the largest domestic cohort study analyzing 30 years of data on pregnancy outcomes in women with VHD.

This 30-year analysis demonstrates that maternal functional status and prior heart failure hospitalization are stronger predictors of adverse pregnancy outcomes than anatomical VHD severity. These findings highlight the importance of integrating both anatomical and functional assessments into preconception risk stratification and pregnancy management. Multidisciplinary care and close third-trimester monitoring were effective in preventing mortality, even in high-risk patients. Future research should focus on developing risk models tailored to Korean populations to optimize outcomes in VHD during pregnancy.
