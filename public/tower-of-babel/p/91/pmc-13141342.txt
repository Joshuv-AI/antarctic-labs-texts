UAV-based mangrove biomass estimation and carbon potential valuation in North Sumatra, Indonesia
Authors: Mohammad Basyuni, Ida Liana Tanjung, Bejo Slamet, Faujiah Nurhasanah Ritonga, Shofiyah Sabilah Al Mustaniroh, Siti Halimah Larekekng, Virni Budi Arifanti, Sigit Bayhu Iryanthony, Elham Sumarga, Shigeyuki Baba, Severino G. Salmo III, Edina Ginting, Novita Kusumawardani, Reiko Omoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mangrove forests are among the most carbon-dense coastal ecosystems, yet ongoing conversion to agriculture and oil palm plantations significantly reduces landscape-scale carbon storage and climate mitigation potential. This study quantified aboveground biomass (AGB) and carbon stocks across six land-use types within Karang Gading and Langkat Timur Laut Wildlife Reserve, North Sumatra, Indonesia, covering 928.42 ha. High-resolution unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV)–derived canopy height models were integrated with field-calibrated allometric equations, using basal area–weighted Lorey’s height to improve biomass estimation in structurally heterogeneous stands. Bias correction was applied to log-transformed models to minimize systematic error. Mean AGB ranged from 0.97 Mg ha⁻¹ in oil palm plantations to 278.54 Mg ha⁻¹ in natural mangroves. Corresponding aboveground carbon (AGC) stocks ranged from 0.46 to 130.92 Mg C ha⁻¹. Natural mangroves (92.82 ha) stored 12,151.99 Mg C (44,597.11 Mg CO₂e), while community forests (98 ha) stored 10,541.86 Mg C. In contrast, mixed agriculture and oil palm systems together contributed less than 2% of total landscape-level aboveground carbon. Under a conservative avoided deforestation scenario, total CO₂-equivalent stocks correspond to an indicative economic value of approximately USD 0.63–6.35 million at voluntary carbon market prices of USD 5–50 Mg CO₂e⁻¹. Although these values represent biophysical carbon stocks rather than verified emission reductions, the integration of UAV-derived structural metrics with field validation provides a robust and scalable framework for spatially explicit carbon accounting. These findings strengthen the empirical basis for evaluating land-use impacts on coastal carbon balance and support mitigation-oriented conservation and restoration planning in tropical mangrove landscapes.

Introduction

Mangrove ecosystems are among the most carbon-dense forest types globally and play a critical role in climate mitigation, coastal protection, and biodiversity conservation [1, 2]. Indonesia, which hosts the largest mangrove extent worldwide, is therefore central to global blue carbon conservation efforts. However, rapid land-use changes, including conversion to aquaculture and oil palm, have led to widespread mangrove degradation, particularly in tropical coastal regions [3, 4]. Accurate, spatially explicit estimation of aboveground biomass (AGB) is therefore essential for carbon accounting, restoration monitoring, and climate policy implementation, such as REDD + and blue carbon initiatives.

Conventional forest inventory methods provide reliable biomass measurements but are labor-intensive, spatially limited, and difficult to implement in remote or waterlogged mangrove environments [5–7]. Advances in unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) photogrammetry now enable high-resolution canopy height modeling through structure-from-motion (SfM) techniques. When integrated with allometric equations and structural metrics such as Lorey’s height, UAV-derived canopy models offer a cost-effective and scalable approach for estimating AGB across heterogeneous landscapes [8–13].

Recent UAV applications in Indonesia have demonstrated their effectiveness across diverse mangrove landscapes, including carbon stock mapping in South Sulawesi [14], detection of degradation in North Sumatra [15], and structural differentiation in Karimunjawa, Central Java [11]. High-resolution photogrammetry has enabled estimation of aboveground biomass (AGB) and carbon stocks, canopy structure analysis, tree distribution mapping, and even surface elevation assessment beneath mangrove canopies. Collectively, these studies highlight the precision and cost-efficiency of UAV-based approaches for blue carbon monitoring [16, 17]. However, despite these advances, few studies explicitly link UAV-derived biomass assessments with carbon potential valuation, which is critical for operationalizing blue carbon strategies at local and national scales. Within voluntary carbon and REDD+ frameworks, avoided deforestation is quantified relative to a counterfactual business-as-usual scenario, where forest carbon stocks are assumed to be lost in the absence of conservation intervention, thereby satisfying the principle of additionality — a core principle for carbon credit eligibility, ensuring that credited emission reductions would not have occurred otherwise [18, 19].

This study addresses that gap by applying UAV-based biomass estimation to the Karang Gading and Langkat Timur Laut Wildlife Reserve (KGLTLWR), North Sumatra, Indonesia, a landscape characterized by mosaics of natural mangroves, restored mangroves, community forests, mixed agriculture and converted land to oil palm plantations. Specifically, this study aims to (i) quantify aboveground biomass and carbon stocks across natural mangrove, restored mangrove, community forest, mixed agriculture, and oil palm systems; (ii) evaluate the performance of UAV-derived canopy height metrics combined with allometric models; and (iii) assess the potential carbon asset value associated with avoided deforestation scenarios. By linking high-resolution structural mapping with carbon accounting, this study provides a scalable framework for blue carbon assessment and MRV support in tropical coastal landscapes.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was conducted in Suaka Margasatwa Karang Gading and Langkat Timur Laut (Karang Gading and Langkat Timur Laut Wildlife Reserve), a designated wildlife reserve located in the Langkat and Deli Serdang Regencies, North Sumatra Province, Indonesia between 3°14′00″ and 4°13′00″ N latitude and 97°52′00″ and 98°45′00″ E longitude (Fig. 1). The area represents a diverse coastal ecosystem comprising natural mangrove forests, restored mangroves, a forest community, mixed agriculture and converted land uses such as oil palm plantations [20]. The reserve lies within a lowland deltaic system and is influenced by tidal flows from the Malacca Strait and Wampu River. It plays a critical role in coastal protection, biodiversity conservation, and climate regulation because of its extensive mangrove cover and organic-rich soils.

Field survey

In the in situ mangrove and other land use surveys, tree height and diameter at breast height (DBH) were recorded within purposively selected plots, following established considerations for sample representativeness [15, 21]. A total of 204 vegetation sampling points were established, representing different mangrove conditions: natural mangrove, restored mangrove, community forest, mixed agriculture, and oil palm.

Handheld GPS units generally have lower positional accuracy than high-precision Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) receivers. GNSS receivers were supplemented with careful selection of trees of similar height to reduce potential mismatches between positioning devices. The tree height data were subsequently converted into Lorey’s height, which was calculated as the basal area-weighted mean height of trees within each plot [3, 11, 22]. UAV flight mission and photogrammetry

A series of unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) missions were conducted over a 7-day period, covering a total area of approximately 928.42 hectares. The flights were conducted via a multirotor drone equipped with a high-resolution RGB sensor. The ground sampling distance (GSD) was approximately 5 cm per pixel. The UAV survey was broken into multiple operational blocks, as displayed in Table A1.

UAV photogrammetry was conducted using a DJI Phantom 4 Professional flown at an altitude of 120 m between 1 and 8 August 2023. Flight missions were designed with 90% image overlap to ensure sufficient coverage for accurate three-dimensional reconstruction. The captured imagery was processed using structure-from-motion (SfM) photogrammetry to generate orthophotos, digital surface models (DSMs), digital terrain models (DTMs), and canopy height models (CHMs).as previously described [15].

Data processing followed a structured workflow including image alignment, mosaicking, incorporation of ground control points (GCPs), dense point cloud generation, mesh and texture development, and 3D modeling. DSMs were further refined via inverse distance weighting (IDW) with noise filtering and surface smoothing to increase accuracy. These products clearly distinguish mangrove stands from adjacent land-use types [4].

Terrain elevation was derived via a Comnav T300 GNSS receiver with real-time kinematic (RTK) correction and interpolated into a DTM representing bare-Earth elevation [4, 11]. The canopy height model (CHM) was derived by subtracting the digital terrain model (DTM) from the digital surface model (DSM), producing spatially explicit estimates of vegetation height that are essential for characterizing forest structure, stand volume, and growth patterns [11, 15]. This approach isolates canopy features by removing ground elevation effects, yielding precise measurements of vegetation height at each sampling location [24]. A total of more than 20,000 georeferenced images were captured and processed into orthomosaics and digital surface models (DSMs) via structure-from-motion (SfM) photogrammetry in Agisoft Metashape and ArcGIS Pro.

Allometric biomass, biomass spatial mapping and classification

Three sets of biome-specific allometric equations were used to estimate aboveground biomass (AGB), as depicted in Table 1. For mangrove forests, AGB was estimated using height-based allometric equations developed for Southeast and East Asian mangroves, where tree height has been shown to be a robust predictor of biomass [22]. For oil palm plantations and mixed agriculture or community forest systems, vegetation-type–specific allometric equations from published sources were employed [25, 26].

For vegetation types using logarithmic allometric equations, AGB was first estimated in log-transformed form and subsequently converted to arithmetic units through exponential back-transformation [27]. Because direct exponentiation of log–linear models can introduce systematic bias, a bias correction approach was applied following standard practice in forest biomass estimation [28]. This procedure accounts for non-linearity during back-transformation and improves the reliability of biomass estimates without introducing additional numerical assumptions beyond those defined by the original allometric models, as commonly recommended in tropical forest biomass studies [27, 28].

Because stand-level biomass is disproportionately influenced by larger trees with greater basal area, Lorey’s height was used as the primary canopy metric for UAV-based biomass estimation. Lorey’s height represents the basal area–weighted mean tree height and provides a more reliable proxy for AGB than arithmetic mean height, particularly in uneven-aged, mixed-species, and structurally complex stands such as mangrove ecosystems [22, 29]. Its strong relationship with field-measured biomass supports its application for spatially explicit biomass mapping at the landscape scale [29].

Biomass spatial mapping and classification

Mangrove aboveground biomass (AGB, Mg ha⁻¹) was estimated via the height–biomass relationship developed by [22], which is regionally calibrated for Southeast Asia and East Asia, including sites in North Sumatra, such as Lubuk Kertang and Pulau Sembilan [3, 15] and Budeng-Perancak, Bali [4]. This approach has been successfully applied in other Indonesian mangrove ecosystems, such as Karimunjawa Island [11]. For oil palm plantations, the AGB was calculated via the allometric equation of [25], whereas for the forest community and mixed agriculture, the AGB was estimated via the model of [26].

These allometric estimates were cross-validated against in situ measurements via the root mean square error (RMSE) approach to quantify the error and bias between field-derived and UAV-derived estimates. This comparison ensured consistency and reliability between conventional and drone-based methods (see Table 2 for details).

AGB values were spatially interpolated via ArcGIS Pro (version 3.2) to produce biomass heatmaps for each land cover class. Spatial interpolation of AGB was conducted in ArcGIS Pro using the inverse distance weighting (IDW) method. IDW was selected because it provides a deterministic interpolation approach suitable for high-density point datasets derived from UAV observations, where nearby samples are expected to have greater similarity than distant observations. A power parameter of 2 was applied, which is commonly used in environmental spatial interpolation to balance local influence and surface smoothness. A variable search radius including the 12 nearest neighboring points was used to ensure that interpolation was influenced primarily by nearby observations while maintaining sufficient spatial coverage. The raster cell size was set to 1 m to match the spatial resolution of the UAV-derived canopy height model. Prior to interpolation, noise filtering and surface smoothing were applied to reduce edge effects and improve spatial continuity in the resulting biomass surfaces. The continuous AGB surface was classified into five biomass density categories: very low (< 50 Mg ha⁻¹), low (50–100 Mg ha⁻¹), moderate (100–200 Mg ha⁻¹), high (200–300 Mg ha⁻¹), and very high (> 300 Mg ha⁻¹). These mapping and classification workflows align with established practices in remote sensing biomass studies [30–32]. To further support sampling design and data binning, Tamiminia et al. (2024) [33] described binning and stratified sampling strategies useful for regional canopy height and biomass mapping.

To scale these estimates spatially, UAV-derived digital surface models (DSMs) and canopy height models (CHMs) were integrated into a GIS framework. The per-hectare canopy heights extracted from UAV photogrammetry were converted to AGB via the respective allometric equations and then further transformed into carbon stock maps. This integration allowed for the production of continuous, high-resolution biomass and carbon distribution layers across mangrove, oil palm, and coconut land uses (see Table 1 and GIS outputs). Following the IPCC (2006) Guidelines for National Greenhouse Gas Inventories, aboveground carbon stock (ACS) was derived by applying a biomass-to-carbon conversion factor of 0.47 to the estimated AGB [34].

Calculating the economic value of carbon

In the voluntary carbon market, pricing varies significantly based on project quality. Many standard conservation and restoration projects are benchmarked at approximately USD 5.00 per Mg CO2e [35–38]. In contrast, premium market segments—particularly those targeting high-quality nature-based or blue carbon credits—can command prices exceeding USD 50.00 per Mg CO2e [35–38]. This premium reflects higher standards of additionality, permanence, and environmental co-benefits. For the calculation of potential avoided deforestation and its associated carbon value, this study adopts a conservative additionality assumption consistent with voluntary carbon market and REDD+ principles. Specifically, a counterfactual business-as-usual scenario is assumed, in which existing mangrove and forested areas would be progressively degraded or converted to lower-carbon land uses (e.g., mixed agriculture or oil palm plantations) in the absence of continued protection or conservation-oriented management [39, 40].

Under this baseline, the standing aboveground carbon stock is treated as a proxy for the maximum emissions that could be avoided through effective prevention of deforestation and degradation. This assumption is used solely to indicate potential carbon asset value; demonstration of additionality, leakage control, permanence, and third-party verification under recognized standards was beyond the scope of this study [39, 40].

Results

Spatial distribution of AGB carbon stocks across land uses

UAV-derived biomass data provided robust estimates of aboveground carbon stocks (ACS) across the study area. AGB was estimated using established allometric models combined with high-resolution canopy height metrics derived from UAV imagery. The resulting AGC values ranged from approximately 0.46 Mg C ha⁻¹ in oil palm plantations to 66–130 Mg C ha⁻¹ in natural mangroves in Deli Serdang and natural forest areas in Langkat.

Vegetation structure and biomass varied markedly among the five land-use types (Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7). Natural mangroves had the highest biomass and structural complexity, followed by restored mangroves and community forests, while mixed agriculture and oil palm plantations exhibited substantially lower biomass. This result provides the basal area–weighted mean canopy height, which reflects the influence of larger trees and serves as a reliable proxy for estimating AGB. Biomass density was represented via color gradients, where blue indicates low biomass and red to yellow denotes areas of high biomass.

Overview of UAV-based aboveground biomass estimation studies in mangrove and coastal ecosystems.

Table 4 summarizes previous UAV-based biomass estimation studies conducted in mangrove and coastal ecosystems. Most studies applied Structure-from-Motion photogrammetry to generate canopy height models combined with allometric equations for aboveground biomass estimation. Reported results generally indicate strong correlations between UAV-derived metrics and field measurements, showing that low-cost UAV systems can provide reliable and scalable biomass assessments across diverse coastal landscapes.

Discussion

UAV-derived AGB estimation and distribution

The integration of UAV-based photogrammetry with Lorey’s height and site-specific allometric models has proven to be a powerful method for estimating AGB in mangrove ecosystems. This approach enables rapid, noninvasive, and spatially extensive assessments that are particularly suited to complex and remote environments such as tidal mangrove forests. The observed AGB values in the KGLTLWR ranging from less than 50 Mg ha⁻¹ in degraded zones to more than 350 Mg ha⁻¹ in mature stands are consistent with findings from Eastern Sumatra and Bali, where natural mangroves have shown high carbon storage potential [3, 4, 15].

Compared with the raw canopy height, the Lorey height, which is calculated as the basal area-weighted average tree height, was strongly correlated with the field-measured AGB and significantly reduced the error associated with biomass prediction. This finding supports earlier conclusions by [22], who emphasized the importance of incorporating Lorey’s height in biomass models for uneven-aged forests such as mangroves.

The use of high-resolution UAV imagery in combination with CHM and DTM processing facilitated detailed mapping of forest structure across the reserve. As demonstrated by [3, 45], UAV-based methods are highly suitable for monitoring the ecology of mangroves, offering flexibility in capturing structural variability and assessing restoration outcomes. In our case, restored mangrove areas presented intermediate AGB values, confirming the effectiveness of rehabilitation practices while also highlighting the need for continued monitoring to ensure carbon accumulation over time.

Early work by [42] in Pembrokeshire, UK demonstrated that UAV-derived canopy height models (CHMs) combined with allometric equations could achieve biomass estimation accuracies within ± 10%, indicating that UAVs are a credible alternative to traditional field plots. Similarly [43], reported that incorporating Lorey’s height into a UAV-derived CHM significantly enhanced AGB model performance in Malaysian mangroves. Advances in machine learning were highlighted by [44], who used UAV-based structure-from-motion (SfM) metrics with random forest modeling in China, showing that UAV photogrammetry could effectively substitute for field-based biomass sampling.

Case studies from Indonesia further underscore the effectiveness of UAVs [3]. applied UAV-derived canopy height and species-specific allometries to mangroves in Lubuk Kertang and Pulau Sembilan and reported marked biomass reductions due to illegal logging (− 56%), thereby emphasizing UAVs as monitoring tools for forest degradation. Follow-up studies by [4, 15] reinforced the utility of UAVs in both loss detection and restoration assessment. For example, in Budeng-Perancak, Bali, UAV-derived Lorey’s height and allometry confirmed that restored mangroves (232 Mg ha− 1) had recovered carbon stocks comparable to those of undisturbed stands (239 Mg ha− 1).

Carbon asset value and market interpretation

The estimated carbon stocks represent potential carbon asset values rather than immediately tradable carbon credits and should therefore be interpreted as a baseline for conservation and avoided-deforestation initiatives, consistent with carbon accounting principles under international climate frameworks [18, 19, 46, 47]. UAV-derived biomass estimates provide spatially explicit data that support blue carbon accounting and can contribute to REDD+ Measurement, Reporting, and Verification (MRV) systems as well as ecosystem-based climate mitigation strategies [48, 49].

While the results highlight the economic potential of mangrove carbon—estimated at USD 0.63–6.35 million under voluntary carbon market scenarios—these values remain indicative, as carbon pricing varies depending on project quality, additionality, and market conditions [35–38]. Participation in carbon markets requires compliance with established standards, including demonstration of additionality, long-term monitoring, leakage control, and third-party verification under recognized certification schemes [39, 40]. Moreover, the current estimates consider only aboveground carbon; inclusion of belowground biomass and soil organic carbon—major carbon pools in mangrove ecosystems—could substantially increase total carbon stock estimates and associated economic value [1, 48].

Overall, UAV-based biomass mapping provides a scalable and reliable foundation for integrating ecological carbon assessments with emerging carbon finance mechanisms, although further validation, institutional alignment, and regulatory clarity are necessary for practical market implementation [18, 19, 46, 47, 50].

Policy relevance

Mangrove forests are critical blue carbon ecosystems that contribute to climate change mitigation, biodiversity conservation, and coastal protection [5, 49]. This study demonstrates that UAV-derived data can provide high-resolution estimates of aboveground biomass and carbon stocks, supporting carbon accounting and sustainable land-use planning [51, 52].

The approach offers a practical and scalable solution for regions with limited access to high-cost technology or extensive field surveys [30]. It enables policymakers, land managers, and stakeholders to integrate mangrove conservation into carbon financing mechanisms such as REDD + and voluntary carbon markets [53–55].

However, translating carbon stock estimates into economic value depends on legal and institutional frameworks, particularly in protected areas where carbon rights and benefit-sharing arrangements remain complex [56]. These governance factors influence the feasibility of implementing carbon-based incentives and highlight the need to align scientific assessments with regulatory frameworks [54].

Importantly, this study distinguishes between measured carbon stocks and potential carbon assets. The economic values presented should therefore be interpreted as indicative rather than tradable carbon credits [18]. By linking high-resolution biomass mapping with economic valuation, this study provides a scientific and economic basis for strengthening mangrove conservation. Such information supports evidence-based policy, enhances transparency in carbon accounting, and highlights the long-term benefits of preserving mangrove ecosystems as valuable natural assets [57–59].

Bias and model performance

The higher bias observed in mixed agriculture systems is likely attributable to pronounced canopy heterogeneity and complex stand structures, which weaken the relationship between UAV-derived canopy height metrics and field-measured biomass. Agroforestry and mixed land-use systems often combine trees, shrubs, and crops with contrasting architectures, vertical layering, and species-specific allometric relationships, thereby increasing uncertainty when generalized biomass models are applied. Similar patterns of reduced accuracy and increased bias in heterogeneous landscapes have been reported in previous UAV-based biomass studies, particularly in agroforestry and mixed vegetation systems where structural complexity limits the transferability of height-based models [45, 60].

Limitation and uncertainties

Several sources of uncertainty should be considered. UAV-derived canopy metrics, while providing high spatial resolution, may be influenced by shadowing, occlusion, and limited canopy penetration in dense or multi-layered mangrove stands. These structural factors can affect height-based biomass estimation [61].

Uncertainty associated with allometric equations also remains inherent to biomass studies. The application of generalized or regionally calibrated models may introduce bias when applied to structurally heterogeneous or mixed-species systems. Although log-transformation bias correction was applied, residual model uncertainty cannot be fully eliminated [62].

Finally, the carbon valuation presented in this study represents a potential carbon asset value rather than certified carbon credits. These requirements were beyond the scope of the present analysis and should be addressed in future studies. Despite these limitations, the integration of high-resolution UAV data with extensive field validation provides a robust framework for landscape-scale biomass estimation [18].

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that UAV-derived canopy height models combined with Lorey’s height provide a reliable and scalable approach for estimating aboveground biomass across heterogeneous mangrove and adjacent land-use systems. In the KGLTLWR, the approach captured clear spatial variation in biomass and effectively distinguished between degraded, restored, and intact stands, supporting spatially explicit carbon assessment for management and monitoring purposes.

These findings contribute to improved biomass quantification in structurally complex coastal ecosystems and offer practical value for landscape-level carbon accounting and ecosystem management initiatives. Future research should integrate advanced sensors such as multispectral or LiDAR systems to enhance structural characterization, extend temporal monitoring for restoration assessment, and incorporate additional carbon pools to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of ecosystem carbon dynamics.
