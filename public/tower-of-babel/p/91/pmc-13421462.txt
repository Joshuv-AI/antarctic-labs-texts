Influenza A/PR8 virus infection in mice suppresses basal and Oncostatin M-induced IL-33 expression in vivo and in vitro
Authors: Leila Somani-Davis, Anisha Dubey, Fernando Botelho, Lily Buder, Kyle MacDonald, Matthew Miller, Carl D Richards
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfluenza A virus (IAV) infection typically induces both innate and adaptive immune mechanisms. IL-33, an “alarmin” cytokine with pro-inflammatory effects, may have roles in IAV infection. IL-33 can be upregulated by the gp130 cytokine Oncostatin M (OSM), which induces Th2-skewed inflammation (eosinophils, Arg1 + Macrophages, IL-33, IL-4, eotaxin-2) in mouse lungs. We here examined both OSM and IL-33 regulation by the IAV-H1N1/PR8 strain. Female C57BL/6 mice infected intranasally with H1N1/PR8 showed time-dependent elevation of OSM expression but a reduction in lung IL-33 protein and mRNA. Since this suggested active suppression of IL-33 by H1N1/PR8, we assessed H1N1/PR8 superinfection in mice with established Th2-skewed inflammation induced by Adenoviral vector overexpressing OSM administered 7 days prior. H1N1/PR8 markedly reduced eosinophil and Arg1 + macrophage accumulation, IL-33, IL-4, and eotaxin-2 expression 5-days post infection. IFNγ partially inhibited OSM-induced IL-33 protein in mouse lung epithelial (C10) cells in vitro, and both basal and OSM-induced IL-33 expression were markedly suppressed by direct H1N1/PR8 infection of C10 cells, whereas TIMP-1 or OSMRβ were not. Collectively, IL-33 was selectively suppressed by H1N1/PR8 infection in part by direct effects of virus on IL-33-expressing epithelial cells. Thus, although influenza infection induces OSM, IAV also selectively suppresses IL-33 and IL-33-mediated downstream Th2-skewed inflammation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51196-5.Keywords: Interleukin-33, Influenza, Oncostatin M, EosinophilsSubject terms: Diseases, Immunology, Microbiology, Pathogenesis

Introduction

Influenza A virus (IAV) is an enveloped negative sense single stranded RNA virus that causes an acute infection that is typically resolved in the young and healthy. However, in certain populations, such as the very young and very elderly, IAV can lead to morbidity and mortality, and this is often exacerbated by superinfection with pneumonia-causing bacteria1,2. Globally, IAV is responsible for 1 billion infections and from 290 000 to 650 000 fatalities annually3. Severe IAV immunopathology is the result of an overactive immune response which involves the infiltration of immune cells and production of cytokines4. These responses are necessary for the clearance of a viral infection, but when uncontrolled, can lead to morbidity and mortality. Hypercytokinemia, otherwise known as a cytokine storm, has been observed in human fatalities of both 2009 pandemic H1N1 influenza5 and H5N16. Pro-inflammatory cytokines associated with cytokine storm, such as TNFα, IL-1β, IL-17, and IL-6 have been observed to be elevated in human IAV infection and/or mouse models of H1N1 IAV infection7–12.

Other cytokines may also play important roles in IAV-induced immunopathology. Interleukin (IL)-33 is an alarmin that is predominantly expressed by endothelial and epithelial cells13,14. It is released from the nucleus following necrotic cell death to act on its receptor complex of membrane bound ST2 and IL1RAP15. IL-33 acts on many different cell types including CD4 + T cells, ILC2s, alternatively activated macrophages (AAMs), eosinophils, basophils, regulatory T cells, dendritic cells16, and CD8 + T cells17,18. While it was previously believed that IL-33 is predominantly a Th2 immune activating cytokine, more recent work has shown it is rather pleiotropic with roles in Th1, Th2, and regulatory immunity15,19. Indeed IL-33 activation of ST2/IL-1RAP positive non-hematopoietic cells such as lung fibroblasts and epithelial cells may participate in responses independent of typical immune cell-induced type II inflammation. IL-33 has been shown to be induced by TLR 3 and 4 ligands such as Poly(I: C) and LPS in mouse bone marrow derived macrophages and mouse RAW 264.7 cells20,21. There is conflicting evidence on the role of IL-33 in IAV infection. One study showed that IL-33 elevation was detected in a murine model of H1N1 IAV infection 3 days post infection22, while others have shown that endogenous levels of IL-33 are low in both infected humans23 and mice24. It has also been demonstrated that IL-33 treatment of IAV infected mice leads to an increased numbers of virus specific T cells and subsequent viral clearance24, neutrophil recruitment to the lungs, and improved mucosal barrier function25.

One potent inducer of IL-33 is Oncostatin M (OSM), a member of gp130 cytokine family produced by macrophages, neutrophils, and activated T cells26,27. There is limited research on whether OSM is induced in lung viral infections, including IAV28, and this area remains understudied. We have previously shown that OSM overexpression in C57BL/6 mice induces elevated levels of Th2-skewed cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5, IL-13 (but not Th1-skewed cytokines such as IFNγ or IL-12), as well as induced levels of eotaxin-1 and 2, eosinophil and alternatively activated macrophage (AAM) accumulation in mouse lung tissues29,30. OSM also induces high levels of IL-33 in C57BL/6 lungs, and the Th2-skewed inflammatory phenotype was completely abrogated in IL-33 KO mice31,32.

We have here assessed IL-33 expression in C57BL6 mice infected with the mouse adapted IAV/H1N1 PR8 strain (referred to as PR8 subsequently here) in a comprehensive manner, examining bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and total lung protein homogenates by ELISA, with additional analysis of total lung homogenate protein by Western blots. We demonstrate that OSM is elevated, but that IL-33 is suppressed in mouse lungs infected with PR8 alone at both the mRNA and protein levels 7 days post infection. We further demonstrate that IL-33 and the downstream Th2-like inflammatory phenotype induced by OSM overexpression30,32,33, are strongly suppressed by PR8 superinfection. Furthermore, we demonstrate that in vitro, direct PR8 infection of C10 mouse type II alveolar epithelial cells leads to suppression of IL-33 at both the mRNA and protein levels, together suggesting PR8 modulation of lung inflammation involves IL-33 suppression.

Results

PR8 Influenza A virus infection induces OSM and IL-6 production while reducing IL-33 expression in vivo

To assess levels of OSM and IL-33 in IAV infection, we administered influenza strain PR8 (1000 PFU) to C57Bl/6 mice intranasally and measured various inflammatory parameters over 7 days (Fig. 1A to E). As expected, neutrophil influx into the BAL was evident at day 2 after infection and maintained to day 7 (Fig. 1A) and histological sections showed elevated inflammatory cell accumulation (Fig. 1B). PR8 infection resulted in elevated OSM (Fig. 1C) and IL-6 (Fig. 1D) in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) as detected by ELISA. Detectable levels of IL-33 were found in the BALF of infected mice (Fig. 1E) which peaked at 2-days post infection then subsequently decreased 4- and 7-days post infection. To assess global lung IL-33 expression at the RNA level we infected mice with both 250 PFU and 1000 PFU of PR8 and examined responses at Day 7 (Fig. 1F-H). We chose to examine both 1000 PFU (as used above for Fig. 1A and E) and 250 PFU to determine whether IL-33 suppression was affected significantly by viral load. 250 PFU represented a relatively low dose of PR8 (Lethal Dose 5%, LD5) whereas the higher dose, 1000 PFU, represented an LD20 using this preparation of PR8 IAV in C57BL/6 female mice. Both OSM and IL-6 were elevated in the whole lung at the mRNA level at both doses of PR8 (Fig. 1F and G). IL-33 mRNA detected in total lung homogenates was reduced significantly after infection with PR8 at either dose at Day 7, but this was not significantly different between the two doses selected (Fig. 1H).

IL-33 is attenuated following PR8/Influenza infection in vivo

To measure total lung IL-33 protein, we used ELISAs of lung homogenates as well as immunoblots (Fig. 2). Infection with 1000 PFU PR8 caused reduced IL-33 protein in homogenates as detected by ELISA (Fig. 2B and C) and by specific immunoblots (Fig. 2A). The immunoblots showed signals corresponding to full length IL-33 and little/no consistent detectable mature/cleavage products (See full blots in Supplemental Figures). 250 PFU induced a similar trend in reduced protein signals (Fig. 2A, B), but there were no significant differences between doses. IL-33 levels in the whole lung homogenates decreased 2- and 4-days post infection and were still reduced at day 7 (Fig. 2C). Marked elevation of IFIT-1 (Fig. 2D), a typical type I interferon response gene, was observed in PR8-infected mouse lungs. The results indicate that PR8 infection actively decreased global protein expression of IL-33 in the lungs in this system, consistent with its effect on total lung IL-33 mRNA (Fig. 1H).

PR8 infection reduces eosinophilic infiltration, but not neutrophil infiltration in vivo

Since PR8 infection alone increased OSM levels, yet reduced IL-33 at day 4 and day 7 post infection, PR8 may engage mechanisms that actively suppress elevated OSM-induced IL-33 expression in vivo. To assess this in a robust fashion, we tested whether influenza superinfection modulates an established state of high IL-33 levels and Th2 skewed inflammation induced in the AdOSM system. To do this we first primed mice with control (AdDel70) or AdOSM vectors to generate the Th2 skewed inflammation and IL-33 upregulation as previously described31,33. Then at day 7, mice were infected intranasally with 250 PFU PR8, and the lungs were examined at day 2 p.i. with PR8 (day 9 after Advectors) or day 5 p.i. with PR8 (day 12 after Advectors), as shown schematically in Fig. 3A and B respectively. As anticipated, AdOSM induced OSM in BALF, as did 2 days of PR8 alone, albeit to lower levels than AdOSM (Fig. 3C). OSM levels at day 12 (5 days post PR8 infection) were maintained in the PR8 infected mice and elevated in the PR8/AdOSM group (Fig. 3D). Concomitantly, AdOSM-induced eosinophil accumulation in the BALF was markedly reduced at day 9 by PR8 super-infection, and completely ablated at Day 12 (Fig. 3E and F) despite sustained elevated levels of OSM ligand upon PR8 superinfection. On the other hand, neutrophil accumulation increased at day 9 and elevation was maintained at day 12 (Fig. 3G and H).

OSM-induced type 2-like inflammation is attenuated 5 days post PR8 infection

Since this indicated that PR8 modulates OSM-induced eosinophil accumulation, we went on to assess other markers of Th2 skewed inflammation including IL-33, arginase-1, IL-4, and eotaxin-2 (all previously reported to be induced by AdOSM30,31,33. This was completed at both day 9 (day 2 pi. PR8, Fig. 4) and day 12 (day 5 pi. PR8, Fig. 5). Western blots showed that either AdOSM or PR8 administration resulted in detection of elevated pSTAT3 (Fig. 4A). The AdOSM-induced elevated protein levels of IL-33 and arginase-1 by Western blots (Fig. 4B, C), or eotaxin-2, and IL-4 in BALF (Fig. 4D, E) were not significantly affected at day 9 (day 2 pi. PR8, Fig. 4). However, IL-33 and arginase-1 (by Western blots), IL-4 and eotaxin-2 (by ELISA on BALF) were markedly reduced at day 12 post Advectors (Fig. 5B, C, D, E), which corresponds to 5 days after PR8 infection. IFNγ was elevated (albeit variably) in all mice 2 dpi with PR8 (Fig. 4F), and elevation was maintained in mice superinfected with AdOSM and PR8 5 dpi (Fig. 5F). Similarly, the neutrophil chemotactic factor KC (CXCL-1) was elevated and maintained at Days 2 and 5 post PR8 infection (Figs. 4G and 5G). We have previously shown33 that AdOSM induces the accumulation of F480+/CD206+/Arginase1 + cells, markers of Alternately Activated Macrophages (AAM) that correlated with IHC staining for Arginase1. Here, IHC staining for arginase1 + in tissue sections showed marked accumulation of Arginase1 + cells in response to AdOSM alone but not in PR8 infected mice (Supplemental S2). Consistent with the decrease in mRNA and protein levels of Arginase-1 (Figs. 5C and 6C), Arg1 IHC staining of AdOSM treated mouse lung Sect. 5 days post PR8 infection was reduced to near control levels (Supplementary Fig S2B) showing ablation of accumulation of arginase-1 + cells by PR8 IAV infection.

To assess whether mRNA of these and other genes was regulated by PR8 infection, we repeated the experiment and used NanoString technology to examine a broader range of genes in this system. This also included a comparator Advector that expressed IL-6 (AdIL-6) that, in contrast to AdOSM, we have previously published does not induce Th2 skewed inflammation33. PR8 superinfection was able to reduce AdOSM-induced mRNA levels for IL-33, eotaxin-2 (CCL24) mRNA at day 9 (2 dpi PR8) and 12 (5 dpi PR8) (Fig. 6A, B) as well as Arginase-1 and Retnla (Relmα) at day 12 (Fig. 6C, D). AdIL-6 had minimal effects on these targets and PR8 superinfection did not detectably modulate any AdIL-6-mediated effects measured here.

The reduction in the Th2 phenotype presence by PR8 infection was not associated with reduction in OSM mRNA expression. OSM mRNA was elevated in AdOSM/PR8 superinfection (Supplemental S3A). This is consistent with Fig. 3C and D where OSM protein levels in BALF were not reduced by PR8. Levels of OSMRβ mRNA (essential for OSM signaling) were unchanged by PR8 infection (Supplemental S3B), while IFIT1 and IFNβ mRNA were elevated in all infected groups as expected in a IAV viral infection (Supplemental S3C, D).

PR8 infection did not suppress AdOSM-induced ECM-modulating genes

OSM also induces various extracellular matrix (ECM) modulating genes in mouse lung including Col1A1, Col 3A1, TIMP-1, and MMP1330. NanoString analysis of the total lungs showed that while AdOSM elevated Col1A1, Col3A1 and MMP-13 mRNA, PR8 superinfection did not modulate the AdOSM-induced levels of these ECM-modulating genes (Fig. 7). PR8 alone induced levels of TIMP-1 mRNA at both 2 and 5 dpi and Coll3A1 at 5 dpi (7B and 7D). Thus, although PR8 itself can modulate some ECM genes, the effects of PR8 on OSM-induced lung inflammation appear manifested by alterations in TH2 skewed phenotype, but not on the ECM-modulating genes examined here.

PR8 infection directly suppresses IL-33 expression in C10 cells in vitro

The results indicate that despite elevated levels of OSM in IAV superinfection, IL-33 is selectively inhibited. The induction of type I interferons and subsequent IFN-response genes by influenza is well established34,35. Thus, since IFNβ (Supplemental S3B) and IFNγ (Fig. 5F) were elevated in the day 2 groups of AdOSM and PR8 superinfection, we also tested potential direct effects of these IFNs on IL-33 expressing cells in vitro. We used mouse alveolar epithelial type 2 cells (C10) which we have previously shown to elevate expression of IL-33 in response to OSM32. OSM stimulation (10 ng/mL) induced both pSTAT3 and IL-33 in C10 cells (Fig. 8A), while co-stimulation with IFNγ (10 ng/mL) inhibited OSM-induced levels of IL-33 by approximately 32% (Fig. 8B), but not pSTAT3 (Fig. 8C). Combination stimulation with OSM (10 ng/mL) showed that IFNα (100 U/mL) did not detectably reduce levels of either IL-33 (Supplemental S4B) nor pSTAT3 (Supplemental S4C). This suggests that IFNγ may contribute to the suppression of IL-33 in IAV infection more so than Type 1 IFNs.

We then assessed another possible mechanism, whereby suppression was mediated directly by viral infection of epithelial cells. C10 cells (as above) were pre-stimulated for 24 h with 5ng/mL of OSM and then infected with PR8 (MOI = 10) or mock-infected. Residual virus was removed after 1 h, replaced with fresh media containing 5 ng/mL of OSM (or 0 OSM), and cells were incubated for 24 h. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that PR8 infection suppressed both basal by 92% and OSM-induced IL-33 mRNA by 73.7% (Fig. 9A). In contrast, OSMRβ and TIMP-1 mRNA were not significantly reduced in these same samples (Fig. 9B and C). This is consistent with the lack of TIMP-1 or OSMRβ inhibition in vivo in the AdOSM-PR8 superinfection data as detected by Nanostring (Fig. 7D and Supplemental S3B). TIMP-1 protein was also not suppressed in cell culture supernatants of PR8 infected cells (Fig. 9D). Assessment of cell lysate protein via Western Blot analysis showed 62% inhibition of IL-33 in PR8 infected cells that had been pre-treated with OSM (Fig. 9F), while OSMRβ and pSTAT3 levels were not suppressed, (pSTAT3 was actually enhanced) as shown in Fig. 9G and H. Additionally, suppression of IL-33 protein was not observed in cells infected with the non-replicating AdDel70 control viral vector (Fig. 9E-H). This data shows that PR8 infection of C10 cells in vitro results in selective suppression of IL-33 expression, but not of TIMP-1 or OSMβ, nor of pSTAT3 activation.

Discussion

Overall, we have demonstrated that PR8 infection reduced IL-33 expression both in vivo and in vitro in the mouse system. We show that, despite a low and transient increase of IL-33 in BALF 2 days post infection (Fig. 1E), overall total lung levels of IL-33 were suppressed (Figs. 1H and 2C) by PR8 IAV. Previous work has shown that IL-33 is induced (~ 500 pg/mL) in the BALF 3-days post IAV infection22, although a much higher dose of virus (106 PFU of PR8) was used in those studies. Further, the levels of IL-33 in BALF are a small fraction of the total lung IL-33 protein expression as assessed by ELISA of lung homogenates. We estimate that the load of IL-33 detected in the lung homogenates is orders of magnitude above that in the BALF. For example, 2-days post infection, there was no more that 50 pg total (~ 100 pg/mL in the entire 0.5mL volume retrieved) in the BALF, whereas in total lung homogenates there was an estimated 180,000 pg of IL-33 (1000pg/mg in 180 mg, the approximate weight of a C57BL6 mouse lung36. The IL-33 detected by ELISA in BALF would likely be limited to extracellular soluble species in alveolar spaces, whereas the IL-33 detected in homogenates could be either extracellular IL-33 in submucosa/tissues or intracellular IL-33 released upon cell necrosis or tissue lysis/homogenization. The results also show that IAV infection markedly suppressed previously elevated pro-form IL-33 expression (Figs. 5 and 7) in vivo and in mouse epithelial cells in vitro (Fig. 8). Full length IL-33 (pro-form) is active at IL-33 receptors, although less bioactive than species enzymatically cleaved by elastase or Cathepsin G37 whereas IL-33 cleaved by Caspase-3 and − 7 are inactive38. In our system, immunoblot analysis of total lung homogenates shows predominantly full-length IL-33 is present in the system with low/undetectable cleaved/mature IL-33 (See full blots in Supplemental figures). Bioactivity of IL-33 may also be affected by oxidation39,40 although the extent of this in IAV infection is not known. IAV does induce strong oxidative stress, which in this environment could induce oxidative modifications of various proteins, but currently there is no direct evidence of specific oxidation of cytokines.

Analysis of other cytokines revealed induction of both OSM (Fig. 1C) and IL-6 (Fig. 1D) by IAV. Cellular source of OSM include monocytes/macrophages and neutrophils, both of which accumulate in lung in IAV infection. Despite OSM being known to induce IL-3332, IL-33 levels had already started to decrease when OSM levels are maximal 4 days post infection (Fig. 1C and E). This observation led us to test whether PR8 directly engages mechanisms to suppress IL-33, including examining superinfection of AdOSM treated mice (Figs. 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7). Here we found that even in a state where IL-33 is markedly elevated in mouse lungs by AdOSM, PR8 was able to significantly suppress mRNA expression at 2- and 5-days post infection (Fig. 7A) and protein levels at day 5 (Fig. 5B). This is consistent with other data in a model of bacterial (both Staphylococcus aureus and Klebsiella pneumoniae)-IAV superinfection25 which showed that IL-33 was suppressed compared to mice infected with bacteria alone, although mechanisms were not explored in that study.

Similar to our previous publications, infection with AdOSM resulted in continuous accumulation of eosinophils to the lung BALF 9- and 12-days post infection (Fig. 3E, F). In mice superinfected with IAV, eosinophil levels began to drop by 9 days post AdOSM infection (2 dpi with IAV) (Fig. 3E.) and were completely ablated by 12 days post AdOSM (5 dpi with IAV) (Fig. 3F). Previous work has indicated that IAV infection is able to inhibit eosinophil recruitment to the lung in an IFNγ dependent mechanism41,42. Increased levels of IFNγ in our AdOSM-PR8 superinfected mice (Figs. 4F and 5F) may be blocking eosinophil recruitment in a similar manner. Mechanistically, this may be due to IFNγ inhibiting the production of the chemokines CCL11 (eotaxin) and CCL24 (eotaxin-2). A pre-incubation with IFNγ has been shown to reduce levels of eotaxin produced by TNFα-stimulated human airway epithelial cells43, and treatment with IFNγ has been shown to inhibit IL-13-induced production of both eotaxin and eotaxin-2 in mouse models44. Our results show that IFNgamma was able to reduce OSM-induced IL-33 in mouse epithelial cells in vitro (Fig. 8B). Additionally, it has been shown that IL-33 promotes eosinophil survival by reducing apoptosis45. It is therefore possible that increased levels of IFNγ prevents eosinophil recruitment, while the reduction in IL-33 promotes eosinophil apoptosis. Together, this may lead to the ablation of the eosinophil accumulation observed in AdOSM-IAV superinfected mice. While eosinophils have been shown to be susceptible to infection with IAV, they minimally support viral replication, and instead act as antigen presenting cells and express CD86 and MHCI in response to infection46. Thus, it is unlikely that direct killing of eosinophils by PR8 is contributing to their reduction in numbers.

In contrast to eosinophils, we observed that neutrophil accumulation in the BALF was maintained by AdOSM-PR8 superinfection (Fig. 3G and H). Neutrophil recruitment to the lung is a hallmark of influenza infection in both humans and mice47, and effector functions of neutrophils in IAV infection include phagocytosis, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, cytokine production, and NETosis48. Others have shown that mice depleted of neutrophils exhibit higher lung viral titers, increased weight loss, and succumb more rapidly to infection49, emphasizing the importance of neutrophil recruitment in influenza infection. In our studies, the increased neutrophil counts present in AdOSM-PR8 superinfected mice may result from the maintained elevation of KC (CXCL-1) (Figs. 4G and 5G), a key neutrophil chemoattractant. Previous work has shown that influenza infected mice treated with an anti-CXCL-1 antibody had reduced neutrophil counts in lung lysates50. Overall, these data indicate that PR8 superinfection alters the lung immune environment by suppressing eosinophil responses and maintaining neutrophil recruitment, possibly through IFNγ- and CXCL-1–mediated mechanisms.

In addition to an ablation of the eosinophil response, AdOSM-PR8 superinfected mice displayed a reduction in other Th2-Type inflammation markers including IL-4 (Fig. 5D), Arginase-1 + cells (Figs. 5C and 6C), and RELMα (Fig. 6D). Although the Arginase1 + IHC staining does not necessarily identify AAM, we have previously shown by flow cytometry that AdOSM induces F480+/CD206+/Arg-1 + cell accumulation correlating well with Arg-1 IHC33. Thus, the observed decrease upon PR8 infection (Supplemental Fig. S2) we believe reflects suppression of AAM, but recognize that other markers of mouse AAM such as CD206 or YM-1 should be assessed to confirm our interpretation. IL-33 can induce the expression of Th2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-13, IL-5) by Th2 helper cells or ILC-2s51 and indeed the effects of AdOSM on these mediators requires IL-33 as assessed in IL-33 KO mice31. Taken together, this data is consistent with active suppression by PR8 of IL-33 and subsequent reduction in IL-33-dependent Th2-skewed inflammation in this system. Whether these effects are restricted to the sex or strain of mice studied here is not known. Others have shown that PR8 can suppress Th2 skewed inflammation (most often induced through the Ovalbumin sensitization model)41,52, and such effects of IAV may be due to the suppression of IL-33 we have uniquely identified here. Other effects of immunopathology or immunity in IAV infection may also be mediated in part by such reductions of IL-33.

We also observed a robust interferon response in mice infected with both high and low doses of virus, as measured by expression of interferon stimulated gene IFIT-1 (Fig. 2D). Because in AdOSM superinfected mice we see elevation of both IFNγ (Fig. 5F) and IFNβ (Supplemental S2D), we tested direct effects of interferons on OSM-induced IL-33 expression in vitro. Western blot analysis showed with OSM induced increased expression of both pSTAT3 and IL-33 (Fig. 8A). However, co-stimulation with OSM and IFNγ maintained levels of pSTAT3 (Fig. 8B), but suppressed IL-33 (Fig. 8C). We additionally tested co-stimulation with OSM and IFNα and did not see downregulation of either IL-33 (Supplemental S4B) or pSTAT3 (Supplemental S4C) in the conditions tested. Previous work has shown that treatment of primary mouse lung fibroblasts with IFNγ reduces basal IL-33 expression and pharmacological inhibition of STAT1 with nifuroxazide reverses this suppression53. IFNβ has been shown to reduce cytokine production and proliferation by IL-33 activated ILC2s in both humans and mice54. This suggests that both type I and type II IFNs are capable of suppressing IL-33 stimulation. Since both a type I (IFNβ) and a type II (IFNγ) are elevated in our AdOSM-PR8 superinfected animals, these IFNs may be partially responsible for the downregulation of IL-33 that we observe in vivo, but IFNγ may be more influential in epithelial cells. Other factors may down regulate IL-33 as well but this is not clear in our system.

Furthermore, we sought to determine whether PR8 infection directly affected IL-33 expression. Our results indicate that PR8 infection of C10 cells reduces IL-33 expression at the mRNA (Fig. 9A) and protein level (Fig. 9F), whereas other targets (OSMRβ, TIMP-1, and pSTAT3) showed no change or an increase in levels in infected cells (Fig. 8B-D and G-H). These findings indicate that PR8 infection selectively suppresses IL-33 of the targets studied, whereas non-replicating DNA virus infection (AdDel70) had no affects. Whether other ssRNA replicating viruses have similar effects awaits further analysis. It is possible that viral pathogen associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) such as viral surface proteins or ssRNA are stimulating toll like receptors such as TLR-3, -4, -7, -8, and − 9, leading to the regulation of IL-33. However, it has previously been shown that stimulation of both bronchial smooth muscle cells55 and airway epithelial cells56 with Pam3CSK4, flagellin, and Poly(I: C) leads to an enhancement in IL-33 signal associated with NF-κB activation, rather than suppression. Another mechanism that mediates IL-33 suppression may be influenza’s non-structural protein 1 (NS1). One way in which NS1 antagonizes the host immune response is through the binding of its C-terminal effector domain to cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factor (CPSF)57 and poly-A binding protein II (PABII)58. This inhibits 3’ processing of pre-mRNAs, which prevents production of mature mRNAs, nuclear export, and protein translation. Infection of A549 cells with a truncated NS1 protein that lacked the C-terminal effector domain has been shown to increase mRNA expression of IL-6, IFNβ, and NFKBIA59. We acknowledge that our observations in the in vitro results in C10 cells may not reflect the responses of type II alveolar epithelial cells in vivo (either in mouse or human). Furthermore, whether columnar epithelial cells or other cells that get infected by IAV respond similarly is not known. Future experimentation could also involve exploring the potential role of NS1 in selectively regulating IL-33 in mouse and human epithelial cells.

Conclusion

Taken together, we show that PR8 infection reduces IL-33 expression both in vivo and in vitro in mouse models, through mechanisms mediated at least in part by direct viral infection of epithelial cells. IL-33 is one of many cytokines modulated by IAV infection, and its suppression we propose is part of the mechanism employed by IAV to inhibit Th2-type inflammation in context of immune responses.

Materials and methods

Animals

Female wild-type C57Bl/6 mice (age = 8–10 weeks old) were purchased from Charles River (Ottawa, Canada). All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions within the McMaster University Central Animal Facility. All experimental procedures using mice were approved by the McMaster University Animal Research Ethics Board (AUP# 14-08-34). All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Adenovirus and influenza A virus administration

Adenovirus vectors AdDel70 (negative control vector), AdOSM, and AdIL-6 were thawed on ice, and diluted in Phosphate-buffered Saline (PBS) to specific concentrations (3 × 107 PFU/mouse, 5 × 107 PFU/mouse, as indicated in figure legends). Mice (8–10 weeks old, approx. 20–25 g each) were anesthetized using aerosol isoflurane in a closed chamber (O2 flow rate set to 2.5), before allowing to recover back in regular cage housing. Each mouse was endotracheally intubated with 50 µl of virus solution. Influenza A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (PR8) aliquots (a kind gift of Dr. Matthew Miller’s laboratory, McMaster Immunology Research Centre, Hamilton, Canada) were thawed on ice and diluted in PBS to specific concentrations (250–1000 PFU/mouse, as indicated in figure legends). 25µL was administered intranasally to each mouse. Mice were monitored daily, with humane endpoint defined as weight loss exceeding 20% or if animals were found to be in severe respiratory distress. For the stated times of experimental endpoint analysis (day 9 and day 12), mice (10–12) weeks, approx. 20–25 g each) were euthanized by aerosolized isoflurane in a closed chamber (O2 flow rate set to 2.5) at time-points post-administration as indicated in figure legends.

BALF collection and lung tissue processing

When mice were culled, lungs were washed twice with 0.3 ml PBS to collect bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). These samples were centrifuged at 13,000 RPM for 2 min at 4 °C, after which supernatants were collected as BALF and stored at -80°C. Cell pellets from each sample were resuspended and counted using Trypan Blue solution (Life Technologies, Burlington, ON). Cells were then cytocentrifuged and stained with Hema 3 Fixative Solutions (ThermoFisher Scientific) for differential cell analysis. After BALF collection, the left lobe of lungs was perfused with 7 ml 1xPBS to remove the remaining blood, followed by perfusion with 10% formalin fixative for 48 h prior to being transferred and stored in 70% ethanol. For histological sections, lungs were embedded in paraffin, cut into 5 μm sections, and stained with H&E. The right lobes were extracted, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C until they were further processed as previously described by Wong and others30.

Cell culture

Cytokine stimulations and in vitro virus infection

C10 cells (a kind gift of Chris Migliaccio, University of Montana) were plated at a density of 100,000 cells/well in 12-well plates then stimulated with recombinant mouse OSM (R&D systems) at 5ng/mL for 24 h. We have previously published responses of these cells to OSM32. Frozen PR8 virus aliquots were thawed on ice and diluted to specific concentrations (MOI = 10) in flu-infection media (10x MEM, MEM Amino Acids Solution, Sodium Bicarbonate Solution, MEM Vitamin Solution, L-Glutamine, Penicillin/Streptomycin, 7.5% BSA, and ultrapure H2O). Stimulation media was then removed, cells were washed with PBS, and 200ul of virus containing media was then added for 1 h at 37 °C. Virus containing media was then removed and 1000uL of fresh RPMI (supplemented with 10% FBS) media was added to wells.

Preparation of cell lysates

After experiment specific time-points and stimulations, supernatants were collected and stored in -20 °C and cells were washed twice with PBS. RIPA buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (200 mM Sodium orthovanadate (Na3VO4), 20 mg/mL phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), aprotinin, and 1 M dithiothreitol (DTT)) was then added on top of cells on ice for 2 min. Wells were then scrapped with cell scrappers and sample was collected in Eppendorf tubes and kept on ice for 1 h. Samples were then sheered using a 23G needle and syringe and centrifuged for 10 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected and stored in -80 °C for future analysis. Protein concentration of cell lysates as well as whole lung homogenates were determined using Bradford Assay. Total cell lysates were diluted (1:32) and whole lung homogenates were diluted (1:100) in PBS and compared to bovine serum albumin (BSA) standard (0–14 µg BSA). 40uL of Bio-Rad Protein Assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, Cat# 500-0006) was added to each sample. The absorbance of each sample was determined using Biotek Synergy plate reader (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) at 595 nm. Alternatively, RNA was isolated from cells following a detailed protocol from a commercially available Qiagen RNeasy extraction kit (Hilden, Germany).

Western blot analysis

Whole lung homogenate or cell lysate samples were prepared using distilled water and 5x reducing buffer (10% SDS, 25% 1 M Tris (pH 6.8), 0.001% bromophenol blue, 5% 2-mercaptoethanol, 50% glycerol, and ddH2O) and boiled at 100 °C for 5 min. Samples were then loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels (20ug protein/well) and separated by electrophoresis at 90 V for 90 min and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes at 400 mA for 1 h by standard methods. Following blocking for 1 h (TBS/Odyssey Blocking buffer – Mandal, Guelph, Canada), primary antibodies mouse IL-33 (R&D Systems Cat#AF3626), mouse Arg-1 (BD Biosciences Cat#610708), pSTAT3 (Cell Signaling Cat#9145), STAT3 (Cell Signaling Cat#4901), OSMRβ (R&D Systems Cat#AF662), and β-Actin (Santa Cruz Cat#sc-1616) were used to probe membranes overnight at 4 °C. Blots were washed with TBS + 0.15%Tween-20, and secondary antibodies (Anti-Goat; Mandel Scientific Cat# LIC92668074, Anti-Rabbit; Mandel Scientific Cat# LIC92632213, or Anti-Mouse; Mandel Scientific, Cat#LIC92632212) were added for approximately 1 h away from light. After 3 subsequent washes, blots were imaged using Odyssey LI-COR Imaging technologies. Image Studio software was used to analyze band intensity through densitometry.

Immunohistochemistry

Histological sections were de-paraffinised, then treated with 0.5% peroxide in methanol for 10 min and blocked with 5% normal goat serum (NGS). Sections were then incubated with an anti-arginase-1 antibody (BD Biosciences Cat#610708) at a 1/100 dilution, followed by incubation with a biotinylated goat anti-mouse antibody (Vector Laboratories) at a 1/500 dilution. Sections were then treated with 1/50 dilution of streptavidin-peroxidase, developed in Nova Red peroxidase substrate (Vector Laboratories) and counterstained with hematoxylin.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Cytokine concentrations in cell culture supernatants or in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of mice were measured using commercially available ELISA kits from R&D Systems or by ELISAs outsourced to Eve Technologies (Calgary, AB).

Reverse-Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR)

Reverse transcription of RNA into complementary DNA (cDNA) was performed using Invitrogen Superscript II Reverse Transcriptase kit (Life Technologies, Burlington, Cat# 18064014). The resulting cDNA was diluted to 2 ng/ml and stored at -20 °C. Samples were prepared in triplicates in 96-well PCR plate (FroggaBio, North York, ON, Cat# 3426-00) with Taqman Universal Master Mix containing Uracil-N-glycosylase (UNG) and predetermined assay reagents (PDAR) purchased from Life Technologies (Burlington, ON) for mouse IL-33, endogenous OSM, IL-6, IFIT-1, OSMR, and TIMP-1. PDAR for 18 S was used as the endogenous control gene. Plates with prepared samples read and analyzed in Quant Studio 3 Real Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The resulting data was corrected to 18 S cycle threshold value (CT value) and expressed at fold change relative to control. Additional mRNA quantification was completed at the McMaster Genomic facility using code sets provided by NanoString (Bothell, WA).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism version 10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) was for all statistical analyses. Figures represent mean values ± SEM. One- or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and either Tukey or Bonferroni post-hoc analyses were used to assess statistical differences between means for multiple groups. Difference between means were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.
