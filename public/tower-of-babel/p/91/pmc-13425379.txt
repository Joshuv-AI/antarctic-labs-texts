Hippocampal Hap1 down-regulation impairs glucocorticoid receptor nuclear translocation and exacerbates Alzheimer’s disease-related neuropathology in APP/PS1 mice
Authors: Taiqi Huang, Meiyu Zhang, Yanyu Zhang, Chonglin Su, Enhao He, Jinrong Wang, Jiawei Yang, Yongxiao Liu, Yan Zeng, Xingxing Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Impaired nuclear translocation of glucocorticoid receptor (GR) has been implicated in hippocampal vulnerability in Alzheimer's disease (AD), yet the molecular basis of this defect remains poorly understood. This study identified Huntingtin-associated protein 1 (Hap1) as a critical regulator of GR nuclear translocation in the hippocampus. Specifically, Hap1 expression progressively declined in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice with advancing age and pathological burden. Hippocampal Hap1 knockdown induced pronounced cognitive deficits and synaptic deterioration, as indicated by reduced dendritic arborization, decreased spine density, impaired long-term potentiation, and exacerbated amyloid-β deposition. Mechanistic analyses showed that Hap1 deficiency increased GR ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation and, more importantly, disrupted ligand-dependent GR translocation to the nucleus, thereby attenuating GR-dependent brain-derived neurotrophic factor transcription. In parallel, Hap1 knockdown elevated corticosterone concentration and induced depression-like behavior, consistent with hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis dysregulation. Collectively, these findings establish defective GR nuclear trafficking driven by loss of Hap1 function as a key pathomechanism linking intracellular transport failure to synaptic dysfunction in AD and highlight Hap1 as a potential therapeutic target.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tau tangles, pathological features that drive synaptic degeneration and cognitive decline (Peng et al., 2022). Increasing evidence has identified chronic stress and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation as critical contributors to AD pathogenesis (Canet et al., 2019; Wallensten et al., 2023). In clinical settings, patients with early-stage AD exhibit elevated glucocorticoid (GC) levels in plasma and cerebrospinal fluid together with impaired glucocorticoid receptor (GR)-mediated negative feedback (Csernansky et al., 2006; Hoogendijk et al., 2006; Notarianni, 2013; Swanwick et al., 1998; Zheng et al., 2020). This disruption of GC/GR signaling increases neuronal susceptibility to stress, particularly in the hippocampus, a central structure for synaptic plasticity and memory formation (Lupien et al., 1998; McEwen, 1999; McLaughlin et al., 2007). In AD, hippocampal abnormalities, including dendritic remodeling, reduced neurogenesis, and impaired long-term potentiation (LTP), are closely associated with GR dysfunction (Arango-Lievano et al., 2019; Finsterwald & Alberini, 2014; Notarianni, 2013; Rothman & Mattson, 2010). Notably, experimental models have shown that defective GR signaling accelerates neuronal damage driven by both Aβ and tau pathology (Arango-Lievano et al., 2016; Dromard et al., 2022), establishing GR dysregulation as a pivotal mechanism in AD progression.

GR, a ligand-activated nuclear receptor expressed at high levels in the hippocampus, acts as a core transcriptional regulator of stress adaptation and cognitive processing (Su et al., 2025). Ligand binding initiates a trafficking program involving the translocation of GR from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, enabling transcriptional regulation of gene networks that tune neuronal excitability and synaptic function. Previous Affymetrix GeneChip profiling of rat hippocampal slices revealed temporally structured transcriptional programs following GR activation and identified GR-responsive genes that participate in neurotransmission and synaptic plasticity (Morsink et al., 2006). GR also engages non-genomic mechanisms and intersects with key signaling pathways implicated in memory consolidation, including cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), calcium/calmodulin-dependent kinase II (CaMKII), and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) signaling (Chen et al., 2012; Finsterwald & Alberini, 2014). Hippocampal GR density is considered a key determinant of cognitive reserve, with age-related GR decline shown to correlate with cognitive deficits (Hassan et al., 1999; Morano et al., 1994; Notarianni, 2013; Peiffer et al., 1991). A decisive step for effective GR action is rapid nuclear translocation, a microtubule-dependent trafficking process that supports timely transcriptional regulation (Galigniana et al., 2001; Harrell et al., 2004). Failure at this trafficking stage impairs downstream signaling. However, the neuronal factors that orchestrate GR nuclear translocation under physiological and disease-relevant conditions remain poorly understood.

Huntingtin-associated protein 1 (Hap1) is a neuron-specific cytoplasmic scaffold with established roles in intracellular transport and receptor stabilization (Chen et al., 2023). Hap1 facilitates the trafficking and subcellular targeting of receptor systems, including tropomyosin receptor kinase A (TrkA), gamma-aminobutyric acid type A receptor (GABAAR), and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), in part through limiting receptor degradation and maintaining appropriate compartmental localization (Kittler et al., 2004; Li et al., 2002; Rong et al., 2006). Our prior work identified Hap1-GR interaction in murine brain tissue and demonstrated that Hap1 depletion reduces GR protein abundance (Chen et al., 2020), implying a functional coupling between Hap1 and GR regulation. Hap1 also shows high expression in primate hippocampal neurons (Chen et al., 2022), but whether it contributes to AD-associated GR dysregulation remains unclear.

The present study demonstrated that reduction of hippocampal Hap1 in APP/PS1 mice exacerbated cognitive deficits and AD-related pathological changes. These effects were accompanied by disruption of GR nuclear translocation and consequent impairment of GR-dependent signaling, including BDNF expression. These findings identify Hap1 as a previously unrecognized regulator of GR nuclear trafficking in hippocampal neurons and support a model in which Hap1 deficiency contributes directly to GR dysfunction in AD. This study not only provides a direct mechanistic link between intracellular transport failure and neurodegeneration but also identifies Hap1 as a potential therapeutic target for the restoration of GR homeostasis in AD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Wuhan University of Science and Technology (WUST, China) (Approval No. 2025224). APPSwe/PSen1dE9 (APP/PS1) transgenic mice (stock #034829-JAX) and non-transgenic littermates used as wild-type (WT) controls were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (USA). Only male mice were used in this study to minimize variability attributable to estrous cycling and reported sex differences in Aβ pathology in AD models (Milà-Alomà et al., 2025). All mice were housed in the WUST animal facility under a 12 h light/dark cycle (lights on at 0800h–2000h) at 21–23°C and 40%–60% relative humidity, with ad libitum access to food and water. Hippocampal Hap1 and GR expression was examined in WT and APP/PS1 mice at 2, 3, 6, and 12 months of age. For behavioral and neuropathological assessments after hippocampal Hap1 knockdown, 6-month-old mice were used. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Group sizes are reported in the corresponding figure legends.

Antibodies and reagents

Mouse monoclonal anti-Hap1 antibody (sc-166245; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA) was generated against amino acids 329–628 at the C-terminus of mouse HAP1B, with its specificity validated in prior studies (Yang et al., 2011). Mouse monoclonal anti-GR antibody (sc-393232; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), which targets amino acids 121–420 of human GR, has been similarly validated (Hong et al., 2020). Rabbit anti-GR antibody (HY-P80687; MedChemExpress, USA) was used in parallel for complementary analyses. Additional antibodies and reagents included: mouse anti-GAPDH (MAB45855; Bioswamp Life Science, China); rabbit anti-vinculin (26520-1-AP; Proteintech, China); mouse anti-Aβ (803004; BioLegend, USA); rabbit anti-NeuN (ab177487; Abcam, China); rabbit anti-ubiquitin (ET1609-21), rabbit anti-BDNF (ET1606-42), and rabbit anti-p150Glued (HA723534; Huabio, China); rabbit anti-TBP (44059; Cell Signaling Technology, USA); dexamethasone (HY-14648), MG-132 (HY-13259), and Protein A/G magnetic beads (HY-K0202; MedChemExpress, USA); DAPI (0100-20; SouthernBiotech, USA); total RNA isolation reagent (R0016), RIPA buffer (P0013B), protease inhibitor cocktail (P1005), Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining Kit (C0105S), and SABC-HRP Kit (P0603; Beyotime, China); Reverse Transcription Kit (BL699A), SYBR Green PCR Mix (BL698A), Nuclear/Cytosol Fractionation Kit (BL670A), and electrochemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (BL520A; Biosharp, China); Mouse Corticosterone (CORT) Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) Kit (JYM0544Mo), Mouse Corticotropin-Releasing Hormone (CRH) ELISA Kit (JYM0508Mo), and Mouse Adrenocorticotropic Hormone (ACTH) ELISA Kit (JYM0422Mo; Jiyinmei Biotech, China); and SuperGolgi Kit (003010; Bioenno Tech, USA).

CRISPR/Cas9 targeting

The guide RNAs (gRNAs) were selected to target Hap1 exon 1: T2 (GCCGCGTACATCGGCCGGAG, PAM: AGG) and T3 (ATGGACCCGCTACGTATTCC, PAM: AGG). A scramble single guide RNA (sgRNA) (ACCGGAAGAGCGACCTCTTCT) served as the control. The gRNAs were driven by the U6 promoter in an adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector (AAV-Hap1-gRNA) that co-expressed red fluorescent protein under the CMV promoter. Cas9 was expressed in a separate AAV vector (AAV-Cas9) under the CMV promoter.

Cell culture and transfections

Mouse neuroblastoma Neuro-2a (N2a) cells (CL-0168) were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (China). Cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; 150210, Procell, China) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; 164210, Procell), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. Cultures were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For Hap1 knockdown, cells at 80% confluence in 6-well plates were transfected using Lipofectamine 8000 (C0533, Beyotime). The knockdown group was transfected with AAV-Hap1-gRNA-T2+AAV-Hap1-gRNA-T3+AAV-Cas9 plasmids at a 1:1:6 ratio (total gRNA:Cas9=1:3). Control cells received AAV-scramble-sgRNA+AAV-Cas9 plasmids at a 1:3 ratio.

Viral packaging and stereotaxic injection

All vectors were packaged into serotype AAV9 by PackGene Biotech (China), with titers quantified at 1×1013 genome copies (GC)/mL. To induce local Hap1 knockdown in the hippocampus of WT and APP/PS1 mice, bilateral stereotaxic co-injections were performed. Knockdown groups (WT-Hap1-KD and APP/PS1-Hap1-KD) received a mixture of AAV9-Hap1-gRNA-T2, AAV9-Hap1-gRNA-T3, and AAV9-Cas9 at a 1:1:6 volumetric ratio (total gRNA:Cas9=1:3), while control groups (WT-control and APP/PS1-control) received AAV9-scramble-sgRNA and AAV9-Cas9 at a 1:3 ratio. A total volume of 300 nL was delivered per hemisphere, corresponding to 3×109 GC per injection site. Prior to surgery, mice were anesthetized using isoflurane inhalation. Injections were guided by coordinates relative to bregma: anteroposterior (AP) −1.65 mm, mediolateral (ML) ±1.2 mm, dorsoventral (DV) −1.99 mm. Viral suspensions were infused at 50 nL/min, followed by retention of the needle for 10 min after infusion. Post-surgery, mice recovered on a 37°C heating pad and were monitored twice daily for 5 days.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were anesthetized and intracardially perfused with 0.9% saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer (PB, pH 7.4). Brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA/0.1 mol/L PB for 24 h, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose for 48 h at 4°C, and equilibrated until sinking. Subsequently, brains were embedded in OCT compound and sectioned coronally at 30 μm using a cryostat (Leica CM1950; Leica, Germany). For dual immunofluorescence, sections were incubated in blocking buffer containing 4% normal goat serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 30 min, followed by primary antibodies (mouse anti-Hap1 and rabbit anti-GR) at 4°C overnight. Sections were then incubated with species-matched secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit and rhodamine-conjugated anti-mouse) and counterstained with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). For Aβ immunohistochemistry, the SABC-HRP Kit and DAB chromogen were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Slides were imaged using a histological slide scanner (Olympus BX61VS, Olympus, Japan). Quantification was performed in ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Western blot analysis

Cells or hippocampal tissue were lysed in RIPA buffer (50 mmol/L Tris, 150 mmol/L NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mmol/L EDTA) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail. Lysates were centrifuged at 12 000 ×g for 15 min at 4°C, and protein concentrations were quantified. Equal amounts of protein were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in PBS for 1 h at room temperature, washed three times (10 min each), and incubated with primary antibodies in 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA)/PBS at 4°C overnight. Following three additional PBS washes, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies in 5% non-fat milk/PBS for 1 h at room temperature. After final PBS washes, immunoreactive bands were detected using ECL reagent and visualized with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Imager 680, Cytiva, UK). Protein quantification was performed using Fiji software v.2.14.0, normalized to loading controls (GAPDH/vinculin).

Immunoprecipitation

Cells or tissue were homogenized in 1% NP-40 lysis buffer (150 mmol/L NaCl, 1% NP-40, 2 mmol/L EDTA, 50 mmol/L Tris, pH 8.0, 1 mmol/L phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF)) with protease inhibitor. Lysates were centrifuged at 12 000 ×g for 10 min at 4°C, and 500 μg of supernatant protein (in 500 μL) was incubated with 1 μg of mouse anti-GR antibody or mouse IgG (isotype control) overnight at 4°C. Protein A/G magnetic beads (25 μL beads/sample), pre-blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min at 4°C, were added to antibody-antigen complexes and incubated for 2 h at 4°C with rotation. Beads were collected by magnetic separation, washed three times with ice-cold lysis buffer, and eluted by boiling in SDS-PAGE sample buffer at 95°C for 5 min. Eluates were analyzed by western blotting.

Fractionation of cytosolic and nuclear fractions

Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were isolated from cultured N2a cells using a Nuclear/Cytosol Fractionation Kit in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, cells (5×106–1×107/mL) were pelleted by centrifugation at 800 ×g for 3 min at 4°C, washed twice with ice-cold PBS (800 ×g, 3 min, 4°C), and fractionated following the manufacturer’s instructions. Separated fractions were analyzed by western blotting, with vinculin and TATA-box binding protein (TBP) used as cytoplasmic and nuclear loading controls, respectively.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from tissues using Total RNA Isolation Reagent per the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized from DNase-treated RNA (1 μg) using a Reverse Transcription Kit with oligo(dT) primers. Quantitative PCR was performed in technical triplicates using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix on a Rotor-Gene Q Real-Time PCR System (QIAGEN, Germany). Relative expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method and normalized to Gapdh. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Y-maze test

The Y-maze consisted of three enclosed arms (30 cm long×8 cm wide×15 cm high) positioned at 120° with removable partitions at the central junction. Arms were randomly designated as novel, start, and other arms. During the training phase, the novel arm was blocked, and mice were placed in the start arm and allowed to explore the start and other arms for 10 min. After a 1 h interval, the partition blocking the novel arm was removed for the test phase. Mice were reintroduced to the start arm and allowed to explore all three arms for 5 min. Sessions were video-recorded to quantify time spent in each arm and the number of arm entries. An arm entry was defined as placement of all four limbs within an arm. Spontaneous alternation (%) was calculated as:

1\begin{document}$ SA (\%) = \frac{N_{{\mathrm{alt}}}}{N_{{\mathrm{total}}}-{2}} \times 100\% $ \end{document}

where SA represents the spontaneous alternation rate (%), Nalt represents the number of alternations (defined as consecutive entries into three different arms), and Ntotal represents the total number of arm entries during the test session.

Novel object recognition (NOR) test

The NOR test was conducted in an open-field arena (40 cm long×40 cm wide×40 cm high). Mice were habituated to the empty arena for 10 min. After 24 h, mice underwent a familiarization session in which two identical objects were placed in the arena and exploration was allowed for 5 min. After a 6 h interval, one familiar object was replaced with a novel object that differed in shape and texture while remaining in the same location, and exploration was recorded for an additional 5 min. Exploration was defined as orientation of the nose toward an object at a distance of 2 cm or less. To control for potential side bias or object preference, object positions and novel-object assignment were fully counterbalanced across mice and groups. The recognition index (%) was calculated as:

2\begin{document}$ RI (\%) = \frac{T_{{\mathrm{novel}}}}{T_{{\mathrm{novel}}}+T_{{\mathrm{familiar}}}} \times 100\% $ \end{document}

where RI represents the recognition index, Tnovel represents the time spent exploring the novel object, and Tfamiliar represents the time spent exploring the familiar object.

Morris water maze (MWM) test

The MWM test was performed in a circular pool (120 cm diameter) containing opaque water maintained at 22±1°C. A circular platform (10 cm diameter) was submerged 1.0 cm below the water surface. During training, mice were given 60 s to locate the platform and were allowed to remain on the platform for 20 s once found. Mice that did not locate the platform within 60 s were gently guided to it. Training consisted of four trials per day for 5 days (consecutive), with an inter-trial interval of 30 min. At 24 h after the last training session, memory retention was assessed in a 60 s probe trial conducted without the platform. Swimming activity was automatically recorded using an overhead video camera connected to a video-tracking system (SuperMaze v.1.0, Shanghai Xinruan Information Technology Co., China).

Measurement of HPA axis-related hormone levels and assessment of depression-like behaviors

Blood samples were collected via tail vein venipuncture from four experimental groups (WT-control, WT-Hap1-KD, APP/PS1-control, and APP/PS1-Hap1-KD) at 4 weeks post-stereotaxic viral injection between 0900h and 1100h. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane prior to venipuncture to minimize distress. Samples were allowed to coagulate for 2 h at 4°C, followed by centrifugation at 3 500 ×g for 20 min, with serum aliquoted and stored at −80°C. Serum levels of CORT, CRH, and ACTH were quantified using commercial ELISA kits. One week after initial blood collection, all groups were subjected to chronic restraint stress (CRS) in 50 mL conical tubes from 09:00 to 13:00 daily for 4 weeks (consecutive). Tail vein blood was collected again 24 h post-CRS for hormone measurements. After a 1 week recovery period, depression-like behaviors were evaluated.

Depression-like behaviors were assessed using the sucrose preference test (SPT) to quantify anhedonia and the forced swim test (FST) to quantify behavioral despair. For SPT, mice were individually housed and acclimated for 24 h to two bottles containing 1% (w/v) sucrose solution. One bottle was then replaced with tap water for 24 h, and bottle positions were switched at 12 h to control for side preference. Prior to testing, mice were water-deprived for 12 h and then presented with two bottles (1% sucrose vs. tap water) with randomized positions. Consumption was measured gravimetrically, with sucrose preference (%) calculated as:

3\begin{document}$ SP (\%) = \frac{C_{{\mathrm{sucrose}}}}{C_{{\mathrm{sucrose}}}+C_{{\mathrm{water}}}} \times 100\% $ \end{document}

Where SP represents the sucrose preference (%), Csucrose represents the consumption of 1% sucrose solution (g), and Cwater represents the consumption of tap water (g).

For FST, mice were individually placed in a 70 cm high plastic cylinder (20 cm diameter) containing water at 24±1°C to a depth of 30 cm for 6 min. Immobility was defined as cessation of active escape-directed movements, with only minimal motions required to keep the head above water. Immobility time during the final 4 min was automatically quantified using FreezeFrame v.5.105 (Actimetrics, USA).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Following transcardial perfusion with ice-cold 0.9% saline and 4% PFA (pH 7.4), brains were post-fixed at 4°C for 24 h, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, and embedded in paraffin. H&E staining was performed using a commercial staining kit according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Coronal sections (5 μm) were stained with hematoxylin solution (7 min), differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid ethanol (5 s), blued under running water (10 min), counterstained with eosin solution (2 min), dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. Histopathological evaluation was performed using bright-field microscopy (Olympus, Japan).

Golgi-Cox staining

Golgi-Cox staining was performed using the SuperGolgi Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Whole brains were dissected post-anesthesia, immersed in Golgi-Cox solution for 10 days under light-tight conditions, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, and sectioned coronally at 200 μm using a vibratome. Sections underwent sequential development, dehydration, clearing, and coverslipping. Hippocampal CA1 pyramidal neurons were imaged using an Olympus BX61VS microscope. Neuronal reconstructions and skeletonization were performed using Fiji/ImageJ (v.1.4) with the NeuronJ plugin. Sholl analysis was performed using the Neuroanatomy plugin to quantify dendritic intersections at radial distances from the soma. Dendritic spine density was quantified manually on secondary and tertiary dendrites using the Cell Counter plugin.

Electrophysiological recording

Acute hippocampal slices (300 µm) were prepared from freshly dissected brains in ice-cold artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) containing (in mmol/L): 124 NaCl, 3.0 KCl, 1.3 MgSO4, 2.5 CaCl2, 26 NaHCO3, 1.25 NaH2PO4, and 10 glucose, continuously oxygenated with 95% O2/5% CO2 (pH 7.4; 300±5 mOsm/kg). Slices recovered in oxygenated ACSF at 32°C for 1 h. During recordings, slices were perfused with oxygenated ACSF (2 mL/min, 32°C) in a submersion chamber. Synaptic responses were evoked using a 64 channel multielectrode array (MED64; Alpha-Med Sciences, Japan) with baseline stimulation (0.033 Hz, 0.2 ms pulse width). LTP was induced by high-frequency stimulation (HFS: 100 Hz for 1 s at 200% baseline intensity). Field excitatory postsynaptic potential (fEPSP) slopes were recorded for 60 min post-HFS, with monitoring, acquisition, and analysis performed using MED64 Mobius (v.2.0). LTP magnitude was calculated as the percentage change in the fEPSP slope relative to baseline (20 min pre-HFS). For statistical analysis, average fEPSP slope during the last 15 min post-HFS was normalized to baseline and expressed as a percentage of baseline.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and transcriptomic analysis

At 4 weeks post-stereotaxic injection, WT-control and WT-Hap1-KD mice (n=4 per group) were anesthetized and decapitated. Hippocampi were rapidly dissected on an ice-cold surface, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and shipped on dry ice to BGI Genomics (China) for total RNA extraction using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and construction of strand-specific RNA-seq libraries using the Optimal Dual-mode mRNA Library Prep Kit (BGI). Libraries were sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads, achieving an average depth of 20 million reads per sample. Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered using SOAPnuke v.2.0 with adapter removal and exclusion criteria of >20% bases with Phred quality score ≤15 or >5% N bases. Clean reads were aligned to the GRCm39 mouse reference genome using HISAT v.2.2.0, yielding a mean alignment rate of 95.42%. Gene expression levels were quantified using RSEM (v.1.3.1). Differential expression analysis and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment were performed using the Dr. Tom Multi-omics Data Mining System (BGI). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DEGseq with thresholds of |log2(fold change)|>0.5 and false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P<0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg method). GO enrichment analysis was conducted using TermFinder, with terms considered significantly enriched at FDR-adjusted P<0.05. Visualization of volcano plots, heatmaps, GO enrichment terms, and GO chord diagrams was performed using the online Bioinformatics platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn).

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are expressed as mean±standard error of the mean (SEM). Sample sizes were determined by power analysis informed by related published studies. Mice were randomly allocated to experimental groups, and investigators were blinded to group assignment during data collection and analysis. For comparisons between two groups, statistical significance was assessed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. For analyses involving multiple groups, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by appropriate post-hoc tests for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism v.9.5.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05. Statistical tests, sample sizes (n), and error bars (SEM) are reported in figure legends.

RESULTS

Down-regulation of Hap1 and GR expression in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice

The hippocampus is essential for learning and memory, and exhibits marked vulnerability to AD pathology. This region also shows abundant expression of GR, consistent with a central role in neural plasticity (Su et al., 2025). Our previous study demonstrated colocalization of Hap1 and GR in the hypothalamus (Chen et al., 2020). In the present study, double immunofluorescence staining confirmed the colocalization of Hap1 and GR in the hippocampus (Figure 1A). To define the temporal patterns of expression, Hap1 and GR levels were quantified in hippocampal tissue from APP/PS1 mice and WT littermates at 2, 3, 6, and 12 months. Quantitative western blotting indicated no significant differences in Hap1 or GR levels at 2 or 3 months compared with age-matched WT controls. In contrast, both proteins showed progressive down-regulation in APP/PS1 mice at 6 and 12 months, with the most pronounced reduction at 12 months (Figure 1B, C). In parallel, Hap1 and GR protein and mRNA levels declined with age in both APP/PS1 and WT mice (Supplementary Figures S1, S2). Importantly, the rate and magnitude of decline were significantly greater in APP/PS1 mice than in WT controls, indicating accelerated loss of Hap1 and GR expression during disease progression.

Impact of hippocampal Hap1 knockdown on learning and memory

The age-dependent down-regulation of Hap1 and GR in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice and its association with AD progression suggest a functional contribution of Hap1 loss to cognitive impairment. Therefore, we hypothesized that Hap1 depletion would directly promote learning and memory deficits. To test this hypothesis, local Hap1 knockdown was induced in the hippocampus of 6-month-old WT and APP/PS1 mice by stereotaxic delivery of viral vectors encoding Cas9 and Hap1-targeting gRNAs. The specificity and efficacy of this approach, as validated in previous research (Chen et al., 2020), were verified in the present study (Supplementary Figures S3–S5). This design yielded four experimental groups: WT-control, WT-Hap1-KD, APP/PS1-control, and APP/PS1-Hap1-KD.

Cognitive performance was assessed using the NOR, Y-maze, and MWM tests (Figure 2A). In the NOR and Y-maze assays, the recognition index and spontaneous alternation rate were significantly reduced in WT-Hap1-KD and APP/PS1-control groups compared with WT-controls, and an additional decrement was observed in APP/PS1-Hap1-KD mice relative to APP/PS1-control mice (Figure 2B, C). During MWM training, escape latency was increased in the APP/PS1-control group compared with WT-control mice, while WT-Hap1-KD mice exhibited increased latency on the final training day relative to WT-controls (Figure 2D). In the probe trial, both the WT-Hap1-KD and APP/PS1-control groups spent significantly less time in the target quadrant than WT-control mice and showed a trend toward fewer platform crossings. Notably, APP/PS1-Hap1-KD mice exhibited further impairment relative to APP/PS1-controls across these metrics (Figure 2E–G). Collectively, these behavioral data indicate that hippocampal Hap1 deficiency impairs learning and memory and exacerbates cognitive dysfunction in AD model mice.

Impact of hippocampal Hap1 knockdown on HPA axis-related serum levels and CRS-induced depression-like behaviors

Given the role of hippocampal GR in regulating the HPA axis and its established interaction with Hap1 (Chen et al., 2020), we hypothesized that Hap1 would modulate stress responses. To determine whether Hap1 deficiency altered endocrine stress responses, serum CORT levels were quantified by ELISA before and after 28 days of CRS in all groups (Figure 3A). Basal (pre-stress) serum CORT levels differed significantly among groups. Specifically, APP/PS1-control mice exhibited higher basal CORT levels than WT-control mice, and both WT-Hap1-KD and APP/PS1-Hap1-KD mice exhibited elevated baseline CORT levels compared with their genotype-matched controls (Figure 3B). CRS increased CORT in all groups; however, post-stress CORT remained higher in Hap1-KD groups relative to corresponding controls (Figure 3C). Similar shifts in serum CRH and ACTH were detected following Hap1 knockdown (Supplementary Figure S6). Together, these endocrine profiles indicate compromised stress adaptation in mice with hippocampal Hap1 deficiency.

Based on established links between chronic stress, GC elevation, and depression-related phenotypes (Keller et al., 2017), behavioral outcomes after CRS were next evaluated. In the FST, immobility time was significantly prolonged in both WT-Hap1-KD mice compared with WT-controls and in APP/PS1-Hap1-KD mice compared with APP/PS1-controls (Figure 3D). In parallel, the SPT revealed marked reductions in sucrose preference in both Hap1-KD groups compared with genotype-matched controls (Figure 3E).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Hap1 deficiency in the hippocampus disrupts HPA axis regulation, increases basal and post-stress CORT, and exacerbates CRS-induced depression-like behaviors.

Impact of hippocampal Hap1 deficiency on AD-related neuropathology

After completion of behavioral testing in WT-control, WT-Hap1-KD, APP/PS1-control, and APP/PS1-Hap1-KD mice, neuropathological endpoints were evaluated to determine whether hippocampal Hap1 loss intensified AD-related tissue injury (Figure 2A). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed significantly higher hippocampal Aβ plaque burden in APP/PS1-Hap1-KD mice compared with APP/PS1-controls (Figure 4A). Consistent with increased pathological burden, H&E staining demonstrated progressive disruption of hippocampal cytoarchitecture across groups. WT-control mice exhibited preserved cellular organization with well-defined neuronal profiles, whereas WT-Hap1-KD and APP/PS1-control mice showed evidence of somatic atrophy and reduced neuronal density. The most pronounced abnormalities were detected in APP/PS1-Hap1-KD tissue, which displayed loss of laminar organization, heterogeneous neuronal size and shape, poorly delineated cellular morphology, and a prominent reduction in neuronal density (Figure 4B). Biochemical assessment supported these histological changes. Western blot analysis revealed decreased NeuN abundance in APP/PS1-control hippocampal lysates relative to WT-control lysates. WT-Hap1-KD mice showed a downward trend in NeuN abundance compared with WT-controls, whereas APP/PS1-Hap1-KD mice showed a further significant reduction compared with APP/PS1-controls (Figure 4C).

Impact of Hap1 deficiency on hippocampal synaptic function

Because synaptic impairment represents an early driver of cognitive decline in AD (Cao & Zhang, 2024; Selkoe, 2002), synaptic structure and plasticity were further examined after hippocampal Hap1 knockdown. At 4 weeks after viral delivery, Golgi-Cox staining revealed significant reductions in dendritic branching complexity and dendritic spine density in hippocampal pyramidal neurons in WT-Hap1-KD and APP/PS1-Hap1-KD mice relative to genotype-matched control groups (Figure 5A–G). The functional consequences for synaptic plasticity were then assessed in hippocampal CA1 by measuring LTP in the Schaffer collateral (SC) to CA1 pathway using fEPSP recordings. As expected, APP/PS1-control mice exhibited significantly reduced fEPSP slopes relative to WT-controls, consistent with impaired synaptic potentiation in the disease model. Notably, Hap1 knockdown produced additional impairment, with significant reductions in fEPSP slope in both WT-Hap1-KD and APP/PS1-Hap1-KD mice compared with their corresponding controls (Figure 5H, I).

Impact of Hap1 down-regulation on the GR-BDNF pathway

To delineate pathways linked to cognitive impairment following Hap1 down-regulation, RNA-seq was performed on hippocampal tissue from WT-control and WT-Hap1-KD mice. Differential expression analysis revealed widespread transcriptomic remodeling in WT-Hap1-KD mice, with group separation evident in volcano plot visualization and hierarchical clustering of DEGs (Figure 6A, B; Supplementary Table S2). Notably, Hap1 depletion resulted in a significant down-regulation of Bdnf expression (Figure 6B). GO analysis of DEG associations revealed significant enrichment in cellular components, including postsynaptic and presynaptic membranes, and in biological processes, including GR signaling, neurotrophic signaling, memory, and synaptic plasticity (Figure 6C). Prior studies have established functional coupling between GR signaling and BDNF-dependent pathways in regulation of synaptic plasticity (Finsterwald & Alberini, 2014; Su et al., 2025). Consistent with this connection, GO chord visualization linked Bdnf to terms related to GR signaling, synaptic plasticity, and memory (Supplementary Figure S7).

Independent validation supported the RNA-seq results. Notably, qPCR detected no change in Gr mRNA abundance after Hap1 knockdown, whereas Bdnf mRNA abundance decreased significantly (Figure 6D). At the protein level, western blotting revealed significant reductions in both BDNF and GR after Hap1 knockdown (Figure 6E). This pattern aligns with prior evidence that Hap1 supports GR stability and Hap1 depletion reduces GR protein half-life (Chen et al., 2020). As reduced GR signaling can suppress BDNF expression (Ridder et al., 2005), the combined reduction in Bdnf transcript abundance and BDNF protein abundance is consistent with diminished GR-dependent transcriptional output. The preservation of Gr mRNA abundance supports post-transcriptional control as the dominant mechanism underlying GR protein loss.

To investigate how Hap1 regulates GR protein levels, their physical interaction was examined. Although we previously identified an interaction between Hap1 and GR in murine brain tissue (Chen et al., 2020), co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) further detected association among Hap1, GR, and dynactin subunit p150Glued (Figure 6F). p150Glued is known to bind to Hap1 and participate in microtubule-based cargo transport (Engelender et al., 1997). These results suggest that Hap1 is involved in GR intracellular trafficking, providing a mechanistic framework linking Hap1 down-regulation to disruption of the GR-BDNF axis.

Impact of Hap1 depletion on GR ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation

To define the mechanism underlying reduced GR abundance after Hap1 loss, GR ubiquitination and proteasome-dependent degradation were evaluated in N2a cells. Endogenous Hap1 was disrupted by CRISPR/Cas9, and immunoblotting confirmed a marked reduction of GR protein abundance in Hap1-KD cells compared with controls (Figure 7A). To quantify GR ubiquitination, cells were treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 and subjected to co-IP with an anti-GR antibody. Analysis revealed a pronounced increase in polyubiquitinated GR in Hap1-KD cells (Figure 7B, C). Together, these results indicate that Hap1 depletion promotes GR polyubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation, thereby reducing GR protein stability.

Impact of Hap1 depletion on GR nuclear translocation

Ligand-dependent GR translocation to the nucleus is required for transcriptional programs that support synaptic plasticity and cognitive function (Su et al., 2025). To determine whether Hap1 loss alters GR nuclear trafficking, endogenous Hap1 was disrupted in N2a cells and GR activation was induced with dexamethasone (Dex). Cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions were isolated at 0, 0.25, 0.5, and 1 h post-treatment for western blot analysis. Notably, Hap1 deficiency significantly delayed Dex-induced GR nuclear translocation. Control cells showed rapid GR enrichment in the nuclear fraction within 0.25 h, whereas Hap1-KD cells showed a delayed increase that became evident at 0.5 h (Figure 8A, B). By 1 h, GR abundance was reduced in both cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions in Hap1-KD cells compared with control cells (Figure 8C). Collectively, these results indicate that Hap1 is required for efficient ligand-induced GR nuclear translocation and for maintaining GR protein stability.

DISCUSSION

Hap1 was initially identified as a huntingtin-interacting protein through yeast two-hybrid screening and is predominantly expressed in the nervous system. Two major isoforms, Hap1A and Hap1B, have been described, with divergence in C-terminal sequence lengths (residues 579–599 in Hap1A and 579–629 in Hap1B) (Li et al., 1995). Although human Hap1 exhibits high sequence similarity to rodent Hap1A (Li et al., 1998), regional brain distribution differs across species. In rodents, Hap1 enrichment is concentrated in the hypothalamus, limbic structures, and brainstem (Chen et al., 2022; Fujinaga et al., 2004), whereas primates, including monkeys and humans, show robust Hap1 expression in the cerebral cortex, hippocampus, and striatum (Chen et al., 2022; Li et al., 1998). Beyond its aberrant interaction with mutant huntingtin in Huntington’s disease, Hap1 also interacts with pathogenic proteins implicated in other neurodegenerative disorders, including androgen receptor (AR) in spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy, ataxin-3 and TATA-binding protein in spinocerebellar ataxias, and AHI1 protein in Joubert syndrome (Prigge & Schmidt, 2007; Sheng et al., 2008; Takeshita et al., 2006, 2011). Although in vitro studies suggest a role for Hap1 in the regulation of amyloid precursor protein (APP) trafficking (Yang et al., 2012), its relevance to AD remains largely unexplored.

This study identified a pronounced reduction of Hap1 in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice compared with age-matched WT littermates. Notably, Hap1 transcript and protein abundance declined progressively with age in both genotypes, with an accelerated age-associated decline in APP/PS1 mice, consistent with an interaction between aging and AD-like pathology that accelerates Hap1 loss. To establish a causal link between Hap1 down-regulation and cognitive impairment, Hap1 expression was knocked down in the hippocampus of WT and APP/PS1 mice. Compared with WT-controls, WT-Hap1-KD mice exhibited significant cognitive deficits accompanied by reduced dendritic branching complexity, decreased dendritic spine density, and impaired LTP. Moreover, APP/PS1-Hap1-KD mice showed increased Aβ plaque burden, consistent with prior observations that Hap1 deletion elevates Aβ levels in cultured cortical neurons derived from APP/PS1 mice (Yang et al., 2012). In combination with APP/PS1 pathology, Hap1 deficiency further worsened synaptic impairment and learning and memory performance relative to APP/PS1-control mice. Overall, these observations indicate that hippocampal Hap1 down-regulation contributes causally to AD-related synaptic and cognitive deficits.

To resolve molecular pathways that could account for these phenotypes while minimizing confounding effects of established AD pathology, transcriptomic profiling was performed in WT hippocampus after Hap1 knockdown. RNA-seq identified significant down-regulation of Bdnf in the hippocampus of WT-Hap1-KD mice compared with WT controls. Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis implicated Bdnf in GR signaling, synaptic plasticity, and memory. These results extended prior evidence showing that Hap1 interacts with GR and that Hap1 loss reduces GR protein levels and half-life (Chen et al., 2020). Mechanistic analyses showed increased GR ubiquitination after Hap1 loss with consequent reduction in GR protein abundance, consistent with ubiquitin-proteasome-mediated turnover, while Gr mRNA abundance remained unchanged. These data support post-transcriptional control of GR as the dominant mechanism for GR depletion after Hap1 loss. Prior studies have shown that GR deficiency reduces hippocampal BDNF expression, while GR overexpression elevates BDNF levels (Ridder et al., 2005). Furthermore, GR has been shown to regulate Bdnf transcription through modulation of CREB activity (Kassel & Herrlich, 2007). Together, the data support a model in which Hap1 loss destabilizes GR at the protein level, limiting GR-dependent transcriptional output and reducing BDNF expression, thereby promoting synaptic failure and cognitive impairment.

At the molecular level, GR is retained in the cytoplasm within a multi-chaperone complex that includes Hsp90, Hsp70, co-chaperones such as p23, and immunophilins that contain peptidyl-prolyl isomerase (PPIase) domains, including FK506-binding proteins (Pratt & Toft, 1997). These immunophilins serve as adaptor elements that couple this complex to cytoplasmic dynein through protein-protein interactions (Galigniana et al., 2001; Silverstein et al., 1999). Ligand binding shifts the complex into an activated state and initiates rapid microtubule-dependent retrograde transport toward the nucleus (Galigniana et al., 2001). After nuclear entry, GR regulates transcription through binding of glucocorticoid response elements (GREs) and through modulation of transcription factors, including CREB (Kassel & Herrlich, 2007), thereby shaping gene programs required for synaptic plasticity and learning and memory (Su et al., 2025). Hap1 functions as a neuronal cytoplasmic adaptor with central coiled-coil domains and multiple N-myristoylation sites that are frequently observed in proteins involved in intracellular trafficking and membrane-associated transport (Li & Li, 2005). Hap1 interacts with dynactin subunit p150Glued and supports microtubule-based cargo transport (Engelender et al., 1997). In vitro evidence further indicates that Hap1 (amino acids 1–473) directly binds to the GR ligand-binding domain (Fujinaga et al., 2011). Co-immunoprecipitation in mouse hippocampal tissue supported a complex containing Hap1, GR, and p150Glued. In parallel, Hap1 knockdown impaired ligand-dependent GR nuclear translocation, consistent with a requirement for Hap1 in efficient microtubule-dependent delivery of activated GR complexes to the nucleus. Importantly, GR complexes that fail to complete nuclear translocation are ubiquitinated by C-terminus of HSC70-interacting protein (CHIP) and subsequently targeted for proteasomal degradation (Galigniana et al., 2004). This sequence provides a mechanistic explanation for the coupled phenotypes observed after Hap1 depletion, including delayed nuclear translocation and decreased GR protein levels.

Hippocampal Hap1 knockdown also increased basal and stress-induced CORT levels. A plausible interpretation involves impaired hippocampal GR signaling, characterized by reduced GR protein levels and disrupted nuclear translocation, which compromised GR-mediated negative feedback control of HPA axis activity. In agreement with an endocrine phenotype characterized by insufficient feedback restraint, Hap1 knockdown exacerbated chronic stress-induced depression-like behaviors, with greater severity in APP/PS1 mice. These results support a mechanistic convergence between AD pathology and depression-related phenotypes.

Despite these insights, this study has several limitations. First, mechanistic inference for the Hap1-GR-BDNF axis relied primarily on the APP/PS1 Aβ mouse model, and generalization to tauopathy-driven AD models and other neurodegenerative settings requires further validation. Second, given the broader role of Hap1 in intracellular trafficking, its deficiency is likely to impact cognition and AD-related pathology through mechanisms beyond defective GR nuclear translocation. Third, the present focus on hippocampal manipulation did not address potential contributions from Hap1 alteration in additional vulnerable regions, such as the cortex and entorhinal cortex, to system-level dysfunction. Fourth, the lack of rescue experiments, including restoration of GR signaling or Hap1 expression, limited causal attribution for reversal of synaptic and cognitive impairment. Fifth, a potential parallel contribution through APP/Aβ dysregulation remains plausible based on previous in vitro evidence (Yang et al., 2012) but requires direct in vivo experimental validation. Finally, translational validation in human tissue remains incomplete, including associations among Hap1 expression, GR-BDNF pathway integrity, cognitive measures, and HPA axis markers in postmortem AD brains. Future studies addressing these gaps will be crucial to solidify the clinical relevance of this pathway.

In conclusion, this study identified hippocampal Hap1 down-regulation as a key driver of AD-associated synaptic and cognitive impairment through ubiquitin-mediated destabilization of GR together with impairment of ligand-dependent GR nuclear translocation. This Hap1-GR-BDNF axis represents a novel pathogenic pathway in AD, highlighting Hap1 restoration as a potential therapeutic target for mitigating synaptic failure in neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric disorders.
