Exogenous microbial consortia modulate rhizosphere microbiome and yield of grafted tomato grown in the mediterranean greenhouse
Authors: Nicolas Al Achkar, Grete Francesca Privitera, Donata Arena, Roberta Nicotra, Luca Ciccarello, Giulio Flavio Rizzo, Alfredo Pulvirenti, Matteo Spatafora, Cristina Restuccia, Ferdinando Branca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe adoption of sustainable agricultural practices for intensive horticultural production could determine less damage to the ecosystem is a fundamental need increasing worldwide. In this trial the effect of two commercial microbial consortia, applied on two hybrid rootstocks of tomato grafted by two scions, were evaluated both on yield components and on the compositions of the rhizosphere microbiome. The rhizosphere was collected from each grafting combination, in both treated and non-treated plots. Microbiome DNA extracted was then sequenced by amplifying two specific regions ITS1-1F for fungus and 16SV34 for bacteria.ResultsAt the morphological level, the effect of microbial consortia application on the total production and yield showed to be highly dependent on the grafting combination, yield increased by 9.1, 10.3 and 12.6% in treated plots of Auto S2, R1/S1 and R1/S2 respectively but registered a reduction of 22.4% in NG.S2 and 9.3% in R2/S2 plots. The metagenomic sequencing revealed that fungal community composition was significantly influenced by both grafting combinations and microbial treatments (especially on the relative abundance of major phyla; Ascomycota and Basidiomycota), whereas bacterial communities exhibited stronger shifts in response to microbial consortia application than to grafting combinations. Correlation analysis between the rhizosphere microbial taxa, yield, and root weight highlighted significant associations supporting the potential of combined use of these practices. Notably, although the inoculated microorganisms were detected at low abundance or were not detectable in treated soils, pronounced shifts in the overall microbiome structure were observed, suggesting indirect yet significant ecological effects of the consortia.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that microbial consortia and grafting synergistically enhance tomato productivity and modulate rhizosphere microbial communities in the monoculture degraded soil under intensive Mediterranean greenhouse conditions. These findings advance current understanding of plant genotype × microbial consortium interactions by demonstrating that microbial inoculant relevant effects are highly modulated by plant genotype and can indirectly restructure rhizosphere microbial assemblages, contributing to the development of more sustainable and resilient horticultural systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08962-4.

Background

Intensive greenhouse production systems often rely on repeated monoculture, a practice that progressively accelerates the incidence of soil borne diseases [1]. In addition, declines in soil organic matter, accompanied with high salinity levels, have reduced the soil microbial diversity and favored the proliferation of pathogenic organisms. The reduction of the soil microbial diversity compromised the key functions of the soil, particularly in relation to the nutrient cycling and the disease suppression, reducing the plant vigor and its related resilient metabolism [2].

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) is the most widely cultivated vegetable crops worldwide, with approximately 192.3 million tons produced (FAOSTAT, 2023). It is especially important in the Mediterranean region, where major producers include Italy, Spain, Turkey, and Egypt (FAOSTAT, 2023), underscoring its economic relevance to southern Europe and North Africa [3]. However, the long production cycles typical of the traditional mediterranean greenhouse systems, combined with the limited land availability, and the continuous market demand, frequently lead growers to adopt tomato monoculture [4]. As a result, disease pressure increases alongside soil degradation and declining microbial diversity, with pathogenic fungi, nematodes, bacteria, and viruses often becoming dominant over the beneficial soil microorganisms [5]. Among these constraints, root-knot nematodes (RKNs; Meloidogyne spp.) are particularly damaging. Beyond direct root injury, RKNs can disrupt the structure of rhizosphere bacterial and fungal communities, thereby altering plant–microbe interactions and potentially facilitating opportunistic pathogens [6–7].

Traditional management of soil-borne diseases and nematodes has included resistant cultivars, chemical fumigants [8], physical methods such as solarization and steam sterilization [9, 10], and agronomic practices including rotation and sanitation [11]. Since the chemical fumigant “methyl bromide” has been forbidden in 2005, due to environmental and human-health concerns [12], the development of sustainable alternatives has become a priority. Among these, two widely adopted strategies are (i) grafting susceptible scions onto resistant rootstocks [13] and (ii) applying beneficial microbial consortium [14].

Grafting onto resistant rootstocks can reduce disease incidence and can improve the yield and the plant water use efficiency [15–18]. Increasing evidence also indicates that the rootstock can shape rhizosphere microbial assembly through differences in root exudation profiles, immune signaling, and root architecture [19, 20], potentially mediating plant–microbe–pathogen interactions [21]. Recent high-throughput sequencing studies have further demonstrated that plant cultivars can induce consistent and genotype-dependent shifts in rhizosphere microbial communities in tomato [22] including changes in both bacterial and fungal assemblages, as well as functional groups associated with nutrient cycling and disease suppression [23–25]. These studies suggest the genetic background of the host mediated recruitment of beneficial microorganisms may contribute to enhanced plant performance beyond simple disease resistance, highlighting the importance of considering belowground ecological processes in grafting systems [26]. In parallel, the application of microbial inoculants, often delivered as microbial consortia (e.g., beneficial bacteria, mycorrhizal fungi, and other plant growth promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR)), is promoted as an eco-friendly approach to enhance plant resilience and to restore soil biological functioning [27–30]. Reported outcomes are frequently context-dependent, influenced by host genotype, soil type, and environmental conditions [27, 29]. Recently, studies have focused on multi-strain microbial consortia specifically designed to enhance functional complementarity, showing improved consistency compared to single-strain inoculants in tomato systems [31]. These consortia influence rhizosphere community structure, modulate plant immune responses, and improve nutrient acquisition [32]. However, their effectiveness remains highly context-dependent, being influenced by host genotype, soil properties, and environmental conditions [33]. In many cases, introduced microorganisms establish poorly or remain below efficacy due to competition with resident communities or environmental constraints. Nevertheless, even when persistence is limited, exogenous taxa can indirectly restructure resident microbial communities through competitive and antagonistic interactions, altering the balance between dominant and rare taxa and ultimately affecting plant traits [34, 35].

Despite these considerations, most studies have investigated plant genotype effects and microbial inoculants on the rhizosphere independently, while their combined influence on rhizosphere microbiome assembly remains poorly understood. Only a limited number of studies have addressed the interaction between plant genotype, particularly rootstock–scion combinations [36], and introduced microbial consortia [25, 37], often reporting context-dependent. These conditions, strongly constrain microbial diversity and the establishment of introduced inoculants [38]. Although the global importance of these systems for horticultural production, studies examining the interaction between the two sustainable practices widely used (grafting and microbial consortia) under these specific conditions remain scarce [39]. This limits the transferability of findings from open-field or non-Mediterranean systems to intensive greenhouse production [40]. Therefore, a mechanistic understanding of how plant genotype and microbial inoculation jointly shape rhizosphere microbial assembly is essential to optimize sustainable management strategies.

We hypothesize that the rootstock genotype and microbial consortium inoculation interact to reshape the tomato rhizosphere microbiome in ways that are associated with improved plant performance under Mediterranean greenhouse conditions.

Accordingly, this study aimed to quantify how the grafted plants and the application of microbial consortium shaped the rhizosphere microbiome composition and its diversity in tomato crops grown in cold greenhouse in Mediterranean climatic conditions, and to correlate the different microbiome patterns to plant performance indicators measured over the growing season.

Methods

Plant material and microorganism application

A protocol based on two commercial microbial consortia products, Klappy® and Klaster® (B4Green S.r.l., Catania, Italy), was adopted for growing different combinations of tomato grafted plants to promote root development and enhance resistance to soil-borne pathogens. Both inoculants were formulated primarily with arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi and rhizosphere-associated microorganisms: Klappy® (1% micorrhiza; Glomus mosseae, Glomus intraradices, 102 CFU.g− 1 rhizosphere bacteria and 109 spore.g− 1 of other microorganism; Pochonia Clamidosporia, and Purpureocillium lilacinum, reported in the product Technical data sheet) and Klaster® (1% mycorrhiza; Glomus mosseae, Glomus intraradices and 1010 CFU.g− 1 rhizosphere bacteria; Bacillus firmus, and Streptomyces avermitilis, reported in the product Technical data sheet). Two commercial rootstocks, OptiFort (Bayer) and RS4 (NewBreed), were grafted by two scions: the commercial hybrid MVS101 (F1) (Mondoverde Semilla, Scicli, Italy) and the Sicilian landrace ‘Pizzutello medio’ (UNICT 3309), provided by the genebank of the Department of Agriculture, Food and Environment (Di3A), University of Catania (UNICT). In addition, four control groups were included: self-grafted plants (scion grafted onto itself) and no-grafted plants for each scion (Table 1).

Grafting combinations and treatments used in the present study

Grafting combinations were designated as R*/S* (where R indicates the rootstock and S the scion), followed by TR or NT to indicate treated or non-treated plants. Non-grafted scions were coded as NGS*, and auto-grafted scions as AUTO S*.

Field experiment and plant characterization

Tomato rootstocks and scions were sown in cellular plastic trays filled with “Semina BR Balance” substrate (Vigor Plant Italia SRL Italy), and manually grafted at a high-tech plant nursery (EcoFaber, Modica, Italy). The experiment was conducted in a 300 m² greenhouse located in Sampieri (RG), Italy (36◦43′25’’N, 14◦41′56′′E; 37 m a.s.l) traditionally used for intensive tomato production during the winter growing cycle in Sicily, Italy and characterized by the high level of salinity in the soil. Physicochemical soil analysis made at the beginning of the trial confirmed the sandy clay loam texture of the soil (63.4% Sand, 12% Silt and 24.6% Clay), a pH of 6.9, a total organic matter of 36 g/kg and a high Electric conductivity of 5.31 ds.m− 1 at 25 °C. The full Physicochemical soil analysis is reported in table S1.

A split-plot design with three replicates was used. Within each replicate, the two main plots corresponded to microbial consortia inoculated (treated) and no inoculated (non-treated) ones. Each rootstock/scion combination was assigned to an elementary subplot represented by five plants. In early October 2023 (50 days after sowing and 27 days after grafting) plants were transplanted at 0.40 m within-row spacing and 1.20 m between rows, resulting in eight elementary subplots per main plot. Microbial consortia inoculation was applied to treated plots via fertigation using a Venturi injector connected to the drip-irrigation line, following the supplier’s recommendations. Klappy® (400 mL per 1000 m²; 0.2 g.plant− 1 each application) and Klaster® (300 mL per 1000 m²; 0.15 g.plant− 1 each application) were applied alternately at weekly intervals (i.e., one product per week), starting from the second week after transplant and continuing until first-truss ripening for a total of seven applications of each product. Plants were grown vertically at single stem with continuous lateral shoots pruning, up to the eighth truss and then pruned above the leaf over it. Fruits were harvested by collecting entire trusses. Drip irrigation was done two times per week for the first three weeks (2.66 L per plant) after that irrigation frequency was reduced to one per week but with bigger quantity (4 L per plant). Nitrogen fertigation was done once every 20 days with the application of the organic nitrogen fertilizer (5% N, 10% C, 30% total amino acid) DYNAMIC® (Hydro Fert, Barletta, Italy) starting with a dose of 0.4 mL.plant− 1 to reach 0.8 mL.plant− 1 per application at the full vegetative growth of the plants and during the fruit development. During the growing season the temperature registered inside the greenhouse was monitored by a Watchdog 2500 series weather station (Spectrum Technologies, Inc., USA). It ranged from a minimum of 11.7 °C to a maximum of 39.4 °C and a mean of 27.2 °C during the day and 16.3 °C during the night. Solar radiations were measured once every two weeks with LI-COR Quantum/Radiometer/Photometer (Model LI-250 Light Meter, Lincoln, Nebraska, USA) and registered a mean of 16.2 MJ m− 2 day− 1 inside the greenhouse.

Yield components were calculated by weighing all harvested fruits per each truss the plant produced; the cumulative production from the eight trusses was used to estimate fruit production per plant and the correspondent yield (Y). At the end of the growing season, roots were characterized by recording root their fresh weight and the related size.

Rhizosphere sampling, DNA extraction and microbiome sequencing

Microbiome sampling was performed 120 days after transplanting. For each elementary subplot, fresh root systems were collected at ~ 20 cm soil depth from two plants (processed separately). Roots were gently shaken to remove loosely attached soil and then immersed in 25 mL of sterile distilled water in sterile 50 mL Falcon tubes to suspend rhizosphere material. In total, six replicates were obtained for each rootstock/scion × treatment combination, and samples were stored at − 80 °C until processing.

Rhizosphere genomic DNA was extracted from the solid pellet obtained by centrifuging the root wash suspension. DNA extraction was carried out using the Soil DNA Isolation Plus Kit (Norgen Biotek, Thorold, Canada) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Library preparation, quality control and sequencing

Library preparation, quality control, and sequencing were performed by Novogene (UK) Company Limited (25 Cambridge Park, Milton Road, Cambridge, CB4 0FW, United Kingdom). Bacterial and fungal communities were profiled by amplicon sequencing of the V3–V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene and the internal transcribed spacer 1 region (ITS1), respectively. The 16 S V3–V4 region was amplified using the primer pair F: CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG and R: GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAA, while ITS1 was amplified using ITS1F (5′-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′) and ITS2R (5′-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′). Amplicons of the expected size were verified and selected by agarose gel electrophoresis. Equimolar amounts of PCR products from each sample were pooled, end-repaired and A-tailed, and ligated to Illumina sequencing adapters. Libraries were quantified using Qubit and real-time PCR, and fragment size distributions were assessed with a Bioanalyzer. The quantified libraries were then pooled based on effective library concentration and required data output and sequenced on an Illumina paired-end platform (2 × 250 bp; PE250).

Bioinformatic analysis

Raw FASTQ files were trimmed with Trim Galore [41] to remove sequencing adapters and low-quality reads. The trimmed reads were processed in QIIME 2 (qiime2-amplicon-2024.10) [42]. Paired-end sequences were imported and quality-filtered, then denoised and merged using DADA2, applying a quality threshold of Q30. Alpha diversity (Shannon index and Chao1) and beta diversity (Jaccard, Bray–Curtis, weighted UniFrac, and unweighted UniFrac) were computed. Differences in alpha diversity among groups were tested using either non-parametric methods (Kruskal–Wallis and Wilcoxon) or parametric methods (Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and Student’s t-test), depending on normality as assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Beta-diversity differences were evaluated using PERMANOVA (permutational multivariate analysis of variance). Taxonomic assignment of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) was performed using UNITE (v10) [43] for fungi and Greengenes (v2022) [44] for bacteria. Feature tables were collapsed to generate phylum-, genus-, and species-level abundance tables.

Differential abundance analyses were conducted in R (v4.5.0) using DESeq2 (v1.48.1). Unless otherwise stated, statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Figures were generated using ggplot2 (v3.5.2) [45] and ggpubr (v0.6.0) [46]. Associations between plant morphometric traits and microbial abundances were assessed using Spearman correlation; only correlations with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Morphometric traits

Root weight (RW) and yield (Y) were compared between treated (TR) and non-treated (NT) plants for each grafting combination. For the R2/S2 combination, RW differed significantly between TR and NT plants (p = 0.04), with TR plants showing a lower root weight (mean ± SD: 133.8 ± 6.0) than NT plants (158.0 ± 11.5). This pattern was consistent at the scion level: when data were pooled across rootstocks, S2 plants in the TR group also exhibited significantly lower RW than S2 plants in the NT group (p = 0.04). In addition, comparisons between scions indicated that S2 had a significantly lower RW than S1 under both TR and NT conditions. These results are shown in Figure S1. Microbial consortia application increased yield in some grafting combinations compared to their non-treated controls. The yield of AUTO S2 treated registered a mean of 2.25 kg plant− 1 compared to 2.01 kg plant− 1 in the non-treated plots (Y increase of 10.3%). A similar increase was observed in treated R1/S1 (9.1%) and R1/S2 (12.6%) compared to their respective controls NT (from 3.9 to 4.3 kg plant⁻¹ in R1/S1 and from 2.26 to 2.59 kg plant⁻¹ in R1/S2). In contrast, microbial consortia application, in other grafting combinations, had registered a reduction in NG.S2 and R2/S2 plots respectively. No significant effect was also noted the yield of the Auto S1 and R2/S1 plots. RW and Y measurements are reported in Table S2.

Microbiome profile

QIIME 2 processing yielded 11,747,676 ITS1 reads (mean: 25,558 reads per sample) and 2,466,761 16 S rRNA gene reads (mean: 25,695 reads per sample). Analysis of fungal relative abundances indicated that the fungal taxa listed in the commercial inoculants were not detected in treated (TR) samples, except for auto-grafted S2, which Purpureocillium was detected at low abundance (0.08%). For the bacterial component of the inoculants, Streptomyces was detected (unclassified at species level) with relative abundances ranging from 1 to 4%, whereas Bacillus accounted for < 0.01%. All TR vs. NT pairs were evaluated for alpha and beta diversity. For ITS1 data, a significant difference in alpha diversity (Shannon index) was observed only for R1/S1 (TR vs. NT; p = 0.032; Fig. 1A).

Bacterial alpha diversity differed significantly between treated (TR) and non-treated (NT) S2 roots. As shown in Fig. 1B, the Shannon index was higher in NT samples than in TR samples. This trend remained consistent when the analysis was restricted to self-rooted S2 plants (non-grafted and auto-grafted), with rooted S2 NT samples showing higher Shannon diversity than rooted S2 TR samples (Fig. 1C).

As shown in Fig. 2A, Ascomycota dominated the fungal communities across all treatments, reaching its highest relative abundance in R2/S2 TR samples (80%), whereas R2/S1 NT samples showed the lowest proportion (43.8%). Notably, R1/S2 TR samples contained only 46% Ascomycota, suggesting a marked rootstock-dependent effect on fungal community composition. For Basidiomycota, NT samples generally exhibited higher relative abundances in several combinations (e.g., S2 NT: 3%; R2/S2 NT: 7%; R1/S1 NT: 12.7%). When comparing TR and NT conditions, microbial inoculation appeared to reduce Basidiomycota abundance in combinations involving the R2 rootstock, while showing the opposite trend (an increase) in combinations involving the R1 rootstock.

In contrast, Actinobacteriota decreased in most treated combinations (with the exception of R2/S1); for example, R1/S1 TR showed 7.77% Actinobacteriota compared with 9.73% in R1/S1 NT. Differential abundance analysis

All TR–NT comparisons were conducted at the phylum, genus, and species levels. No clear treatment-associated shifts were detected at the phylum level. At the genus level, however, several differentially abundant taxa emerged. In the fungal ITS dataset, the R1/S1 TR vs. NT comparison revealed a significant decrease in the genera Alternaria, Arthrobotrys, Cephaliophora, Dothideomycetes (unclassified at lower rank), Melanocarpus, Nigrospora, Subramaniula, Thermoascus, and Zopfiella, while Malassezia showed a significant increase. Under the same treatment contrast for R2/S1, Bjerkandera, Microascaceae (unclassified genus), and Thermoascus increased, whereas Parasarocladium and Schizophyllum decreased (Fig. 3A).

In auto-grafted S1, TR vs. NT showed significant increases in Penicillium, Metarhizium, Paragibberella, and Malassezia (Fig. 4A). In auto-grafted S2, treatment was associated with marked increases in Alphoboma, Tolypocladium, Chaetosphaeria, and Purpureocillium (Fig. 4A).

Morphometric traits and microorganism correlations

To assess treatment-associated links between plant traits and the microbiome, morphometric parameters were correlated with absolute genus abundances. In fungi, R1 treated (TR) samples showed a negative correlation between Alternaria abundance and yield (Y), a relationship not detected in non-treated (NT) plants. Root weight (RW) correlated positively with Aspergillus, particularly A. ochraceus, while yield correlated positively with A. niger. Zopfiella displayed opposite correlations with yield in R1 (negative in TR, positive in NT), potentially reflecting species-level heterogeneity not resolved by the analysis. In R2 TR samples, Pseudoidium neolycopersici—the causal agent of tomato powdery mildew—was negatively correlated with yield, and its relative abundance was lower in treated than non-treated R2 plants (0.08% vs. 0.5%; Table S2) [47, 48].

For bacteria, Rhodococcus abundance was negatively correlated with RW in S1 TR and R2 TR samples, and also negatively correlated with yield in S1 NT plants. In S2 TR samples, RW was positively correlated with Flavobacterium, Pseudoxanthomonas, and Achromobacter see Figure S2.

Discussion

In this study, we examined how two eco-friendly agricultural practices; grafting and microbial inoculation, shape tomato rhizosphere microbiomes under intensive greenhouse conditions. Both approaches have been reported independently to enhance yield and crop quality, particularly in disease-prone areas and monoculture systems [49, 50]. Thus, the synergistic effect of applying both techniques simultaneously has not yet been evaluated, particularly in vivo under highly saline and infected soil typical of the traditional mediterranean protected cultivation [51]. Here, we applied a commercial microbial inoculant consortium containing Glomus mosseae, Glomus intraradices, Pochonia chlamydosporia, Purpureocillium lilacinum, Bacillus firmus, and Streptomyces avermitilis (as cited in the technical datasheet of the commercial products). Notably, employing amplicon sequencing, these inoculant taxa did not increase in relative abundance in treated plots compared with controls (with only a marginal signal for Purpureocillium in one group), yet treatment seemed to induce shifts in other members of the rhizosphere community. This pattern limited detectability of inoculant taxa coupled with measurable restructuring of resident communities has been reported in other consortium studies [52, 53] and is consistent with the notion that commercial products can act through indirect mechanisms (e.g., competitive interactions, niche modification, or plant-mediated effects) rather than sustained colonization by inoculated organisms [54].

Fungal microbiome

Fungal alpha diversity (ITS) responded to treatment in a graft-combination–specific manner. The significant increase in Shannon diversity observed in R1/S1 treated plants (p = 0.032) suggests that microbial inoculation promoted a more even and diverse fungal community in this context [55], a feature often associated with higher functional redundancy and greater resistance to disturbance, including pathogen invasion [56]. At the taxonomic level, shifts in the relative abundance of major phyla (Ascomycota and Basidiomycota) further indicated that both inoculation and rootstock genotype contributed to community restructuring. The contrasting Basidiomycota response between rootstocks (decreasing with treatment in R2 but increasing in R1) supports a genotype-by-treatment interaction that likely reflects differences in root traits and rhizosphere conditions that select for distinct fungal guilds [36, 57]. Genus-level differential abundance provided more ecologically interpretable signals. In R1/S1, treatment reduced several genera that include known pathogens or opportunists (e.g., Alternaria and Nigrospora) [58, 59], consistent with a shift toward a less disease-favorable rhizosphere. Importantly, Alternaria abundance was negatively correlated with yield in treated R1 plants, supporting a link between reduced pathogen-associated taxa and improved performance [60]. In contrast, treatment also reduced Arthrobotrys, a genus widely studied for nematode antagonism, including biocontrol of root-knot nematodes via trapping structures [61]. Rather than contradicting a beneficial treatment effect, this decrease could indicate reduced nematode pressure in treated plots (and thus weaker selection for nematode-trapping fungi), or competitive displacement by other antagonists promoted under treatment [62]. Finally, Malassezia consistently increased across multiple treated groups. Although Malassezia is best known from animal-associated microbiomes [63], its occurrence in soil has been documented, and dispersal via soil fauna has been proposed [64]. The repeated enrichment of Malassezia suggests a treatment- and/or host-mediated niche shift that merits functional follow-up, particularly given its consistency across otherwise different plant types and grafting conditions.

Bacterial microbiome

Bacterial alpha diversity showed a contrasting pattern from fungi in specific contexts: S2 roots (including self-rooted controls) exhibited higher Shannon diversity in non-treated than treated plants. While reduced diversity can sometimes indicate community simplification, it can also reflect increased dominance of taxa better adapted to the treated rhizosphere, potentially improving specific functions. In line with this, phylum-level profiles were largely stable, whereas genus/species-level analyses revealed targeted treatment-associated changes, consistent with functional reorganization without broad compositional turnover.

Several signals point to rootstock-dependent microbiome modulation. The decrease of Sphingobacterium nematocida in treated RS4 (R2/S1) plants, but not in OptiFort (R1/S1), suggests that the impact of inoculation on putatively nematode associated bacteria depend on host genotype and/or local microbial context [65]. Conversely, the enrichment of Nitrosotenium in treated R1/S1 plants is noteworthy because ammonia-oxidizing archaea can influence nitrification and nitrogen availability in the rhizosphere [66], potentially contributing to improved nutrient turnover under treatment. Rhizobium displayed a complex, context-dependent response, decreasing in treated rootstock–S1 comparisons but increasing in treated S2 contexts (including non-grafted S2). Given the broader plant growth promoting roles attributed to Rhizobium beyond classical symbiosis, this pattern likely reflects genotype-specific root exudation and competitive dynamics shaping recruitment and persistence [67]. Auto-grafted plants further deviated from grafted and non-grafted patterns (e.g., Pseudorhizobium decreases in Auto S1 and Bryobacter increases in Auto S2), reinforcing that grafting configuration itself can alter the microbial habitat.

Trait–microbe correlations provided additional functional hypotheses. The negative association between Rhodococcus and root weight in treated samples, together with its negative association with yield in S1 non-treated plants, suggests that this genus may behave as a context-dependent indicator of rhizosphere status rather than a simple “beneficial” marker. Although Rhodococcus includes taxa linked to biodegradation and biocontrol potential [68–70], its abundance may rise under specific stress or substrate conditions, and correlations should therefore be interpreted cautiously. In S2 treated plants, the positive correlation of root weight with Flavobacterium, Pseudoxanthomonas, and Achromobacter is consistent with a rhizosphere more enriched in taxa associated with organic matter turnover, nutrient cycling, and plant-growth–promoting traits [71, 72]. Together, these patterns suggest that inoculation reshaped bacterial community function more than high-level taxonomy, and that the direction and magnitude of change depended strongly on grafting genotype.

Earlier agricultural microbiome studies have often emphasized management contrasts (e.g., organic vs. conventional systems [73, 74] or tillage [75]) while less frequently integrating host genetic effects and their interaction with management. Using tomato grafting as a model, our results reinforce that rootstock genotype is a major determinant of rhizosphere microbiome assembly [36]. Genotype-specific traits such as root architecture and exudation profiles can modulate the recruitment of microbial taxa [76], and are also known to influence plant physiological responses and fruit quality under specific stress conditions [77–78]. Our data align with this framework by showing rootstock-dependent numbers and identities of differentially abundant taxa. Importantly, the variable response of graft combinations to microbial inoculation highlights that inoculant efficacy cannot be assumed to be uniform across host genotypes, consistent with previous reports that host genetics can condition outcomes of microbial consortia application [79–81]. Overall, these findings support the need for genotype-informed deployment of microbial inoculants and for mechanistic studies that link rootstock traits, microbiome shifts, and agronomic outcomes.

Despite the limitations in the quantitative assessment of the inoculated taxa particularly, the absence of a positive control and of a qPCR-based quantification, the study highlights the interaction between grafting combinations and microbial inoculation for shaping fungal and bacterial assemblages, with potential implications for plant the health and productivity. Furthermore, the lack of a carrier-only control and the absence of viable cell count data for the commercial product limit definitive attribution of the observed effects to the inoculated microorganisms, independently of potential contributions from the carrier substances. Nevertheless, the consistency of the observed patterns suggests a likely contribution of the microbial amendment to rhizosphere community structuring the tomato plant growth and development especially when it is grown in sub-optimal highly saline and infected by soil-borne diseases, as usual for the traditional mediterranean cold greenhouse farming systems, where they are tested.

Conclusions

This study highlights the effects of grafting and microbial inoculation as agricultural practices shaping the rhizosphere microbiome and its community structure. While the inoculated taxa were not consistently detected using amplicon sequencing, the observed shifts in the overall microbiome suggest that the treatments influenced microbial community composition, potentially through indirect ecological interactions rather than persistent colonization.

Furthermore, interactions between grafting combinations and microorganism application were observed, indicating a genotype-dependent modulation of microbial community responses. These findings underscore the importance of plant genotype in shaping the activity and outcomes of microbial consortia. Future work should incorporate quantitative approaches, such as targeted qPCR, alongside appropriate controls to confirm the definitive establishment of inoculated taxa and their efficiency. Moreover, future research should aim to resolve the functional roles of unclassified species present and to clarify the role of the environmental conditions on the exogenous rootstocks, particularly under biotic stress conditions, to inform the rational design of microbial consortia for enhanced plant performance and resilience.
