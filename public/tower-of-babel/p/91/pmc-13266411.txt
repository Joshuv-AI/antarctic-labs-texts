Distinct Physiological Mechanisms Drive Grey Matter Plasticity in Complex Versus Simple Sequence Learning
Authors: Jhelum Paul, A. T. P. Jäger, J. Huck, C. L. Tardif, A. Villringer, C. J. Gauthier, P. L. Bazin, Christopher J. Steele
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUltra‐high field magnetic resonance imaging at sub‐millimeter resolution opens the possibility to assess subtle longitudinal changes in brain structure and function. Identifying the regions and putative mechanisms involved in learning specific motor sequences is a key step towards understanding neuroplasticity. To disambiguate sequence‐specific learning from simple motor execution, the present study trained an experimental group over five consecutive days on a complex motor sequence and contrasted them with a control group who performed a simple sequence. Both groups were scanned at 4 time points with magnetic resonance imaging at 7 Tesla with MP2RAGE. Training‐related grey matter (GM) structural plasticity was assessed with voxel‐based morphometry (VBM: time by group interaction) on T1w uniform intensity images and followed up with a focused investigation of quantitative T1 values (qT1) to probe the physiological changes driving GM plasticity. Our interaction analyses identified significant differences in the precuneus, superior parietal cortex (SPC), and angular gyrus (AG) where GM increases were greater in the experimental group. Of these regions, only the left SPC (during slow learning) exhibited sequence‐specific plasticity—where the magnitude of change was greater in the experimental vs. the control group. Interestingly, while the experimental group showed increased GM volume, the control group was characterized by decreases. Post hoc comparisons revealed that the control group exhibited increasing T1 across days 1, 2 and 5, while the experimental group initially increased (day 1 to 2) and then decreased (day 2 to 5), suggesting that the two groups underwent differential physiological change as a function of training. These findings align with invasive animal studies showing that learning simple tasks is characterized by angiogenesis while learning complex sequences initially leads to synaptogenesis and is then followed by intracortical myelination as the sequence is well learned. Our findings provide initial evidence in humans that learning a complex sequence induces GM increases that are likely driven by synaptogenesis for initial encoding and myelogenesis for later learning and consolidation, while habituating to a simple repeated task leads to GM decreases due to attenuated blood flow.

Introduction

Daily activities like driving, typing and playing musical instruments involve executing a series of motor actions in a specific order, requiring precise motor coordination and practice to achieve proficiency. Research on humans and non‐human animals shows strong evidence for structural and functional reorganization as a result of learning new movement sequences—sequence learning (Coynel et al. 2010; Draganski et al. 2004, 2006; Gryga et al. 2012; Hikosaka, Nakamura, et al. 2002; Hikosaka, Rand, et al. 2002; Hyde et al. 2009; Karim et al. 2017; Kleim et al. 1996; Scholz et al. 2009; Steele and Penhune 2010; Sun et al. 2007; Taubert et al. 2010; Yokoi and Diedrichsen 2019). The majority of complex motor skills involve grouping movements into sequences, which has led to extensive research on how functional changes reflect motor sequence performance and learning in the human brain (Berlot et al. 2020; Duff et al. 2007; Karni et al. 1995, 1998; for review see, Doyon 2008; Doyon and Benali 2005). In contrast, fewer studies have sought to identify how brain structure undergoes plastic change as a function of sequence learning, particularly regarding which grey matter (GM) changes are linked with sequence‐specific learning (Draganski et al. 2006; Filippi et al. 2010; Gryga et al. 2012; Tavor et al. 2020). How these regions exhibit plasticity across the learning stages remains unclear, and few studies investigate the links between MRI‐measured structural change and the underlying physiological change (Sampaio‐Baptista et al. 2013; Tardif et al. 2016). Evaluating the overall architecture responsible for structural plasticity that is involved in learning a complex sequence task will deepen our understanding and present a novel perspective on motor sequence learning.

Motor sequence learning (MSL) involves the development of neural representations and connections enabling the execution of the sequence with greater speed, accuracy and efficiency over time (Doyon et al. 2018; Steele et al. 2012; Wiestler and Diedrichsen 2013). This has been identified in the literature as consisting of a minimum of two phases: an initial learning phase, often known as fast/early learning where there are rapid improvements within a single training session, followed by a slow/late learning phase during which progress is made gradually over multiple sessions of practice‐ spaced by hours and/or days (Dayan and Cohen 2011; Doyon and Benali 2005; Doyon and Ungerleider 2002; Gonzalez and Burke 2018; Karni et al. 1995; Ungerleider et al. 2002). Learning complex motor sequences leads to long‐lasting, experience‐dependent functional restructuring of the adult primary motor cortex (Karni et al. 1995, 1998). Sequence learning is characterized by rapid skill improvement in the early phase that is dependent on corticostriatal and corticocerebellar networks (increased activity in the cerebellum, prefrontal cortex, and lateral premotor cortex, along with decreased visual processing) (Berlot et al. 2020; Doyon 2008; Jenkins et al. 1994; Penhune and Doyon 2005; Penhune and Steele 2012; Steele et al. 2012). As learning proceeds and the skill becomes more fixed (i.e., consolidated), motor processing shifts primarily to the corticostriatal circuit, particularly within the associative/premotor and sensorimotor networks (Coynel et al. 2010; Debas et al. 2014; Doyon 2008; Doyon et al. 2002; Karim et al. 2017; Penhune and Doyon 2002; Ungerleider et al. 2002). Overall, MSL involves complicated activity changes across various timescales (Wymbs and Grafton 2015), with decades of research illustrating the functional neural patterns associated with different learning stages. However, there is a lack of research on how structural alterations support the acquisition of sequences of movements (sequence‐specific learning) across the learning stages. In addition, there has been relatively little work to distinguish between structural plasticity linked with general task demands versus plasticity linked to acquiring a specific acquisition of a sequence. This is of key importance, as it can help inform targeted skill development to enhance rehabilitation strategies for motor impairment (Dahms et al. 2020).

Sequence‐specificity focuses on the learning of specific sequential associations within a motor sequence. Sequence‐specific learning plays a vital role in skill acquisition, performance improvement, and differentiation of learning processes. This type of learning enhances planning processes both before and concurrently with motor execution, starting with motor preparation and optimization of sequenced movements (Ariani and Diedrichsen 2019; Babu 2020). Previous research has separated these two different aspects of motor behaviour (complex sequence‐specific learning vs. motor execution) through rsfMRI or task‐based functional studies (Jäger et al. 2022; Penhune 2013; Seidler et al. 2002; Steele and Penhune 2010), as well as through observations of WM microstructure (Scholz et al. 2009; Tremblay et al. 2021) and cortical surface morphometry (Yokoi and Diedrichsen 2019). Starting with the pioneering study of Karni and colleagues in 1995, which demonstrated rapid within‐session functional changes during MSL, there is strong evidence that learning is characterized by rapid changes in brain function that reflect gradual improvements in performance throughout different phases (Karni et al. 1995, 1998). Identifying the corresponding structural change due to learning a complex motor sequence can provide additional insight into the underlying mechanisms of brain plasticity associated with MSL.

Morphological changes in the brain can occur over the course of hours, days, months, or years of training, with evidence showing that GM volume (GMV) is a sensitive marker of GM structure that may be linked to neuroplastic change (Boyke et al. 2008; Draganski et al. 2004, 2006; Driemeyer et al. 2008; Mediavilla et al. 2022; Olivo et al. 2022; Taubert et al. 2010, 2016; Wenger et al. 2017). While sensitive, GMV is physiologically unspecific; therefore, any identified “changes” in brain structure identified with GMV cannot be used to directly infer which physiological mechanisms lead to structural modifications. These structural modifications often reflect alterations in neuronal architecture and connectivity through multiple processes such as synaptogenesis, dendritic arborisation, and intracortical myelin adaptation (Kleim et al. 1996, 1997; McKenzie et al. 2014; Turner and Greenough 1985; Volkmar and Greenough 1972; Zatorre et al. 2012), all of which are impossible to disentangle using standard GMV analysis approaches performed on T1‐weighted (T1w) images. In contrast, quantitative T1 imaging provides T1 values that are more closely linked to biophysical tissue parameters (Tardif et al. 2015, 2016) and, in the context of plasticity, may be more directly tied to the degree of cortical myelination, cellular density, and/or phospholipid/fat/fluid content (Filo et al. 2019; Shir et al. 2024; Stüber et al. 2014). Since the vast majority of studies of GM plasticity in humans rely on T1w imaging and GMV, the physiological mechanisms that underlie structural alterations have yet to be fully explored or identified.

GMV alterations have often been investigated with “pre/post” frameworks where volume changes are typically measured before and after sequence training sessions (Gryga et al. 2012; Penhune and Steele 2012; Steele et al. 2012). However, traditional pre/post designs may miss critical intermediate stages of structural plasticity provided the rapid timescale of these changes and fail to capture the temporal dynamics of brain adaptation during learning. Thus, the extent to which structural brain changes are accompanied by neuronal processes that are related to learning, particularly in complex motor sequential tasks, remains unclear. Learning can be measured with various metrics, such as post‐training performance, average performance during training, or the rate of improvement. These measurements are influenced by study designs, which range from cross‐sectional (over a single day) to longitudinal (over days, weeks, or months of training). To better analyze neuroplastic structural changes and account for varying sensitivity of behavioural learning metrics to detect training‐related improvements, we designed a longitudinal study where participants engaged in a complex visuomotor task using their dominant hand over a 5‐day training period, followed by a retention test 12 days later. Structural MRI scans, including Quantitative T1 imaging (MP2RAGE), were acquired four times across training and once at retention—with participants randomly assigned to either a learning (sequence‐specific training) or control group (matched for motor execution). This design allowed for the identification of structural plasticity that is linked to the acquisition of sequence‐specific skill as opposed to non‐sequence‐specific changes that might occur with general motor practice in the control group. Based on our previous functional and microstructural findings, we hypothesized that there would be distinct sequence‐specific GMV changes observed in different learning stages. Crucially, we also hypothesized that the underlying physiological changes driving GM alterations could be identified by assessing T1 changes in regions exhibiting significant sequence‐specific change.

Methods

Participants

All data used in this study were collected at the Max Planck Institute for Human Cognitive and Brain Sciences in Leipzig, Germany as part of a larger multimodal project (Jäger et al. 2022, 2024; Tremblay et al. 2021). Forty right‐handed healthy individuals (20–32 years old, M ± SD: 24.5 ± 2.44, 21 females) with no record of neurological disorder and with no evidence of any previous motor or non‐correctable visual impairments were enrolled in the study. Subjects who did not meet the protocol criteria and/or had contraindications for the MRI measurements were excluded from participation. Participants completed demographic questionnaires and the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (EHI) (M ± SD: 83.7 ± 16.9) to confirm right‐handedness (Oldfield 1971). Prior to the task, participants were asked about their years of musical and athletic experience to account for individual differences that could affect motor learning. All participants were task naive prior to this study and provided written informed consent according to the declaration of Helsinki. Participants were randomly distributed into two groups: the experimental group (LRN: N = 20) and the control group (SMP: N = 20) who performed a complex and a simple repetitive visuomotor sequence, respectively. Participants' demographic and clinical data are detailed in Table 1. The study was certified by the ethics review board of the Medical Faculty of the University of Leipzig and all participants were financially compensated for their involvement.

Motor Sequence Task

MSL was assessed with the sequential pinch force task (SPFT), which has previously been shown to induce short‐term neuroplastic alterations in GM (Gryga et al. 2012) and described in detail in our previous work (Jäger et al. 2022; Tremblay et al. 2021). The SPFT is a visuomotor sequencing task that requires fine force control of the index finger and thumb (Figure 1a). The task is to match the height of a visually presented, manipulable force bar (FOR, controlled by the participant) to the adjacent non‐manipulable, up and down moving blue reference bar (REF, controlled by a pre‐set sequence) by pinching the pinch‐force (Figure 1b). Both the FOR and REF bars are visually presented on a screen. Participants use the index finger and thumb of the dominant right hand to control the height of FOR, which is recorded at a rate of 80 Hz. The height of the REF bar changed over time according to one of two 18 s sequences, a complex learning sequence (LRN) or a simple control condition (SMP). The complex LRN sequence was difficult to predict, while SMP was a simple predictable and unvarying sinusoidal sequence (Figure 1c). The SMP sequence was designed to serve as a tight sensorimotor control for the LRN sequence: matched according to the total magnitude of force, duration and frequency of maximum power. Rest (RST) trials were also included, where participants fixated their gaze on the upper edges of the static REF and FOR bars fixed at 50% of their maximum height.

(a) Sequential Pinch Force Task (SPFT) device, (b) Force bar (FOR) in yellow and Reference bar (REF) in blue as presented in the screen to the participant, (c) complex sequence in blue (for the LRN group) and simple sequence in green (for the SMP group) (d) Experimental design with timeline of training sessions. High resolution MRI scans were acquired on specific days as depicted in the timeframe by brain images.

The experimental group performed the SPFT on each training day, which was made up of three blocks each consisting of 3 SMP, followed by 3 RST, and then 3 LRN trials for a total of 9 trials of each type. The control group completed twice the number of SMP trials and did not perform the LRN sequence with the same repeating block structure as above (i.e., 3 SMP, 3 RST, 3 SMP). This was done to ensure that the total motoric effort was consistent across groups. Feedback of the average temporal accuracy in matching the heights of FOR and REF bars was provided to the participants at the end of each of the LRN and SMP blocks.

Experimental Design

The two groups, LRN and SMP, performed the SPFT for five consecutive days (Monday–Friday; days 1–5 [d1–d5]) (Figure 1d). In addition to the five training sessions (d1–d5), there was also one familiarization session (d0, 3 days prior to d1) and one retention session (d17, 12 days after d5). The familiarization session served to inform the participant about the task, collect maximum voluntary contraction (MVC: maximum of force exerted on the device over 5 s), and to get acquainted with the pinch force device by performing 9 trials of the SMP sequence. The bar height was set to vary between 5% and 30% of individual MVC (min‐max height) to ensure that each participant was matched on the amount of relative force required to perform the task. Participants executed the SPFT while lying down in a supine position inside the scanner on d0, d1, d2, d5, and d17, and while comfortably seated outside of the scanner in a separate testing room on d3 and d4. The experimental sessions were conducted at approximately the same time of the day to avoid potential time‐of‐day effects on MRI measurements (Steel et al. 2019; Voldsbekk et al. 2020). The structural anatomical scans were acquired before the sequence training during which participants viewed a white fixation cross against a black background with no motor task being performed. The MVC calibration from the familiarization session was therefore used solely to normalize force requirements and did not affect head motion during acquisition.

MRI Acquisition Protocol

T1‐weighted uniform intensity images (T1w UNI) and quantitative T1 (qT1) maps were acquired before the sequence training with an MP2RAGE (Magnetization Prepared 2 Rapid Acquisition Gradient Echo) sequence on a Siemens 7 T scanner (MAGNETOM, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) with a 32‐channel Nova head coil to assess GMV and T1. The acquisition parameters were: time of repetition (TR) = 5000 ms, time of excitation (TE) = 2.45 ms, inversion time (TI): TI1/TI2 = 900/2750 ms, flip angle 1 = 5°, flip angle 2 = 3°, field of view (FOV) = 320 × 320 × 240 mm3, slice thickness = 0.7 mm, 240 slices, bandwidth = 250 Hz/Px, voxel size = 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.7 mm. No additional correction for bias, inversion efficiency or qT1 accuracy was performed as part of this study, beyond the corrections intrinsic to the MP2RAGE method. However, the longitudinal design and repeated measures statistical approach of the study provides an internal test of measurement repeatability and stability (Boudreau et al. 2025; Yu et al. 2024). Along with this structural MRI, diffusion‐weighted imaging and resting‐state functional MRI were also obtained. During the whole structural MRI acquisition, participants had their eyes open and fixated their gaze on a cross. For the current study, we used the T1w UNI images and qT1 maps. The structural scans were acquired before the sequence training on each scanning day to avoid confounding structural measurements with acute physiological effects from motor practice. Immediate post‐training changes in CBF, metabolic activity and tissue water content can temporarily alter qT1 relation times (Tardif et al. 2016), hence this scan ordering ensured observed qT1 changes reflected cumulative training‐related plasticity rather than transient post‐exercise effects.

Voxel‐Based Morphometry

VBM pre‐processing and analyses were conducted with the longitudinal pipeline of the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12) (version 1830, build r1907, Christian Gaser, Structural Brain Mapping group, Jena University Hospital, Jena, Germany) for SPM12 (version 7487, Statistical Parametric Mapping, Institute of Neurology, London, UK) running under MATLAB (version 2021A, The MathWorks Inc., USA). The longitudinal pipeline was designed to be more sensitive to detect voxel‐wise effects over smaller time periods and is therefore ideal for identifying putative neural plasticity. Prior to spatial normalization, preprocessing steps involved intra‐subject realignment across all time points using inverse‐consistent rigid registration and bias field correction (Ashburner and Ridgway 2012; Reuter et al. 2010; Reuter and Fischl 2011). This non‐linear registration resulted in a mean deformation value which was then applied to tissue segmentation from all time points. A reference brain (template) was used for non‐linear spatial registration and SPM12 tissue probability maps were used for segmentation (Ashburner and Friston 2005). An optimised shooting algorithm (Ashburner 2007) was used to spatially normalise all images to the common space. Subsequently, the resulting images provided the spatially normalised, modulated GM and white matter (WM) segmented maps for each time point. CAT12's segmentation uses adaptive maximum a posteriori (AMAP) estimation with partial‐volume modelling to estimate fractional GM/WM/CSF content per voxel, thereby correcting for mixed‐tissue effects at cortical boundaries. Modulated GM images were generated by scaling voxel‐wise GM probability maps by Jacobian determinants of the non‐linear deformation fields, preserving local GM volume (in mm3) and enabling assessment of absolute volume changes over time. All images were thoroughly checked for quality by visual inspection of the segmented GM tissue by JP. We applied an assessment of sample homogeneity (inter‐subject spatial similarity) within the entire unsmoothed dataset as a quality assessment strategy and excluded any outliers that had low quality. A correlation matrix illustrating the dataset's homogeneity in a boxplot was used to evaluate quality and volumes with a lower overall correlation (below two standard deviations) was assessed thoroughly. Final modulated GM maps were smoothed with an isotropic Gaussian kernel of 4 mm FWHM prior to statistical analyses.

Statistical Analyses

GM Volume Change During MSL Across Learning Stages

To determine GM regions where change was significantly different in LRN versus SMP, we used an interaction model with factors group (LRN, SMP) and time. All analyses were performed within the CAT12 toolbox using a longitudinal flexible factorial design. To restrict our analysis to GM regions, we applied an explicit mask. We created a mean GM probability map by averaging the modulated GM maps of all participants across all time points. This approach ensured that only voxels consistently identified as GM across our sample were included in the analysis. The mean GM map was then minimally smoothed with a 2 mm gaussian kernel to reduce isolated noise voxels and then binarized at a threshold value > 0.1 (i.e., voxels with ≥ 10% average GM probability). This threshold was chosen to balance specificity and sensitivity as they effectively excluded non‐GM voxels (higher thresholds like 0.2 might overly restrict the mask and lead to exclusion of plausible GM regions at tissue boundaries where structural plasticity might have occurred). With this design, putative changes between time points were compared across groups (LRN > SMP) and learning stages to assess fast learning (d1 vs. d2), slow learning (d2 vs. d5), overall learning (d1 vs. d5) and retention (d5 vs. d17). Significant interaction effects, therefore, identified GMV changes that were significantly greater in LRN than in SMP, thereby identifying putative regions for MSL induced plasticity for separate learning stages. These comparisons were defined a priori to test separate hypotheses about stage‐specific structural plasticity mechanisms and were treated as independent hypothesis families. Significance was evaluated using non‐parametric Threshold Free Cluster Enhancement (TFCE) with randomization (5000 iterations) (Smith and Nichols 2009) and results were corrected for multiple comparisons using family‐wise error (FWE) correction at p < 0.05 for each contrast independently and using a cluster‐extent of at least 100 voxels.

Behaviour

Performance was assessed by measuring temporal synchronization (SYN), which was identified as a marker of behavioural plasticity in previous work (Jäger et al. 2022; Tremblay et al. 2021). SYN depicts the deviation between the performed FOR and presented REF bars and was computed by calculating the lag (in ms) exhibiting the maximum cross‐correlation between REF and FOR patterns for each trial and has been shown to be a sensitive marker of SPFT performance (Gryga et al. 2012). A SYN score of 0 denotes perfect synchronization, with positive values denoting the amount of ms lag in participants' responses. SYN scores were evaluated for each block per day. Post hoc pairwise comparisons between consecutive days were conducted using paired t‐tests with FWE correction for multiple comparisons.

ROI Analyses

Any significant clusters resulting from the TFCE interaction analysis were extracted separately to serve as regions of interest (ROI) to characterize the trajectory of change and for correlation with behavioural improvements. This analysis determines whether interactions are driven by LRN increases or SMP decreases or bidirectional changes, which are not provided by the interaction test alone. A 5 mm sphere centred at the peak of each significant cluster was used to extract mean signal from each GM ROI over all days to characterize proportion signal change relative to d1. All ROI extractions were performed within a group‐level mean GM mask (mentioned in Section 3.1) ensuring that extracted values reflected GM‐specific signal and excluded contributions from CSF or WM. Post hoc analyses were carried out to check for percent change for mean GM volume change across all timepoints within the ROIs to illustrate the pattern of results that was driving significant findings. In line with our previous research (Jäger et al. 2022; Tremblay et al. 2021), our strategy for assigning sequence‐specific function to a region was dependent on that region exhibiting greater mean GMV change in the LRN group than the control group. We subtracted mean relative change for fast (d2‐d1), slow (d5‐d2), and overall (d5‐d1) stages for each significant cluster and sequence specificity was reported if the magnitude of change in the SMP group was less than the change in the LRN group. For any cluster, if we observe greater magnitude of GMV change in the SMP group, we would attribute that change to be driven by the control group and its interpretation as non‐sequence‐specific.

GMV change was associated with behavioural performance improvements in LRN sequence synchronization (within the LRN group only) using Pearson's correlation to determine if sequence‐specific ROIs that changed with learning reflected changes in behaviour. We identified the GMV change by subtracting mean GMV in d1 from d2 (fast learning), d2 from d5 (slow learning), d1 from d5 (overall learning) of the significant sequence‐specific regions and then correlated them with the respective SYN changes. Since the SPFT training happened after the structural imaging scan (see Section 2.3), Block3 SYN scores were best represented in the d2 MRI scan and similarly for the other days, resulting in the following calculations for SYN change scores—fast: Block3‐Block1; slow: Block12‐Block3; overall: Block12‐Block1.

We conducted a secondary ROI analysis to investigate how alterations in T1 values (as a quantitative indicator of physiological/microstructural change) vary within the sequence‐specific regions over different learning stages. To identify specific T1 changes, we similarly extracted the significant binarized GM ROIs from above and extracted mean T1 values from co‐registered individual T1 maps for each subject and day. We also conducted Pearson's correlation analysis between mean T1 and mean GMV in the sequence‐specific significant ROIs across different learning stages along with subsequent correlational analysis between GM ROI T1 changes and SYN performance changes to examine the relationship between biophysical alterations and behavioural outcomes. We performed Leave‐One‐Subject‐Out (LOSO) cross‐validation method on all Pearson's correlation analyses to acquire stable, robust and bias‐corrected brain‐behaviour associations.

Results

During the segmentation quality control process (visual inspection as mentioned in Section 2.5), we identified 7 (out of a total of 200) images that exhibited blurring (likely as a result of movement during MRI acquisition) and excluded them from all further processing. The 7 excluded images were distributed across timepoints as follows, for d0: 3, for d1: 1, for d2:1 and d6:2 and were not considered for the GLM analyses. Missing observations were handled in the GLM analysis using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation which appropriately weights each subject's contribution based on available data while preserving within‐subject temporal trajectories. Image quality was assessed using CAT12's automated quality control metrics. Weighted overall image quality ratings (IQR) showed no significant difference between groups (LRN: M = 87.56% ± 2.50%, range 83.03%–92.10%; SMP: M = 88.28 ± 2.62%, range 82.32%–92.13%; t(191) = −1.95, p = 0.052). Noise‐to‐contrast ratio was comparable between groups (LRN: M = 0.105 ± 0.026; SMP: M = 0.098 ± 0.026; t(191) = 1.70, p = 0.091). Inhomogeneity‐to‐contrast ratio showed no group differences (LRN: M = 0.188 ± 0.029; SMP: M = 0.192 ± 0.038; t(191) = −0.81, p = 0.422). Euler characteristic numbers, indicating surface reconstruction quality, were comparable between groups (LRN: M = 92.5 ± 33.4; SMP: M = 88.0 ± 45.1; t(173) = 0.74, p = 0.459), although we did not perform any surface‐based morphometry analyses. These results confirm that image quality was consistent across groups and did not confound subsequent analyses. One participant from the LRN group had two timepoints that exhibited T1w image blurring and failed the quality check (as described above) and was therefore excluded from the behavioural and ROI analyses. All remaining participants were retained in the analyses. As described in our previous work with the same cohort (Jäger et al. 2022; Tremblay et al. 2021), the SMP group did not exhibit any significant change in SYN between the days of training because they were already at maximum performance. In contrast, the LRN group exhibited a significant decrease in SYN scores from d1 to d2 (t = 5.97, FWEp < 0.001, Hedges' g = 0.75) and from d2 to d3 (t = 4.99, FWEp < 0.0005, Hedges' g = 0.47), indicating learning of the complex sequence over the course of the training period. A smaller but still significant improvement was observed between d3 and d4 (t = 3.78, FWEp < 0.0043, Hedges' g = 0.42). Critically performance plateaued thereafter, with no significant differences between d4 and d5 (t = −0.75, FWEp = 1.00) or between d5 and d17 (t = −1.78, FWEp = 0.46), demonstrating successful performance maintenance and retention of the learned complex motor sequence. Interaction Effects—Familiarization (d0 vs. d1)

There were no regions that exhibited a significant interaction when comparing LRN and SMP to assess potential grey matter volume change between the familiarization day (d0) and first training day (d1) (TFCE, p > 0.05), indicating a stable baseline.

Interaction Effects—Fast Learning (d1 vs. d2)

There were no regions that exhibited significant increases or decreases in GM volume in the LRN group relative to the SMP group between d1 and d2, or vice versa (all TFCE p > 0.05).

Interaction Effects—Slow Learning (d2 vs. d5)

There were two clusters where LRN had significantly greater increased GMV at d5 when compared with d2 than the SMP group: left superior parietal cortex (SPC; TFCE, FWEp = 0.017) (Figure 3a) and left precuneus (TFCE, FWEp = 0.039) (Figure 3b, Table S1). Our post hoc ROI analysis revealed the left SPC as the only region exhibiting sequence‐specificity, which was characterized by an increase in GMV from d2 to d5 that was greater in magnitude in LRN than the decrease in SMP (See Table 2). Interestingly, in both regions, increases in GMV in the LRN group were mirrored by decreases in the SMP group (bottom panel of Figure 3, Table 2).

Discussion

The study investigated sequence‐specific motor learning by analyzing GM plasticity between experimental and control groups across multiple stages (fast, slow, overall, retention), identifying structural changes that uniquely associate with complex sequence learning rather than general motor execution. Between‐group analyses revealed significant sequence‐specific GMV changes in the left parietal region: the left SPC during slow learning. Other significant regions (Left Precuneus, Right SPC, Left AG) showed volume changes in our interaction design but those changes were not driven by the experimental (LRN) group, indicating the involvement of these regions for general motor execution rather than specifically learning the complex movement sequence. We further discovered distinct changes in T1 (a quantitative indicator of physiological change) between groups in left SPC: increases in relative T1 across d1, d2, d5 in the control (SMP) group and an initial increase (d1 to d2) and then later decrease (d2 to d5) in the experimental group. This indicates specific neurophysiological mechanisms underlying the structural alterations in both the groups.

Sequence‐Specificity

We operationalized sequence‐specificity as GMV changes that were significantly greater in the group trained on the complex sequence in comparison to controls. We observed sequence‐specific change in GMV in the left SPC during slow learning and a significant positive correlation with behaviour scores (post hoc ROI analysis). Our findings align with implicit sequence learning showing heightened activation in SPC during later stages of training/learning (Grafton et al. 1995; Ohbayashi 2021; Parsons et al. 2005; Sakai et al. 1998; Shadmehr and Holcomb 1997; Tzvi et al. 2016). These studies suggest that the SPC is involved in sequence encoding and retrieval during the later stages of learning an implicit sequence. This is also supported by Anderson's (1982) theory of progression where SPC's involvement is observed from the initial declarative learning stage (requiring more attentive processes) through to the later procedural stages as automaticity develops. Some studies on explicit sequence learning (Graydon et al. 2005; Jenkins et al. 1994; Müller et al. 2002; Toni et al. 1998) report that SPC involvement in the early stages of learning may be crucial for visuomotor integration, spatial attention and sensorimotor coordination. In contrast to the sequence‐specific structural changes observed in the SPC, we did not observe any volume changes in M1 during the learning stages. This aligns with previous findings that demonstrate that M1 primarily represents individual finger movements rather than sequences of multiple finger movements (Yokoi et al. 2018) and that its role in sequence learning is more readily identified using functional neuroimaging methods during initial learning phases (Ohbayashi 2021). Structural changes in M1 might tend to emerge more gradually and might be contingent on factors such as training duration, task complexity or methodological sensitivity. Regarding the temporal specificity of our SPC findings, structural changes were not retained at d17. This suggests that SPC might not be involved in long term sequence representation but rather helps in visuomotor coordination during the slow phase of sequence learning when sensorimotor mapping and control are most demanding. The SPC volume increase likely supports the critical transition from controlled to automatic sequence execution after which the learned sequence may be maintained by other neural systems (Berlot et al. 2020). Similarly, we previously documented an increase in resting‐state functional connectivity in bilateral SPC during the overall (d1‐d5) learning phase for the same dataset. In this case, the functional change was larger in the control group than in the experimental group and was therefore identified as non‐sequence‐specific and more relevant for motor execution (Jäger et al. 2022). This differentiating finding could represent distinct temporal dynamics of structural (slower) versus functional plasticity (more rapid) (Dayan and Cohen 2011). While SPC GM volume changes in the present study were evident only during the slow learning phase, alterations in functional connectivity were identified on the first day of training (Jäger et al. 2022). The specifics of our experimental protocol might have also contributed to these temporal differences. Notably, the significant GM volume change that we identified in the SPC during slow learning is consistent with established evidence that parietal recruitment is task‐dependent (Sakai et al. 1998) and varies with spatial computations required for sequence execution (Grafton et al. 1995; Hikosaka, Nakamura, et al. 2002; Sakai et al. 2003). This highlights the SPC's critical role in integrating and automating movement sequences. We identified a positive correlation between change in GMV and SYN scores, which indicated that greater behavioural improvements were associated with GMV decreases (and vice versa). Although this pattern may appear counterintuitive, non‐linear plasticity trajectories have been described in motor learning, with an initial expansion phase followed by renormalization as networks are refined and redundant connections are pruned (Lövdén et al. 2020; Mediavilla et al. 2022; Wenger et al. 2017). Our compact 5‐day design potentially provides a rare view of such timing‐sensitive dynamics. Specifically, while the group average shows a net GMV increase from d2 to d5, there is substantial inter‐individual variability with some individuals showing GMV increases and others showing decreases. Critically, the correlation reveals that greater GMV decreases are associated with better behavioural outcomes. Individuals may progress through these phases at different speeds, so that at a given measurement point some participants are still in an expansion stage while others have already entered refinement (Olivo et al. 2022). This variability could explain the positive correlation we observed: participants showing greater GMV decreases may represent those who progressed further into the refinement phase and consequently demonstrated larger behavioural improvements, while those with larger GMV increases may still be in earlier learning stages with corresponding smaller performance gains. Overall, our study captures a nuanced phase immediately after initial learning that is usually missed in more sparsely sampled pre‐post designs.

Physiological Mechanisms Driving Sequence‐Specific GMV Alterations

To further understand which physiological mechanism(s) (e.g., synaptogenesis, angiogenesis, myelogenesis) drive GMV changes in the two groups, we analyzed regional quantitative T1 changes across learning in the significant sequence‐specific SPC ROI. Changes in T1 can be caused by various physiological processes, and using a head‐only 7 T scanner (such as that used in this study) introduces additional complexity because inflowing blood is not inverted and therefore appears dark in the image (i.e., without the inversion, inflowing blood appears closer to the T1 of WM). This is in contrast to whole body systems, where inflowing blood is inverted and therefore appears brighter, having a T1 more similar to GM. As a result, in our experiment increased T1 may reflect either increased synaptogenesis and/or decreased cerebral blood flow (CBF), while decreased T1 may indicate increased myelination (i.e., myelogenesis) and/or increased CBF that could be driven by either angiogenesis (Tardif et al. 2017) or higher baseline activity.

The control group exhibited consistent increases in T1 across days, which could be associated with decreased CBF due to the simple repetitive tasks, as has been previously reported (Sidtis et al. 2004). The alternative interpretation, increased synaptogenesis, is not consistent with simple motor exercises as shown in previous animal studies (Black et al. 1990; Isaacs et al. 1992; Kleim et al. 1997). The experimental group exhibited a biphasic neurophysiological response distinct from the control group: increased T1 during fast learning and decreased T1 during slow learning. During fast learning, increased T1 may reflect synaptogenesis (supporting novel complex sequence acquisition) rather than decreased CBF in this group. This interpretation is consistent with the non‐human animal work (Black et al. 1990; Kleim et al. 1997) and inconsistent with an alternative explanation of reduced CBF as a result of repetition suppression (i.e., reduced neural response due to repeated stimuli), since this is not observed during initial exposure to complex sequences (Sidtis et al. 2004). In slow learning, the observed subsequent decreases in T1 align well with increased myelogenesis (Mediavilla et al. 2022) and increased CBF (Xiong et al. 2009), which could potentially be driven by angiogenesis. These findings illustrate distinct physiological processes between the two groups trained on different types of tasks: while the control group showed monotonic T1 increases indicative of attenuated blood flow due to repetitive task habituation, the experimental group demonstrated dynamic putative plasticity mechanisms as a result of novel task learning: synaptogenesis for initial encoding and myelogenesis for later learning and consolidation (Fields 2015). In summary, this temporal progression suggests that neurophysiological processes occur nonlinearly during complex sequence learning, with our work tentatively suggests a transition from synapse formation during fast learning (linked to increasing T1) to intracortical myelination for slow learning (linked to decreasing T1) and potentially supporting accurate sequence performance and/or retrieval. However, our study lacks direct measurements to specify a causal relationship between angiogenesis and cerebral blood flow in our simple group, further validation using multimodal neuroimaging (e.g., perfusion‐based MR imaging) is warranted to disentangle these interrelated mechanisms.

It is important to note that experience‐dependent structural changes occur across multiple levels concurrently and involve numerous interrelated processes. At the cellular level, modification may reflect alterations in neuronal architecture through synaptogenesis and dendritic arborization (Turner and Greenough 1985; Volkmar and Greenough 1972), supported by molecular mechanisms involving protein synthesis (Bullock et al. 1987; Kuhl et al. 1992). Additionally, transient GMV changes may also stem from astrocyte swelling, spine and synapse production with subsequent maturation and pruning (J. L. Chen and Nedivi 2010; Fu et al. 2012; Fu and Zuo 2011), as well as changes in extracellular matrix and other non‐neuronal components that influence tissue density (Bechler et al. 2018; Sampaio‐Baptista et al. 2020). Furthermore, alterations in axon and dendrite morphometry and cell body size could also contribute to observed changes (Blumenfeld‐Katzir et al. 2011; Lerch et al. 2011). Our observed structural changes suggest that the SPC is involved in learning complex motor sequences, and that this region concurrently undergoes dynamic physiological changes that support learning of those specific sequences. Importantly, we provide evidence that GM plasticity is characterized by a timing‐dependent blend of changes in neural cell bodies (i.e., synaptogenesis) and axonal structure (i.e., intracortical myelination) that are thought to be supported by vascular change (e.g., angiogenesis, CBF) (Taubert et al. 2012). The complete dynamics of how these physiological changes relate to volumetry‐based measurements of GM remain to be fully determined.

Non Sequence‐Specific Regions

We identified three additional regions (4 clusters) with significant interactions that did not surpass our threshold for sequence‐specific effects. Since our study design was constructed to identify regions that were involved in learning the continuous complex sequence—defined as regions where the magnitude of change in the experimental group was greater than the change in the control group—these three significant clusters may be more involved in the general process of motor execution. However, in all cases there was an apparent increase in GMV in the experimental group that was paralleled by decreases in the control group—indicating that even though these regions did not surpass our criterion, similar mechanisms may be at work. The left Precuneus (identified in both slow and overall learning), with its roles in higher cognitive functions such as spatial awareness, attention, and integration of sensory information (Cavanna and Trimble 2006; Tanglay et al. 2022) may play a supportive role in the planning, coordination, and sensorimotor integration required for all motor movements. The left AG (identified during overall learning) is responsible for spatial attention (Chen et al. 2012; Seghier 2013) and plays a known role in perceptual and ordered sequence learning (Van Opstal et al. 2008, 2009). The right SPC also did not surpass our criteria for sequence‐specificity, in contrast to the left SPC. Given that our task was performed with the right hand, the left hemisphere's specialization for encoding sequence identity and temporal structure likely dominated over the right hemisphere's role in online spatial guidance. This pattern aligns with established parietal asymmetries, where the left SPC supports abstract, rule‐based and symbolic aspects of movement sequences while the right SPC contributes to continuous monitoring and visuospatial control of motor execution (Haaland et al. 2004; Wu et al. 2016). Though it is difficult to draw strong conclusions from the absence of an effect, the lack of effect in the right SPC might reflect analytic sensitivity, sample size, modality differences or individual variability. Our previous functional study using this paradigm demonstrated bilateral SPC involvement during task performance, indicating clear functional engagement of both hemispheres. Therefore, the absence of a right SPC structural effect might be due to differences in sensitivity between structural and functional measures rather than a true lack of involvement. Overall, our findings are consistent with studies showing a shift from bilateral parietal engagement during learning to a left‐lateralized representation of abstract sequence knowledge (Brodt et al. 2016) during consolidation. Together, all these findings suggest that the right SPC, left AG and left precuneus may contribute more to task general execution processes than specific learning of novel sequences.

Limitations

Several potential cellular mechanisms may underlie the structural brain plasticity that we have observed here (Wenger et al. 2017), including modifications to intracortical myelin (Keiner et al. 2017; Kougioumtzidou et al. 2017; McKenzie et al. 2014), increased neurogenesis (Van Praag et al. 1999), enhanced dendritic spine density (Keifer et al. 2015), and changes in astrocyte volume (Kleim et al. 2007; Woo et al. 2018). These various processes likely contribute differently across experimental stages. However, non‐invasive MRI techniques face challenges in specifically detecting these transient microscopic changes that might not be fully represented in anatomical images (Tardif et al. 2016, 2017). Moreover, in our study, the T1 values alone cannot definitively distinguish between different cellular processes, and our interpretations are based on indirect inference. More specific myelin measurements, such as myelin water fraction (MacKay and Laule 2016), could be used in future work to distinguish between myelin changes and synaptogenesis. Additionally, the absence of direct CBF measurements limits our ability to confirm blood flow‐related hypotheses for the control group. Our study design followed previously established SPFT protocols, where the SMP sequence was matched to the LRN sequence in duration, amplitude, range and spectral power and was used as a validated motor control. Although we did not evaluate force transitions or variability, our baseline behavioural data confirmed equivalent motor capabilities between groups and our approach aligns with validated methods in prior MSL research. Regarding the qT1 mapping, some studies have assessed the accuracy of T1 mapping with MP2RAGE (Boudreau et al. 2025; Marques et al. 2010), but we did not perform additional validation of this established method for the current study. Related 7 T longitudinal motor learning work has also shown that structural findings can be highly task‐sensitive, as Garzón et al. (2023) reported no reliable training‐related structural changes using a comparable MP2RAGE‐based approach. Future work could include additional correction (e.g., B1 mapping). Furthermore, future work would benefit from prospective power simulations informed by biophysically grounded effect size estimates derived from computational brain models and informed by the present study, which could also be used to determine optimal sample sizes necessary for longitudinal neuroimaging studies of motor learning. Future studies combining multiple imaging modalities (Arterial Spin Labeling: to measure CBF, Magnetization Transfer Saturation: to measure myelin density and microstructural integrity, Proton Density: to measure more accurate tissue composition or Myelin‐Water Fraction: to provide a map of myelin content) with higher temporal sampling and direct physiological measurements would help to address these limitations.

Conclusion

In this study, we provide evidence for structural GMV modifications as a result of sequence‐specific motor learning across 5 days and spanning multiple stages of learning. We identified the SPC (involved in spatial transformation, encoding) as the region most strongly engaged in sequence‐specific learning. This region was significantly correlated with behavioural scores (smaller GMV change linked with performance improvement) and qT1 mapping (inverse relationship) revealed distinct physiological mechanisms underlying sequence learning that differed depending upon sequence complexity. The simple sequence control group likely showed primarily habituation effects, whereas the complex sequence experimental group exhibited two different processes (initial synaptogenesis and later myelogenesis) over the course of training. In agreement with invasive experimental work in non‐human animals, our work provides initial evidence that learning complex sequences leads initially to synaptogenesis that is subsequently followed by intracortical myelination as the sequence is well learned.
