Tyrosinase-Mediated Conjugation for Antigen Display on Ferritin Nanoparticles
Authors: Margarida
Q. Rodrigues, Sara Patão, Mónica Thomaz, Tiago Nunes, Paula M. Alves, António Roldão
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Ferritin (Ft) nanoparticles have become versatile platforms for displaying antigens, being a promising technology for vaccine development. While genetic fusion has traditionally been the preferred method for antigen display, concerns about improper folding and steric hindrance that may compromise vaccine efficacy or stability have prompted alternative approaches. Bioconjugation offers the advantage of preserving native protein structure and function, with recent advancements improving efficiency and specificity. In this study, we used tyrosinase (TYR) to bioconjugate the receptor binding domain of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, tagged with a tyrosine (RBD-Y), to native cysteines on Ft, resulting in RBD-Y-Ft nanoparticles. We quantified available cysteines on ferritin using Ellman’s assay and monitored their reduction during the reactions. Denaturing analytics (via SDS-PAGE, Western blot, and LC-TOF-MS) confirmed the formation of RBD-Y-Ft monomers with an expected molecular weight of 46 kDa. Mass photometry and HPLC estimated a molecular weight of RBD-Y-Ft nanoparticles of 680 kDa, which was higher than that of nonfunctionalized ferritin (480 kDa), indicating successful binding of up to eight RBD-Y antigens per 24-mer Ft nanoparticle. This work enhances our understanding of how Ft nanoparticles can be engineered to present antigens, leveraging them as a robust scaffold for producing tailored-made candidate vaccines in a timely manner.

Introduction

Protein-based nanoparticles are a vaccine modality that uses specific scaffolds to display antigens, aiming to elicit robust immune responses upon delivery. Unlike traditional live-attenuated or inactivated vaccines, these antigen-anchored particles mitigate safety concerns and exhibit increased immunogenicity compared to soluble subunits.1

Among these scaffolds, the 24-mer ferritin emerges as an exceptional protein for biotechnological applications.2 It features characteristics such as thermal and pH stability (withstanding temperatures up to 80–100 °C and pH ranges of 3–10), biocompatibility, biodegradability, cost-effective production, a hollow cavity with reversible assembly/disassembly, and amenability to surface conjugation by chemical or genetic approaches.3 Leveraging these attributes, ferritin nanoparticles can be functionalized with antigens leading to ferritin-antigen conjugates with potential as vaccines.4,5

Genetic fusion has traditionally been the preferred method for functionalizing ferritin nanoparticles.4 Various vaccine candidates have advanced to Phase 1 clinical trials targeting different viruses, e.g. influenza (NCT03186781, NCT03814720 and NCT04579250),5−7 Epstein–Barr (NCT04645147 and NCT05683834),8 SARS-CoV-2 (NCT04784767),9 and HIV (NCT05903339).10 However, this approach comes with inherent drawbacks, including the requirement for engineered linkers, folding issues associated with the expression of non-native chimeras of considerable size, and steric hindrance that prevents proper nanoparticle assembly. Furthermore, genetic fusion is constrained to attachment at the N- or C-terminals.

Bioconjugation between proteins and peptides has emerged as an attractive alternative, especially with recent advances that offer increased conjugation efficiency and specificity while preserving the native conformation and function of proteins. Chemical cross-linking, which uses native reactive groups on protein surfaces, commonly employs linkers like NHS and maleimide that bind to primary amines and thiol groups, respectively.11,12 However, this method lacks region-specificity. Approaches such as the Tag/Catcher technology13−18 and click chemistry,19,20 provide highly efficient and site-specific options, but may require genetic or metabolic engineering, thus introducing complexity to the bioprocess.

Enzyme-mediated conjugation uses enzymes that target specific amino acids on consensus sequences or tags, allowing for site-selectivity. For example, Sortase A (SrtA) mediates the conjugation of a protein containing a LPXTG motif at the C-terminal to a second protein containing one or more glycines at the N-terminal;21 microbial transglutaminases (mTGs) catalyze the conjugation between a glutamine and the ε-amino group of a lysine;22,23 and formylglycine-generating enzymes (FGlys) convert the cysteine on its consensus sequence (CXPXR) to a formylglycine, containing a bioorthogonal aldehyde that can be modified by aldehyde-reactive conjugation chemistry.24

Tyrosinase is an oxidoreductase that converts phenols to o-quinones, highly reactive electrophiles that undergo 1,6-Michael addition reactions with nucleophilic groups. The chemical reactivity of o-quinones formed by tyrosinase with amino acids has been extensively reviewed.25,26 Tyrosinase was used to conjugate phenol derivatives to N-terminal proline residues27 and primary amines,28 achieving high yields. Moreover, this enzyme mediated the conjugation of solvent-exposed tyrosine residues to thiol groups.29−31 These reactions were conducted under physiological conditions and completed in less than 1 h.

Tyrosinase-mediated conjugation exhibits immense potential for therapeutic purposes, having already been used to enhance the cellular delivery of CRISPR-Cas9 for gene therapy,29 and to generate antibody-drug conjugates for targeted chemotherapy.31 To our knowledge, this approach has not been previously employed for vaccine design.

This study aimed to establish a bioconjugation method that is simple, site-specific and results in stable bonding, addressing the limitations of genetic fusion and other conjugation techniques. Tyrosinase-mediated conjugation was applied to display antigens on the surface of ferritin for vaccine development. As a proof-of-concept, the Receptor Binding Domain of the SARS-CoV-2 virus Spike protein, which includes a tyrosine tag (RBD-Y), was conjugated to native thiol groups on the surface of human ferritin (Ft) nanoparticles. This resulted in the generation of RBD-Y-Ft nanoparticles as potential vaccine candidates (Figure 1).

Results

Production of Ferritin (Ft) Nanoparticles

Characterization of Purified Ferritin

The human ferritin heavy chain was selected as the scaffold for RBD display given its native cysteines, eliminating the need for genetic engineering to enable tyrosinase-mediated conjugation of tyrosine-tagged RBD. After expression in E. coli and subsequent purification, the ferritin structure was characterized.

Analysis using native gel electrophoresis (Figure 2A) revealed the presence of fully assembled 24-mer Ft nanoparticles of approximately 500 kDa; reduced sodium dodecyl-sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (Figure 2B) confirmed that Ft monomers had the expected molecular weight of 21 kDa. Negative staining transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Figure 2C) showed the characteristic spherical shape of ferritin nanoparticles, and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) demonstrated their homogeneity (Figure 2D).

M: molecular weight marker. (E) Thiol quantification using the Ellman’s assay for the proteins employed in this study: ferritin (Ft), receptor binding domain of the SARS-CoV-2 Spike protein with and without the tyrosine-tag (RBD and RBD-Y, respectively), and tyrosinase (abTYR). Thiol levels estimated for RBD and RBD-Y (#) fell below the measurable range.

Ellmańs Assay for Quantification of Reduced Thiols

The presence of reduced cysteines on the surface of ferritin nanoparticles plays a crucial role in enabling thiol groups to undergo a nucleophilic attack on the oxidized tyrosine of RBD-Y. The Ellmańs assay validates these reactions by quantifying the thiol groups before and after the conjugation experiments.

Traditionally conducted at mL-scale using cuvettes, the Ellman’s assay was adapted to μL-scale using 96-well plates allowing for high-throughput quantification while minimizing biologics wastage. This modification enabled the quantification of up to 96 reactions in a single measurement assay, with a demonstrated deviation of <4% compared to the cuvette-based method (Figure 2E). Consequently, the μL-scale method in 96-well plates was used for all subsequent Ellman’s experiments.

The thiol content of each ferritin monomer was determined to be 0.7, suggesting that each 24-mer Ft nanoparticle typically contains 16–17 monomers with a reduced cysteine (-SH). Additionally, the thiol content of RBD, with or without the tyrosine-tag, was assessed (Figure 2E), with no available thiol groups being detected. This finding aligns with their design, which aimed to minimize nonspecific binding during bioconjugation by featuring an even number of cysteines in the RBD protein to facilitate intramolecular disulfide bond formation.

Agaricus bisporus tyrosinase (abTYR), a tetramer with six cysteines in which two are involved in thioether bonds and the rest potentially participating in hydrogen bonds,32 was estimated to possess two thiols (as perFigure 2E). Considering that ferritin and RBD-Y will be present in significantly higher concentrations during the conjugation reactions, any influence exerted by the thiol groups of abTYR is expected to be negligible.

Ferritin Storage and Thiol (−SH) Maintenance

Stability and thiol (-SH) content of purified ferritin nanoparticles were conducted over a six-month period under various storage conditions.

HPLC analysis (Figure 3A-C) indicate that the 24-mer oligomerization state of ferritin is maintained regardless of storage temperature (−80 or 4 °C) and freeze–thaw cycles (2x or 3x); minor peaks tend to appear reflecting ferritin nanoparticles’ disassembly (see Figure 3A) or aggregation (see Figure 3B–C). The number of reduced thiols (-SH) on ferritin nanoparticles is similar in all storage conditions tested, i.e. ∼ 16–17 out of 24 monomers of Ft contain a reduced thiol (Figure 3D); observed fluctuations in the values of reduced thiols can derive from measurement inaccuracies.

Despite the minor variations observed in ferritin oligomerization state and thiol content under different storage conditions, their overall impact on subsequent ferritin nanoparticles functionalization was deemed not significant, highlighting the potential for extended storage of these particles for vaccine development.

Optimization of Conditions for Tyrosinase-Mediated Conjugation

Having confirmed the presence of reduced thiols on ferritin nanoparticles, the next critical step for successful tyrosinase-mediated conjugation is ensuring that the enzyme can effectively oxidize the tyrosine of RBD-Y. To achieve this, the activity of abTYR was assessed under different buffer formulations and reaction conditions (i.e., temperature, time and mixing).

In terms of buffer formulations, different combinations of sodium phosphate (NaP) concentration (20 and 100 mM) and pH (6.5 and 8.0) were tested (Figure 4A). Tyrosinase activity (U/mL) was found to be most effective in 20 mM NaP, regardless of pH, consistent with previous findings.27 However, under this formulation, the estimated number of thiol groups on ferritin (-SH/Ft) was significantly lower than the expected (approximately 0.7, see Figure 2E) when using the optimal conditions of the Ellman’s assay (i.e., 100 mM NaP at pH 8.0). Given that abTYR activity is similar across all conditions tested, 100 mM NaP at pH 8.0 was used for subsequent conjugation experiments.

(B) abTYR activity under different temperatures (4, 20, and 37 °C). Data are presented as mean ± SD of three replicates; ns indicates no significance, *p< 0.05, **p< 0.01, ***p< 0.001, ****p< 0.0001 by Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparison test.

The impact of temperature on abTYR activity was also investigated. Although abTYR exhibited enhanced activity at 37 °C (Figure 4B), consistent with previous findings,33 such temperature was deemed unsuitable for bioconjugation involving ferritin and RBD-Y due to potential thermal damage.

To identify which temperature, 4 or 20 °C, provides a more favorable environment for effective conjugation, ferritin (10 μM) was mixed with RBD-Y (5 μM) and abTYR (167 nM). Based on previous studies using different ratios of ferritin to RBD-Y, these molarities ensure that RBD-Y is in molar excess relative to ferritin (data not shown). Conjugations were performed in 100 mM NaP at pH 8.0 (reactions were terminated at specific time points by adding tropolone, a tyrosinase inhibitor,32 to a final concentration of 2 mM), and binding of RBD-Y to ferritin assessed by Elman’s assay, SDS-PAGE and Western blot. Over time, the thiol content on ferritin decreased in both temperatures, indicating successful RBD-Y conjugation (Figure 5A). At 4 °C, the maximum relative thiol variation was 60%, achieved in approximately 1 h, while at 20 °C this value was 70%, achieved in approximately 15 min. The successful formation of RBD-Y-Ft was further confirmed by SDS-PAGE (Figure 5B) and Western blot (Support Information Figure S1). These results suggest that conjugation efficiency is maximized under room temperature (20 °C) after just 15 min.

Mixing regimes impact abTYR’s performance as this enzyme requires dissolved O2 to operate, i.e. convert l-tyrosine to L-DOPA (Support Information Figure S2A). For example, under nonmixing conditions, this conversion was restricted to the surface as indicated by the presence of an orange-colored band (Support Information Figure S2B), highlighting the need for a homogeneous mixture during bioconjugation. To avoid potential protein damage derived from vortexing, subsequent conjugations were conducted by pipetting.

Conjugation of RBD-Y to Ft

Following the establishment of optimized conditions for tyrosinase-mediated conjugation, the conjugation of RBD-Y to Ft nanoparticles was conducted (reaction mix: 10 μM Ft, 10 μM RBD-Y, and 167 nM abTYR) and the RBD-Y-Ft monomers and nanoparticles characterized.

Characterization of RBD-Y-Ft Monomers

The conjugation process between Ft and RBD-Y did not occur in the absence of abTYR, as the number of available thiols (-SH/Ft) remained similar to the control (i.e., Ft alone), underscoring the essential role of the enzyme in enabling the coupling (Figure 6A). Likewise, RBD lacking the tyrosine-tag did not bind to Ft in the presence of abTYR (i.e., number of available thiols similar to the control), highlighting the specificity of the conjugation reaction and the importance of an accessible tyrosine. These findings confirm that only the tyrosine in the tag participates in the conjugation. Typically, hydrophobic tyrosine residues are packaged within the protein core, involved in secondary and tertiary structural elements, making them seldom solvent-exposed and unable to access tyrosinase’s deep active-site binding pocket.29

Data are presented as mean ± SD of three replicates; ns indicates no significance, ****p< 0.0001 by Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparison test. (B) LC-TOF-MS analysis. Top: Ft (blue) and Ft+abTYR (pink). Middle: RBD-Y (blue) and RBD-Y+abTYR (pink). Bottom: Ft+RBD-Y+abTYR. (C) SDS-PAGE analysis. Samples were run under reducing conditions and normalized to contain 0.5 μg/well of Ft and/or RBDs. MW: molecular weight. (D) HPLC analysis of RBD-Y (dashed blue), RBD-Y+abTYR (solid blue), RBD (dashed brown), and RBD+abTYR (solid brown).

The only condition favoring bioconjugation is when RBD-Y and Ft are in the presence of abTYR, as demonstrated by the decrease in available thiols on ferritin from 0.7 to 0.5; this reduction suggests that approximately 30% of the available thiols on ferritin were successfully conjugated to RBD-Y.

In the LC-TOF-MS analysis, the peaks of Ft and Ft+abTYR are superimposed, meaning that abTYR does not directly interact with ferritin (Figure 6B). A 12-Da molecular weight shift is observed for the peaks of RBD-Y vs RBD-Y+abTYR, suggesting the enzymatic modification of the solvent exposed tyrosine residue of RBD-Y, consistent with prior studies.29 The successful formation of RBD-Y-Ft monomers was confirmed by the appearance of an entity with a molecular weight of 46 kDa, matching the combined weights of Ft (21 kDa) and RBD-Y (25 kDa).

The formation of the RBD-Y-Ft monomers was further confirmed through reducing SDS-PAGE, where a band corresponding to the combined molecular weight of Ft and RBD-Y (46 kDa) was visible (Figure 6C, see Lane 7); these results were validated by Western blot analysis (Support Information Figure S3). In addition, the influence of abTYR on the oxidation of RBD-Y was evident from the appearance of a faint band at 48–50 kDa in wells containing both species, suggesting the formation of RBD-Y dimers.

HPLC analysis of RBD and RBD-Y alone or in the presence of abTYR confirmed that abTYR-oxidized RBD-Y can lead to the formation of RBD-Y dimers, as denoted by the appearance of a shoulder-shaped peak prior to the main RBD-Y peak, albeit to a limited extent (Figure 6D). Indeed, the formation of entities double the size of RBD-Y (48–50 kDa) was confirmed by LC-TOF-MS, although at a significantly lower extent compared to the formation of the 46-kDa RBD-Y-Ft monomer (Support Information Figure S4).

Characterization of RBD-Y-Ft Nanoparticles

Ellman’s assay provides a quantitative assessment of thiol content, but its usefulness in estimating the number of bound RBD-Y to a ferritin nanoparticle is limited. This is because the assay relies on the absorbance ratio at 512 and 280 nm, which can be strongly influenced by the presence of other proteins contributing to absorbance at 280 nm. Therefore, Ellman’s assay is inadequate for estimating the number of RBD-Y bound to a ferritin nanoparticle. Analytical techniques performed under native conditions, such as DLS, HPLC and mass photometry, allow the estimation of the number of bound RBD-Y by assessing the size and molecular weight shifts between nonfunctionalized and functionalized ferritin.

DLS analysis showed a similar size distribution for both nonfunctionalized and RBD-Y-functionalized ferritin nanoparticles, with an average diameter of 12 nm, matching the expected size of native ferritin34 (Supporting Information Figure S5A). Contrary to the antecipated, no increase in hydrodynamic diameter was observed following RBD-Y conjugation. This could be attributed to the low protein concentration, resulting in weak scattering (Supporting Information Figure S5B), the detector’s insensitivity to the smaller RBD-Y additions compared to the full nanoparticle, or the DLS settings, which are optimized for spherical particles and may not account for variations in shape.35

HPLC analysis revealed the presence of four major protein species: RBD-Y, RBD-Y dimers, Ft nanoparticles, and RBD-Y-Ft nanoparticles (Figure 7A). By interpolating their retention times with those of a protein standard mix, it was possible to have an estimation of each protein’s molecular weight (Support Information Figure S6). The estimated weights were 13 kDa for RBD-Y and 280 kDa for Ft nanoparticles; a minor peak at 27 kDa corresponding to RBD-Y dimers was observed in samples containing abTYR. In the conjugation samples, ferritin peaks shifted entirely to 400 kDa, indicating the formation of RBD-Y-Ft nanoparticles. This 120 kDa shift suggests that RBD-Y-Ft nanoparticles may display an average of eight RBD-Y antigens (assuming a 13 kDa molecular weight of RBD-Y).

Characterization of RBD-Y-Ft nanoparticles. RBD-Y-Ft nanoparticles were obtained using Ft stored at 4 °C (curves in orange) and −80 °C (curves in blue). (A) HPLC chromatograms of Ft+RBD-Y (light) and Ft+RBD-Y-abTYR (dark). (B) Mass photometry curves of Ft alone (light) and Ft+RBD-Y-abTYR (dark).

Mass photometry revealed a shift of 170–200 kDa between Ft and RBD-Y-Ft nanoparticles (Figure 7B), indicating the presence of up to eight RBD-Ys per ferritin nanoparticle, consistent with the HPLC results.

The analysis from both HPLC and mass photometry showed that ferritin stored at 4 °C and −80 °C exhibited comparable degrees of conversion, with the resulting RBD-Y-Ft peaks displaying skewness and indicating a uniform distribution of the functionalized particles.

The reactions were validated through SDS-PAGE and Western blot (Support Information Figure S7); densitometry analysis provided RBD-Y-Ft formation yields of 30–50%. This analysis further supports the successful binding of at least eight RBD-Ys per particle.

Discussion

The tyrosinase-mediated conjugation described in this study relies on the presence of specific molecular components: a substrate with a terminal tyrosine accessible to the enzyme, an active tyrosinase capable of oxidizing the tyrosine residue, and a molecule with nucleophilic groups for conjugation. Among naturally occurring nucleophilic groups on amino acid side chains, cysteine thiol groups are particularly potent.26 Therefore, we utilized the human ferritin heavy chain containing native cysteines as the nucleophile.

The electrophile RBD-Y was designed with an even number of cysteines involved in disulfide bonds,36 preventing them from acting as nucleophiles and competing with ferritin during bioconjugation. When abTYR was present, RBD-Y dimerization was observed; however, this did not occur with RBD lacking the tyrosine-tag. This suggests the potential involvement of other nucleophilic groups, such as primary amines on lysines or at the N-terminal,37 in attacking oxidized tyrosine residues on RBD-Y. Indeed, o-quinones are known to preferentially react with strong nucleophilic groups such as thiol or amine, followed by amide, indole, and imidazole at lower rates,26 and less commonly with carboxylic acids and alcohols, as these are typically not nucleophilic enough to readily react with electrophilic quinones.25 Notably, RBD-Y dimerization occurred to a much lesser extent compared to the conjugation observed between RBD-Y and ferritin.

Although RBD-Y lacks free thiols, it contains several amines in the N-terminal and in residues such as lysines, proline and arginine, which would be expected to increase unspecific dimerization. The low abundance of RBD-Y dimerization may indicate that the tyrosine tag is inaccessible in some RBD-Y molecules, possibly stabilized by hydrogen bonds or facing inward, rendering it unavailable to tyrosinase. To enhance conjugation, a future modification could involve altering the linker between RBD and the tyrosine. Currently, the linker comprises a double glycine (-GG), but a more flexible and soluble linker, such as a glycine-serine (GS)-rich linker,38 could be evaluated to determine if it improves the availability of the tyrosine group.

The human ferritin heavy chain features three cysteine residues: Cys91, Cys103, and Cys131.39 Cys91 is located in the extended loop connecting the B and C α-helices, while Cys103 and Cys131 are situated in the C and D α-helices, respectively. Among these, Cys131 resides within the interior of the ferritin particle34 and is inaccessible for conjugation with antigens. The remaining cysteines, Cys91 and Cys103, are positioned on the exterior,34 but their proximity may limit simultaneous conjugation to both cysteines due to steric hindrance. Consequently, each ferritin particle is likely restricted from forming conjugates with more than 24 antigens.

The Ellman’s assay revealed an estimated average of 0.7 cysteines in the reduced form per ferritin monomer, containing nucleophilic thiol groups available for tyrosinase-mediated conjugation. It is important to note that the DTNB reagent, employed in the assay, can penetrate the ferritin cage through 3-fold channels on the protein surface,40 thereby quantifying both accessible and inaccessible thiols. While theoretically, a 24-mer ferritin nanoparticle could accommodate a maximum of 16–17 antigen conjugates, the presence of steric hindrance and the inaccessibility of inner-particle thiols may result in a lower number. These findings emphasize the necessity of optimizing approaches to maximize thiol accessibility on ferritin nanoparticles, essential for enhancing their utility in bioconjugation applications. Cysteines can exist in various oxidation states (-SH, -SO, −SO2, −SO3), with the thiol (-SH) form being essential for conjugation. Adding reducing agents (e.g., β-ME, DTT, TCEP) during purification, formulation, or as a treatment step followed by desalting before bioconjugation could help maintain the -SH form or convert -SO back to -SH. However, once irreversible forms (−SO2 and −SO3) are present, those cysteines can no longer participate in bioconjugation.

Design of experiments (DoE) allows for the simultaneous testing of multiple variables, potentially shortening the time needed to obtain results and reducing the number of experiments required. DoE could have been beneficial in achieving an optimized reaction conditions more efficiently. However, given the low number of variables involved, we opted to assess each variable independently to determine its specific impact without the influence of other factors. Under optimized conditions, Ellman’s assay confirmed the bioconjugation, suggesting that around 30–60% of ferritin thiols were conjugated with RBD-Y. However, this estimate may not fully reflect the actual bioconjugation yield. The assay considers both accessible and inaccessible thiols, potentially leading to overestimations of available thiols for binding antigens and, consequently, underestimating the conjugation efficiency. Additionally, relying on the absorbance ratio at 512 and 280 nm poses challenges in accurately estimating thiols on ferritin, especially with multiple species contributing to the absorbance at 280 nm. Methods such as LC-TOF-MS, HPLC, mass photometry, and Western blot provided a more conservative estimate, suggesting that 20–30% of ferritin monomers were indeed conjugated with RBD-Y. Nondenaturing analytics, such as HPLC and mass photometry, revealed a shift of ferritin nanoparticles’ molecular weight, indicating homogeneous RBD-Y-Ft conjugate nanoparticles, each containing, on average, eight antigens per particle.

The Ellmańs assay is a straightforward, high-throughput analytical method that demonstrated a 30% decrease in free thiols, suggesting that most, if not all, of the RBD-Y is being conjugated to ferritin via these thiol groups. To verify whether other functional groups on ferritin could also conjugate to RBD-Y, ferritin could be pretreated with the cysteine-capping reagent N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) before the conjugation reaction. If no conjugation is observed after NEM treatment, this would confirm that the conjugation is specific to thiols, thereby supporting the reliability of Ellman’s assay for confirming site-specificity on reduced thiols. Alternatively, mass spectrometry could provide valuable insights into the nature of the conjugation bond, particularly whether the o-quinone is being attacked by a thiol or other nucleophile, such as a primary amine.

While ideally, ferritin would have more than 0.7 thiols per monomer to enhance antigen display on its surface, our findings show that these thiols remain available for conjugation for up to six months of storage at both 4 °C and −80 °C without loss of activity. Moreover, this storage period did not significantly affect ferritin’s structural stability. Samples stored at either temperature showed similar efficiencies in forming RBD-Y-Ft conjugates, producing nanoparticles of uniform size. These results have significant implications for vaccine technology: supporting the cost-effective bulk production of ferritin with long-term storage capabilities; enabling the storage of ferritin for extended periods, making it suitable for conjugation with functional antigens when needed, such as in response to emerging pathogens and vaccine demands; and simplifying vaccine distribution by eliminating the need for extremely cold storage temperatures and handling.

To maximize ferritin’s potential for antigen display through tyrosinase-mediated conjugation, exploring optimizations in production, purification, and storage processes could increase the number of reduced cysteines, thus improving binding site availability to conjugate antigens. Alternatively, a more targeted approach could involve using a ferritin variant lacking native cysteines. For example, archaeal ferritins from Pyrococcus furiosus and Archaeoglobus fulgidus can be genetically modified with single-point mutations to introduce a cysteine at a specific position for conjugation purposes.40

To enhance the robustness of this system at a pilot scale, recombinant expression of tyrosinase would be advantageous. For instance, tyrosinase from Bacillus megaterium has demonstrated the ability to oxidize phenol to o-quinones with excellent selectivity for binding to reduced cysteines.30 This enzyme can be efficiently expressed in E. coli, maintaining high levels of activity, and provides a cost-effective alternative to commercially available tyrosinase. Furthermore, its 35-kDa size enables straightforward separation from conjugated ferritins using high cutoff filters or size-exclusion chromatography.31

Finally, evaluating the immunogenicity and efficacy of RBD-Y-Ft nanoparticles through potency assays could provide valuable insights into their potential as vaccine candidates. Ferritin-based vaccines obtained via bioconjugation have previously shown immunogenic potential. Hemagglutinin (HA) was conjugated to ferritin using NHS and maleimide chemistry and the resultant HA-Ft nanoparticles conferred protection against H1N1 and H3N2 influenza viruses upon immunization in mice.12 The Tag/Catcher approach was used to display the antigens preS1 from hepatitis B virus (HBV)17 and RBD from SARS-CoV-2.14,15,18 preS1-Ft nanoparticles resulted in efficient viral clearance and partial seroconversion in a chronic HBV mouse model,17 whereas RBD-Ft nanoparticles induced persistent and potent neutralizing antibody responses in mice14,15,18 and macaques.14 Importantly, these studies have shown that nanoparticle vaccines elicit a more robust immunogenic response than subunit vaccines.

A caveat of protein-based vaccines is that potency assays significantly rely on animal immunization studies. It is noteworthy that biolayer interferometry,18 capable of estimating binding kinetics between antigens and antibodies, and in vitro cell-based assays14 (e.g., dendritic cell maturation and T-cell activation upon incubation with protein-based vaccines41) have been successfully applied to estimate the potency of ferritin-based vaccines, circumventing the need for animal challenge studies.

Conclusions

The optimization of reaction conditions and use of robust characterization techniques enabled the successful conjugation of SARS-CoV-2 RBD to ferritin nanoparticles. This demonstrates tyrosinase-mediated bioconjugation as a powerful tool for developing innovative vaccine candidates. This approach could potentially enable the display of antigens from WHO-prioritized pathogens such as Ebola and Lassa viruses. Additionally, this method holds promise for other biotechnological applications, including cancer treatment or gene therapy, by conjugating tumor or receptor antigens to the surface of ferritin nanoparticles and encapsulating therapeutic agents such as chemotherapeutics or siRNA within.
