Peroneus brevis tenosynovitis associated with hypertrophied peroneal tubercle: Comparative analysis of 2 cases and their etiologies
Authors: Antonios Michailidis, Ioannis Tsifountoudis, Dimitrios Kitridis, George Paparoidamis, Konstantinos Tasoulis, Danae Makri, Evangelos Petsatodis, Stefanos Finitsis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTenosynovitis of the peroneal brevis tendon is a relatively uncommon condition that can present with lateral foot pain, difficulty ambulating, and, in certain cases, the presence of a hypertrophied tubercle near the insertion site. Although many factors can predispose patients to peroneal tendon pathology—such as biomechanical abnormalities, inflammatory disorders, and connective tissue diseases—presentation can differ widely among individuals. This paper reports 2 distinct cases of peroneal brevis tenosynovitis with hypertrophied tubercles, illustrating how tubercle size, patient demographics, and underlying comorbidities can influence clinical decision-making and outcomes. The first case involves a middle-aged man with a notably large (10 mm) hypertrophied tubercle and minimal comorbidity. In contrast, the second case details an older woman with a smaller (5 mm) tubercle but significant comorbidities, including rheumatoid arthritis and a likely connective tissue disorder, whose weight-bearing pattern contributed more to her condition than the tubercle size itself. Advanced imaging, particularly MRI, played a pivotal role in delineating the underlying mechanisms of tendon pathology by characterizing tubercle size, tendon impingement, peritendinous inflammation, and associated joint or soft-tissue changes. These imaging findings directly influenced management decisions, guiding conservative vs surgical treatment strategies. These cases highlight the importance of individualized treatment approaches that account for both anatomical and systemic factors.Keywords: Peroneal brevis tendon, Tenosynovitis, Hypertrophied peroneal tubercle, Lateral foot pain, MRI, Musculoskeletal imaging

Introduction

Tenosynovitis is a condition characterized by inflammation of the tendon sheath, often leading to pain, swelling, and functional impairment. When affecting the peroneal tendons (particularly the peroneal brevis and longus), the patient typically experiences discomfort along the lateral side of the foot and ankle [1]. The peroneal brevis tendon inserts onto the base of the fifth metatarsal, frequently at or near a bony prominence (sometimes referred to as a tubercle) that can vary in size among individuals [2]. In some instances, this tubercle may be hypertrophied, possibly exacerbating symptoms by altering the tendon’s mechanical environment or by creating an area of increased friction and stress [3].

Although many factors can lead to tenosynovitis—repetitive stress, trauma, degenerative changes, inflammatory arthritis—relatively few case reports focus on the interplay between hypertrophied bony structures and preexisting connective tissue disorders in producing or perpetuating peroneal tendon pathology [4]. Understanding why certain patients present with symptomatic hypertrophied tubercles and subsequent tendon inflammation, while others remain asymptomatic, is vital for tailoring appropriate treatment strategies [5].

In this paper, we present 2 cases of peroneal brevis tenosynovitis with a hypertrophied tubercle. The first case features a middle-aged man with a significantly enlarged tubercle (approximately 10 mm in maximal dimension), who experienced persistent lateral foot pain but had no significant systemic or rheumatologic comorbidities. The second case highlights an older woman with rheumatoid arthritis and other potential connective tissue abnormalities, who presented with a smaller tubercle (around 5 mm) but had more pronounced systemic health complications that may have contributed to her symptoms and protracted healing process. Despite the difference in tubercle size, each case shed light on how factors such as inflammatory comorbidities, weight-bearing patterns, and mechanical stress can collectively determine clinical outcomes.

While peroneal tendon disorders are well described, comparative analyses examining the relative contribution of hypertrophied peroneal tubercle size vs systemic and biomechanical factors remain scarce. In particular, the interaction between localized bony morphology and systemic inflammatory disease has not been adequately explored. Imaging plays a central role in identifying these distinct etiologic drivers, allowing differentiation between mechanical impingement and inflammation related to altered biomechanics or systemic disease. The present report addresses this clinical gap by comparing 2 patients with peroneus brevis tenosynovitis and hypertrophied tubercles, highlighting how imaging findings guide individualized management.

Case presentations

Case 1: A middle-aged man with a large hypertrophied tubercle

Patient profile

A 48-year-old man presented to the orthopedic clinic with a chief complaint of persistent lateral foot pain. He reported a gradual onset of symptoms over the past 6 months, with mild discomfort initially emerging during his daily runs. Over time, the pain intensified and was no longer restricted to athletic activities, eventually impacting his ability to perform prolonged standing and walking. His medical history included only mild hypertension controlled with lifestyle modifications; there were no known connective tissue disorders or systemic inflammatory conditions.

Clinical examination

On examination, the patient exhibited tenderness along the lateral aspect of the foot, specifically near the insertion of the peroneal brevis tendon at the base of the fifth metatarsal. Palpation revealed a prominent bony landmark, which he described as “a lump” on the outside of his foot. The tubercle’s dimensions were estimated at 10 mm in diameter on palpation and subsequent imaging. Passive range of motion of the ankle was relatively preserved, but resisted eversion elicited significant pain.

There were no overt signs of rheumatoid arthritis, such as ulnar deviation or other joint deformities in the hands, nor any mention of autoimmune disease in his history. He denied ankle instability or any acute traumatic events. The remainder of his foot exam showed normal alignment, with a mild tendency toward a higher arch, which can predispose to lateral foot issues by increasing strain on the peroneal tendons.

Initial imaging assessment

Radiographs of the right foot (anteroposterior, lateral, and oblique views) revealed a prominently hypertrophied tubercle, measuring approximately 10 mm in maximal diameter, at the base of the fifth metatarsal. No significant osteoarthritic changes or osteophytes were noted elsewhere in the foot. The bony prominence was distinctly localized to the peroneal brevis tendon insertion.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

MRI provided further characterization, demonstrating a large bony outgrowth in direct contact with the peroneal brevis tendon. T2-weighted and STIR sequences showed marked peritendinous hyperintensity surrounding the tendon sheath, consistent with active tenosynovitis. The tendon appeared mildly thickened without evidence of partial or full-thickness tear. Adjacent soft tissues exhibited subtle edema, and there was clear mechanical impingement between the hypertrophied tubercle and the tendon (Fig. 1).

Conservative management was initiated, including:

After 6 weeks, the patient reported modest improvement, but he remained limited in high-impact activities. A corticosteroid injection into the tendon sheath was performed under ultrasound guidance to ensure accurate placement within the peroneal tendon sheath, providing significant pain relief for 2 months. However, residual pain persisted, largely due to the mechanical irritation from the hypertrophied tubercle.

Surgery was eventually offered, consisting of resection of the hypertrophied tubercle and debridement of any inflamed peroneal tendon sheath. Intraoperatively, the tubercle was found to be consistently larger than anticipated, measuring around 10 mm in its maximal diameter. Following the procedure, the patient was placed in a short walking boot for 4 weeks and thereafter transitioned to physical therapy. By 3 months postoperatively, he reported near-complete resolution of pain and had resumed moderate running activities with minimal discomfort.

Case 2: An older woman with a small hypertrophied tubercle and rheumatoid arthritis

Patient profile

A 72-year-old woman presented with complaints of chronic lateral foot pain, worse over the past year. She had a long-standing history of rheumatoid arthritis, diagnosed in her mid-fifties, for which she was on disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs). Additionally, she had a history of osteoporosis and degenerative joint disease in her knees, hips, and spine, partly attributable to rheumatoid arthritis and her advanced age. She used a cane for ambulation and reported difficulty walking even short distances around her home.

Clinical examination

On examination, the patient had obvious hallmarks of rheumatoid arthritis in her hands, such as ulnar drift and metacarpophalangeal joint deformities. In her feet, she displayed moderate deformities including bunions and hammertoes, though these were more pronounced in the medial and dorsal aspects rather than the lateral foot. Palpation over the peroneal brevis tendon insertion revealed tenderness and slight swelling. A small bony prominence of approximately 5 mm was noted at the base of the fifth metatarsal. Resisted eversion induced pain, although it was tolerable compared to the examination in Case 1.

Significantly, her gait suggested a propensity to place excessive pressure on the lateral border of her foot. This abnormal weight-bearing pattern may have contributed to chronic stress on the peroneal brevis tendon. Despite the smaller tubercle size, her compromised connective tissue integrity and altered biomechanics likely led to persistent inflammation.

Imaging studies

Radiographic evaluation

Plain radiographs of the left foot (anteroposterior, lateral, and oblique views) revealed a mildly hypertrophied tubercle at the base of the fifth metatarsal, measuring approximately 5 mm. No significant osteophytes or fractures were present. The osseous contours of the metatarsals were thinned, and there was generalized osteopenia, consistent with chronic rheumatoid arthritis and age-related bone loss. Mild joint space narrowing was visible in the midfoot, without gross subluxation.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

MRI provided a comprehensive assessment of the soft-tissue and bony structures. T2-weighted and STIR sequences demonstrated a small bony prominence at the peroneal brevis insertion, surrounded by a moderate amount of peritendinous fluid, indicating mild tenosynovitis. The peroneal brevis tendon itself appeared slightly thickened but retained homogeneous low signal on T1 and T2, with no partial or full-thickness tears, splits, or tendinosis. Axial images confirmed that the hypertrophied tubercle did not cause direct impingement on the tendon.

Importantly, the MRI also revealed evidence of chronic synovitis in adjacent tarsometatarsal and intermetatarsal joints, with synovial thickening and patchy areas of increased fluid signal. Subcortical cysts, mild erosions, and periarticular edema were seen in the neighboring bones—findings highly suggestive of longstanding inflammatory arthropathy. No discrete bursitis or soft-tissue masses were present. Mild edema was evident in the subcutaneous tissues along the lateral aspect of the foot, consistent with chronic low-grade inflammation and altered pressure distribution (Fig. 2).

Imaging synthesis and interpretation

The combination of a small hypertrophied tubercle, peritendinous fluid, and soft-tissue edema, in the context of chronic joint and bone changes, indicated that the tenosynovitis was multifactorial. Over several months, her lateral foot pain gradually subsided. Though she experienced mild discomfort during extended periods of standing or walking, she had a more marked improvement in day-to-day function once her gait was corrected and systemic inflammatory control improved. Surgical intervention was deemed unnecessary. Unlike the first case, in which the tubercle’s prominent size directly contributed to tendon impingement, this patient’s persistent symptoms appeared related more to her underlying connective tissue disorder, rheumatoid arthritis, and maladaptive weight-bearing than the modest tubercle itself.

By her 6-month follow-up, her pain levels had decreased substantially, and she felt more stable on her feet. She continued conservative management, with periodic reassessment. Ultimately, the case highlighted that in an older patient with significant comorbidities, addressing systemic and biomechanical issues may be more critical than focusing on the modest bony hypertrophy.

Discussion

Tenosynovitis of the peroneal brevis tendon is often under-recognized or overshadowed by other foot and ankle pathologies such as lateral ankle sprains, fibular stress fractures, or tendinopathies of the peroneus longus [6]. When a bony prominence or tubercle at the insertion site of the peroneal brevis tendon is present, it can become a primary consideration in diagnosis and management [7]. However, as demonstrated in these 2 cases, the mere presence of a bony hypertrophy does not uniformly dictate symptom severity or prognosis.

Case 1 illustrates a scenario where a large hypertrophied tubercle (10 mm) was likely the principal contributor to persistent tendon inflammation, as friction and mechanical impingement led to repeated microtrauma [8]. Despite attempts at conservative management, the patient’s symptoms improved only partially until surgical resection of the tubercle relieved the anatomic stressor. It is notable that the patient had no significant systemic comorbidities or inflammatory conditions. This underscores that, in individuals without major systemic risk factors, a large bony prominence can be a primary problem amenable to surgical correction when conservative measures fail [9,10].

By contrast, Case 2 involves an older patient with rheumatoid arthritis—a systemic inflammatory disease that not only affects joint integrity but can also compromise the structure and function of tendons, ligaments, and bursae [11]. Additionally, advanced age and long-standing disease often lead to changes in gait and weight-bearing patterns, placing abnormal stresses on certain foot structures. Although she also had a hypertrophied tubercle, its size (5 mm) was relatively small. Imaging did not strongly suggest that the tubercle was the direct source of impingement or tendon irritation. Instead, her connective tissue disorder and malaligned gait likely played a more significant role in sustaining the inflammatory state of the peroneal brevis tendon. This aligns with well-known principles that in rheumatoid arthritis, tendon inflammation can be multifactorial, impacted by synovial and extra-synovial inflammation, bone erosion, and biomechanical changes [12].

In both reported cases, the pathology included an element of structural abnormality (the hypertrophied tubercle), but the overall contribution of this feature varied depending on patient-specific factors. In a younger or middle-aged adult with otherwise normal biomechanics, a larger tubercle might be the leading driver of pain [15], while in an older patient with systemic disease, a smaller tubercle can still be overshadowed by diffuse inflammatory processes and gait deviations.

Differential diagnostic considerations

Lateral foot pain associated with peroneal tendon abnormalities requires careful differentiation from other entities with overlapping clinical and imaging features. Peroneus longus tenosynovitis may present similarly but typically involves pathology distal to the cuboid tunnel and may be associated with os peroneum abnormalities. Os peroneum syndrome is characterized by pain related to fracture, diastasis, or degeneration of the os peroneum and is best identified on radiographs and MRI by marrow edema or discontinuity. Stress reaction or fracture at the base of the fifth metatarsal can also mimic peroneal tendon pathology and is distinguished by bone marrow edema patterns and cortical disruption on MRI. In the present cases, MRI excluded these alternative diagnoses by demonstrating isolated peroneus brevis sheath inflammation, absence of os peroneum pathology, and lack of stress-related osseous changes.

Clinical implications

Clinicians must perform thorough evaluations—both local (foot structure, range of motion, bony prominences) and systemic (rheumatologic status, weight-bearing patterns)—to arrive at an individualized treatment plan. Although imaging can confirm tenosynovitis and quantify tubercle size, it is equally crucial to assess alignment, gait, muscle imbalances, and the possibility of inflammatory flares, especially in patients with rheumatologic disease.

Case 1 highlights that even a well-designed conservative regimen (physical therapy, NSAIDs, orthotics) might not suffice if the underlying mechanical irritant (in this instance, a large tubercle) remains unaddressed. Case 2, however, suggests that in a patient burdened by systemic disease and gait abnormalities, the smaller tubercle is not necessarily the prime culprit; conservative measures aimed at the underlying inflammatory condition and biomechanics can lead to significant relief, often avoiding surgery.

Surgical vs conservative management

When deciding between conservative and surgical treatment, factors such as patient age, comorbidities, level of physical activity, and the extent of anatomical abnormality must be weighed. A large tubercle that clearly impinges on the tendon or causes mechanical friction is more likely to require surgical excision if non-operative measures fail. Conversely, if the tubercle is minimal or only mildly symptomatic, and the patient has significant medical risks (eg, advanced rheumatoid arthritis, poor bone quality, high surgical risk profile), an extended trial of conservative management with orthotic adjustments, optimized systemic treatment, and physical therapy is prudent.

In rheumatoid arthritis, it is important to collaborate with rheumatologists to ensure that disease activity is controlled, as unchecked inflammatory activity may render local orthopedic interventions less effective. Corticosteroid injections can provide temporary relief in some cases but may carry risks such as tendon weakening, especially in fragile rheumatoid arthritic tendons.

Reported management outcomes for peroneal brevis tenosynovitis associated with a hypertrophied peroneal tubercle vary according to symptom severity, tubercle size, and patient comorbidities. Conservative treatment—including activity modification, orthotics, physical therapy, and anti-inflammatory medication—often yields satisfactory results in cases without clear mechanical impingement. However, surgical excision of the hypertrophied tubercle combined with tendon sheath debridement has demonstrated favorable outcomes in patients with persistent symptoms and imaging-confirmed mechanical irritation. The present cases align with existing literature, supporting surgical intervention when a large tubercle is the dominant etiologic factor [16], while favoring conservative management when systemic or biomechanical contributors predominate.

Long-term outlook and prevention

The prognosis for peroneal brevis tenosynovitis with hypertrophied tubercle varies widely and depends on early recognition and tailored management. A patient without systemic disease who undergoes prompt surgical correction of a large tubercle (if conservative measures fail) has a good chance of recovery, returning to high-level activities with minimal sequelae. Conversely, older patients with rheumatologic disorders might experience chronic, relapsing tendon and joint symptoms that require ongoing medical therapy and periodic physical therapy.

Emphasis on biomechanical optimization—through custom orthotics, supportive footwear, and targeted exercises—plays a vital role in both initial treatment and long-term prevention of recurrence. Additionally, maintaining a healthy weight, if possible, and engaging in routine low-impact exercise can help reduce stress on the foot and ankle, thereby decreasing the risk of persistent tenosynovitis.

Conclusion

These 2 case reports illustrate contrasting presentations of tenosynovitis of the peroneal brevis tendon in the presence of a hypertrophied tubercle. In the first case, a middle-aged man had a pronounced (10 mm) tubercle that provoked mechanical irritation, requiring eventual surgical resection for definitive relief. Meanwhile, the second case—a 72-year-old woman with rheumatoid arthritis—demonstrated how a modest (5 mm) bony outgrowth could be overshadowed by systemic inflammatory factors, altered gait, and weight-bearing patterns. Her improvement resulted predominantly from addressing these systemic and biomechanical concerns rather than surgically targeting the tubercle.

These findings highlight the multidimensional nature of foot and ankle pathologies, urging clinicians to incorporate comprehensive assessments—orthopedic, rheumatologic, and functional—when formulating treatment plans. Anatomical anomalies, such as a hypertrophied tubercle, can be a crucial element in some instances. In others, the complex interplay of systemic disease, connective tissue fragility, and chronic malalignment may demand a broader therapeutic focus. Ultimately, individualizing management by balancing local and systemic considerations can lead to optimal outcomes in peroneal brevis tendon tenosynovitis.

These cases underscore the importance of integrating high-resolution imaging with clinical and systemic assessment to accurately identify the dominant etiologic factor and guide appropriate, patient-specific management.
