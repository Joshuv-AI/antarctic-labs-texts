iBody-mediated tuning of synthetic cytokine receptor activation via rational nanobody interface engineering
Authors: Richard Kunze, Fabian Navratil, Joana Beichert, Felix Geyer, Doreen M Floss, Harald Kolmar, Jürgen Scheller, Silke Pudewell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNanobodies are small, single-domain antibody fragments derived from heavy chain-only antibodies. They combine high binding affinity with advantages such as compact size, stability, solubility, and flexible epitope recognition, making them attractive tools in molecular biology and therapeutic applications. In this study, we engineered and optimized nanobodies for controlled activation of synthetic cytokine receptors, aiming to expand options for receptor customization. Specifically, we used nanobodies as extracellular domains of the gp130 receptor to induce dimerization upon antigen binding. To enable receptor activity, we introduced framework mutations that promote the formation of an i-shaped nanobody (iBody) dimer, adapted from i-shaped antibodies. These mutations enhanced dimerization and enabled low-level ligand-independent receptor activation. AlphaFold modeling identified the key amino acids responsible for forming the iBody interface. Additional modifications reduced intermolecular affinity, thereby minimizing background activation while preserving the structural features necessary for ligand-induced stimulation. This approach effectively broadened the receptor’s activation range. Importantly, these framework mutations were not limited to the gp130-specific nanobody GP11 but were also functional in AIP3, an anti-idiotypic nanobody targeting palivizumab. Here, the modified nanobody fusion receptor could be activated by palivizumab, overcoming prior steric hindrance.KEYWORDS: Nanobody, single-domain antibody, iBody, synthetic cytokine receptor, receptor dimerization, interface engineering

Introduction

Specific and high-affinity protein-binding domains are incorporated in numerous biopharmaceuticals, including monoclonal antibodies (mAbs). Typically 150 kDa, mAbs target antigens using the variable domains of their light and heavy chain. They are well established in the clinical treatment of various diseases due to their long serum half-life of several weeks, compared to small molecules, and reduced off-target effects, which result from their high affinity for the target antigen – typically in the low nanomolar to picomolar range – combined with strong specificity.1–3 Nevertheless, mAbs lack the ability to effectively penetrate tissues, they interact with FcγR and FcRn, which is beneficial for increased serum half-life, but might be unfavorable due to Fc-mediated effector functions or immune activation, and they are more difficult to customize based on their structure. Smaller antibody units such as antigen binding fragments (Fabs) or single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) represent one approach to address some of these drawbacks, but they are less effectively expressed in vitro. Another solution is the variable domain of camelid-derived heavy chain only antibodies (hcAbs), called VHH, nanobody, or single-domain antibody (sdAb). These small (~15 kDa) antigen-binding proteins exhibit strong binding affinity and bind to a broad range of linear and structural binding epitopes, some of which may be inaccessible for mAbs. They also have high stability in respect to pH and temperature and the ability to cross the blood–brain barrier, as well as good tissue penetration.4–7

Nanobodies have already found their way into clinical treatment and are also playing a greater role in research. The relatively simple handling of these highly specific antibody units makes them perfect for “mixing and matching” with other proteins, thus increasing the flexibility and affinity to their binding partners.8 Nanobodies as antagonists are approved for the treatment of acquired thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) (caplacizumab9 and ozoralizumab,10 respectively), and for different types of solid tumors and chronic hepatitis B in China, and for soft tissue sarcoma and biliary tract cancer (envafolimab)11 in the US. Nanobodies as agonists are not approved for clinical use, but there are many examples in the current research, such as multimeric nanobodies against death receptor 5 (DR5), which is thought to trigger apoptosis in cancer cells with high DR5 expression but no other TRAIL binding receptors.12 Furthermore, nanobodies are used in other applications, such as detecting antigens for diagnostic purposes and imaging.13

Over the past 20 y, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy has emerged and evolved as a successful immunotherapy against cancer, specifically several types of leukemia. The principle is based on the fusion of the scFv of mAbs to different intracellular receptor domains that can activate (CD3ζ), co-stimulate (e.g., 4-1BB, CD28)14,15, or induce cytokine signaling (e.g., IL-12, IL-2 Rβ)16 in the modified T cells, which is then directed against the cancer cells.17 Optimization of these receptor fusion proteins has produced five generations of CARs, adding and switching receptor domains mentioned before, but also changing the targeting domain for different antigens (reviewed in)16,18. The scFv domain itself was exchanged with nanobodies to utilize their positive benefits, for example, to avoid domain swapping. The first approved nanobody-based CAR, ciltacabtagen autoleucel, targets B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) and is used for the treatment of relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma.19 Other nanobody CAR-approaches focus on the treatment of solid tumors,20–22 while bispecific nanobody CARs are being developed for the treatment of T cell lymphoma.23 Nanobodies have also been used to alter the microenvironment of solid tumors via CAR-T cells secreting anti-PD-L1, anti-CTLA-4 or anti-CD47 nanobodies.24 The “quick and easy” production of nanobodies, their cloning in libraries and their expression as CARs make them an optimal building block for the production of new receptor fusion proteins.25 As receptor clustering is the basic principle of CAR signaling, mutations that favor receptor interaction might be interesting to intensify CAR-T cell activation and response.

This study focuses on nanobody-fusion constructs, utilizing the intracellular domain (ICD) of glycoprotein 130 (gp130) fused to an extracellular nanobody. This platform has been successfully established by our group using different receptor combinations and fluorophores as ligands.26,27 Inducing dimerization of gp130ICD leads to activation of the JAK-STAT pathway and subsequently to proliferation. The positioning of the extracellular domain and the flexibility of the linker between the transmembrane domain and the nanobody is important for JAK trans-phosphorylation and therefore signaling.28 When receptors are supposed to be activated by (artificial) ligands, the orientation of the binding site, as well as the size and rigidity of the ligand needs to be considered carefully. This is also a reason why antibodies are usually used as antagonists, rather than agonists. Decreasing the distance of antigen binding modules in mAbs was observed naturally in a broadly neutralizing antibody (bnAb) against human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) envelope glycoprotein, called 2G12.29 Here, changes in the framework of the variable domain of the heavy chain (VH) led to domain-swapping and intramolecular Fab dimerization. Other bnAbs directed against glycosylated proteins were identified, which induced Fab dimerization without domain swapping.30 Based on these antibodies, Romei et al. selected a set of mutations, which allowed intramolecular interactions of the VH domains in mAbs, creating an i-shaped antibody structure and therefore decreasing the distance of the two paratopes.31 Their study showed the activation of receptors of the tumor necrosis factor super family (TNFRSF), which are known to act as homo- or hetero-trimers, using their i-shaped antibodies as ligands. Transferring these mutations to our synthetic cytokine receptor system, we aimed to design and engineer nanobodies, instead of antibodies, as an extracellular domain (ECD) with an i-shaped structure, here called iBodies. With that we wanted to characterize framework mutations of nanobodies to increase dimerization (and clustering) and reduce the spacing of the interacting units.

To achieve this goal, we produced nanobody-receptor fusion proteins of gp130-specific nanobodies (GP01, GP11, GP13 and GP20)32 with gp130ICD and tested them for activity. Subsequently, we introduced the iBody mutations into the strongest binder GP11 and characterized the ligand-dependent and ligand-independent receptor activation. Moreover, we optimized the system by selecting specific mutations that reduce intermolecular iBody interactions and fine-tune ligand-independent signaling, while retaining their stimulatory behavior. Finally, these mutations were transferred to another nanobody and tested as a general proof-of-concept system.

Results

Fusion receptors of gp130-specific nanobodies and gp130ICD are inactive

Nanobodies as inhibitors and extracellular domains of receptors were used in our group in diverse settings and combinations. Our latest nanobodies against gp130 provided encouraging results as antagonists for the majority of IL-6-type cytokine signaling.32 In this study, we reversed the nanobody-ligand combination to generate synthetic cytokine receptors that can get activated by soluble gp130ECD dimers (sgp130-Fc). The anti-gp130 nanobodies GP01, GP11, GP13, and GP20 were fused to the transmembrane- and intracellular domain of gp130, either without an additional linker (containing the amino acids EF), or with a TEV-linker (ENLYFQSGSEPEF) (Figure 1A). As the linker can have an immense influence on the signaling ability of a receptor,33 these two combinations were tested simultaneously. The expression and binding ability of the fusion receptors were analyzed in an immunofluorescence-based binding assay using sgp130-Fc, and anti-human-Fc-PE (Supplemental Figure S1). The synthetic receptor variants without linker had the tendency to be either a little more expressed at the cell surface of the HEK293T cells (which do express gp130 endogenously), or bind sgp130-Fc better, compared to the TEV-Linker variants. Nevertheless, both receptor/linker-variants were able to associate with sgp130-Fc, but none could be activated and induce downstream signaling toward STAT3 (Figure 1B).

iBody mutations lead to ligand-dependent but constitutive activation of the nanobody-receptor fusion proteins

Potentially the nanobody-receptor fusion proteins could not be activated by sgp130-Fc because the distance between the two engaged receptors is too large. Therefore, specific mutations based on the i-shaped antibody DH851.331 were introduced into the framework of the nanobody GP11 (Figure 1C), consequently called iGP11. These mutations are predicted to form non-covalent nanobody dimers by direct hydrophobic interactions that are very similar to those found in the DH851.3 interface (Figure 1D, Supplemental Figure S2). Ba/F3 cells, which do not express gp130 endogenously, were used for the following experiments as a low-background model system for cytokine signaling and cytokine-dependent proliferation. Both nanobody-receptor fusion proteins (GP11- and iGP11-gp130) could be detected at the cell surface of Ba/F3 cells and were shown to be able to bind their target, using an immunofluorescence-based binding assay with sgp130-Fc and anti-human-Fc-PE (Figure 1E). The surface expression of the iGP11-gp130 receptor was reduced in comparison to the GP11-gp130 receptor, which might be based on receptor internalization upon ligand-independent dimerization.34 It is assumed that the binding affinity of iGP11 to its antigen remains unchanged, as the CDRs should not be affected by the framework mutations (Supplemental Figure S2) and the nanobodies, as Fc-fusion proteins, exhibited a similar half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) in Ba/F3-gp130 cells cultured with Hyper-IL-6-containing cell supernatant (Supplemental Figure S3). This time, the iBody receptor could be activated by sgp130-Fc and display concentration-dependent phospho-STAT3 (Y705) levels upon stimulation (Figure 1F,G, Supplemental Figure S4). The stimulatory effect of sgp130-Fc on the iGP11-gp130 receptor could be quantified by proliferation assays and resulted in an EC50 of 0.84 ± 0.39 nM (Figure 1H). The proliferation was normalized to Ba/F3-iGP11-gp130 cells stimulated with IL-3-conditioned cell supernatant (WEHI) and to unstimulated cells, which exhibit approximately 50% of the maximum growth of the positive control in the unstimulated state (Figure 3B). Therefore, these cells were considered autoproliferative, as the iGP11-gp130 receptor seems to be constitutively active. Titration of the JAK inhibitor Pyridone 6 (P6) on iGP11-gp130 cells resulted in an inhibition of autoproliferation with an IC50 of 124.37 ± 18.24 nM, indicating ligand-independent signaling of the iGP11-gp130 receptor toward JAK-STAT pathway as expected (Figure 1I).

Structural model-based optimization of iBody mutations leads to reduced and minimal nanobody variants

In order to fine-tune ligand-dependent and reduce ligand-independent synthetic cytokine receptor activity, the iBody mutations were structurally modeled, and two more variants were generated based on the amino acids predicted to be most important for dimerization. The first one contained five of the eight original iBody mutations (called iGP11-Reduced or iGP11-R) and the second one contained only four mutations (called iGP11-Minimal or iGP11-M) (Figure 2A,B). Utilizing Jalview to analyze the set of mutations, affected amino acids were grouped into conservative, semi- and non-conservative substitutions, based on the change in physicochemical properties and the BLOSUM 62 substitution matrix.35,36 Further in silico quantification of changes in single-residue affinity (ΔΔG) using DDMut-PPI suggested that the minimal mutational set, consisting of only four essential mutations, would retain some ability to homodimerize (Figure 2C).37 The reduced and minimal variants of iGP11 were supposed to interact with a much weaker affinity. The Kd was predicted using PRODIGY, and showed values of 1.09 ± 0.23 µM, 6.13 ± 5.31 µM, and 34.25 ± 22.17 µM (at 25°C, n = 4) for iGP11, iGP11-R, and iGP11-M homodimers, respectively (Figure 2D). This is accompanied by an increasingly deviating orientation of the predicted homotypic nanobody interface from the experimentally determined DH851.3-VH interface (Supplemental Figure S2). Low internal confidence-values of our dimer-predictions (ipTM ≤ 0.3) and a significantly increased standard deviation for iGP11-R and especially iGP11-M indicate the limitations of the in silico simulations.

Reducing iBody mutations results in lower ligand-independent synthetic cytokine receptor activity of the fusion-receptors but retains the ligand-dependent activation capacity

The new fusion receptors iGP11-R-gp130 and iGP11-M-gp130 were cloned and transduced to Ba/F3 cells (Supplemental Figure S5) and tested in stimulation assays with sgp130-Fc. The cells displayed lower ligand-independent activity but could still be activated toward STAT3 signaling (Figure 3A). Cell proliferation assays confirmed these observations by significantly lower proliferation of the unstimulated cells in the reduced and minimal receptor variants, still able to be activated by sgp130-Fc (Figure 3B). The EC50 of sgp130-Fc to the Ba/F3-iGP11-R-gp130 and Ba/F3-iGP11-M-gp130 showed values comparable to the original Ba/F3-iGP11-gp130 cells, with 0.33 ± 0.12 and 0.36 ± 0.26 nM, respectively (Figure 3C,D). The discrepancy of lower pSTAT3 levels in stimulated cells and a similar EC50 value for cell proliferation with the same ligand pointed to a difference in stimulation kinetics, as the phosphorylation levels of STAT3 were only observed at one specific time point. Therefore, time-dependent phosphorylation was analyzed for iGP11, iGP11-R, iGP11-M, as well as the wildtype gp130 receptor using sgp130-Fc and Hyper-IL-6 as indicated (Figure 3E). The iGP11-gp130 cells, which were autoproliferative, show high ligand-independent phosphorylation and maximal-level stimulation after 5 min, with no negative feedback after 90 min. The reduction in iBody mutations appears to delay receptor activation to 30 min by sustaining the level of maximal stimulation. Furthermore, iGP11- M-gp130 displays a little lower pSTAT3 levels after 5 and 15 min compared to iGP11-R-gp130, going along with stimulation experiments in Figure 3A. Downregulation of STAT3 signaling could be observed after 90 min, indicating a functioning negative feedback response. The wildtype receptor reached its maximal activation, which was about two times stronger, after 15 min. The downregulation could be detected after 45 min, overall indicating a much slower JAK-STAT signaling of the receptor chimeras compared to the wildtype signaling with Hyper-IL-6.

Anti-gp130 nanobody-receptors interact autocrine but not juxtacrine with gp130

Interestingly, transduction of GP11- and the iGP11-gp130 receptor variants to Ba/F3 cells, which artificially express human gp130 (called Ba/F3-gp130), leads to a strong autoproliferative phenotype. This can be explained by drastically increased ligand-independent levels of pSTAT3 in all described cell lines, while the introduction of the iBody mutations retained the sensitivity to sgp130-Fc stimulation (Figure 4A,B). The responsiveness to the stimulation with sgp130-Fc is reduced in the iGP11-gp130 receptor cell line, which might be due to lower surface expression of the receptor (Supplemental Figure S5). To analyze whether this ligand-independent activation derives from the interaction of the anti-gp130 nanobody receptors with endogenous gp130 or the juxtacrine interaction with neighboring cells bearing the gp130 receptor, co-culture experiments were performed using green fluorescent Ba/F3-gp130-puro-GFP cells (Figure 4C). No difference could be spotted in the proliferation of GP11-gp130, iGP11-gp130, iGP11-R-gp130, or iGP11-M-gp130 in Ba/F3-gp130 cells with or without Ba/F3-gp130-puro-GFP cells, which could possibly interact juxtacrine with the gp130-specifc nanobody-fusion receptors and stimulate cell proliferation (Figure 4D). Furthermore, the extracellular anti-gp130 nanobodies could not stimulate Ba/F3-gp130-puro-GFP proliferation (Supplemental Figure S6). To confirm the formation of heteromeres, competition experiments were performed, using the soluble GP11-Fc protein on the three autoproliferative cell lines Ba/F3-iGP11-gp130, Ba/F3-gp130-iGP11-gp130, and Ba/F3-gp130-GP11-gp130. Here, excess amounts of GP11-Fc act as a competitive inhibitor for binding to the gp130 receptor. A reduction in proliferation could be observed for cells bearing the gp130 receptor, indicating the formation of heteromers at the cell membrane, but not for Ba/F3-iGP11-gp130 cells, which showed some non-concentration-dependent changes in proliferation (Figure 4E). In contrast, Ba/F3-gp130-GP11-gp130 cells displayed a concentration-dependent reduction of proliferation, whereas the iGP11 cells sustained proliferation at a reduced level. This observation leads to the assumption that the GP11-Fc interferes with heteromerization but cannot disturb the iBody homodimers, which keep the ligand-independent proliferation at a constant level.

iBody mutations enable activation of AIP3-gp130 by palivizumab and can be adjusted by reduced and minimal variants

The synthetic nanobody-receptor fusion proteins could possibly be used to specifically trigger cell proliferation, for example, in the context of CAR-T cell exhaustion.38 Generally, sustaining a low level of ligand-independent stimulation might be favorable in the expansion and anti-tumor activity of CAR-T cells as well39 and could be achieved by adapting the iBody mutations in the synthetic receptors. As the previously tested system is not suitable for in vivo, applications, mainly because almost every cell type (including T cells) expresses the gp130 receptor, we aimed to transfer our knowledge of the iBody interface to another nanobody-receptor system. Therefore, the anti-idiotypic nanobody against palivizumab (AIP3) was used. AIP3 binds the complementary-determining regions of palivizumab (Synagis®, AstraZeneca), a marketed humanized IgG1κ mAb against the respiratory syncytial virus (RSV). Palivizumab has no natural target in the human body. Therefore, a nanobody-receptor fusion protein could be stimulated by palivizumab without other side effects. AIP3-gp130 fusion proteins still struggled to get activated by the mAb. Optimizations of the size and rigidity of the linker in both the receptor and the ligand led to stimulation but were unable to use the palivizumab without Fc cross-linking antibodies.28 The iBody mutations were transferred to the framework of AIP3, creating a receptor called iAIP3-gp130 (Figure 5A). Furthermore, the reduced and minimal set of mutations were applied to the iAIP3 framework to confirm the adaptability of the interaction. The fusion proteins were each transduced to Ba/F3 and Ba/F3-gp130 cells. Next, all cells were stimulated with palivizumab, resulting in different EC50 values, displayed in Figure 5B, and STAT3 phosphorylation levels (Figure 5C,E). In Ba/F3 cells, pSTAT3 could not be detected for the unmutated AIP3-gp130 receptor, but could for the full mutated iAIP3- and the reduced iAIP3-R-gp130 receptor variants. The minimal variant did not show any pSTAT3 signal after 20 min of stimulation or any proliferation (Figure 5C). The proliferation assays reflect the decreasing affinity of the modified iAIP3 constructs (Figure 5D). Ba/F3-gp130 cells, which are primed to proliferate solely on STAT3 stimulation, and are therefore more sensitive to manipulations of this pathway, indicate a decrease in STAT3 phosphorylation levels after 20 min with reducing numbers of framework mutations (Figure 5E). Anyways, all Ba/F3-gp130 cells were autoproliferative, as even small ligand-independent signaling could lead to this phenotype in STAT3 (hyper)sensitive cells. In proliferation assays, the reduced variant performed even better than the iAIP3-gp130 receptor (EC50: 13.09 ± 12.93 nM), with an EC50 of 2.15 ± 0.73 nM. With an EC50 of 15.74 ± 5.92 nM, the minimal variant was in a similar range to the fully mutated nanobody, but failed to hit the same maximal proliferation (Figure 5F). Stimulation kinetics of Ba/F3 and Ba/F3-gp130 cells with the iAIP-gp130 receptor variants revealed a delay in STAT3 phosphorylation for the reduced and minimal variants compared to the fully mutated iAIP-gp130 (Supplemental Figure S7). These results are comparable to the data from the iGP11-gp130 reduced and minimal variants and support the selection of amino acids to reduce the self-assembly and affinity.

The results are displayed the mean of fluorescence of pSTAT3/STAT3, normalized to the positive control (striped bar) Ba/F3-gp130 cells stimulated with 2 µl/ml Hy (Hyper-IL-6-conditioned cell supernatant). Each datapoint represents the mean ± SD of three technical replicates.

Discussion

Nanobodies are widely used in research and are shifting more into focus as novel therapeutics. Custom engineering of nanobodies can be used to create bispecific molecules, increase serum half-life, and enhance or inhibit (self-)interactions. Our work utilizes and modifies a set of mutations that enables self-dimerization between nanobodies and allows downstream signaling of nanobody fusion receptors by reducing the distance of their intracellular domains. The flexible adaptation of such distances is important not only for the synthetic gp130 receptor activation but can likely be transferred to many other kinds of transmembrane receptors that are activated by homodimerization.40,41 Furthermore, this could be interesting for the modulation of nanobody-based ligands, which are fairly sensitive to the orientation of the receptors to match their intracellular domains in order to signal.42

Initially, the eight mutations (here called iBody mutations) were found in broadly neutralizing antibodies. They occurred naturally and led to a dimerization of two VH domains, causing an i-shaped antibody structure. Romei et al. reported the activation of TNFRSF receptors, which act as homo- (and in some cases hetero-) trimers,31,43,44 by i-shaped antibodies that specifically bind their ECDs. This observation caused us to speculate about the role of clustering while using iBody mutations. Clustering is a central aspect of TNFRSF activation and was shown to be crucial for downstream activation of the NFκB pathway.45,46 Inducing an i-shaped antibody structure, which reduces the distance between the two paratopes, could lead to a comparatively closer proximity of the bound receptors. However, as numerous studies have shown, this is not enough to fully activate most receptors of the TNFR super family.47–49 Here, low or high order clustering is necessary in order to trigger downstream effects. The framework mutations in the VH domain enables their affinity for each other and leads to an i-shaped antibody structure. The appearance of these intramolecular homodimers (VH-VH) is determined by their association and dissociation, which could not only induce intramolecular dimers but high order clustering between different antibodies, by intermolecular domain exchange as it was observed for IgG1 mAbs.50 In our case, cluster formation helps explain one of the main questions we had about the iBody system. Our concept is illustrated in Supplemental Figure S8. The unmutated nanobody-receptor chimeras (GP11-gp130 and AIP3-gp130) showed little or no activation by their respective ligands (sgp130-Fc and palivizumab). The most straightforward reason is that the ligands do not reduce the distance between the receptors (specifically their intracellular domains) enough to induce trans-phosphorylation of the JAK proteins. The iBody mutations may enable this by either inducing homodimerization between the two bound receptors within the same ligand or allowing the mutated receptor to recruit an additional receptor chimera. This could lead to clustering of at least four receptors or even higher-order clusters. AIP3-gp130, which is hardly activated by palivizumab alone, can get activated with an EC50 of 2.71–17.21 nM when an anti-Fc antibody is added in a ratio of 1:6.51 This value is close to the results of iAIP3-gp130, stimulated with only palivizumab. Even though it was not possible for the GP11-gp130 receptor to induce signaling with the sgp130-Fc and an anti-Fc antibody as a crosslinker (Supplemental Figure S9), the theory of multimeric ligand-connected clustering now driven by the iBody interactions may hold true. On the other hand, interactions, which are happening between the receptors bound by the same ligand, might also be enough to stimulate the JAK-STAT pathway, especially because the reduction in dimensionality is increasing the probability of intramolecular interactions with even low affinities. The affinity of gp130 and GP11 is approximately 1.1 nM,32 whereas the predicted affinity between iGP11 dimers is in the low micromolar range – about 1,000 times weaker. Therefore, intramolecular dimerization of the complex is likely only possible if the ligand is sufficiently flexible and is not forcing the binding partners apart from each other.

Generation of the reduced and minimal iBody mutation variants by reverting three and four mutations, respectively, resulted in delayed STAT3 phosphorylation in the iGP11-gp130 synthetic cytokine receptors. EC50 values did not change significantly, nor did the maximal proliferation. Nevertheless, the ligand-independent level of pSTAT3 was reduced in the R- and M-variants, resulting in a non-autoproliferative phenotype in contrast to the fully mutant iGP11-gp130 in Ba/F3 cells. In gp130-WT expressing cells, heterodimerization of the nanobody-carrying fusion receptor and gp130 led to dimerization of the two intracellular gp130 domains and consequently to constitutive signaling toward STAT3. The GP11-gp130 receptor is therefore not suitable for cells expressing gp130, which unfortunately are almost all cells of the human body, including T cells.52 If the fusion receptors were expressed in cells lacking gp130, juxtacrine stimulation of gp130 of a neighboring cell would be unlikely, according to our data.

More applicable for the use in vivo is the AIP3-gp130 fusion receptor, which, after introducing the iBody mutations, could be activated by palivizumab. Palivizumab is approved as an inhibitor for the RSV F protein to prevent serious lower respiratory tract disease in children with higher risk of infection. The adverse effects comprise local erythema and pain at the site of injection, fever, and rash in about 1–3% of patients.53 Therefore, the mAb has a favorable safety profile and could be used as a ligand for the synthetic receptors in, for example, CAR-T cells. Furthermore, the intracellular domain could be exchanged, as has already been done with the Fas receptor.51 In addition, other anti-idiotypic nanobodies against palivizumab are known, which could act as a competing “emergency stop” signal.

The reduced and minimal iAIP-gp130 variants showed a delayed STAT3 phosphorylation as well and were able to reduce signaling in terms of proliferation. Our data support the predicted AlphaFold model and provide a new option for customizing framework mutations, potentially for all nanobodies. Thus, chimeric nanobody-receptor combinations, which are in need of pre-clustering or clustering events during signaling, as well as systems that require closer proximity of interaction partners, are good candidates for modification with the iBody mutations. This could be, for example, the tetrameric interferon gamma signaling, which needs the pre-clustering of the subunits prior to stimulation,54,55 the optimization of nanobody-based CARs,56 or for nanobody-based ligands.

Limitations of this study

Anti-gp130 nanobody-based constructs serve as a valuable model for studying receptor engineering and tunable signaling. They are not optimal for translational applications such as CAR-T cell therapies due to the ubiquitous expression of gp130 in almost all cell types. Anti-idiotypic nanobodies against palivizumab could be more applicable for synthetic receptors in vivo, for example in CAR-T cells, but need to be evaluated further in future studies.

Materials and methods

Cells, proteins and reagents

Ba/F3 (ACC 300) and HEK293T (ACC 635) cells were sourced from the Leibnitz Institute DSMZ-German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Culture (Braunschweig, Germany). Phoenix-Eco cells were obtained from Ursula Klingmüller (DKFZ, Heidelberg, Germany). ExpiCHOTM cells were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (#A29127, Waltham, USA). Ba/F3, HEK293T, and Phoenix-Eco cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) high glucose (GIBCO, Life Technologies, Darmstadt, Germany), supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS) (GIBCO, Life Technologies, Darmstadt, Germany) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (60 mg/l penicillin and 100 mg/l streptomycin) (Genaxxon Bioscience GmbH, Ulm, Germany). The growth medium for Ba/F3 or Ba/F3-gp130 cells was further supplemented with 0.2% conditioned medium from IL-3-secreting WEHI-3B murine myelomonocytic leukemic cells (ACC 26, DSMZ) called WEHI, or from a stable CHO-K1 cell clone secreting Hyper-IL-6 (stock solution approximately 10 µg/ml, as determined by ELISA) called Hy. All cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. ExpiCHOTM and cells were cultured in ExpiCHOTM expression medium in an orbital shaker (135 rpm) at 37°C with 8% CO2.

GP11-Fc and sgp130-Fc were expressed in ExpiCHOTM cells and purified via affinity chromatography. The JAK inhibitor P6 was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (cat #420097). Palivizumab (Synagis®, AstraZeneca) was kindly provided by AG Temme of the Department of Anesthesiology (UKD).

Primary antibodies in this study include: STAT3 ((79D7), #4904), phospho-STAT3Tyr705 ((D3A7), #9145) from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, USA), STAT3 (#560391) and (p)STAT3Tyr705 (#557815) (1:200) (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Secondary antibodies include: UNICORN™ 800 CW anti-Rabbit IgG (#926–32213) and NAP™ 680 RD anti-Mouse IgG (#926–68070) from LI-COR (Lincoln, USA) and the R-Phycoerythrin (PE) labeled anti-human-Fc antibody (#109–115–098, Jackson ImmunoResearch, Baltimore).

Cloning

The receptors were purchased (BioCat, Heidelberg, Germany) or cloned in pcDNA3.1(+) vectors. For retroviral transduction, inserts were transferred to pMOWS-puro vectors.

Transfection and protein expression in Expi293TM or ExpiCHOTM

Protein expression in ExpiCHOTM cells was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol using the Expression System Kit (#A14635, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). Briefly, cells were split to achieve exponential growth phase. Transfection was carried out using ExpiFectamineTM and OptiPRO medium, with the gene of interest cloned into a pcDNA3.1 vector. For secreted proteins, the vector included an N-terminal signal peptide to facilitate secretion. Twenty hours post-transfection, Enhancer and Feed reagents were added to the culture to enhance protein production. The cells were maintained on an orbital shaker for up to 10 d before harvesting the supernatant.

Protein purification

Protein purification of Fc-tagged proteins was performed using ProteinA MabSelectTM HiTrapTM columns (GE Healthcare, Chalfont St Giles, UK) and the ÄKTA Start system with UNICORN™ start 1.3 software (Cytiva, Marlborough, USA). The cell culture supernatant from ExpiCHOTM cells was centrifuged twice and filtered through a 0.45 µm pore size membrane. Both the ÄKTA Start system and the ProteinA column were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) before loading the filtered supernatant. All steps were conducted at a flow rate of 1 ml/min. After loading, the column was washed with PBS followed by citrate buffer at pH 5. Fc-tagged proteins were eluted using citrate buffer at pH 3.5. The eluate was immediately neutralized by adding Tris-buffer at pH 9, adjusting the pH to between 6.5 and 7.5. Subsequently, NAP™-25 columns (GE Healthcare, Chalfont St Giles, UK) were used to exchange the buffer to PBS (pH 7.5). Samples were concentrated using Amicon filters with an appropriate molecular weight cutoff (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and protein concentration was measured using the NanoDrop 2000c (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) at 280 nm, accounting for the corresponding extinction coefficient. Protein purity was assessed via SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis followed by Coomassie staining. Gels were documented using the LI-COR Odyssey® XF Imaging System (Lincoln, USA) and Image Studio software version 5.2.

Retroviral transduction of Ba/F3 cells

Gene transfer via retroviral transduction was performed using the virus-producing Phoenix Eco cell line (Ursula Klingmüller, DKFZ, Heidelberg, Germany) and the target Ba/F3 cell line. Phoenix cells were seeded and transfected with pMOWS plasmids containing the gene of interest along with puromycin or hygromycin B resistance genes, following the manufacturer’s protocol for TurboFect™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA, cat. #R0532). Six hours post-transfection, the medium was replaced with DMEM supplemented with 30% FCS and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (5000 U/mL) (P/S). After 24 h, the supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.45 µm pore size filter (Carl Roth, Karlsruhe, Germany). A total of 1 × 105 Ba/F3 cells were added to 250 µl of the virus-containing supernatant in a final volume of 300 µl, supplemented with 8 µg/ml polybrene (#TR-1003-G, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and centrifuged for 3 h at 1800 rpm at room temperature. After centrifugation, the cells were resuspended in 5 ml of growth medium and cultured under standard conditions. Puromycin (#0240.1, Carl Roth, Karlsruhe, Germany) selection began 48 h post-infection at a concentration of 1.5 µg/ml. pMOWS-GFP vector containing puromycin resistance was used as positive control for transduction and selection. The pEGFP vector served as a positive control for Phoenix Eco cell transfection and a negative control for the selection of transduced Ba/F3 cells.

Proliferation assay

Ba/F3 cell lines were washed three times with PBS to remove residual cytokines. The cells were then resuspended in DMEM supplemented with 10% FCS and 1% P/S. A total of 5,000 cells per well were seeded into a 96-well plate and incubated for 72 h with the ligand sgp130-Fc or palivizumab in varying concentrations, in a total volume of 100 µl. As a positive control, Ba/F3 cells were stimulated with 2 µl/ml WEHI, an IL-3-conditioned cell supernatant from WEHI-3B murine myelomonocytic leukemic cells. Ba/F3-gp130 cells were stimulated with 2 µl/ml cell supernatant from a stable CHO-K1 cell clone secreting Hyper-IL-6 (stock solution approximately 10 µg/ml, as determined by ELISA) called Hy. Proliferation was assessed by adding 20 µl of CellTiter-Blue® Reagent (Promega, Karlsruhe, Germany, cat. #G808A) to each well. Fluorescence was measured using the Tecan Infinite M200 PRO Reader (Tecan, Crailsheim, Germany) with excitation at 560 nm and emission at 590 nm, every 20 min for 2 h. The background signal at time zero was subtracted from the final fluorescence intensity. Data are presented as relative proliferation, normalized to the positive control and negative control as indicated. Error bars represent the standard deviation.

Transactivation assay

Ba/F3 cell lines and green fluorescent Ba/F3-gp130-puro-GFP cells were washed three times with PBS to remove residual cytokines. The cells were then resuspended in DMEM supplemented with 10% FCS and 1% P/S. For the negative controls, a total of 5,000 cells per well were seeded. For the transactivation 5,000 cells of Ba/F3-gp130-puro-GFP and 5,000 cells of the respective Ba/F3 cell lines, the nanobody-receptor variant was seeded into a 96-well plate and incubated for 72 h without additional cytokines, in a total volume of 200 µl. Cell proliferation was assessed via flow cytometry using a FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). To each well 1 µl of unlabeled calibrate Beads (BD CaliBRITEM beads) was added acting as an internal standard. The measurement was stopped after 2,000 beads. The data was further analyzed with FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, USA). The cells were analyzed by gating the green fluorescent- (Ba/F3-gp130-puro-GFP) and not fluorescent (Ba/F3 cell lines) cells in an FSC-AlexaFluor 488 dot plot. The resulting cell counts for each cell line of the transactivation were put in relation to the cell counts of the respective negative controls. Data are presented as relative proliferation, normalized to the negative controls. Error bars represent the standard deviation.

Stimulation assays

For the stimulation assay, Ba/F3 cell lines were washed three times with PBS and starved for 3 h in DMEM+/+. The cells were then stimulated with sgp130-Fc or palivizumab as indicated. Cell supernatant containing Hyper-IL-6 (Hy) was used as a positive control on Ba/F3-gp130 cells, while unstimulated cells served as the negative control for each experiment. Following stimulation, the cells were washed with PBS and lysed in 50 µl of lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.8, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40, 1 mM sodium vanadate, 10 mM MgCl₂, and a complete, EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail tablet (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany)). The lysates were incubated at 4°C for 10 min. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected for further analysis. To prepare Laemmli samples, cell lysates were mixed with 6x Laemmli buffer and boiled at 95°C for up to 10 min.

Western blotting

Equal amounts of total protein lysates were used for Western blotting as described earlier.57 Briefly, proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane using the Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA). Membranes were blocked with Intercept® (TBS) Blocking Buffer (LI-COR, Lincoln, USA) and incubated overnight with the primary antibody at a 1:1000 dilution. The following day, membranes were washed three times with TBS-T (TBS +0.2% Tween-20) and incubated with the secondary antibody at a 1:10,000 dilution for 1 h at room temperature. Blots were scanned using the LI-COR Odyssey® XF Imaging System (Lincoln, USA) and analyzed with Image Studio software version 5.2.

Intracellular STAT3/pSTAT3 staining

To measure the phosphorylation state of STAT3 using flow cytometry, intracellular staining was conducted following stimulation. First, 1.3x105 cells were plated in a V-bottom plate, centrifuged for 5 min at 300 × g and 4°C, and all centrifugation steps were carried out similarly. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were fixed in a 2% paraformaldehyde solution for 10 min at 37°C. After centrifugation, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were permeabilized using 90% ice-cold methanol, incubating for 10 min at −20°C. The cells were then washed twice with wash buffer (1% bovine serum albumin (BSA), 0.5 mM EDTA in PBS) and incubated overnight at 4°C in 50 µl of buffer containing anti-pSTAT3Y705-Alexa647 (#557815) (1:200) (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and anti-STAT3-PE (#560391) (1:50) (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). On the following day, the cells were washed twice and resuspended in 200 µl of wash buffer. Fluorescence was measured using a FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and the data were analyzed with FACS DIVA (BD Biosciences) and FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, USA).

Immunofluorescence-based binding assay

The expression of nanobody-fusion receptors on Ba/F3 cells was assessed using an immunofluorescence-based assay. Ba/F3 cells were transduced with retroviruses as previously described. A total of 5x105 cells per well were plated in a V-bottom 96-well plate and washed with 200 µl PBS by centrifuging the plate at 1200 rpm for 5 min at room temperature. All washing steps were performed identically. The cell pellet was resuspended in 100 µl PBS + 1% BSA, mixed with 200 ng of sp130-Fc or palivizumab, and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. After the first incubation, the cells were centrifuged and washed once with PBS. The cells were then resuspended in 50 µl PBS + 1% BSA containing the secondary antibody anti-human-Fc (#109–115–098, Jackson ImmunoResearch, Baltimore) at a 1:100 dilution. The plate was incubated for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. Washing was performed as described earlier, and the cells were resuspended in 200 µl PBS + 1% BSA for flow cytometry analysis using a FACS Canto II with FACSDiva software (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, USA) in the PE channel. The total cell number analyzed was 10,000 cells. Data are presented as the mean fluorescence in the PE channel, analyzed using FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, USA).

AlphaFold-based modeling

AlphaFold2 modeling was performed with ColabFold version 1.5.5.58–61 Multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) were generated by MMseq262,63 UniRef100 databases.64 The final model was relaxed by the AMBER force field.65 For the prediction of correct dimerization, VHH:VHH complex was modeled, and three iterations with five models each were analyzed. Initial predictions of binding energies were conducted using PRODIGY,66,67 while the dissociation constant (Kd) was calculated for a temperature of 25°C. Therefore, the top-ranked structure of the dimeric VHH:VHH complex was selected based on residue confidence scores (pLDDT) and error plots (PAE) based on structure prediction by AlphaFold 3.68 Detailed analysis of the VHH:VHH interface (iGP11, iGP11-R and iGP11-M) was based on 1,000 unique AlphaFold v3.0.1 models (10 samples, 20 recycles). The resulting complexes were ranked according to their respective ipTM and pTM scores, and 50 models with the best complex-confidence were characterized further. Finally, utilizing Predicted Aligned Error (PAE) plots, four top-ranking VHH:VHH models were selected for each complex. Analogously to the preliminary analysis, binding energies and the dissociation constant at 25°C was predicted by PRODIGY. Mapping of amino acid residues participating in the non-covalent VHH:VHH interface was accomplished by using DIMPLOT69,70 and alanine scanning interface analysis within DDMut-PPI.37 Additionally, the influence of iBody mutations on single-residue affinity was evaluated using DDMut-PPI.

Statistical analysis

IC₅₀ values in the proliferation assays were determined using nonlinear regression with a variable slope model in GraphPad Prism 8.0 (version 8.0.2 for Windows; GraphPad Software, www.graphpad.com) with a dataset of three technical replicates. Results are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three biological replicates. For direct comparisons, unpaired two-tailed t-tests were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001).
