Volume Rendering–Based Phrenic Nerve/Pericardiophrenic Bundle Identification Using Cardiac Computed Tomography

Phrenic nerve injury during catheter ablation and diaphragmatic stimulation during cardiac resynchronization therapy remain significant clinical issues.1, 2, 3 Preprocedural identification of the phrenic nerve is useful in avoiding iatrogenic injury and has been achieved via electrocardiogram-gated contrast-enhanced computed tomography.4 The approach includes slice-by-slice detection of the pericardiophrenic bundle using axial images with the optional support of postprocessing volume rendering techniques, offering 20% to 51% and 74% to 87% visibility for the right and left phrenic nerves, respectively.5, 6, 7 However, preprocedural identification of the phrenic nerve is difficult to apply in clinical practice, presumably because it is time-consuming with substantial uncertainty and difficulty in precise structural tracing in each axial plane. With continuous advancements in computed tomographic scanners and software, volume rendering techniques can now detect subtle differences in density and visualize submillimeter voxel-size differences for fine structures such as the phrenic nerve, even if conventional slice-by-slice structural tracing is challenging with the naked eye. Although the fundamental principle of radiological reading is to observe the structures in axial images, visualization using volume rendering techniques has a distinct advantage for the detection and visualization of fine structures and their anatomical course. In this report, in addition to representative dissection images of the phrenic nerve (Figure 1), we demonstrate our modified approach for the identification of the phrenic nerve primarily based on volume rendering reconstruction without slice-by-slice tracing and compare it to a review of the traditional slice-by-slice tracing of axial images (Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure 5, Figure 6, Figure 7, Figure 8, Figure 9, Figure 10). We advocate for the use of the volume rendering approach, which offers fast and easy three-dimensional identification of the phrenic nerve. In addition, we distinguish between contrast-enhanced (Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure 5, Figure 6) and noncontrast (Figure 7, Figure 8, Figure 9, Figure 10) cardiac computed tomographic images and establish feasibility of phrenic nerve identification without a contrast material. All image reconstructions were performed using a commercially available workstation (Ziostation2, version 2.9.8.5; AMIN Co, Ltd; Ziosoft Inc). Lastly, for successful volume rendering to identify the phrenic nerve and pericardiophrenic bundle, high-quality prerendering computed tomographic data are essential. Motion artifacts from cardiac pulsation can be reduced using high-pitch fast acquisitions or electrocardiogram-gated scanning. Low-noise images can be achieved through radiation dose optimization and the use of iterative or deep learning–based reconstruction techniques.8

Anatomy of the Phrenic Nerve

This figure illustrates the anatomy of the phrenic nerves/pericardiophrenic bundle. The phrenic nerves (asterisks) descend anterior to the pulmonary hila (A). They are sandwiched between the pericardium and parietal pleura (B), thus running extrinsically to the heart and lungs. (C) Magnified image at the level of the white dotted line (A), showing the left pericardiophrenic bundle, as further revealed in a histologic section (D). In this case, the phrenic nerve, including the epineurium, exhibits a 1.0 mm × 1.4 mm thickness on the pericardium (D). It is accompanied by 2 pairs of pericardiophrenic arteries and veins, surrounded by loose fibroadipose tissue. This is the anatomical basis of why the phrenic nerves/pericardiophrenic bundle can be detected as linear vertical protrusions on the pericardium via electrocardiogram-gated computed tomography.

Volume Rendering–Based Phrenic Nerve Detection Using Arterial Phase Contrast-Enhanced Cardiac Computed Tomography Data Sets

Right (A) and left (B) lateral views exhibit volume-rendered images of the heart with multiple threshold settings. By simply changing the window levels, the pericardiophrenic bundle (asterisks) can be readily visualized on the pericardium at a certain window setting (C and D). The dotted arrows denote the interlobar fissures of the right lung. Electrocardiogram-gated thoracic computed tomography was performed using a commercially available 2 × 192-section dual-source computed tomographic scanner (SOMATOM Force; Siemens Healthineers) with the following parameters: a tube voltage of 70 kV, a section thickness of 0.60 mm, a field of view of 240 mm, and a matrix of 512 × 512. The images used for analysis were reconstructed at 79% of the R-R interval.

Axial Images Used for the Arterial Phase Volume-Rendered Images

Original axial images are reviewed after the successful volume rendering–based detection (Figure 2) of the right pericardiophrenic bundle (dotted circles). The images are aligned in cranio-caudal order from the upper-left to bottom-right panels. The window length and width were 360 HU and 1311 HU, respectively. The pericardiophrenic bundle can be observed as a subtle lateral protrusion and/or an iso-density area embedded within the mediastinal fat extrinsic to the pericardium in each section. When primarily referring to the original axial images, slice-by-slice tracing of the pericardiophrenic bundle is highly challenging due to spatial resolution limitations and continuity detection difficulty by visual inspection. The dotted arrow denotes the interlobar fissure of the right lung.

Magnified View of the Superior Mediastinum Reconstructed Using Noncontrast Cardiac Computed Tomography Data Sets

This image shows a magnified view of the superior mediastinum, where the left phrenic nerve enters the thoracic cavity using the same dataset as shown in Figures 7 and 8. The figure inset exhibits the same image before digital colorization. The left pericardiophrenic artery (red) originating from the left internal thoracic artery can be observed. The left phrenic nerve (yellow, asterisks) descends posterior to the left subclavian vein, anterior to the left subclavian artery, and anterolateral to the aortic arch. The left vagus nerve (yellow) and left recurrent laryngeal nerve can also be observed posterolateral to the left phrenic nerve, coursing posterior to the left pulmonary hilum. These details are extremely difficult to recognize and identify when solely referring to the original axial images, indicating the value and utility of the volume rendering technique and its complementary role in image analysis.

Other Mediastinal Structures Identified With Volume Rendering Reconstruction Using Noncontrast Cardiac Computed Tomography Data Sets

Volume-rendered images of the right thoracic cavity viewed from the right anterior oblique direction enhance feasibility and utility of volume rendering reconstruction for identifying other mediastinal structures, including the right intercostal veins (white asterisks) draining into the azygos vein, right intercostal arteries (red asterisks) originating from the descending aorta (A), azygos vein, thoracic sympathetic trunk, and ganglia, and even the greater splanchnic nerve (B) by adjusting the window settings (A, −70 HU<; B, −250 HU<). The same electrocardiogram-gated noncontrast computed tomographic datasets were used (Figure 7, Figure 8, Figure 9). Comparable real dissection image of the right thoracic cavity of a different donated body (C) is demonstrated to show the ability and precision of volume rendering–based visualization and identification of these thin but important anatomical structures. Numbers indicate the ribs.
