MCVAE-based multi-omic anomaly detection in Fragile X Syndrome
Authors: Wassila Khatir, Marco Lorenzi, Irene Balelli, Carole Gwizdek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFragile X Syndrome (FXS) is a neurodevelopmental disorder caused by mutations in the FMR1 gene, resulting in the loss of FMRP, an RNA-binding protein regulating translation of hundreds of mRNAs. The Fmr1 knock-out mouse models this deficiency and is used to study molecular perturbations in the FXS brain. Omics analysis shows that FMRP loss disrupts coordination between transcriptomic and translatomic layers. But limited sample availability and dataset heterogeneity hinder detection of subtle, coordinated multi-omic dysregulations. To address this, we trained a Multi-Channel Variational Autoencoder (MCVAE) on wild-type samples to learn a shared latent representation of transcriptomic and translatomic modalities via cross-modal reconstruction. Testing MCVAE on Fmr1-knock-out samples revealed deviations from wild-type as anomalies, uncovering known and novel perturbations. Compared to alternative methods, MCVAE shows stronger enrichment for FMRP mRNA targets and improved genotype discriminative power in post-hoc tests. Translatomic anomalies exhibited coordinated relationships with transcriptomic anomalies, as supported by publicly available databases exploration. Moreover, these anomalies mapped to validated FMRP regulators and neurodevelopmental pathways, establishing MCVAE as a framework to uncover coordinated molecular perturbations underlying the FXS pathophysiology and guide biomarker and therapeutic target identification.

Introduction

Biological systems can be represented as multi-level graphs linking distinct molecular layers collectively referred to as “the omics” [1]. These layers are connected through the central dogma of molecular biology (). DNA is composed of thousands of coding genes whose transcription produces messenger RNAs (mRNAs), which together constitute the transcriptome (Fig. 1 A ⓐ). mRNAs serve as templates for protein synthesis by interacting with the translational machinery, of which ribosomes are the central component. The population of ribosome-bound mRNAs collectively defines the translatome (Fig. 1 A ⓑ). Beyond this straightforward flow, some proteins perform specific molecular functions that add complexity to omic communication notably through feedback loops. (Fig. 1 A ⓒ). Hence, proteins such as FMRP regulate the translation of a pool of specific mRNAs, generating correlated patterns within the translatomic layer. Others, such as transcription factors (TFs) or cofactors, or epigenetic regulators, influence transcription, creating additional cross-omic covariances that overlay those induced by the direct transcribed gene mRNA ribosome-bound mRNA protein cascade. Lastly, protein-protein interactions participate in signaling pathways that modulate transcription, translation, and ultimately cellular phenotype.

Fragile X Syndrome (FXS) is a rare disease, described as the most common inherited form of intellectual disability and the first identified monogenic cause of Autism Spectrum Disorders. The disorder results from mutations in the FMR1 gene, generally leading to transcriptional silencing and the loss of the Fragile X Messenger Ribonucleoprotein (FMRP) [2].

FMRP is an RNA-binding protein capable of interacting with hundreds of mRNAs and regulating their translation [3, 4], thus potentially impacting many cellular pathways that are disrupted in the context of FXS. Hence, numerous omics datasets have been generated to characterize dysregulations across different omic layers in the disease. Markedly, loss of FMRP disrupts intra- and inter-omic interactions, causing widespread dysregulation that extends from the transcriptomic () and translatomic () layers to the proteome, therefore perturbing major signaling pathways and processes involved in brain development, homeostasis, and function. These disruptions contribute to impaired neurogenesis, glial cell dysfunction, abnormal connectivity, and altered neuronal plasticity, underlying core neurodevelopmental symptoms, including intellectual disability, language and communication delays, autistic traits, behavioral and emotional dysregulation, and sensory hypersensitivity [5, 6]. Despite this extensive knowledge, no specific therapy is available to treat the disease. This partly arises because many mechanisms driving pathogenic alterations are still incompletely characterized. Targeting isolated components without a holistic view of dysregulated molecular interactions risks compensation by alternative pathways. Conversely, targeting hubs may be more effective but carries the risk of adverse effects. Fully leveraging omics data to uncover intra- and inter-omic structures is, therefore, essential to apprehend the altered mechanisms underlying FXS pathophysiology and to identify hubs where therapeutic intervention could yield maximal impact. However, such approaches face analytical challenges, including high dimensionality, data sparsity, and complex dependencies [1], limiting the mechanistic insights.

Dimensionality reduction (DR) methods provide a powerful approach to capture structure within omic data. Since FXS is characterized by broad but subtle transcriptomic and translatomic perturbations, DR offers a framework to disentangle these complex patterns and reveal molecular signatures associated with the pathology. Among DR techniques, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) identifies linear combinations of features and has been adapted for multi-omic integration. Statistical approaches, such as Partial Least Squares (PLS) and Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA), explicitly define a shared multi-omic space by optimizing joint projections across modalities. Non-linear methods employ autoencoders (AEs) [7] or, more frequently, variational autoencoders (VAEs) [8], which are neural network models designed to capture complex, non-linear relationships in a low-dimensional latent space. These architectures are particularly well-suited for high-dimensional molecular datasets with intricate geometric structures. Examples of such frameworks include OUTRIDER and OntoVAE [9, 10].

Multi-modal VAEs can integrate data using either vertical or diagonal strategies. The vertical approach uses paired samples from the same biological entities, and is generally considered optimal [11]. In our study, we adopted this strategy. There are several ways to implement vertical integration. Some methods, such as MOSA and OmiEmbed [12, 13], employ a simpler strategy by concatenating modalities prior to encoding them into a unified latent space. While this approach can achieve good reconstruction, it may disproportionately emphasize modality-specific signals and obscure cross-omic patterns shared across datasets [14]. DeepIMV [15] addresses this limitation by employing a Product-of-Experts (PoE) framework that aggregates information across modalities. PoE captures common signals efficiently but has a well-documented drawback: the latent distributions can become overly confident due to the “precision miscalibration of experts” [16]. In practice, a noisy or high-variance modality may disproportionately influence the joint latent space, reducing representational flexibility. The Multi-Channel Variational Autoencoder (MCVAE) [17] uses separate encoders for each omic to learn both intra- and inter-omic dependencies within a unified latent space via cross-reconstruction. This architecture enables robust multi-omic integration while avoiding the overconfidence and dominance issues inherent to PoE-based models.

DR methods are classified as unsupervised or supervised. Unsupervised approaches, such as those listed above, identify intrinsic structures without relying on class labels, which may cause them to overlook subtle biological structures associated with specific groups. In contrast, supervised methods, such as Data Integration Analysis for Biomarker discovery using Latent cOmponents (DIABLO) [18], leverage labels to highlight differences between classes of interest. However, these models are prone to overfitting, especially when sample sizes are small or classes are imbalanced, a common issue in rare disease studies where pathological samples are underrepresented in public databases.

This overfitting has contributed to a reproducibility crisis in which many reported biomarkers fail to replicate across cohorts due to limited sample sizes, analytical variability, and substantial biological and technical heterogeneity [19]. Consequently, only a small fraction of proposed molecular markers demonstrate robust generalizability across independent datasets, limiting their translational utility [20]. To address this limitation, within-omic cross-study integration approaches, also referred to as horizontal integration in multi-omics, have been proposed as a principled strategy to enhance robustness by prioritizing signals that are consistent across studies rather than study-specific effects [21]. Importantly, recent work suggests that embracing heterogeneity can improve generalization performance [22].

In this study, we use a MCVAE as the backbone of an anomaly detection framework to address the challenges of multi-omic integration in FXS, including complex intra- and inter-layer connections, limited sample sizes, and subtle biological signals. Anomaly detection provides an effective compromise by learning a baseline representation of physiological variability from heterogeneous wild-type datasets, thereby increasing statistical power and enabling identification of reproducible patterns. Deviations from this baseline are then identified as anomalies, highlighting disease-associated molecular perturbations.

A total of 158 paired transcriptomic () and translatomic () samples were curated and pre-processed, and a subset of samples was held out as an independent validation dataset. Using the remaining data, the MCVAE was trained on wild-type (WT) samples to learn physiological transcriptome-to-translatome mappings and was subsequently applied on Fmr1 knock-out (KO) data to capture features that deviate from these established physiological relationships. We evaluated the results obtained using MCVAE as a backbone and compared them to those obtained with the DR approaches described above. An enrichment score for mRNAs that physically interact with FMRP, calculated using published CLIP-seq data (crosslinking and immunoprecipitation followed by high-throughput sequencing), indicates that MCVAE detects more efficiently translatomic anomalies associated to direct FMRP mRNA targets compared to alternative approaches. In addition, the combination of and anomalies identified with this framework showed greater WT versus Fmr1-KO discriminative power on a validation dataset. These results supported the identification of multi-omic patterns relevant to FXS through MCVAE-based anomaly detection, and motivated further mechanistic investigation. We later examined the potential downstream consequences of the translatomic dysregulation of the direct FMRP targets. Several of these targets were annotated as transcriptional regulators, which raised the hypothesis that their altered function could contribute to secondary transcriptional perturbations. To test this hypothesis, we examined publicly available chromatin immunoprecipitation coupled to sequencing (ChIP-seq) data that map protein–DNA interactions. We found that anomalies annotated as transcriptional regulators physically interacted with nearly all anomalies. This result provided a plausible mechanistic link between primary translational defects and secondary transcriptional alterations. Functional annotation of detected anomalies further supported this view. Dysregulated direct targets and their downstream effects aligned with known features of the FXS phenotype. Through this integrative, stepwise analysis, we reconstructed a cascade of potential regulatory events that connect primary translational defects to secondary transcriptional consequences, revealing candidate cross-modal mechanisms underlying the molecular phenotype of FXS. By uncovering previously uncharacterized regulatory anomalies, our framework provides new molecular insight into FXS pathogenesis and establishes1 a generalizable strategy for dissecting disease mechanisms in complex disorders.

Materials and methods

Data collection and pre-processing

We conducted a systematic search in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database [23], and identified 35 bulk sequencing datasets, containing both transcriptomic () and translatomic () data derived from the same biological samples. Our focus on these two omic modalities is motivated by biological and technical considerations relevant to FXS. Biologically, the interplay between these layers is known to be disrupted by FMRP loss in FXS. Technically, transcriptomic data are commonly used to normalize translatomic measurements, making paired sampling a standard experimental design.

Our search identified ten GSE accessions from studies conducted in the Fmr1-KO FXS mouse model, comprising 26 WT and 23 Fmr1-KO samples. One of these studies (GSE127845) included two distinct sample categories corresponding to different brain regions, resulting in seven unique sample types. To increase dataset size, WT samples from 25 additional mouse brain studies were incorporated, yielding a final dataset of 23 Fmr1-KO and 107 WT samples. This dataset spans multiple brain regions, cell types, and developmental stages, which we subsequently grouped into three categories: embryonic, postnatal/early development (up to 6–8 weeks), and adult (Table 1).

To ensure consistency across datasets, gene-level expression was used. For studies that reported isoform-level quantification, we selected the isoform with the highest mean expression across all samples to represent each gene. Features with zero counts in all samples were excluded from the analysis.

To correct batch effects, we applied ComBat-seq [24], which requires integer count values as input. Therefore, for dataset GSE143330, counts were rounded prior to processing, as some feature counting tools may return non-integer values. Batches were defined as groups of samples available under a unique GEO accession, except for GSE141204, GSE141899, and GSE141979, which contained single-sample batches unsupported by ComBat-seq. As these three GEO accessions originated from the same experimental study, they were pooled as a single batch. Batch correction was performed using condition (WT versus Fmr1-KO) as the primary covariate of interest, to preserve the biological signal associated with genotype differences. Although age group and tissue type could act as biologically relevant covariates, they were excluded from the model due to their collinearity with the batch variable. Once batch effects were addressed, we applied a standard omics pre-processing pipeline, similar to those used in other multi-omics integration workflows [25]. Normalization using the Median of Ratios method was performed with the R package DESeq2 (version 4.4.2) to normalize for library size and sequencing depth. Rlog transformation from the same R package was applied to stabilize variance, especially for low-expression genes. Four GSE dataset from FXS studies were reserved for external validation. Finally, min–max normalization was applied to the remaining data to bring features to a common scale. This process resulted in one finalized dataset per omic modality, with samples paired across modalities for integrative analysis. The resulting transcriptomic () profile comprised 12 182 features, and the translatomic () profile comprised 4724 features (Fig. 2A, and Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Fig. S1).

MCVAE model training

We employ the MCVAE to learn a shared latent representation between transcriptomic () and translatomic () data, a low-dimensional summary capturing the main biological patterns across both layers. Separate encoder-decoder pairs, parameterized by and , are used for each modality. The encoders map and to modality-specific posteriors over a latent variable of dimension . The model optimizes a loss composed of two elements: (i) a reconstruction term, including both self-reconstruction (reconstructing a modality from its own posterior) and cross-reconstruction (reconstructing it from the other modality’s posterior), which aligns the modality-specific posteriors; and (ii) a Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence term between each posterior and a standard Gaussian prior. Together, these terms produce a shared latent space that captures complex cross-omic dependencies (Fig. 2B).

Each encoder and decoder consists of three sequential layers: two hidden layers that progressively reduce dimensionality by a factor of two at each step, followed by a final projection to the latent space of fixed size . The MCVAE enforces sparsity in the latent representation, allowing pruning of dimensions with high uncertainty. Dropout rates derived from the uncertainty of each latent feature are used to guide the selection of the effective latent space size (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Model architecture and hyperparameters were chosen to minimize the mean squared reconstruction error (MSE) on the validation dataset.

LeakyReLU activations were applied to all hidden layers. The model was trained using the Adam optimizer for up to 20 000 epochs with a learning rate of . A validation set was used for early stopping to prevent overfitting and to monitor generalization performance (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Anomaly detection

We implemented a Monte Carlo cross-validation protocol with 20 independent random train/validation/test splits to account for biological heterogeneity (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S4). In each split, the 107 WT samples were partitioned into 76 training, 8 validation, and 23 test samples. The test set size was chosen to match the 23 Fmr1-KO samples, ensuring balance (Fig. 2A), and at least one WT sample from each of the six FXS studies was included. We selected 20 splits for practical reasons, as MCVAE training is computationally intensive due to data volume. Training a single MCVAE model on an NVIDIA GTX 1080 Ti GPU (11 GB) required 48 min per split. In addition to the samples used for training, validation and testing, 14 WT samples and 14 Fmr1-KO samples were set aside to form an independent dataset for the external validation stages (Fig. 2A).

Each resulting MCVAE model was therefore shaped by different subsets of WT samples, capturing slightly different facets of what constitutes physiological - coupling. By applying this approach, we were able to identify molecular anomalies in Fmr1-KO samples that consistently emerged across age and brain region variation.

After training, the model was used to compute the reconstruction error, defined as the absolute difference between the observed test data and the data reconstructed by the MCVAE. This error reflects deviations from the physiological – relationships captured during training. Reconstruction error was computed for independant held-out Fmr1-KO samples, with the reconstruction error of WT samples serving as the baseline. For each molecular feature in each sample, a single absolute error value was obtained. Aggregating these values across all samples yielded two distributions per feature: one for WT samples and one for Fmr1-KO samples. Statistical differences between these distributions were assessed using the generalized Wilcoxon test (Brunner–Munzel test). Within each modality, this procedure was applied independently to each omic feature. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini–Yekutieli (BY) method via the statsmodels Python package (version 3.11.11). Features exhibiting significantly elevated reconstruction error in Fmr1-KO samples relative to WT (adjusted ) in at least one partition were classified as anomalies (Fig. 2C).

To assess how often anomalies are detected by chance in the absence of a true signal, we implemented a null model in which WT and Fmr1-KO labels were randomly permuted 1000 times. For each permutation, reconstruction error was recomputed across the 20 splits using the pre-trained MCVAE models, following the same anomaly detection workflow.

Benchmarking

To establish the advantage of the MCVAE model, we benchmarked the approach against multiple dimensionality reduction (DR) methods. Classical linear DR methods included principal component analysis (PCA), canonical correlation analysis (CCA), and partial least squares (PLS). For PCA, a scree plot confirmed that the first 70 components accounted for over 80% of the total variance (Supplementary Fig. S2A).

For the linear baseline models, we used the PCA, CCA, and PLS classes implemented in the scikit-learn library. Each model was trained on WT samples using the fit() method. Test samples were then projected into the learned latent space using the transform() method, which provides the encoded representation. Reconstruction in the original feature space was obtained using the inverse_transform() method, corresponding to the decoding step.

In addition, several deep generative models based on VAEs were evaluated (Fig. 4A). The deep generative models tested included standard single-modality VAEs, the unsupervised component of OmiEmbed (FcSepVaeAB class) for integrating two omic modalities [12], and DeepIMV [15], following the training protocol described in the original implementation. To ensure a fair comparison, all models were trained with identical architectural and training parameters as the MCVAE, including a latent space dimensionality .

CLIP database

FMRP directly binds specific mRNAs and acts as a translational regulator. Hence, to assess the biological relevance of the detected translatomic () anomalies, we investigated whether they were enriched for previously reported mRNA targets of FMRP. To evaluate this enrichment, we compiled a reference set of high-confidence FMRP-bound RNAs derived from multiple CLIP-seq (crosslinking and immunoprecipitation followed by high-throughput sequencing) experiments performed on mouse or human tissues or cells.

Mouse datasets included: (i) the stringent set from Darnell et al. 2011 [26], defined as RNAs detected with both tested antibodies in at least six of seven experiments and filtered at a false discovery rate (FDR) of 1%; (ii) Maurin et al., 2018 [27], which retained mRNAs with at least 50 counts in one or more tissues; and (iii) Hale et al., 2021 [28] and Sawicka et al., 2019 [29], which reported targets with CLIP scores > 1. For human data, we referred to the lists from Li et al., 2020 [30], presenting FMRP RNA targets filtered using an adjusted -value threshold of 0.05, and the previously reported list from Tran et al., 2019 [31], also used by Li et al. We defined two consensus target sets. The first was obtained by intersecting all mouse CLIP-seq experiments, and the second by crossing all human and mouse CLIP-seq datasets into a cross-species consensus list (Supplementary Table S2). For this purpose, human FMRP mRNA targets were mapped to their mouse orthologs using the HOM_MouseHumanSequence.rpt file from the Mouse Genome Informatics (MGI).

Enrichment score for consensus FMRP mRNA targets

Models performance was assessed based on the enrichment of consensus physical mRNA interactors of FMRP, derived from curated FMRP CLIP-seq datasets. Consensus targets were specifically used to ensure that the ground truth for benchmarking was highly reliable, comprising only the most robust, experimentally validated interactions. This metric was selected because the loss of FMRP directly perturbs the transcriptome–translatome coupling of its bound mRNAs, a well-documented and reproducible effect across species.

Data Integration Analysis for Biomarker discovery using Latent cOmponents (DIABLO)

DIABLO is a supervised multivariate framework for multi-omic dimensionality reduction that integrates signals across omic modalities to identify discriminative cross-omic patterns. Although DIABLO can perform feature selection directly, this supervised strategy is prone to overfitting in small or unbalanced datasets (cf. Introduction), such as those used here. Therefore, we employed DIABLO not as a biomarker discovery approach, but as a post hoc validation framework to assess whether sets of features identified by anomaly detection collectively encode a consistent and discriminative multi-omic signal.

Anomaly detection datasets cross-omic block correlation score

DIABLO was applied to the original dataset used for anomaly detection, comprising the categories “WT-only studies used for training” and “FXS studies used for model training and testing” (Table 1). The objective was to assess the ability of the selected anomalies to separate WT and Fmr1-KO samples. In DIABLO, each omic layer (here, and ) constitutes a block, and, in our analysis, each block is summarized into a single latent component capturing the main sources of variation across samples within that omic layer. The original datasets were filtered to retain only and features flagged as anomalies and were then submitted to DIABLO to project samples onto the first latent component of each block (component 1 for and component 1 for ). A cross-block correlation coefficient was computed between these two components to quantify the degree to which and anomalies co-vary across samples. A discriminative multi-omic signal requires not only that each omic layer separates WT from Fmr1-KO samples, but also that the two layers vary in a coordinated manner; which is directly captured by this cross-block correlation (Fig. 3C).

Leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) to compare benchmarked methods

For quantitative assessment in benchmarking, the independent validation dataset (Fig. 2A) was analyzed using LOOCV. For each method, the translatomic and transcriptomic anomalies it identified were used as input features to train DIABLO, using one latent component per block. At each LOOCV iteration, one sample from the independent dataset is held out; DIABLO was then trained on the remaining samples and used to classify the held-out sample as either WT or Fmr1-KO based on its WeightedPredict.class output. A correct classification contributes a score of 1 and an incorrect one a score of 0; the mean score across all held-out samples provided the mean LOOCV accuracy (Fig. 4C). This procedure enabled a rigorous, unbiased comparison of how different anomaly detection methods select features across modalities that jointly capture discriminative multi-omic signals. Models were also assessed for correlation between modality-specific latent components, LOOCV F1 score, LOOCV precision, and LOOCV recall (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Stratified and leave-one-study-out validation (LOSO)

To assess heterogeneity effects on multi-omic discriminative signal detection, anomaly detection (Fig. 2C), followed by DIABLO-based evaluation, was repeated under two perturbation schemes (Supplementary Fig. S8).

In the stratified setting, leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) was applied to the independent dataset using anomalies identified by MCVAE on test datasets grouped by category (tissue, technique, and developmental stage) to assess whether within-group heterogeneity impacted the detection of discriminative signals. In the leave-one-study-out (LOSO) setting, LOOCV was repeated while excluding during the testing phase all samples from one GSE dataset at a time, allowing evaluation of whether study-specific effects and batch structure influenced the identification of discriminative signals.

Single anomaly weight in DIABLO-driven WT versus Fmr1-KO discrimination

Each feature contributes differently to its block latent component; this contribution is quantified by a loading weight, where a higher absolute value indicates a stronger role of that feature in driving the separation between WT and Fmr1-KO samples. The distribution of absolute loading weights from the DIABLO model was examined to assess the individual contribution of each anomaly to the multi-omic discrimination (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA)

To evaluate the combined effect of and anomalies, in addition to the DIABLO visualization, the filtered anomalies were concatenated into a single feature matrix and subjected to Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA). PERMANOVA tests whether the centroids of predefined groups differ significantly in a multivariate space by comparing the variance between groups to the variance within groups using permutation testing provided by the permanova function from the skbio.stats.distance module of scikit-bio (version 0.7.0).

Annotation analysis of anomalies

To functionally characterize the set of translatomic () and transcriptomic () anomalies, GO annotation was performed using the DAVID functional analysis platform [32]. GO Direct terms were retained by setting both the Count and EASE score thresholds to 1 (i.e. no cutoff), allowing the collection of the full set of annotations. As many databases are better annotated for human genes, human orthologs were used for interrogations. For this purpose, translatomic () and transcriptomic () anomalies were mapped to their human orthologs using the HOM_MouseHumanSequence.rpt file from the Mouse Genome Informatics (MGI) (Supplementary Tables S3 and S7).

To dissect the regulatory architecture underlying the interplay between transcriptomic () and translatomic () anomalies according to the molecular FXS cascade, Molecular Function (MF) annotations for anomalies were first filtered for terms associated with transcriptional regulation, including transcription factors, transcriptional regulators, and histone modification-related activities (Supplementary Table S3).

Then, to validate the upstream regulatory links inferred from database annotations, ChIP-seq (Chromatin Immunoprecipitation coupled to sequencing) data were leveraged through ChIP-Atlas 3.0 [33]. This resource enabled the identification of genomic targets physically bound by transcription factors and epigenetic modifiers. Queries were performed against the mm10 mouse genome assembly. ChIP-seq interactions were extracted using peaks located within 5 kb of transcription start sites (Supplementary Table S4).

Finally, to evaluate the relevance of the identified multi-omic signatures for neurodevelopmental physiological and pathophysiological processes, translatomic anomalies were annotated using curated resources covering both molecular pathways and human disease associations (Figs 7 and 8). Pathway annotation was conducted using the DAVID functional analysis platform requiring the following pathway databases: KEGG Pathway (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) [34], Reactome Pathway Database [35], WikiPathways [36], BioCarta [37], and Enzyme Commission (EC) annotations [38]. To complement these analyses with disease-specific insights, both () and () anomalies annotations were performed using Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) [39], DisGeNET [40], UniProt Knowledgebase (UniProtKB) [41], and the Genetic Association Database (GAD) [42] (Supplementary Tables S3 and S7).

Results

MCVAE identifies a multi-omic anomaly signature separating WT and Fmr1-KO samples

Meta-analytic integration has been proposed by the biomedical community as a principled approach to identify an unbiased feature set from individual studies, which often yield large and sometimes contradictory results [22]. Such a meta-analysis has not yet been conducted for FXS. A major limitation for FXS using existing tools was the scarcity of publicly available data suitable for this analysis.

To address this issue, we set up a workflow based on MCVAE-driven anomaly detection and conducted a systematic search of the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database [23] to identify bulk sequencing datasets generated from wild-type (WT) and Fmr1-KO mouse models of FXS. This search yielded 35 datasets spanning six FXS-focused studies. Because deep learning approaches require substantial sample sizes for robust training, we further incorporated WT samples from 25 additional mouse brain studies. This resulted in a final dataset comprising 23 Fmr1-KO and 107 WT samples. Notably, the dataset spans multiple brain regions, cell types, and developmental stages (Table 1), thereby enabling the model to leverage shared signals across heterogeneous conditions. These studies include both transcriptomic () and translatomic () measurements derived from matched biological samples.

We first trained MCVAE on a subset of the omic data collected from WT mouse brain models, thereby capturing the covariance structure between transcriptomic and translatomic layers in physiological steady-state conditions, stabilized after cascading and feedback regulatory effects.

Therefore, we tested the model on a new set of 23 WT samples as well as the 23 samples of Fmr1-KO mouse model, to detect anomalies in the Fmr1-KO condition relative to the WT-derived baseline (Fig. 2). This process was repeated 20 times on different splits of WT samples for each independent run to account for biological heterogeneity in physiological – coupling. Anomalies detected in at least one run were considered in this study. We identified 27 anomalies (% of translatomic features) in the modality and 655 anomalies (% of transcriptomic features) in the modality (Fig. 3B), sharply narrowing down both feature sets (Supplementary Table S5). Substantially more anomalies were detected at the transcriptomic () layer than in the translatomic () layer, with anomalies being roughly ten times more frequent than anomalies when the difference is expressed as a percentage. This could be attributable to the larger number of features. The layer’s richer intra-modality structure likely enhances sensitivity to small deviations, whereas the smaller feature set reduces detection power, explaining the lower number of detected anomalies across splits.

Besides, we observed a limited overlap between and anomalies. As the input dataset does not cover the input dataset, the absence of a corresponding anomaly cannot be interpreted as evidence that the perturbation is not propagated to the translational level. Furthermore, many transcriptional deviations are likely buffered at the translation level, which is consistent with post-transcriptional regulation mitigating propagation of certain transcript-level perturbations in FXS [43].

Our objective was to determine whether the identified sets of and anomalies capture a coordinated multi-omic signature capable of discriminating between WT and Fmr1-KO conditions, thereby reflecting the cascading regulatory effect of FXS. To this end, we employed DIABLO, a supervised multi-omic dimensionality reduction framework designed to identify correlated structures across data modalities that jointly contribute to phenotype discrimination. Cross-omic block correlations in this context reflect coordinated variation between feature sets and are indicative of shared regulatory or functional dependencies. Such dependencies may arise, for example, when translatomic features correspond to regulatory factors whose downstream effects are captured at the transcriptomic level.

By explicitly maximizing inter-block correlations while enforcing discrimination between WT and Fmr1-KO samples, DIABLO enables the identification of latent components that capture coordinated dysregulation across omic layers. These properties make DIABLO particularly well suited for assessing whether the extracted anomaly lists reflect cross-modal regulatory programs impacted by the Fmr1-KO.

Accordingly, the original datasets were filtered to retain only and features previously identified as anomalies, and these reduced feature sets were used as input to the DIABLO model. For each omic block, a single latent component was learned by jointly optimizing pairwise correlations between blocks and classification performance between WT and Fmr1-KO samples.

The cross-omic block correlation reached 0.816, and WT and Fmr1-KO samples were clearly separated, indicating that the combined anomaly set captures correlated genotype-discriminative signals (Fig. 3 C).

Consistently, when all selected features from both modalities were concatenated, PERMANOVA analysis confirmed significant separation between WT and Fmr1-KO samples ().

Noteworthy, the ability of individual sample types to discriminate genotypes was correlated with their prevalence in the training set. Early developmental hippocampus and cortex FXS samples, which constituted the two major categories in the training data, were most distinctly separated from WT samples. In contrast, separation was less pronounced for GSE112502, corresponding to a minor category represented by adult neural stem cells (aNSCs). This observation underscores the direct relationship between the representativeness of the training dataset and the accuracy of genotype discrimination.

A substantial fraction of anomalies in both omic layers were observed in only a single split. In our heterogeneous dataset, many biologically relevant events are not uniformly represented across samples, even within the same biological category. In addition, variational autoencoder-based methods are sensitive to the composition of the training data, meaning that the set of features they prioritize can change depending on which samples are used for training, particularly in small-sample settings. We therefore adopt a strategy in which the composition of training samples is varied across splits, while preserving the biological heterogeneity (Supplementary Fig. S4). This means that each split provides a slightly different but still valid view of what constitutes the “normal” biological state. Accordingly, observing some anomalies in only a single split is expected. Statistical validation through 1000 random label permutations, each evaluated across 20 splits, demonstrated that no feature was ever detected as anomalous by chance, providing evidence that single-split anomalies reflect genuine biological signals rather than statistical artefacts.

Together, these results demonstrate that our approach effectively prunes features while retaining core biologically meaningful steady-state signals.

Benchmarking

By explicitly modeling information shared across molecular layers, a multi-omic approach prioritizes signals that are consistently supported across omics. Such shared signals are intrinsically more robust, as random noise is unlikely to manifest in a coordinated manner across independent layers. In theory, this strategy reduces the risk of detecting spurious anomalies, thereby improving the reliability of subtle signal detection. Additionally, in practice, such models can indirectly highlight network hubs.

These hubs, including transcriptional and translational regulators, are often central to disease mechanisms. Even modest perturbations affecting them can propagate through molecular networks and lead to substantial downstream effects. Such key actors may be overlooked by single-layer analyses, which primarily capture the largest sources of variance within individual modalities. The use of a multi-modal architecture is therefore grounded in these mechanistic considerations.

While the selection of MCVAE is supported by prior literature, given its suitability for high-dimensional omics data, its ability to capture shared structure, its capacity to model non-linear relationships inherent to biological systems, as well as its reconstruction-based formulation enabling anomaly detection, we performed benchmarking on our dataset to empirically confirm its relevance in practice.

The MCVAE method was benchmarked against other dimensionality reduction approaches, including linear models (PCA, PLS, and CCA), as well as non-linear methods, namely OmiEmbed [12], DeepIMV [15], and independent VAEs [8]. Single-modality VAEs were also included as a baseline to assess the contribution of multi-omic integration relative to VAE-based models lacking cross-modal coupling (Fig. 4A). For each other method, we obtained lists of and anomalies (Supplementary Table S5 and Supplementary Fig. S7), which we then evaluated for relevance to FXS using a two-step analysis strategy: first, assessing translatomic anomalies against known FMRP mRNA targets, and second, evaluating the discriminatory power between WT and Fmr1-KO samples of the combined transcriptomic and translatomic anomalies using DIABLO.

As FMRP regulates translation through direct binding to its target mRNAs (Fig. 1Aⓐ), we hypothesized that anomalies would show at least a trend toward enrichment for FMRP-bound transcripts. Using FMRP targets lists identified in crosslinking and immunoprecipitation experiments followed by high-throughput sequencing (CLIP-seq) therefore provides the most direct reference to assess relevance in FXS. For this purpose, we collected multiple lists from the literature. We derived two consensus CLIP-seq lists from widely cited studies in FXS research [26–31], ensuring that benchmarking relied on robust and consistently observed FMRP–mRNA interactions. One set comprised mRNAs shared across four mouse brain studies [26–29], and the other included targets common to both mouse brain and human neuronal model datasets [30, 31] (Supplementary Table S2; see Materials and methods). We then calculated enrichment scores for FMRP targets among the anomaly lists generated by each method.

Linear methods (PCA, PLS, and CCA) failed to detect anomalies, consistent with the well-known non-linear nature of biological regulation.

We next focused on non-linear approaches. Among these methods, the VAE identified 28 TL anomalies, while the MCVAE detected 27 anomalies. Interestingly, more than half of the anomalies in both lists corresponded to FMRP mRNA targets (19 and 15, respectively). Notably, unlike the VAE, the MCVAE showed a trend toward enrichment (, one-sided hypergeometric test) of FMRP targets in the mouse-only consensus set and the combined mouse–human dataset (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Table S6).

Focusing on CLIP-defined FMRP targets that display translational defects captures the earliest, direct consequences of Fmr1 dysregulation. But this analysis omits indirect effects mediated via regulatory hubs that can give rise to other anomalies (Fig. 1Aⓒ).

To evaluate these broader effects, we tested whether combined and anomalies could robustly discriminate between WT and Fmr1-KO samples in an independent validation dataset. For each method, anomalies were filtered and used as input to DIABLO.

A leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) scheme was applied, with each held-out sample classified using a model trained on all remaining samples. Accuracy per fold was scored as 1 for correct classification and 0 otherwise, and the mean across folds provided the final performance estimate. Importantly, this evaluation was performed on an independent dataset distinct from that used to identify the anomaly signatures, thereby directly assessing their transposability. MCVAE achieved the highest average accuracy (0.893), indicating that the anomaly-derived representation generalizes well to unseen data and effectively discriminates WT from Fmr1-KO samples (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Table S6). In contrast, single-modality VAEs exhibit inferior performance, likely because they fail to integrate cross-modal dependencies and therefore learn less transferable representations. Other performance metrics (F1 score, precision, recall, and cross-omic block correlation) showed that MCVAE consistently outperforms competing methods (Supplementary Fig. S5).

We next evaluated the individual participation of each anomaly in the genotype-discriminative multi-omic pattern using DIABLO loading weights. Both in the and the layer, mean feature weights were comparable across anomalies regardless of detection frequency, consistent with the interpretation that lower detection consistency reflects biological heterogeneity across samples rather than reduced signal quality. Taken together with permutation evidence from the “Results: MCVAE identifies a multi-omic anomaly signature separating WT and Fmr1-KO samples” section, these results suggest that the detected anomalies, including those observed in a single fold, likely represent true biological signals (Supplementary Fig. S6).

To further validate the meta-analytic integration strategy, we assessed how datasets heterogeneity impacts the anomalies detection performance. For this purpose, we performed on one hand leave-one-study-out (LOSO) cross-validation, where each GSE datatset was successively excluded during the testing phase, and on the other hand stratified analyses where testing datatsets were subgrouped across biologically defined categories. Discriminative performance was evaluated in each configuration using DIABLO on the same independent validation dataset. Across all configurations, the average classification accuracy was either not significantly different from or lower than that obtained using the full integrated dataset (Supplementary Fig. S8). These results indicate that aggregating multiple datasets does not negatively impact the identification of a transferable discriminative signal between WT and Fmr1-KO samples.

Together, the reproducible enrichment for FMRP targets across species, along with superior predictive accuracy on an independent dataset, confirm that MCVAE-detected anomalies are biologically meaningful and warrant further mechanistic investigation.

Additionally, we conducted an annotation analysis of the anomalies by tracing the pathological cascade of FXS, progressing from primary translational dysregulation of FMRP targets, through downstream propagation via regulatory hubs and signaling pathways, to neuronal and phenotypic associations (Fig. 5). We focused on anomalies because functional annotations, pathway databases, and phenotype associations are predominantly protein-centric, and translatomic measurements provide a more faithful proxy for protein-level effects than transcriptomic data.

Restricting the mechanistic analysis to anomalies therefore yields a framework that is biologically more relevant.

To determine whether anomalies nonetheless converge on biologically coherent processes, gene ontology annotation analysis was performed on anomalies that are not present in the dataset (Supplementary Table S7).

Translatomic anomalies align with direct consequences of FMRP loss

The pathological cascade of FXS is initiated by the loss of the RNA-binding protein FMRP, which directly regulates translation of a defined subset of mRNA targets (Fig. 5A). To determine whether MCVAE-detected translatomic anomalies reflect primary molecular perturbations, we examined their overlap with established FMRP targets using CLIP-seq datasets from mouse and human studies [26–31]. Of the 27 translatomic anomalies, 15 were identified as FMRP targets in at least one dataset. Eleven of these were found in at least one mouse dataset, with four (Pkp4, Stox2, Nedd4, Pum1) recovered in three or more mouse studies. Homologues of ten of these eleven targets were also detected in human central nervous system samples (Fig. 6), highlighting the conservation and functional relevance of these direct translational perturbations.

These results confirm that a substantial fraction of anomalies corresponds to direct FMRP targets, representing the initial molecular events in FXS. The remaining 12 anomalies lack evidence of direct FMRP binding, suggesting that additional, indirect regulatory mechanisms contribute to translatomic dysregulation (Fig. 5B).

Translatomic and transcriptomic anomalies align with regulatory hubs potentially underlying indirect FXS effects

To examine how anomalies propagate beyond direct FMRP targets, we investigated the roles of the 15 FMRP-bound anomalies and analyzed their potential to act as regulatory hubs (Fig. 5B). Gene Ontology annotation revealed DNA- or chromatin-binding, RNA-binding, and transcriptional regulatory activities for 8 anomalies besides FMRP (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Table S3): PKP4, STOX2, NEDD4, PUM1, STAG2, ZNF207, SMG5, and FIZ1. In addition, TDRD3 and ASCL1 anomalies, that are not direct targets of FMRP, were also annotated as DNA/chromatin binding proteins and transcription regulators (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Table S3).

A deeper investigation through bibliographic analysis indicates that these proteins cover different steps of mRNA biogenesis (a detailed description of their roles is provided in the Supplementary Results section). ASCL1 [44], STOX2 [45], STAG2 [46], ZNF207 [47–49], and TDRD3 [50] are direct actors of transcription or modulators of this process. ZNF207 also participates in the regulation of alternative splicing [51, 52] while PUM1 and SMG5 impact mRNA stability [53, 54]. Interestingly, TDRD3 and PUM1 also regulate translation [54, 55]. Among PUM1 targets, Stox2 and Shb mRNAs are both identified as translatomic anomalies (Fig. 7B).

Unlike Stox2, Shb is not an FMRP target, suggesting that its dysregulation in FXS could plausibly arise from altered PUM1-mediated regulation.

To identify the gene targets for anomalies associated with transcription, we screened mouse ChIP-seq (Chromatin Immunoprecipitation followed by Sequencing) databases that compile protein/DNA binding sites. This exploration should provide the transcriptomic () anomalies that would be expected if dysregulation of the anomalies we identified propagates to the transcriptome. We obtained genome-wide DNA binding sites for ASCL1 and STAG2 (Supplementary Table S4) and found that the Fmr1 gene is targeted by ASCL1, while Pkp4, Arc, and Ascl1 itself are targeted by both ASCL1 and STAG2. Strikingly, more than 99% of transcriptomic anomalies identified in this study overlapped with ChIP-derived targets of these two translatomic regulators, with ASCL1 accounting for the majority of these interactions (Fig. 7C). This overlap suggests that ASCL1 dysregulation could propagate feedback effects onto direct translatomic anomalies.

This observation may explain the strong cross-omic block correlation observed in DIABLO (Fig. 4C): when the translatomic block is enriched in transcriptional regulators and the transcriptomic block contains their downstream targets, the two blocks can exhibit high correlation despite sharing very few identical features (Fig. 7C).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MCVAE successfully captures a multimodal regulatory signature encompassing both direct and indirect components. CLIP-seq data exploration confirms that a substantial fraction of the translatomic anomalies correspond to established FMRP targets, while indirect effects mediated by hubs such as PUM1, STOX2, and ASCL1 could propagate perturbations across both the translatome and transcriptome.

Together, these direct and indirect components delineate a coherent regulatory signature, illustrating that MCVAE can integrate cross-modal signals to recover both primary targets and downstream regulatory cascades. This integrated approach highlights the added value of MCVAE anomaly detection for identifying biologically meaningful hubs and understanding the complex pathophysiology of FXS.

However, not all anomalies detected by MCVAE can be explained by the direct disruption of FMRP-mediated regulations, or by indirect cascade effects stemming from the dysregulation of hub proteins involved in mRNA biogenesis and controlled by FMRP. This gap in causality may result from the lack of available data on targets of certain hubs, such as the transcription factor STOX2, for which ChIP-seq data are unavailable. Nonetheless, links between anomalies may be explained by even more indirect paths, for example, through the alteration of signaling pathways that also modulate transcription and translation.

Signaling pathways linked to translatomic anomalies

Cell signaling refers to the mechanisms by which cells detect, interpret, and react to a variety of internal and external signals. A typical signaling cascade will be initiated when a signaling ligand binds to its cellular receptor, leading to the activation of downstream signaling pathways by modulating protein–protein interactions or enzymatic activities. This often ultimately culminates in the activation of transcription factors which then reprogram gene expression. In the central nervous system, signaling cascades enable neurons and glial cells to form, mature, and adjust in response to activity, notably by modulating synaptic transmission or by inducing long-term changes in gene expression. To explore additional mechanisms that could contribute to the MCVAE-detected translatomic and transcriptomic anomalies, we examined whether features mapped onto canonical signaling pathways. Such pathways can propagate regulatory effects across translational and transcriptional layers, providing a framework to contextualize anomalies whose mechanisms are not fully accounted for by direct FMRP targets or hub-mediated effects. Furthermore, as many of these signaling pathways have been extensively characterized in FXS, this analysis allows us to relate the coordinated and anomaly patterns to well-established disease-relevant processes.

Consistent with our hypothesis, multiple features map to the “Reactome Signal Transduction” term (R-HSA-162582), including ASCL1, ARC, PRKAR2A, NEDD4, STAG2, SHB, PKP4, TMOD3, and KLC2 (Supplementary Fig. S9 and Supplementary Table S3). Existing literature further associates PUM1 [56], FUNDC1 [57], VPS4B [58, 59], SNX6 [60], AAGAB [61], ANAPC10 [62–64], FIZ1 [65, 66], STOX2 [45], CLIC4 [67, 68], and ZNF207 [69–72] with various signaling processes. Altogether, 19 of the 27 anomalies are involved in cell signaling, as effectors, modulators, or targets (a detailed description of these roles is available in the Supplementary Results section and in Supplementary Fig. S9). Annotations further highlight two of the main mechanisms for transducing signals upon ligand binding to receptors: the G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) which, apart from ligand-gated ion channels, represent the large majority of neurotransmitter receptors, and the Receptor Tyrosine Kinases (RTKs), a large family of cell surface receptors that transmit signals from extracellular ligands, including growth factors, neurotrophins, hormones, and cytokines, to downstream effectors that modulate transcription or translation. Remarkably, many of the pathways associated with anomalies are implicated in neurotrophic and growth factor-mediated neurodevelopment and brain function (Supplementary Table S3). Interestingly, gene ontology annotation analysis for biological processes (GO BP) performed on anomalies that are not present in the dataset reveals that among the 30 GO BP terms with the highest number of matches, two groups stand out: one focused on protein modification, particularly through phosphorylation, and the other on protein localization, two processes that are important for neuronal physiology through signalling and compartmentalization. Similarly, the terms “neurogenesis” and “neuronal differentiation” also appear. Overall, these results indicate that anomalies also correspond to pathways important for neuronal development and function (Supplementary Table S7).

In summary, by integrating translatomic, transcriptomic, and signaling data, our analysis identifies ASCL1, NEDD4, ARC, PKP4, KLC2, TMOD3, and PRKAR2A as candidate hubs linking primary FMRP loss to downstream regulatory cascades (Supplementary Results and Supplementary Fig. S8). Together with the CLIP-seq and ChIP-seq analyses, these results demonstrate that MCVAE captures both direct molecular perturbations and broader signaling-mediated indirect effects, integrating cross-modal anomalies into a coherent, disease-relevant regulatory landscape and providing testable mechanistic hypotheses on FXS pathophysiology.

Involvement of translatomic anomalies in brain physiology, FXS, and other neurodevelopmental disorders

The molecular anomalies identified in the preceding analyses operate at two complementary levels following the FXS pathological cascade (Fig. 1): direct translational dysregulation of FMRP targets, and indirect effects propagated through regulatory hubs and signaling pathways. While direct effects were examined primarily at the molecular level, indirect effects were assessed across both molecular and pathway scales (Fig. 5). To extend this multi-layered perspective, we next evaluated whether these convergent perturbations manifest at a higher-order scale, examining associations between translatomic anomalies, brain physiology, and neurodevelopmental disorder phenotypes, thereby grounding the molecular signature of FXS in its broader neuropathological context.

Neurogenesis at the cellular level encompasses both proliferation and maturation, with the latter being closely tied to neuronal morphology. Dysregulated genes implicated in neuronal morphogenetic processes include the anomalies ARC, NEDD4 and PRKAR2A (Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table S3). Additionally, anomalies ZNF207, CLIC4, TMOD3, PKP4 (Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table S3), and HAUS2 [73, 74] participate in the actin or microtubule cytoskeleton organization and regulation, which underlies dendritic spine formation and axonal growth, the structural basis for synapse establishment. Together, these structural cascades converge on the structural support for neuronal circuit assembly.

Furthermore, anomalies SNX6 and ARC directly impact the regulation of membrane neurotransmitter receptor levels such as AMPA receptor (A detailed description of the above mentioned roles of anomalies is available in the Supplementary Results section), thereby influencing synaptic plasticity and long-term potentiation, are SNX6 and ARC (Supplementary Table S3).

Strikingly, at the pathophysiological level, six anomalies were directly connected to FMRP or FXS. ARC levels are decreased in the prefrontal cortex of Fmr1-KO mice [75]. In mouse embryonic stem cells, reduced expression of FMRP leads to increased Ascl1 mRNA levels and promotes faster neuronal differentiation [76]. ANAPC10 is a member of the anaphase-promoting complex (APC), a ubiquitin ligase that triggers FMRP degradation, a process required for proper synaptic plasticity and synapse pruning [77, 78]. The TDRD3 protein interacts with FMRP, and a pathogenic point mutation within FMRP prevents this binding [79]. In mouse neural stem cells and in mouse brain, the PUM1 protein interacts with FMRP, and both RNA-binding proteins share several thousand common mRNA targets [54, 80]. Lastly, in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs), FMRP associates with the Pkp4 mRNA and downregulates PKP4 protein expression. In the absence of FMRP, excess of PKP4 impairs actin cytoskeletal organization and proper neuronal architecture [81].

Several anomalies were also associated with signaling pathways altered in FXS or phenotypical traits overlapping those of FXS. Notably, dysregulation of the signaling modules ERK, AKT-mTOR, and cAMP/PKA has been reported in FXS, and 5 of the MCVAE-detected anomalies, i.e., ASCL1, ARC, STAG2, SHB, and PRKAR2A, converge on these pathways (for a detailed description of these connections, see the Supplementary Results).

Lastly, anomalies were also associated with other neurodevelopmental disorders or neurodegenerative diseases. Notably, both ARC and PRKAR2A are implicated in schizophrenia (Concept ID: C0036341). Additional genes are linked to intellectual disability, including STAG2, ASCL1, PUM1, and NSMCE2. STAG2 is associated with global developmental delay (Concept ID: C0557874), reflecting a broad clinical phenotype. In addition, it impacts sex-specific cognitive outcomes [82]. PUM1 contributes to neurodevelopmental disorders with motor abnormalities and seizures (620719) and is associated with epilepsy (KW-0887). Neurodegeneration-related genes include KLC2, which participates in Alzheimer’s disease (hsa05010), Parkinson’s disease (hsa05012), Huntington’s disease (hsa05016), and broader neurodegeneration pathways (hsa05022), as well as SHB, which is linked to Alzheimer’s disease.

In conclusion, the anomalies identified by our model define a continuum of neuronal processes, spanning early neurogenesis and differentiation, morphogenesis, cytoskeletal organization, synaptic plasticity, learning, cognitive function, intellectual disability, neurodegeneration, and psychiatric disorders (Fig. 8).

Together, our results demonstrate that MCVAE captures a coherent, multi-layered regulatory signature of FXS. By integrating direct translational perturbations, indirect effects propagated through regulatory hubs, downstream transcriptional dysregulation, and signaling-mediated modulation, our approach reconstructs a molecular cascade linking FMRP loss to neuronal and phenotypic consequences. This integrated framework highlights potential mechanistic mediators of FXS pathology, providing a foundation for targeted experimental follow-up (Fig. 9).

Discussion

FXS is a neurodevelopmental disorder caused by loss-of-function mutations in the FMR1 gene, resulting in the absence of FMRP, a pivotal RNA-binding protein that interacts with hundreds of mRNAs to regulate translation. Although transcriptional and translational perturbations in FXS have been previously described, integrating multi-omic datasets to capture the full regulatory landscape remains challenging. Here, we applied the MCVAE architecture to jointly analyze transcriptomic and translatomic data from FXS and wild-type (WT) samples, enabling the identification of consistent multi-omic anomaly signatures.

Traditional differential expression analyses often aggregate signals across studies, potentially masking biologically meaningful dysregulation when expression changes occur in opposite directions across datasets, leading to cancellation of the aggregated signal. In contrast, our anomaly detection framework, by capturing deviations from normative multi-omic relationships, is agnostic to directionality. It is thus particularly effective for detecting regulatory disruptions not reflected in mean expression differences, preserving biologically meaningful perturbations that would otherwise be obscured.

The MCVAE method relies on cross-modal integration to learn both intra- and inter-omic dependencies within a unified latent representation of transcriptomic and translatomic modalities under physiological conditions and to detect anomalies relative to this pattern in FXS. Such cross-reconstruction-based architectures, as demonstrated in Shi et al. [16], effectively capture inter-modal signals and improve robustness to noise in multi-omic datasets. This framework highlights genes in regulatory hubs mediating transcription–translation communication, while retaining the potential to capture modality-specific anomalies. Benchmarking on independent datasets using leave-one-out cross-validation confirmed that MCVAE-detected anomalies generalize across independent studies and experimental conditions, with superior performance over competing models.

Functional annotation of MCVAE-derived anomalies revealed multi-layered regulatory cascades affected by FMRP deficiency. Central neurological regulators, including ASCL1 and PUM1, emerged as key nodes, while additional candidates such as STOX2 were highlighted despite the absence of complementary ChIP-seq data. Identified actors in FXS pathophysiology, such as ARC, ASCL1, PUM1, and PKP4, validated the approach, while less-characterized genes relevant to FXS phenotypes, including NEDD4 and PRKAR2A, were also identified. PKP4, a high-confidence CLIP-validated FMRP target, displayed anomalies across both and layers. PKP4 contributes to cytoskeletal organization and dendritic morphogenesis through Rho GTPase signaling. Beyond direct FMRP targets, MCVAE detected indirect anomalies linked to dysregulation of regulatory hubs. Notably, PUM1 may functionally overlap with FMRP, as it also regulates translation and influences neurogenesis, with evidence suggesting contributions to pathologies overlapping FXS traits [83]. ASCL1 emerged as a central regulatory node in our hypothesized FXS network, potentially mediating anomaly propagation and aligning with observed transcriptomic disruptions. During neurodevelopment, NEDD4 is required for proper proliferation of neural progenitors, axon branching, synaptogenesis, and myelination by ubiquitinating and controlling the turnover of substrates such as growth factor receptors or cytoskeletal proteins. PRKAR2A, a regulatory subunit of PKA identified as a translatomic anomaly, is positioned at the intersection of multiple signaling routes, linking receptor activation to nuclear transcriptional outputs.

Concordance between and anomalies supports the biological relevance of the detected multi-omic patterns and aligns with mechanistic expectations of FMRP’s direct and indirect regulatory roles. Enrichment for experimentally validated FMRP targets across mouse and human CLIP-seq datasets demonstrates cross-species validity.

MCVAE detects deviations from the reference state; however, without information on whether regulatory interactions are activating or inhibitory, these signals cannot be directly mapped to causal biological regulatory mechanisms such as gene activation or repression. While anomalies suggest putative regulatory relationships, experimental validation is required. Integrating causal inference frameworks with multi-omic analyses, constrained by known regulatory interactions from ChIP-seq and CLIP-seq, will enable more accurate reconstruction of regulatory hierarchies and in silico perturbation experiments to identify candidate genes.

In future work, the analysis of anomaly signatures should be extended to finer levels of granularity, moving beyond robust cross-condition patterns toward stratified subgroup, regional, and developmental analyses, consistent with normative modeling approaches that enable multiscale interrogation of population heterogeneity [84].

Lastly, the present method holds the potential to be extended to additional omics layers, such as proteomics and phosphoproteomics, to further refine regulatory networks and capture feedback mechanisms, ultimately enabling systematic identification of functionally relevant perturbations in contexts where subtle, heterogeneous disruptions challenge conventional analyses. Moreover, this framework provides a generalizable strategy for integrating heterogeneous omics datasets in complex diseases beyond FXS, encompassing neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric disorders such as autism spectrum disorder and schizophrenia, as well as cancers with extensive multi-omic characterization, exemplified by The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cohorts. By focusing on deviations from normative multi-omic relationships rather than mean expression differences, MCVAE identifies coherent, biologically relevant regulatory nodes that inform targeted interventions.
