Genetic diversity assessment of edible-podded peas (Pisum sativum var. saccharatum and Pisum sativum var. macrocarpon) using morphological traits and SSR markers
Authors: Haritika Sharma, Rajinder Kumar Dhall, Hanish Sharma, Neha Verma, Saurabh Yadav, Priti Sharma, Neha Rana, Manpreet Kaur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEdible-podded peas viz., snow peas and sugar snap peas are valued for their tender edible pods, nutritional quality, export potential and increasing market demand. However, limited information is available regarding their genetic diversity and population structure. Therefore, the present study was undertaken to assess agro-morphological and molecular diversity among 25 edible-podded and two garden pea genotypes under field conditions during 2022–2024. Genetic diversity was evaluated using 27 phenotypic traits comprising 14 qualitative and 13 quantitative descriptors based on DUS guidelines, along with 40 SSR markers through Shannon Diversity Index, Mahalanobis D² statistics, principal component analysis, cluster analysis, AMOVA and STRUCTURE analysis.ResultsAmong qualitative traits, pod curvature showed highest variability (Shannon Diversity Index, H = 1.25) followed by seed colour (H = 1.21). Mahalanobis D² analysis grouped genotypes into 5 clusters, with highest inter-cluster distance (2061.31) among Cluster III (PED-18-1, PED-18-7, PED-21-6, Arka Sampoorna) and Cluster V (Him Palam Mithi Phali-2, Honey Snap, PED-21-2, Oregon Sugar Pod, PED-21-3, Royal Snow, Tarvedo Sugar), indicating considerable genetic divergence. Out of 40 SSR markers screened, 16 were polymorphic and generated 2–6 alleles per locus with moderate to high polymorphic information content of 0.50, while primer AA332 exhibited the highest PIC value (0.77). SSR-based clustering grouped the genotypes into two major clusters with distinct sub-clustering patterns. AMOVA revealed 91% of the genetic variation existed within populations and STRUCTURE analysis identified four subpopulations (K = 4), indicating moderate genetic heterogeneity among the genotypes.ConclusionsBased on the combined agro-morphological and SSR marker analyses, PED-18-1, PED-18-7, PED-21-6, Arka Sampoorna, and PED-21-2 emerged as the most distinct genotypes and represent valuable parental resources for edible-podded pea improvement. These genotypes can be utilized to enhance productivity, strengthen food and nutritional security and promote sustainabe breeding in pea growing regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09572-w.Keywords: Edible-podded peas, Genetic diversity, Morphological traits, SSR markers, Mahalanobis D² analysis, Population structure

Introduction

Garden pea (Pisum sativum L.), belonging to the Fabaceae family, is a vital cool-season vegetable crop cultivated worldwide for its tender green pods and seeds [1]. It is widely grown across temperate and subtropical regions owing to its nutritional value, short duration, and economic importance in vegetable production systems. Among the pea cultivars commonly grown by farmers, shelled types, viz., garden peas and field peas, are most prevalent. However, edible-podded types, specifically snow peas (P. sativum var. saccharatum) and sugar snap peas (P. sativum var. macrocarpon), are gaining popularity. These peas are consumed as whole immature pods because they lack a hard parchment layer and are valued for their sweetness, crispness, tenderness, and rich nutritional composition, including vitamins, dietary fiber, antioxidants, and essential minerals [2]. Snow peas have flat, thin-walled pods with underdeveloped seeds that seem like green beans in appearance, whereas sugar snap peas have thicker, fleshier pod walls containing sweeter or more developed seeds [3]. Being leguminous crops, edible-podded peas also contribute to soil fertility improvement through biological nitrogen fixation and support sustainable cropping systems. The edible nature of these pods is primarily governed by recessive genes, wherein the p gene eliminates the sclerenchymatous parchment layer on the inner pod wall, v reduces pod wall thickness, and n promotes the development of thick and succulent pod walls, especially in sugar snap types [2, 4]. These genetic factors not only influence pod morphology and edibility but also contribute significantly to quality traits, including texture, flavour, nutritional composition, and consumer acceptability. Despite their increasing commercial importance, edible-podded peas remain genetically less explored, with limited genetic variability and susceptibility to biotic and abiotic stresses restricting breeding progress and cultivar improvement.

The success of any crop improvement program largely depends on the availability of sufficient genetic variability within the breeding material, which serves as the foundation for the development of promising cultivars [5, 6]. Therefore, assessment of genetic diversity and inter-genotypic relationships among edible-podded pea genotypes is essential for the identification of diverse parental lines and their effective utilization in future breeding programmes. Traditionally, genetic diversity and varietal identification have been assessed using phenotypic evaluation of morphological traits. However, many of these traits are polygenic and strongly influenced by environmental factors, thereby limiting their reliability. Moreover, the extent of polymorphism in morphological characters, particularly within elite germplasm, is often insufficient for effective differentiation among genotypes [7]. Thus, relying solely on phenotypic markers for cultivar identification and genetic diversity assessment is constrained by low levels of polymorphism and significant environmental interference. Therefore, integrating molecular marker analysis with traditional morphological assessments offers a more robust and accurate approach. DNA-based molecular markers, which are stable and independent of environmental factors, provide a reliable tool for dissecting genetic relationships and evaluating the degree of similarity among genotypes. Several molecular markers, such as RAPD, AFLP, STM, and SSR, have been utilized for the genetic characterization of both cultivated and wild relatives of pea [8–10]. However, each of these has certain limitations, such as low reproducibility in RAPD, technical complexity in AFLP or limited availability in STM. SSR markers remain highly suitable for germplasm characterization and diversity analysis due to their co-dominant inheritance, multi-allelic nature, high reproducibility, locus specificity, informativeness, and cost-effectiveness, particularly in laboratories with limited access to advanced sequencing platforms [8, 11–14]. Multivariate statistical approaches such as Shannon diversity index, principal component analysis (PCA), Mahalanobis D² statistics, and cluster analysis are widely employed to assess phenotypic diversity and genetic divergence among garden pea genotypes [15, 16]. Similarly, molecular marker-based analyses including principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA), and STRUCTURE analysis provide reliable insights into genetic relationships, population structure, and genetic differentiation among pea germplasm accessions [15, 17]. Pea diversity panels comprised of garden pea cultivars have previously been characterized but very limited information is available on genotypic diversity of edible peas using molecular markers. Therefore, this study is focused on assessing genetic diversity among pea genotypes by combining qualitative and quantitative morphological traits with SSR marker analysis for harnessing available diversity in pea. The findings of the study will contribute to developing high-yielding and commercially viable edible podded pea varieties.

Materials and methods

Description of experimental site

The field experiment was conducted during the winter seasons of 2022–2023 and 2023–2024 at the Vegetable Research Farm, Department of Vegetable Science, Punjab Agricultural University, Ludhiana, Punjab, India (30◦53′57.7′′ N latitude,75◦47′37.7′′ E longitude and 247 m above mean sea level). The experimental site is characterized by a subtropical climate with cool winters suitable for pea cultivation. The soil of the experimental field was sandy loam in texture with a pH of 8.5. Meteorological data were included to describe the environmental conditions prevailing during the experimental period (both years), which may influence the expression of quantitative traits (Fig. 1).

Meteorological data for year 2022-23 and 2023-24 during experimental period

Genetic material, design and experimental procedure

A total of twenty-five edible podded pea genotypes along with two garden pea varieties collected from diverse ecological regions (Supplementary Table S1) were utilized for assessing genetic diversity and inter-genotypic relationships. The geographical distribution of the collected genotypes is presented in Fig. 2. The experiment was laid out in a Randomized Block Design (RBD) with three replications during both cropping seasons. The genotypes were randomly assigned to plots within each replication to minimize environmental variation and facilitate unbiased comparison among genotypes. Each genotype was sown in a single 3 m long row comprising 100 seeds on flat beds, maintaining a spacing of 45 cm between rows and 10 cm between plants. Standard agronomic and plant protection practices were followed throughout the crop growth period according to the Package of Practices for Vegetable Crops recommended by Punjab Agricultural University [18].

Geographical distribution of collection sites of edible-podded pea genotypes used in the study. Numbers in parentheses indicate the number of genotypes collected from each location

Data collection

Phenotypic data

Phenotypic evaluation of all genotypes was carried out for 14 qualitative and 13 quantitative traits which were selected based on their agronomic importance and distinctiveness according to UPOV/DUS guidelines for pea [19]. Morphological analyses were performed using pooled mean data across two growing seasons (2022–2023 and 2023–2024). Observations were recorded from the five randomly selected plants in the middle rows of each genotype in every replication. Qualitative traits were visually assessed at appropriate vegetative, flowering, and pod development stages as per the DUS guidelines, whereas quantitative traits were measured manually using standard agronomic procedures at crop maturity. Since qualitative traits are governed by a few major genes with limited environmental influence, while quantitative traits are continuous, polygenic and highly influenced by environmental factors, both were analyzed separately using appropriate statistical approaches for accurate assessment of phenotypic diversity and genetic relationships among the pea genotypes [15, 20]. Qualitative traits were assigned descriptor scores and subsequently transformed into a binary data matrix for diversity analysis. Quantitative observations including node at which first flower appeared, days to 50% flowering, days to first flower appearance, days to pod development, plant height (cm), number of branches, seeds per pod, pod breadth (cm), pod length (cm), pods per plant, pod weight (g), 100-seed weight (g) and pod yield per plant (g) were recorded. Mean values across replications were calculated and used for further statistical analysis.

Genetic data

The genotypes were grown under field conditions at the Vegetable Research Farm, Punjab Agricultural University, Ludhiana, following recommended agronomic practices. Genomic DNA was extracted from young healthy leaves collected from five randomly selected 30-day-old pea plants of each genotype, pooled as a composite sample, using a modified CTAB (Cetyl Trimethyl Ammonium Bromide) method described by Doyle and Doyle [21]. Fresh leaf samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground to a fine powder using a pre-chilled mortar and pestle to prevent DNase activity and facilitate efficient cell disruption. Approximately 800 µl of preheated CTAB extraction buffer (65 °C) was added to the powdered tissue in 2 ml microcentrifuge tubes, followed by incubation at 65 °C for 1 h with intermittent mixing. Subsequently, an equal volume of chloroform: isoamyl alcohol (24:1) was added, mixed gently and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at room temperature. The upper aqueous phase was carefully transferred to fresh 1.5 ml tubes and DNA was precipitated using 600 µl of chilled isopropanol followed by overnight incubation at − 20 °C. The DNA pellet obtained after centrifugation was washed with 200 µl of 70% ethanol, air-dried and dissolved in 50 µl of 1× TAE buffer. DNA quality was assessed through 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and concentration was quantified using a Nanodrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Purified DNA samples were stored at -20 °C until further use.

Selection of SSR primers

Forty SSR markers [14] were tested across 25 edible podded peas and 2 garden peas genotypes due to their consistent amplification and clear banding profiles. Primers that did not generate clear or polymorphic band patterns were excluded from further analysis. Consequently, a final set of 16 polymorphic SSR primers was selected for detailed study and scoring (Supplementary Table S2).

Amplification of DNA and gel electrophoresis

In vitro DNA amplification was performed using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) in a 96-well microtiter plate on an Eppendorf Master Cycler. The PCR mixture with a total volume of 20 µl consisted of 2 µl of template DNA and 18 µl of reaction components including 1.0 µl each of forward and reverse primers 10.2 µl of master mix and 5.8 µl of nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling conditions were set as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min followed by 38 cycles of denaturation, annealing at specific temperatures for each primers (Supplementary Table S2) for 30 s and extension at 72 °C for 5 min. A final extension was carried out at 72 °C for 5.5 min before storing the samples at 4 °C. The PCR products were then separated on a 2.5% agarose gel prepared with 0.5X TBE buffer containing 0.5 µg/µl ethidium bromide. Electrophoresis was conducted at 5 V/cm for 2 h and the DNA bands were visualized under UV light. Images were captured using a UVP gel documentation system (Model GDS 7600).

Assessment of phenotypic variation

Phenotypic data for 13 quantitative traits were analysed using Mahalanobis’ D² statistics [23] conducted in R Studio using the ‘cluster package’ to measure genetic divergence, and genotypes were then grouped using Tocher’s method [24, 25]. Mahalanobis D² statistics effectively quantify multivariate genetic divergence among genotypes. Additionally, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed in R Studio [25] using appropriate statistical functions, and graphical visualization was generated using the ggplot2 package based on the correlation matrix among genotypes. The eigenvalues and percentage variation explained by the principal components facilitates dimensional reduction and identification of major traits contributing to variation.

Molecular analysis

The amplified fragments were recorded as 1 (presence) or 0 (absence) based on their molecular size (base pairs). STRUCTURE analysis enables identification of genetically distinct subpopulations using Bayesian clustering. The genetic structure of 27 pea genotypes was analyzed using the Bayesian model-based program STRUCTURE (v. 2.3.3). The analysis was performed with the number of clusters (K) set from 1 to 10, applying an admixture model with 10,000 burn-in iterations followed by 10,000 Markov Chain Monte Carlo replicates. Ten independent runs were performed for each K value to facilitate consistency of population structure analysis. The optimal K value was determined using the Structure Harvester online tool, where the optimum number of genetic clusters was determined based on the highest ΔK value according to the method of Evanno et al. [28]. Additionally, genetic variation within and among populations was assessed through Analysis of Molecular Variance (AMOVA) and the Fixation Index (FST) were calculated using GenAlEx version 6.5. Population grouping for AMOVA was based on the four subpopulations (K = 4) identified through STRUCTURE analysis. AMOVA partitions genetic variation within and among populations. Morphological and molecular distance matrices were generated using Euclidean distance and Jaccard’s similarity coefficient, respectively. Cophenetic correlation coefficients (CCC) were estimated to assess the goodness of fit between the dendrograms and distance matrices. Mantel test analysis was performed using 999 permutations in RStudio [25] with the vegan package.

Results

Agro-morphological characterization

Qualitative characteristics are vital descriptors which significantly influence by customer choices, socioeconomic needs and natural selection. The Shannon Diversity Index (H) for the different genotypes ranging from 0 to 1.25 with average value of 0.53 which indicates different levels of diversity for 14 characteristics (Table 1). Stipule type showed no diversity (H = 0), being normal and uniform in all 27 genotypes (100%). In contrast, pod curvature exhibited the highest diversity (H = 1.25), with categories distributed as: absent in 4 genotypes (14.81%), weak in 12 genotypes (44.44%), medium in 8 genotypes (29.63%), and strong in 3 genotypes (11.11%). Seed colour also displayed high diversity (H = 1.21), with green in 14 genotypes (51.85%), yellow in 3 (11.11%), cream in 5 (18.52%), and brown in 5 (18.52%). Foliage colour (H = 0.94) showed moderate diversity, with green foliage observed in 16 genotypes (59.26%), light green in 7 genotypes (25.93%), and dark green in 4 genotypes (14.81%). Similarly, pod type (H = 0.87) exhibited moderate diversity, with snow pea type being the most frequent in 16 genotypes (59.26%), followed by snap pea type in 9 genotypes (33.33%) and garden pea type in 2 genotypes (7.41%). Leaflets were present in 26 genotypes (96.30%), showing limited variability (H = 0.16). Standard petal colour showed moderate diversity (H = 0.57): white in 22 genotypes (81.48%), pink in 4 (14.81%), and purple in 1 (3.70%). The absence of leaf axil colour was recorded in 22 genotypes (81.48%), leading to an H value of 0.48. Pod colour was mainly green in 24 genotypes (88.89%), yellow in 2 (7.41%) and purple pigmented in 1 (3.70%) (H = 0.42). Seed shape (H = 0.57) was wrinkled in 20 genotypes (74.07%) and round in 7 (25.93%). Pod shape of the distal segment showed an H of 0.48, with blunt tips in 22 genotypes (81.48%) and pointed in 5 (18.52%). Both stem anthocyanin colouration and leaf axil colour had H values of 0.48, with the absence of colour being more common (81.48%, 22 genotypes in each case). Overall, the genotypes were genetically stable for traits such as stipule type but showed high variability in traits like pod curvature and seed colour (Table 1). Further, the dendrogram based on DUS descriptors grouped 27 genotypes into two major clusters (Cluster A and Cluster B) at a euclidean distance of 40. Cluster A included 22 genotypes which were further subdivided into two sub-clusters: A1 containing 10 genotypes sharing traits such as absent stem anthocyanin coloration, green foliage, white flower colour, blunt pod shape of distal part, wrinkled seeds and snap pea pods and cluster A2, with 12 genotypes characterized by absent stem anthocyanin coloration, green foliage, white flower colour, round seeds, and snow pea pods. The remaining five genotypes fell under Cluster B which was further divided into sub-clusters B1 having 3 genotypes with common traits such as presence of stem anthocyanin coloration, dark green foliage colour, purple coloured flowers, brown seeds and snap pea type pod and cluster B2 comprised of 2 genotypes with traits like presence of stem anthocyanin coloration, light green coloured foliage, purple flowers, absence of any pod curvature, brown coloured seeds and snap pea pods (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S3).

Cluster II was the largest, comprising 9 genotypes, followed by clusters V (7 genotypes), III and IV (4 genotypes each), and cluster I with 3 genotypes (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table S4). Principal component analysis (PCA)

In this investigation, a total of 27 pea genotypes, including 25 edible-podded types and 2 non-edible podded types, were evaluated using 13 quantitative traits through Principal Component Analysis (PCA) based on two years of pooled data. The total variability was divided into 13 principal components (PCs). Among these, the first five components with eigenvalues greater than 1 were considered significant, collectively accounting for 79.32% of the total variance. PC1 contributed the most, explaining 30.05% of the variation, primarily due to pod weight, pod yield per plant and pod length. PC2 accounted for 16.14% of the variation, influenced mainly by days to 50% flowering, days to first flowering and the number of branches. The third principal component (12.48%) was shaped predominantly by the number of pods per plant and the node at which the first flower appeared. PC4, contributing 11.23%, was driven by 100-seed weight and the number of seeds per pod, while PC5 mainly explained variation in days to pod formation (Fig. 6(C) and Supplementary Table S6). The PCA biplot visually distinguished the genotypes based on their pod classification inside the PC space. The 95% confidence ellipses revealed considerable spatial overlap between the edible-podded types (snap pea and snow pea), indicating a high phenotypic similarity. Conversely, the non-edible garden pea genotypes (Genotypes 26 and 27) positioned distinctly away from the edible clusters along the negative axis of PC1 (Fig. 6(A)), validating the model’s classification power. Finally, the percentage contribution of each trait to the total phenotypic diversity among the genotypes (Fig. 7) revealed that the number of branches contributed the highest (32.6%), followed by plant height (24.2%) and 100-seed weight (14.6%).

Diversity analysis using SSR markers

Molecular markers serve as essential tools for evaluating genetic variability and indicating genetic relationships among crop genotypes. In this study, a total of 40 SSR primers were initially screened out of which 16 primers producing clear, scorable bands were selected for amplifying genomic DNA from 25 edible podded and 2 garden pea genotypes (Table 3). The number of alleles detected per locus ranged from two to six (Supplementary Fig. 1). The polymorphic information content (PIC) values across these 16 microsatellite loci varied between 0.335 (AA206) and 0.776 (AA332) with an average of 0.5007. Observed heterozygosity (Ho) ranged from 0.00 for markers AA332, AD146, AA242, AB114 and AB77 to a maximum of 0.407 (AB55). The expected heterozygosity (He) spanned from 0.434 (AA206) to 0.819 (AA332), averaging at 0.5927. Shannon’s information index ranged from 0.365 (AA332) to 0.642 (AA206).

The analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) revealed that most of the genetic variation (91%) existed within populations, whereas only 9% was attributed to differences among populations (Supplementary Table S8). Reflecting this distribution of variance, the fixation index (FST) value of 0.0652 indicated moderate genetic differentiation among the identified populations/groups of edible-podded pea genotypes. This high level of genetic partitioning within populations highlights considerable intra-population diversity and suggests a shared genetic background among the evaluated genotypes, while still maintaining distinct, moderately differentiated group identities. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) was employed to assess the relationships among genotypes by reducing multidimensional data into a few principal axes. PCoA clustered the 27 genotypes into four distinct populations. The first three axes collectively explained 37.80% of the total genetic variation, with PC1, PC2, and PC3 contributing 15.59%, 12.56%, and 9.65%, respectively (Fig. 9). Most of the diversity was captured by the first two axes, indicating moderate genetic differentiation among closely related genotypes.

Association and cluster validation analysis

Cophenetic correlation coefficient (CCC) values of 0.7869 for morphological traits and 0.7652 for SSR markers indicated a good fit between the respective dendrograms and the corresponding distance matrices, demonstrating the reliability of cluster formation and the effectiveness of both marker systems in assessing genetic relationships among the edible-podded pea genotypes. Further, Mantel test analysis revealed a significant low positive correlation between the morphological and molecular distance matrices with a Mantel correlation coefficient (r) of 0.233 and a significance value (p) of 0.028 based on 999 permutations, indicating partial correspondence between phenotypic variation and SSR-based genetic diversity among the studied genotypes.

Discussions

Genetic enhancement of crops through selective breeding relies heavily on a clear understanding of population structure and the extent of genetic variability present in the germplasm [29]. Studies on genetic diversity among various vegetable genotypes have been instrumental in identifying promising parental lines with favorable agronomic traits. A broader genetic base in local cultivars is crucial for breeding efforts, necessitating comprehensive characterization of genetic variation. In the present study, a total of 27 pea genotypes, including 25 edible podded and 2 garden pea varieties, were carefully selected based on their distinct agro-morphological traits, Eco-geographical origin, and genetic background, comprising both released varieties and advanced breeding lines. This strategic selection support representation of a wide genetic spectrum to support robust diversity analysis and meaningful interpretation of population structure. Morphological characterization has been utilized for decades to assess genetic diversity and genotypic relationships within garden pea [30]. However, an integrated strategy encompasses both molecular and traditional approaches provides a deeper insight into patterns of variation and supports the development of improved cultivars [31]. The assessment of population structure and genetic variation is significantly influenced by the population size and the type of molecular markers utilized. Among the molecular markers, Simple Sequence Repeat (SSR) markers are particularly effective for evaluating genetic relationships and diversity due to their high level of polymorphism, reproducibility, co-dominant inheritance, and widespread distribution across plant genomes [32, 33].

In this investigation, twenty-five podded edible and two garden pea genotypes from diverse regions were examined for morpho-molecular genetic diversity employing fourteen qualitative descriptors. These descriptors indicated a high degree of variation among the genotypes, with pod curvature showing the highest Shannon Diversity Index (1.25) and facilitated the clustering of genotypes into four groups. Morphological descriptors were effective in identifying and classifying genotypes that can be useful for hybridization programs and development of improved varieties. Comparable outcomes were obtained by Singh and coworkers [34], who classified 35 garden pea varieties into different groups for each trait based on 19 descriptors. Applying D2 analysis using 13 morphological characteristics classified the genotypes into five clusters in dendrogram as per Tocher’s approach. Similar clustering patterns have been previously documented in garden pea [15].

The largest inter-cluster distance was observed among Cluster III (with genotypes PED-18-1, PED-18-7, PED-21-6, and Arka Sampoorna) and Cluster V (comprising Him Palam Mithi Phali-2, Honey Snap, PED-21-2, Oregon Sugar Pod, PED-21-3, Royal Snow, and Tarvedo Sugar), pointing toward ample genetic variation among these two sets of groups. The high inter-cluster distance observed between clusters suggested the presence of appreciable genetic divergence, indicating the potential utility of these genotypes in hybridization programmes. Hybridization among genetically distant and agronomically better-performing genotypes may facilitate the development of desirable edible-podded pea cultivars possessing desirable traits such as higher pod yield, improved pod quality, earliness, and wider adaptability. Sharma et al. [35] and Sanwal et al. [20] have also previously suggested, choosing parents from different clusters for hybridization schemes are effective to obtain good transgressive segregants. On the other hand, the shortest inter-cluster distance in this study was observed between Clusters I and IV, indicating reduced genetic diversity.

PCA identified key traits governing genetic variation among edible podded genotypes. PCA revealed 13 principal components and the cumulative proportion of variance reaches approximately 79.33% by five principal component, explaining over half of the genetic variability present in the data. This highlights a broad genetic base essential for breeding edible podded peas for improved horticultural traits. Traits with higher loading values contributed notably toward overall genetic divergence and may serve as important selection criteria in breeding programmes. These findings corroborate the results of Devi et al. [36], indicating that the first four components accounts for the maximum estimated variation (74.88%). Thus, it emphasized the utility of PCA in selecting key traits for breeding programs aimed at enhancing productivity. Traits exhibiting high contribution toward total variability may be prioritized in selection programmes for the development of high-yielding edible-podded pea cultivars with improved horticultural performance. The identification of genetically divergent genotypes based on multivariate analyses also provides opportunities for selecting suitable parental combinations for future hybridization programmes. Differences in trait contribution observed between Mahalanobis D² analysis and PCA are expected because both methods estimate genetic variation using different statistical frameworks. Mahalanobis D² statistics assess divergence among clusters based on generalized distance, whereas PCA identifies traits contributing to overall multivariate variation across genotypes.

The Mahalanobis D² analysis revealed that number of branches, plant height, and 100-seed weight contributed maximally toward genetic divergence among the pea genotypes. The clustering pattern observed in the PCoA further supported the presence of considerable genetic diversity and reflected the combined influence of these quantitative traits on genotype differentiation. However, phenotypic characterization is influenced by different environmental regimes which makes it difficult to assess genetic diversity. As phenotype (P) results from genotype (G) and environment interactions (P = G+E + G*E), field evaluations may not reflect the true genetic potential and ultimately affect genotype classification and distort inter and intra cluster variations [35, 37, 38]. To surmount the limitation of morphological characterization and obtain a closer estimation of genetic diversity, SSR markers were utilized to measure genetic variability and differentiate genotypes from various regions of the nation. Molecular markers, especially SSRs, have evolved to be a preferred choice for estimating genetic diversity in field pea [39]. This study utilizes 16 polymorphic SSR primers to examine genetic diversity among edible podded pea genotypes and found two to six alleles per marker with a mean PIC value of 0.50, reflecting the genetic richness and diversity at a given locus. The moderate to high PIC values indicated the effectiveness of SSR markers in detecting genetic polymorphism among edible-podded pea genotypes. Moderate to high allelic diversity, with the number of alleles ranging from 2 to 10 [40] and PIC values from 0.45 to 0.75 [41] was also found utilizing SSR markers among genotypes in pea germplasm. Furthermore, observed heterozygosity (HO) measures the proportion of heterozygous individuals in a population, and it varied from 0.00 to 0.407 in the present study. In legume crops, Ho values ranging from 0.05 to 0.40 highlight varying levels of genetic diversity [39]. The expected heterozygosity (He) represents the probability of two randomly chosen alleles being different. The He value varied from 0.434 to 0.819, with a mean value of 0.592 in the provided genotypes. The comparatively low observed heterozygosity (Ho) relative to expected heterozygosity (He) may be attributed to the predominantly self-pollinating nature of pea, which generally results in reduced heterozygosity within populations [42]. Shannon’s information index-I varied between 0.365 and 0.642, which showed a moderate level of genetic diversity in the studied genotypes [43].

Assessment of genetic diversity among genotypes by UPGMA clustering revealed genetic relationships and diversity with well-defined clusters and distinct genotypes. The two main clusters were identified by UPGMA clustering, A and B, which represent moderate to high genetic similarity among genotypes (similarity coefficients ranged from 0.57 to 0.89, averaging 0.73). Cluster A consists of five genotypes, while cluster B consists of 22 genotypes, showing greater genetic variation, and sub-clustering further highlights intra-cluster relationships. The presence of distinct genetic clusters suggests that the germplasm contains sufficient variation to expand the genetic pool and develop new cultivars with improved traits [35]. The considerable genetic diversity detected among the genotypes also highlights the importance of conserving edible-podded pea germplasm, as these accessions represent valuable reservoirs of distinct alleles that may be useful for future crop improvement and sustainable utilization programmes. The genotypes categorized within the separate group exhibit higher levels of genetic diversity [44]. Utilization of genetically distant genotypes can enlarge the genetic basis of pea germplasm. The genetically diverse accessions identified in the present investigation may further serve as potential candidates for the development of a representative core collection, which can facilitate efficient germplasm utilization, minimize duplication, and support long-term gene bank management strategies. Although two garden pea genotypes (Punjab-89 and AP-3) were included in the analysis to represent the broader diversity of Pisum sativum, however, they did not form separate clusters in the dendrogram or STRUCTURE analysis. These genotypes primarily differed from edible podded types by the presence of a parchment layer in their pods—an important distinguishing trait rendering them non-edible. Since the parchment layer trait was not assessed in the current study due to the focus on podded edible characteristics, these garden pea lines did not contribute significantly to trait-based variation or influence clustering patterns. Nonetheless, their inclusion helped demonstrate the genetic proximity of some garden pea and edible pod lines in other traits and justified the need to include parchment-related descriptors in future investigations.

The AMOVA revealed that the majority of genetic variation (91%) was present within populations, while only a small portion (9%) existed among populations. Higher variation within populations suggested the existence of considerable genetic heterogeneity among evaluated genotypes. Comparable findings have been reported in garden pea genotypes, where within-population variation ranged from 81% to 94%, and among-population variation from 19% to 6% [40, 45]. In the present study, the FST value was 0.065, indicating a moderate level of genetic differentiation among the evaluated edible-podded pea groups. This result is highly consistent with previous genetic diversity studies in Pisum sativum. For instance, Zhao et al. had also reported low-to-moderate differentiation (FST = 0.129) among a core collection of 144 pea accessions, noting that the bulk of genetic diversity was maintained within populations [46]. These results suggest considerable gene flow and genetic exchange within populations, which is consistent with the predominantly self-pollinating reproductive behavior of pea [47]. Such findings highlight the significance of individual genotypic variation for targeted breeding. The majority of genetic variation within populations provides an opportunity to utilize intra-population diversity in breeding programs [48]. The high within-population diversity observed in the present study suggests the availability of sufficient exploitable variability for selection and genetic enhancement of edible-podded pea through population improvement and targeted breeding approaches. Breeders can select genotypes within populations to develop promising cultivars with desirable traits. Additionally, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) is a widely used technique to validate the hierarchical clustering or population structure analysis results [49]. The PCoA differentiated the 27 edible podded pea genotypes into four populations and described 37.80% of the total genetic variation spread across three axis. Yousef et al. [45] similarly reported 24% and 8% variation along PC1 and PC2, respectively. PCoA clusters populations based on similarity coefficients or variance-covariance values, offering a broad perspective on population relationships. Conversely, cluster analysis provides precise resolution among closely related populations, and PCoA is more effective for visualizing major group structures.

Model-based population structure analysis using STRUCTURE offers insights into the genetic admixture within the studied gene pool, helping to identify introgression events [50]. This approach provides a more refined understanding of population grouping compared to traditional dendrogram-based clustering. The population structure at K = 4 was consistent with pedigree information, demonstrating a strong correlation between the molecular marker data and the genetic background of the genotypes. The groupings of STRUCTURE show the genetic diversity and relatedness within the population., P1 and P2 have clear, distinct membership with 100% assignment for most genotypes, whereas P3 and P4 show mixed memberships that reflect a level of introgression. Structure grouping of garden pea genotypes using SSR markers was also studied by Yousef et al. [45], which showed consistency with pedigree data and identified mixed memberships indicative of genetic admixture.

The integration of morphological, molecular, and multivariate analyses in this study provided a more comprehensive understanding of genetic diversity in edible podded pea genotypes. Cross-validation among STRUCTURE, PCA, PCoA, and UPGMA revealed consistent population stratification, with the four subgroups identified at K = 4 in STRUCTURE also reflected in the clustering patterns of PCoA and UPGMA dendrograms. Genotypes such as Dwarf Grey Sugar and PED-21-2, which exhibited admixed ancestry in STRUCTURE, were similarly positioned between clusters in PCoA and shared intermediate similarity coefficients in UPGMA, supporting their hybrid or introgressed nature. AMOVA results, showing that 91% of the genetic variation resides within populations, aligned with these observations and validated the continuous variation captured by PCA. These congruent findings across multiple statistical methods underscore the robustness of the genetic structure detected. Moreover, PCA identified key traits such as pod weight, yield per plant, days to first flowering, and days to 50% flowering as major contributors to variability, making them suitable candidates for selection. The marked genetic divergence between Cluster III (e.g., PED-18-1, PED-18-7, PED-21-6, Arka Sampoorna) and Cluster V (e.g., Him Palam Mithi Phali-2, Honey Snap, PED-21-2, Oregon Sugar Pod, PED-21-3, Royal Snow, Tarvedo Sugar) highlights their potential utility as divergent parents in hybridization. Furthermore, cophenetic correlation coefficient (CCC) values for morphological traits and SSR markers indicated a good fit between the dendrograms and their respective distance matrices. The Mantel test revealed a significant, low positive correlation which indicate partial correspondence between phenotypic and SSR-based genetic diversity. This aligns with similar low-to-moderate correlations reported in pea by Taran et al. (r = 0.353) and Noren et al. (r = 0.219) [51, 52]. The combined agro-morphological and molecular characterization employed in the present investigation provided a more comprehensive understanding of the genetic diversity available in edible-podded pea germplasm. The considerable variability detected among genotypes indicates the availability of valuable allelic resources that can be effectively utilized in future breeding programmes aimed at broadening the genetic base of cultivated pea.

Conclusions

The agro-morphological and molecular characterization of 25 edible podded and 2 garden pea genotypes revealed moderate genetic diversity, especially in traits like pod curvature (H = 1.25) and seed colour (H = 1.21). Mahalanobis D² analysis grouped genotypes into five clusters, with the highest inter-cluster distance between Clusters III and V indicating considerable genetic divergence. SSR markers revealed moderate polymorphism with 2–6 alleles per locus (average 3.25) and a mean PIC of 0.5007, with primer AA332 showing the highest PIC (0.776). Further, molecular clustering divided genotypes into two main groups with sub-structuring, while AMOVA revealed 91% variation within populations. STRUCTURE analysis identified four genetic subpopulations (K = 4). Among the evaluated germplasm, PED-18-1, PED-18-7, PED-21-6, Arka Sampoorna, and PED-21-2 were identified as the most distinct genotypes based on the combined agro-morphological and molecular analyses. In addition, the highly divergent genotypes belonging to Cluster III (PED-18-1, PED-18-7, PED-21-6 and Arka Sampoorna) and Cluster V (Him Palam Mithi Phali-2, Honey Snap, PED-21-2, Oregon Sugar Pod, PED-21-3, Royal Snow and Tarvedo Sugar) constitute valuable parental resources for hybridization to broaden the genetic base and accelerate edible-podded pea improvement. The observed diversity among edible-podded pea genotypes also highlights the importance of conserving these genetic resources to prevent erosion of valuable alleles associated with pod quality and adaptation traits. This can be strategically utilized to broaden the genetic base of edible podded peas cultivars for future breeding programmes.
