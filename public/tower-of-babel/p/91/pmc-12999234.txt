Association Between the Affinity for Hikikomori (Prolonged Social Withdrawal) and Increased Hikikomori Tendencies in University Students
Authors: Shunsuke Nonaka, Tomoya Takeda, Hiroaki Kubo, Makoto Suda, Yuki Tanaka, Motohiro Sakai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective University students are at a developmental stage in which they may be more vulnerable to experiencing hikikomori (prolonged social withdrawal). Therefore, we focused on this population and examined the relationship between the tendencies toward and affinity for hikikomori in two studies. Methods Study 1 examined the relationship between the tendencies toward and affinity for hikikomori among 300 university students recruited online. Study 2 examined how stress coping, social support, and the affinity for hikikomori can explain hikikomori tendencies for a sample of 364 university students recruited offline. Both studies used the Adaptive Behaviors Scale for Hikikomori and Affinity for Hikikomori Scale, while Study 2 also used the Social Support Questionnaire and Coping Orientation to Problems Experienced Inventory. Results In both studies, hierarchical multiple regressions indicated that, among the affinity components for hikikomori, a stronger desire for hikikomori was associated with higher hikikomori tendencies. Conversely, higher empathy for others with hikikomori was associated with lower hikikomori tendencies, contrary to the implicit assumptions. Higher social support, active coping skills, and the use of instrumental support explained lower hikikomori tendencies. Conclusion These findings suggest that individuals at potential risk of developing hikikomori may be better identified by considering environmental (e.g., social support), behavioral (e.g., coping with stress), and cognitive and affective (e.g., desire for hikikomori) variables. Notably, empathy for others with hikikomori may reflect general empathic traits rather than being specifically related to hikikomori. Future longitudinal research should identify any causal relationships.Keywords: Hikikomori, Social withdrawal, Affinity, Risk.

INTRODUCTION

Hikikomori, or prolonged social withdrawal, is a state characterized by 1) a lack of social participation, including working, attending school, and having social interactions outside one’s home, and 2) staying at home almost every day except solitary outings for at least 6 months [1,2]. This phenomenon has attracted increased social attention because of the problems related to isolation caused by the COVID-19 pandemic and the associated public health measures. Hikikomori has also received increased academic attention, as shown by its addition to the “Cultural Concepts of Distress” section of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition [3]. Recent systematic reviews have demonstrated that individuals with hikikomori exhibit a wide range of functional impairments [4]. Recently, such individuals have been reported not only in Eastern countries such as Japan, China [5], South Korea [6], and Singapore [7] but also in Western countries such as the United States [8], Finland [9], France [10], Italy [11], and Spain [12]. In 2009, a national project dedicated to supporting individuals with hikikomori was established in Japan. The Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare (MHLW) has established and operated various specialized support centers addressing individuals with hikikomori in numerous prefectures and larger cities. Furthermore, it has been training practitioners and peer supporters under its various projects. Since FY2022, the MHLW expanded the functions of these support centers to local governments, providing individuals with hikikomori with counseling and a place to communicate with others (called “ibasho”). Many local governments have recently implemented epidemiological surveys on hikikomori in response to national measures and social needs. According to the MHLW, 937 of the 1,741 local governments in Japan had implemented these surveys by March 2023. Many epidemiological surveys aim to estimate the number of people with a high “affinity for hikikomori” and those with confirmed hikikomori.

Recently, the concept of affinity for hikikomori, including a desire for and empathy toward those experiencing it [13-15], has attracted attention. Affinity for hikikomori is defined as an attitude whereby individuals who are not currently withdrawn either feel they can understand the feelings of people who withdraw from society or desire to withdraw themselves (Cabinet Office, 2010). It has been conceptualized as a two-dimensional construct comprising desire for hikikomori and empathy for others with hikikomori. This affinity is considered an indicator for people who could potentially develop hikikomori in the future, as well as a risk factor in previous scientific and epidemiological research by both national and local governments. The affinity for hikikomori is associated with various psychiatric symptoms, including depression, social phobia, difficulties with interpersonal skills [15], difficulties in social interactions similar to those present in people with autism [16], and school nonattendance [13]. However, there is no clear evidence of the criteria for a high affinity for hikikomori because of the lack of data supporting the idea that people with an affinity for hikikomori are at an increased risk of developing this condition. Despite the lack of clear evidence that people with such an affinity could potentially develop hikikomori, many local governments have included this concept in their surveys and policies as a definitive factor when considering them as potential individuals with hikikomori. While identifying potential reservoirs helps planning support measures to prevent hikikomori, incorrectly identifying individuals may hinder the identification of the actual population and lead to the wastage of support resources. Therefore, recognizing the protective or risk factors for hikikomori, including affinity, is essential.

Aside from the lack of clear evidence to support the inclusion of people with an affinity for hikikomori as potential members of this population, concerns have been raised regarding the differences in the aspects they reflect and their tendencies based on gender. First, while hikikomori is a concept composed of behavioral elements, the affinity for it, which consists of “the desire for hikikomori” and “empathy for others with hikikomori,” includes cognitive and affective elements rather than behavioral ones. In addition, focusing on “the desire for hikikomori” as a component of affinity does not necessarily indicate whether affinity is a risk factor. This is because, for individuals with hikikomori, the desire to withdraw and that to not withdraw can easily coexist. For example, individuals with hikikomori often seek support that provides them with ibasho [17], a space where they can interact with others. Thus, if all individuals with hikikomori only have the desire to withdraw and not interact with others, they would never seek ibasho support. Alternatively, because of this affinity, the desire for hikikomori could simply be a temporary psychological defense against daily stressors. The “empathy for others with hikikomori” component may reflect empathic concern for these individuals rather than a risk factor. Gender stereotypes exist for empathy, and women often show more empathy than men [18]; thus, the affinity for hikikomori tends to be higher among women [15,19,20]. However, many reports have shown that more men than women exhibit hikikomori [1,12,19-21]. The lack of correlation between empathy for individuals with hikikomori and quality of life [13] also supports this possibility.

Therefore, other definitive risk or protective factors that influence the association between hikikomori and the affinity for it may exist. For example, many individuals with hikikomori experience stressful events such as resignation, failed interpersonal relationships, illness, workplace maladjustment, school non-attendance, and failed job hunting [20,22]. The coping strategies used for these stressors may be associated with the risk of developing hikikomori. Previous studies [23,24] found that stress coping using active coping, instrumental support, behavioral disengagement, and planning are associated with hikikomori, indicating that such behavioral variables may be protective or risk factors. Another possible protective factor against hikikomori is the social support from others, including family and friends. Furthermore, gender differences in terms of both hikikomori and the affinity for it suggest that helpseeking tendencies, which are higher in women than men [25], may be a protective factor. Reports [19,20] that more than 60% of individuals with hikikomori are men and that 65%–66% of individuals with hikikomori do not want to receive help in institutions for their condition also suggest that social support may be a protective factor. Specifically, these findings likely reflect avoidance of forms of help perceived as intrusive or threatening, rather than a refusal of all help. Accordingly, informal support from non-professionals (e.g., family, friends, peers) may be a crucial gateway to the uptake of formal support.

Therefore, establishing evidence-based early detection and support measures is essential. In Study 1, we focused on university students, who were at an age when they were more likely to experience hikikomori, and examined the relationship between hikikomori tendencies and affinity. In Study 2, we examined how stress coping and social support, in addition to the affinity for hikikomori, explained hikikomori tendencies. Given the lack of social interaction associated with hikikomori, an online sample was collected in Study 1 and an offline sample in Study 2 to reduce the result bias based on social interaction levels.

METHODS

Study 1

We examined the association between the tendencies toward and affinity for hikikomori in university students.

Data collection

Data were collected by an Internet research company with large research panels in Japan in February 2023. The inclusion criterion was university students living in Japan. Ten thousand 18–24-year-olds were screened, and 300 responses were collected for the given percentages of residence and gender.

Measures

Demographics

Participants reported their age, gender, and whether the following conditions were currently or previously met for over six months: “not socializing outside one’s home” and “staying at home on most days, except solitary outings [1].” Participants were asked about the frequency of outings per week. The qualitative aspects of outings were provided using a four-point scale, ranging from 0 to 3 for three items: “Going out freely,” “Outings with interpersonal interaction,” and “Outings without interpersonal interaction [26].”

Adaptive Behaviors Scale for Hikikomori

The Adaptive Behaviors Scale for Hikikomori (ABS-H), self-report version, has 26 items and measures social interaction [27]. Responses were provided on a four-point scale ranging from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating more frequent social interaction behaviors and lower hikikomori tendencies. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.93 in this study.

Affinity for Hikikomori Scale in University Students

The Affinity for Hikikomori Scale in University Students (AHS) has 16 items and assesses two dimensions of affinity for hikikomori: the desire for hikikomori and empathy for others with hikikomori [13]. The desire for hikikomori includes items that assess tendencies, such as a wish to avoid going out when experiencing stress. In contrast, empathy for others with hikikomori assesses a situation-specific empathic stance [13,16] (e.g., the belief that some people may have reasons for being unable to go out). The empathy subscale may also partially capture general empathic traits. Responses were provided on a four-point scale ranging from 1 to 4, with higher scores indicating higher levels of affinity for hikikomori. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.86 and 0.87 for the desire and empathy subscales, respectively.

Data analysis

jamovi version 2.3 [28] was used for analysis. Welch’s t-tests were conducted with gender and whether the participants currently had at least one of two hikikomori symptoms, namely “not socializing outside one’s home” and “staying at home on most days, except solitary outings,” as independent variables, and ABS-H, AHS, and frequency of outings as the dependent variables. Correlation coefficients were calculated for each variable. Hierarchical multiple regression analysis was performed with the ABS-H as the dependent variable, previous hikikomori experience and gender in Step 1, AHS subscales in Step 2, and an interaction term in Step 3. The assumptions of no outlier problems, normality of residuals, independence of residuals, and multicollinearity were confirmed. A post hoc power analysis was conducted because of the lack of previous studies.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Tokyo Future University (number: 2022-027). The potential participants were informed that their responses to the questionnaire would indicate their consent to participate in the study.

Study 2

We examined whether hikikomori tendencies were explained by the affinity for hikikomori, coping with stress, and social support.

Data collection

Data were collected between September and November 2023 from participants affiliated with four Japanese universities. Of the four universities, two were in the Kanto region, one was in the Shikoku region, and one was in the Kyushu region. The inclusion criterion was university students living in Japan. A priori power analysis was conducted based on the results of the correlation analysis between the ABS-H and AHS subscales in Study 1. The analysis suggested a sample size of 285, given an effect size f2 of 0.10, 14 explanatory variables, power of 0.95, and alpha of 0.05. The target number of distributions was set at 570, assuming a response rate of 50%.

Measures

Demographics

The participants were asked to indicate the university to which they belonged in addition to the items in Study 1. The participants were also asked whether anyone close to them had hikikomori.

ABS-H

This scale was identical to the one used in Study 1. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.91 in this study.

AHS

This scale was identical to the one used in Study 1. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.86 and 0.83 for the desire and empathy subscales, respectively.

Social Support Questionnaire

The Social Support Questionnaire (SSQ) is a six-item index of social support [29,30]. It consists of two parts: the number of others available for support and the level of satisfaction with perceived support. In the first part, responses were provided on a seven-point scale ranging from 0 to more than six people. In the second part, responses were provided on a six-point scale, ranging from 1 to 6. Higher scores indicated higher social support. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.93 and 0.92 for the first and second parts, respectively.

Brief Coping Orientation to Problems Experienced Inventory

The Japanese version of the Brief Coping Orientation to Problems Experienced (COPE) inventory assesses various stress-coping strategies and involves 14 subscales consisting of two items each [31,32]. Based on previous research showing an association with hikikomori tendencies, four subscales were used: Active Coping, Instrumental Support, Behavioral Disengagement, and Planning [24]. The responses were provided on a four-point scale, ranging from 1 to 4, with higher scores indicating better coping skills. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.69 for Active Coping, 0.89 for Use of Instrumental Support, 0.75 for Behavioral Disengagement, and 0.72 for Planning.

Daily stressors

Participants were asked about their experiences of stressful events in four domains: family, interpersonal, academic, and career. Each of these four areas required responses on an 11-point scale, ranging from 0 to 10. Higher scores indicated more frequent stressors. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.69 in this study.

Data analysis

Welch’s t-test and correlation analyses were performed as described in Study 1. Welch’s t-tests were conducted with the independent variable of whether anyone close to them experienced hikikomori. Hierarchical multiple regression analysis was performed with ABS-H as the dependent variable, previous hikikomori experience, gender, affiliated universities, and stressors in Step 1, AHS subscales in Step 2, SSQ and COPE subscales in Step 3, and an interaction term in Step 4. With these steps, we addressed participants’ past experiences with hikikomori and the high proportion of female participants. The assumptions of no outlier problems, normality of residuals, independence of residuals, and multicollinearity were confirmed.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Tokyo Future University (number: 2023-007). The potential participants were informed that their responses to the questionnaire would indicate consent for their participation in the study.

RESULTS

Study 1

The participants comprised 150 men and 150 women (Mage=21.09 years, standard deviation [SD]=1.34) (Table 1). Fifty participants met at least one hikikomori criterion, and 250 participants did not meet any hikikomori criteria. Compared to those who did not meet the hikikomori conditions, those who met either of the two conditions had significantly lower ABS-H scores (t=7.56, p<0.001, d=1.22), higher AHS desire subscale scores (t=3.84, p<0.001, d=0.61), and less frequent outings (t=5.85, p<0.001, d=0.93) (Supplementary Table 1). However, AHS empathy subscale scores did not differ significantly. Compared to men, women had significantly higher desire (t=3.35, p<0.001, d=0.39) and empathy (t=3.44, p<0.001, d=0.40) subscale scores. However, ABS-H scores and outing frequency did not differ significantly.

Study 2

Questionnaires were distributed to 825 students, of whom 368 responded. Four students who did not provide consent were excluded, and data from 364 students were included in the analysis. The response rate was 44.1%. Participants included 69 men, 292 women, and three others (Mage=19.49 years, SD=1.29) (Table 1), of whom 137 came from A University, 61 from B University, 47 from C University, 116 from D University, and three were unknown. Fifty participants met at least one hikikomori criterion, and 312 participants did not meet any hikikomori criteria.

Compared to those who did not meet them, those who currently met either of the two hikikomori conditions had significantly lower ABS-H scores (t=5.19, p<0.001, d=0.85) and less frequent outings (t=3.45, p=0.001, d=0.55) (Supplementary Table 2). However, the AHS desire and empathy subscale scores did not differ significantly. Compared to men, women had a significantly higher desire subscale score (t=2.15, p=0.03, d=0.29). However, the empathy subscale, ABS-H, and outing frequency scores were not significantly different. Compared to those who did not have someone close to them, those who had someone close to them had significantly higher desire subscale scores (t=4.40, p<0.001, d=0.49). However, the empathy subscale, ABS-H, and outing frequency scores were not significantly different.

The AHS desire for hikikomori subscale (Supplementary Table 3) showed positive correlations with daily stressors (r=0.32, p<0.001) and behavioral disengagement coping (r=0.40, p<0.001), and negative correlations with ABS-H (r=-0.47, p< 0.001), SSQ number subscale (r=-0.41, p<0.001), outing frequency (r=-0.20, p<0.001), going out freely (r=-0.28, p<0.001), outings with interpersonal interaction (r=-0.25, p<0.001), SSQ satisfaction subscale (r=-0.25, p<0.001), active coping (r=-0.27, p<0.001), use of instrumental support (r=-0.13, p=0.01), and planning coping (r=-0.20, p<0.001). However, the correlation with outings without interpersonal interaction was not significant. The empathy subscale of the AHS showed weak positive correlations with active coping (r=0.13, p=0.02), use of instrumental support (r=0.13, p=0.02), and planning coping (r=0.16, p=0.003). However, the correlations with other variables were not significant.

Hierarchical multiple regression analyses (Table 4) showed a significant adjusted R2 (0.05, p=0.001) in Step 1 and significant ΔR2 in Steps 2 (0.22, p<0.001) and 3 (0.30, p<0.001), but not significant ΔR2 in Step 4 (0.03, p=0.22). The results of Step 2 showed that a higher desire for hikikomori was associated with higher hikikomori tendencies, whereas higher empathy for others with hikikomori was associated with lower hikikomori tendencies. The results in Step 3 showed that a higher number of people available for support and satisfaction with perceived social support, and higher active coping and instrumental support with stress coping were associated with lower hikikomori tendencies.

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to examine the relationships between the tendencies toward and affinity for hikikomori, coping with stress, and social support among university students. We hypothesized that the affinity for hikikomori would not necessarily explain hikikomori tendencies, but that coping with stress and social support would be stronger explanatory variables. One strength of this study is the use of different samples from online and offline research for analysis, given the characteristics of individuals with hikikomori, who often avoid social situations.

Regarding the component of hikikomori affinity, the desire for hikikomori was positively associated with hikikomori tendencies, whereas empathy for others was inversely associated with hikikomori. This is an important finding of this study. In addition to the desire for hikikomori, coping with stress and social support explain hikikomori tendencies. Our results suggest that potential hikikomori risk should be captured not only by cognitive and affective variables, such as affinity, but also by behavioral and environmental variables. Furthermore, evidence indicates that individuals with hikikomori experience symptoms of depression, anxiety, loneliness, and negative cognitive tendencies [33-36]. However, this does not mean that individuals with the potential for hikikomori can only be identified based on cognitive and affective concepts such as affinity. This confuses the understanding of the pathophysiology and development of a preventive approach. Although the frequency of going out is often used as a representative behavioral variable indicating hikikomori tendencies, a previous study reported the need to focus not only on frequency but also on qualitative aspects, such as the presence of social interactions [26].

This study’s findings support the results of Shimono et al. [13], who found that depressive symptoms were positively correlated and the quality of life negatively correlated with the desire for hikikomori, but did not correlate with the empathy for others with hikikomori. Shimono et al. [16] also found that difficulties in social interactions positively explained subsequent desire for hikikomori but negatively explained the subsequent empathy for others with hikikomori. Individuals with high empathy for others with hikikomori may not be at high risk for hikikomori, but rather more adaptive. Depression may impair empathic abilities [37] and hikikomori is associated with higher depressive tendencies [35]. Furthermore, the finding that the presence of a close person with hikikomori was not associated with empathy for others indicates that empathy for others with hikikomori may reflect general rather than hikikomori-specific empathy.

Gender difference trends also provide some implications for hikikomori tendencies among university students. Despite Hoffman’s [18] finding that women are more empathetic than men, according to gender role stereotypes, the gender difference in empathy for others with hikikomori was not significant or, if significant, only slightly. The gender difference in the desire for hikikomori was significant, yet small, in both studies. Additionally, contrary to multiple reports that men are more likely than women to exhibit hikikomori [1,12,19-21], no significant gender differences in hikikomori tendencies were observed. At least among university students, no apparent gender differences in either hikikomori tendencies or affinity appeared to be present. However, differences in recruitment were accompanied by variations in participant characteristics between studies, particularly in gender composition. Study 1 had an approximately balanced gender distribution, whereas Study 2 included about 80% women. Although the associations between hikikomori tendencies and affinity for hikikomori were generally consistent between studies, gender-related patterns in affinity differed in part. In Study 1, women scored higher than men on the empathy subscale, whereas no gender difference was observed in Study 2. These between-study differences may reflect the influence of recruitment procedures and gender composition.

The observed absence of gender differences in hikikomori tendencies may be influenced by using the ABS-H, which focuses on behavioral characteristics. Men, who are often expected to be engaged in work outside the home, may be more readily labeled as hikikomori when socially withdrawn. By contrast, women, traditionally viewed as homemakers or caregivers, may not be similarly classified, even when exhibiting comparable behaviors. Therefore, cognitive characteristics such as cultural stereotypes may influence whether people consider themselves to have hikikomori. Conversely, viewing hikikomori as a behavioral characteristic, such as social interaction behavior, may allow us to view hikikomori appropriately and without being influenced by stereotypes. Additionally, the results of this study on gender differences may reflect the possibility that gender-role stereotypes have declined among Japanese university students in recent years. In Japan, both women and men currently tend to attend university and obtain jobs. As such, many university students, regardless of gender, may not have hikikomori themselves, even if they empathize with individuals who do.

Significant interactions were found in Study 1, indicating that the inverse correlation between empathy for individuals with hikikomori and hikikomori tendencies was present only when the desire for hikikomori was low. Thus, focusing on reducing desire for hikikomori may help prevent it. The regression models in Study 2 showed that social, active coping, and instrumental support explained hikikomori tendencies, consistent with research showing lower social support in individuals with hikikomori [34,38,39], as well as previous studies showing that higher active coping and instrumental support are associated with lower hikikomori tendencies [23,24]. Psychosocial treatment focusing on these environmental factors and problemsolving coping strategies may prevent hikikomori. Thus, the prevention of hikikomori may require a particular focus on students with low levels of social support and ineffective problem-solving coping. The effects of the Community Reinforcement and Family Training (CRAFT) [40] approach on increased social interaction behavior and engagement in treatment have been demonstrated in families of individuals with hikikomori who do not seek help [41-44]. The CRAFT approach emphasizes engaging with the community, including family and social resources. Assessments and psychoeducation using this approach may benefit the prevention and early intervention of hikikomori among university students. Non-family involvement, such as peer relationships, has also been suggested to be beneficial [45]. Problem-solving training [46] and Lee et al.’s [35] home visitation program for young people with hikikomori may help improve social support.

Furthermore, at the organizational level, initiatives that facilitate friendship formation and group participation, together with the promotion of peer support within the university, can help enhance social support. In parallel, psychoeducation grounded in stress-and-coping theory is likely to aid in preventing hikikomori among university students. Given that individuals at risk for hikikomori often show avoidant tendencies in social situations, psychoeducation that teaches alternative coping repertoires—such as problem-solving coping—may broaden students’ options for dealing with stressors and thereby help prevent hikikomori. These components are feasible to implement within university counseling centers.

Limitations

This study was cross-sectional and, thus, could not identify causal relationships between variables. Therefore, a longitudinal study and an intervention study are required to identify causal relationships. These approaches are necessary to clarify the influence of affinity for hikikomori on the behaviors of individuals with this condition. Gender differences in the likelihood of hikikomori tend to vary by culture, and gender differences in affinity for hikikomori may also vary by culture. Further studies are needed to clarify the association between hikikomori tendencies and affinity in countries other than Japan. Cross-cultural comparative research will also be useful for assessing the generalizability of the present findings. Study 2 revealed a gender imbalance, and statistical adjustments were applied; however, gender-stratified analyses may be beneficial for a deeper understanding in future work. This study introduced the concepts of empathy toward others with hikikomori and desire for hikikomori; however, the ambiguity of these constructs complicates the interpretation of the findings. Empathy toward others with hikikomori may involve cognitive empathy, emotional empathy, or a combination of both. Regarding the constructions of affinity for hikikomori, future research should use semi-structured interviews and open-ended questionnaire items to explore the subjective meaning of hikikomori itself as expressed by students. In this study, participants’ psychiatric status was not assessed, and the presence of such disorders could confound the association between hikikomori tendencies and affinity for hikikomori. Therefore, future research should incorporate standardized psychiatric assessments.

Conclusions

Contrary to the existing implicit assumptions, the affinity for hikikomori does not necessarily explain hikikomori tendencies. The association with hikikomori tendencies differed depending on construct, with a high desire for hikikomori being associated with high hikikomori tendencies, whereas the empathy for others with hikikomori was not. Moreover, higher levels of social support and problem-solving coping were associated with lower hikikomori tendencies. Policymakers and researchers should select and include evidencebased factors in surveys to identify potential members of the hikikomori population.
