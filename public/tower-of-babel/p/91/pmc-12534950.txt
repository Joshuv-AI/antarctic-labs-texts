Maternal dyslipidemia in the first trimester and adverse pregnancy and offspring outcomes: based on the Tianjin birth cohort
Authors: Jinnan Liu, Qiangrong Zhai, Jing Wang, Xi Li, Jiayu Chen, Yuanyuan Guo, Fengxia Su, Chang Jiang, Fangyi Ren, Min Li, Guohong Zhang, Gongshu Liu, Ya Gao, Shuo Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited studies demonstrated the relationships between first-trimester maternal dyslipidemia and adverse pregnancy-offspring complications. This investigation aimed to probe (1) the relationships of first-trimester maternal dyslipidemia with adverse pregnancy and birth outcomes, and (2) the potential influence of first-trimester maternal lipid profiles on offspring growth trajectories.MethodsA prospective observational investigation was conducted within the structure of the Tianjin Birth Cohort, in which maternal blood samples were acquired (median at 11th gestational week) to measure lipid levels. Group-based trajectory modeling was employed to classify latent offspring growth trajectories. Poisson regression incorporating robust standard error was employed to analyze the associations of maternal dyslipidemia with adverse pregnancy-offspring complications.ResultsFirst-trimester maternal dyslipidemia significantly increased gestational diabetes mellitus risk after adjustment for confounders (relative risk: 1.36, 1.20–1.54), but did not link to gestational hypertension (1.13, 0.89–1.43) risk. In addition, first-trimester maternal dyslipidemia elevated preterm birth (1.24, 1.03–1.49) and large for gestational age (1.27, 1.12–1.43) risks. However, first-trimester maternal dyslipidemia showed no significant link with either small for gestational age risk (0.83, 0.67–1.03) or offspring growth trajectories from birth to 24 months (lower growth trajectory: 0.99, 0.93–1.06; higher growth trajectory: 0.97, 0.88–1.08).ConclusionsFirst-trimester maternal dyslipidemia elevated adverse pregnancy and offspring status risks, specifically maternal gestational diabetes mellitus, as well as offspring preterm birth, and large for gestational age, but did not link to maternal gestational hypertension, small for gestational age, or variations in offspring growth trajectories from birth to 24 months.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02751-y.Keywords: Gestational diabetes mellitus, Growth, Large for gestational age, Dyslipidemia, Preterm birth

Background

Lipids, as essential organic compounds, are involved in numerous biological processes. Emerging evidence indicated that dyslipidemia, characterized by elevated total cholesterol (TC), high triglycerides (TG), reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), or increased low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), was related to various metabolic disorders, specifically obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and cardiovascular diseases (CVD) [1, 2]. Throughout gestation, maternal lipid profiles naturally increase, with TC and TG increasing moderately during the first trimester and substantially in the later trimesters to support fetal development [3], a condition termed maternal physiological hyperlipidemia. However, when maternal lipids exceed physiological thresholds, resulting in maternal supraphysiological hyperlipidemia, adverse pregnancy outcomes increase significantly. For example, a retrospective cohort study (n = 10,699) indicated that women diagnosed with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) exhibited elevated TC, TG, and reduced HDL-C during the later trimesters than women without GDM [4]. Additionally, a case-control study (n = 1,541) found that elevated TG levels at 12-16th gestational weeks independently increased risk of gestational hypertension (GH), after adjustment for LDL-C and glucose levels [5]. While most research have examined maternal lipid levels during the later trimesters, fewer large-scale studies have focused on first-trimester associations. Understanding the influence of first-trimester supraphysiological dyslipidemia on adverse pregnancy outcomes could contribute to enhance early detection strategies in high-risk populations, enabling preventive interventions.

The Developmental Origins of Health and Disease theory pointed out intrauterine environment and maternal metabolic status during pregnancy significantly influenced both immediately after birth and well into adulthood [6]. Among maternal metabolites, the abnormal lipid levels change during pregnancy was considered critical in fetal growth. Previous studies investigating the impact of maternal dyslipidemia on adverse birth outcomes remained contradictory and inconclusive. A cohort study (n = 4,008) demonstrated that increased first-trimester TG levels elevated large for gestational age (LGA) risk but did not correlate with preterm birth (PTB) or small for gestational age (SGA) [7]. A retrospective cohort study (n = 57,516) indicated that relatively high early-pregnancy TG levels could increase LGA risk and decrease SGA risk [8]. Another retrospective study (n = 6,963) showed that increased first-trimester TG could elevate PTB risk [9]. Given these varying findings, this research had the objective of exploring the links between first-trimester maternal dyslipidemia and adverse birth outcomes, including PTB, SGA, and LGA, using a large birth cohort.

Currently, the worldwide prevalence of obesity has risen substantially, with early-life obesity predisposing children to elevated risks of adverse health outcomes, including CVD and T2DM in adulthood [10, 11]. Hence, identifying early-life obesity risk factors is critical for timely intervention. Limited research investigated the relationship of maternal dyslipidemia with offspring obesity using longitudinal follow-up data. A birth cohort study (n = 1,727) found positive correlations between maternal TC levels and offspring fat percentage, as well as between maternal TG and offspring waist-to-height ratio at age 5–6 years [12]. Several evidence indicated that childhood growth trajectories were superior predictors of later disease risks compared to single-point measurements [13], potentially offering valuable insights into early-life determinants of health. Therefore, exploring the impact of first-trimester maternal lipid profiles on offspring growth trajectories warrants attention.

In this context, the Tianjin Birth Cohort (TJBC) with a large sample size was employed to probe (1) the relationships of first-trimester supraphysiological dyslipidemia with adverse pregnancy and birth outcomes, and (2) the potential influence of first-trimester maternal lipids on offspring growth trajectories.

Methods

Study design and participant selection

The TJBC enrolled 11,014 pregnant women through a three-tier antenatal healthcare system from August 2017 to December 2021 in Tianjin, China. Inclusion criteria comprised the following conditions: (1) pregnant women aged over 18 years with a gestational weeks less than 15 weeks at enrollment; (2) pregnant women and their spouses of Chinese nationality with permanent residency in Tianjin; (3) pregnant women receiving antenatal care and planning delivery at Tianjin-based hospitals; and (4) offspring participating in periodic physical examinations in Tianjin before the age of 3 years. The TJBC received approval from the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Women and Children’s Health Center (No. 201706012-1), with all participants providing their written consent before participation [14].

This prospective observational investigation was conducted within the framework of the TJBC. Among the 11,014 pregnant women, 345 were excluded owing to pregnancy termination, 89 were excluded to reduce potential confounding and treatment-related effects on lipid levels (66 with pre-pregnancy CVD or use related medications before enrollment, and 23 with pre-pregnancy immune diseases or use related medications before enrollment), and 288 were excluded owing to lack of blood lipid measurements at enrollment. After these exclusions, 10,292 pregnant women remained, corresponding to 10,416 offspring. Among the offspring, 24 were excluded owing to fetal death, 20 owing to infant death, and 157 owing to missing information. Data collection and outcomes definition

Data were obtained from the questionnaire surveys, physical examinations, blood sample measurements, and Tianjin Maternal and Child Health Information System. A detailed description of data collection was available elsewhere [14]. From the database, the following maternal information were retrieved: gestational week, age, height, weight before and during gestation, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), free thyroxine (FT4), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), fasting plasma glucose (FPG) at enrollment, and parity. Information regarding family history of diabetes and hypertension, pre-pregnancy diabetes, chronic hypertension, hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, smoking status, and alcohol consumption were obtained through the first-trimester questionnaire and Tianjin Maternal and Child Health Information System. Pre-pregnancy body mass index (pBMI) was computed using the formula: pre-pregnancy weight (kg) divided by squared of height (m2), and categorized into the following groups: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (≥ 18.5 to < 24 kg/m2), overweight (≥ 24 to < 28 kg/m2), and obesity (≥ 28 kg/m2) based on the classification established by the Working Group on Obesity in China [15]. Gestational weight gain (GWG) was computed using the formula: the maternal weight change divided by the corresponding change in gestational weeks. In addition, TC, TG, HDL-C, and LDL-C levels were quantified at enrollment (median at 11th gestational week) using an enzymatic method. According to the criteria of Williams Obstetrics 24th edition, maternal dyslipidemia in the first trimester was diagnosed when at least one of the following thresholds was exceeded: TC > 5.44 mmol/L, TG > 1.80 mmol/L, HDL-C < 1.04 mmol/L, or LDL-C > 3.96 mmol/L [16].

The adverse pregnancy outcomes included GDM and GH. GDM screening was performed at 24-28th gestational weeks using the approaches consisting of FPG test and oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Specifically, pregnant women were initially screened for FPG. Those with FPG ≥ 5.1 mmol/L were categorized into the GDM group, while those with FPG < 4.4 mmol/L were categorized into the non-GDM group. Participants with FPG levels ranging from 4.4 to 5.1 mmol/L required a standard 75-g OGTT with a 2-h duration after 8-h of fasting [17]. Based on the diagnostic standards established by the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups, GDM was confirmed when at least one FPG threshold was exceeded: FPG ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1-h post-load ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or 2-h post-load ≥ 8.5 mmol/L [18]. In addition, GH was defined based on the definition provided by the International Society for the Study of Hypertension in Pregnancy, characterized by SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg initially recorded subsequent to 20 gestational weeks [19].

The adverse birth outcomes included PTB and abnormal birth weight categories, specifically SGA and LGA. PTB was diagnosed if delivery occurred before 37 completed weeks of gestation. SGA and LGA were identified according to Chinese standards, with birth weights less than 10th percentile and more then 90th percentile at the same sex and delivery week, respectively [20]. This study prospectively documented the weight and length of the offspring at birth, and at 6, 12, 18, and 24 months. Weight for length z-score (WLZ) was used to assess nutritional status and growth among children below 5 years [21]. Weight for length was computed by dividing weight (kg) by length (cm) and converted into sex-specific WLZ through the World Health Organization (WHO) standards [22]. WLZ was classified into four groups on the basis of the WHO criteria for children under 5 years: wasting (< −2 standard deviation, SD), normal weight (≥ −2 to ≤ 2 SD), overweight (> 2 SD), and obesity (> 3SD).

Statistical analysis

R software (version 4.3.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Statistical Analysis System (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) were performed to analyze the data, with statistical significance considered as a two-tailed P value < 0.05. The normality of continuous variables was assessed with Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD and evaluated the differences between maternal dyslipidemia and normal lipid groups through the Student t test, while non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and were evaluated through Wilcoxon rank sum test. Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages) and were evaluated through the Chi-square tests.

Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) served to uncover latent growth trajectories by analyzing longitudinal WLZ data from birth to 24 months [23]. Three distinct groups were identified as lower, medium and higher growth trajectories, each exhibiting similar WLZ growth patterns (Fig. 2). Poisson regression incorporating robust standard error was performed to compute the relative risk (RR) and associated 95% confidence interval (CI) of first-trimester dyslipidemia for the risks of dichotomous outcomes, including GDM, GH, and PTB. Multinomial regression based on Poisson regression incorporating robust standard error was employed to analyze the associations between maternal dyslipidemia and the risks of three-category outcomes, including birth weight group and offspring growth trajectories [24]. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, with 1000 repetitions of 10-fold cross-validation, was used to choose the confounders that eventually entered each outcome model.

Results

Demographic characteristics of pregnant women

In the present research, the median maternal age and gestational age at enrollment were 29.9 (IQR: 27.4, 32.7) years and 11 (IQR: 10, 13) weeks, respectively. In total, 29.3% (n = 3,015) of participants were exhibited dyslipidemia in the first trimester, 16.8% (n = 1,637) developed GDM, and 2.9% (n = 301) developed GH during pregnancy. Compared with normal lipid levels, pregnant women with dyslipidemia demonstrated elevated gestational age, maternal age, pBMI, SBP, DBP, TSH, and lower FT4 and GWG (Table 1). They also exhibited a higher prevalence of overweight or obesity, multiparity, family history of diabetes or hypertension, pre-pregnancy diabetes, chronic hypertension, hypothyroidism, smoking status, GDM, and GH.

Demographic characteristics of offspring

This study included a total of 10,215 offspring, with a median delivery gestational age of 39 (IQR: 38, 40) weeks. Offspring of mothers with first-trimester dyslipidemia exhibited higher birth weights than their counterparts from normolipidemic mother (Table 2). These offspring also showed higher rates of PTB, multiple births, and LGA, but lower rate of SGA. Throughout the follow-up period, offspring of mothers with dyslipidemia in the first trimester had higher WLZ values at birth, and at 6, 12, 18 and 24 months. Furthermore, they exhibited a greater propensity for a higher rather than a lower growth trajectory.

Associations of maternal dyslipidemia with adverse status in mothers

First-trimester dyslipidemia elevated the risk of GDM (RR: 1.65, 95%CI: 1.50–1.82) in univariate model (Table 3). After controlled for confounding factors, including gestational age at registration, maternal age, pBMI, SBP, FT4, TSH, FPG, parity, family history of diabetes and hypertension, pre-pregnancy diabetes, hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, smoking status, GWG, and GH, the association remained significant but attenuated (RR: 1.36; 95%CI: 1.20–1.54). Additionally, the RR of maternal dyslipidemia for GH risk in univariate model was 1.49 (95%CI: 1.18–1.88). However, adjustment for pBMI, SBP, chronic hypertension, and GWG rendered this association non-significant.

Associations of maternal dyslipidemia with adverse status in offspring

First-trimester dyslipidemia significantly elevated PTB (RR: 1.48, 95%CI: 1.25–1.76) and LGA (RR: 1.41, 95%CI: 1.25–1.58) in univariate models. Conversely, it decreased SGA (RR: 0.73, 95%CI: 0.61–0.88) risk (Table 3). After adjustment for confounders, these associations became reduced as follows: the RR for PTB decreased to 1.24 (95%CI: 1.03–1.49), controlled for age, pBMI, FPG, GDM, multiple births, pre-pregnancy diabetes, offspring sex, birth length, and birth weight; and the RR for LGA decreased to 1.27 (95%CI: 1.12–1.43), controlled for age, pBMI, FPG, pre-pregnancy diabetes, hyperthyroidism, GDM, GWG, delivery week, multiple births, and offspring sex. The association with SGA became non-significant after adjustment for confounders. Regarding offspring growth trajectory, maternal dyslipidemia in the first trimester decreased lower growth trajectory (RR: 0.90, 95%CI: 0.84–0.97) and elevated higher growth trajectory (RR: 1.12, 95%CI: 1.01–1.24). However, these relationships were no longer significant after adjustment, with RRs of 0.99 (95%CI: 0.93–1.06) and 0.97 (95%CI: 0.88–1.08), respectively.

Discussion

This prospective observational study found that (1) first-trimester maternal supraphysiological dyslipidemia was associated with elevated maternal GDM and adverse birth outcomes, specifically PTB and LGA; and (2) maternal Lipid profiles during early pregnancy demonstrated no significant association with offspring growth trajectories from birth to 24 months.

Maternal lipid levels exhibited distinct physiological change during pregnancy. Compared with standard reference ranges for Chinese non-pregnant women, maternal lipid levels typically increased throughout pregnancy, reaching peak levels during the second or third trimester [16, 25]. This increase represented a physiological adaptation in response to the elevated energy requirements imposed by fetal development [26]. However, maternal supraphysiological dyslipidemia could raise adverse pregnancy outcomes risks. Previous research had reported the relationships between later-trimester maternal lipid profiles and GDM risk. A meta-analysis of 292 studies (n = 97,880) found GDM exhibited elevated TC, TG, LDL-C, and reduced HDL-C compared to non-GDM [27]. Among these studies, 151 reported second-trimester lipid data, 104 reported third-trimester lipid data, and only 52 reported first-trimester lipid data. The current study extended prior evidence by demonstrating that abnormal first-trimester lipid levels also elevated GDM risk. However, first-trimester maternal dyslipidemia showed no significant linked with GH risk. Consistent with these results, another prospective birth cohort (n = 5,690) also indicated first-trimester maternal lipid profiles were not related to GH [28].

The mechanisms by which dyslipidemia contributed to GDM risk remained complex and incompletely understood, but several lines of evidence supported this association. Elevated TG levels could deteriorate glucose homeostasis by the induction of peripheral insulin resistance (IR) and compromising beta cell function. Excessive fatty acids resulting from TG accumulation could activate serine/threonine kinases to inhibit the signaling insulin receptor by suppressing tyrosine phosphorylation and impairing insulin receptor substrate activation, subsequently leading to the development of peripheral IR [29]. Additionally, excess storage of TG could impair insulin secretion and trigger beta cell apoptosis via multiple causative pathways, including ceramide accumulation, reactive oxidative specie, inflammation, and endoplasmic reticulum stress [30].

Multiple studies had explored whether maternal dyslipidemia could increase adverse status in the offspring, both perinatally and throughout the life course. A meta-analysis including 11 studies (sample sizes ranging from 108 to 5,535) evaluated the relationship between maternal dyslipidemia and PTB risk, including 9 studies with second-trimester lipid data and 3 with first-trimester lipid data [31]. This meta-analysis showed a link between maternal dyslipidemia throughout gestation and an elevated PTB risk. Similarly, another meta-analysis involving 46 studies (n = 31,402) found that maternal dyslipidemia throughout pregnancy elevated LGA risk and reduced SGA risk [32]. However, most of studies in this meta-analysis also measured maternal lipid levels in the later trimesters. Corroborating these results, this present study suggested that first-trimester maternal dyslipidemia was related to increased risks of PTB and LGA. In contrast, limited studies further explored the impact of maternal dyslipidemia on offspring growth trajectories. A small macrosomia birth cohort (n = 293) found that no association between maternal dyslipidemia and longitudinal growth patterns in offspring weight and abdominal circumference from gestation through 5 years of age [33]. Similarly, in this large birth cohort (n = 10,215), first-trimester maternal dyslipidemia was not related to offspring WLZ growth patterns from birth to 24 months after adjustment for confounders.

The findings that maternal dyslipidemia in the first trimester could influence PTB and birth weight were biologically plausible. Evidence suggested that circulating lipids may contribute to oxidative stress and systemic inflammation, potentially leading to placental dysfunction or microvascular injuries that induced spontaneous PTB [34]. Furthermore, abnormal lipid levels may facilitate blood clot formation, subsequently inducing PTB through placental abruption [9]. Additionally, elevated circulating lipids raised fatty acid concentrations, thereby stimulating growth factors and interfering with hormones activity through competitive albumin binding. This competition raised the levels of free hormone, thereby affecting intrauterine fetal growth [35].

Strengths and limitations

The primary strengths stem from the TJBC, which boasted a substantial sample size and broad geographic coverage, encompassing six urban and five suburban regions of Tianjin. The cohort included extensive demographic, behavioral, and health data, as well as blood detection of both pregnant women and their children. This cohort has been monitored from maternal pregnancy (first, second and third trimesters) through delivery and into childhood (at 42 days postpartum, 6 months, and then annually up to 6 years), with ongoing monitoring. These features enhanced the reliability and representativeness of the findings. In addition, this study also held important insights for the development of public health strategies and the refinement of clinical guidelines. First-trimester maternal dyslipidemia was strongly related to maternal GDM, fetal PTB, and LGA risks. Accordingly, early identification and monitoring of women with dyslipidemia, together with the implementation of appropriate clinical interventions, may reduce adverse outcomes for both mothers and offspring.

However, the study had several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, lipid profiles were measured only once during gestation, precluding evaluation of lipid profiles change before conception, throughout pregnancy, and after parturition. Second, despite adjustment for numerous confounding factors, the potential influence of other residual factors, including dietary habits, exercise, and family history of dyslipidemia, cannot be excluded. Third, the relatively high median maternal age (29.9 years) in this cohort might be due to socioeconomic influences (e.g., education/career priorities) and national policy shifts (e.g., two- and three-child policies). Although maternal age was adjusted for in analyses, the external validity of these findings to populations with divergent age distributions required careful consideration.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this investigation indicated first-trimester maternal dyslipidemia was associated with increased maternal GDM risk, as well as offspring PTB and LGA, but not with maternal GH, offspring SGA, or offspring growth trajectories from birth to 24 months. These findings highlight that first-trimester maternal supraphysiological dyslipidemia warrants serious consideration as a risk factor for adverse pregnancy and birth status. Appropriate interventions, such as dietary modifications and exercise guidance, should be implemented to minimize the incidence of these adverse complications and enhance maternal and child health.
