Rare Neurosarcoidosis Involvements
Authors: Gülce Cansu Serin, Serhat Erol, Gökhan Çelik, Özlem Özdemir Kumbasar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNeurosarcoidosis is a rare manifestation of systemic sarcoidosis. Establishing a diagnosis can be challenging for clinicians, particularly in cases of isolated neurosarcoidosis. In this context, we aimed to present nine patients with rare manifestations of neurosarcoidosis.Keywords: magnetic resonance imaging, neurosarcoidosis, optic neuritis, sarcoidosis, treatment

Introduction

Neurosarcoidosis typically occurs as part of systemic sarcoidosis; however, isolated neurosarcoidosis cases have also been reported. Neurological involvement is observed in 5%–10% of patients with systemic sarcoidosis. The most common manifestation is cranial neuropathy, with the facial nerve being the most frequently affected (55%) [1]. Other neurological involvements are less common [2]. In a significant proportion of neurosarcoidosis cases, tissue diagnosis is obtained outside the nervous system. Cases are classified as possible, probable, or definite neurosarcoidosis according to clinical, radiological, and histopathological findings [3]. Establishing a diagnosis of neurosarcoidosis imposes a significant challenge. Herein, we present nine patients with rare manifestations of neurosarcoidosis.

Case Series

Among the nine cases, five had a previously established diagnosis of sarcoidosis, whereas four initially presented with neurological manifestations. Patients' age at diagnosis ranged from 22 to 49 years. Three were female.

Of the four patients with initial neurological presentations, two had neuroendocrine symptoms due to involvement of the pituitary gland and the mammillary bodies. One patient presented with epileptic seizures, and another with headache, vomiting, and dizziness secondary to hydrocephalus (Table 1A). One patient was referred after granulomatous inflammation was identified in the surgical specimen obtained for a pituitary lesion, and the endobronchial ultrasound‐guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS‐TBNA) performed based on chest X‐ray findings was also consistent with non‐caseating granulomatous inflammation. The other three patients were referred after bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy was detected on chest X‐rays obtained during the evaluation of neurological symptoms, and the diagnosis was established by EBUS‐TBNA (Figure 1).

Among the five patients with a prior diagnosis of sarcoidosis, neurosarcoidosis was suspected due to epileptic seizures in two patients, neuroendocrine symptoms related to pituitary involvement in one patient, Guillain–Barré syndrome in one patient, and vision loss secondary to optic neuritis in one patient (Table 1B). Neurological involvement was confirmed by imaging findings in four patients. One patient underwent surgery for an intracranial mass, which revealed granulomatous inflammation on histopathological examination (Figure 1).

All patients were treated with systemic glucocorticoids and additional second‐line immunosuppressive therapy. One patient with hydrocephalus was referred to our department 30 years ago after a ventriculoperitoneal shunt operation, and her disease was controlled with glucocorticoids monotherapy. Second‐line immunosuppressive therapy included methotrexate in four patients, mycophenolate mofetil in four patients, and azathioprine in one patient. Pulse steroid therapy was administered to one patient due to the development of optic neuritis. Third‐line therapy consisting of infliximab was required in two patients. One patient was treated with rituximab as fourth‐line therapy for neurosarcoidosis. Antiepileptic treatments were administered to patients with epileptic seizures, and hormone replacement therapy was given to those with neuroendocrine involvement (Tables 1A and 1B). Clinical improvement was observed in all patients.

Discussion

Cranial neuropathy is the most common presentation in neurosarcoidosis; however, patients can present with a broader spectrum of neurological involvements, including meningitis, hydrocephalus, neuroendocrine involvement, spinal cord injury, cerebral parenchymal lesions, peripheral nervous system involvement, and vascular‐related strokes [4]. Thus, neurosarcoidosis may mimic many other diseases.

From the clinical and diagnostic perspective, patients with suspected neurosarcoidosis may be classified as having possible neurosarcoidosis when magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), cerebrospinal fluid findings, and/or electromyography/nerve conduction study findings are compatible with granulomatous inflammation and alternative diagnoses have been excluded. When pathological evidence of systemic granulomatous disease consistent with sarcoidosis is present, the diagnosis of probable neurosarcoidosis may be considered. A definite diagnosis of neurosarcoidosis requires pathological confirmation consistent with neural or extraneural sarcoidosis. Obtaining pathological evidence can be particularly challenging in patients with isolated neurosarcoidosis [2]. Therefore, establishing a definitive diagnosis remains difficult in most neurosarcoidosis cases. In our series, only two of the neurosarcoidosis cases had tissue confirmation obtained from the nervous system. In the remaining patients, granulomatous inflammation was identified outside the nervous system, and the diagnosis of neurosarcoidosis was established based on compatible MRI findings and the exclusion of other possible causes.

A systematic review and meta‐analysis reported an overall neurosarcoidosis mortality rate of 5%, with rates ranging from 0% to 33% across studies. While remission rates have been reported to be 27% in published studies, incomplete remission and disease deterioration occur in approximately 38% of patients [1]. Therefore, neurosarcoidosis is a condition with an absolute indication for treatment [5, 6]. Disease control was achieved with treatment in all of our neurosarcoidosis cases.

Corticosteroids are the preferred first‐line therapy. Second‐line immunosuppressive treatment options include methotrexate (MTX), azathioprine, and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF). In cases of neurosarcoidosis refractory to first‐ and second‐line therapies, anti‐TNF agents, especially infliximab, are recommended as third‐line treatment [6]. Although the 2021 ERS sarcoidosis guidelines conditionally recommend methotrexate as second‐line therapy and infliximab as third‐line therapy for neurosarcoidosis, based on very low‐quality evidence, no randomised controlled trials demonstrate the superiority of any agents used in the second or third line [6]. While rituximab is recommended as a fourth‐line therapy for refractory pulmonary sarcoidosis in the ERS guidelines, evidence supporting its use in refractory neurosarcoidosis remains limited to clinical case reports [6, 7]. Treatment decisions are therefore based on a risk–benefit assessment guided by expert opinion and evidence from case series [6, 8]. Supportive/adjunctive treatments, such as antiepileptic drugs and hormone replacement therapy, may be administered accordingly [6]. In our series, MTX was the most frequently preferred second‐line treatment. MMF was used in cases where MTX‐related adverse effects occurred or in patients with pre‐existing liver disease. In the patient who developed optic neuritis, all therapeutic lines were required due to refractory pulmonary and cardiac involvement. During the optic neuritis attack, high‐dose intravenous pulse glucocorticoids therapy was administered.

In conclusion, neurosarcoidosis may cause significant morbidity and mortality. A comprehensive systemic evaluation is essential to enable early detection and timely initiation of treatment. Nevertheless, for the reasons mentioned above, establishing the diagnosis of neurosarcoidosis and formulating an appropriate treatment plan remain significant challenges for clinicians.
