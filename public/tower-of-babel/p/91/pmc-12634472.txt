Ozoile‐Stable Ozonides, Derived From Organic Extravirgin Olive Oil, Enhance Wound Healing Process in Patients With Diabetic Chronic Ulcers
Authors: Barbara De Angelis, Fabrizio Orlandi, Margarida Fernandes Lopes Morais D’Autilio, Stefano Mogavero, Ruggiero Marrano, Lorenza Marconi, Leonardo Palombi, Giovanna Scioli Maria, Augusto Orlandi, Paolo De Ninis, Lorenzo Secondi, Valerio Cervelli, Pietro Gentile
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe article describes the outcomes of a single‐centre investigation on the use of OZOILE to improve the healing process in patients with chronic diabetic ulcers. This is a non‐randomised interventional study which aims to assess the differences between two groups (total 200 patients) by evaluating healing time at 15, 30, 45, 60, 75 and 90 days. Pain assessment with VAS scale at 15, 30 and 45 days, biofilm test and tissue regeneration by histological evaluation were also taken into consideration. The outcomes show faster healing, reduced pain, control of local infection, aesthetically pleasing and qualitatively better healing. Our treatment strategy involves applying OZOILE detergent without rinsing, Ozoile spray oil and Ozoile cream followed by a non‐adherent dressing (paraffin gauze). This integrated protocol shows a safe and effective treatment for diabetic chronic wound healing in a cost‐effective manner. The group treated with the Rigenoma/Ozoile protocol demonstrated significantly superior outcomes compared to the control group, including accelerated healing, reduced pain levels, effective management of unpleasant odour, and high levels of compliance from both patients and surgeons. Overall, Ozoile markedly reduced healing time compared with standard care, with results robust across multiple analytical approaches.Keywords: diabetic chronic ulcers, extra virgin olive oil, ozone therapy, ozonised oil, wound care

Introduction

Diabetic ulcers are a major clinical challenge, as diabetes induces a persistent REDOX imbalance and chronic inflammation that severely impair wound healing. In this context, antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory treatments may help restore tissue homeostasis and promote ulcer repair.

Extra virgin olive oil (EVOO), rich in phenolic compounds such as hydroxytyrosol, has been shown to exert beneficial effects on skin repair. In animal models, diets enriched in EVOO enhanced wound closure, reduced oxidative stress, and modulated inflammatory cytokine expression, leading to improved tissue regeneration [1, 2]. These findings suggest that modulation of REDOX balance by EVOO and its components could play a therapeutic role in chronic wound management, including diabetic ulcers.

Beyond EVOO itself, ozone has attracted attention in wound care. Ozone is a highly reactive gas with potent oxidising and antimicrobial properties, and when stabilised in vegetable oils (e.g., olive, sesame, sunflower), it generates ozonides with enhanced biological activity [3, 4, 5]. Ozonated oils have demonstrated efficacy in diverse clinical fields, ranging from dermatology and wound healing to otolaryngology, ophthalmology and gynecology [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Preclinical and clinical studies reported accelerated wound closure, improved histological repair, and infection control following treatment with ozonated oils [4, 10, 11]. Importantly, the efficacy of ozonated oils appears closely related to both the oil substrate and the quality of the ozonation process.

Ozoile is a patented formulation of stable ozonides obtained by reacting ozone with the olefin bonds of fatty acids from organic EVOO. Recent investigations have defined its chemical composition and documented its biological activities, including the ability to promote re‐epithelialisation, stimulate fibroblast proliferation, enhance angiogenesis, reduce infection risk and mitigate inflammation and pain [12, 14]. Clinical studies have confirmed its safety and efficacy in chronic leg and venous ulcers, demonstrating significant improvements in lesion size, re‐epithelialisation time, pain and infection control [4, 15].

Building on these premises, the present study investigates the effect of Ozoile in the treatment of diabetic leg ulcers, a setting in which impaired healing mechanisms make the search for effective therapeutic options particularly urgent.

Materials and Methods

This is a non‐randomised interventional study with historical control, conducted from January 2018 to December 2022, involving patients of both genders aged between 35 and 98 years who were diagnosed with well‐managed type 2 diabetes and affected by chronic leg ulcers (graded as 1B according to the Texas University Scale).

Before admission, informed consent and authorisation to use sensitive data were obtained from each patient. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration. The study protocol was part of a university master's degree project and was approved by the Ethics on Research Committee of the School of Medicine, “Tor Vergata” University, Rome, Italy (Rectoral Degree D.R. n. 1693/2016, registration number #27696). All patients received detailed oral and written information about the study, including the risks, benefits, and alternative therapies, and signed an informed consent form before any study procedures were performed.

The study group was composed of 100 patients (see Appendix S2) who received topical treatment with a combination of Ozoile‐based products, followed by an inert paraffin gauze dressing and bandaging. The products used are commercially available advanced wound dressings, not experimental formulations.

Specifically:

Ozoile detergent without rinsing is an advanced cleanser based on Ozoile, decyl glucoside, and lactic acid, with a pH of 4.2; it was applied to both the wound bed and perilesional skin to allow gentle and atraumatic cleansing.

Ozoile spray oil is a spray‐form primary dressing containing ozoile, thioctic acid, and melaleuca alternifolia leaf oil; it was applied directly to the wound bed to promote re‐epithelialisation.

Ozoile cream is a cream‐form primary dressing based on ozoile and sodium hyaluronate; it was applied to the perilesional area to support skin trophism and integrity.

The control group, also composed of 100 patients (see Appendix S2), matched by inclusion criteria, received standard care consisting of wound disinfection, paraffin gauze application, and bandaging, but without the use of Ozoile products.

In both groups, dressings were applied every 5 days. At each dressing change, wounds were cleansed with a sodium hypochlorite solution, and curettage was performed after the application of a 5% lidocaine hydrochloride cream.

Prior to initiating the protocol, all patients underwent wound swab cultures and antibiogram analysis to assess for microbial colonisation and resistance profiles.

Pain Assessment

Pain was assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), a validated self‐reported tool where patients indicate their level of pain on a 10 cm line, with 0 corresponding to minimal pain and 10 to the maximum pain perceived [16, 17, 18]. This method was chosen for its simplicity and reliability in clinical wound care settings. Individual pain scores are reported in Appendices S1 and S2.

Ulcer Area Measurement

Wound area measurements were performed at baseline (T0) and at each follow‐up visit: T1 (15 days), T2 (30 days), T3 (45 days), T4 (60 days), T5 (75 days), and T6 (90 days). The ulceration area was approximated to a rectangle, calculated by multiplying two linear dimensions (length × width) obtained using a sterile graduated ruler [19]. The unit of measurement was centimetres squared (cm2). The percentage reduction in ulcer area was then calculated at each time point.

A diagram illustrating this measurement method is provided in Figure 1.

Histological Study

Biofilm assay and tissue regeneration were assessed with histological exams: incisional punch biopsies of ulcers (3 mm in diameter) were obtained at baseline T0 and after 15, 30 and 45 days in the treated group (T1, T2 and T3, respectively). Microscopic evaluation of serial 4‐μm thick paraffin sections stained with haematoxylin and eosin and Masson's trichrome was performed in order to verify the healing process, and images were acquired by using a digital camera (E600 Eclipse, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical Analysis

Given that treatment assignment was not randomised, potential confounding was addressed through the study design and subsequent analysis. Initially, the inclusion criteria were restricted to patients with well‐managed type 2 diabetes who had chronic leg ulcers classified as Stage B1 according to the Texas University Scale—superficial ulcers not involving the tendon, capsule, or bone, with infection.

To further account for baseline differences between groups, a multivariable regression analysis was conducted. This analysis included, as covariates, ulceration area size, age, and sex, which are known prognostic factors. The primary outcome, Healing Time, was analysed using Cox Proportional Hazards Regression. To obtain a comprehensive estimate that considers all reasonably fitted models, Multimodel Inference through Model Averaging was conducted.

Finally, adjusted curves were plotted after estimating a Marginal Structural Model [20] with the g‐formula [21]. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis using the restricted mean survival time (RMST) method was performed to further validate the results.

Complete healing occurrences in the groups were analysed using a Bayesian Logistic Regression model, both with and without covariates, including mean ulceration area at T0, age and sex. A sensitivity analysis was conducted using Pearson's χ 2 test.

For the longitudinal outcome of ‘healed wound percentages at six fortnightly follow‐ups,’ a linear mixed effects regression model was employed to account for the repeated measures on each patient. The model incorporated ulceration area size at T0 as a covariate, the Treatment Group factor, and a second‐degree polynomial for time in days, along with their interactions as fixed effects. The random effects structure included intercepts and slopes for the second‐degree polynomial time, as well as their correlations for each patient. Additionally, weights were applied to address heteroscedasticity.

The R software was used [22].

However, these data do not rule out the possibility that the covariates act as confounding variables. Therefore, under the assumption that the above set is sufficient to guarantee treatment exchangeability (NUCA), they were incorporated to compute several Cox models and a Marginal Structural Model, with estimates obtained through the G‐formula method. Additional key assumptions required for identifiability include consistency—the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA) and absence of effect modification—and positivity, the latter ensured by categorising the continuous variables age and wound area.

Main Outcome: Healing Time

At first glance, the unadjusted cumulative events plot and its log‐rank test suggest a substantial advantage of the Ozoile treatment over the control group (Figure 6—left).

Kaplan–Meier unadjusted and adjusted cumulative events plot of healing time between two groups: control and treated.

However, since this is not a randomised study, it was essential to carefully investigate potential confounding factors. We therefore fitted multiple multivariable Cox regression models and systematically evaluated the proportional hazards (PH) assumption. Issues such as covariate functional form, model misspecification, and potential treatment–covariate interactions were considered [23, 24].

To avoid reliance on a single specification, we adopted a multimodel strategy: models were compared according to maximum log‐likelihood and corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc), which penalises excessive complexity. Because no single “best” model exists in this setting, we used Multimodel Inference to account for model uncertainty and provide robust estimates of the treatment effect [25, 26]. A detailed description of these analyses, including effect modification and smoothing approaches, is provided in Appendix S3.

A key limitation emerged: in the control group only eight healing events occurred, with no variance in healing times. Although the overall number of events was adequate, this lack of variability generated instability in complex models, especially those with multiple interactions or small stratified subgroups. Some models produced inflated hazard ratios (HRs) approaching implausibly large values (e.g., > 400 000). To ensure interpretability, we excluded models with log(HR) estimates > 5, which, despite higher likelihoods, yielded wide confidence intervals and limited practical value.

Across all admissible models, the treatment effect remained consistently strong. In the unadjusted analysis, the HR for Ozoile versus Control was 56.5 (95% CI: 25.9–123.4, p < 0.001). In the fully adjusted model, the conditional HR increased to 66.0 (95% CI: 30.0–145.1, p < 0.001), with a Nagelkerke R 2 of 0.697 (Table 2).

Target Trial Emulation

Finally, to complement the regression‐based approach, we applied a target trial emulation framework, which allows estimation of adjusted Kaplan–Meier curves interpretable in the same way as unadjusted curves. By applying the g‐formula, we obtained marginal survival estimates that, under the proposed identifiability assumptions, approximate the causal Average Treatment Effect (ATE). The resulting adjusted curves are shown in Figure 6 (right panel).

Sensitivity Analysis (RMST)

As a sensitivity analysis, we estimated the RMST over a 90‐day follow‐up. The unadjusted results confirmed the primary findings, with an RMST of 90 days in the Control group versus 67.5 days in the Ozoile group. After covariate adjustment, the RMST was 87 days for the Control group and 64 days for the Ozoile group. Among baseline covariates, only Ulceration Area reached statistical significance (coef = 0.078, p < 0.003). Despite differences in scale, both approaches consistently show that larger baseline ulcerative areas are associated with delayed healing. Full details of the RMST analysis are available in Appendix S3.

Average Healing Time Gain in the Ozoile Group

As for the second research question—how much sooner Ozoile heals compared to the Control—no median estimate for the Control group could be determined, as less than 50% of the Control group patients achieved complete healing by the end of the follow‐up period. Extrapolating from the observed data, one might estimate a median difference in complete healing time of approximately 2 months. However, this rough visual approximation implicitly assumes that all patients are capable of achieving complete healing, which contrasts with the clinical definition whereby wounds persisting beyond 90 days are considered chronic.

Histological Findings

Discussion

Chronic wounds pose a challenge to both the surgeon and the patient, as they entail significant socio‐economic repercussions. Therefore, it is essential to comprehend the underlying mechanisms behind the formation of these injuries to establish a rationale for their treatment [28].

Any wound, regardless of its origin, results in a discontinuity in our skin barrier, enabling foreign bodies to penetrate our tissues and organs. Hence, it is imperative to manage the wound optimally to ensure timely healing [29, 30]. An acute wound is characterised by swift healing, facilitated by a process spanning 3–14 days, involving successive phases: coagulation, inflammation, cell proliferation, and tissue remodelling [31]. Macrophage/monocyte cells play a pivotal role in this intricate tissue repair mechanism [32]. Demonstrating significant plasticity, these cells provide the requisite regenerative stimulus at the appropriate time [33]. Indeed, they are the primary regulators of physiological tissue regeneration across our body [34], operating in both acute and chronic wounds [35] by orchestrating tissue regenerative processes through signalling to other cells (fibroblasts, endothelial cells, lymphocytes, etc.). Their crucial functions can be summarised as follows [36, 37]: promotion of arteriogenesis and angiogenesis [38], release of growth factors and cytokines, activation of resident MSCs (mesenchymal stem cells), polarisation of M1/M2 macrophages.

Every chronic wound originates from an acute wound that, instead of progressing through a normal healing process, undergoes chronicity due to an imbalance in the mechanisms responsible for physiological tissue regeneration. A wound is defined as chronic when it fails to heal within 12 weeks [39]. Several factors contribute to the progression from an acute to a chronic wound. Major contributors include advanced age, where the inadequate inflammatory response impedes tissue healing; diabetes, which alters physiological regenerative processes and collagen production; vascular changes (arterial and venous), reducing nutrient supply to the injury site; and the role of infections that sustain chronic inflammation, preventing the resolution of the inflammatory response seen in normal wound healing [40, 41]. In diabetic patients, macrophages exhibit altered functionality, producing pro‐inflammatory cytokines, reduced phagocytic activity against pathogens, and diminished capacity for polarisation and vasculogenesis/arteriogenesis. These alterations predispose individuals to ulcer formation, transitioning from acute to chronic wounds [42, 43].

Skin wound healing occurs through four distinct phases: haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and tissue remodelling, each characterised by the dominance of different cell types, culminating in tissue regeneration [44]. Successful physiological healing requires all four phases to function adequately. If any phase fails, the skin wound healing process is compromised, leading to the evolution of an acute wound into a chronic one. In this context, the role of macrophages is crucial. Due to their dynamic plasticity, they play a significant role in both the initiation and resolution phases of the inflammatory process, mediating tissue destructive as well as reparative actions [45].

During the hemostatic phase, circulating platelets and coagulation factors accumulate at the wound site, triggering the recruitment of mononucleated polymorphs, initiating the inflammatory phase [46]. Subsequently, circulating monocytes differentiate into macrophages and dendritic cells within the wound. Throughout wound evolution, macrophages transition from an inflammatory to an anti‐inflammatory phenotype [47]. This shift involves differential production of cytokines and chemokines within the wound [48]. Macrophages with an inflammatory phenotype secrete cytotoxic and pro‐inflammatory factors such as IL‐1B, TNF‐α, and IL‐6, resulting in tissue destruction via the production of radical oxygen species (ROS) [49]. Conversely, macrophages with an anti‐inflammatory phenotype primarily produce anti‐inflammatory factors and tissue growth factors like IL‐10 and TGF‐β, maintaining a role in the clearance of apoptotic cells [50].

One hypothesis explaining the macrophage phenotype switch suggests that the transition from the inflammatory to the anti‐inflammatory state is related to the phagocytosis of PMNs or other apoptotic immune cells. These dying cells produce signalling molecules, such as lysophosphatidylcholine, attracting macrophages via G2A receptor chemotaxis [51]. Upon phagocytosis, they induce macrophages to release growth factors like TGF‐β and VEGF, stimulating tissue repair [52]. In disease states, the phenotypic switch from a pro‐inflammatory to an anti‐inflammatory state may be delayed or not occur at all, hindering the transition from the inflammatory to the resolving phase of the healing process [53].

Following the resolution of the inflammatory phase, the proliferative phase commences. Here, fibroblasts and other cells reconstruct the extracellular matrix (ECM) by depositing collagen fibres, initially type III and subsequently type I [54]. The final phase of the healing process is tissue remodelling, where the extracellular matrix expands, and tissue strength is restored [55].

Normally, the skin and mucous membranes of human beings are colonised by different groups of microorganisms, which can be divided into two groups: resident, non‐pathogenic flora that plays a protective role from colonisation by pathogenic species, and transient flora, which can consist of pathogenic or non‐pathogenic microorganisms, and it is present for periods ranging from hours to weeks on the skin or mucous membranes. The most common bacterial species are Staphylococcus aureus (37%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (17%), Proteus mirabilis (10%), Escherichia coli (6%), and Corynebacterium spp. (5%) [56].

When skin or mucosal injury occurs, resulting in the loss of barrier function, pathogenic microorganisms penetrate the wound. However, in the absence of local and systemic predisposing factors, and with proper wound hygiene, wound infection and evolution to chronic wounds usually do not occur [57]. Instead, the same pathogenic microorganisms that colonise the wound can organise into polymicrobial aggregates, encapsulated within a protective matrix of extracellular polymers (biofilm), conferring pathogen resistance to antibiotics and immune defenses [58]. It has been estimated that at least 65% of chronic wound infections are closely associated with the presence of biofilms [59]. The microbial profile obtained from a wound culture swab influences the prognosis of the wound [60].

Most chronic wounds contain multiple bacterial species that produce a synergistic effect, resulting in the virulence of otherwise non‐virulent bacterial species causing injury [61]. The same factors influencing wound chronicity also promote the development of infection. In chronic wounds, the microorganisms most commonly involved in biofilm production are Pseudomonas aeruginosa , Staph spp., and E. coli [62].

Biofilm represents a mechanism of bacterial resistance against the immune system and antibiotics. The latter act particularly on metabolically active bacterial cells involved in the production of proteins or nucleic acids with bacteriolytic and/or bacteriostatic effects. However, in the cell aggregates forming the biofilm, in addition to metabolically active cells, dormant cells are also recognised, generally expressing antibiotic resistance [63]. Additionally, the matrix that composes the biofilm acts as a barrier to drugs and the immune system, reducing the availability of bacterial cells to combat infection [64]. Antibiotic resistance within the biofilm is strongly favoured by a horizontal genetic exchange mechanism occurring between various bacterial species within the biofilm. Species that have developed antibiotic resistance can transfer resistance‐determining information, contained in the genetic code in the form of plasmids, to other bacterial species present within the biofilm that acquire these plasmids [65].

Wound infection plays a crucial role in tissue regeneration's evolution. Bacteria, both in planktonic and biofilm forms, employ strategies to evade the host immune system early and induce a form of tolerance. This particularly occurs in chronic wounds with biofilm [66]. From what has been stated, therefore, it is clear that a winning strategy is to ensure the perfect functioning of the barrier at the epidermal level in order to avoid the attachment of pathogenic microorganisms capable of forming the biofilm that is difficult to eradicate. The application of Ozoile ensures an excellent barrier effect by preventing the attack of pathogenic microorganisms on lesions and ulcers, thus ensuring rapid healing. The barrier effect of Ozoile has proven useful in protecting against pathogen attacks through mechanical action and by creating an environment hostile to their proliferation.

In the absence of targeted treatments to prevent the formation of biofilm and subsequent dramatic infections, the lesions become persistent and difficult to treat, so much so that the only effective form of treatment against biofilm is surgical wound bed dressing because it facilitates the active and efficient removal of cellular/tissue debris, along with bacteria [67].

The opposite convexities observed in the wound area reduction curves may indicate a qualitative difference in the recovery trajectories of the two groups. One possible explanation is that Ozoile might stimulate a stronger initial immune and reparative response, thereby accelerating the healing process and enabling ulcers that would otherwise remain chronic to evolve along an acute‐like trajectory. However, an alternative explanation cannot be ruled out: the apparent divergence may simply reflect residual confounding due to the intrinsic distinction between “healers” and “non‐healers,” a phenomenon repeatedly reported in the literature [27] and independent of treatment allocation. In this perspective, Ozoile could have been administered to a subgroup with a higher baseline healing potential, despite the formal comparability of the two cohorts. These competing interpretations highlight the need for cautious evaluation of our results, particularly in the absence of precise baseline measures of comorbidities, which would be necessary to definitively disentangle a treatment effect from underlying patient heterogeneity.

In addition to the theoretical rationale outlined above, our clinical findings support the beneficial role of Ozoile‐based products in the management of chronic leg ulcers. Patients in the treated group showed a progressive reduction in pain intensity, as measured by the VAS. Histological analysis confirmed a decrease in inflammatory infiltrate and signs of active tissue regeneration. No adverse reactions were reported, indicating good tolerability of the treatment protocol.

Although microbiological cultures were limited to pre‐treatment analysis, the clinical absence of superinfection or exudate during treatment suggests that the barrier properties and antiseptic potential of the Ozoile formulations contributed to microbial control.

These results, while preliminary, are promising and should be further validated in larger, randomised controlled trials. Nevertheless, they strengthen the hypothesis that a topical therapeutic approach combining cleansing, anti‐inflammatory action, and skin trophism support can be effective in promoting wound healing in complex patients such as those with diabetes and vascular impairment.

Conclusion

Ozoile‐based products exhibit high activity by means of mechanical effects. The barrier effect has proven to be a successful strategy that promotes rapid healing of treated lesions. Within its limitations, this study suggests that Ozoile is effective in counteracting biofilm formation, promoting and accelerating tissue regeneration, facilitating the removal of necrotic and fibrinous debris, and reducing pain compared with the control treatment. The Ozoile‐based products are user‐friendly and adaptable for both healthcare providers and patients. The mechanisms of action at the ultrastructural level require further exploration, particularly in their application to deeper wounds involving exposure of soft or hard tissues.
