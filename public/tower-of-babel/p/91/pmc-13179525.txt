Hepatitis B Surface Antigen Loss and Improved Clinical Outcomes in US Individuals With Chronic Hepatitis B Virus Infection
Authors: Myriam Drysdale, Rose Chang, Shuang Wang, Anna Coutinho, Vera Gielen, Theo Man, Rui Song, Mei Sheng Duh, Mandana Khalili, Dickens Theodore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) loss is associated with improved clinical outcomes in patients with chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection based on studies predominantly in Asian populations. We assessed real‐world associations between HBsAg loss and long‐term clinical outcomes in a diverse US population with chronic HBV infection. This retrospective cohort study (January 2012–December 2019) used Optum electronic health records data from patients with chronic HBV infection, identified via diagnosis codes or laboratory assessments. Patients with advanced liver disease and hepatitis C, hepatitis D or HIV co‐infection at baseline were excluded. Associations between HBsAg loss, as a proxy of functional cure, and clinical outcomes were assessed using doubly robust inverse probability of treatment‐weighted Cox proportional hazards models. In total, 15,760 patients with chronic HBV infection were included; 5.6% were receiving stable antiviral treatment. Overall, 4.2% (n = 667) experienced HBsAg loss across the study period. HBsAg loss was associated with an 89% and 62% reduced risk of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC; adjusted hazard ratio [aHR]: 0.11, 95% CI: 0.01–0.76) and all‐cause mortality (ACM; aHR: 0.38, 95% CI: 0.20–0.74), respectively. Risks of compensated cirrhosis (aHR: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.52–1.35) and decompensated liver disease (aHR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.37–1.48) were lower after HBsAg loss, but did not reach significance. In this large, diverse population of patients with chronic HBV infection without advanced liver disease, HBsAg loss was associated with reduced risks of HCC and ACM compared with those without HBsAg loss.Keywords: all‐cause mortality, compensated cirrhosis, decompensated liver disease, diverse, functional cure, hepatocellular carcinoma

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection poses a major public health concern, affecting 254 million people worldwide in 2022 [1]; the estimated prevalence of HBV in the United States was approximately 1.6 million in 2022 [2]. The non‐US‐born population in the United States is disproportionately impacted; the highest proportion of chronic HBV infections is seen among people born in Asia, the Caribbean and Africa [3]. Liver complications (liver cirrhosis, liver failure and hepatocellular carcinoma [HCC]) are common causes of HBV‐related death; approximately 15%–40% of individuals with chronic HBV infection progress to cirrhosis, liver failure, or HCC [4]. In 2023, 1769 HBV‐related deaths were reported in the United States [5].

Chronic HBV infection is defined as the persistence of serum hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) for ≥ 6 months [6, 7]. High levels of HBsAg exhaust T‐cell immunity against HBV, resulting in failure to clear the infection [8]. Guidelines recommend managing chronic HBV infection with nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs) or pegylated interferon‐α (PEG‐IFN), with the primary therapeutic goal of suppressing viral replication [7]. The ideal goal of therapy is functional cure (FC), defined as sustained HBsAg loss and HBV DNA less than the lower limit of quantitation (< 10 IU/mL) at 24 weeks off‐treatment [9]. HBsAg seroclearance is infrequent but can occur spontaneously (~1%–2% per year but not at a linear rate) [10, 11] or after treatment (PEG‐IFN, 2%–8%; NA, < 1%–4%) [12, 13, 14]. HBsAg loss observed after NA cessation [15], may be due to reactivation of the host immune system.

The low rate of seroclearance despite treatment is primarily due to the persistence of covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) in the hepatocyte nucleus, as well as HBV DNA that has integrated into the host genome [9]. PEG‐IFN has a complex mode of action involving T cells, resulting in degradation of cccDNA, while NAs inhibit HBV polymerase activity but do not prevent de novo cccDNA formation in newly infected cells [16]. As a result, new hepatocytes may become infected, perpetuating the viral cccDNA reservoir [16].

New HBV therapies are being developed to achieve FC, and HBsAg loss is a key endpoint of ongoing clinical trials [9]. However, there are limited data on the impact of HBsAg loss on HBV outcomes across different US populations in the real‐world setting. We conducted a comparative retrospective cohort study to assess the association between HBsAg loss and long‐term clinical outcomes among an ethnically diverse US cohort of adults chronically infected with HBV, the majority of whom were not receiving antiviral therapy.

Methods

Data Source

The Optum electronic health record (EHR) database is one of the broadest longitudinal clinical repositories in the United States (> 80 million patients), with an average of 40 months of observed data per patient. Data elements include patient demographics, medications prescribed and administered, immunisations, comprehensive laboratory results, microbiology results, vital signs and other observable measurements, clinical and inpatient stay administrative data, coded diagnoses and procedures, and all‐cause death (the cause of death was not provided).

Study Outcomes

The following clinical outcomes were assessed: compensated cirrhosis, decompensated liver disease, HCC and all‐cause mortality. The list of ICD codes used for the study outcomes and key variables is available in Tables S2 and S3.

Data Analysis

A Cox proportional hazards model using stabilised inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was employed to assess the association between HBsAg loss (loss vs. no loss) and time to each clinical outcome of interest. IPTW was applied to control for confounding and was estimated via propensity score logistic regression with HBsAg loss as the dependent variable. The propensity score models included: (1) demographics and time since the date of chronic HBV infection diagnosis; (2) Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) during the 12‐month baseline period [17]; (3) clinical comorbidities during the 12‐month baseline period or at any time prior to the index date and (4) HBV treatment status at index. A full list of patient characteristics is presented in Table 1. The distribution of baseline patient characteristics was compared between the weighted HBsAg loss and no HBsAg loss cohorts using standardised differences, with a standardised difference of > 10% considered to indicate meaningful imbalance [18]. Covariates that were imbalanced after weighting were included in the Cox model in a doubly robust approach. Crude and weighted hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were generated.

Results

In total, 15,760 patients with chronic HBV infection were included in the study. Of these, 667 patients (4.2%) experienced HBsAg loss, with a mean time from date of chronic HBV infection to loss index date of 21 months; 15,093 (95.8%) experienced no HBsAg loss (Figure 2). The mean (standard deviation [SD]) number of HBsAg tests was 1.3 (2.8) and 1.1 (5.2) per person per year in the HBsAg loss and no‐loss groups, respectively. The median follow‐up was 25 months for both groups. Most patients were untreated at the index date (HBsAg loss patients, 91.5% [n = 610]; no HBsAg loss patients, 90.5% [n = 13,665]). Treatment was received by 12.0% of Asian patients, 9.1% of White patients, 7.9% of other/unknown race patients and 6.2% of African American patients. Among the overall cohort, 888 patients (5.6%) were receiving stable NA treatment as of the index date; of these, 33 patients (4.9% of HBsAg loss population) experienced HBsAg loss and 855 patients (5.7% of no HBsAg loss population) did not.

Patient Characteristics at Baseline

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

Prior to IPTW and Cox regression adjustments, HBsAg loss patients were older than the no HBsAg loss patients (mean age of 49 vs. 45 years at index; Table 1). When comparing those with and without HBsAg loss, a higher proportion of those with HBsAg loss were White (44.2% vs. 20.1%), whereas a higher proportion of those without HBsAg loss were Asian (44.7% vs. 25.8%). Similar percentages of patients in the HBsAg loss and no HBsAg loss populations were African American (18.9% vs. 19.1%) or female (52.2% vs. 56.2%). HBsAg loss patients had a slightly higher mean CCI score (1.8 vs. 1.5) compared with the no HBsAg loss patients. A higher proportion of HBsAg loss patients had a history of hypertension (32.1% vs. 17.8%), obesity (31.5% vs. 21.0%), hyperlipidaemia (23.1% vs. 15.0%), type 2 diabetes mellitus (13.8% vs. 8.5%), a history of non‐HCC cancer (9.1% vs. 5.7%) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) on dialysis (6.9% vs. 1.3%) compared with the no HBsAg loss patients. A numerically greater percentage of HBsAg loss patients had evidence of steatotic liver disease (6.6% vs. 4.3%) and evidence of alcohol‐use disorder (4.3% vs. 1.6%) vs. no HBsAg loss patients (Table 1).

Biomarkers

For most patients (> 90%) in both populations, the most recent HBeAg status prior to index was non‐positive (including negative, indeterminate or missing). HBV DNA levels were undetectable/missing at index in 77.8% (53.4%/24.4%) of the HBsAg loss population and 42.3% (15.9%/26.4%) of the no HBsAg loss population. The median HBV DNA level in the HBsAg loss and no HBsAg loss groups was 19.0 IU/mL and 230.0 IU/mL, respectively. At baseline, 14.2% of the HBsAg loss population and 22.4% in the no HBsAg loss population had ALT values ≥ 1 × upper limit of normal (ULN), respectively. More patients with no HBsAg loss had ALT levels between 1 and < 2 × ULN than HBsAg loss patients (16.3% vs. 8.4%). However, the proportion of patients with ALT values of 2–< 5 × ULN and ≥ 5 × ULN was similar in the HBsAg loss and no HBsAg loss populations (4.2% vs. 4.5% and 1.6% vs. 1.5%, respectively).

Following application of standardised IPTW, most characteristics were well balanced between the HBsAg loss and no‐loss groups, accounting for known or measured confounding (Table 1).

Association Between HBsAg Loss and Clinical Outcomes

The number of events and crude incidence rates (IRs, per 1000 person‐years) for compensated cirrhosis, decompensated liver disease, HCC and all‐cause mortality in the HBsAg loss and no‐loss groups are reported in Table S4.

In the primary analysis, HBsAg loss (defined as ≥ 1 negative HBsAg test following diagnosis) was associated with an 89% reduced risk of HCC (adjusted HR [aHR]: 0.11, 95% CI: 0.01–0.76) and a 62% reduced risk of all‐cause mortality (aHR: 0.38, 95% CI: 0.20–0.74) (Figure 3). Patients with HBsAg loss also had a lower but non‐significant risk of compensated cirrhosis (aHR: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.52–1.35) and decompensated liver disease (aHR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.37–1.48). Sensitivity Analyses

Using a more restrictive definition of HBsAg loss (two consecutive negative HBsAg test results at least 6 months apart), the HBsAg loss population included 224 patients (1.42% of the overall study population); only 17 of these patients (7.6%) were treated with NA. Weighted and adjusted results showed a consistently reduced risk of all four clinical outcomes among the HBsAg loss patients (defined as two consecutive negative HBsAg tests ≥ 6 months apart) compared with the no HBsAg loss group, though only the association for all‐cause mortality remained statistically significant (aHR: 0.12, 95% CI: 0.05–0.32). The risk of HCC (aHR: 0.59, 95% CI: 0.08–4.34), compensated cirrhosis (aHR: 0.75, 95% CI: 0.42–1.35) and decompensated liver disease (aHR: 0.71, 95% CI: 0.31–1.63) appeared lower after HBsAg loss but did not reach significance, reflecting the smaller exposed cohort and fewer outcome events (Figure S1).

Discussion

In this large, US‐based, real‐world, retrospective, longitudinal cohort study of patients with chronic HBV infection in an ethnically diverse population with a median follow‐up of 25 months, HBsAg loss was found to be associated with a decreased risk of HCC and all‐cause mortality. A reduced risk of compensated cirrhosis and decompensated liver disease was also observed, although these results were not significant. These observations are consistent with previous literature in predominantly non‐US cohorts [19, 20, 21].

Serum HBV DNA levels have previously been identified as an independent predictor of HCC risk, regardless of serum ALT levels or the presence of liver cirrhosis [22, 23]. Although the precise mechanisms underlying HBV‐induced HCC remain unclear, viral load may act as a potential effect modifier [24, 25]. HBsAg loss, representing persistent suppression of viraemia with sustained immune control of HBV infection, is considered a favourable outcome for patients with chronic HBV infection and is a key component of FC [8, 9]. HBsAg has also been observed to have direct oncogenic potential, further supporting the potential therapeutic benefits of achieving HBsAg loss in HBV‐associated HCC [26]. Indeed, antiviral treatment‐induced HBsAg seroclearance has been associated with a lower risk of HCC occurrence than spontaneous clearance, suggesting the protective effect of treatment‐induced HBsAg loss [11]. Moreover, in our study, persistent HBsAg loss was significantly associated with a reduction in all‐cause mortality, as well as a non‐significant reduction in the risk of HCC and liver disease progression.

Hepatic necroinflammation and injury may be key factors contributing to hepatic events in patients with chronic HBV infection achieving HBsAg loss. Patients experiencing long‐term inhibition of HBV replication over HBsAg loss may have the benefit of reduced liver inflammation and risk of progression to cirrhosis [27]. Consequently, relatively short‐term or intermediate liver‐related outcomes, such as compensated cirrhosis and decompensated liver disease, may not be significantly mitigated by HBsAg loss, especially when seroclearance happens later in the disease course. A numerically reduced risk of compensated cirrhosis and decompensated liver disease was identified in the current study. The lack of a significant association may be attributed to a small effect size or inadequate power [28]. Cirrhosis diagnosed soon after HBsAg loss may not be truly caused by HBsAg loss but is likely due to the timing of diagnosis, thus leading to a more conservative effect estimate of HBsAg loss [20].

We identified a number of baseline characteristics that were potentially different between the HBsAg loss and no HBsAg loss groups; patients in the HBsAg loss group were numerically older, more likely to be White and less likely to be Asian and lower proportions had abnormal ALT and HBV DNA levels than patients in the no HBsAg loss group. These findings are consistent with those reported for a cohort of North American patients from the Hepatitis B Research Network with chronic HBV infection [29]. Lower treatment rates have been reported previously among Asian patients with chronic HBV infection in Western US regions than in non‐Asian or Asian patients from Eastern US regions [30]. Additionally, HBV genotype C, which is associated with more accelerated disease progression, is more common among Asian than non‐Asian patients [31]. However, we observed that a greater proportion of Asian than non‐Asian patients received treatment. Although we were unable to account for the potential impact of genotype, race was included as a variable in the propensity score model; the distribution of race was well balanced between the HBsAg loss and no HBsAg loss cohorts (standardized mean difference < 10%). The lowest proportion of treated patients by race in the current study was among African Americans. While African American or Black participants have been reported to be less likely to meet treatment criteria for chronic HBV infection, among those who did, the rates of treatment initiation do not differ significantly [32]. Additional studies may be warranted to investigate the racial disparities in treatments and FC of chronic HBV infection.

Increased serum ALT levels and fibrosis also indicate disease progression; however, the association between serum ALT levels and clinical outcomes in patients with HBV infection is ambiguous. Increased ALT levels as well as persistently normal ALT levels are observed in HBV‐infected patients [23, 33]. HBeAg levels and detectable HBV DNA were observed to be lower in the HBsAg loss group than in the no HBsAg loss group. HBeAg, together with high levels of HBV DNA, is reported as a marker of viral replication [23], possibly suggesting that patients in the HBsAg loss group were in a less active phase of infection compared with the no HBsAg loss group. While stratification of results by disease phase would be of interest, the small numbers of events in the HBsAg loss group (as reported in Table S4) would likely have resulted in disease‐phase subgroups with even smaller numbers of events and therefore limited statistical power. However, markers of disease phase (such as HBV DNA, HBeAg, HBsAg and ALT) were included in the IPTW and doubly‐adjusted outcome models to account for baseline disease activity.

Our study has several strengths. A racially, economically and geographically diverse population covering all parts of the United States from the Optum database was utilised, ensuring external generalisability. The use of propensity score approaches, creating a pseudo‐population with balanced characteristics, allowed for the adjustment of a large number of confounders. Additionally, the availability of laboratory data and structured clinical information allowed accurate identification of patients with chronic HBV infection as well as assessment of multiple potential confounders. These included adjustment of the association analysis for underlying conditions such as metabolic abnormalities, history of cancer and history of CKD on dialysis, which may increase the likelihood of receiving treatment for HBV and therefore achieving HBsAg loss. The Optum EHR database provided longitudinal individual records with a sufficient follow‐up period, enabling assessment of long‐term and more advanced disease stages.

Several potential limitations common to observational studies using real‐world data should be considered. Patients with severe liver disease were excluded from the study. Combinations of ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 codes for cirrhosis are reported to offer the highest sensitivity and specificity essential for the diagnosis of cirrhosis and cirrhosis‐related complications, but they can underestimate the true prevalence of the individual underlying disorder [34]. This could possibly contribute to misclassification. Future studies may benefit from incorporating alternative definitions that utilise biomarkers, fibrosis scans or other clinical assessments to enhance outcome accuracy. Change in exposure status based on potential subsequent seroreversion after the first HBsAg negative test may also have led to potential misclassification bias. However, the impact on the results was likely low given the low prevalence of seroreversion reported in published studies [35]. Furthermore, low baseline HBsAg levels may increase the likelihood of HBsAg loss [36], thereby impacting the clinical outcomes. However, quantitative HBsAg levels are not routinely measured in the United States; these laboratory results were therefore not available in the Optum EHR database, and could not be included in the adjusted models. We did adjust for other available markers of disease activity and prognosis (baseline HBV DNA, ALT, cirrhosis status, antiviral treatment), and results were qualitatively consistent. Additionally, potential misclassification bias for exposure was mitigated by performing a sensitivity analysis. Also, while baseline HBV DNA was well balanced after IPTW, an impact of residual compounding related to HBV viral suppression cannot be excluded. Lack of data on causes of death, as well as irregular data recording and potential lag in mortality reporting may have introduced misclassification bias. Other unmeasured baseline and/or time‐varying confounders such as HBV genotype may lead to residual confounding. In this study, only a small percentage of patients were identified as receiving stable NA treatment, which precluded subgroup analyses among patients treated with NA. This may have been due to the timing of assessment (at study index date) and the definition being restrictive; for example, patients receiving long‐term NA treatment may have stocks of drugs at home and may not need a new prescription every month, potentially underestimating the percentage of patients receiving stable NA treatment. It is also possible that EHR data may not capture all prescriptions or dispensing records, such as those dispensed outside the contributing health system network. Excluding patients with evidence of liver disease at baseline may also have contributed to the small proportion of treated patients, since these patients are more likely to be treated than those with no evidence of liver disease. Finally, the duration of follow‐up (median of 25 months) was relatively short despite a long observation period. Patients can be lost to follow‐up due to care migration, death or administrative censoring at the end of the study period. Database studies typically use long, variable observation periods to capture real‐world practice (rather than fixed follow‐up periods) in order to mitigate selection bias.

Conclusions

HBsAg loss was associated with a reduced risk of HCC and all‐cause mortality in a diverse population of patients with chronic HBV infection in the United States. Although the risks of compensated cirrhosis and decompensated liver disease also appeared lower following HBsAg loss, these were not statistically significant. Further research with a larger sample size targeting specific subgroups is warranted to gain a deeper understanding of the potential impact of HBsAg loss on these outcomes.
