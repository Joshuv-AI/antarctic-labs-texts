Introduction

Transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTR) is a systemic disorder characterized by the extracellular accumulation of amyloid fibrils. It is classified as either mutant (ATTRv, v for variant) or wild‐type (ATTRwt), based on the genetic sequence of the transthyretin (TTR) protein. ATTR results from age‐related failure of homeostatic mechanisms in ATTRwt or destabilizing mutations in ATTRv: the protein becomes structurally unstable, leading to the formation of misfolded intermediates that eventually aggregate and precipitate as amyloid. ATTRv typically affects the peripheral nervous system (PNS), both large and small fibers, and the cardiovascular system, to varying degrees depending on the type of TTR gene mutation; however, the phenotype is not always uniform, even among patients with the same mutation [1]. The clinical presentation of ATTRwt is dominated by cardiac symptoms, such as congestive heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, atrial fibrillation, and refractory arrhythmias [2]. Although ATTRwt was initially thought to be an exclusively cardiac disease (formerly known as senile cardiac amyloidosis), wild‐type TTR amyloid deposits can also be found in extra‐cardiac tissues, and most patients report a history of bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) or lumbar stenosis at diagnosis [3, 4]. Nevertheless, evidence of peripheral neuropathy, besides CTS, can still be found in ATTRwt patients [5, 6], but information on the prevalence of polyneuropathy and its association with amyloid deposition has not been adequately investigated.

Materials and Methods

Patients with a diagnosis of ATTRwt confirmed by cardiological investigation (echocardiography and cardiac scintigraphy with bone tracers) and a negative genetic test for the TTR gene mutation were prospectively evaluated. This multicentric study involved patients referred to the Agostino Gemelli Polyclinic and Sapienza University of Rome, and was carried out in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki's principles, after approval of the Territorial Ethics Committee Lazio Area 3 at the Polyclinic Foundation Agostino Gemelli IRCCS (protocol ID 5470). Data were collected between January 2023 and January 2024. Patients with diabetes or monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) were excluded, as their underlying condition was deemed a potential confounding factor capable of independently accounting for the observed neuropathy. Laboratory findings, including FT3, FT4, TSH, anti‐thyroid antibodies, serum vitamin B12 and folate, hepatic enzymes, creatinine, antinuclear antibodies (ANA), anti‐extractable nuclear antigens (ENA), anti‐DNA antibodies, anti‐neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), circulating C3 and C4, anti‐Hu antibodies, serologic tests for HBV, HCV and HIV, and screening for infections, malignancies, malabsorption, and systemic autoimmune disorders, ruled out other common causes of acquired neuropathy. To better investigate the frequency of neuropathy in this elderly population, we prospectively included a control group matched for age and gender among patients admitted to our ward for cerebrovascular disease, excluding those suffering from diabetes, MGUS, or with alcohol abuse, and a second control group with diabetic patients, both matched for age and gender. Patients underwent neurological examination and nerve conduction studies (NCS), but we did not investigate the presence of small fiber neuropathy. Sural nerve biopsy has been proposed to patients with confirmed neuropathy. Neuropathy was defined, according to NCS, by a reduction of sural nerve sensory action potentials according to our normative values [7]. Among the enrolled participants, patients who agreed to undergo sural nerve biopsy signed a specific informed consent form that authorized histopathological investigations aimed at detecting amyloid deposits or identifying other potential pathological mechanisms responsible for nerve damage.

Results

A total of 30 patients, 24 men and six women, were examined. The mean age at evaluation was 81.1 years (range 60–96; median 80.5; standard deviation 6.65; M/F 5/1). Sensory symptoms were reported by 10 patients (33%). The absence of lower‐limb distal tendon reflexes was present in 53% of cases. Orthostatic hypotension (OH), fulfilling the criteria of neurogenic OH, was detected in 7 patients (23%) with no other possible causes. Unilateral or bilateral CTS was present in 70% of cases. Considering NCS, a sensory axonal polyneuropathy was present in 18 patients (60%). In the control group (30 subjects matched for age and gender: age range 62–94, mean age 80.4 years, median 81, standard deviation 7.03, M/F 5/1), a sub‐clinical neuropathy, detected by NCS, was found in four patients (12.9%). We excluded patients with diabetes or impaired glucose tolerance based on glycosylated hemoglobin, MGUS, or with alcohol abuse. In the control group of diabetic patients (30 subjects matched for age and gender affected by type 2 diabetes mellitus: age range 66–93, mean age 80.6 years, median 80.5, standard deviation 6.65, M/F 5/1), a subclinical neuropathy, detected by NCS, was found in 10 patients (30%). Considering ATTRwt patients, other possible secondary causes of polyneuropathy were identified only in one case: a patient with cancer undergoing chemotherapy.

Demographic, clinical, and electrophysiological data of ATTRwt patients are summarized in Table 1. Among the patients with neuropathy, four agreed to undergo sural nerve biopsy: in two patients, histological examination revealed the presence of amyloid deposits (positive for Congo red staining) in the epineurium (Figure 1A,B,F), around the epineural vessels (Figure 1A,E), and more scantily in the endoneurium (Figure 1C,G). Semithin sections stained with Toluidine blue revealed an almost complete fiber loss in all four biopsies (Figure 1D). Direct immunofluorescence was performed on cryostat sections exclusively in patients with amyloid deposits positive for Congo red staining, using anti‐TTR sera (Dako‐Denmark), confirming the presence of transthyretin (Figure 1H). Congo red positive deposits exhibited green birefringence under polarized light (Figure 2A,B).

Discussion

ATTRwt is thought to be a common age‐related disease, as more than 10% of people over the age of 80 have wild‐type TTR deposition in post‐mortem studies [8]. The increasing aging of the population, combined with the availability of new non‐invasive diagnostic tools, such as bone tracer scintigraphy, and the growing awareness among the medical community, has led to a dramatic increase in the number of ATTRwt diagnoses in the developed countries [9]. In older patients, aging is associated with an increasing prevalence of osteo‐articular complications, such as lumbar stenosis and rotator cuff syndrome, and neuromuscular symptoms, including reduced distal limb sensation and neuropathic pain, which coincide with the increasing prevalence of ATTRwt. Concerning extra‐cardiac involvement, CTS is one of the most common manifestations of ATTRwt and is exceptionally common at the time of diagnosis, affecting 94% of patients [10]. Peripheral nerve involvement may extend beyond the presence of CTS: several patients affected by ATTRwt cardiomyopathy may develop slowly progressive neuropathy, which is usually milder than the typical progressive neuropathy associated with ATTRv, and without relevant motor involvement. However, the clinical relevance of ATTRwt neuropathy is still not well understood: the late onset of typical ATTRwt cardiomyopathy correlates with a high prevalence of multiple comorbidities associated with neuropathy, including impaired glucose tolerance and monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance, and older age in itself is a risk factor for the development of polyneuropathy [11, 12]. Furthermore, signs of neuropathy, such as the absence of distal tendon reflexes as well as lower limb sensory symptoms, may be common in the elderly population and can be caused by lumbar stenosis. Thus, the unclear pathogenesis of sensory neuropathy in elderly patients highlights the need for further exploration. Indeed, we investigated with NCS the possible involvement of the PNS in a group of ATTRwt patients. In line with previous studies, unilateral or bilateral CTS was confirmed as a common finding [9], but we also observed sensory neuropathy in more than half of the enrolled patients, further supporting the systemic nature of this disease. Although the prevalence of neuropathy increases in the elderly, in our ATTRwt cohort, we found a higher frequency (p = 0.0005) compared with an age and gender matched control group [13]. Furthermore, the frequency of neuropathy in our ATTRwt cohort was also higher (60% vs. 30%) when compared to a control group of diabetic patients, although not statistically significant (p = 0.0701).

In our study, we did not document the presence of small fiber neuropathy; therefore, it cannot be ruled out either in patients with minimal clinical changes and normal NCS, or in those with large fiber neuropathy, where it could represent an additional mechanism of damage caused by amyloid deposits.

We also found the presence of amyloid deposits in the nerve biopsy of two patients affected by ATTRwt, confirming a possible pathogenic link; on the other hand, in the remaining two cases subjected to nerve biopsy, we certainly cannot exclude such a link, considering that even in the hereditary forms (ATTRv) amyloid deposits may sometimes not be detected [1]. However, we can hypothesize, as for ATTRv, amyloid deposits in the proximal nerve segments or in the dorsal root ganglia, or a toxic effect of TTR [14].

In conclusion, sural nerve biopsy and histological confirmation of amyloid deposition in nervous tissue may confirm a causal relationship between ATTRwt and PNS involvement, where medical history, laboratory tests, and electrophysiological study alone may not be sufficient.
