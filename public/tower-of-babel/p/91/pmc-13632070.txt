A machine-learning-derived online prediction model for risk during the activity period in SLE patients: a retrospective historical cross-sectional study
Authors: Xuanhua Yu, Siyu Liu, Shiwei Yuan, Xuebing Lyu, Huhan Lin, Shanting Zeng, Weizhen Zhang, Anning Zhu, Huijuan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveGiven the challenges in predicting systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) disease activity and the limitations of existing assessment tools, this study aimed to integrate multidimensional clinical and laboratory indicators to develop and validate a machine learning-based predictive model for SLE disease activity. It further sought to explore the association and potential mechanisms linking N-acetylglucosamine-1-phosphotransferase (NAG1) to SLE disease activity, thereby offering clinical risk assessment tools and identifying novel therapeutic targets.MethodsClinical data from 201 SLE patients were retrospectively collected. Patients were stratified according to Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) scores. Core predictive variables were selected using multivariate logistic regression, LASSO regression, and the Boruta algorithm. Seven machine learning algorithms were employed to construct predictive models, with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) used for model interpretation. The role of NAG1 and the mediating effect of fibrinogen (FIB) were analyzed using multivariate logistic regression, restricted cubic splines, and a quasi-Bayesian approach. Finally, a nomogram and an interactive dynamic risk calculator were developed based on the optimal model.ResultsJoint involvement, renal involvement, fibrinogen (FIB), immunoglobulin G (IgG), complement 3 (C3), and NAG1 were identified as core predictive indicators for SLE disease activity. The support vector machine (SVM) model demonstrated balanced predictive performance, while the logistic regression model was selected for nomogram construction due to its high predictive accuracy and interpretability. NAG1 was the most critical predictive factor; elevated NAG1 levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of SLE disease activity, with a more pronounced correlation in high-risk subgroups, such as patients positive for anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) or with low C3 levels. FIB mediated 9.3% of the pro-flare effect of NAG1. The constructed model and supporting tools showed favorable clinical applicability. Study limitations include its retrospective design and limited sample size.ConclusionA predictive model for SLE disease activity, based on six core indicators, was successfully developed and validated. The logistic regression model demonstrated excellent performance. The nomogram and dynamic risk calculator enable non-invasive, personalized risk assessment for SLE disease activity. As a key predictive factor, NAG1 may act as a pathogenic mediator in SLE, and its mediating relationship with FIB suggests novel crosstalk between metabolic dysregulation and the coagulation-inflammation pathway. This model can facilitate early risk stratification and individualized management of SLE patients. NAG1 represents a promising biomarker for evaluating therapeutic response and predicting relapse in SLE, and may serve as a potential therapeutic target. Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by multisystem involvement and a loss of immune tolerance. It has a complex pathogenesis and exhibits significant heterogeneity in its clinical manifestations [1]. A core challenge in the clinical management of SLE is the alternation between disease disease activity and remission. disease activity not only accelerate organ damage and reduce quality of life but are also closely associated with poor prognosis [2]. Accurate prediction of SLE disease activity is therefore crucial for enabling early intervention, optimizing therapeutic strategies, and improving long‑term patient outcomes. Currently, tools such as the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) are widely used in clinical practice to assess disease activity. These tools primarily rely on clinical symptoms, signs, and a limited set of laboratory parameters [3]. However, immune dysregulation in SLE involves extensive disturbances in both the innate and adaptive immune systems. Existing assessment systems remain limited in their ability to reflect the underlying immunopathological mechanisms or to predict the risk of imminent disease activity [4]. Consequently, identifying biological markers that can more sensitively and specifically predict disease activity, and establishing robust prediction models based on these markers, has become an urgent priority in SLE research.

With the explosive growth of biomedical data and advances in computational science, machine learning algorithms have emerged as powerful tools for integrating high‑dimensional, heterogeneous clinical and omics data to construct predictive models. In the field of autoimmune diseases, previous studies have successfully used machine learning models to distinguish between healthy controls, rheumatoid arthritis, and SLE patients based on nailfold capillaroscopy image features, demonstrating the feasibility of constructing classification models using objective imaging data [5]. Nevertheless, studies that focus specifically on predicting SLE disease activity using easily accessible routine clinical and hematological indicators via machine learning remain scarce. Traditional statistical methods are limited in handling complex nonlinear relationships and interactions among multiple variables, whereas machine learning algorithms such as random forest and gradient boosting enable more effective feature selection and model construction. These approaches have shown promise in biomarker discovery for other diseases; for example, the random forest algorithm has been applied to screen for urinary metabolite markers of lupus nephritis (LN) (N. [6]). Therefore, systematically applying multiple machine learning algorithms to identify key predictors from multidimensional hematological and clinical indicators, and to establish and validate prediction models for SLE disease activity, holds significant methodological innovation and clinical translational potential.

Among numerous potential biomarkers, the function of N‑acetylglucosamine‑1‑phosphotransferase (NAG1) has attracted increasing attention. It is involved in glycosylation modification, cell signal transduction, and inflammatory regulation, leading to the reasonable hypothesis that it may be associated with SLE‑related pathological processes such as immune cell activation, cytokine production, or tissue damage repair. Investigating the association between NAG1 and SLE disease activity could not only identify a novel disease activity marker but also provide new insights into disease mechanisms. Furthermore, mediation analysis could be performed to examine whether NAG1 acts as a mediator between known immune or inflammatory pathways and SLE disease activity, thereby positioning it within a broader pathophysiological network. Such analysis would help clarify its mechanism of action and evaluate its potential value as a therapeutic target.

In summary, predicting SLE disease activity is a complex, multisystem, and multilevel challenge. The current trend is shifting from reliance on single biomarkers toward integrating multidimensional data—including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and clinical phenotypes—and building comprehensive prediction models using advanced computational biology methods [4; 7]. A two‑sample Mendelian randomization study has provided genetic evidence for the complex causal relationship between blood cell phenotypes and SLE risk, suggesting that dysregulation of specific immune cell functional responses may be an upstream driver of SLE pathogenesis, while the genetic risk of SLE may, in turn, feedback to modify cellular behavior [7]. This highlights the importance of incorporating both genetic susceptibility and dynamic immune cell functional phenotypes in predictive analyses. Against this background, the present study aims to conduct a retrospective clinical investigation, classifying SLE patients into active and inactive groups based on SLEDAI scores, and systematically collecting and integrating multidimensional hematological and clinical indicators. We will apply multiple machine learning algorithms to identify key predictors, construct flare prediction models, and perform cross‑validation, with the goal of providing an objective and accurate risk assessment tool for clinical use. Additionally, this study will explore the association between NAG1 and SLE disease activity and analyze its potential mediating effects, thereby revealing part of the biological basis of SLE disease activity from a novel perspective and offering a theoretical foundation for the future development of new therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Study ethics and design

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the People’s Hospital Affiliated to Fujian University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval No. 2024KY-67) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) assessment and data collection. The overall study workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1, comprising the following stages: application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, data collection, variable selection and integration, model development, performance evaluation, and interpretability analysis in the validation set.

Data collection and preprocessing

Clinical and hematological data were retrospectively collected from outpatients and inpatients treated in the Department of Rheumatology and Immunology at the People’s Hospital Affiliated to Fujian University of Traditional Chinese Medicine between January 2019 and January 2024. A total of 220 patients with a confirmed diagnosis of SLE were included.All included patients met the 2019 European League Against Rheumatism/American College of Rheumatology (EULAR/ACR) classification criteria for systemic lupus erythematosus.

Patients were stratified into inactive and active SLE groups based on the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI), using a cutoff score of 4. The SLEDAI assesses disease activity over the preceding 10 days, with scores ranging from 0 to 105. Specific manifestations are weighted as follows: seizures, psychosis, organic brain syndrome, visual disturbances, cranial neuropathy, lupus headache, cerebrovascular accident, and vasculitis each score 8 points; arthritis, myositis, urinary casts, hematuria, proteinuria, and leukocyturia each score 4 points. Disease activity is categorized as: 0–4 (inactive), 5–9 (mild), 10–14 (moderate), and ≥15 (severe).

Participants with a history of malignant tumors or major cerebrovascular events were excluded. Demographic information (sex and age) was obtained through electronic medical record review. The following laboratory parameters were collected: PTSEC, INR, APTT, TT, FIB, DD, FDP, BNP, troponin, PCT, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, IgA, IgM, IgG, complement 3, complement 4, WBC, Neu, Lym, Mon, Eos, Bas, RBC, Hb, HCT, MCV, MCH, MCHC, P-LCR, RDW, RDW/PLT, PLT, MPV, MPV/PLT, PCT, PDW, CRP, AST, ALT, BUN, Cr, UA, FT3, FT4, TSH, UCr, UAlb, UTRF, UIgG, α1-MG, NAG1, NAG1/CREA_U, UAlb/CREA_U, UIgG/CREA_U, UTRF/CREA_U, α1-MG/CREA_U, Renal_impairment, UR, 24h_UPQ, ANA, Anti-Sm, Anti-dsDNA (IFA), and Anti-dsDNA (ELISA).

Variable selection and model development

The dataset was partitioned into training and validation sets using five-fold cross-validation, with an 80:20 split.Three independent methods—multivariate logistic regression, LASSO regression, and the Boruta algorithm—were employed to screen baseline variables and identify potential predictors within the training set. Detailed methodologies for each approach are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Final variable selection was based on the intersection of significant features identified by all three methods.

Seven machine learning algorithms were implemented to construct prediction models: eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Artificial Neural Network (ANN), Decision Tree (DT), Logistic Regression, and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM). To enhance model stability and accuracy, 10-fold cross-validation was applied during training. Hyperparameter tuning was performed via grid search, with the optimal model for each algorithm selected based on the highest area under the receiver operati.

Model validation

Prediction models were trained on the designated training dataset, and the optimal model was subsequently evaluated on the validation cohort. Model performance was quantified using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, Cohen’s kappa, and the Youden index. Classification results were visualized using a confusion matrix.Decision curve analysis (DCA) was further employed to evaluate the model’s clinical applicability. DCA assesses the net clinical benefit of predictive models across a range of threshold probabilities, demonstrating their value in guiding clinical decisions [8, 9].

We evaluated both multivariate regression and machine learning algorithms to capture diverse data structures, including linear effects (logistic regression), nonlinear relationships (artificial neural networks [ANN], support vector machines [SVM]), and high-order feature interactions (tree-based ensemble models). Model selection emphasized not only discriminative ability but also calibration and clinical interpretability.

Model interpretation

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were used to quantify the contribution and correlation of each feature to the final classification outcome. Features with higher SHAP values were considered to exert a greater influence on model predictions. The derived feature importance scores were presented to facilitate interpretation of the optimal prediction model [10, 11]. Additionally, the Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) method was applied to provide supplementary instance-level interpretability [12–14].

Correlation study

This study investigated the association between NAG1 as a predictive factor and the occurrence of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) flare as the outcome. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to further examine their relationship and potential interactions across different age groups.Three logistic models were used to calculate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) of NAG1 for SLE flare risk:Model 1:NAG1, Age, fibrinogen (FIB), complement C3 (C3), Serositis.*Model 2: Model 1 + Anti-dsDNA (ELISA), Renal Involvement, Platelet count (PLT).Model 3:Model 2 + C-reactive protein (CRP), 24-hour urine protein (UP_24h), White blood cell count (WBC).

Interaction analysis was performed to explore the potential mediating effect of FIB between the independent variable (NAG1) and the binary outcome. We decomposed the total effect of NAG1 on the outcome into a direct effect and an indirect effect (mediated by FIB) to quantify the proportion of the association explained by the proposed mediator. The dose-response relationship was visualized using restricted cubic splines, with knots placed at the 3rd quantile.

Mediation analysis was performed using a quasi-Bayesian approach with 1000 simulations to estimate the total, direct, and indirect effects, along with the mediation proportion. Confidence intervals were calculated using the quasi-Bayesian Monte Carlo method.

Construction and visualization of the nomogram

A clinical prediction model was developed to estimate the probability of an SLE flare. The selected variables were incorporated into a logistic regression model fitted on the training dataset. A risk nomogram was constructed using the `nomogram` function to visually represent the contribution of each variable and provide a personalized risk assessment, with appropriate axis length control. Predicted probabilities were derived from the linear predictor via the logistic function (`plogis`) and were calibrated across a wide range of thresholds (0.001 to 0.999) in the validation set.An interactive dynamic nomogram was implemented using the `regplot` and `DynNom` packages. This web-based risk calculator allows clinicians to input patient-specific parameters to immediately obtain a personalized prediction of SLE flare risk.ng characteristic curve (AUC) in the training data.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), based on their distribution. Between-group comparisons for normally distributed variables were performed using Student’s T-test, while the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, with between-group differences assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P-value < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Version 27.0 (IBM Corp.) and R software Version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). SHAP and LIME analyses were performed in Python Version 3.10.4 (Python Software Foundation) using the “shapviz” and “lime” packages.

Feature selection

To identify robust predictors associated with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) flare, three independent feature selection methods were applied: (1) univariate screening followed by multivariate logistic regression with LASSO (Supplementary Table 2; Fig. 2A,B), (2) LASSO regression (Supplementary Figure 1), and (3) the Boruta algorithm (Fig. 2C,D). As shown in Fig. 2, the LASSO model minimized binomial deviance at an optimal penalty parameter of log(λ) = −0.01573788, retaining 18 variables with non-zero coefficients. The Boruta algorithm further confirmed the relative importance of these features (Fig. 2C,D). The intersection of the three methods ultimately identified joint involvement, renal involvement, FIB, IgG, C3, and NAG1 as the most stable and clinically meaningful predictive indicators.

Model development and performance comparison

Seven predictive algorithms were developed to estimate the probability of SLE disease activity. Model hyperparameters were optimized via grid search and cross-validation, with the final tuned parameters summarized in Table 3. As shown in Table 3 and Fig. 3, among the machine learning methods, the support vector machine (SVM) model exhibited the most balanced and robust performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.845 (95% CI: 0.716–0.935; Fig. 3A), accuracy of 0.818, and the highest F1-score of 0.794. The model also demonstrated favorable specificity (0.800) and sensitivity (0.771), indicating strong discriminative ability and clinical reliability (Fig. 3B). Construction and validation of the nomogram

To ensure practical and clinical applicability, we selected the Logistic regression model as optimal and retrained it using the following predictors: Joint_Involvement, Renal_Involvement, FIB, IgG, C3, and NAG1. The model was subsequently evaluated on an independent validation set (Fig. 5).

Performance metrics for the Logistic prediction model (Fig. 5A–C) were as follows: AUC: 0.869; Accuracy: 0.8; Precision: 0.844; Sensitivity: 0.771; Specificity: 0.833; F1 Score: 0.806. Based on this optimized model, a clinical nomogram incorporating these six independent predictors was constructed to estimate the probability of SLE flare.Each predictor was assigned a weighted score proportional to its regression coefficient, with the total score corresponding to an individual’s probability of developing an SLE flare. Discussion

Core predictors and model framework

In this study, we successfully developed and validated a clinical historical cross-sectional model for predicting systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) disease activity by integrating multiple machine learning algorithms with rigorous statistical methods. We identified joint involvement, renal involvement, fibrinogen (FIB), immunoglobulin G (IgG), complement C3, and N-acetylglucosamine-1-phosphotransferase (NAG1) as core predictive indicators. Among the models tested, Logistic regression and Support Vector Machine (SVM) demonstrated the best predictive performance, with validation AUC values of 0.869 and 0.835, respectively. Remarkably, NAG1 emerged as the most influential predictor: elevated NAG1 levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of SLE active state, and FIB was found to partially mediate this effect. These findings offer a convenient, non-invasive framework for assessing the risk of localized and systemic disease activity.

Biological context of NAG1 and metabolic crosstalk

The central finding of this study is the identification of NAG1 as a critical biomarker of SLE disease activity. NAG1 is a multifunctional protein involved in inflammation and metabolic regulation. Our quantitative results indicated that each 1-unit increase in continuous NAG1 was associated with a 7% elevated risk of SLE activity, while the risk in the highest quartile (Q3) group was 4.72-fold that of the lowest quartile (Q1) group. This strong association was further corroborated by SHAP analysis, suggesting its prominent indicative value in clinical assessment.

Recent lines of investigation have increasingly highlighted the roles of dynamic metabolic disturbances and cytokine networks in SLE pathogenesis. For example, Ji et al. [15] identified farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP) as a metabolic alarmin in keratinocytes that systematically potentiates immune cell behavior and exacerbates disease pathology. As a lipid mediator-related protein, upregulated serum NAG1 may represent another crucial biological pathway reflecting metabolic-immune dysregulation in active SLE.

Model comparison and clinical applicability

For model construction, we combined penalty-based LASSO regression with the Boruta algorithm. The final set of six core predictors encompassed accessible clinical phenotypes, routine immune metrics, and novel hematological markers, showcasing the value of multi-dimensional data integration. Compared with previous computational tools, our framework demonstrates competitive predictive performance. For instance, Li et al. [5] developed a multiclass machine learning model utilizing nailfold capillaroscopy image features to distinguish autoimmune disease phenotypes, and Pang et al. [16] identified peripheral blood transcriptomic gene signatures—including GRN and KLRB1—to evaluate clinical activity. While those advanced imaging or high-throughput omics models have strong merit, our predictive model relies entirely on readily accessible routine laboratory indicators and a single serum biomarker, thereby drastically improving its bed-side generalizability and clinical translation potential.

Stratification analysis and organ-specific associations

Our stratification analysis offers valuable insights into the heterogeneous nature of SLE phenotypes. The association of NAG1 was particularly pronounced in aggressive or high-activity clinical subgroups, namely those positive for anti-dsDNA antibodies, presenting with hypocomplementemia (low C3), thrombocytopenia, and heavy proteinuria ($24\text{−h UP}\ge 3.5\text{g}$). These markers line up with standard diagnostic tracking; for example, the cGAS-STING pathway transcriptomic profile signature (M7core) has been strongly correlated with anti-dsDNA levels, complement consumption, and lupus nephritis development [4]. Thrombocytopenia is likewise tightly linked to active disease states [17].

Notably, the robust correlation between NAG1 and heavy proteinuria links our model directly to lupus nephritis management. Finding non-invasive indicators remains an ongoing clinical challenge. Recent studies have successfully profiled urinary extracellular vesicle metabolomics to distinguish active lupus nephritis from quiescent SLE (N. [18]), and highlighted how vesicle-derived miR-1290 modulates the Th17/Treg balance in renal pathology [19]. Our study adds to this diagnostic landscape by establishing the value of serum NAG1 in identifying systemic activity. However, whether its predictive ability is superior or fully complementary to these novel urinary vesicle panels requires prospective clarification.

Exploratory mediation and coagulation-inflammation networks

Although causal mediation analysis indicated that FIB statistically mediated 9.3% of the effect of NAG1 on SLE disease activity, given the historical nature of this study and the lack of companion experimental verification, we explicitly position NAG1 as a biomarker strongly associated with disease activity status, rather than a definitive pathogenic driver or a direct therapeutic target. FIB serves as a key protein in the coagulation cascade, an acute-phase reactant, and an immunomodulatory molecule. Its statistical mediation effect merely provides an exploratory statistical clue regarding potential crosstalk between metabolism-related markers and coagulation-inflammation pathways.

Since SLE patients often exhibit complex prothrombotic states that intertwine with systemic active phases [20], future prospective studies are warranted to map out these network links. Furthermore, the tripartite motif-containing (TRIM) protein family acts as a crucial regulator of immune and inflammatory signaling via ubiquitination pathways, showing widespread dysregulation in autoimmune conditions [21]). Subsequent basic experimental designs should cautiously explore whether NAG1 modulates FIB synthesis or function through specific downstream TRIM intermediates to robustly test this potential regulatory network in functional models.

Study limitations

Retrospective Framework: As a historical cross-sectional study, selection and information biases are inherently unavoidable, meaning our exploratory associations require formal validation in large prospective cohorts.

Sample Size and Subtypes: The current sample size ($n = 154$), while mathematically sufficient for model development, remains underpowered for deeply investigating organ-specific involvements like neuropsychiatric SLE. This subtype is a highly challenging domain; for example, diffusion tensor imaging has caught glymphatic system impairment even in neurologically asymptomatic SLE patients, which correlates with clinical activity scores [22].

Lack of Multi-Omics Data: This study focused entirely on routine serum parameters and did not integrate histological, genomic, or single-cell datasets. Targeted gene sequencing in diverse populations has revealed rare pathogenic variants in loci such as REL, IRAK1, and PTPRC that likely modify biomarker expressions [17]. Therefore, our model’s multi-ethnic generalizability demands wider external validation.

Clinical significance and future horizons

The practical translational value of this non-invasive tool is direct, providing clinicians with an interactive bedside scoring system to identify patients in high-activity phases and tailor individualized care. Furthermore, tracking biomarker changes over time could aid in monitoring therapeutic responses. For instance, hydroxychloroquine therapy has been shown to downregulate the expression of the cGAS-STING transcriptomic signature [4]. It remains highly worthwhile to see if NAG1 tracks responses to standard medications or modern biologics like anti-B-cell agents and type I interferon inhibitors.

In advanced immune interventions, CD19 CAR-T cells have successfully induced deep drug-free remission in refractory, severe SLE cases [23]. Observing serum biomarker fluctuations before and after such potent resetting of the immune system could clarify their utility in evaluating complete molecular response. If future laboratory knock-down validation confirms that NAG1 actively drives downstream vascular inflammation via molecules like FIB, selective pathway interventions might yield therapeutic benefits, mirroring strategies that target soluble Fas ligand to safely control abnormal cell death and tissue damage in other dermatological and systemic diseases [24].

Looking ahead, several research tracks emerge:First, integrating this framework into multicenter trials alongside emerging tools like urinary extracellular vesicle metabolites (N. [18]) or single-cell transcriptomic profiles [16] will optimize risk stratification.

Second, functional cellular and animal validation using MRL/lpr lupus mouse models is essential to map NAG1 expression changes against the loss of B-cell intrinsic tolerance, which represents a hallmark event in lupus pathogenesis [16].

Third, its tissue-specific expressions must be teased out across lupus nephritis (N. [6; 19; 25]) and neuropsychiatric SLE [22, 26]. Given early evidence that the type I interferon receptor antagonist anifrolumab shows efficacy in neuropsychiatric manifestations [26], testing whether NAG1 is tied to type I interferon response elements represents a vital prospective goal.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and validated a robust predictive framework for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) disease activity states using an integrated machine learning approach, establishing six readily accessible core variables: Joint_Involvement, Renal_Involvement, FIB, IgG, C3, and NAG1. The optimized logistic regression model demonstrated excellent discriminative ability with a validation AUC of 0.869, forming the basis for a non-invasive web-based clinical calculator (accessible at: https://hhj2025.shinyapps.io/dynnomapp/) to facilitate bedside risk screening.

NAG1 was identified as the most critical clinical indicator, displaying strong risk associations that were highly pronounced in active phenotypic subgroups presenting with anti-dsDNA positivity, low complement levels, thrombocytopenia, or heavy proteinuria. Causal mediation analysis showed that FIB explained 9.3% of the total effect, highlighting a relevant statistical crosstalk between metabolism-related profiles and the coagulation-inflammation cascade. While these findings offer an innovative stratification tool to optimize personalized medical management, future multicenter prospective cohorts and basic laboratory validation are required to fully delineate the structural biological mechanisms of NAG1 across organ-specific SLE subtypes.
