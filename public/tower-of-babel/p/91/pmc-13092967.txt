Precise, specific gene editing via a compact GoCas12m–FokI chimeric nuclease
Authors: Tin Marsic, Sivakrishna Rao Gundra, Mustapha Aouida, Maazallah Masood, Alexander Salibi, Fabian Schmidt, Reem Alquwayzani, Magdy M Mahfouz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCRISPR gene editing technologies have transformed functional genomics and biotechnology. Despite these advances, challenges such as limited delivery capacity and off-target activity continue to hinder their therapeutic translation. We developed a chimeric gene editing platform by fusing the compact, catalytically inactive Cas12m guiding module (GoCas12m) with the FokI nuclease domain. GoCas12m–FokI system integrates the programmable DNA-binding capability of Cas12m with the dimerization-dependent cleavage mechanism of FokI, enabling precise genome editing. Our engineered XTEN-fused GoCas12m–FokI editor exhibits robust activity on both surrogate reporters and endogenous human loci, achieving high-efficiency editing at clinically relevant targets-including CLTA1, HBB, AIFM1, and ABL with no detectable off-target activity at in silico-predicted sites, as confirmed by targeted deep sequencing. Notably, GoCas12m–FokI is nearly half the size of conventional Cas9- or Cas12a-based editors, facilitating delivery via adeno-associated virus and other cargo-limited vectors. This dual-guided editor showed comparable editing efficiency to previously reported FokI–dCas9 systems on endogenous loci, while possessing a different PAM requirement and domain orientation. By combining compact architecture, high specificity, and modular programmability, the GoCas12m–FokI editor offers a powerful alternative for therapeutic genome editing and a promising tool for in vivo gene therapy applications.

Introduction

The CRISPR (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats) system has revolutionized genome editing, enabling precise and programmable modification of DNA across a wide range of organisms [1–4]. Among the CRISPR effectors, Class II Type V Cas12 proteins have attracted considerable attention due to their diverse functional properties and structural variations [5–7]. The Cas12 family encompasses a broad range of subtypes (V-A through V-H), which evolved independently from TnpB-like transposases through modular evolution and horizontal gene transfer [8–11]. These subtypes differ in PAM recognition profiles, cleavage mechanisms, and substrate specificities, with Cas12a being widely adopted for genome editing and diagnostics [12–16].

A major challenge for translational application of Cas12 variants is their large size, which exceeds the cargo capacity of viral vectors. Engineered compact variants of CRISPR-Cas12, such as Cas12f and enEbCas12a, directly address the size-related delivery challenges of traditional Cas12 enzymes, enabling efficient packaging and delivery in single adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors while balancing size reduction with genome-editing activity [17–20]. Traditional Cas12 enzymes (e.g. AsCas12a) are too large for single AAV vector packaging (∼4.7 kb limit) due to the combined gene size and accessory elements like promoters and CRISPR RNA (crRNA). For example, enEbCas12a, a compact variant of Cas12a (∼3.47 kb), enhanced with a single mutation for improved editing efficiency and specificity, was successfully packaged into all-in-one AAV systems, delivering robust in vivo editing (e.g. PCSK9 gene in mice) [20]. Other ultra-compact systems (e.g. Cas12j-SF05, Cas12j3) show promise but require further improvement for consistent editing efficiency [21, 22]. Compact Cas12 variants combined with crRNA and necessary elements achieve high delivery efficiency, as demonstrated in both therapeutic (e.g. retinitis pigmentosa, cholesterol reduction) and plant genome editing contexts [20, 23, 24]. Key advancements, such as Cas12MINI_v3.1/ge4.1 and μCas, balance compact size with editing performance through optimized structural and biochemical properties [17, 25]. While systems like Cas12f and enEbCas12a ensure compatibility with AAVs and other constrained platforms, ongoing trade-offs in editing efficiency highlight the need for continuous optimization to maximize therapeutic and research applicability [26].

Among compact CRISPR effectors, Cas12m (∼596 amino acids) stands out due to its unique structural and functional properties [27]. Cas12m possesses a noncanonical His-Asp-Asp (HDD) catalytic triad in its RuvC domain, and an arginine-rich DNA recognition region that enables tight DNA binding rather than cleavage in certain variants (e.g. Cas12m2) [27]. Cas12m2 acts as a transcriptional silencer via DNA binding instead of double-stranded DNA cleavage, showcasing functional divergence within its orthologs. PAM preferences are relaxed but distinct (e.g. “TTN”) across studied subtypes, enhancing target versatility [7]. Recently, Cas12m has been repurposed for base editing, with the fusion of GoCas12m to adenine deaminase (TadA-8e) demonstrating precise A-to-G base editing in Escherichia coli and human cells [28, 29]. The compact size and programmable DNA binding capability of Cas12m make it attractive for engineering novel genome editing tools.

FokI nuclease fusions with CRISPR-associated DNA-binding proteins, particularly FokI–dCas9 systems, have been extensively designed and validated for highly specific DNA cleavage, emphasizing dual-guide RNA-directed dimerization, linker engineering, and spacer optimization to balance specificity and efficiency [30–33]. Cheng et al. [34] demonstrated the catalytic activity of dMb2Cas12a-FokI fusion on endogenous targets, establishing a precedent for Cas12-based dimeric editors. Previously, we and others have engineered catalytically inactive versions of Cas9 enzymes to improve their on-target specificities and overcome the off-target challenge through the fusion with FokI nuclease. Such fusions improved specificity and on-target activities [30–33, 35]. Improving PAM sequence flexibility, on-target cleavage efficiency, and minimizing trade-offs associated with compact architecture remain critical for therapeutic and biotechnological applications.

In this work, we harnessed the compactness of catalytically inactive Cas12m proteins and the catalytic activity of FokI nuclease upon dimerization to develop GoCas12m–FokI fusions to facilitate programmable double-strand breaks (DSBs) for precision genome editing in mammalian cells. The resulting GoCas12m–FokI fusion proteins are nearly half the size of conventional FokI–dCas9 enabling AAV packaging along with the two crRNA expression units. We demonstrated robust editing efficiencies on endogenous human loci, multiplex editing, no detectable off-target editing and efficient AAV-mediated delivery, establishing GoCas12m–FokI as a compact, specific, and versatile genome editing platform suitable for diverse translational applications, including in gene therapy.

Materials and methods

Construction of protein expression plasmids, crRNA or single-guide RNA (sgRNA) expression plasmids, and surrogate reporters

Human codon optimized Cas12m gBlocks were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) and flanked with NotI and AgeI sites. Inserts and expression plasmids were digested by NotI and AgeI and ligated in 3:1 ratio using T4 DNA Ligase (Promega) for 3 h at 23°C, 2 h at 16°C and 4°C until transformation into E. coli competent cells (One Shotâ TOP10 Chemically Competent E. coli, Invitrogen) with 2 μl of the ligation mix by heat-shock method. Colonies were picked and grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with ampicillin overnight and the plasmid DNA was isolated from the culture using QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (Qiagen). Successful cloning was confirmed by Restriction Enzyme digestion and Sanger sequencing.

Surrogate reporters were produced as follows. Briefly, target inserts were purchased as top and bottom single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) from IDT, with overhangs corresponding to EcoRI and BamHI cleavage products. Oligonucleotides were phosphorylated using T4 PNK (Promega) before annealing and ligated with 10 ng of digested plasmid. Ligation, transformation, and clone confirmation was carried-out as mentioned above.

Plasmids encoding for crRNAs used in the study were cloned as top and bottom ssDNA ordered from IDT, with overhangs corresponding to BbsI cleavage overhangs in the same manner as described above. Successful cloning was confirmed by RE digestion and Sanger sequencing. To produce polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplicons of U6-crRNA or U6-sgRNA for transfection experiments, 1 ng of purified plasmid was used as a template and amplified using the Phusion® High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase [31]. The amplicons were purified using QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen) in sterile environment and eluted in endotoxin-free TE buffer. The concentration was adjusted to 250 ng/μl. All plasmid sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1, and the corresponding crRNA and sgRNA architectures are detailed in Supplementary Tables S2 and S6.

Cell culture conditions, lipofection and nucleofection

Cell culture experiments were performed in the HEK293T line (wild-type, Abcam: ab255593) grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with high glucose (Thermo Fisher Scientific – 31966), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific: 10082147) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific: 15140122). HEK293T cells were grown at 37°C and 5% CO2. Cells were tested for mycoplasma contamination and passaged at 90% confluency.

Lipofection using Fugene (Promega – E2312) was performed according to manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 8 × 104 cells in 500 µl of antibiotic free medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS) were seeded in 24-well plate (previously treated with 0.1% gelatin) and grown for ∼16 h at 37°C to reach 50%–70% confluency. Cargo for lipofection (editor plasmid – 500 ng, each crRNA or sgRNA PCR amplicon – 250 ng) was combined with OPTI-MEM reduced serum medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific – 31985062) mixed well and vortexed. Per each reaction, 4 µl of Fugene regent was added, mixed well and incubated for ∼10 min before direct application to the cells. Reactions were incubated for 72 h at 37°C and 5% CO2 before extracting genomic DNA.

K562 cells (RIKEN BRC – RCB1897) were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Gibco™: 21875034) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cultures were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. K562 cells were cultured in suspension and passaged at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells/ml. For nucleofection, 2 × 10⁵ cells were electroporated with 750 ng of protein expression plasmid and 350 ng of crRNA or sgRNA PCR amplicon using the SF Cell Line 4D-Nucleofector™ X Kit S (Lonza: V4XC-2032) with the FF-120 pulse code, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Genomic DNA was harvested from the cells after 72 h of nucleofection for downstream analysis.

All-in-one AAV vector construction and packaging

Two endogenous human genomic loci (HBB, and AIFM1) were selected as targets for evaluating the GoCas12m–FokI nuclease activity. Recombinant AAV (adeno associated virus) vectors were constructed by VectorBuilder using a dual-guide expression cassette design. The guide 1 and guide 2 in HBB and AIFM1 vectors are driven by human U6 promoter and mouse U6 promoter, respectively, along with the GoCas12m–FokI expression unit. HBB plasmid (pAAV[Exp]-U6 > HBB_mU6 > HBB_G6-CMV > GoCas12m:bGH pA) with the Vector ID: VB251205-1010fmy and AIFM1 plasmid (pAAV[Exp]-U6 > AIFM_mU6 > AIFM_G2-CMV > GoCas12m:bGH pA) with the Vector ID: VB251205-1015squ are available at VectorBuilder. Briefly, each all-in-one AAV transfer plasmid was designed with AAV2 inverted terminal repeats (ITRs) flanking the entire expression cassette. The cassette comprises two independent crRNA expression units: the first crRNA-guide 1 under the human U6 (hU6) promoter and the second crRNA-guide 2 under the mouse U6 (mU6) promoter, followed by the GoCas12m–FokI open reading frame driven by a CMV promoter with bGH polyadenylation signal. The use of distinct U6 promoters for the two crRNA cassettes was intended to minimize the risk of recombination between repeated sequences during AAV vector production. The total insert size of each construct was ∼4.5 kb, well within the 4.7 kb packaging capacity of AAV. Vector cloning, sequence verification, large-scale plasmid preparation, AAV packaging, purification, and titer determination were performed by VectorBuilder as a custom service. The transfer plasmid carrying the gene of interest (GOI) was co-transfected with a RepCap plasmid encoding AAV-DJ Rep and Cap proteins and a helper plasmid providing adenoviral helper functions into HEK293T packaging cells using a standard triple-transfection protocol. Viral titer was determined by quantitative PCR targeting the ITR sequences, yielding titers of 1.40 × 1012 genome copies (GC)/ml for HBB and 1.47 × 1012 GC/ml for AIFM1 vectors. Quality control results confirmed negative results for bacterial, fungal, and mycoplasma contamination. Purified virus was supplied in storage buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2) and stored at −80°C until use.

AAV transduction

Transduction experiments were performed in HEK293T cells in different virus dose treatments. As mentioned above 8 × 104 HEK293T cells were seeded in 24-well plate. After ∼16 h, AAV was added directly on top of the medium at different multiplicities of infection 5000, 10 000, 30 000, 50 000, 75 000, 100 000, 150 000 vg/cell. Later, infected cells were incubated for 96 h at 37°C and 5% CO2 before extracting genomic DNA. HBB and AIFM all-in-one AAV plasmids were also transfected independently using Fugene reagent as mentioned above and used as positive control, NT cells were used as negative control. All the experiments were conducted in three biological replicates (n = 3).

Western blot analysis and ponceau S staining

We conducted western blot analysis to evaluate fusion protein expression in HEK293T cells. HEK293T cells were seeded in six-well plate (in each well, 3.2 × 105 cells in 2 ml of antibiotic free DMEM) and transfected with the different fusion constructs as mentioned above. After 72 h at 37°C, cells from each well were lysed directly with Radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors (Pierce™ Protease Inhibitor Tablets, Thermo Scientific™ – A32965). Lysates were briefly mixed and clarified by centrifugation. For sample preparation, 100 µl of each lysate was mixed with 34 µl of 6× sodium dodecyl sulphate sample buffer, heated at 95°C for 5 min, and 10 µl denatured sample was loaded per lane. Proteins were separated on by 8% sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred on to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane as described by Jiang et al. [36].

To detect the FLAG-tagged fusion proteins, membrane was incubated with anti-FLAG primary antibody (1:2000 dilution, Invitrogen) for 1 h at room temperature followed by horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:5000 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. Membrane was further washed and treated with ECL detection reagent (Pierce™ ECL Western Blotting Substrate, Thermo Scientific™ – 32209) and bands were visualized under Invitrogen™ iBright imaging system (Invitrogen™ – A44114). To assess the total protein loading and transfer uniformly, membrane was rinsed briefly in distilled water and treated with 0.1% Ponceau S stain (Thermo Scientific™– A40000278) for 3–5 min and images were captured after short wash with distilled water.

Flow cytometry

HEK293T cells were lipofected with 250 ng of reporter, 250 ng of editor and 250 ng of each crRNA using Fugene lipofection reagent as previously described, and maintained in antibiotic-free DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS for 48 h. Cells were trypsinized and resuspended in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Single-cell suspensions were analyzed using BD LSRFortessa™ (BD Biosciences). Nontransfected (NT) cells and cells transfected with reporters alone were used as controls.

Genomic DNA extraction and PCR amplification of target loci

Genomic DNA was extracted following the quick extraction protocol. Cells were harvested from the wells and centrifuged for 10 min at 1200 × g. Residual medium was removed and the cells were thoroughly resuspended in 100 μl of Quick Extract buffer (Lucigen). The suspension was incubated at 68°C for 15 min followed by a 95°C for 8 min. The genomic DNA was stored at −20°C before use. PCR amplification was performed using AccuPrime Taq High Fidelity DNA polymerase (Invitrogen) according to manufacturer’s instructions with 1.5 μl of extracted genomic DNA as template input.

T7 endonuclease I surveyor assays of genomic modifications

PCR amplicons from genomic targets were purified by QIAquick PCR purification kit. Reannealing reaction was prepared by combining 200 ng of amplicons, 2 µl of 10X NEBuffer 2 (NEB – B6002) and nuclease-free H2O to a total of 19.5 µl volume. Reaction was heated to 95°C for 5 min followed by stepwise cooling to 25°C (−0.1°C/s) after which 0.5 µl T7 endonuclease I (T7EI) endonuclease was added. Reaction was incubated at 37°C for 60 min, stopped by adding 4 μl of loading dye (6× Gel Loading Dye Purple, NEB) and resolved on 2% agarose gel at 110 V for 1 h and 30 min. Resolved gels were visualized under Invitrogen™ iBright imaging system (Invitrogen™ – A44114).

Multiplex targeting

For the multiplex targeting, we co-transfected 500 ng of editor protein expression plasmid along with one, two, or three pairs of crRNAs encoding amplicons (each 250 ng) for targeting one or two or three genes simultaneously, using the Fugene® HD transfection kit. Cells were harvested after 72 h of post-transfection. For each target cleavage analysis, the genomic DNA extracted from the co-transfected HEK293T cells. Later, each targeted regions in the genomic DNA were independently amplified using target specific primers (Supplementary Table S5). Further, T7EI assay conducted for each amplicon independently.

Amplicon sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated as described above. Genomic regions of interest were PCR-amplified using target-specific primers (Supplementary Table S5). Second round of PCR amplification was performed to incorporate 33-nucleotide adapter sequences were using adapter-linked primers (Supplementary Table S5). In the final step, amplicons were indexed with Illumina barcoded primers and the resulting products were quantified using Qubit 2.0 fluorometer, pooled in equimolar concentrations, and used for library preparation. Prepared libraries were sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform and analyzed using Cas-Analyzer software to determine editing outcome [37].

In silico off-target prediction

Potential off-target sites were identified using Cas-OFFinder against the human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38; Supplementary Table S7). The top ranked sites were selected for experimental validation by targeted deep amplicon sequencing described above.

Results

Design, construction and expression of compact GoCas12m-FokI gene editors

GoCas12m, from Gordonia otitidis, has been characterized as a programable DNA expression silencer [27, 29], and we attempted to harness the programmability of Cas12m to generate chimeric fusions with FokI nuclease to generate a programable molecular scissor (Fig. 1A). The FokI cleavage domain is derived from a type IIS restriction enzyme that cleaves nonspecifically at a fixed distance from its recognition site (Supplementary Tables S1 and S3). It has no intrinsic DNA-binding capability and instead requires dimerization for its cleavage activity [35]. Using a flexible peptide linker, we tethered the FokI cleavage domain to the N-terminus or C-terminus of GoCas12m (Fig. 1B). The design of the linker is critical to ensure proper spatial positioning and flexibility of the fusion protein while minimizing steric hindrance. We tested three linkers, GGS3x, L8, and XTEN, with lengths ranging from 10 to 30 amino acids, as they balance flexibility and stability (Fig. 1B). To enable dimerization of the FokI domains, two GoCas12m fusion proteins are required to bind proximal target sites on opposite DNA strands. This dual-crRNA (crRNA) system directs the fusion of GoCas12m–FokI protein to spatially arranged target sequences, which can be oriented in PAM-in, PAM-out, and PAM-parallel configurations (Fig. 1C). The spacer length, the number of base pairs between the crRNA binding sites, is a critical factor for optimal activity. Cleavage occurs if the GoCas12m fusions enable the dimerization of FokI monomers (Fig. 1A). Optimal cleavage occurs with a defined range of spacer length, as deviations outside this range disrupt FokI dimerization and abrogate the cleavage activity. Therefore, we constructed different N-terminus and C-terminus fusions between a humanized codon optimized Cas12m and FokI catalytic domain sequences with bipartite nuclear localization signals (bpNLS; Fig. 1B and Supplementary Table S3). Resulting Cas12m–FokI or FokI–Cas12m proteins are significantly more compact in comparison to traditionally used LbCas12a and SpCas9, and roughly half the size of conventionally used FokI–dCas9 paired nuclease (Table 1). Fusion constructs were engineered under the CMV promoter in a mammalian expression vector to enable their production in cultured human cells.

Linker and spacer requirements for GoCas12m–FokI activity on surrogate reporter systems

Fluorescent surrogate reporters are widely used tools to evaluate the activity and efficiency of genome editing systems, including CRISPR-Cas nucleases, in mammalian cells. These reporter constructs allow rapid and quantitative determination of targeted DSBs on transiently delivered plasmids. To assess the activity of GoCas12m–FokI chimeric fusion constructs, we co-transfected HEK293T cells with mammalian expression plasmids with codon optimized sequences, and respective dual-crRNAs (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3) together with a surrogate plasmid containing a GFP reporter gene separated from a RFP reporter via spacer that contains increasing number of nucleotides and a stop-codon [38] (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Table S4). After GoCas12m–FokI binding and cleavage at the spacer region we expect some fraction of GFP to fall in frame with RFP resulting in an RFP+ GFP+ signal that can be quantified using flow cytometry (Fig. 2A). We employed the flow cytometry-based assay and the fluorescence microscopy analysis to assess the activity of various N- and C-terminal fusion constructs with XTEN linker (Fig. 2B). We used the LbCas12a enzyme as a positive control (Supplementary Fig. S2). Notably, the C-terminal FokI fusion with the XTEN linker (referred to hereafter as GoCas12m–FokI) demonstrated the detectable editing activity when using two crRNAs, validating the potential of this architecture for targeted DSB induction in mammalian cells (Fig. 2B). These findings informed the design of dual-crRNA configurations for targeting endogenous genomic loci in the subsequent experiments.

GoCas12m–FokI fusions enable editing of endogenous genomic loci

To validate the genome editing activity observed with surrogate reporter assays, we next tested the GoCas12m–FokI constructs at endogenous genomic loci in human cells. We selected three representative targets—CLTA1, HBB, and AIFM1—and compared editing performance across various linker types and crRNA configurations. LbCas12a, which recognizes a TTTN PAM, was used as a positive control in all assays. We systematically screened GoCas12m–FokI fusion constructs containing GGS3x, L8, or XTEN linkers, evaluating editing activity across a broad range of spacer lengths and guide orientations, including PAM-in, PAM-parallel, and PAM-out configurations. Notably, we observed catalytic activity exclusively with crRNAs in the PAM-in orientation, across spacer lengths ranging from 6 to 19 bp, when using the GoCas12m-XTEN-FokI fusion (Fig. 3A), unlike the previously reported FokI–dCas9 which displayed activity only with PAM-out orientations [30, 31, 33, 35]. These results corroborated the editing activity observed in our surrogate reporter assays (Supplementary Figs S3–S5). In contrast, only a single instance of activity was detected with GoCas12m-GGS3x-FokI at the AIFM1 locus, and even then, the editing efficiency was lower than that observed with the XTEN linker (Supplementary Fig. S4). Similarly, GoCas12m-L8-FokI exhibited cleavage activity at the HBB target in only one instance (Supplementary Fig. S5). Based on these findings, we selected GoCas12m-XTEN-FokI as the preferred architecture for further experiments and refer to it as GoCas12m–FokI throughout the manuscript. Editing efficiency at endogenous targets—CLTA1, HBB, and AIFM1—was confirmed by T7EI assay across all three loci but only when dual crRNAs were employed in the PAM-in configuration with appropriate spacer lengths (Fig. 3A). Quantitative analysis using Illumina MiSeq further validated detectable levels of editing efficiencies at each locus, with variability depending on spacer and target context (Fig. 3A). To characterize the nature of the editing outcomes, we analyzed the indel patterns generated following DSB repair. Deletion events were the predominant outcome, and their distribution consistently mapped to the region between the two crRNA binding sites, consistent with cleavage by FokI dimerization. Moreover, we observed that shorter spacer lengths were associated with shorter deletion footprints, suggesting that the physical distance between guides influences both cleavage precision and repair signature (Fig. 3B).

GoCas12m–FokI enables multiplex editing at genomic loci

To evaluate whether the GoCas12m–FokI system can support multiplexed genome editing, we co-delivered dual-crRNAs targeting two or three distinct endogenous loci simultaneously. Specifically, we focused on three genomic targets—CLTA1, AIFM1, and HBB—which had previously shown editing activity when targeted individually with dual-crRNAs. For initial validation, we assessed the potential of GoCas12m–FokI for simultaneous dual gene editing by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with GoCas12m–FokI and two dual-crRNA pairs designed to simultaneously target gene pairs such as CLTA1-AIFM1, CLTA1-HBB, and AIFM1-HBB. Editing efficiency was evaluated using the T7EI assay at both loci. T7EI analysis revealed clear evidence of editing activity across all tested gene pair combinations. Encouraged by these results, we expanded our analysis to triplex editing, co-delivering dual-crRNAs targeting all three loci—CLTA1, AIFM1, and HBB—in a single transfection. T7EI assays confirmed detectable editing at all three sites, though editing efficiencies varied among targets.

Further deep amplicon sequencing of dual- and triple-gene targeting experiments revealed that the highest editing activity occurred at the HBB locus when co-targeted with either CLTA1 or AIFM1, with an average editing frequency of ∼6%. This was followed by the AIFM1 locus showing ∼3% editing when targeted alongside either CLTA1 or HBB, and the CLTA1 locus exhibited ∼1% editing when co-targeted with either AIFM1 or HBB (Fig. 4B). Consistently, in triple-gene targeting conditions, the HBB locus showed highest editing efficiency when co-targeted with CLTA1 and AIFM1 genes (Fig. 4C).

These results indicate that GoCas12m–FokI can enable concurrent editing across multiple genomic sites, albeit with differences in efficiency likely influenced by factors such as chromatin accessibility, crRNA performance, and local sequence context. The successful demonstration of triplex genome editing underscores the potential of GoCas12m–FokI for multiplexed applications, including combinatorial gene perturbation and pathway-level modulation. However, the variability in editing efficiencies also highlights the need for further optimization of crRNA design, spacing, and orientation to enhance catalytic output across diverse genomic contexts. These results set the stage for future efforts to tune multiplex editing by modulating spacer length, delivery dosage, and possibly implementing engineered variants with enhanced dimerization dynamics.

GoCas12m–FokI enables editing at genomic loci comparable to FokI–dCas9

To benchmark the performance of the novel GoCas12m–FokI dual-guide editor, we compared its editing efficiency to that of established nucleases, including the conventionally used FokI–dCas9 paired editor, LbCas12a, and SpCas9. These experiments were conducted across three representative endogenous loci such as CLTA1, HBB, and AIFM1. As anticipated, SpCas9 exhibited the highest editing efficiency at all loci, consistent with its well-established activity as a robust single-guide nuclease. LbCas12a demonstrated intermediate editing levels, varying by target site. In comparison, GoCas12m–FokI showed variable efficiency depending on the genomic locus, performing comparably to FokI–dCas9 at certain sites but trailing behind SpCas9 and LbCas12a overall (Fig. 5A–C). Specifically, FokI–dCas9 outperformed GoCas12m–FokI when targeting the CLTA1 and HBB loci, achieving higher editing levels as measured by both T7EI assay and deep sequencing (Fig. 5A and B). Interestingly, at the AIFM1 locus, GoCas12m–FokI exhibited nearly equivalent editing efficiency to FokI–dCas9, highlighting locus-dependent performance and the potential for application-specific use (Fig. 5C). We also observed that GoCas12m–FokI and LbCas12a achieved nearly identical editing efficiency at the HBB locus, reinforcing the context-sensitive nature of genome editing outcomes. Differences in editing performance are likely influenced by several factors, including crRNA secondary structure, chromatin accessibility, target site architecture, and local sequence context. Despite its overall lower efficiency relative to SpCas9, GoCas12m–FokI offers significant advantages in terms of size. At approximately half the size of FokI–dCas9, it is considerably more amenable to delivery via AAV or other cargo-limited platforms, making it an attractive candidate for translational genome editing applications.

In addition to quantifying editing efficiency, we compared the editing footprints of GoCas12m–FokI and FokI–dCas9 at matched target sites. Analysis of deep sequencing data revealed that GoCas12m–FokI generates deletions within a narrower window and typically induces smaller indels localized between the two crRNA binding sites. In contrast, FokI–dCas9 produced larger and more variable deletions (Supplementary Fig. S6A–C). These differences suggest that GoCas12m–FokI may offer finer control over cleavage positioning, which could be beneficial for applications requiring precise genome editing. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that GoCas12m–FokI is a viable compact alternative to FokI–dCas9 for site-specific genome editing, capable of achieving comparable editing efficiency at select loci while offering potential advantages in specificity, footprint precision, and vector compatibility.

GoCas12m–FokI exhibits no detectable off-target activity at in silico-predicted sites

A key rationale for the development and use of paired nucleases is their inherently higher specificity compared to single-guide systems. Unlike single-guided nucleases, which can tolerate partial mismatches and frequently introduce off-target DSBs, paired nucleases such as GoCas12m–FokI require the coordinated binding of two crRNAs at adjacent sites to induce cleavage via FokI dimerization. This dual-binding requirement imposes a spatial constraint, thereby reducing the likelihood of off-target activity and improving genomic precision. To evaluate the off-target profile of GoCas12m–FokI, we conducted a comparative analysis against LbCas12a, a widely used single-guide nuclease. We used computational prediction tools to identify three high-confidence off-target sites for each of two target genes, CLTA1 and AIFM1, and for HBB, we identified one potential off-target site for each of the two HBB guides (guide 5 and guide 6). Among these sites, CLTA1 and AIFM1 were compatible with either LbCas12a or with a single guide from the GoCas12m–FokI dual-guide system. We then performed targeted deep sequencing of these loci following editor delivery in HEK293T cells to assess off-target activity. Remarkably, GoCas12m–FokI showed no detectable off-target editing at any of the predicted sites, indicating a high degree of specificity and supporting the hypothesis that dual-guide-dependent FokI dimerization provides robust discrimination against mismatched sites, including the HBB off-target sites (Supplementary Fig. S7), indicating the high degree of specificity (Fig. 6). Notably, AIFM1 off-target site 3 is a paired off-target containing binding sites for both crRNAs with a 16bp spacer distance, that exactly matches the AIFM1 on-target. Despite both binding sites being present, no editing was detected by GoCas12m–FokI, whereas LbCas12a exhibited 21.4% indel frequency at this site. These findings are consistent with previous reports highlighting LbCas12a’s tolerance for mismatches and its potential for unintended cleavage in genomic regions with partial complementarity. While LbCas12a achieved higher on-target editing efficiencies at the tested loci, the superior specificity of GoCas12m–FokI underscores a key trade-off between editing activity and off-target risk. These results emphasize the unique value of GoCas12m–FokI in applications where precision is paramount, such as therapeutic genome editing, where unintended DSBs can lead to genotoxicity or disruption of critical regulatory elements.

Comparable editing efficiency of GoCas12m–FokI and canonical editors at the ABL locus in K562 cell line

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of the newly developed GoCas12m–FokI editor, we tested its genome editing efficiency at the ABL gene locus in K562 cells, a human chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) model (Fig. 7A). The ABL locus is a clinically relevant target due to its involvement in the BCR–ABL fusion gene, which drives leukemogenesis in CML. Targeted disruption of ABL has been proposed as a strategy to eliminate oncogenic signalling and overcome resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors [39]. We compared GoCas12m–FokI to two well-characterized editors: the FokI–dCas9 paired nuclease and the single-guide LbCas12a system. All constructs were delivered under standardized conditions, and editing outcomes were measured using T7EI assay and high-throughput sequencing (Illumina MiSeq). GoCas12m–FokI achieved an average editing efficiency of 6.0%, closely matching the performance of FokI–dCas9 (7.4%) and LbCas12a (7.97%) at this locus (Fig. 7B). Notably, the editing efficiencies were consistent across biological replicates, underscoring the reproducibility of the system in a therapeutically relevant cell line. Deep amplicon sequencing results also revealed that the predominant editing outcomes are narrower deletions when using GoCas12m–FokI editor (Fig. 7C). These results demonstrate that GoCas12m–FokI is capable of efficient genome editing at disease-associated loci, comparable to established editing platforms. While SpCas9 and LbCas12a are known for their robust activity, their relatively large size can limit delivery using AAV vectors. In contrast, GoCas12m–FokI retains efficient on-target activity in a compact form factor, making it well-suited for in vivo therapeutic applications where vector cargo capacity is limited. Moreover, the dual-guide architecture of GoCas12m–FokI offers inherent advantages in target specificity, reducing the risk of off-target effects, an important consideration in therapeutic settings. The successful application of GoCas12m–FokI to edit the ABL gene in leukemia cells highlights its translational potential as a precision genome editing tool for hematological malignancies. Together, these findings support GoCas12m–FokI as a promising, compact, and effective platform for therapeutic gene disruption, particularly in scenarios requiring high specificity, efficient delivery, and compatibility with compact vectors.

Genome editing by AAV-delivered GoCas12m–FokI

To evaluate the editing activity of AAV-delivered GoCas12m–FokI on HBB and AIFM1 genomic targets, we extracted genomic DNA from the virus-treated HEK293T cells and performed the T7EI assay. T7EI digestion of reannealed PCR products confirmed the dose-dependent editing at both loci with cleavage bands increasing intensity with virus dose (Fig. 8A and B). All-in-one AAV plasmids showed visibly more intense bands. NT and AAV-GFP (50K vg/cell) negative controls showed no detectable cleavage after T7EI treatment. InDel frequencies were further measured by deep amplicon sequencing. Results indicated that detectable editing of HBB gene and AIFM1 gene was observed using > 30 000 vg/cell. Intriguingly, the editing efficiencies on HBB target are consistently higher when compared to the AIFM1 gene across all virus loads (vg/cell; Fig. 8C and D). All the results are consistent with the T7EI assays. All-in-one AAV plasmids displayed higher editing efficiencies on both targets, whereas, NT showed no cleavage.

Discussion

CRISPR systems continue to revolutionize genome engineering by offering precise tools for therapeutic correction of pathogenic mutations. However, the widespread clinical adoption of these technologies depends on the availability of compact, highly specific, and efficient gene editors with minimal off-target activity. In this study, we introduce GoCas12m–FokI, a dual-guide, dimeric genome editing system that merges the programmability of the compact Cas12m protein with the conditional cleavage capability of the FokI nuclease domain.

Our data demonstrate that GoCas12m–FokI enables robust and site-specific DSB induction at multiple endogenous loci in human cells. Unlike monomeric nucleases such as SpCas9 or LbCas12a, which cleave DNA using a single gRNA or crRNA and an active nuclease domain, GoCas12m–FokI relies on FokI dimerization. This obligate dimerization introduces an added layer of specificity by requiring simultaneous binding of two crRNAs to sites in a defined orientation and with a constrained spacer length. Our optimization experiments revealed that a PAM-in orientation with a 6–19 bp spacer yielded the highest editing activity, establishing clear design parameters for efficient cleavage.

GoCas12m–FokI is significantly more compact than FokI–dCas9, occupying roughly half the coding size, which is a substantial advantage for delivery using vectors with strict packaging limits such as AAV. The successful editing at the ABL locus in K562 cells, achieving comparable efficiency to both FokI–dCas9 and LbCas12a, demonstrates the feasibility of GoCas12m–FokI for clinically relevant applications, such as targeted disruption of the BCR–ABL fusion gene in leukemia [39].

Importantly, off-target analysis via deep sequencing revealed that GoCas12m–FokI exhibited no detectable activity at predicted off-target sites, in stark contrast to LbCas12a, which produced high indel frequencies at several mismatched loci. This exceptional specificity stems from the system’s reliance on dimerization of the FokI domains, which is only possible when both crRNAs bind their target sequences in the correct spatial configuration. Even minor deviations in guide spacing or orientation abolished activity, highlighting the system’s inherent resistance to unintended cleavage.

Central to specificity, these systems require two guide RNAs positioning dCas9 for FokI dimerization at optimal spacer lengths (13–18 bp), showing reduced off-target activity compared to monomeric Cas9 [30, 31]. In our designs, we have tested diverse linker types and sizes; however, protein flexibility and orientation might be enhanced with designs involving different linkers, which may improve enzyme activity and stability [31, 33]. Innovations in linker flexibility and dimerization kinetics could enhance cleavage rates [40]. FokI domains can be modified to increase specificity, such as engineering heterodimeric variants to reduce homodimerization and off-target cleavage [41]. Notably, this specificity advantage aligns with previous work on FokI–dCas9 systems, where dimerization reduces nonspecific cleavage and allows for programmable target recognition without introducing DSBs from a single guide [33, 42, 43]. Moreover, future improvements such as heterodimeric FokI variants could reduce homodimer-related off-targeting, while truncated or chemically modified guide RNAs (trun-gRNAs) may further enhance fidelity [32, 44]. Similarly, linker engineering remains a promising area for increasing stability and flexibility, potentially boosting cleavage efficiency and reducing structural hindrance.

Although GoCas12m–FokI showed slightly lower overall editing efficiency compared to SpCas9 or LbCas12a, the trade-off between efficiency and specificity may be advantageous in therapeutic genome editing, stem cell engineering, or in vivo delivery applications where minimizing collateral damage is paramount. Furthermore, the shorter deletions and localized mutation footprints observed with GoCas12m–FokI suggest a more precise and controllable editing outcome.

The compact size of GoCas12m–FokI renders it suitable for delivery via AAV vectors, which have a limited packaging capacity of ∼4.7kb. Our data establish GoCas12m–FokI as a functional genome editing nuclease capable of dose-dependent editing when delivered via AAV-DJ. The clear dose-dependent editing observed on both the HBB and AIFM1 targets, demonstrated by T7EI and corroborated by amplicon deep sequencing, highlights its potential as a promising candidate for in vivo genome editing in gene therapy applications.

Looking ahead, future development of PAM-relaxed Cas12m variants, incorporation of ultra-compact homologs, and application of optimized delivery platforms (e.g. nonviral or mRNA-based) could substantially broaden the utility of this system. Beyond nuclease-based editing, dCas12m fusions with transcriptional regulators, epigenetic modifiers, or base editors offer routes to precise gene modulation without inducing DSBs. Such constructs would be particularly valuable in therapeutic contexts where minimal genomic disruption is essential.

Finally, further structural optimization, including rational linker design and cryogenic electron microscope (cryo-EM) guided modelling, could improve cleavage efficiency and stability, particularly in chromatin-dense environments. Expanding GoCas12m–FokI to nonmammalian systems, such as plants, microbes, or organelles, could also unlock applications in agriculture, metabolic engineering, and synthetic biology. Altogether, this work establishes GoCas12m–FokI as a versatile genome engineering platform and lays a foundation for the broader development of compact, programmable CRISPR tools tailored for precision editing across diverse biological systems and translational settings.

Conclusions

GoCas12m–FokI represents a compact, precise, and programmable genome editing tool with a favourable specificity profile and robust activity at endogenous targets. Its unique dual-guide design and reliance on FokI dimerization provide exceptional control over DNA cleavage, offering a compelling alternative to conventional nucleases where precision is critical. The architecture of this system makes it a strong candidate for applications demanding minimal off-target activity, and it sets the foundation for engineering next-generation synthetic editors leveraging compact CRISPR scaffolds. Our data highlight the power of compact CRISPR proteins as programmable DNA-binding modules, enabling fusions to diverse functional domains for targeted modifications with high specificity and efficiency. GoCas12m–FokI could serve not only as a programmable nuclease but also as a modular chassis for applications extending beyond DNA cleavage—including transcriptome regulation, epigenetic remodelling, and base conversion—through fusion to effector proteins. These findings underscore the value of exploring uncharacterized, compact Cas effectors to develop programmable genome and epigenome engineering tools with translational impact.
