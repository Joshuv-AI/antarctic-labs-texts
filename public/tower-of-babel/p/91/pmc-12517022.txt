Evaluating diabetes-specific meal replacements for glycaemic control in overweight and obese patients with T2DM: a protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Wan Ain Nadirah Che Wan Mansor, Suzana Shahar, Noorlaili Mohd Tohit, Roslee Rajikan, Munirah Ismail
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUncontrolled type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) causes microvascular and macrovascular issues that hike healthcare costs and threaten global health. Previous studies have suggested meal replacement (MR) therapy for T2DM, but there were inconsistencies in the results. Thus, a randomised controlled trial is proposed to determine the efficacy of a diabetes-specific MR product on weight loss, glycaemic control, satiety, quality of life, metabolic gene expression and cost benefit in overweight and obese patients with T2DM.Methods and analysis164 overweight and obese participants with T2DM will be randomly assigned to either an intervention group (n=82) or a control group (n=82). All participants will receive dietary counselling; however, only the intervention group will receive MR for 12 weeks. Glycaemic control and obesity indicators are the study’s main outcomes. Secondary outcomes include cardiovascular disease risk markers, metabolic risk, metabolic gene profile analysis, dietary data, physical activity, satiety level, quality of life and cost analysis. Baseline data will include sociodemographics, anthropometry, blood pressure, diet, physical activity, satiety level, quality of life, blood profile, metabolic gene expression profile and cost-benefit analysis. A follow-up is planned at intervention weeks 6 and 12. Week 6 will assess only anthropometry, blood pressure, diet, physical activity and satiety level. For compliance assessment, intervention group participants will bring their MR container. Week 12 will measure the same baseline parameter, except sociodemographic data. Individuals who consume less than 80% of the MR will be deemed non-compliant. All parameter modifications will be documented and analysed for comparison. All statistical analyses will be conducted using IBM SPSS V.29.0 software, with a significance level of p<0.05. The cost-benefit analysis will be determined using the net present value.Ethics and disseminationThis research protocol was approved by the Ethical Committee of the National University of Malaysia (JEP-2024-695) and registered on International Standard Randomised Controlled Trial Number. Study findings will be disseminated in peer-reviewed journals, conference presentations and social media.Trial registration numberISRCTN57040303).Keywords: NUTRITION & DIETETICS; GENETICS; Diabetes Mellitus, Type 2

Introduction

Obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are steadily rising in prevalence globally. Obesity exacerbates the influence of genetic vulnerability and environmental factors on the development of T2DM, as they share significant genetic and environmental characteristics in their pathogenesis. The increasing prevalence of T2DM is associated with microvascular and macrovascular complications that result in increased healthcare costs, which makes it a public health burden worldwide, including in Malaysia.1 2 In Malaysia, 15.6% of adults have been diagnosed with diabetes, with only 44% of them having their blood sugar under control, which is haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) lower than 6.5%.3 Among the various challenges faced by patients with T2DM, the primary challenge remains to be the achievement and long-term maintenance of optimal glycaemic control.4

Overweight or obese individuals have a higher risk of developing T2DM, and the frequent co-occurrence of both is termed as ‘diabesity’.5 T2DM is believed to be associated with chronic low-grade inflammation in adipose tissue that affects the pathogenesis of diabetes in obese patients and highlights the shared insulin action deficiencies in both disorders.6 Additionally, Taylor (2025)7 asserts that excess fat accumulation is the primary factor for T2DM, whereby it involves the interplay of two metabolic cycles involving the liver and pancreas. The fat buildup in the liver causes insulin resistance and overproduction of glucose, while fat accumulation in the pancreas impairs insulin production. These cycles reinforce each other, where the liver’s excessive glucose production forces the increasingly fat-laden pancreas to work harder until it cannot produce enough insulin, leading to diabetes.7 A systematic review indicated that more than 30% of patients with T2DM were obese across 38 of 44 trials, whereas a study conducted in Malaysia revealed that 91% of diabetic patients are classified as obese or overweight.8 9

A balanced and well-structured diet plays a critical role in the clinical management of T2DM, primarily to achieve glycaemic and weight control.10 Evidence from the Diabetes Prevention Program Research Group indicated that, over the long term, an intensive lifestyle intervention (low-calorie diet and moderate physical exercise) resulting in weight reduction could reduce the incidence of T2DM in overweight or obese individuals and improve impaired glucose tolerance by 27%–58%.11 Weight loss can be challenging due to the weight-gaining effects of anti-diabetic medications, as well as barriers to long-term lifestyle changes, including inadequate dietary education, reduced motivation, delays in medical follow-up and limited family support.12 However, the weight gaining-effects of antidiabetic medications are primarily from improved glycaemic control reducing glycosuria rather than direct drug effects.13 Though implementing dietary intervention proves effective in attaining desired weight and glycaemic control among patients with T2DM, this strategy can pose a great challenge for both patients and healthcare providers as it relies on patient compliance with the suggested lifestyle modification.14

Meal replacement (MR) therapy has been recognised as one of the strategies to control weight and blood sugar levels among patients with T2DM.15 Additionally, MR has also been suggested as a strategy to help in lowering the cardiometabolic risk factors and reduce the risk for complications in T2DM.16 MR refers to pre-packaged or commercially available food products or drinks used to replace one or more meals.17 Most of the diabetes-specific MR are lower in glycaemic index (GI), high in fibre and lower in sugar. These foods slow gastric emptying, resulting in a slower rate of glucose absorption and a gradual release of glucose into the bloodstream. This helps regulate blood sugar levels, reduce insulin spikes, reduce food intake and promote weight loss.18

Studies have found that partial or complete MR can be an effective, extended strategy of medical nutrition therapy in T2DM management.19 20 However, the study’s outcome is influenced by the duration of the intervention,20,22 the type of intervention,23 24 the type of MR,25 and frequency of the MR given,26 which play a role in the outcome of the study. Additionally, a study among pre-diabetic and overweight patients found that low GI MR increased hormone satiety and lowered hunger hormones after 2 months of intervention.27

MR can mitigate the risk of problems linked to T2DM, including diabetic foot ulcers and retinopathy, by assisting in the regulation of blood sugar levels, facilitating weight loss, ensuring adequate nutrition and enhancing dietary compliance. This may subsequently enhance the patient quality of life (QoL) by lowering the functional limits imposed by these issues. Variations in study protocols and routine dietary advice exist because T2DM management guidelines differ by country based on local staple foods. While the latest study in Malaysia focused on the effect of the MR formulation containing cinnamon extract among obese and overweight T2DM patients on hormone levels,15 there has not yet been extensive exploration of the impact of MR containing chromium on metabolic gene expression. Therefore, this study will determine the efficacy and cost-benefit analysis of partial diabetes-specific MR therapy on weight reduction, glycaemic control, physical activity, QoL and metabolic gene expression among overweight and obese patients with T2DM.

Methods

Study design and settings

This study is a two-armed, randomised controlled trial. The data collection will occur at the Primer and Warga Clinic, Hospital Canselor Tuanku Muhriz (HCTM) of the National University of Malaysia. Participant recruitment started on 1 December 2024, with the aim of completing recruitment by 1 December 2025. The intervention and final evaluation will end on 30 April 2026. The flow of the study is visually represented in figure 1.

Flowchart of the study design. A total of 5124 individuals were assessed for eligibility, with 164 participants recruited and randomised into two groups: (1) meal replacement group (n=82) receiving meal replacement and dietary consultation and (2) control group (n=82) receiving dietary consultation only. Both groups underwent a 12-week intervention period with assessments at baseline, 6 weeks and 12 weeks.

Eligibility criteria

164 Malaysians aged between 30 and 59 years who had been diagnosed with T2DM for at least 6 months, with baseline HbA1c levels above 7.5%–12%,15 a body mass index (BMI) above 23 kg/m228 and able to communicate and understand in English or Malay were included in the study.

Patients were also excluded from the study if he/she was diagnosed with chronic kidney diseases (estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <30 MI/min/1.73), hepatic diseases (alanine transaminase (ALT) >120 IU/L), cancer; pregnancy and lactating women; consuming any weight reduction products, slimming prescriptions, MR; on hormone replacement therapy treatment; on treatment for cancer; involved in any clinical trial and weight loss programmes.

Participants’ recruitment and randomisation

Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, participants were selected using purposive sampling. Eligibility was determined through a review of electronic medical records databases. Potential participants were recruited during scheduled medical appointments, provided with detailed study information, and if eligible, asked to provide informed consent (online supplemental material 1). Subsequently, a third-party statistician randomly allocated participants to either the intervention or control group using the minimisation approach in R statistical environment. This randomisation was stratified by age, gender and HbA1c during the screening visit. Table 1 shows a summary of parameters collected during the study based on primary and secondary outcomes.

Intervention group

The intervention group received a meal replacement product (Resurge DM, Quantum Upstream Sdn Bhd) along with a single, one-to-one dietary consultation session. This consultation adhered to the standard Medical Nutrition Therapy Guidelines for T2DM, second edition,29 and was conducted during the baseline data collection visit. The suggested calorie intake for each participant was individually calculated based on their current BMI and emphasised during the dietary consultation session. Participants were instructed to replace their two main meals with the MR product, consisting of 53 g of powder mixed with 200 mL of water. This MR product has been chosen based on the chromium content. Each serving was fortified with 100 mcg of chromium, ensuring that the prescribed dose of two servings per day fulfilled the suggested daily chromium intake for patients with T2DM. Chromium appears to enhance insulin sensitivity, and a systematic review reported that chromium supplementation of 100–400 mcg/day can lower HbA1c level.30 Other than that, the product featured a low GI, incorporated a dual-fibre blend and contained 800 mg of inositol per serving. The dual-fibre blend content was designed to assist in weight management and facilitate the regulation of blood sugar levels. Meanwhile, inositol may improve insulin sensitivity with the recommended amount of 500–1000 mg/day. Individualised instructions on how to prepare the MR were also provided. Nutritional information for the MR product is displayed in table 2. Participants were given a weekly checklist form and an adverse effect form, provided alongside the MR product with clear instructions for completion. Participants in the intervention group received two supplies of the MR product: an initial supply of 72 packets was provided during baseline visit, and a second supply of 72 packets was given at the week 6 follow-up, together with detailed preparation instructions. To facilitate the transition from solid food to liquid meals, participants were also supplied with oats and chia seeds during the first two weeks of the intervention. Participants were instructed to mix one serving of oats (75 kcal) and chia seeds (34 kcal) with their MR. This initial two-week period served as a transition phase, implemented to allow patients to gradually adjust to the new diet regimen. This gradual transition was designed to reduce participant dropout rates associated with difficulty in dietary adaptation. Following the two-week transition phase, participants continued to replace two main meals with a serving of MR alone for the rest of the study. A visual analogue scale (VAS) for measuring subjective satiety was distributed during the baseline visit, requiring participants to record their satiety scores both before and after consuming meals. Study materials (checklist and adverse effect form) were returned at the Week 12 follow-up. A member of the study team contacted participants weekly to verify adherence to the dietary intervention and potential adverse events.

Control group

The control group received a single one-to-one dietary consultation session during the baseline collection. This consultation adhered to the standard Medical Nutrition Therapy for T2DM.29 The dietary consultation covered key areas including personalised weight loss goals, optimal macronutrient and micronutrients distribution, and recommended physical activity levels (online supplemental material 2). Participants’ suggested calorie intake was also calculated based on their current BMI. Furthermore, a VAS of satiety level was distributed to the participants during the baseline visit since participants needed to record it before and after their main meal intake.

Primary outcome

Glycaemic control

Glycaemic control in this study was assessed by measuring three parameters: HbA1c, fasting blood glucose and fasting insulin levels. For blood sample collection at both baseline and the 12th week follow-up sessions, participants were required to fast overnight for at least 10 hours for blood sample collection purposes. Peripheral vein blood samples were drawn by a trained phlebotomist. A total of 14 mL of blood was collected into specific tubes (plain tube, sodium fluoride tube and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tube) and immediately stored in an ice box for delivery. The HbA1c and fasting blood glucose were analysed at the medical laboratory of Hospital Canselor Tuanku Muhriz (HCTM), National University of Malaysia. Meanwhile, samples designated for insulin resistance level measurement were sent to an external lab (Lablink M Sdn. Bhd) as this analysis was not available in the medical laboratory of HCTM, National University of Malaysia. Based on the fasting blood glucose and fasting insulin levels, the Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated. The blood test results were mailed or given to the participants during their next follow-up visit. Contact details were provided along with the results so that participants could contact the investigators or study physicians with any questions.

Obesity indices

The obesity indices included weight, body mass index (BMI), body composition (measurements of fat percentage, fat mass, fat free mass and muscle mass), waist circumference, hip circumference and waist hip ratio (WHR). Anthropometric measurements were taken twice at the baseline, Week 6 and Week 12, with the mean value for subsequent data analysis. Body weight was measured in kilograms (kg) using an electronic weighing scale, InBody 270 with participants wearing only light indoor clothing. To ensure accuracy, the scale was positioned on a level, firm and sturdy surface, and participants were requested to remove items that could introduce measurement error, including socks. Weight was calculated to the nearest 100 g (0.1 kg). To confirm precision, two sequential measurements were required to match within 100 g. Height was measured in centimetres (cm) using the SECA 217 stadiometer. To eliminate parallax error, measurements were taken at eye level with the headboard and read to the nearest 0.1 cm. Participants’ BMI was calculated from the measured height and weight values according to the standard formula. For categorisation, BMI was categorised using the Clinical Practice Guideline Management of Obesity28 cut-off points as follows: underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–22.9 kg/m2), overweight (23–26.9 kg/m2) and obese (≥27.0 kg/m2). Next, waist circumference was measured using a SECA measuring tape (SECA, Germany). Participants were requested to stand up upright and breathe normally before beginning the measurement. The measurement site was located midway between the lowest palpable rib and the highest point of the hip bone. The measuring tape was held tightly against the waist put between these two anatomical spots. The spots are the highest point of the iliac crest and the last floating rib. At the conclusion of a regular expiry, the waist circumference is measured. The waist circumference was recorded at the conclusion of a normal expiration. Individuals were identified as being at increased risk if their measurements were >90 cm for men and >80 cm for women.31 For hip circumference, the identical measuring tape was used (SECA measuring tape). The participants were asked to stand upright with their arms at their sides and feet together. The measurer knelt on the participants’ right side, holding the tape horizontally across the maximal circumference across the buttocks. The Waist-to-Hip Ratio (WHR) was then calculated based on the measurement taken and was categorised based on gender, which is >0.90 for men and >0.85 for women.31 Prior to the measurements, the equipment used in this study was calibrated. All anthropometric measurements were performed with subjects wearing light clothing without socks and shoes.

Secondary outcome

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk markers

In this study, the cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk markers assessed included blood pressure and lipid profile. Ambulatory blood pressure was measured using an electronic blood pressure machine with an appropriate cuff size (Omron Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Readings were taken twice on the seated participants’ left arm, and the mean value of the two measurements was used for data analysis. High blood pressure reading was defined when systolic blood pressure (SBP) >140 mm Hg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) >90 mm Hg.32 Meanwhile, for the lipid profile, the blood samples collected for the sugar profile were also sent for lipid profile analysis, which consisted of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), total cholesterol and triglyceride level. The baseline and week 12 readings were compared.

Blood profile

Other biochemical profiles assessed included triglycerides, HDL-C, LDL-C, total cholesterol, triglycerides, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine level, sodium, potassium, total protein, albumin, bilirubin and ALT. These blood samples were analysed at the medical laboratory of Hospital Canselor Tunku Mukhriz, National University of Malaysia. The blood test results were mailed or given to the participants during the next follow-up visit. To address any participant inquiries, contact details for the study investigators or physician were included with the results.

For metabolic gene expression profile analysis, an additional 12 mL of fasting blood was collected using an EDTA tube from 12 participants (six from the control group and six from the intervention group) at baseline and Week 12. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were isolated from the blood samples following the protocol33 and stored in a cryobox at −80°C prior to RNA extraction.

Metabolic gene profile analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the PBMC using the Blood/Cell RNA Mini Kit with DNase from Geneaid, following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. All extracted RNA samples were treated with DNase to remove DNA contamination. The RNA quality was determined using Nanodrop spectrophotometer. Pure RNA had an A260/A280 ratio of 1.9–2.1. All RNA samples were stored at −80°C until gene expression analysis. All RNA samples were sent to the Genomax Technologies laboratory for gene expression analysis. This analysis was performed using the nCounter Human Metabolic Pathway by Nanostring following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. The nSolver software was used to analyse the gene expression dataset to determine the changes that occurred to the metabolic gene networks and pathways.

Dietary data

At the beginning of the study, an experienced research dietitian conducted dietary assessments for each participant. To monitor dietary intake throughout the study period, participants were required to maintain a 3-day dietary record at baseline, Week 6 and Week 12.34 This record included documenting the food and beverages consumed by the participants over two weekdays and one weekend day within a week. Participants were instructed to provide detailed information about their meals, including the types of foods consumed, quantities, preparation methods, brand names of food items, sauces and ingredients used. To assist participants in estimating portion sizes accurately, they were shown pictures of various household measurements based on the Atlas of Food Exchanges & Portion Sizes.35 Additionally, any consumption of supplements, such as vitamins or minerals, by the participants was also recorded. The mean caloric and nutrient intake data collected from these dietary records were analysed using the Nutritionist Pro software (Axxya Systems, Stafford, Texas, USA) for computer-based analysis.

Physical activity

The physical activity levels of the participants were assessed by using the validated Malay version of the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) for Malaysian population.36 This questionnaire was primarily self-administered, however, for participants with literacy issues, the questionnaire was administered by the researcher. The GPAQ collects information across three domains of physical activity which are work, transport and recreation, as well as the average time per day spent in sedentary behaviour. The GPAQ data was scored in minutes per day (min/d). Based on the guidelines provided by this questionnaire, the metabolic equivalent and total daily energy expenditure for each participant were calculated using the MET value. This standardised instrument enabled the researchers to quantify the participants’ engagement in various physical activities and determine their overall energy expenditure levels.

Satiety level

To evaluate the perceived satiety of participants after consuming the MR (in the treatment group) or a regular meal (in the control group), a VAS questionnaire was utilised.37 Participants were instructed to complete the VAS immediately before consuming their meal/MR, and then again at specified intervals of 30 min, 60 min, 120 min, 180 min and 240 min after consumption. The average appetite score was calculated at each time point using a formula as described and shown below.38 This approach allowed the researchers to quantify and compare the subjective feelings of satiety experienced by participants across the two groups over an extended period following their respective meal or MR consumption.

Average appetite=desire to eat+hunger+(10−fullness)+prospective food consumption.

Quality of life

The study used a validated Malay translation of the ADDQOL-19 questionnaire to evaluate the QoL of participants. This tool has demonstrated good validity and reliability in previous research.39 The ADDQOL-19 comprises 21 items, out of which 19 items assess diabetes-dependent domains, while the remaining two items provide an overview of general aspects.40 The questionnaire uses a Likert scale which ranges from −3 to 3. The overall ADDQOL-19 score was calculated by combining the averages of the two domains and the equation shown below. Higher score indicated a better QoL. Finally, to facilitate accurate responses, researchers were available during administration to assist participants in comprehending and completing the questionnaire items.

Overall ADDQOL-19 score=((2×Present life domain average)+Diabetes-dependent domain average)/3

Adverse reaction record

Based on a previous study,22 a few adverse events had been recorded, such as gastrointestinal discomfort, flatulence and nausea. As a result, an adverse events form was given to the participants at the start of the study, and those who experienced adverse events were required to report them immediately. Those who experienced an adverse event were eligible to receive treatment at Klinik Primer, HCTM.

Adherence and withdrawal/discontinuation

Participants were promptly removed from the research if they: (1) experienced a serious adverse effect, (2) became pregnant, (3) initiated or modified the use of drugs or supplements mentioned in the eligibility criteria or (4) no longer fulfilled the inclusion criteria. Participants in the MR group were required to return all MR bags, whether empty or not, during the visits on Week 6 and 12. The bags used in the trial were calculated, and participants were eliminated from the study if there was more than 30% of the product remaining inside the bags. Furthermore, participants had the option to discontinue their involvement in the study at any given moment. Despite elimination or voluntary withdrawal, all participants were included in the final analysis using the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, which included those eliminated for inadequate adherence.

Data analysis

This intervention study aimed to determine the efficacy of partial diabetes-specific MR on weight reduction, glycaemic control and metabolic gene expression among overweight and obese patients with T2DM. An ITT analysis was employed in this study to minimise bias and uphold the integrity of the original randomisation. All of the statistical analyses were performed by using the IBM SPSS V.29.0 software with a significance level of p<0.05. Normality of data was analysed by using the Shapiro-Wilk test and significant value p>0.05 which indicates normal distribution. The mean differences of the baseline data between the intervention and control group were analysed using the Independent-t or Mann-Whitney test for continuous parameters.

On the other hand, χ2 was selected for the analysis of categorical variables. The effects of Resurge DM over time, the effect of group, and its interaction were determined by using mixed design repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). The Mauchly’s test of Sphericity was used to analyse the assumption of sphericity for each dependent variable. In cases where the assumption was violated p≤0.05, the Greenhouse-Geisser adjustment was applied to adjust the degrees of freedom. Bonferroni’s post-hoc test was used to analyse the specific indication of differences within and between the periods of supplement consumption. Subgroup analysis was conducted when required.

Discussion

This randomised controlled trial was conducted to assess the efficacy of a diabetes-specific MR product in the management of T2DM in overweight and obese individuals with T2DM. This study employed a comprehensive approach to manage T2DM by considering a few outcomes such as weight loss, controlled blood sugar levels, blood pressure, satiety level, QoL and changes in the gene expression profile. The incorporation of cost-benefit analysis additionally improved the practical applicability of the results, providing a valuable perspective for healthcare practitioners and policymakers, especially in Malaysia.44 In addition, identifying the metabolic gene expression profile clarified the molecular pathways responsible for the effects of the intervention, thereby enhancing the scientific comprehension of T2DM.45 46 Nevertheless, the study has certain constraints that require cautious interpretation of the findings. Firstly, the accuracy of compliance measurement was potentially compromised as it relied on the returned MR containers, hence potentially undermining the validity of the adherence data. Secondly, the reliance on self-reported assessments for nutritional consumption, physical activity and satiety level introduced the potential for recall bias and mistakes. Thirdly, the calculated sample size, while sufficient for an initial inquiry, may restrict the ability to apply the findings to a broader population. Finally, this study did not assess postprandial glucose due to practical constraints related to time and financial resources. Although there are several limitations, the study’s results are anticipated to offer crucial insights into the efficacy of MR treatment in managing type 2 diabetes.

The potential enhancements in glycaemic management, weight reduction and QoL have the ability to greatly improve clinical practice and patient outcomes. The study’s identification of alterations in metabolic gene expression may facilitate the development of personalised dietary therapies according to individuals' genetic profiles. Ultimately, this study has the capacity to provide a valuable foundation of evidence supporting the utilisation of diabetes-specific MR products in the management of T2DM. It has the potential to provide valuable insights for the creation of focused interventions that enhance the well-being of individuals with T2DM based on the factors associated with T2DM and decrease the overall impact of this condition on public health worldwide.47 It is advisable to conduct additional research with extended observation periods and larger groups of participants in order to confirm and build on these findings.23

Ethics and dissemination

This study was approved by the National University of Malaysia’s Human Research Ethics Committee (JEP-2024-695). This study is registered on International Standard Randomised Controlled Trial Number and in accordance with Good Clinical Practice Guidelines and the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki 1964. Potential participants were provided with verbal and written explanations about the informed consent process prior to enrolment.

All personal information was kept strictly confidential, and participation was anonymous. Participants were assigned a study ID, which was kept separated from any personal information collected. A master list with identifiable information and study IDs was protected and stored at the Centre for Health Ageing and Wellness (H-CARE). In the event of participant withdrawal, permission was sought to use data collected up to that point; however, if permission was denied, the participant’s data were immediately destroyed. Any changes to the study protocol were submitted to the ethics committee and trial registry for approval. Modifications were authorised and signed by the principal investigator, researcher, ethics committees and ISRCTN registry. Following data collection, analysis and review of findings, manuscripts were prepared for submission to peer-reviewed journals and results presented in national and international conferences. Study findings were also disseminated through social media. Data was published regardless of outcomes, with the National University of Malaysia retaining the right to publish.

Data monitoring committee

A formal Data Monitoring Committee (DMC) was not established for this study due to the classification of the intervention as not involving a high-risk group. However, all adverse events and adherence to the protocol were monitored by the research team and reported periodically to the principal investigator, the ethics committee of the project and Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia every 6 months.
