Antimicrobial resistance patterns and difficult-to-treat resistance in uropathogens among diabetic and non-diabetic patients: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ankita Priyadarshini, Priyal Kalola, Hemantkumar Patadia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a global challenge, data comparing Difficult-to-Treat Resistance (DTR) in diabetic versus non-diabetic urinary tract infection (UTI) patients is scarce. This study uniquely evaluates DTR and first-line resistance patterns to inform targeted empirical treatment strategies for these distinct patient cohorts.MethodsA prospective observational cross-sectional study was conducted from August 2021 to January 2024, at a tertiary care teaching hospital in Vadodara, Gujarat, India. During the period, 2408 patients with symptoms of UTI were screened for urine culture and sensitivity, of which 954 individuals tested positive for UTIs.ResultOf the 2,408 symptomatic patients screened, 954 (39.61%) tested positive. The within-group UTI positivity rate was 27.4% (280/1,023) among diabetic patients and 48.7% (674/1,385) among non-diabetic patients. Among the isolated uropathogens, Escherichia coli (E. coli) (56.78%, 62.90%) was the predominant organism followed by Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) (13.57%, 5.78%) and Klebsiella spp. (13.21%, 17.35%) in diabetic and non-diabetic respectively. Among all the isolated uropathogen tetracycline (81.25%) and penicillin (71.86%) groups of antibiotics showed high resistance in diabetic patients, whereas fluoroquinolones had highest resistance in non-diabetic with 67.59%. However, the carbapenem, phosphonic and nitrofuran group of antibiotics showed least resistance in both diabetic and non-diabetic populations. Among all the antimicrobial drugs tested in our study, the first line of drugs was further analyzed, and P. aeruginosa had the highest DTR in both diabetic and non-diabetic individuals.ConclusionDespite lower culture positivity rates among diabetic patients, diabetic UTI cases demonstrated substantially higher AMR burden and DTR compared to non-diabetics. P. aeruginosa showed highest DTR prevalence. Penicillin and tetracycline resistance were markedly elevated in diabetic UTIs, while nitrofurantoin and fosfomycin maintained high effectiveness in both groups. These findings emphasize that diabetes is strongly associated with greater therapeutic complexity and multidrug resistance burden, necessitating resistance-guided empirical therapy and regional antibiogram surveillance for optimal UTI management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13969-1.Keywords: Urinary tract infections, Diabetes mellitus, Drug resistance, Bacterial, Microbial sensitivity tests, Antimicrobial stewardship, Cross-sectional studies

Background

Any infection caused by the microbial infiltration of the urinary tract tissues is referred to as a urinary tract infection (UTI) [1]. Anatomically, UTIs can be classified as infections of the upper and lower tracts. Men can get prostatitis, epididymitis, cystitis, and urethritis as lower-tract illnesses. However, pyelonephritis, an upper-tract illness, is identical in both sexes [2]. Patients with diabetes mellitus are among those who most frequently contract UTI due to glycosuria, immune dysfunction, and bladder abnormalities, which can cause significant morbidity if left undiagnosed or untreated [3]. Diabetes mellitus is characterized by raised blood glucose levels brought on by abnormalities in either insulin action or synthesis, or both [4]. The upper urinary tract and bladder are generally sterile under normal conditions, though the distal urethra may harbor commensal flora. Adhesions in the urinary tract caused by pili and outer membrane proteins allow uropathogens to cause UTIs [1]. Obesity, feminine sex, and prostate syndrome in males, nausea, vomiting, chills, temperature spikes, exhaustion, dysuria, urgency, lower back, flank, and abdomen discomfort, and in more extreme situations, altered consciousness are examples of risk factors for UTI. Moreover, certain risk factors for UTI include inadequate immunity, bladder dysfunction, and glycosuria—all of which are linked to diabetes mellitus [3, 5]. UTIs can be categorized as complicated or uncomplicated based on their severity. Uncomplicated urinary tract infections primarily involve the lower urinary tract, are often mild, more common in women, and typically resolve spontaneously without treatment, with patients seeking medical attention primarily for symptomatic relief [3, 6]. In more than 95% of cases, bacteria are the common causative agents of UTIs. Most urinary tract infection occurs due to uropathogen or normal flora of urogenital tract. More than 80% of UTIs are caused exclusively by Escherichia coli (E. coli), the most frequent uropathogen [7]. Other common organisms causing UTI are Enterococcus, Klebsiella spp., Pseudomonas spp. Proteus mirabilis, Streptococcus, Staphylococcus aureus and Candida spp. [8]. Patients having symptoms of UTI are prescribed empirical treatment before the diagnosis of the causative pathogens and its confirmation. This window of empirical treatment can become more challenging when organisms are resistant to a specific antibiotic or newly develop resistance during the empirical treatment window. Prophylactic antibiotics are occasionally used to treat recurrent urinary tract infections. This requires a six- to eight-month pharmaceutical regimen [9, 10]. Treatment with antibiotics usually lasts anywhere from three days to six weeks. First-generation cephalosporins, fosfomycin, nitrofurantoin, and sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim are among the first-line treatments used for UTIs [11]. Since first line antibiotics are the preferred choices during empirical treatment, resistance against them becomes a matter of clinical concern.

To address this, the concept of Difficult-to-Treat Resistance (DTR) was introduced. DTR is defined as resistance to all high-efficacy, low-toxicity first-line agents, specifically all β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, carbapenems, and fluoroquinolones [12]. Unlike traditional categories like Multidrug Resistance (MDR) or Extensively Drug-Resistant (XDR)—which are based solely on the number of antibiotic classes - DTR identifies a specific subset of infections where no reliable, safe empirical options remain. This necessitates the use of ‘reserve’ drugs that may be more toxic or less effective, leading to higher mortality rates and prolonged hospitalizations. Recent evidence suggests that DTR development is strongly influenced by prior antibiotic exposure and healthcare-associated contacts. Therefore, utilizing the DTR designation provides clinicians with a more practical assessment of treatment limitations than older classification systems [12, 13].

Antibiotic selection for empirical UTI treatment is influenced by geographic resistance patterns, infection severity (uncomplicated vs. complicated), and patient comorbidities. In India, increasing resistance to first-line agents such as fluoroquinolones and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole necessitates regular surveillance of local antibiograms [14, 15]. Despite increasing antimicrobial resistance in urinary tract infections, limited data exist comparing resistance patterns and DTR between diabetic and non-diabetic populations in India. This study aims to address this gap by evaluating uropathogen distribution, antimicrobial resistance profiles, and DTR burden in both groups to guide empirical treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and study setting

This was a prospective observational cross-sectional study conducted at a tertiary care teaching hospital and clinical laboratory of Vadodara district situated in East Central Gujarat from August 2021 to January 2024. Consecutive patients presenting with clinical symptoms of UTI (dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain, and/or fever) were enrolled. Culture results and antibiotic sensitivity data were recorded at a single encounter; no longitudinal patient follow-up was performed. This cross-sectional design was chosen to capture the prevalence of uropathogens and resistance patterns across the study period, with prospective data collection ensuring standardized specimen handling and data recording.

Data collection

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee for Human Research (PU-IECHR) with the approval number PUIECHR/PIMSR/00/081734/4006 and informed consent was obtained from participants. A predesigned data collection form was used to record patient demographics, clinical history, and relevant study information. Data collected included age, sex, medical history, and, for diabetic patients, disease duration and glycemic parameters (HbA1c, random glucose or fasting blood glucose levels). Study variables, including unique participant identification number, bacterial isolates, Gram-stain status, and antibiotic susceptibility and resistance patterns, were entered into Microsoft Excel for data management. Data confidentiality, privacy, and integrity were maintained throughout the study.

Patient classification

Participants were categorized as diabetic (DM) based on [16]: (1) physician-confirmed diagnosis in medical records; (2) fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL; (3) HbA1c ≥ 6.5%; or (4) random glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL with symptoms. Non-diabetic (NDM) status required no prior DM history and fasting glucose < 100 mg/dL or HbA1c < 5.7%.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion Criteria: Adults (≥ 18 years) presenting with ≥ 2 clinical UTI symptoms (e.g., dysuria, frequency) and providing informed consent.

Exclusion Criteria: To ensure diagnostic and data integrity, we excluded patients with: Genital tract disease, neurogenic bladder, or recent renal transplantation, asymptomatic catheterization. Conditions interfering with HbA1c accuracy, including hemoglobinopathy, hemolytic anemia, sickle cell disease, recent blood transfusion (within 3 months), or severe weight loss (> 10% within 3 months). Incomplete or missing records were also excluded from final analysis.

Sample collection and processing

The selected individuals were directed to collect midstream clean-catch urine in sterile, wide-mouth containers following peri-urethral cleansing to minimize commensal contamination. Samples were labeled with laboratory identification numbers for records. Samples were processed immediately or refrigerated at 4 °C if a delay > 2 h was anticipated to preserve bacterial count integrity. Urine culture was performed by adhering to the standard protocol. The urine specimens were directly inoculated onto three distinct media plates (Nutrient agar, Blood agar, and MacConkey agar) using a sterilized wire loop containing 1 µl. The streaked plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. After 24 h, cultures were observed and growth of at least 105 CFU/ml was considered as significant bacteriuria [17]. For further analysis, a single colony was selected using a sterile wire loop and suspended in nutritional broth. The identification of uropathogens was done by Gram staining and VITEK-2 system was employed to perform thorough confirmation of isolated uropathogens. Candida spp. isolated on blood agar and nutrient agar were identified using Gram staining morphology (budding yeast and pseudohyphae) with species-level confirmation via VITEK-2 automated identification system using fungal-specific biochemical modules. For antibiotic sensitivity test, VITEK 2 Compact System was used. For Gram-negative bacilli, AST-N405, AST-N406, and AST-N235 cards were utilized, while AST-P628 cards were used for Gram-positive cocci. A standardized saline suspension of each isolate was then prepared for automated identification. Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (AST) and Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) determinations were performed using appropriate AST cards in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [18]. Isolates were categorized as sensitive (effectively inhibited at standard therapeutic concentrations), intermediate (showing reduced or borderline susceptibility), or resistant (not inhibited at clinically achievable concentrations). Final interpretations were made using the manufacturer’s protocol (bioMérieux) and the system’s Advanced Expert System as resistant (R), intermediate (I), or sensitive (S).

Data analysis

All the collected data were coded and arranged in Microsoft Office Excel followed by data cleaning. Descriptive data was analyzed by using Jamovi 2.8.28. (jamovi (Version 2.5)) Different variants like gender, and isolated etiologies were present by percentages and frequencies, continuous variables such as age were present by mean and standard deviation (SD) were analyzed by Jamovi 2.8.28. Descriptive statistics were performed for the socio-demographic and clinical variables. The categorical variables were compared using a Chi-square test with 95% CI, analyzed using IBM SPSS 20 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Versions 20.0 & 21.0. Australia) and Rstudio was used to create chord diagram (RStudio 2024.04.0 Build 735 © 2009–2024 Posit Software, PBC). Following Kadri et al. (2018), DTR was defined as non-susceptibility (resistant or intermediate) to all tested agents within three specific first-line drug classes: fluoroquinolones, beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations (e.g., piperacillin-tazobactam), and carbapenems (e.g., meropenem, imipenem). Isolates missing susceptibility data for any of these three classes due to incomplete laboratory panels were excluded from DTR classification but retained in organism-level prevalence counts. This requirement means DTR rates reported reflect only isolates with complete phenotypic characterization across all three classes, avoiding potential misclassification from incomplete data.

The DTR rate was calculated as: (Number of DTR isolates of an organism/Total isolates of that organism tested) * 100.

Results

UTI culture positivity

During the time period from August 2021 to January 2024 total 2408 patients visited the tertiary care teaching hospital and clinical laboratory of Vadodara district with symptoms of UTI. Out of 2408 patients, 1023 had a confirmed history of diabetes and 1385 were non-diabetics. Out of the total 2,408 symptomatic patients screened during the study period, 954 were culture-positive for UTI, yielding an overall UTI positivity rate of 39.61%. In the non-diabetic group of patients, 674 (48.66%) were characterized with positive UTI as compared to 280 (27%) patients from the diabetic group. Among all diabetic patients (Mean age: 53.18 ± 12.59 years), 114 were male and 166 were females, whereas in non-diabetic patients (Mean age: 54.88 ± 17.56 years), 223 were male and 451 were female. The ratio of male: female having UTI in diabetic versus non-diabetic subgroups was significantly higher (p = 0.025; CI: 95%) indicating an increased incidence of UTI in diabetic males. In diabetic individuals, 236 patients out of 280 were having random blood glucose level 110–200 mg/dl and 44 patients were having blood glucose level > 200 mg/dl (p = 0.001). As seen in Table 1, amongst the patients with diabetes who were tested positive for any UTI; 189 had less than five years of diabetic history, 83 had five to ten years history, and 8 had more than ten years history of diabetes (p = 0.001) (Table 1).

Etiology of uropathogen in patients

First line of drug resistance pattern of uropathogens

Commonly preferred first-line drug classes for uncomplicated UTIs are nitrofuran, sulphonamides, phosphonic, first-generation cephalosporins and fluoroquinolones. First line drugs are a popular choice because they have a low risk of side effects and developing resistance [19]. The choice of the first line of drug becomes easy after performing an Antibiotic sensitivity test and determining its resistance pattern (resistant, intermediate, sensitive) based on which empirical treatment can be prescribed. Multivariate predictors of multidrug resistance (MDR)

To identify independent predictors of multidrug resistance (MDR) and address potential confounding factors, a multivariate binary logistic regression analysis was performed with MDR as the outcome variable and diabetes status, age (continuous), and gender as covariates (n = 954). An overall MDR rate of 76.9% (734/954) was observed, with significantly higher rates in diabetic patients compared to non-diabetic patients (82.5% vs. 74.6%; χ² p = 0.011) [Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study cohort are presented in Supplementary Table S1]. On multivariate analysis, diabetes was identified as an independent predictor of MDR, indicating that diabetic patients had 64.3% higher odds of harboring a multidrug-resistant uropathogen after controlling for age and gender (Table 5). Age was also a significant independent predictor, with each additional year of age associated with a 1.6% increase in MDR odds [Supplementary Table S2]. However, Gender was not independently associated with MDR. Organism-level analysis revealed that the diabetes–MDR association was most pronounced for P. aeruginosa (86.8% MDR in diabetics vs. 59.0% in non-diabetics; p = 0.013) and Klebsiella spp. (83.8% vs. 64.1%; p = 0.040), while no significant difference was observed for E. coli (Table 6). Full model statistics including β coefficients and model fit indices are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Discussion

Diabetes mellitus is a well-established risk factor for urinary tract infections, as glycosuria and impaired immune function collectively promote uropathogen colonization [4]. Our study recorded an overall UTI positivity rate of 39.61% among all symptomatic patients screened, surpassing the 35.1% prevalence reported in a multicentric cross-sectional study conducted across India [20]. In our current study, the culture positivity rate of UTIs among diabetic individuals was observed to be 27%, and a higher culture positivity rate of 48.66% in non-diabetic individuals. This discrepancy diverges from the study by Ekwealor Chito Clare et al. (2016) [21], which reported a UTI prevalence of 27.39% among diabetic individuals and 17.83% among non-diabetic individuals, highlighting potential variability in UTI prevalence rates across different populations or study settings. Importantly, the more clinically significant finding in our study is the distinctly adverse resistance profile among diabetic UTI cases - including higher P. aeruginosa isolation, elevated carbapenem resistance, and greater DTR burden - which mechanistically corroborates the established biological susceptibility of diabetic individuals to more resistant and treatment-challenging uropathogens.

In this study, we observed notable variations in UTI prevalence across different age groups, with individuals aged 41 to 60 years exhibiting the highest prevalence (38.57%). This aligns with previous findings reporting higher UTI rates among individuals aged 40–49 (54.2%) and 50–59 years (34.9%), as well as studies in Nigeria and Saudi where the 30–60 age bracket remained the most affected [17, 21–23]. The predominance of UTIs in the 41–60 year age group (51.42% in diabetics, 33.67% in non-diabetics) likely reflects the convergence of multiple risk factors in middle age: declining innate immune competence, increasing prevalence of structural urological abnormalities (prostatic hypertrophy in men, pelvic floor dysfunction in women), and in the diabetic cohort, the additive effects of peripheral neuropathy impairing bladder sensation and emptying (neurogenic bladder), glycosuria promoting bacterial growth, and cumulative prior antibiotic exposure [24]. The relatively lower representation of the > 60 age group in the diabetic UTI cohort may reflect mortality selection or more aggressive infection control in elderly diabetic patients managed in specialized diabetic clinics [25].

The etiological agents causing UTIs in diabetic individuals were similar to those in non-diabetic individuals, although differences in the frequency of isolates were noted (Table 2). Consistent with previous studies [17, 26], the majority of isolates were gram negative bacteria, with E. coli being the major pathogen at 40.38%, aligning with other reports [27–29]. The high incidence of E. coli is attributed to its commensal presence in the intestines and frequent infections due to fecal contamination resulting from poor hygiene. Contrarily, our findings differ from those reported by [30], which identified Coagulase-Negative Staphylococcus as the most prevalent isolate (37.5%). Furthermore, our study identified Enterococcus faecalis in 2.85% of diabetic and 5.63% of non-diabetic individuals, contradicting earlier findings [26] that reported Enterococcus faecalis as the least common UTI pathogen. Conversely, we found Staphylococcus aureus to be the least common causative agent. Additionally, yeasts, particularly Candida spp. were identified as a common influencing factor for UTIs, with Candida albicans isolated in 1.04% of diabetic and 1.67% of non-diabetic individuals, results comparable to a study in Turkey reporting a 2.2% incidence in diabetic groups [17].

The comparison of antibiotic resistance between diabetic and non-diabetic individuals reveals significant disparities, with diabetic individuals generally showing higher resistance rates. In the present study, among the diabetics, the resistance of gram-negative bacteria against the common antibiotics, i.e. tetracycline, penicillin, cephalosporins were 81.25%, 71.86%, 65.24% respectively. However, the study by Juan P. Horcajada et al. [31] found that quinolones-resistant uropathogens are isolated more frequently in diabetics contradicting our findings. According to the published literature, the nitrofuran class of antibiotics has demonstrated efficacy in treating urinary tract infections in both diabetic and non-diabetic individuals [26]. Our findings are similar to these results, indicating that nitrofuran, along with carbapenem and phosphonic, exhibit substantial susceptibility against the isolated uropathogens. Upon comparing the susceptibility of fluoroquinolones between diabetic and non-diabetic cohorts, our study observed a susceptibility rate of 66.64%. This finding diverges from the results reported in the study conducted by Mohapatra S et al. [32], which documented a fluoroquinolone susceptibility rate of 50.2% against Gram-negative bacteria.

In our cohort, the elevated carbapenem resistance (44.29% in DM vs. 35.68% in NDM) is clinically alarming. This trend is likely attributable to factors specific to the diabetic population, including frequent prior antibiotic exposure due to recurrent UTIs, and increased healthcare contact, which facilitates the acquisition of healthcare-associated pathogens [33]. Additionally, the immunocompromised state associated with chronic hyperglycemia may promote the persistence of resistant strains. These carbapenem resistance rates significantly exceed those reported by the European Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance Network (EARS-Net) for community-onset E. coli UTIs (~ 2–5%), suggesting a higher burden of healthcare-associated pathogens in our tertiary care setting [34].

Similarly, the high tetracycline resistance (81.25% in diabetics) aligns with global trends driven by widespread agricultural use, promoting resistance via horizontal gene transfer in gut flora [35]. Conversely, the low resistance to nitrofurantoin and fosfomycin in our study, particularly for E. coli, supports their continued utility as primary oral agents for uncomplicated UTIs, consistent with current EAU and IDSA guidelines [11].

Among all first-line drugs investigated in this study, P. aeruginosa demonstrated a notable DTR of 5.35% in diabetic patients and 3.56% in non-diabetics, emerging as the most prevalent DTR pathogen in both cohorts (41.6% and 55.81%, respectively). This burden significantly surpasses that of other uropathogens. While our observed rates are high, they are part of a broader trend; for instance, Kyungmin Huh et al. (2020) reported that P. aeruginosa (17.7%) constituted the majority of DTR isolates in a large-scale Korean study [36]. Our findings align with the escalating global trends described by Cosentino et al. (2024), which conclude that DTR criteria provide a more accurate clinical classification for P. aeruginosa than traditional MDR/XDR categories. By identifying infections with a near-total loss of high-efficacy, low-toxicity options, the DTR designation directly correlates with higher mortality risks and limited therapeutic alternatives in Gram-negative uropathogens [13].

The elevated multidrug resistance (MDR) and difficult-to-treat resistance (DTR) rates observed in our cohort, particularly among diabetic patients, likely reflect the cumulative consequences of antimicrobial misuse in this population. Diabetic patients frequently receive empirical antibiotics for presumed UTI without awaiting culture confirmation, often resulting in repeated exposure to broad-spectrum agents. Our findings underscore the critical importance of antimicrobial stewardship programs that emphasize: culture-guided rather than empirical prescribing, adherence to local antibiograms, de-escalation from broad-spectrum to targeted agents once susceptibility results are available and education of patients and healthcare providers regarding appropriate antibiotic use. Implementation of such stewardship approaches in tertiary care settings has been shown to reduce resistance burden and improve clinical outcomes, making it a priority for institutions treating high-risk diabetic populations.

A limitation of this study is that UTIs were not classified based on factors such as prior hospitalization, urinary catheterization, and previous healthcare exposure on bacterial profiles and antibiotic resistance patterns. Hence, their impact on resistance profiles could not be assessed separately. Furthermore, although diabetes status and advancing age were identified as independent predictors of MDR, the relatively low explanatory power of the logistic regression model suggests that other unmeasured factors, including previous antibiotic exposure and underlying clinical conditions, may also contribute to MDR risk. In addition, long-term outcomes, recurrence of infection, and the influence of glycemic control on UTI outcomes were not longitudinally evaluated. Future studies should address these factors to provide a more comprehensive understanding of antimicrobial resistance and UTI outcomes.

Conclusion

This prospective cross-sectional study of 2,408 symptomatic patients identified an overall UTI culture positivity rate of 39.61%, with significantly lower within-group rates among diabetic patients (27.4%) compared to non-diabetics (48.7%). Despite the lower positivity rate, diabetic UTI cases demonstrated substantially higher antimicrobial resistance burden, particularly to first-line agents, and significantly elevated difficult-to-treat resistance (DTR) rates. Our key novel findings are: (1) Diabetic patients harbor disproportionately resistant uropathogens, especially P. aeruginosa (OR 2.56), which showed highest DTR prevalence across both cohorts; (2) penicillin and tetracycline resistance is markedly elevated in diabetic UTIs (72% and 81%, respectively), rendering these agents unreliable for empirical treatment; and (3) nitrofurantoin and fosfomycin remain highly effective alternatives with low resistance in both groups. Overall, these findings emphasize that diabetes may not necessarily increase UTI occurrence within symptomatic populations but is strongly associated with greater therapeutic complexity, multidrug resistance burden, and the need for diabetes-informed antimicrobial stewardship. Regional antibiogram surveillance and resistance-guided empirical therapy are therefore essential for optimizing UTI management and mitigating the progression of antimicrobial resistance.
