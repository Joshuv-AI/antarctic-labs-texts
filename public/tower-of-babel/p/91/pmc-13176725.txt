Comprehensive Nursing Management of Anticoagulation and Heart Failure Surveillance in a Chinese Patient With Mirror‐Image Dextrocardia Post‐MitraClip Surgery: A First Case Report
Authors: Juelian Li, Chun Wu, Chao Shang, Yu Deng, Jinxia Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Background Mitral regurgitation (MR) is the valent type of valvular heart disease, caused by primary leaflet lesions or annular dilatation secondary to left ventricular dysfunction. It can lead to a series of complications such as volume overload, exacerbated pulmonary congestion, and heart failure, significantly impacting patient prognosis. Surgical mitral valve repair or replacement is currently the gold standard for MR treatment but is associated with substantial trauma, numerous complications, and poor tolerance in elderly high‐risk patients. As an emerging minimally invasive technique, transcatheter mitral valve repair with MitraClip alleviates MR by tethering the edges of the anterior and posterior leaflets to create a bileaflet orifice (Peng et al. 2025). Compared with surgical procedures, it offers advantages of minimal trauma and rapid recovery, making it more suitable for elderly high‐risk patients with multiple comorbidities. Mirror‐image dextrocardia is a rare anatomical variation with an incidence of approximately 1/10,000. The heart is located in the right hemithorax with the apex pointing to the right lower quadrant, and the positions of great vessels and cardiac chambers are completely reversed from normal. This unique anatomy poses significant challenges to MitraClip surgery. This article summarizes the nursing experience of the first domestic case of a patient with mirror‐image dextrocardia complicated by MR who underwent MitraClip implantation, aiming to explore the key perioperative management points for such high‐risk cases and provide references for future similar surgeries.

Case Presentation

We report the case of a 72‐year‐old female with mirror‐image dextrocardia and severe functional MR who underwent successful TEER using the MitraClip system (Abbott). Due to the anatomical reversal, several procedural adaptations were required, including modified transesophageal echocardiography views, adjustments to atrial septal puncture technique, and novel strategies for delivering the clip and resolving the “aortic hug.” [1]

Discussion

A 72‐year‐old female patient, Ms. Xie, was admitted to our hospital on November 22, 2023, due to “recurrent shortness of breath with chest tightness after activity for more than 4 years, which worsened over the past year.” Her medical history included stable angina pectoris, chronic heart failure, non‐rheumatic mitral regurgitation, lacunar infarction, persistent atrial fibrillation (CHA2DS2‐VASc score: 4 points), dextrocardia, and mixed hyperlipidemia. She denied a history of hypertension or diabetes mellitus. Physical examination: Consciousness was clear, with pale complexion and jugular vein distension. Moist rales were audible in both lungs. No precordial prominence was noted in the right chest. The heart rate was 98 beats per minute with an irregular rhythm. A grade 3/6 systolic scratchy murmur was heard at the mitral valve area. The liver and spleen were not palpable below the costal margins. Mild pitting edema was present in both lower extremities. Auxiliary examinations: Echocardiography revealed left atrial enlargement, normal left ventricular systolic function (EF: 67%), severe mitral regurgitation, moderate tricuspid regurgitation, and mild to moderate aortic regurgitation; mitral regurgitation area was 12 cm2, regurgitation volume was 45 mL, pulmonary artery systolic pressure was 60 mmHg, and LVEDD was 58 mm. Coronary angiography showed no significant stenosis (the diagnosis of coronary heart disease was mainly based on the previous history of angina pectoris). Cardiac CT confirmed mirror‐image dextrocardia accompanied by abdominal visceral situs inversus. Diagnoses: (1) Severe mitral regurgitation; (2) Acute exacerbation of chronic heart failure (NYHA class III); (3) Coronary heart disease (based on typical symptoms and risk factors, despite negative coronary angiography); (4) Persistent atrial fibrillation; (5) Dextrocardia, and so forth. After admission, relevant examinations were completed, and a multidisciplinary consultation was conducted. Considering the patient's indication for MitraClip surgery, she underwent general anesthesia for MitraClip implantation on December 7, 2023. One clip was implanted in the A2‐P2 region, and the surgery was successful without complications. Postoperative echocardiography showed a significant reduction in mitral regurgitation (regurgitation area decreased to 4 cm2, regurgitation volume to 15 mL, and transvalvular pressure gradient to 3 mmHg), pulmonary artery pressure decreased to 35 mmHg, LVEDD reduced to 52 mm, and heart function improved (6‐min walking distance increased from 250 m preoperatively to 420 m on the 7th postoperative day, and NT‐proBNP decreased from 1500 pg/mL preoperatively to 600 pg/mL postoperatively) (Table 1).

Diagnosis and Treatment Analysis

Preoperative Preparation

The anatomical structure of mirror‐image dextrocardia is completely opposite to that of normal individuals, presenting numerous difficulties for MitraClip surgery, such as the need for corresponding adjustments in intracardiac operation positioning and transducer orientation. Therefore, detailed preoperative echocardiography and CT examinations were performed to accurately assess the course of cardiac great vessels, providing a basis for formulating the surgical plan. Meanwhile, anesthesia and surgical risk assessments were conducted, and preoperative preparations were improved, including discontinuing anticoagulant drugs, preventing infections, correcting anemia and hypoalbuminemia, and stabilizing underlying diseases such as coronary heart disease and heart failure. Echocardiography was used to clarify the etiology, type, and severity of MR, as well as the anatomical morphology and function of leaflets, annulus, left atrium, and left ventricle. Coronary CT was performed to evaluate the presence of coronary artery stenosis and calcification, and to determine the contraindications for surgical procedures. Preoperative laboratory examinations, including blood routine, biochemistry, and coagulation function, were also completed to correct electrolyte imbalances, supplement albumin, and improve the body's tolerance. For this patient with persistent atrial fibrillation (CHA2DS2‐VASc score: 4 points), warfarin was routinely administered orally for anticoagulation preoperatively, with INR controlled between 2 and 3, and switched to subcutaneous injection of low‐molecular‐weight heparin calcium 3 days before surgery.

Intraoperative Cooperation

MitraClip surgery for mirror‐image dextrocardia was performed under general anesthesia, and the anesthesia method and drug selection were individualized. Intraoperative echocardiography was used for precise localization of leaflets, and the delivery system was inserted under fluoroscopy with the deviation direction opposite to that of normal hearts. Vital signs were closely monitored, the fluid infusion rate was regulated, and hemodynamic stability was maintained. Arrhythmias occasionally occurred during the operation, so defibrillators and vasopressors were prepared in advance. Before anesthesia induction, midazolam and fentanyl were routinely administered for sedation and analgesia, and induction drugs included etomidate, propofol, and sufentanil [2]. After intubation, mechanical ventilation was performed to maintain general anesthesia. After the intraoperative echocardiographic probe was inserted into the esophagus, the anatomical structures of the heart were found to be completely opposite to those of normal individuals, requiring multiplanar adjustments to obtain clear images. Under fluoroscopic guidance, femoral vein puncture was performed, the clip delivery system was inserted, the leaflets were grasped, the grasping position was rechecked, and the clip was released after confirmation of satisfactory positioning. Throughout the surgical process, anesthesiologists, interventional physicians, and echocardiographers maintained close cooperation and real‐time communication, adjusting strategies at any time. Particularly in key steps such as transseptal puncture and clip release, meticulous operations and multifaceted evaluations were essential to avoid injury to adjacent tissues and ensure the accuracy and success of the surgery.

Postoperative Management

Patients with mirror‐image dextrocardia are at high risk of postoperative complications, requiring enhanced focused monitoring. Oxygen therapy, analgesia, and sedation were provided, and vital signs and coagulation status were closely monitored. Due to visceral situs inversus, it was necessary to adjust the auscultation position to the right chest, and palpate the right upper abdominal area during abdominal assessment to determine the presence of hepatic congestion. For electrocardiographic examinations, electrode placement was performed according to the standards for dextrocardia, that is, the red electrode was placed under the right clavicle, the yellow electrode under the left clavicle, and the rest were the same as routine. Postoperative echocardiography was regularly rechecked (including quantitative indicators of mitral regurgitation such as regurgitation area/volume, pulmonary artery pressure, LVEDD, etc.) to evaluate the degree of mitral regurgitation and the recovery of heart function (objective indicators such as dynamic changes in 6‐min walking test and NT‐proBNP levels). The dosages of diuretics, cardiotonic drugs, and other medications were adjusted individually. Appropriate ambulation was encouraged to prevent deep vein thrombosis. Health education was provided, advising the patient to adopt a low‐salt and low‐fat diet, moderate exercise, smoking cessation and alcohol restriction, and regular rechecks. Regarding postoperative anticoagulation, this patient with persistent atrial fibrillation (CHA2DS2‐VASc score: 4 points) was at high risk of stroke and required lifelong anticoagulation. Considering that warfarin is recommended by guidelines for postsurgical valve replacement, while there is no consensus on the use of NOACs after minimally invasive MitraClip surgery, warfarin was continued orally for anticoagulation postoperatively, with INR controlled between 2.0 and 3.0, and regular monitoring was performed [3]. Echocardiographic follow‐up was conducted to assess the degree of mitral regurgitation and the recovery of heart function, and drug dosages were adjusted accordingly. The fluid intake within 1 week was strictly controlled to < 1000 mL/d, and urine output and body weight changes were monitored to prevent exacerbation of heart failure. Psychological counseling was provided to alleviate anxiety and depression. Postoperatively, close attention was paid to changes in the patient's mental status, neck circumference was measured, and signs of airway compression such as airway edema and hematoma were vigilantly monitored. The operative limb was immobilized for 24 h, and activities were performed gently and slowly to avoid damaging the blood vessel wall at the puncture site and causing bleeding. A breathing trainer was used to guide the patient in deep breathing and cough exercises to prevent atelectasis, pulmonary infection, and other complications. A reasonable diet was arranged, including high‐protein and easily digestible foods, supplemented with vitamins to improve nutritional status. Skin care was maintained to keep it clean and dry, and the patient was turned regularly to prevent pressure ulcers.

Nursing Experience

Through participating in the diagnosis and treatment of this first domestic case of a patient with mirror‐image dextrocardia undergoing MitraClip surgery, we deeply recognized the high difficulty and high risk of such surgeries. Mirror‐image dextrocardia is a rare anatomical variation that can easily cause severe hemodynamic changes. Coupled with the patient's advanced age and multiple underlying diseases, the risk of perioperative complications is extremely high. Therefore, every detail of perioperative management is directly related to the patient's prognosis and requires meticulous attention. The concept of “moving the gateway forward” must be adhered to, with risk control measures implemented preoperatively and throughout all links.

Preoperatively, a comprehensive assessment of the patient's physical and mental status is essential, including symptoms, signs, heart function, and the degree of anatomical variation, to formulate an individualized treatment plan. Adequate preparations should be made, including completing auxiliary examinations, correcting electrolyte imbalances, improving nutritional status, and stabilizing underlying diseases. Particularly importantly, a multidisciplinary team should conduct repeated discussions to pool ideas and formulate a detailed surgical plan and emergency response plan [4].

Intraoperatively, close cooperation is crucial, with anesthesia, nursing, interventional, and echocardiographic teams working in harmony to strictly implement the surgical plan. Vital signs, hemodynamics, and other important indicators should be monitored dynamically in real time, and timely communication should be maintained to adjust strategies as needed. Special attention should be paid to the fact that the direction of insertion of echocardiographic probes, catheters, and other instruments into the heart is opposite to that of normal individuals, and the interventional path should be adjusted accordingly.

Postoperatively, intensive care is a top priority, especially strengthening the monitoring of vital signs, paying attention to coagulation abnormalities, and preventing complications such as embolism and bleeding. Postoperative echocardiographic rechecks are necessary to evaluate the effectiveness of MitraClip implantation and the recovery of heart function, focusing on the reduction in quantitative indicators of mitral regurgitation such as regurgitation area/volume, transvalvular pressure gradient, and changes in left ventricular function parameters such as LVEDD and ejection fraction, to guide the timely adjustment of dosages of diuretics, cardiotonic drugs, and other medications. Postoperative anticoagulation regimens should strictly follow guidelines. For patients with combined atrial fibrillation, stroke risk assessment should be performed, and warfarin or NOACs should be selected with regular monitoring of INR or anticoagulant effects. Moderate ambulation can prevent complications such as lower extremity deep vein thrombosis [5]. Meanwhile, attention should be paid to the placement of electrodes and cuff positions of electrocardiographic and blood pressure monitoring instruments during the activities of patients with mirror‐image dextrocardia. Postoperative negative emotions such as pain and anxiety should not be ignored, and psychological counseling should be provided, with pharmacological intervention if necessary.

In addition, health education should be conducted throughout the patient's hospitalization. Before discharge, the patient should be repeatedly informed of postoperative precautions, including regular medication intake, regular rechecks, and warning signs of complications, to enable them to master self‐management skills. A sound internal and external hospital communication mechanism should be established, with regular telephone follow‐ups after discharge to understand the recovery status and correct bad behaviors to improve compliance.

In conclusion, the perioperative management of patients with mirror‐image dextrocardia complicated by MR is a complex systematic project that requires adhering to a “patient‐centered” service philosophy, leveraging the advantages of multidisciplinary collaboration, and implementing refined and individualized nursing care. Evidence‐based guidelines should be combined with the anatomical variation characteristics of specific cases to formulate individualized nursing measures. Humanistic care should be integrated with evidence‐based nursing to minimize risks and improve prognosis. Only by continuously accumulating experience, innovating nursing models, and enhancing professional capabilities can we bring more benefits to patients and contribute to the early realization of the “Healthy China” goal.
