Diversity and potential ecological roles of viruses in Pleistocene permafrost
Authors: Changhua He, Yuntong Du, Karen G Lloyd, Tatiana A Vishnivetskaya, Elizaveta M Rivkina, Hongchen Jiang, Renxing Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAncient permafrost, formed during past glacial periods, is widespread in Siberia and other Arctic regions. Even though these soils have remained below 0 °C over geological time periods, the widespread presence of bacteria and archaea in permafrost is well documented. However, the diversity of viruses in ancient permafrost of different geological ages and their potential ecological roles are still poorly understood.ResultsWe applied metagenomics to characterize viruses from Middle to Late Pleistocene permafrost sediments from Siberia. A total of 2697 viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs) were recovered through metagenomic assembly and virus identification. Viral diversity in the Middle Pleistocene permafrost was much higher than that in the Late Pleistocene. The virus communities at different depths of the Middle Pleistocene permafrost showed great similarity to each other but were significantly different from those of the Late Pleistocene, with Azeredovirinae, Peduoviridae, and Fiersviridae being the predominant viruses in Pleistocene permafrost. The viruses were predicted to carry auxiliary metabolic genes potentially involved in cold adaptation and elemental (C and N) biogeochemical cycling in ancient permafrost. The virus-host prediction revealed that microorganisms such as bacteria and archaea are the main hosts and only a few eukaryotic hosts were identified.ConclusionsOur results show that permafrost viral communities in Siberia exhibit remarkable diversity and may have played a significant ecological role in ancient permafrost over geological time. While the release of viruses from thawing deep permafrost could pose a relatively small risk to human health and environment, it could have significant impacts on the carbon cycle, potentially influencing climate change feedback in permafrost regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12915-026-02685-6.Keywords: Ancient permafrost, Viral diversity, Auxiliary metabolic gene, Biogeochemical cycling, Climate change

Background

Permafrost refers to permanently frozen layers of deposit or soil in which the temperature remains at or below 0 °C for at least two consecutive years [1]. Because most permafrost in the Arctic and Subarctic formed during past glacial periods, ancient permafrost sediments have remained frozen for thousands or even millions of years since the early Pleistocene [2]. The cold and nutrient-poor conditions have led to the adaptation and development of unique microbial communities in ancient permafrost over geological time [3]. Previous studies have documented a large diversity of bacterial and archaeal communities from ancient permafrost in Siberia, Alaska, and the high Arctic of Canada [4–7]. Most of these studies have focused on the viability of bacteria and archaea in ancient permafrost and their role in maintaining metabolic activity at subzero temperatures across geologic timescales [8–10]. However, the prevalence of viruses in ancient permafrost and their potential ecological roles in such cold ecosystems remain underexplored [11–14].

Microorganisms in permafrost have adapted to survive in cold and low-energy environments. Previous studies have confirmed that global warming accelerates the microbial breakdown of organic carbon and emission of greenhouse gas, partly through the enhanced activity of viruses that influence microbial carbon cycling in thawed permafrost [8, 11, 13, 15–17]. The activities of viruses and their interactions with microbial hosts in permafrost ecosystems have complex effects on biogeochemical cycling as well as greenhouse gas emissions [15]. On the one hand, viruses play a crucial role in regulating microbial growth in permafrost ecosystems. By infecting and lysing microbial hosts, viruses enable the release of organic matter components that are easily accessible and usable by other members of the microbial community [18]. This virus-induced breakdown of organic matter promotes nutrient and energy cycling in permafrost ecosystems [19, 20]. On the other hand, the AMGs (auxiliary metabolic genes) encoded by viruses improve the metabolic capabilities of their hosts and facilitate the efficient utilization of organic matter [21, 22]. For example, the genes associated with carbon and nitrogen metabolism may influence greenhouse gas emissions, thereby affecting the dynamics of permafrost ecosystems in a warming climate [11, 13, 15]. Therefore, understanding the intricate interactions between viruses, microbial hosts and organic matter decomposition is crucial for predicting the consequences of permafrost thaw and its potential contributions to climate change.

Aside from the ecological impacts of climate change, the release of viral pathogens as permafrost thaws also poses potential risks to human health, agriculture and livestock [23]. In particular, viruses found in permafrost can infect both prokaryotes and eukaryotes such as humans, plants, or animals [24]. For example, previous studies have shown that thawing permafrost can lead to the release of viruses originating from various animals such as mammoths, bears, ancient birds, and rodents [25]. Furthermore, recent studies have discovered various giant viruses from permafrost sediments [9, 12]. Moreover, other studies suggested that thawing permafrost could release previously unknown pathogens that could potentially cause illness in humans, crops, and livestock [23, 26]. In this regard, it is crucial to investigate the diversity of viruses in ancient permafrost and their potential ecological risks in the context of global climate change.

Despite the risks of frozen viruses as potential pathogens and their important roles in driving carbon cycling and potentially influencing climate change, the diversity, adaptability, and host interactions of viruses in ancient permafrost remain largely unknown. To address these critical questions, we focused on the viromes of Siberian permafrost with a chronological sequence from the Middle to the Late Pleistocene. The main objectives were to investigate the diversity, host associations, and metabolic potential of viruses across Middle- to Late-Pleistocene permafrost strata, with emphasis on their potential contributions to carbon–nitrogen cycling and climate feedback. Our results contribute to a deeper understanding of the ecology of viromes in ancient permafrost and shed light on their potential ecological consequences due to the ongoing climate warming.

Results and discussion

Viral diversity from Pleistocene permafrost

The 2697 viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs) represent a dereplicated set, ensuring that each vOTU corresponds to a unique viral population. A complete list of all identified vOTUs, including contig ID, length, completeness, coverage, taxonomy, and predicted hosts, is provided in Additional file 1: Table S1. In general, the number of vOTUs from the Middle Pleistocene permafrost was much higher at deeper depths than those from the Late Pleistocene (Additional file 1: Table S1). Likewise, the Chao1 index values were higher for deeper horizons than for shallow layers, suggesting that the Middle Pleistocene samples yielded more viral sequences (Fig. 1A). However, the observed lower diversity in the upper layers (1.8 and 3.4 m) could be influenced by insufficient sequencing depth, as indicated by the rarefaction curves (Additional file 1: Fig. S1), and therefore should be interpreted cautiously. It is possible that the depth-related differences observed here are due, in part, to the sequencing depth variability and may not fully represent true ecological differences.

B NMDS analysis of a Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix calculated from the coverage of vOTUs. Points of different colors on the right represent different depths of the permafrost layers

The differences in viral communities at different depths were further confirmed by NMDS analysis (Fig. 1B). Permafrost layers (1.8 and 3.4 m) from the Late Pleistocene showed high similarity, while all samples from the Middle Pleistocene clustered together (Fig. 1B). The clear separation suggested a depth-dependent pattern in the distribution of virus communities within the Siberian permafrost, which has been frozen since the Middle Pleistocene. The different virus communities also reflected the geochemical gradient between the Late (brackish water) and Middle Pleistocene (Marine) permafrost sediments on the Eastern Siberian coast. The salinity gradient between the Late and Middle Pleistocene layers has been previously recorded based on the depositional settings and increasing concentration of Cl− and Br− in the marine horizon from the Middle Pleistocene [10, 27]. The high similarity of viral communities from the Middle Pleistocene (13.9–18.3 m) confirmed that these deeper permafrost layers were formed from marine sediments with similar geochemistry [28]. Within the Middle Pleistocene samples, the two upper samples (13.9 and 14.8 m) cluster together relative to the two deeper samples (16.9 and 18.3 m). A recent study also found that virus communities clustered together at multiple depths of permafrost deposits on the Tibetan Plateau [29]. Therefore, the depth-dependent viral community composition observed in both Siberia and the Tibetan Plateau suggested that strong ecological stability prevailed in ancient permafrost, possibly due to long-term preservation in cold temperatures over geological timescales.

Taxonomically novel viruses from Pleistocene permafrost

Further classification of vOTUs highlighted different virus community compositions at different depths (Fig. 2). A total of 54 viral family- or subfamily-level groups were identified and the dominant viral taxonomic groups differed between the Late and Middle Pleistocene permafrost samples (Fig. 2). Notably, over 50% of vOTUs could not be taxonomically assigned at the class level (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). In general, more family- or subfamily-level viral groups could be taxonomically identified in the Middle Pleistocene than in the Late Pleistocene permafrost (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). The predominance of unidentified viruses highlights the spread of uncharacterized viral diversity from ancient permafrost to all depths (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). The novelty of these unclassified vOTUs should be further confirmed with publicly available datasets or viruses from other environments. Among vOTUs classified at the class level, the majority of vOTUs were assigned to Caudoviricetes, and only a small fraction belonged to Megaviricetes (Additional file 1: Fig. S2 and Fig. 2). Among vOTUs identified at the family level, many viruses were taxonomically assigned to unknown families or genera (Fig. 2B). Previous studies also reported that a large number of viruses belonged to Caudoviricetes, but could not be further classified [13]. The most abundant virus families/subfamilies in the Middle Pleistocene permafrost were Azeredovirinae, Peduoviridae, Fiersviridae, Triavirus and Pclasvirinae, whereas the viral communities in the Late Pleistocene were dominated by Peduoviridae and Dolichocephalovirinae (Fig. 2B). These differences suggest that viral community composition varied between Middle and Late Pleistocene permafrost layers, likely driven by the geochemical gradient reported previously [30, 31]. Previous studies have shown that large double-stranded DNA viruses, including members of Pithoviridae and Orpheoviridae, can be important components of viral communities preserved in ancient Siberian permafrost [9]. Cultivation-based studies have also recovered giant viruses such as Cedratvirus, Pithovirus, Megavirus, and Pacmanvirus from similar permafrost environments [24]. These findings provide an important context for our study, which focused on metagenomically recovered double-stranded DNA viral sequences from Middle to Late Pleistocene permafrost. Compared with cultivation-based approaches, our metagenomic analysis captures a broader range of viral genetic signatures, including uncultivated viral lineages, but it does not directly demonstrate viral infectivity. Because the present study focused on DNA viral sequences, the diversity and prevalence of RNA viruses in these ancient permafrost deposits remain unknown and should be investigated in future studies.

Past viruses and eukaryotic hosts from perennially frozen sediments

Long-term preservation of DNA at low temperatures across geologic timescales enables the reconstruction of ancient relationships between viruses and their eukaryotic hosts from ancient permafrost [9]. In this study, our systematic analysis predicted potential eukaryotic hosts for 67 viral taxa, including 32 families, 22 subfamilies, and 13 genera. These taxa are associated with a broad range of eukaryotic hosts including plants, invertebrates and vertebrates. For example, Adenoviridae, detected in the Middle Pleistocene permafrost, include viruses known to infect primarily vertebrates, such as mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, and fish [32]. In contrast, Geminiviridae detected in Late Pleistocene layers are primarily plant-associated viruses that are commonly transmitted by insect vectors [33, 34]. Middle Pleistocene samples were enriched in Baculoviridae and Nudiviridae, which are mainly associated with arthropod hosts, particularly insects [35, 36]. Previous cultivation-based work recovered giant viruses, including Pandoravirus, Cedratvirus, Megavirus, Pithovirus, and Pacmanvirus, from ancient Siberian permafrost using Acanthamoeba as a host [24]. However, most of these eukaryotic viruses were not detected in the present metagenomic dataset. Such difference may reflect methodological differences between cultivation-based approach and metagenomic detection. Notably, cultivation approaches identify certain viruses capable of infecting the selected host under laboratory conditions whereas metagenomics only detects partial viral genetic signatures regardless of infectivity. Therefore, the absence of these cultivated giant viruses in our dataset should not be interpreted as evidence of their absence from these ancient permafrost environments. Overall, our results indicate that eukaryotic virus-associated sequences represent a minor component of the recovered viral communities. Since metagenomic data alone cannot determine viral infectivity or pathogenic potential, further studies combining metagenomics, host prediction, and cultivation-based approaches are needed to better evaluate the activity and potential impacts of eukaryotic viruses released from thawing permafrost.

Virus-prokaryotic host linkages and viral lifestyles

Unclassified viruses were predicted to infect various known prokaryotic hosts using iPHoP (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). In particular, the analysis of the 20 most abundant unclassified viruses revealed that Clostridiaceae and BA1 in Bathyarchaeia were their major hosts (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). Furthermore, the taxonomically classified viruses were grouped into different virus-host correspondence networks based on their geological era (Fig. 3). These networks revealed clear differences between the two geological periods. The Middle Pleistocene network was more complex than the Late Pleistocene network and included a broader range of predicted bacterial and archaeal host associations. In contrast, the Late Pleistocene network was relatively simpler and was mainly associated with bacterial hosts (Fig. 3). Species of Peduoviridae from the Middle Pleistocene are predicted to be capable of infecting both bacteria and archaea (Fig. 3B). Additionally, certain viruses, including Peduoviridae, Pclasvirinae, and Fuselloviridae, can infect multiple hosts. Conversely, certain hosts such as Clostridiaceae, are susceptible to infections by multiple viruses.

The Late Pleistocene layers originated from low-salinity brackish water deposits along the eastern Siberian coastline [30, 31]. Because brackish coastal sediments can receive both terrestrial inputs and marine influence, we interpreted viral environmental associations using both the depositional context of the core and the inferred taxonomy of viral hosts. Predicted hosts of Late Pleistocene viruses were mainly associated with bacterial groups reported from terrestrial, freshwater, or coastal sediment environments [37–39]. Viruses likely originated from terrestrial environments based on their association with hosts such as UBA9973 in Paceibacteria, UBA10450 in Chthoniobacterales, and IMCC26256 in Acidimicrobiia (Fig. 3). Among the classified viruses, the most abundant families include Peduoviridae, Dolichocephalovirinae, and Gclasvirinae (Fig. 3A). Phylogenetic analysis suggests that viruses from this period are largely terrestrial or closely related to terrestrial viruses, including unclassified lineages of 1-75_252236, BK051466.1, NC073676.1, and MZ574429.2 (Fig. 4). While terrestrial microorganisms are the primary hosts, there is also an admixture of marine species (Figs. 4 and 5). For instance, Peduoviridae is a common phage found in oceanic environments [40]. Therefore, these results suggest that the Late Pleistocene viral assemblages reflect a brackish coastal setting with strong terrestrial input and detectable marine influence.

In contrast, the Middle Pleistocene samples reflect a more marine-dominated ecosystem, characterized by higher salinity associated with marine sedimentary environments [30, 31]. In the phylogenetic tree, Middle Pleistocene viruses partially cluster with those from marine and nearshore environments (Fig. 4). In this period, archaea from marine environments, such as SOKP01 in Lokiarchaeia, Thorarchaeaceae, and BA1 in Bathyarchaeia, were present (Fig. 3B). For instance, SOKP01 is associated with deep-sea archaea and bacteria near oil seeps, while Thorarchaeaceae have been reported in coastal sediment studies [41, 42]. BA1 is a marine archaeon common in deep ocean sediments hypothesized to be methanogenic [43]. Other marine-associated families, such as Halothiobacillaceae, Phycodnaviridae, Cyanophages, and Bacilladnaviridae, also dominate sediments from this period, supporting the marine influence of Middle Pleistocene ecosystems [44–47]. These results suggest stronger marine or nearshore signals in the Middle Pleistocene viral assemblages than in the Late Pleistocene assemblages. Notably, terrestrial-associated viral signals were also detected in Middle Pleistocene sediments. These signals may reflect terrestrial runoff, erosion, resuspension, or the deposition of terrestrial organic material into coastal or marine sediments before permanent freezing. The phylogenetic placement of several giant viruses related to terrestrial eukaryotic hosts further supports the possibility that these sediments preserved mixed viral signals from both marine and terrestrial sources (Additional file 1: Fig. S4).

Potential roles of viral AMGs in biogeochemical cycling and greenhouse gas emission

We then explored whether the predicted viruses encode AMGs that might be involved in biogeochemical cycling of nitrogen, sulfur and carbon such as glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and other pathways for degrading organic compounds. Although multiple AMG annotation tools were simultaneously used, the viral origin and genomic context of the predicted AMGs were validated, and a set of representative viral contigs encoding carbon-, phosphorus-, and sulfur-related metabolic genes were selected for the validation processes (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). These AMGs were embedded within contigs containing hallmark viral genes and were not located near contig termini, reducing the likelihood of host contamination or misannotation. Furthermore, each contig demonstrated a consistent gene orientation, with AMGs flanked by other viral structural or functional genes, supporting their classification as virus-encoded auxiliary metabolic genes (Additional file 1: Fig. S5A–D). Nevertheless, all the predicted AMGs should be interpreted as putative functional signals requiring future verification.

Carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) were primarily annotated as glycosyltransferases (GTs) like GT2 and GT4, accessory activities (AAs), carbohydrate esterases (CEs), and polysaccharide lyases (PLs) (Fig. 5A). AMGs from the GH family facilitate the breakdown of polysaccharides like cellulose, starch, chitin, and mannan. Additionally, the abundance of AMGs associated with the glycolytic pathway is higher in Late Pleistocene samples. Several AMGs were associated with the TCA cycle and the glyoxylate cycle, including those involved in the conversion of succinate to malate, isocitrate to succinate, and isocitrate to glycolate (Fig. 6). The glyoxylate cycle allows microorganisms to survive in nutrient-poor environments by utilizing simple carbon sources such as acetate [48]. Additionally, the mmoB gene encodes a regulatory protein involved in methane monooxygenase, an enzyme essential for converting methane to methanol in methanotrophic bacteria [49]. Viral AMGs involved in carbohydrate decomposition and glycolysis may enhance carbon dioxide production, potentially contributing to climate warming. However, AMGs related to mmoB might promote methane oxidation and thereby mitigate the greenhouse effect to offset climate change.

In addition, viruses were annotated to harbor AMGs that are involved in the degradation of various peptides, including γ-glutamyl, dipeptidyl, glutamic acid carboxypeptidase, and carboxypeptidase (Fig. 5B). We also identified predicted AMGs related to nitrogen metabolism including ammonia monooxygenase genes (amoC), nitrite reductase genes (octR, nirK), and nitrous oxidase genes (nosD) (Fig. 6) [50–53]. Notably, genes related to nitrogen cycle (octR and nosD) were identified in the Middle Pleistocene, while amoC and nirK genes were detected in the Late Pleistocene samples. In this regard, viral AMGs may be linked to nitrous oxide reduction pathways (Fig. 6) and potential mitigation of the greenhouse effect by conversion of nitrous oxide to dinitrogen.

Viral AMGs and cold adaptation

Viruses were predicted to harbor several putative stress-related AMGs, including genes associated with cold shock proteins, RNA helicases, osmotic and oxidative stress, carotenoid synthesis, and membrane and peptidoglycan modifications (Fig. 6). These stress-related AMGs were detected across different depths (Fig. 6). For example, the cspA gene encodes a cold shock protein that can act as an RNA chaperone and help destabilize secondary mRNA structures, thereby supporting transcription and translation under stressful conditions [54–56]. General stress proteins may also help microorganisms respond to environmental stresses such as low temperatures. The detection of the trehalose biosynthesis gene otsA at 18.3 m suggests a potential link between viral AMGs and host osmotic stress responses [57]. Trehalose has also been hypothesized to aid in adaptation to long term non-growth conditions in deeply buried marine sediments [58]. Furthermore, AMGs related to DNA replication and repair such as RNA helicases and RecA/RadA recombinases are likely involved in genome maintenance and long-term survival under cold conditions through geological time [59]. Additionally, viruses encode AMGs for enzymes like peroxidase and thioredoxin reductase, which might help remove harmful oxidative substances and protect cells from oxidative damage [60]. These various cold-adaptive functional genes may facilitate host survival and growth in cold environments over extended geological timescales. It should be pointed out that these AMGs cannot be confidently attributed to cold-specific adaptation mechanisms without comparable viral genomes from warmer environments and experimental validation.

Conclusions

In this study, we investigated viral diversity and potential ecological functions in Middle to Late Pleistocene Siberian permafrost using metagenomic approaches. The metagenomic analysis revealed highly diverse viral communities and depth-dependent patterns in ancient permafrost sediments. The prevalence of unclassified viruses highlights the dominance of unclassified virus species in these permanently frozen marine sediments. Further AMGs predicted related to carbon and nitrogen metabolism suggest that permafrost viruses may carry genes with potential ecological roles in biogeochemical cycling and contribute to greenhouse gas emissions in warming Arctic permafrost regions. It should be pointed out that metagenomic annotations alone cannot demonstrate gene activity or direct effects on greenhouse gas emissions. Only a small fraction (0.69%) of the recovered viral communities was associated with eukaryotic hosts, including plant-, animal-, and arthropod-associated viruses. However, the infectious or pathogenic properties of the identified viruses should be confirmed through cultivation under laboratory conditions. Collectively, our results highlighted that the diversity and ecological importance of virus populations in the Siberian permafrost could be influenced by environmental factors such as geochemistry and the geological age of the permafrost. The discovery of uncharacterized Pleistocene viral sequences underscores the need for further studies combining metagenomics, geochemical measurements and cultivation-based approaches to better evaluate the ecological roles and potential impacts of viruses released from thawing of ancient permafrost.

Methods

Sample collection

A permafrost sediment core (~ 22 m) was previously drilled along the eastern Siberian coastline (Additional file 1: Fig. S6) using aseptic techniques [30, 31]. The intact sedimentary cores were sectioned at various intervals using sterile tools and transported to the laboratory under controlled frozen conditions. Multiple depths were selected to represent a chronological sequence from the Middle to the Late Pleistocene and a geochemical transition from brackish water to coastal marine sediments (Additional file 1: Fig. S6) [30, 61]. Samples for metagenomic sequencing were taken from core segments ranging from 1.8 to 18.3 m (Additional file 1: Fig. S6). The permafrost at 1.8 and 3.4 m represents brackish water deposits frozen during the Late Pleistocene (26–43 kyr ago) whereas deeper layers (13.9–18.3 m) are marine sediments frozen since the Middle Pleistocene (100–120 kyr ago) according to previously reported sedimentological and geochemical records [30, 31, 61].

Metagenomic assembling and binning

Metagenomic datasets were downloaded from the NCBI database using project accession number (PRJNA680161 and PRJNA601698). These datasets were previously generated from genomic DNA extracted from bulk sediments without virus enrichment and the sequencing libraries were constructed using the Nextera DNA Flex Library Prep Kit (Illumina, CA), with unique DNA barcodes added to each library as mentioned in our previous study [30, 61]. The metagenome libraries were pooled and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (2 × 150 bp) as previously described [31]. The raw sequences were quality filtered using Trimmomatic v0.39 to remove Illumina sequencing adapters and low-quality sequences, applying the following parameters: SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15, LEADING:3, TRAILING:3, and MINLEN:36 [62]. Duplicate sequences from the previously metagenomic datasets were removed using BBMap v39.01 from the BBTools suite, specifically using the jgi.Dedupe script with the settings threads = 8, ac = f, and Xmx633276m for memory allocation to simplify the dataset [63]. Quality-controlled sequences were then assembled with MEGAHIT v1.2.9 using meta-sensitive preset parameters [64]. The resulting contigs were binned using metabat2 v2.16, maxbin2 v2.2.7, vamb v4.1.3, and semibin2 v1.5.1 [65–68]. The bins were integrated, de-replicated, and filtered using DAS Tool v1.1.6 and dRep v3.4.3 to retain metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) with over 50% completeness and less than 10% contamination [69, 70]. Finally, the retained MAGs were classified into archaea and bacteria using GTDB-tk v2.3.2 [71].

Identification of viral contigs

Viral contigs were initially identified using a combination of several software programs: geNomad v1.6.1 (length > 5000, sensitivity = 4.2, evalue < 0.0001), VirSorter2 v2.2.4 (length > 5000), VirFinder v0.0.3 (length > 5000, pvalue < 0.05), Virsorter v1.0.6 (length > 5000), DeepVirFinder v1.0 (length > 5000, pvalue < 0.05), and VIBRANT v1.2.1 [72–76]. The quality of the filtered viral contigs was assessed using CheckV v1.0.1, which also identified provirus DNA sequences, excised host chromosome sequences, and calculated completeness along with viral and host marker genes [77].

Prodigal-gv v2.11.0 was used to predict viral open reading frames (ORFs), and fetchMG v1.2 was used to identify bacterial genomic marker genes [72, 78]. The predicted viral ORFs were annotated using VOG-kit v0.0.1, which performs HMM-based searches against viral orthologous groups with the equipped hmmscan option [79]. The resulting viral gene annotations were used for subsequent functional summaries. Viral sequences were retained if at least one viral gene was identified by CheckV. If no viral gene was detected, sequences were kept if the following criteria were met: vs2_score > 0.95 and geNomad_score > 0.95, genomad_n_hallmark, vs2_n_hallmarks ≥ 2. Viruses identified by geNomad or Virsorter2 were retained if the host gene count was 0 and the virus gene count was ≥ 2. CoverM v0.6.1 was used to calculate the coverage of viral contigs, reflecting their relative abundance in the metagenomic samples, with calculations performed using methods such as count, mean, RPKM, and TPM [80]. Mapping was performed using minimap2 v2.26, and the results were standardized using RPKM values for comparison [81]. A horizontal coverage threshold of 50% was applied to the viral contigs, with RPKM set to 0 for contigs failing to meet this threshold. This approach ensures consistency in downstream analyses, such as PCoA. The identified viral sequences were clustered into vOTUs based on ≥ 95% average nucleotide identity (ANI) across ≥ 80% of the sequence length (alignment fraction, AF), using fastANI, in accordance with established species-level demarcation thresholds for viral genomes [82].

Viral taxonomic identification

Viral classification and identification were conducted using PhagCN2 with default parameters [83]. PhaGCN2 is a deep learning model based on graph convolutional networks (GCN) trained for viral classification with a reference database from the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV). Additionally, species annotation was performed using the geNomad annotation module [72]. The geNomad annotation module was used for species annotation with 85,315 marker genes. Each viral ORF was annotated at the sequence level, and a support score for each taxonomic unit was calculated using the Bitscore-weighted algorithm. Taxonomic units with support scores above 50% were selected for downstream analyses. These results were used to complement and cross-reference the findings from PhagCN2.

Identification of auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs)

Virus-encoded auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) were identified using a curated multi-step workflow to ensure high accuracy of annotation. Coding DNA sequences (CDSs) were first predicted from viral contigs using Prodigal-gv in metagenomic mode, based on previously generated results. The predicted genes were then analyzed with DRAM-v and VIBRANT, two widely used tools for viral genome annotation and AMG detection [74, 84]. These tools automatically curate gene functions, evaluate genomic context, and flag genes potentially located on host-derived fragments or at contig boundaries, thereby reducing false-positive identifications. Candidate AMGs recovered by DRAM-v and VIBRANT were further annotated against the KEGG, Pfam, and VOG databases using HMMER (e-value < 1 × 10⁻5, coverage ≥ 40%) to refine functional classification [85–87]. Conserved viral genomic regions were identified via sequence clustering approaches to support AMG localization within bona fide viral genomic neighborhoods. Only genes supported by DRAM-v or VIBRANT and located in genomic regions flanked by typical viral structural or replication genes were retained as high-confidence AMGs. Functional summaries were derived from KEGG pathway mapping and Pfam domain annotations, and representative AMG-carrying viral genomes were visualized with Easyfig v2.2.5 to illustrate genomic context and confirm viral origin [88]. Carbohydrate-active enzymes were identified by aligning viral sequences with the CAZy database using the HMMER software [89–91]. Peptidases were annotated by mapping viral sequences with the MEROPS database using emapper, with parameters set to e value < 1e − 5 and coverage = 0.4 [90, 91]. The final functional annotation of AMGs was integrated through manual screening, which involved the removal of viral proteins and host genes. The results were primarily derived from DRAM-v and VIBRANT annotations, with supplementary functional information obtained from the metabolic, KEGG, and MEROPS databases.

Virus-host prediction

The iPHoP software was used to predict host-virus associations, incorporating the taxonomy of bacterial and archaeal MAGs obtained from previous binning efforts into the iPHoP database as host references. The taxonomy of bacterial and archaeal MAGs was incorporated into the iPHoP database as host references [71, 92]. The results for host-virus associations were filtered to retain those related to MAGs. Virus-host predictions were further confirmed through a comprehensive review of related publications and databases to identify potential virus-host relationships. The Sankey diagram was created to visualize the connection between the families of identified viruses and their corresponding hosts at the family and phylum level. The width of the lines in the diagram indicates the abundance of the viruses.

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using two independent approaches: (i) a marker-gene phylogeny based on DNA polymerase B (PolB), a core gene of nucleocytoplasmic large DNA viruses (NCLDVs) [93] and (ii) a phylogenetic analysis of high-quality vOTUs. For the NCLDV PolB-based phylogeny, reference PolB sequences were retrieved from the NCBI viral database. Sequences shorter than 700 bp were manually removed to reduce the influence of fragmented genes. The curated PolB reference sequences were compared against the identified viral contigs using DIAMOND v2.1.8 with an e-value threshold of 1e − 5 and the output format set to 6 [94]. Putative PolB regions in viral contigs were extracted according to the start and end coordinates of the Diamond alignment results using a custom Python script. The extracted PolB sequences and reference PolB sequences were aligned using MAFFT, and the phylogenetic tree was reconstructed using IQ-TREE with the parameters -bb 1000, -m TEST, -alrt 1000, and –bnni [95, 96]. Separately, a phylogenetic analysis was performed for high-quality vOTUs. Viral contigs with an estimated completeness greater than 70% were selected. Closely related reference viral genomes for these vOTUs were retrieved from the NCBI database using BLAST. The selected vOTU sequences and their corresponding reference sequences were aligned using MAFFT, and phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using IQ-TREE with the parameters -bb 1000, -m TEST, -alrt 1000, and –bnni. Reference viruses were annotated according to their NCBI taxonomic information and habitat origin. In the phylogenetic trees, well-characterized reference viruses, predicted environmental vOTUs, and unclassified sequences were distinguished to clarify which taxonomic assignments were supported by known reference genomes and which were inferred from predicted viral sequences. Given the distinct terrestrial and marine layers along the core, subsequent viral and host environmental associations were determined according to host taxonomic assignments and the distinct habitat origins of the selected reference viruses.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.0). Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) was used to analyze the similarity of samples using the vegan package metaMDS function. The input data were standardized and then NMDS analysis was performed based on the Bray–Curtis distance matrix. The richness, Shannon index, and Chao1 index of vOTUs for each sample were also calculated using the diversity functions within the vegan package.
