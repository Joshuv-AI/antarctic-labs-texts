Early mortality-associated factors in pediatric fulminant myocarditis patients treated with venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation: a single-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Huiling Zhang, Sheng Ye, Zihao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFulminant myocarditis (FM) is a life-threatening pediatric condition that may progress rapidly to cardiogenic shock and multiorgan dysfunction. This study evaluated early clinical and laboratory factors associated with in-hospital mortality among pediatric FM patients treated with venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO).MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 44 consecutive children (<18 years) with FM treated with VA-ECMO from 2018 to 2025. Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Because only 7 deaths occurred, regression analyses were prespecified as exploratory. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) was used for variable screening, followed by univariable and exploratory 3-variable logistic models. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to describe discrimination of selected admission markers.ResultsThirty-seven patients survived to discharge and seven died in hospital, yielding an in-hospital survival rate of 84.1%. Compared with survivors, non-survivors had lower admission systolic blood pressure [73 (64–81) vs. 90 (83–105) mmHg, P=0.003], lower left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) [31.0% (29.5–34.0%) vs. 49.0% (40.0–55.0%), P=0.002], and higher blood urea nitrogen (BUN) [14.2 (9.7–15.1) vs. 7.3 (5.8–8.7) mmol/L, P=0.01]. In univariable logistic models, systolic blood pressure (SBP), LVEF, and BUN were associated with in-hospital mortality; however, estimates were imprecise after exploratory adjustment because of the limited number of events. The areas under the curve (AUCs) for SBP, LVEF, and BUN were 0.86, 0.87, and 0.79, respectively.ConclusionsAdmission SBP, LVEF, and BUN were early markers associated with in-hospital mortality in this single-center cohort of pediatric FM patients treated with VA-ECMO. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive prognostic evidence and require validation in larger multicenter cohorts.

Introduction

Pediatric fulminant myocarditis (FM) is an uncommon but critical inflammatory myocardial disease characterized by abrupt ventricular dysfunction, malignant arrhythmia, and cardiogenic shock (1,2). Children may initially present with extracardiac symptoms such as fever, vomiting, abdominal pain, fatigue, or altered consciousness, which can delay recognition until severe circulatory compromise occurs (2-4). For children with refractory shock or cardiac arrest, venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) can provide temporary circulatory support while myocardial recovery is awaited (1,5-7).

The diagnosis of myocarditis in children remains challenging. Endomyocardial biopsy is the histopathological reference standard but is invasive and may not be feasible or safe in unstable children requiring vasoactive support or ECMO (2,5). Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) can support the diagnosis by detecting myocardial edema and injury in hemodynamically stable patients, but CMR does not identify viral pathogens; virologic evidence requires pathogen testing such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or serology from appropriate clinical samples (2,5). Accordingly, pediatric clinical practice often integrates symptoms, electrocardiography, cardiac biomarkers, echocardiography, CMR when feasible, and exclusion of alternative causes (2,3).

Previous pediatric VA-ECMO studies have linked mortality to hemodynamic collapse, lactate, renal dysfunction, ventricular function, arrhythmia, and inflammatory markers (8-12). Low systolic blood pressure (SBP) and reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) are expected markers of shock severity and pump failure. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) is also a readily available marker of renal hypoperfusion and critical illness severity, but its value in pediatric FM patients receiving VA-ECMO has been less clearly described than conventional hemodynamic parameters (13,14).

This study aimed to reassess early mortality-associated factors in a single-center cohort of pediatric FM patients treated with VA-ECMO, using the original clinical data to correct statistical reporting, reduce excessive precision, and present estimates cautiously in view of the small number of mortality events. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0457/rc).

Methods

Study population and design

We retrospectively reviewed consecutive pediatric patients (<18 years) diagnosed with FM and managed with VA-ECMO at the Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine between 2018 and 2025. Patient selection is shown in Figure 1. Sixty records of suspected or confirmed pediatric FM were screened, and 44 children who received VA-ECMO and met the study eligibility criteria were included in the final analysis. Patients with congenital heart disease, endocardial fibroelastosis, myocardial infarction, non-ischemic cardiomyopathy, or known genetic disease were excluded. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. 2026-IRB-0047-Y-01). Written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design and use of de-identified clinical data. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Diagnostic criteria

The clinical diagnosis of myocarditis was based primarily on the Diagnostic Recommendation for Myocarditis in Children (Version 2018) and the American Heart Association scientific statement on pediatric myocarditis (2,3). Diagnostic evidence included acute cardiac insufficiency or shock, dynamic elevation of cardiac troponin or creatine kinase (CK)-MB, electrocardiographic abnormalities, echocardiographic evidence of ventricular dysfunction, cardiac enlargement, and/or CMR findings when the patient’s hemodynamic status allowed imaging. FM was defined as acute myocarditis with rapid progression to severe heart failure, cardiogenic shock, malignant arrhythmia, or the need for mechanical circulatory support. No patient underwent endomyocardial biopsy during the acute phase. CMR was not required for inclusion and was not used for pathogen identification. Pathogen testing was performed with PCR or serology on blood and respiratory samples according to clinical availability.

Data collection

Clinical data were extracted from medical records on 27 February 2026. Collected variables included demographic data, presenting symptoms, hemodynamic parameters, echocardiographic findings, laboratory tests, complications, and treatment data. Admission variables were defined as the first available results within 24 hours after hospital admission or immediately before/at ECMO initiation when ECMO was started urgently. Peak CK and peak CK-MB were defined as the highest documented values during the index hospitalization before discharge or death. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were inspected for distribution and are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons used the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. To avoid overstating precision, laboratory and clinical values were rounded to clinically meaningful units. Because the cohort included only 7 mortality events, variable selection and logistic regression were treated as exploratory. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression was used only as a screening method to identify candidate admission variables. Univariable logistic regression and an exploratory 3-variable logistic model were then performed for SBP, LVEF, and BUN with clinically interpretable scaling. Odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and P values are reported. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and bootstrapped 95% CIs for areas under the curve (AUCs) were used to describe discrimination. No external validation was performed.

LASSO screening selected admission SBP, LVEF, and BUN as candidate early markers. In univariable logistic models, higher SBP and higher LVEF were associated with lower odds of in-hospital death, while higher BUN was associated with higher odds of death (Table 3). In the exploratory 3-variable model, the point estimates remained directionally consistent but the CIs widened and BUN was no longer statistically significant after adjustment, which is expected given the limited event number. ROC analysis showed AUCs of 0.86 for SBP, 0.87 for LVEF, and 0.79 for BUN (Figure 2).

Discussion

In this single-center cohort of pediatric FM patients treated with VA-ECMO, non-survivors had more severe circulatory compromise, lower ventricular function, and greater evidence of renal and systemic involvement at presentation. The revised analysis supports SBP, LVEF, and BUN as early markers associated with mortality, but the small number of deaths prevents definitive claims about independent prognostic effects.

The associations of low SBP and reduced LVEF with mortality are clinically expected and consistent with prior reports in children with FM or myocarditis requiring mechanical circulatory support (8-10,12). These variables reflect the severity of cardiogenic shock and myocardial pump failure rather than novel pathobiology. Therefore, the clinical value of identifying these markers lies mainly in confirming the need for rapid risk recognition and intensive support in children who arrive with profound hemodynamic instability.

BUN should also be interpreted cautiously. Elevated BUN is not a disease-specific marker and has been associated with renal hypoperfusion, cardiorenal interaction, and critical illness severity in broader heart failure populations (13,14). In the present cohort, BUN was higher among non-survivors and showed moderate discrimination in ROC analysis, but the adjusted estimate was imprecise. This suggests that BUN may be a practical bedside warning sign in pediatric FM on VA-ECMO rather than a standalone or novel prognostic test.

Inflammatory and organ-injury markers also differed between groups. Higher PCT in non-survivors may reflect severe systemic inflammation or tissue injury; however, bacterial infection cannot be excluded solely from the available retrospective data. Similarly, elevated transaminases, creatinine, CK, and CK-MB likely mark systemic hypoperfusion and multiorgan injury, but these mechanisms were not directly measured. Recent studies have evaluated inflammatory indices, cytokine profiles, lactate, and renal markers in pediatric FM (15-17), highlighting that prognosis is driven by more than myocardial function alone (10-12,18-20).

The observed survival rate of 84.1% is encouraging but cannot be attributed to any specific institutional ECMO or immunomodulatory strategy because this study had no comparator cohort. Nearly all patients received corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and VA-ECMO; therefore, treatment effects cannot be separated from disease severity, timing of support, referral patterns, and center-specific practice. Future multicenter studies should combine hemodynamic, metabolic, inflammatory, and treatment-timing variables and should validate candidate risk models before clinical implementation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective single-center study with 44 patients and only 7 deaths, which severely limits statistical power and makes multivariable estimates unstable. All regression findings should be interpreted as exploratory and associative, not as definitive independent predictors. Second, the diagnostic approach was primarily clinical. No patient underwent endomyocardial biopsy during the acute phase, and CMR was not uniformly available because many patients were hemodynamically unstable. Third, pathogen testing was based on available clinical samples and could not identify etiology in most patients. Fourth, treatment exposure was highly homogeneous, which prevents assessment of the independent effect of corticosteroids, IVIG, or the local ECMO protocol. Fifth, post-discharge follow-up data were unavailable, so long-term cardiac recovery, neurologic outcomes, and late mortality could not be evaluated. Finally, no external validation or reclassification analysis was possible.

Conclusions

Admission SBP, LVEF, and BUN were early markers associated with in-hospital mortality among pediatric FM patients treated with VA-ECMO in this retrospective cohort. The revised analysis confirms expected hemodynamic risk signals and suggests that BUN may provide a simple bedside marker of systemic hypoperfusion and renal involvement. Because the cohort was small and the number of mortality events was limited, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger multicenter studies.
