Danon Disease in a 14-Year-Old
Authors: Enrique C Guerra, Kerstin Beutelspacher-Fernandez, Jorge A Silva-Estrada, Maria Jose Santa-Ana-Bayona, Pavel Martinez-Dominguez, Cristina P Agredano-Chavez, Miguel Angel Pardiño-Vega, Ana Lucia Yanez-Felix, Emiy Yokoyama-Rebollar, Nilda Espinola-Zavaleta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents the case of a 14-year-old boy with Danon disease exhibiting exclusive cardiac involvement, initially suspected as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Significant deterioration raised suspicion of an alternative etiology. A clinical exome revealed a previously unreported likely pathogenic variant, LAMP2:c.645_651del (p.LysAsnfs∗25), in hemizygous state, confirming the diagnosis of Danon disease.Key Words: cardiomyopathy, Danon disease, genetic variant, LAMP2

History of Presentation

A 14-year-old boy presented at the pediatric cardiology department due to oppressive chest pain, palpitations triggered by physical exertion, and daily headaches. Physical examinations yielded no significant findings.

Past Medical History

The patient had chronic daily headaches since 6 years of age, with no other significant personal or family history.

Differential Diagnosis

Initial work-up included chest radiograph, electrocardiogram (ECG), 24-hour Holter monitoring, stress test, transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE), and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR). With confirmation of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) phenotype, the initial approach was to evaluate common genetic causes. A normal enzymatic activity assay of acid alpha-glucosidase excluded Pompe disease. Consequently, the patient was started on β-blocker therapy with carvedilol (6.25 mg daily) and scheduled for implantable cardioverter-defibrillator following American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology guidelines for sudden death risk staging.

Investigations

Chest radiograph revealed cardiomegaly (cardiothoracic ratio of 0.6) (Figure 1A); ECG showed sinus rhythm, short PR interval (102 milliseconds), and complete left bundle branch block (Figure 1B). The 24-hour Holter displayed a sinus rhythm, alternating with accelerated idioventricular rhythm (Figure 1C).

Initial Work-Up Tests

(A) Chest radiograph demonstrating cardiomegaly (cardiothoracic ratio of 0.6). (B) Twelve-lead electrocardiogram with short PR interval (102 milliseconds), and complete left bundle branch block. (C) A 24-hour Holter monitoring showing an accelerated idioventricular rhythm.

Subsequent Bruce stress protocol revealed chest pain and poor chronotropic response at maximum exertion (NYHA functional class I, 10.4 METs). Moreover, TTE indicated nonobstructive left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy and mild LV systolic dysfunction (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] 50% and global longitudinal strain −6.7%) (Figure 2A). CMR demonstrated an LVEF of 50% with global biventricular hypertrophy, myocardial edema, along with late gadolinium enhancement quantified as 31.7% LV fibrosis. Maximum thickness was in the anteroseptal wall (27 mm in the basal third and 29.5 mm in the middle third) (Figure 3B).

Shortly after, the patient presented an episode of syncope. A TTE revealed a 17 × 18 mm thrombus in the LV apex (Figure 2B), which was treated successfully with fibrinolytic therapy (alteplase 0.01 mg/kg/h over 8 hours). TTE also showed decreased LV function, confirmed by CMR (LVEF 36%). Additionally, late gadolinium enhancement CMR revealed an increase in LV fibrosis (50%), progression of hypertrophy with right ventricular extension, and LV dilation suggesting end-stage disease (Figures 3A and 3C). The patient underwent placement of an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator and was discharged with carvedilol 12.5 mg, sacubitril/valsartan 50 mg, spironolactone 50 mg, dapagliflozin 10 mg, amiodarone 100 mg daily, and enoxaparin 60 mg twice daily. Subsequent follow-up was conducted closely.

In the absence of HCM family history, the abrupt decrease in LV systolic function, increase in LV fibrosis, and marked thickening of the LV wall are uncommon features within the nonobstructive HCM phenotype, for ruling out other genetic causes. A clinical exome with 289 genes related to this condition was requested. A hemizygous likely pathogenic variant was reported: LAMP2:c.645_651del (p.Lys215Asnfs25), resulting in diagnosis of Danon disease (DD).

Management

The patient was assessed for other complications, which were ruled out. Given the disease progression, and following guideline-directed management, the patient underwent heart transplantation at 15.8 years of age. Histopathologic findings demonstrated cardiomyocytes with glycogen inclusions and cytoplasmatic vacuoles (Figure 4).

Discussion

DD is a lysosomal disorder with a triad of cardiomyopathy, intellectual impairment, and skeletal myopathy.1 Genetic alterations in LAMP2, localized in Xq24, are the cause of this condition, following an X-linked inheritance pattern.2 The gene codes for the lysosome-associated membrane protein 2, a glycoprotein in the lysosomal membrane, that when deficient, results in impaired macroautophagy.1

Due to X-linked inheritance, heterozygous women for DD are somatic mosaics and present with isolated cardiomyopathy, whereas men commonly exhibit the classic clinical triad.3 In men, cardiomyopathy is usually progressive with hypertrophic phenotype in the initial stages, progressing to dilated features in 11% to 12% of cases.3

Although cases of DD with exclusively cardiac involvement have been documented, this patient presented a previously unreported genetic variation. The clinical exome sequencing confirmed a hemizygous deletion within the coding region of LAMP2, characterized by deletion of 7 nt between positions 645 to 651, causing frameshift and producing a truncated protein. This variant was classified as likely pathogenic according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics by fulfilling very strong pathogenic criteria, given that it is a null variant in a gene where loss of function is a known mechanism of disease, and moderate pathogenic criteria due to its extremely low frequency in gnomAD population databases.4

Two recently published cases of DD with exclusive heart affection share the same gene region and protein domain involvement as in this case. Sun et al5 reported a case of a man with right ventricular hypertrophy and dilation with a hemizygous nonsense variant c.35C>A, (pSer12∗) in LAMP2 with DD and only cardiac involvement. Shalata et al6 reported on a 13-year-old boy with DD presenting palpitations and a Wolf-Parkinson-White pattern. At 18 years of age, echocardiography demonstrated increased echogenicity and molecular testing confirmed a hemizygous deletion of 110.5 kb at Xq24, encompassing the entire LAMP2 gene.

Considering previous literature, genotype-phenotype relationship in DD remains unclear, and some cases could be milder or exclusively cardiac, depending on the site and type of mutation in LAMP2, exposing the diagnostic challenge and the importance of genetic testing. Limited access to molecular tools in regions such as Mexico can lead to delayed diagnosis and treatment of genetic conditions, in addition to a poor contribution to genotype-phenotype description.

Maintaining diligent clinical and imaging follow-up is crucial for considering alternative diagnoses in HCM. During initial evaluation, the absence of family history emerges as the main indicator that leads to consider an isolated, rather than syndromic HCM, and the possibility of HCM with autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. Family history not only aids in diagnosis but also underscores the risk of sudden death. In this regard, echocardiographic assessment stands as an alternative for conducting family screening, especially when genetic testing access is limited. TTE could also prove valuable in monitoring DD and other cardiomyopathies.7,8

Notably, another characteristic of DD is a short PR interval identified on ECG,1 as observed in this patient.

The substantial clinical and imaging worsening prompted suspicion of HCM phenocopy, rather than a hereditary HCM. Unlike the generally benign course of HCM with a nonobstructive phenotype,9 DD is characterized by rapid clinical and imaging deterioration associated with heart failure.10 Thus, any significant deterioration within a short time frame should raise suspicion of DD. In this case, the syncope episode, increased ventricular fibrosis (30% to 50%), reduction in LVEF (50% to 36%), and rapid progression of hypertrophy with end-stage ventricular dilatation emerged as pivotal indicators to consider an alternative etiology.

Follow-Up

The patient had no complications within 2-month follow-up and will continue with post-transplant therapy and subsequent evaluation by pediatric cardiology.

Conclusions

This case not only sets a precedent by describing the phenotype of a previously undescribed genetic variant but also highlights the importance of imaging studies and diligent clinical follow-up in the approach of difficult-to-diagnose cardiomyopathies. Our clinical and imaging approach demonstrates that this detailed delimitation of HCM can bring us closer to rare diagnoses and early interventions, particularly in situations where access to genetic studies is limited.
