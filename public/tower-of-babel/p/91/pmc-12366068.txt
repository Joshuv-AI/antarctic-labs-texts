Epidemiology of tuberculosis and HIV coinfection among inpatients in chengdu, china, 2018–2022
Authors: Xian-Zhen Tang, Yan Ma, Yao Ma, Qing Chen, Li-Ping Zou, Xiao-Yan Fu, Tao Huang, Shen-Jie Tang, Gui-Hui Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis (TB) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection are infectious diseases that pose serious threats to human health. TB and HIV can interact to promote the progression of diseases. This study aims to provide basic evidence for the formulation of disease prevention and control strategies.MethodA retrospective study was conducted to analyze the characteristics of demographics and comorbidity, in an attempt to identify factors associated with HIV and TB coinfection among TB inpatients at Public Health Clinical Center of Chengdu, China from January 2018 to December 2022.ResultsAmong 37,587 TB inpatients, HIV-positive and HIV-negative TB cases accounted for 6.1% (2,301) and 93.9% (35,286), respectively.11.9% (4,468) were extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB), of which 6.2%(278) were HIV-positive EPTB. Among TB, the most common types of HIV-positive TB were pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) (87.9% [2,023]), tuberculous pleurisy (34.4% [791]), and lymph node tuberculosis (33.1% [762]). The proportion of HIV-positive TB decreased from 7.0% in 2018 to 5.1% in 2022 (P < 0.001), showing a downward trend. Factors associated with HIV-positive TB included being 18–59 years of age, male sex, divorced or widowed status, and urban residence. The comorbidity factors associated with HIV-positive TB included hepatitis C infection and syphilis. Those with HIV-positive TB were more likely to be complicated with opportunistic infections, including cryptococcal infection, toxoplasma infection, cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection, and Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PjP).ConclusionEarly screening for HIV in TB with associated factors, comprehensive management, and control of comorbidities may significantly contribute to achieving the WHO End TB Strategy targets.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Human immunodeficiency virus infection, Epidemiology, Comorbidity

Background

TB is caused by the infection with Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which mainly infects the lungs to induce PTB and can also infect other parts of the body. TB remains a chronic infectious disease posing a serious threat to human health and is one of the leading causes of death worldwide. According to the Global Tuberculosis Report 2023, there were an estimated 10.6 million new TB cases in 2022, and the TB incidence increased by 3.9% between 2020 and 2022, reversing the approximately 2% annual decline observed over most of the past 2 decades [1]. An estimated 1.3 million deaths among people died from TB worldwide in 2022 [1]. China remains a high-TB-burden country, accounting for 7.1% of the global TB incidence and ranking third globally [1], with an estimated 748,000 new TB cases in 2022 [2]. Overall, poverty, malnutrition, HIV infection, smoking, and diabetes are risk factors for TB [1].

In high-burden settings, HIV coinfection increases the susceptibility to primary infection or reinfection of TB and also increases the risk of latent TB progressing to active TB [3]. HIV infection can increase the risk of active TB by 26-fold [4], while people living with HIV on ART have a four-fold higher TB risk [5]. TB is also the leading cause of deaths among people living with HIV. It was estimated that 671,000 new TB with HIV worldwide of whom 85% antiretroviral therapy coverage and 167,000 deaths in 2022 [1]. Due to the aggravation of HIV infection-associated immunosuppression and the effects of severe disease, TB advances more rapidly and TB is susceptible to spread [6]. In addition, when HIV is complicated by TB, the clinical symptoms and imaging manifestations may be atypical, and the positive rate of sputum smear is low [7], resulting in delayed diagnosis and adverse outcomes. TB treatment success rates are lower in HIV-positive versus HIV-negative individuals [8]. A cohort study reported a mere 66% success rate among HIV-positive TB patients in Finland [9]. Conversely, TB infection impairs HIV immune control, accelerating HIV progression to AIDS [3]. T cell exhaustion, granulomatous formation, glutathione depletion, and TNF-α up-regulation caused by mycobacterium tuberculosis infection would increase the severity of HIV-associated diseases [10]. Moreover, TB and HIV coinfection would exacerbate the epidemic of TB and even deaths, presenting challenges to programs and treatments around the world.

The prevalence of HIV and TB varies significantly in different regions. Hence, a more thorough understanding of local trends in HIV and TB coinfection can inform the development of targeted diagnostic and treatment strategies. While some countries and regions have conducted relevant epidemiological studies, data from our country and regions has been limited in recent years. Meanwhile, TB inpatients often have more severe disease and higher comorbidity burden, some of which may increase the risk of HIV coinfection. Given that Sichuan is a high-risk area for TB and HIV, and our hospital is the largest infectious disease hospital in the province, studying the epidemiological characteristics of HIV and TB coinfection is of great significance. This study aims to fill gaps in the current knowledge by analyzing underexplored regions in the field of HIV and TB coinfection epidemiology. Through this study, we aim to provide empirical data to inform the development of HIV and TB coinfection prevention and control strategies, offer comprehensive insights, and drive advancements in related fields. In this study, the baseline demographics and clinical characteristics of HIV and TB coinfection among inpatients in Chengdu, Sichuan, China, from January 2018 to December 2022 were retrospectively reviewed. The objectives were to analyze the proportions of various HIV and TB co-infection, comorbidities, and TB subtypes, thereby identifying factors associated with HIV-positive TB.

Methods

Data collection and criteria

Baseline demographics (including age, gender, marital status, address) and clinical data (comorbidities, tuberculosis types) of TB patients admitted to the Public Health Clinical Center of Chengdu from January 1, 2018 to December 31, 2022 were retrospectively collected using the hospital’s electronic patient record system. Epidemiological characteristics were then analyzed and described. These patients were divided into two groups based on HIV serostatus: HIV-positive TB patients and HIV-negative patients. While “HIV-positive/HIV-negative” terms were used for analytical clarity, we acknowledge that individuals living with these conditions are more than their serostatus and have used people-first language throughout this narrative. All included cases are hospitalized patients. Therefore the study subjects are referred to as “patients” without any discriminatory intent. The Public Health Clinical Center of Chengdu is a Class A tertiary hospital specializing in infectious diseases, providing healthcare services for TB, HIV/AIDS, liver disease, and other infectious diseases. located in southwest China, it is a designated medical institution for TB, with 575 beds in its TB and HIV/AIDS wards. The hospital primarily serves TB patients from downtown Chengdu and surrounding areas, and receives referrals of severe TB cases from low-level hospitals in the Southwest region. The hospital’s electronic patient record system contains records of TB inpatients’ diagnosis, treatment, and care. Additionally, disease diagnoses are coded according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10).

Patients suspected of or diagnosed with TB attending this hospital were advised to undergo TB etiology-related examinations whenever possible, including microscopic examination, mycobacterial culture, or molecular testing. Tissue specimens were further analyzed in the hospital’s pathology laboratory for histopathological confirmation. EPTB is classified into TB of pleura, lymph nodes, meninges, bone and/or joint, genitourinary, abdomen, pericarditis, and other extrapulmonary sites excluding the lungs [11]. Active TB was diagnosis based on at least one of the following criteria: sputum, exudate, pus, or pathological tissues from the lesion site, smear-positive for acid-fast bacilli (AFB), culture-positive for Mycobacterium tuberculosis, positive molecular detection of tuberculosis (e.g., Mycobacterium tuberculosis DNA and/or Xpert MTB/RIF PCR); or strong clinical evidence consistent with active TB [12, 13]. Clinically diagnosed active TB cases with bacteriologically negative results were also included. Patients were required to undergo HIV antibody testing through at a Ministry of Health-certified HIV confirmation laboratory using Western blot, strip immunoblot assay, or linear immunoblot assay to confirm the HIV infection. All included inpatients had undergone HIV testing.

Comorbidities of patients included a self-reported history or new diagnosis confirmed through hospital examinations. In this study, comorbidities were classified into infectious and non-infectious diseases. Infectious comorbidities included hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, syphilis, PjP, hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection, non-tuberculous mycobacterium (NTM) infection, cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection, toxoplasma infection, and cryptococcus infection. Non-infectious comorbidities included diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, liver cirrhosis, and malnutrition.

Definition

Malnutrition

Body Mass Index (BMI) < 18.5.

HBV infection

A positive result for HBsAg or HBV DNA confirms HBV infection.

HCV infection

A positive result for anti-HCV antibody and/or HCV RNA confirms HCV infection.

Diagnostic criteria for PjP

The onset is subacute, with fever, dry cough, exertional dyspnea, and progressive respiratory distress as the main manifestations. Chest X-ray shows diffuse reticulonodular interstitial infiltration in both lungs starting from the hilar regions. Lung CT shows ground-glass changes in both lungs. Definitive diagnosis requires etiological examination, such as identification of Pneumocystis jirovecii cysts or trophozoites in sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, or lung biopsy specimens. Pneumocystis PCR testing is positive.

Statistical analysis

Demographics data, comorbidities, and TB types associated with HIV-positive/HIV-negative TB incidence were tabulated using Excel to show the number and proportion of patients. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS27.0. The count data were expressed as a rate (%), and comparisons of group differences were performed using the chi-squared test. The p-value < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference in the distribution of categorical variables between HIV-positive TB and HIV-negative TB groups. Parameters conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s). The differences between the two groups were compared using the Student’s t test. Skewed distributions were reported as median (interquartile range) [M (Q1, Q3)], and differences across groups were analyzed using nonparametric methods. Single and multivariable logistic regression models were conducted to identify factors associated with HIV coinfection in TB patients. In the multivariate logistic analysis, all independent variables (sex, age, residence, marriage status, HBV infection, HCV infection, syphilis, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, cirrhosis, and malnutrition) were included, excluding variables with fewer than 20 events (PjP, NTM infection, CMV infection, toxoplasma infection, and cryptococcosis infection).

Results

Demographics, clinical characteristics and comorbidities of HIV-positive/HIV-negative TB

From 2018 to 2022, a total of 37,587 TB patients were hospitalized at the Public Health Clinical Center of Chengdu. Among these patients, HIV-positive TB and HIV-negative TB patients accounted for 6.1% (2,301) and 93.9% (35,286), respectively. There were statistically significant differences in gender, age, marital status, and residence between the two groups (p < 0.05) (Table 1). Compared with the HIV-negative TB group, the HIV-positive TB group had higher prevalence of HBV infection, HCV infection, CMV infection, syphilis, PjP, toxoplasma infection, cryptococcosis infection, cirrhosis, and malnutrition, with statistically significant differences (p < 0.05). In contrast, the proportion of diabetes was lower in HIV-positive TB cases than in HIV-negative TB patients, and this difference was also statistically significant (p < 0.05).

Among 2,301 HIV-positive TB patients, the six most prevalent TB types were PTB (87.9% [2,023]), tuberculous pleurisy (34.4% [791]), lymph node tuberculosis (33.1% [762]), tuberculous pericarditis (16.4% [377]), tuberculous meningitis (11.4% [263]), and digestive system tuberculosis (10.4% [240]). The proportion of lymph node tuberculosis, tuberculous pericarditis, tuberculous meningitis and digestive system tuberculosis was higher in HIV-positive TB patients, whereas the proportion of tuberculous pleurisy was significantly lower (p < 0.05 for all comparisons). However, the intergroup comparison of PTB prevalence showed no significant statistical difference (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

In this study, there were 33,119 (88.1%) people with PTB and 4,468 (11.9%) people with EPTB. Among these EPTB cases, the proportions of HIV-positive EPTB and HIV-negative EPTB were 6.2% (278) and 93.8% (4,190), respectively. In 278 HIV-positive EPTB patients, the top six TB types were lymph node tuberculosis (39.9% [111]), tuberculous pleurisy (19.8% [55]), tuberculous meningitis (10.8% [30]), tuberculous pericarditis (10.4% [29]), Bone/Joint TB (10.1% [28]), and digestive system tuberculosis (9.7% [27]) (Table 2). Compared with HIV-negative EPTB patients, HBV infection, HCV infection, syphilis, CMV infection, NTM infection, PjP, toxoplasma infection, cryptococcosis infectionand, and tuberculous meningitis were more common in HIV-positive EPTB patients, and the difference was statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Table 2).

Associated Factors of HIV-positive TB patients

This study compared the demographic and clinical characteristics between HIV-positive TB/EPTB and HIV-negative TB/EPTB patients (Tables 1 and 2). Among TB patients, male sex, older age, urban residence, and married, widowed, or divorced status were associated with HIV positivity (Table 3). Additionally, significant differences were observed in comorbidities. HCV infection, syphilis and diabetes mellitus were identified as associated factors for HIV-positive TB (Table 3). Further analysis of six distinct HIV-positive TB types revealed additional associations (Tables 4 and 5). Specifically, malnutrition was significantly correlated with HIV-positive PTB.

Trends in HIV-positive TB

The epidemiological trend of HIV-positive TB from 2018 to 2022 were also analyzed. Over the five-year period, the proportion of HIV-positive TB decreased significantly from 7.0% in 2018 to 5.1% in 2022 (P < 0.001), showing a downward trend (Fig. 1). Among HIV-positive TB patients, type-specific trends showed fluctuations in PTB (85.6–89.9%), lymph node tuberculosis (29.3-37.2%), and tuberculous pericarditis (14.0-18.6%). The proportion of tuberculous pleurisy increased from 34.2% in 2018 to a peak of 37.5% in 2020 before declining to 30.9% in 2022. Tuberculous meningitis exhibited a decrease from 9.6% in 2018 to a nadir of 8.9% in 2020, followed by an increase to 13.9% in 2022. Notably, the proportion of digestive system TB rose sharply from 1.7 to 19.7% during this period (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The HIV infection rate among TB patients from 2018 to 2022 ranged from 5.1 to 7.0%, with an overall decreasing trend in the study. The epidemiology of TB and HIV coinfection exhibits marked geographical heterogeneity, with prevalence rates closely aligned with regional HIV burden [1, 14, 15]. High HIV-incidence areas, such as Sub-Saharan Africa, report elevated coinfection rates: 50.5% in Cape Town Metro East (2011–2012) [16], 23.4% in Ethiopia (2003–2018) [17], and 34.4% in eastern/southern Sub-Saharan Africa (1990–2017) versus 27.3% in western/central regions [18]. Similarly, Nigeria shows regional disparities, with north-central states at 34.3% versus 19.3% in the southeast [19]. Singapore reported lower rates (2.6–4.7%, 2000–2014) [20]. Conversely, declines in coinfection prevalence were observed in England, Wales, Northern Ireland (2000–2014) [21] Sub-Saharan Africa (1990–2017) [13] and Brazil (2008–2019) [22] following implementation of targeted policies and care strategies. These findings highlight the need for region-specific interventions to address HIV and TB comorbidity. According to the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), from 2015 to 2019, the HIV testing proportion increased from 46.0 to 64.0% in TB patients, the HIV-positive rate ranged from 0.1 to 2.4%, and provinces such as Sichuan (2.4%), Guangxi (2.4%), Fujian (1.8%), Chongqing (1.5%), Yunnan (1.2%) exceeded 1% [23]. The efficacy of anti-TB treatment was observed in 87.6% of individuals with HIV and TB [23]. Chengdu, located in Sichuan Province, has a high prevalence of HIV among TB patients in China. As a specialized infectious diseases hospital, our institution treats a higher proportion of TB and HIV patients, which may explain the higher rate of HIV coinfection in TB patients compared to national CDC reports. In recent years, improvements in HIV screening, diagnosis, and treatment among TB patients have increased treatment success rates for HIV-TB coinfection. According to the UNAIDS date, the proportion of co-managed TB and HIV cases in China from 2018 to 2021 ranged from 43.7 to 49.3%, and the proportion of people living with HIV newly enrolled in care with active TB decreased from 2.1% in 2018 to 1.3% in 2021 [24]. From 1995 to 2015, China’s annual expenditure on HIV/AIDS prevention and control grew by 10.1% [25], reaching $1.05 billion in 2022 [24]. Initiated in 2003, free antiretroviral therapy (ART) has reached over 80% of diagnosed HIV patients, attaining a 91% viral suppression rate [25]. These achievements are attributed to political commitment, comprehensive care programs, expanded testing, timely antiretroviral treatment, societal participation, and international support [25]. However, substantial endeavors are needed to achieve the UNAIDS 95-95-95 target by 2030. Early HIV diagnosis and effective antiretroviral therapy are critical for reducing HIV-TB coinfection [20]. Given the high prevalence and incidence of TB among people living with HIV [26], screening for TB in this population is equally important. A collaborative mechanism between TB and HIV control programs should be established [27], involving governments, specialized hospitals, and prevention institutions. A dedicated registration and monitoring system could improve patient tracking and care. Accessible, free HIV screening services should be expanded, and healthcare workforce capacity must be strengthened to meet the needs of comprehensive prevention and control strategies. As a region with a high burden of TB and HIV, we should intensify efforts to prevent HIV transmission, reduce TB incidence, and advance toward the goal of end TB.

In this study, 11.9% of TB cases were EPTB, with 6.2% of EPTB patients being HIV-positive. HIV-positive TB patients had a higher proportion of lymph node TB, tuberculous pericarditis, tuberculous meningitis, and digestive system TB than HIV-negative TB patients. Tuberculous meningitis was particularly prevalent in HIV-positive EPTB. HIV and TB coinfection was associated with a higher incidence of EPTB, increased risk of latent TB reactivation, and dissemination via bloodstream and lymphatic pathways [28]. A meta-analysis suggested a significant association between HIV and EPTB [29]. A study in Africa reported a high incidence of EPTB among people living with HIV/AIDS [30]. Being HIV-positive was a risk factor for EPTB [31]. The intricate interplay of host T cells, Tregs, cytokine production, and compromised Th1 responses increased the likelihood of extrapulmonary infections in individuals with HIV coinfection [32]. Moreover, compared with HIV-negative EPTB, those HIV-positive EPTB were diagnosed later and showed poorer survival outcomes [33]. Autopsies of people who died from AIDS-related TB have revealed disseminated TB with multi-organ involvement [34]. In addition to the screening of PTB, it is also necessary to identify whether PTB people living with HIV/AIDS are also complicated by EPTB. This contributes to accurately assessing the condition of TB patients and the formulation of appropriate treatment strategies. This study found an interesting result: the absolute number of HIV-positive TB patients gradually decreased, while the number of TB in the digestive system increased year by year, showing an upward trend. This may be related to advances in diagnostic technology. High resolution CT, MRI and endoscopic techniques (such as colonoscopy with biopsy) can enhance the detection of previously missed gastrointestinal tuberculosis lesions (such as ileocecal ulcer and stenosis) [35]. PCR based analysis (such as Xpert MTB/RIF and/or TB PCR) and improved histopathological staining can accurately identify TB in biopsy samples, reducing misdiagnosis [35].

This study identified being male as an associated factor for HIV-positive TB. The male bias was hypothesized to be similar to the effect of gender on TB attributed to behavioral and biological differences that influence infection risk and disease progression [36]. The global incidence of TB is consistently higher in males than in females, although the ratio of males to females varies in different regions [36]. Males living with HIV had the largest relative additional burden which was observed in Asia-Pacific, Oceania, and Central Latin America with a high income (age-standardized male/female incidence ratio: 1.8-2.0 in China), however the proportion of females living with HIV was higher in Africa [37]. Therefore, the effect of gender on HIV and TB may be influenced by regions.

The results of this study showed that HIV and TB patients aged 18–59 years accounted for 81.8% of all TB patients. Among TB patients, the proportion of HIV-positive TB was higher in those aged 18–59 years compared with those over 60 years. HIV-positive TB patients showed distinct age patterns compared to HIV-negative TB. In Wuxi, China, HIV and TB coinfection risk peaked in patients aged 30–59 years (42.8%) compared to those < 18 years [38]. In Singapore, 31.7% (242) of HIV-positive patients were 40–49 years old, vs. 22.6% (173) aged 30–39 and 21.2% (162) aged 50–59 [20]. Japanese data indicated peak HIV and TB coinfection in 25-64-year-olds [8], while in England, Wales, and Northern Ireland, HIV and TB coinfection were highest in 35-44-year-olds (11.5%, 2,281) [21]. These findings highlight age-specific HIV and TB burden disparities requiring targeted interventions.

This study also showed that opportunistic infections such as PjP, CMV infection, toxoplasma infection, and cryptococcus proportion in HIV-positive TB patients were more common. HIV/AIDS can be combined with opportunistic infection, which correlate with CD4 + T-cell counts and ART effectiveness [39]. Besides, TB itself is an opportunistic infection. Among TB patients, co-occurrence of other opportunistic infections is strongly associated with HIV seropositivity [40], consistent with our findings. HIV-1 Tat may unintentionally generate an environment conducive to opportunistic infections by causing damage to the expression of disrupting cytokines in immune cells of hosts, inducing neuronal apoptosis, and interacting with other cellular factors to promote viral persistence [41]. Additionally, compared with HIV infection alone, people with HIV and TB coinfection were 2.46 times more likely to be virology unsuppressed [42]. Early identification of concurrent infections based on clinical symptoms, imaging, and experimental examinations is critical for achieving the prompt diagnosis and co-treatment of comorbidities. Timely antiretroviral treatment, treatment of latent TB infections, and preventive treatment of opportunistic infections are crucial for disease control among people living with HIV/AIDS [14].

It showed that syphilis and HCV infection were associated factors of HIV-positive TB in this study. This may be explained by the fact that syphilis, HCV, and HIV had the same infection routes. Epidemiological studies have identified significant associations between overlapping infections (TB, HIV and HCV) and high-risk behavioral patterns, including prolonged substance use, injection drug use, and unprotected sexual contact with HIV-positive people [43, 44]. TB, HIV, and HCV overlapping infection also present higher challenges for clinical treatment. When TB with syphilis and HCV infection are diagnosed, it is necessary to pay attention to the possibility of HIV infection, and HIV screening is required. A “one-stop service” healthcare integration model can be established, including a hospital or primary care clinic, which can provide the diagnosis and treatment of TB, HIV, HCV, and syphilis through the same doctor in the same place. This can facilitate the treatment and improve the compliance of patients. Meanwhile, medical care related to drug dependence should be provided for drug-dependent patients.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first study to explore the epidemiological characteristics and comorbidities of HIV and TB patients in a hospital in southwest China. The prevalence, hospitalization rate, demographics, TB types, and comorbidities among HIV and TB coinfection patients were analyzed. Additionally, factors associated with HIV and TB coinfection were examined, including the trend prevalence of HIV and TB coinfection from 2018 to 2022. These findings may inform policy-making to guide targeted screening for HIV-TB coinfection. However, the study has several limitations. Firstly, some HIV and TB cases were clinically diagnosed without definitive bacteriological confirmation, which may have introduced diagnostic inaccuracies and affected the results to some extent. Second, the analysis of associated factors lacked comprehensive data on HIV transmission routes, potentially limiting the ability to refine high-risk screening populations. Third, as a retrospective study, it did not fully explore HIV staging or the timing of antiretroviral therapy initiation. Future research into the epidemiological characteristics of HIV-TB coinfection will help develop region-specific prevention, control, and treatment strategies for HIV-TB coinfected individuals.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the results of this study revealed that the prevalence of HIV-positive TB inpatients showed a downward trend in Chengdu, China from 2018 to 2022. Factors such as 18–59 years of age, male sex, urban residence, hepatitis C infection, and syphilis were identified as associated with HIV-positive TB. HIV and TB can potentiate each other and accelerate disease progression. HIV-positive TB patients are more likely to have infectious comorbidities, which complicates disease treatment and management. HIV screening should be prioritized among TB patients with these associated factors. Strategies for the management and treating related comorbidities in HIV and TB coinfected individuals are crucial for controlling TB epidemics.
