Lateral Snapping Elbow Caused by Entrapment of the Degenerative Annular Ligament: A Case Series
Authors: Hideyuki Kobayashi, Yuichiro Matsui, Hiroyuki Kato, Masanori Hayashi, Makoto Motomiya, Daisuke Momma, Takeshi Endo, Hirofumi Miyaji, Norimasa Iwasaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLateral snapping elbow is a rare condition, and cases attributed to intra-articular lesions of the annular ligament are particularly rare. This study was performed to analyze cases of lateral snapping elbow due to annular ligament entrapment to clarify its pathogenesis and clinical characteristics.MethodsWe reviewed 4 patients who underwent surgical treatment for painful lateral snapping elbow caused by annular ligament entrapment at 3 institutions from 2010 through 2018. The patients included 2 men and 2 women with a mean age of 59 years (range, 45–69 years). Preoperative evaluations included physical examination, x-rays, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and the Mayo Elbow Performance Score (MEPS).ResultsAll patients were followed up for an average of 10.5 months (range, 6–12 months). Significant tenderness at the lateral epicondyle of the humerus and snapping were noted preoperatively. MRI consistently demonstrated high signal intensity at the origin of the extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB). All 4 patients were diagnosed with snapping elbow associated with lateral epicondylitis, for which surgical intervention was performed because of resistance to conservative treatment. Intraoperatively, the degenerative, entrapped annular ligament within the radiocapitellar joint was observed to reposition during elbow flexion in forearm pronation. Partial resection of the degenerative, entrapped annular ligament was performed, along with ECRB debridement in all patients. The mean MEPS improved from 73.8 preoperatively to 100 postoperatively.ConclusionsDegeneration of the annular ligament can lead to its entrapment in the radiocapitellar joint, resulting in snapping elbow. Surgical partial resection of the annular ligament and debridement of the ECRB effectively resolve symptoms and restore function. Snapping elbow caused by annular ligament entrapment may be distinguished by snapping that occurs during pronation and flexion, indicating ligament relocation.

Snapping elbow is a relatively rare condition. Intra-articular causes include abnormalities of the annular ligament, synovial plicae, intra-articular loose bodies, and meniscuslike structures, while extra-articular causes involve triceps abnormalities and ulnar nerve dislocation.1) In a review of snapping elbow cases by Bjerre et al.,1) only about 5% (5 of 105 cases) were attributed to the annular ligament among intra-articular causes, suggesting it is a relatively uncommon etiology.

Although several case reports have described lateral snapping elbow caused by abnormalities of the annular ligament, the mechanism by which the annular ligament produces snapping remains unclear. Moreover, distinguishing snapping elbow due to annular ligament involvement from other potential causes, such as synovial plicae, is often difficult preoperatively in clinical practice.

We herein report 4 cases of snapping elbow localized to the lateral compartment. All cases were caused by annular ligament entrapment, and partial resection of the ligament led to favorable outcomes. Based on clinical and surgical findings, we discuss the pathophysiology of snapping elbow due to annular ligament entrapment.

METHODS

This research has been approved by the Ethical Review Board for Life Science and Medical Research, Hokkaido University Hospital (IRB No. 025-0029). Written informed consent was obtained from each patient so that data from their cases could be submitted for publication.

We studied 4 elbows in 4 patients who underwent surgical treatment for painful lateral snapping elbow due to annular ligament entrapment at 3 institutions from 2010 through 2018. The group included 2 male and 2 female patients, with an average age of 59 years (range, 45–69 years) at the time of surgery. We evaluated preoperative physical findings, x-ray and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) results, the Mayo Elbow Performance Score (MEPS) both preoperatively and at final follow-up, intraoperative findings, and the presence or absence of recurrence after surgery. Pathohistological examination was performed in only 1 case.

RESULTS

All patients were followed up for an average of 10.5 months (range, 6–12 months). At the initial visit, all patients showed marked tenderness at the lateral epicondyle of the humerus, along with snapping. Short tau inversion recovery (STIR) MRI revealed high signal intensity areas at the origin of the extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) in each patient. None of the patients exhibited elbow instability. All patients were diagnosed with snapping elbow associated with lateral epicondylitis. Surgical intervention was performed primarily for persistent, painful lateral snapping that failed to respond to adequate conservative management. Intraoperatively, the degenerative annular ligament was found entrapped within the radiocapitellar joint, and snapping was reproduced during elbow flexion and extension with the forearm in pronation (Table 1).

In all cases, partial resection of the degenerative, entrapped annular ligament was performed, along with ECRB debridement. The degenerative, entrapped portion of the annular ligament during supination and flexion was resected as completely as possible. No patient experienced recurrence of lateral elbow pain, snapping phenomena, radial head instability, or varus instability of the elbow joint at final follow-up. The MEPS improved after surgery, and all patients were satisfied with the surgical outcomes.

Case Presentation

A 58-year-old woman developed right elbow pain after throwing a stone into a river. The pain persisted, and snapping symptoms appeared 1 month later, prompting a visit to a local clinic. She was diagnosed with lateral epicondylitis and received a triamcinolone injection at the lateral epicondyle of the humerus, which alleviated the pain. However, elbow snapping and pain recurred the following year without any clear trigger, leading to referral to our hospital. At presentation, elbow range of motion was extension/flexion 10°/130° and pronation/supination 80°/90°, with no significant limitation. Both Cozen’s test and Maudsley’s test were positive. Painful snapping was observed during elbow flexion and extension in pronation. Imaging revealed an 8 × 7-mm cystic radiolucent lesion in the capitellum on plain x-rays. STIR MRI showed high signal intensity at the ECRB origin on the lateral epicondyle (Fig. 1A). Valgus–varus stress tests under fluoroscopy were negative. Based on these findings, we diagnosed snapping elbow associated with lateral epicondylitis and a simple bone cyst of the right humeral capitellum. Synovectomy and partial annular ligament resection according to Boyd’s method were planned, along with curettage and bone grafting of the bone cyst.2) Intraoperatively, the ECRB attachment was degenerative, and the thickened annular ligament within the radiocapitellar joint was seen to reposition during elbow flexion at 100° in pronation, causing the snapping phenomenon (Fig. 1B). Partial resection of the entrapped portion of the annular ligament with ECRB debridement was performed using an open approach. No postoperative elbow joint instability was observed. The treatment resulted in complete symptom resolution. Pathological examination of the resected annular ligament revealed degenerative and mucinous ligament tissue (Fig. 1C). No recurrence or pain exacerbation was observed at the 1-year postoperative follow-up.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that annular ligament entrapment, accompanied by degenerative changes at the ECRB origin associated with lateral epicondylitis, is a potential cause of snapping elbow. Macroscopically, the entrapped annular ligament exhibited thickening, while histopathological analysis revealed mucoid degeneration. These findings suggest that chronic degenerative changes in the lateral elbow complex, particularly involving the annular ligament, may play a critical role in the pathophysiology of snapping elbow. Previous studies have reported thickening and histological degeneration of the entrapped annular ligament in snapping elbow cases.345) Our findings further support this by highlighting the association between annular ligament degeneration and lateral epicondylitis. Because the annular ligament forms part of the lateral capsuloligamentous complex and lies adjacent to the ECRB origin, chronic tendinopathy at the lateral epicondyle may alter local load transmission and secondarily increase mechanical stress on the annular ligament. Given that lateral collateral ligament dysfunction has been implicated in lateral epicondylitis, chronic stress on the lateral elbow complex may contribute to a cascade of degenerative changes, including annular ligament thickening, microtears, and increased laxity, which could lead to a painful snapping phenomenon.56) Additionally, in 1 case, snapping elbow occurred acutely after a throwing motion, suggesting a possible traumatic etiology. Trauma-induced snapping elbow has been previously documented, and our findings reinforce the notion that annular ligament rupture due to acute trauma may also contribute to the pathogenesis.7)

Notably, in all 4 cases of annular ligament entrapment, snapping accompanied by reduction consistently occurred during elbow flexion at approximately 100° in the pronated position. Entrapment of the annular ligament is commonly reported to be triggered during elbow joint extension and reduced during flexion.345) In 1 reported case, reduction was observed during pronation.8) This phenomenon may be explained by the anatomical morphology of the radial head and the kinematics of the proximal radioulnar joint (PRUJ). It is known that the radial head is not perfectly round but rather elliptical, which causes the radius to undergo slight anterior–posterior and mediolateral movements relative to the ulna at the PRUJ during pronation and supination. During pronation, the radial head moves anteriorly within the radial notch of the ulna, while the posterior annular ligament tightens.910) Additionally, previous cadaveric studies have shown that the relative position of the radius to the ulna shifts proximally during pronation and becomes more pronounced with flexion.11) Specifically, when a degenerative and loosened annular ligament becomes entrapped within the joint during transition from supination to the neutral position, anterior translation of the radial head during pronation increases tension in the ligament. Subsequent elbow flexion, accompanied by proximal migration of the radial head, may facilitate reduction of the ligament to its normal position (Fig. 2).

Synovial plicae can also cause lateral elbow pain and may be implicated in painful snapping elbow.112) Although synovial plicae are normal structures, they can hypertrophy due to repetitive mechanical stimulation, leading to pain and snapping phenomena.12) In this study, annular ligament entrapment induced snapping in pronation in all cases, suggesting that this observation may aid in differentiating it from other causes of snapping elbow, such as synovial plicae. To the best of our knowledge, there have been no reports describing positional differences in synovial plica-related snapping with forearm pronation and supination. Therefore, given that snapping due to annular ligament entrapment is typically provoked during elbow flexion in pronation, this positional characteristic may serve as a useful clinical clue in distinguishing it from synovial plica-related snapping. Conversely, pulled elbow—resulting from sudden traction on the forearm—is a well-known condition in preschool children but presents rarely in adults.1314) In adult cases, it is typically caused by forceful traction on the forearm and is traumatic in nature. Once appropriately reduced, the elbow remains stable, without recurrent snapping during flexion and extension. Therefore, this condition differs fundamentally from the snapping phenomenon observed in the present study.

Partial resection of the entrapped portion of the annular ligament has been reported to provide good outcomes in cases of snapping elbow caused by annular ligament involvement.3458) Similarly, our case series showed favorable clinical results. Kerver et al.15) reported successful outcomes after partial resection of the annular ligament in 23 cases, performed either under direct visualization or arthroscopically, with additional annular ligament reconstruction in cases where PRUJ instability developed following resection. Based on these findings, to prevent PRUJ instability after annular ligament resection, it is crucial to assess for PRUJ instability preoperatively and to limit resection to the portion responsible for the snapping phenomenon. Specifically, resection should be confined to the macroscopically degenerative areas, focusing only on the segments that induce snapping and become entrapped within the joint during elbow flexion in pronation (Fig. 2). In the present study, no cases of PRUJ instability were observed postoperatively.

The limitations of this study include its retrospective nature, the small number of cases because of the rarity of the condition, and the lack of histopathological examination in all cases. To further investigate the etiology, it may also be necessary to examine cases of snapping elbow caused by other pathological conditions.

We experienced 4 cases of snapping elbow caused by annular ligament entrapment, suggesting that the condition arises from chronic stress on the lateral elbow complex and microinjury-induced laxity of the annular ligament. Our findings suggest that degeneration of the annular ligament leads to its entrapment within the radiocapitellar joint, resulting in snapping elbow. Snapping elbow due to annular ligament entrapment may be distinguished by the positional characteristics of the snapping, particularly its occurrence during elbow flexion in pronation.
