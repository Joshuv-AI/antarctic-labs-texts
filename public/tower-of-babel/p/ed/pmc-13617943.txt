Comparative evaluation of remineralization agents in severe enamel hypomineralization using a porcine enamel model
Authors: Aslı Aşık, Dilşah Çoğulu, Arzu Aykut Yetkiner, Nazan Ersin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo comparatively evaluate the effects of fluoride varnish, casein phosphopeptide–amorphous calcium phosphate (CPP-ACP) varnish, and self-assembling peptide P11-4 on the microhardness and surface morphology of hypomineralized enamel using a porcine model simulating molar incisor hypomineralization (MIH).Materials and methodsEighteen hypomineralized primary canines and needle teeth (the erupted third incisors of newborn piglets) obtained from three stillborn Yorkshire piglets were included in this in vitro study. Following stereomicroscopic examination, samples were randomly allocated into three groups (n = 6): Group 1: sodium fluoride (NaF) varnish, Group 2: NaF + CPP-ACP varnish, and Group 3: P11-4. Baseline microhardness (T0) was measured using a microhardness tester under a 300 g load for 15 s. Remineralization agents were applied according to manufacturers’ instructions. P11-4 application included prior sodium hypochlorite cleaning and phosphoric acid etching. Samples were stored in artificial saliva at 37 °C. Microhardness measurements were repeated at 1 week (T1) and 1 month (T2). One representative sample from each group was analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Statistical analysis was performed using two-way repeated measures ANOVA and Bonferroni post hoc tests (p < 0.05).ResultsMicrohardness values significantly increased over time in all groups (p < 0.001). Significant differences were observed between all-time points (T0–T1, T0–T2, T1–T2) within each group. At T2, the P11-4 group demonstrated the highest microhardness values, followed by NaF + CPP-ACP and NaF groups (p < 0.05). Intergroup comparisons revealed statistically significant differences, particularly between P11-4 and the other groups at T2. SEM images demonstrated an irregular surface morphology related to mineral deposit accumulation after the application of remineralization agents. Nevertheless, in the P11-4 group, the covering deposits and crack-like lines appeared less scattered and more integrated than in the other treatment groups.ConclusionAll remineralization agents improved enamel microhardness; however, P11-4 demonstrated superior efficacy compared to NaF + CPP-ACP and NaF varnish. Porcine enamel appears to be a reliable and biologically relevant model for simulating MIH-affected enamel in vitro.

Introduction

Investigations into the condition now known as molar incisor hypomineralization (MIH) began in the late 1970s, when Swedish dentists observed an increasing number of pediatric patients presenting with hypomineralization characterized by demarcated opacities in the enamel [1]. However, the specific term “molar incisor hypomineralization” was not introduced until 2001 [2]. Over the subsequent years, substantial progress has been made in defining the etiology, diagnosis, and management of MIH. As the body of contemporary literature has expanded and the prevalence of the condition has increased, MIH has become a prevalent and clinically significant challenge for pediatric dentists and is now recognized as a global public health concern [3, 4].

MIH is a qualitative developmental defect of enamel characterized by hypomineralized, protein-rich, and structurally compromised enamel, clinically presenting as well-demarcated opacities ranging from creamy-white to yellow-brown chalky lesions. These lesions exhibit increased porosity, reduced mineral density, and inferior mechanical properties, including decreased microhardness and elastic modulus, which predispose affected teeth to post-eruptive enamel breakdown, hypersensitivity, and rapid caries progression [5–7].

Clinically, MIH may present as small, localized white or yellowish opacities or, in more severe cases, as extensive brown discoloration accompanied by post-eruptive enamel breakdown. The protein content of the affected area increases with the darkening of the demarcated region [8, 9] Affected teeth often exhibit pronounced hypersensitivity to thermal and mechanical stimuli. The porous enamel structure, combined with hypersensitivity, complicates oral hygiene maintenance and promotes plaque accumulation, establishing a significant risk factor for caries development. Consequently, MIH-affected teeth frequently require atypical restorations, and adhesion-related challenges due to high protein content often lead to repeated restorative interventions [3, 5, 7].

For MIH-affected enamel surfaces identified early, remineralizing agents are recommended to prevent caries, enhance mineral content, and alleviate hypersensitivity. Studies evaluating these agents on MIH-affected enamel have predominantly been conducted in clinical settings, often relying on patient-reported outcomes [10]. While research on fluoride-based and CPP-ACP-based agents is relatively common [11, 12], only one pilot study has investigated the effects of the self-assembling peptide P11-4 [13], a contemporary biomimetic remineralization agent, on MIH-affected teeth. The efficacy of P11-4 in promoting mineral gain has been demonstrated in studies on initial enamel caries, post-orthodontic white spot lesions, dental erosion, and after tooth whitening procedures [14, 15]; however, evidence regarding its effects on MIH-affected enamel remains scarce.

Despite the growing interest in remineralization therapies, conducting robust clinical and in vitro studies on MIH-affected teeth remains inherently challenging. The heterogeneity in lesion severity, distribution, and patient-related factors limits standardization, while ethical considerations restrict invasive sampling. Furthermore, extracted MIH teeth are scarce and often unsuitable due to prior restorations or extensive structural breakdown, resulting in limited sample sizes and reduced reproducibility [16, 17].

In vitro studies examining mineral gain and microhardness values on MIH-affected enamel surfaces are limited in the literature, primarily because extracted MIH-affected teeth often present with extensive crown destruction and caries, rendering them unsuitable for such investigations. While enamel and dentin carious lesions can be artificially created in laboratory settings, MIH-affected enamel surfaces cannot be experimentally replicated [6, 18]. To address this limitation, the present study aims to comparatively evaluate the effects of different remineralization agents on microhardness values using porcine teeth.

Porcine teeth have been proposed as a suitable model to mimic MIH-affected enamel due to their high protein content and low mineral content during the early post-eruptive period, a characteristic shared with MIH-affected enamel [19]. Proteomic analyses have further supported this model, demonstrating elevated albumin levels within porcine enamel that parallel the inhibitory effect on crystal growth observed in MIH [19–21].

The null hypothesis of this study is that there is no statistically significant difference in microhardness values among the remineralizing agents fluoride varnish, NaF + CPP-ACP, and the P11-4 when applied to porcine teeth with enamel hypomineralization. As this study is the first to utilize porcine teeth as an in vitro model for MIH and to investigate the mechanism of P11-4 on MIH-affected enamel surfaces, it is anticipated to provide novel mechanistic and comparative insights into biomimetic remineralization strategies.

Materials and methods

A priori sample size estimation was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.4 software (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). The sample size calculation was based on a previous study with a similar experimental design that used six samples per group (total n = 18) for evaluating enamel microhardness changes following remineralization [22]. Assuming an effect size of 0.4, a significance level of α = 0.05, and a statistical power of 80%, the analysis indicated that a total of 18 samples (n = 6 per group) would be required for the present study. This study utilized dental tissues obtained from three stillborn Yorkshire piglets. The samples were sourced from a private experimental animal production and supply center (BOLUMED Experimental Animal Services Industry and Trade Ltd., Bursa, Turkey) licensed by the Republic of Türkiye Ministry of Health. The procurement and use of the animal-derived material were conducted in consultation with the Ege University Local Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments. Following acquisition, the samples were transported under cold-chain conditions to preserve tissue integrity.

Tooth selection was guided by the eruption chronology of porcine primary dentition. In newborn piglets, the primary canines and third incisors, commonly referred to as “needle teeth,” have already erupted into the oral cavity at birth and were therefore selected for this study. It has been previously demonstrated that porcine teeth are hypomineralized during eruption, exhibiting porous, opaque demarcation zones and containing high levels of albumin. Post-eruption, these teeth undergo a not-yet-fully-elucidated process of deproteinization, leading to a smooth, glossy surface and the gradual disappearance of the initial hypomineralized appearance. Considering this physiological process [19–21], the use of teeth from stillborn piglets provided a unique opportunity to study the enamel in its native, post-eruptive state.

The primary canine and third incisor teeth were extracted atraumatically using dental forceps and elevators, with meticulous care taken to avoid introducing any fractures, cracks, or scratches on the enamel surface. A total of eight teeth (four canines and four third incisors) were collected from each animal, yielding an initial sample pool of 24 teeth. All extracted teeth were subsequently stored in distilled water.

Prior to the main experimental procedures, all samples were evaluated under a stereomicroscope to assess their surface integrity. Teeth exhibiting any fractures, cracks, or surface scratches were excluded from the study. Stereomicroscopic examination of the included samples revealed distinct demarcation zones characterized by dark brown discoloration, reminiscent of severe cases of MIH in humans, as shown in Fig. 1.

Microhardness testing and tooth morphometry

Baseline surface microhardness (T0) was measured prior to the application of the remineralization agents. Measurements were obtained using a Shimadzu Type M microhardness tester (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) with a 300 g load applied for a 15-second dwell time [23].

Due to the location of the hypomineralized demarcation zones, which were consistently observed as a band in the incisal region rather than near the cementoenamel junction, all microhardness indentations were performed specifically within these demarcated areas. To account for the limited dimensions of these zones, one indentation was made on the buccal surface and one on the lingual surface within the hypomineralized demarcated zone, and the mean of these two measurements was recorded as the final microhardness value for each sample.

To characterize the samples, tooth dimensions were recorded using an electronic digital caliper. The total tooth length was measured from the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) to the incisal tip. Additionally, the maximum mesiodistal diameter was measured at the equatorial region of the tooth. The recorded mesiodistal diameters ranged from 2 to 3 mm.

Group 2 (NaF + CPP-ACP varnish): A fluoride varnish containing 2% RECALDENT™ (CPP-ACP) and 5% sodium fluoride (MI Varnish, GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) was applied as a thin and uniform layer once to the enamel surfaces using an applicator brush and left in place for 5 min [25].Group 3 (P 11-4): Prior to application, the enamel surfaces were cleaned for 20 s with cotton pellets soaked in 2% sodium hypochlorite (Microvem AF, Istanbul, Turkey). The P11-4 (Curodont™ Repair, vVardis, Zug, Switzerland) applicator was activated by removing the safety clip and pushing the two cylinders together. The P11-4 was then applied to the prepared enamel surfaces and left undisturbed for 5 min to allow for diffusion [24].

Following the application of the remineralization agents, the samples were stored in freshly prepared artificial saliva, and the saliva in the Eppendorf tubes was replaced daily. Composition of the artificial saliva (Testonic Laboratory, Colin® Specific Solutions, Istanbul, Turkey) used: per liter of distilled water: 2.560 g sodium chloride (NaCl), 0.333 g calcium chloride (CaCl₂), 0.250 g magnesium chloride hexahydrate (MgCl₂0.6 H₂O), 0.189 g potassium chloride (KCl), 3.015 g potassium acetate (CH₃COOK), 0.772 g tripotassium phosphate trihydrate (K₃PO₄0.3 H₂O), and 0.1 mL phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) (85%), providing a physiochemically relevant ionic environment. Except for the application of remineralization agents and microhardness measurements, the samples were incubated in artificial saliva at 37 °C (Nüve EN 055 Incubator, Turkey).

Remeasurement of microhardness and scanning electron microscopy analysis

Microhardness measurements were repeated at the end of the first week (T1) and at the end of the first month (T2) using the same parameters. At the conclusion of the one-month period, the samples were prepared for qualitative surface analysis using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). One sample was randomly selected from each experimental group (Groups 1, 2, and 3) for examination. Additionally, one untreated porcine tooth from the reserve samples (not included in any experimental group) was used solely as a reference for baseline SEM morphology; this sample was not subjected to microhardness testing. Reserve samples, including the SEM reference, were stored in distilled water at room temperature throughout the study period.

Prior to imaging, all samples were dried at room temperature for 24 h and sputter-coated with a gold–palladium layer. The samples were then examined under a scanning electron microscope (Apreo S, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Images were captured at magnifications of 1.000×, 5.000×, 10.000×, and 25.000×, using an accelerating voltage of 10 kV and an average working distance of 21 mm. The resulting micrographs were used to document and compare the topographical characteristics and surface morphology of the enamel following the different remineralization treatments relative to the untreated control. Statistical analysis

In this study, descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum) of the data were presented. The normality assumption was checked using the Shapiro–Wilk test. To investigate differences between dependent and independent groups, along with their interaction effects, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was performed when the normality assumption was satisfied. Post hoc Bonferroni tests were used to identify the group or groups causing the difference. All analyses were performed using IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) for Windows, version 27 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The significance level was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Representative SEM images are presented in Fig. 4. After group allocation, one hypomineralized tooth that was not included in any experimental group and had been kept as a reserve sample was used as the control SEM sample to represent the baseline surface morphology. In this sample, SEM images revealed a heterogeneous, granular, and microporous surface morphology. Microcrack-like lines, irregular depressions, and small voids were observed in several areas. This appearance was consistent with the immature hypomineralized enamel structure of newly erupted porcine needle teeth, which is characterized by lower mineral content and higher protein content.

In Group 1 (NaF varnish), the surface retained its heterogeneous and irregular character after 1 month of remineralization. At 5.000× magnification, widespread micropits and small voids were evident. At higher magnifications, irregular covering or deposit-like accumulations, together with crack-like boundaries and microvoids, were observed on the enamel surface. Overall, this group exhibited an irregular surface morphology associated with mineral deposit accumulation.

In Group 2 (NaF + CPP-ACP varnish), the enamel surface also appeared markedly heterogeneous and porous. At 5.000× magnification, irregular depressions and voids were prominent, whereas at 10.000× and 25.000× magnifications, more evident lamellar or plaque-like structures, irregularly shaped pores, and partially covering surface accumulations were observed.

In Group 3 (P11-4), an irregular surface morphology associated with mineral deposit accumulation was also observed; however, the covering deposits and crack-like lines appeared less scattered than in the other treatment groups. At 5.000× magnification, the surface showed a more continuous and integrated appearance, while at 10.000× and 25.000× magnifications, more compact merged areas and a more organized surface topography were evident. Nevertheless, localized voids and surface irregularities were still present.

Overall, SEM examination showed irregular surface morphologies associated with mineral deposit accumulation in all treatment groups following the application of remineralization agents. However, the P11-4 group exhibited less scattered covering deposits and crack-like lines, with a relatively more integrated surface morphology compared with the other treatment groups.

Discussion

The present study is the first to comparatively evaluate the effects of NaF varnish, NaF + CPP-ACP varnish, and the P11-4 on the microhardness of hypomineralized enamel using a novel porcine model. The primary finding is that while all three remineralization agents significantly increased enamel microhardness over one month, P11-4 demonstrated superior efficacy compared to both NaF + CPP-ACP and NaF varnish alone. This study also introduces porcine teeth as a biologically relevant and reproducible model for simulating MIH-affected enamel, addressing a critical limitation in the literature, namely the scarcity of suitable extracted MIH-affected teeth for in vitro research.

MIH represents a clinically significant enamel defect due to its compromised structural and mechanical properties. The reduced microhardness of MIH-affected enamel is a critical factor underlying its susceptibility to post-eruptive breakdown, hypersensitivity, and restorative failure [5]. The decreased microhardness of hypomineralized enamel not only predisposes the tooth to structural failure but also compromises the adhesion and longevity of restorative materials, thereby increasing the need for repeated clinical interventions [26].

Fluoride varnish remains the gold standard due to its well-established ability to promote fluorapatite formation and enhance enamel resistance to acid dissolution [27]. NaF + CPP-ACP varnish acts as a calcium-phosphate reservoir, stabilizing ions in an amorphous state and facilitating their incorporation into enamel [28]. P11-4 represents a biomimetic approach, designed to mimic enamel matrix proteins and induce de novo hydroxyapatite formation [29]. The finding that P11-4 outperformed the other agents aligns with its proposed mechanism of forming a three-dimensional scaffold that guides enamel-like crystal growth.

The present finding that all three agents improved microhardness is consistent with the established literature on remineralization. NaF varnish, our gold standard comparator, produced a steady increase in microhardness, likely due to the formation of fluorapatite and the inhibition of demineralization [30]. The NaF + CPP-ACP group performed better than fluoride alone, which aligns with studies showing that the addition of a calcium and phosphate reservoir can enhance the remineralization potential beyond that of fluoride alone, particularly in porous enamel lesions [31, 32].

The most notable finding is the superior performance of P11-4. At the T2 time point, the P11-4 group demonstrated a mean microhardness of 323.65 VHN, which was significantly higher than both the NaF + CPP-ACP (294.59 VHN) and NaF (279.01 VHN) groups. This finding is clinically significant, as it suggests that P11-4 is not merely a source of ions but actively promotes deeper, more structural remineralization. This aligns with the biomimetic mechanism of P11-4, where it self-assembles into a 3D scaffold that mimics the enamel matrix, guiding the de novo formation of hydroxyapatite crystals within the hypomineralized lesion [29, 33, 34]. While a pilot study has touched upon its use in MIH [13], present study provides quantitative microhardness data supporting its superior efficacy in a controlled, longitudinal in vitro model.

The present findings are broadly consistent with the existing body of literature on MIH remineralization, yet they also address several critical gaps identified in recent systematic reviews [10, 35].The majority of clinical studies have relied on laser fluorescence (DIAGNOdent) as the primary outcome measure [13, 36–41] which, while useful for detecting mineral changes, provides indirect information and is influenced by surface moisture and debris. In contrast, the present study employed microhardness testing, a direct mechanical property measurement that is highly relevant to clinical performance, such as resistance to post-eruptive breakdown. Studies comparing fluoride varnish and CPP-ACP have yielded mixed results. While some clinical trials found no significant difference between these two agents [11, 40, 41], others reported that fluoride varnish with TCP was more effective for mild lesions [36]. The present in vitro data, showing that NaF + CPP-ACP led to higher microhardness values than NaF varnish alone at T2 (294.59 vs. 279.01 VHN), suggests that the addition of a calcium-phosphate reservoir provides a measurable benefit in a controlled, longitudinal setting, even if this difference may not always be detected clinically.

Crucially, the present study provides the first quantitative microhardness data on the effect of P11-4 on hypomineralized enamel. To date, only one pilot study has investigated P11-4 in MIH-affected teeth, reporting a significant decrease in mean fluorescence scores after one month, indicating remineralization [13]. The findings of the present study not only corroborate this pilot observation but substantially extend it by: (i) providing objective microhardness values that quantify the magnitude of mechanical improvement, (ii) comparing P11-4 head-to-head against both NaF varnish and NaF + CPP-ACP within the same experimental model, and (iii) demonstrating that the superior effect of P11-4 is sustained and even increases over the one-month period (T1 to T2 microhardness gain of 44.9 VHN). Furthermore, while Amaechi et al. [42]demonstrated that acid etching pretreatment enhances the remineralization effect of hydroxyapatite toothpaste on MIH lesions, the present study confirms that a more complex pretreatment protocol (NaOCl deproteinization followed by phosphoric acid etching) is highly effective when combined with P11-4, likely by removing the protein barrier and creating a receptive scaffold for guided mineralization. Therefore, the present study bridges a critical gap between a single pilot report [13] and the need for rigorous, quantitative, and comparative evidence data on biomimetic remineralization strategies for severe enamel hypomineralization.

The time-dependent increase in microhardness in all groups (T0→T1→T2) indicates that the hypomineralized porcine enamel remains responsive to remineralization therapies. However, the magnitude of increase was dramatically different. Between T1 and T2, the NaF group increased by only 9.5 VHN, and the NaF + CPP-ACP group by 13.2 VHN. In contrast, the P11-4 group increased by 44.9 VHN during the same period.

We propose that this difference is due to the distinct mechanisms of action: NaF and NaF + CPP-ACP primarily facilitate surface-driven remineralization via ion exchange and precipitation, resulting in the occlusion of superficial porosities. This explains the initial increase in hardness (T0→T1), but the effect plateaus as the superficial pores become occluded.

P11-4, however, is designed to penetrate deeper into the protein-rich, porous hypomineralized enamel. The required pre-treatment with NaOCl (to degrade intrinsic proteins) and phosphoric acid (to etch and open pores) is critical. Once inside, P11-4 self-assembles into a fibrillar scaffold that mimics the natural enamel matrix, actively recruiting calcium and phosphate ions from the surrounding artificial saliva to form new hydroxyapatite within the body of the lesion, not just on the surface [34, 43]. This “biomimetic regeneration” would explain the sustained and significantly greater increase in microhardness observed at T2.

The SEM findings provided qualitative insight into the surface changes induced by the tested remineralization agents in this hypomineralized enamel model. The untreated control sample exhibited a heterogeneous, granular, and microporous morphology, which is consistent with the immature and protein-rich structure of newly erupted porcine needle teeth and supports their use as a model for enamel hypomineralization. Following treatment, all experimental groups showed irregular surface morphologies associated with deposit-like mineral accumulations. This suggests that the surface changes observed after remineralization were not limited to simple smoothing of the enamel surface but rather involved the formation of covering deposits superimposed on the underlying porous and hypomineralized substrate. Among the tested agents, the P11-4 group exhibited less scattered covering deposits and crack-like lines, together with a relatively more integrated and compact surface morphology than the varnish-treated groups. These observations are in line with the higher microhardness values recorded in the P11-4 group at 1 month and may indicate a more organized surface restructuring pattern. Consistent with this interpretation, SEM-related reports on P11-4 suggest that post-application enamel surfaces are not necessarily completely smooth but may instead show partial surface coverage and fibrillar or globular surface accumulations reflecting peptide self-assembly and mineral deposition [24, 44, 45]. In contrast, the NaF and NaF + CPP-ACP varnish groups continued to show more scattered deposits, persistent porosity, and greater surface irregularity. Nevertheless, these SEM findings should be interpreted with caution, as the analysis was based on representative samples and therefore reflects qualitative surface characteristics rather than quantitative ultrastructural differences.

A major strength of this study is the use of a porcine enamel model. Unlike MIH in humans, which represents a pathological developmental defect, hypomineralization in porcine enamel during early post-eruptive stages is a physiological condition. As noted in the introduction, obtaining human MIH-affected teeth with sufficient structural integrity for in vitro testing is exceptionally challenging. The use of stillborn piglet teeth, which naturally erupt with a protein-rich, hypomineralized, porous enamel, provides a biologically relevant and reproducible alternative. Therefore, using teeth from stillborn piglets allows the capture of enamel at a stage that closely mimics severe MIH, particularly before post-eruptive breakdown occurs. This is consistent with Würzburg MIH Treatment Need Index (MIH-TNI) level 3, where severe opacity is present without structural loss, and remineralization strategies are clinically indicated [4]. The stereomicroscopic appearance of these porcine teeth, including the dark brown demarcated zones, closely mimicked severe human MIH. This model allows for standardized sample selection and controls experimental conditions, overcoming the heterogeneity that plagues clinical MIH research. Therefore, present study validates the porcine enamel model as a reliable and practical platform for screening remineralization agents for MIH.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this is an in vitro study. The artificial saliva environment, while standardized, cannot fully replicate the complex oral environment, including the pellicle layer, fluctuating pH, oral microbiome, and mechanical forces from brushing and mastication. Second, the follow-up period was limited to one month. While P11-4 showed superior short-term results, the long-term stability of the regenerated mineral is unknown. Third, microhardness, while a key mechanical property, does not fully capture the clinical performance of a material, such as its ability to reduce hypersensitivity or prevent post-eruptive breakdown. Finally, the sample size, while statistically powered, was relatively small (n = 6 per group), and only one sample per group was examined by SEM, which provides qualitative rather than quantitative surface data.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, it can be concluded that all tested remineralization agents’ NaF varnish, NaF + CPP-ACP varnish, and P11-4 significantly improve the microhardness of hypomineralized enamel in a porcine model. However, the P11-4 demonstrated superior efficacy compared to both NaF + CPP-ACP and NaF varnish after one month. Furthermore, the porcine enamel model appears to be a valid and reliable surrogate for severe human MIH-affected enamel, facilitating future research in this area.
