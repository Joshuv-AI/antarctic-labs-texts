Device Interrogation Unmasking Flecainide-Induced Wide Complex Tachycardia
Authors: Matthew W Segar, Kaleb D Lambeth, Addison J Hastedt, Joanna E Molina-Razavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWide-complex tachycardia poses diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, particularly when class IC antiarrhythmic toxicity mimics ventricular tachycardia (VT).Case SummaryAn 85-year-old woman with a dual-chamber pacemaker, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation on flecainide, and chronic kidney disease presented with dyspnea and a wide-complex tachycardia initially diagnosed as VT. Intravenous amiodarone was administered. Initial device interrogation revealed ventricular sensing at 496 ms cycle length without atrial tracking. Ventricular overdrive pacing demonstrated that the atrial cycle length did not accelerate to the pacing rate, confirming an independent atrial tachycardia at 551 ms and excluding atrioventricular reentrant tachycardia and VT with retrograde conduction. The Ventricular Intrinsic Preference algorithm subsequently demonstrated intact atrioventricular conduction, proving the wide QRS was aberrancy. Recognition of potential amiodarone-flecainide interaction prompted drug cessation and sodium bicarbonate therapy, leading to clinical recovery.DiscussionThis case demonstrates how pacemaker diagnostics can differentiate flecainide-induced aberrancy from VT and highlights the important drug-drug interaction between amiodarone and flecainide via CYP450 inhibition.Take-Home MessageIn patients with pacemakers presenting with wide-complex tachycardia, device interrogation and knowledge of programmed parameters are helpful to differentiate supraventricular tachycardia with aberrancy from VT.Key words: cardiac pacemaker, electrocardiogram, supraventricular arrhythmias

History of Presentation

An 85-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with progressive dyspnea and lower-extremity edema over 1 week. On examination, she was mildly dyspneic with a respiratory rate of 22 breaths/min, blood pressure of 118/72 mm Hg, and heart rate of 125 beats/min. Bilateral pitting edema extended to the mid-shins. Jugular venous pressure was elevated. A 12-lead electrocardiogram demonstrated a wide-complex tachycardia at approximately 125 beats/min with left bundle branch block morphology, left-axis deviation, and negative precordial concordance (Figure 1).

Past Medical History

The patient's history was notable for sick sinus syndrome with subsequent dual-chamber pacemaker implantation, preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (55%), paroxysmal atrial fibrillation with subsequent electrical cardioversion (on apixaban), mild diastolic dysfunction, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage III (baseline creatinine: 1.4 mg/dL, estimated glomerular filtration rate: 42 mL/min/1.73 m2). Her weight was approximately 60 kg. Medications included metoprolol succinate 50 mg daily and flecainide 150 mg twice daily.

Differential Diagnosis

The electrocardiographic pattern appropriately raised concern for ventricular tachycardia (VT). Alternative diagnoses included: 1) supraventricular tachycardia with aberrancy; 2) antiarrhythmic drug toxicity; 3) pacemaker-mediated tachycardia; and 4) electrolyte and/or metabolic derangements. The initial tracing, which was concerning for VT, lacked definitive signs such as atrioventricular (AV) dissociation, fusion beats, or capture beats. The QRS morphology closely resembled the patient's baseline paced rhythm (Figure 2), albeit with marked prolongation (174 ms vs ∼130 ms at baseline).

Investigations

Laboratory studies revealed stable CKD (creatinine: 1.4 mg/dL, estimated glomerular filtration rate: 42 mL/min/1.73 m2) and no electrolyte disturbances (potassium: 4.2 mEq/L, magnesium: 2.1 mg/dL). B-type natriuretic peptide was elevated at 580 pg/mL. Given the patient's pacemaker history, the electrophysiology service was consulted. Device interrogation demonstrated an Abbott dual-chamber pacemaker programmed DDD with the following parameters: base rate, 60 beats/min; Max Track Rate, 110 beats/min (cycle length: 545 ms); Paced AV Delay, 200 ms; Sensed AV Delay, 180 ms; Rate Responsive PVARP (postventricular atrial refractory period)/V Ref, high; and Ventricular Intrinsic Preference (VIP) enabled.

On initial interrogation (Figure 3), the device revealed ventricular sensing (VS-VS) at a cycle length of 496 ms (121 beats/min). No atrial tracking (atrial sensed [AS]) markers were present on the marker channel despite visible atrial electrograms on the intracardiac tracing. The ventricular rate exceeded the upper tracking rate and was programmed Max Track Rate of 110 beats/min. This absence of AS markers is explained by the device's Rate Responsive PVARP behavior: with Rate Responsive PVARP/V Ref set to “high,” this Abbott pacemaker automatically extends the PVARP to approximately (Max Track Rate interval − Sensed AV Delay) = (545 − 180) = 365 ms after each ventricular sensed event. For an atrial event to generate an AS marker, it must arrive after this 365 ms PVARP window and require an AV conduction time of <131 ms (ie, 496 − 365 ms). In the setting of severe flecainide toxicity with a QRS of 174 ms reflecting marked His-Purkinje slowing, an AV conduction time this short is physiologically implausible. All atrial events therefore fall within the extended PVARP, generating no AS markers on the marker channel. Furthermore, if the atrial and ventricular rhythms were truly dissociated at different cycle lengths (∼496 ms vs ∼551 ms), the phase relationship between them would drift ∼55 ms per beat. Across 14 consecutive beats, atrial events would inevitably emerge outside the PVARP window and generate visible AS markers on some cycles. The complete, cycle-to-cycle absence of any AS marker is therefore consistent with 1:1 AS:VS conduction above the Max Track Rate rather than AV dissociation.

To further characterize the tachycardia mechanism, ventricular overdrive pacing at 496 ms cycle length was performed (Figure 4). The atrial cycle length did not accelerate to the ventricular pacing rate and remained stable at 551 ms throughout pacing, confirming that the atrium was not captured. This demonstrates AV dissociation during ventricular pacing and confirms an independent atrial tachycardia. This response effectively excludes atrioventricular reentrant tachycardia (AVRT), in which the atrium is an obligate circuit limb and would be captured retrogradely to the pacing rate, and excludes VT with 1:1 retrograde ventriculoatrial conduction, in which ventricular pacing would similarly accelerate atrial activation to the pacing rate. After pacing cessation, the atrial rhythm continued undisturbed at 551 ms and the first ventricular event occurred at 879 ms (AS-VP). The device immediately resumed 1:1 AV tracking at the atrial tachycardia cycle length with an AV delay of 176 ms. This immediate AV resynchronization at the stable atrial rate confirms atrial-driven ventricular activation and argues against VT, where overdrive pacing typically results in either persistent VT or variable AV relationships upon resumption.

Further observation (Figure 5) demonstrated the VIP algorithm. Once tracking resumed, VIP extended the AV delay to search for intrinsic conduction. The rhythm transitioned from ventricular paced (VP) to ventricular sensed (VS) at cycle lengths of 551-563 ms, proving intact AV nodal conduction. The patient was conducting the atrial rhythm through her native conduction system. The widened QRS (174 ms vs baseline ∼130 ms) therefore represented aberrancy from flecainide toxicity, not VT.

Management

In the emergency department, the rhythm was initially interpreted as VT and intravenous amiodarone was administered. Although appropriate under diagnostic uncertainty, this decision inadvertently risked exacerbating flecainide toxicity. Amiodarone inhibits multiple cytochrome P450 isoenzymes, most notably CYP2D6 and CYP1A2, the primary metabolic pathways for flecainide.1 Coadministration can double flecainide plasma concentrations within days, prolonging its half-life (normally 12-27 hours) and magnifying QRS prolongation.2 Amiodarone also independently prolongs the QT interval, while flecainide primarily widens the QRS via sodium-channel blockade, creating additive proarrhythmic risk through distinct electrophysiologic mechanisms.

Recognition of the pharmacologic interaction prompted immediate cessation of both antiarrhythmics and initiation of targeted therapy for flecainide toxicity. Intravenous sodium bicarbonate was administered after multidisciplinary discussion with the advanced heart failure team, balancing the risk of volume overload against the benefit of sodium loading and alkalinization. Sodium bicarbonate reverses flecainide toxicity through serum alkalinization, which reduces sodium-channel binding affinity, and by sodium loading that competitively overcomes channel blockade. Despite the patient's volume sensitivity, cautious dosing (50 mEq boluses titrated to QRS narrowing) successfully narrowed the QRS and stabilized conduction.

Outcome and Follow-Up

This intervention, combined with supportive rate control using metoprolol, led to gradual resolution of the arrhythmia as flecainide levels declined. Over subsequent days, the QRS narrowed progressively and the rhythm reverted to sinus with normal ventricular pacing. The patient's dyspnea and edema improved with diuresis. After withdrawal of flecainide and recovery from toxicity, she remained hemodynamically stable.

At discharge, flecainide was permanently discontinued given the toxicity event and her renal impairment. Rate control with metoprolol was continued, and anticoagulation with apixaban was maintained for stroke prevention. At the 1-month follow-up, she remained in sinus rhythm with appropriate pacemaker function and no recurrence of symptoms.

Discussion

Flecainide is a potent class IC sodium-channel blocker that binds preferentially to open sodium channels and dissociates slowly during diastole, producing use-dependent QRS widening. At normal heart rates, conduction delay is modest. During tachycardia, incomplete channel recovery exaggerates depolarization delay through the His-Purkinje system. In this patient, chronic flecainide exposure, low body weight, age-related reduction in clearance, and CKD led to drug accumulation. Although flecainide is primarily hepatically metabolized, approximately 30% is renally cleared.3 Thus, even moderate CKD significantly prolongs elimination. When the atrial tachycardia accelerated, His-Purkinje conduction further slowed, producing a markedly widened QRS and a left bundle branch block–like configuration.

While absolute exclusion of VT requires invasive electrophysiology study, multiple findings make VT exceedingly unlikely: 1) AV dissociation confirmed by independent atrial rate during ventricular overdrive pacing, excluding AVRT and VT with 1:1 retrograde conduction; 2) VIP algorithm demonstration of intact AV conduction, proving aberrancy; 3) rate-dependent QRS widening (34% increase) characteristic of flecainide toxicity; 4) sodium bicarbonate response with progressive QRS narrowing; and 5) QRS morphology similar to baseline paced rhythm. Although bundle branch reentry VT can occur with preserved left ventricular function, the immediate stable 1:1 AV relationship and clinical response argue against this mechanism.4

This case also illustrates an important device-specific diagnostic pitfall. In Abbott DDD pacemakers programmed with a Rate Responsive PVARP/V Ref of “high,” the PVARP is automatically extended to approximately (Max Track Rate interval – Sensed AV Delay) to prevent tracking above the Max Track Rate. When an atrial tachycardia exceeds the Max Track Rate and intrinsic AV conduction is markedly prolonged (as occurs with flecainide toxicity), atrial events may fall entirely within this extended PVARP window and generate no AS markers on the marker channel—mimicking AV dissociation. Awareness of programmed parameters, particularly the Max Track Rate and Rate Responsive PVARP setting, is therefore essential for correct interpretation of marker channel data during device interrogation in this clinical context.

Flecainide toxicity has been shown to mimic VT both electrocardiographically and hemodynamically.5, 6, 7 Severe flecainide toxicity is characterized by marked QRS prolongation (>160 ms), while QTc prolongation >500 ms may occur when toxicity is compounded by electrolyte abnormalities or concomitant QT-prolonging agents. In patients with pacemakers, flecainide can also raise capture thresholds or cause noncapture, although this was not observed here. Current guidelines recommend against flecainide use in patients with structural heart disease or reduced ejection fraction because of increased mortality observed in the CAST trial.8 In our patient, her advanced age, low body weight, and renal impairment increased her susceptibility to toxicity. The 2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS guidelines for the diagnosis and management of atrial fibrillation emphasize the importance of dose adjustment in renal impairment and vigilance for proarrhythmic effects.9

Conclusions

This case illustrates the diagnostic complexity of wide-complex tachycardia in patients on class IC antiarrhythmics. Although definitive exclusion of VT requires invasive electrophysiology study, the convergence of the following device-based findings: 1) AV dissociation confirmed by independent atrial rate during ventricular overdrive pacing, excluding AVRT and retrograde VT; 2) VIP algorithm demonstration of intact AV conduction; 3) rate-dependent QRS widening consistent with flecainide toxicity; and 4) dramatic response to sodium bicarbonate—provided sufficient evidence to guide appropriate acute management. Device interrogation with knowledge of device-specific upper-rate behavior was important for differentiating supraventricular tachycardia with aberrancy from VT in real-time and avoiding potentially harmful escalation of antiarrhythmic therapy. The potentially dangerous pharmacokinetic interaction between amiodarone and flecainide underscores the need for awareness of drug-drug interactions when managing arrhythmias.
