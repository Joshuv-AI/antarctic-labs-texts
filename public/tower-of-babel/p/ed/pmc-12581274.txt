Background

Accurate and timely estimation of the effective reproduction number (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document}) is essential for understanding epidemic dynamics and guiding public health interventions. As a measure of the average number of secondary cases generated by an infectious individual at a given point in time, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} serves as a dynamic indicator of transmission intensity. It has been widely used to monitor the trajectory of infectious disease outbreaks, evaluate the impact of control measures, and inform decisions about the timing and scale of interventions [1, 2].

Several methodologies [3–6] have been developed to estimate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} from surveillance data. Among the most widely used are the Wallinga and Teunis (WT) method and the approach proposed by Cori, both of which infer transmission potential based on case incidence data and assumptions about the serial interval distribution. These methods have gained popularity due to their ease of implementation, minimal data requirements, and ability to produce near real-time estimates. As a result, they have become standard tools in public health surveillance, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic. While these methods have demonstrated value in diverse settings, they typically focus on estimating transmission dynamics within individual geographic units, based on the assumption that most new infections observed in a region can be attributed to transmission events occurring locally. In practice, this means that regional case counts are interpreted as the result of internal chains of transmission, without explicitly accounting for the possibility that some infections may have originated from outside the region through population movement.

This assumption has proven operationally effective in many applications. However, in contexts where inter-regional mobility plays a significant role in shaping epidemic patterns, not incorporating such movement into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} estimation may limit the accuracy of regional risk assessments. For example, areas with low observed incidence but substantial inter-regional movement from higher-incidence regions may face elevated transmission risks that are not immediately evident from local case data alone. Conversely, regions that appear to experience rapid increases in cases may in fact be recipients of externally seeded infections rather than sites where outbreaks are primarily driven by local transmission.

The COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted the importance of considering such dynamics. In countries like South Korea, where the early phases of the outbreak were geographically concentrated in specific areas, subsequent spread to other regions was closely linked to population mobility. Events such as the large-scale outbreak in Daegu in February 2020 were followed by increased transmission in surrounding and distant provinces, even in the absence of major local superspreading events [7–9]. These patterns suggest that cross-regional transmission pathways—mediated by human movement—played a central role in shaping the nationwide trajectory of the epidemic. Recognizing this, several recent studies have proposed extensions to traditional \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} estimation frameworks to better account for spatial structure [10]. For instance, White et al. [11] introduced a method that incorporates region similarity matrices to reflect potential cross-region transmission. Others, such as Trevisin et al. [12] and Creswell et al. [13], have taken steps toward integrating mobility data into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} estimation by using mobility flows to adjust infection likelihoods. These contributions have laid important groundwork for incorporating spatial relationships into the analysis of transmission potential. Recent studies also have emphasized the importance of incorporating spatial heterogeneity and human mobility into transmission models [14–18].

Nonetheless, there remains a need for approaches that more fully integrate empirical movement data into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} estimation in a transparent and scalable manner. Early approaches, such as that of Wallinga and Teunis (WT) [3], provided a framework to infer \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} solely from intra-regional case incidence data and serial interval distribution, without explicit assumptions regarding the structure of inter-regional transmission. Their method infers potential transmission links based only on local case data and therefore does not fully account for the possibility that new infections may be seeded by cases imported from other regions. That is, the WT method estimates the likelihood of infection between cases based only on temporal proximity, without considering spatial separation or regional mobility patterns. Recognizing this limitation, White et al. [11] extended the WT method by introducing spatial similarity matrices to capture heterogeneity across different regions, adjusting the probability of infection by incorporating a measure of spatial proximity. However, such similarity measures are typically averaged across the entire study period and thus may not adequately capture temporal changes in spatial relationships, which can significantly influence transmission dynamics.

To address these challenges, this study proposes a mobility-adjusted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} estimation framework that incorporates region-to-region movement patterns directly into the transmission inference process. Using anonymized telecommunication data collected during the first wave of COVID-19 in South Korea, we construct detailed mobility matrices that quantify daily population flows between all pairs of provinces. These matrices are then embedded into an extended \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} formulation that weights the probability of infection between cases not only by their temporal relationship, but also by the observed volume of inter-regional movement. This allows us to more accurately represent the role of mobile infectious individuals in spreading disease across administrative boundaries.

Our approach estimates, for each region and time point, the expected number of secondary infections attributable to individuals who became infectious in that region, regardless of where the subsequent infections occurred. In doing so, we move beyond a purely region-bound interpretation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} to one that also captures the potential for transmission beyond regional boundaries. This contribution is twofold. First, we propose a framework that integrates real-world mobility into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} estimation, thereby extending the spatial scope of traditional methods while preserving their interpretability. Second, we demonstrate its application in a setting where inter-regional connectivity clearly influenced epidemic dynamics, showing that mobility-adjusted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} can complement conventional approaches. In particular, it provides more balanced estimates in low-incidence or highly connected regions, where conventional methods often yield inflated or unstable values, thereby clarifying situations in which external connectivity may have played a role.

Methods

Data description

South Korea offers a compelling case for examining the spatial heterogeneity of COVID-19 transmission dynamics. Despite the country’s unified and centralized pandemic response, significant regional differences in epidemic progression were observed during the early phase of the outbreak [7–9]. These differences arose from multiple factors, including variations in regional characteristics, population mobility, and localized superspreading events from mass gatherings [19–21]. Furthermore, South Korea’s extensive contact tracing infrastructure and high-resolution epidemiological data make it an optimal setting for evaluating the role of spatial factors in disease spread, prior to the confounding effects of mass vaccination and population immunity [22].

For this reason, we collected confirmed case data during the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic (February 11 to April 30, 2020) for the 17 provinces of South Korea from the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) [23]. The KDCA conducted comprehensive and systematic epidemiological investigations, ensuring high-quality data collection. This rigorous method facilitates robust analytical procedures, enabling the identification of significant patterns in the dynamics of disease transmission. The first wave of COVID-19 in South Korea originated from a superspreading event associated with a mass gathering in Daegu on February 18, resulting in regional differences in transmission dynamics and outbreak sizes. Mobility-adjusted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} with sliding window

In this study, we propose an estimation methodology that incorporates a sliding window approach and region-specific mobility patterns into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} estimation to capture inter-regional transmission risk. Our approach simultaneously addresses two key aspects: temporal localization and mobility-adjusted similarity. Temporal localization is achieved by restricting potential infectors to a sliding window defined by the plausible serial interval range, thus preventing contamination from temporally distant cases. Mobility-adjusted similarity refines the infection likelihood by weighting potential infectors according to empirical inter-regional mobility data, thereby reflecting realistic contact opportunities between regions. This approach allows us to account for the possibility that infections may not be confined to the region in which an infector resides, but may also occur in other regions where individuals from the origin region travel. To incorporate this spatial interaction, we quantified the relative weight of inter-regional movement using population flow data derived from telecommunication records (for an example of the data, see Table 1 in Appendix 1). An example of the resulting mobility matrix, shown in Fig. 4 in Appendix 1, is a daily movement matrix representing the relative inter-provincial mobility pattern. Each element in the matrix indicates the relative weight of movement from the origin region (rows) to the destination region (columns), adjusted by the total number of movements originating from that region. Therefore, the matrix captures the proportion of individuals who are more likely to travel to other provinces rather than stay within their own, effectively quantifying inter-regional movement relative to within-region retention.

To formalize the integration of temporal and spatial factors, we define the pairwise infection probability between cases. Specifically, consider an individual who developed symptoms at time a in region i. The probability that this individual was infected by another case with symptom onset at time b in region j is determined by combining two components: (i) the temporal proximity between onset times, represented by the serial interval distribution, and (ii) the relative weight of inter-regional movement between the two regions, captured by the mobility matrix. Formally, this probability is given by:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} p(a_i,b_j) = \frac{w(a-b) \cdot d^{t}(i,j)}{\sum _{k=1}^{r} \sum _{t=a-s}^{a-1} w(a-t) \cdot d^{t}(i,k) \cdot n(t,k)}, \quad \text {for } b \in [a-s,a-1] \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where r is the total number of regions, s is the window size (i.e., maximum serial interval), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w(\tau )$$\end{document} denotes the value of probability distribution function corresponding to a serial interval equals \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d^{t}(i,j)$$\end{document} is the weighted mobility value between the origin region j and the destination region i at time t (the detailed calculation procedure is provided in Appendix 1), and n(t, k) is the number of new cases at time t in region k.

In this study, we set the window size to 7 days, which was determined based on the sensitivity analysis presented in Appendix 2, and assumed that the serial interval follows a Weibull distribution with a mean of 3.1 and a standard deviation of 3.9, referencing a previous study [24] that estimated the serial interval during the first COVID-19 wave in South Korea. To incorporate this continuous distribution into our discrete-time framework, we discretized the serial interval distribution into a probability mass function by computing the probability that the serial interval (SI) falls within each integer-day interval:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} w(\tau ) = P(\tau -1 < SI \le \tau ) = F(\tau ) - F(\tau -1) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F(\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the cumulative distribution function of the Weibull distribution. This discretization ensures that the weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w(\tau )$$\end{document} sum to one across integer days and can be directly applied within the transmission probability formulation in Eq. (1).

Having defined the pairwise infection probability, we next extend this formulation to the effective reproduction number. Conceptually, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} represents the expected number of secondary infections generated by an infectious individual at a given time. In our framework, this is obtained by summing over all potential future cases across regions, each weighted by its probability of being linked to the focal case. Formally, the effective reproduction number at time b in region j, denoted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{b_j}$$\end{document}, is estimated as:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} R_{b_j} = \sum _{t=b+1}^{b+s} \sum _{k=1}^{r} p(t_k, b_j) \cdot n(t,k) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where the first summation extends over all possible infection times within the sliding window, and the second summation extends over all possible destination regions. This formulation captures both temporal proximity and inter-regional travel, thereby providing a more realistic representation of transmission intensity under spatial connectivity.

Results

In Seoul and Gyeonggi—the two epicenters in terms of either mobility connectivity or case intensity—the two methods produced broadly similar trajectories throughout most of the study period. This gap was most evident in mid- to late February, shortly after the Daegu superspreading event, suggesting that our approach may capture latent transmission pressure associated with connectivity-mediated exposure before it becomes evident in local incidence data. This implies that Daegu was not only experiencing intense internal transmission but was also exporting infections to other regions—a dynamic consistent with our model’s structure, in which mobility-adjusted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} accounts for outward transmission potential.

By contrast, in regions with moderate connectivity, such as Gyeongbuk and Busan, both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} estimates showed similar overall trends, with the WT \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} occasionally slightly higher. This resemblance likely reflects their geographic proximity to Daegu, which resulted in rapid and recurrent exposure to imported infections. In such cases, early imported cases may have seeded local outbreaks quickly enough that WT-based inference approximated the mobility-weighted dynamics. Thus, the added value of mobility adjustment was less pronounced in these regions.

More substantial differences were observed in most of the remaining 12 provinces, including Jeonbuk, Jeonnam, Chungbuk, and Ulsan. This pattern suggests that the WT method may overestimate internal transmission pressure, particularly in low-incidence regions where sporadic imported cases or small clusters dominate the epidemic curve. For instance, as illustrated in Fig. 3, provinces such as Jeju, Jeonnam, and Sejong reported daily confirmed cases that rarely exceeded one or two. Nevertheless, the WT-based \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} displayed sharp spikes—sometimes above 2 or 3—reflecting the method’s sensitivity to stochastic fluctuations in sparse datasets. By contrast, the mobility-adjusted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} distributed infection attribution across connected regions and therefore produced smoother and more conservative trajectories, more consistent with the limited potential for sustained local transmission in these settings.

Taken together, Figs. 2 and 3 demonstrate that the mobility-adjusted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} does not uniformly outperform the WT method across all contexts. Instead, its advantage is context-dependent. The method provides added value in regions with very low incidence or high connectivity, where conventional approaches are prone to overestimating local transmission or failing to capture outflow-driven risk. In contrast, in high-incidence or geographically adjacent regions, both methods yield broadly consistent results. This nuance suggests that the proposed framework may be particularly useful for clarifying the role of cross-regional seeding and for stabilizing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} estimation under conditions of data sparsity, rather than serving as a universal replacement for existing approaches. Discussion

This study introduced a mobility-adjusted effective reproduction number (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document}) designed to explicitly incorporate inter-regional connectivity into epidemic risk assessment. Unlike the conventional Wallinga–Teunis (WT) method, which attributes all observed infections within a region to local transmission, our framework reallocates infection pressure based on observed mobility flows. Conceptually, the mobility-adjusted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} quantifies the expected number of secondary infections attributable to individuals who became infectious in a given region, whether those infections occur locally or externally. This broader interpretation enables assessment not only of local outbreak intensity but also of the outward transmission pressure that regions exert on others.

The comparative results with conventional method show that the advantages of this approach are not universal but context-dependent. Its added value is most evident in settings where either outflow-driven transmission risk is high or local incidence is extremely low. In such contexts, the mobility-adjusted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} stabilizes estimates that would otherwise be distorted by stochastic fluctuations or misattribution of sporadic imported cases. At the same time, it can highlight elevated risk in highly connected urban centers before local amplification is evident in reported case data. These properties suggest that the proposed framework should be regarded as complementary to existing methods rather than as a wholesale replacement.

From a public health perspective, the mobility-adjusted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} enhances the spatial interpretability of epidemic indicators. By linking transmission potential directly to empirical mobility patterns, it provides a tool for identifying regions that are vulnerable to external seeding despite low local incidence, as well as regions that may act as major exporters of infection. This capacity supports more geographically targeted intervention strategies, such as prioritizing surveillance in areas with high connectivity or allocating resources to regions with disproportionate outward transmission pressure. In this way, the measure extends epidemiological monitoring toward operational decision-making, offering complementary perspectives that are not readily captured by conventional \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} estimates.

Together, these findings demonstrate that the mobility-adjusted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} represents a conceptual and methodological advance that links population movement with transmission potential. By extending the conventional reproduction number beyond local boundaries, it provides a scalable and adaptable tool for understanding regional epidemic dynamics in an increasingly interconnected world. Its ability to integrate locally driven outbreaks and externally seeded transmission offers tangible value for both scientific inquiry and public health preparedness.

Despite the strengths of the proposed framework, several limitations should be acknowledged. Our study was conducted within a specific national context and during a particular epidemic wave, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The applicability of the framework will depend on the availability and quality of mobility and case data, as well as the epidemiological characteristics of the disease in question. In addition, other sources of uncertainty are likely to influence the estimates. For example, the serial interval distribution may vary across viral variants or intervention settings, and mobility matrices may change substantially across epidemic phases or shifts in population behavior. Furthermore, viral lineage evolution can alter transmissibility and serial interval characteristics, necessitating recalibration of the framework in future epidemic contexts [25]. Policy measures such as home quarantine and changes in recovery definitions may also affect case reporting and infection periods, thereby influencing the input data underlying \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} estimation [26]. Similarly, the introduction of novel vaccines can substantially modify population susceptibility and disease dynamics in ways not captured by the present model [27].

Additionally, the model implicitly assumes applicability to the general population and does not explicitly account for heterogeneity in subpopulations with underlying conditions, which may limit its precision for vulnerable groups [28]. Such variability and contextual dependencies could introduce additional layers of uncertainty into the estimates, and in future work we plan to explicitly incorporate these factors to more comprehensively evaluate the robustness of the proposed framework. While the framework already employs daily mobility matrices to capture temporal fluctuations, we also plan to refine the approach by conducting sub-interval analyses and more granular examinations of pairwise regional mobility dynamics. These efforts will provide clearer insights into the origins of imported infections and the role of shifting policy responses across epidemic waves, thereby strengthening both the utility and interpretability of mobility-adjusted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document}.

Conclusions

This study presents a spatially-informed framework for estimating the effective reproduction number (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document}) by incorporating real-world inter-regional mobility patterns. By redistributing infection pressure across regions based on observed movement flows, the proposed method accounts for the possibility of spatial seeding—where new cases in a region may be driven by connectivity-mediated exposure rather than local transmission. This mobility-adjusted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} offers a more context-aware interpretation of transmission dynamics, particularly when compared to conventional methods like the Wallinga–Teunis (WT) estimator, which is typically applied independently to each region, effectively treating them as isolated and therefore not capturing cross-regional infections.

Beyond its methodological contributions, the proposed framework offers practical value for epidemic preparedness and control. By explicitly linking inter-regional movement to transmission risk, it primarily identifies regions that act as hubs of exportation, thereby supporting the design of geographically targeted interventions and the prioritization of surveillance resources. While our current study emphasizes outward transmission dynamics, the framework can be readily extended to characterize importation hubs as well, provided that additional analyses on inflow or bidirectional flow are conducted. This capability is especially important in settings where public health capacity is limited and efficient allocation is essential.

Another strength of the framework lies in its structural flexibility. Although our study focused on telecommunication-based mobility data within a single national context, the approach is not restricted to such inputs. In principle, any dataset that quantifies inter-regional connectivity or similarity—such as transportation flows, commuting surveys, or spatial interaction indices—can be incorporated to reallocate infection pressure across regions. This means that, with appropriate data, the framework can be adapted to diverse epidemiological contexts, disease systems, and geographic settings. Such adaptability ensures that the framework serves not only as a refinement of conventional \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} metrics, but also as a foundation for more resilient approaches to epidemic monitoring in an increasingly interconnected world.

Taken together, these features demonstrate that the mobility-adjusted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} refines conventional reproduction number metrics while extending their scope to incorporate inter-regional connectivity, thereby offering a practical and scalable tool to inform epidemic monitoring and public health strategies in an interconnected world.
