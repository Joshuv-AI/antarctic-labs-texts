Parent-child communication on sexual and reproductive health: a qualitative study exploring the perspectives of parents, adolescents and young adults in the region of Kaolack, Senegal
Authors: Joséphine Wouango, Thérèse Delvaux, Kristien Michielsen, Cheikh Moussa Camara, Thierno Dieng, Ramatoulaye Diallo, Khadidja Diawara, Ernesto Papa, François Sarramagnan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescents and young adults in Senegal face several challenges related to their sexual and reproductive health (SRH). International research shows that family communication on SRH can be a protective factor. However, cultural norms and taboos often hinder communication between parents and young people on SRH. This study explores and assesses the state of communication between parents, adolescents and young adults about SRH in the Kaolack region of Senegal, intending to improve their relationships on the issue.MethodsWe set up a qualitative study consisting of 26 focus group discussions with adolescents and young adults aged 10–24, six focus group discussions with parents, 14 in-depth interviews with adolescents and young adults using SRH services and 17 key informant interviews with SRH service providers and local leaders. All transcripts were analysed using Nvivo 12.ResultsThe state of parent-child communication about SRH varies from effective but selective communication (on some SRH topics) to no communication at all. Most parents do not feel confident or comfortable discussing certain topics with their children, partly because of what they describe as a culture of embarrassment and taboos regarding SRH. Communication is generally gendered, mostly between daughters and mothers. Communication approaches between parents and adolescents range from advice to commands, and communication on sexuality and contraception is almost non-existent.The results show that parents recognise the need to communicate with their children on various adolescent SRH topics. However, they are ill-equipped to do so. Adolescents and young adults also desire to discuss these issues with their parents, whom they consider to be the “closest to their children”.ConclusionEffective communication between parents and their children is necessary, yet it currently remains a challenge for many parents in the studied sites. Our findings complement the literature on adolescent SRH in West Africa, which shows that the parent-child relationship is of paramount importance. The study results informed the design and implementation of an intervention focused on training parents to improve their knowledge, skills and confidence to initiate an open and trustful dialogue with their children and better engage in the SRH of adolescents and young adults.Keywords: Adolescents and young adults, Parents, Communication, Sexual and reproductive health, Qualitative research

Introduction

Adolescence in particular is seen as a crucial transitional period that entails challenges. In Senegal, this is compounded by several sexual and reproductive health (SRH) decisions and situations that adolescents (aged 10 to 19 years) and young adults (aged 20 to 24 years) often, including adolescent pregnancies, sexually transmitted diseases and sexual and gender-based violence. The Evidence to Action evaluation conducted in six regions of Senegal in 2019 revealed that young people in the region of Kaolack had limited knowledge of SRH issues or of how they can access SRH services for adolescents and young adults, which resulted in low utilisation of the SRH services [1]. The 2019 Demographic and Health Survey in Senegal indicated that the use of modern contraception remains low among adolescent girls aged 15–19 (1.9%) and young women aged 20–24 (6.6%). Among adolescents aged 15–19, 4.9% of girls and 1.9% of boys have had sexual intercourse before the age of 15 [2]. The Strategic Plan for the sexual and reproductive health of young people in Senegal for 2014–2018 objectives were to contribute to the health and well-being of young people 10 to 24, to promote their SRH and to increase the utilisation rate of SRH services by young people by 80% by 2018 [3].

Various factors contribute to these low SRH outcomes. A key factor, identified in research, is the lack of effective communication between parents and their adolescent children on these issues [4, 5]. International research points to the family circle as a reliable and confidential source of information for adolescents and young adults, where they can receive SRH making-making support when based on accurate information tailored to their age and level of understanding is made available to them [6]. Furthermore, studies conducted in Ghana, Uganda and Nigeria have shown that adolescents prefer their parents as an SRH information source [7–9], that effective parent-adolescent communication has significant potential to reduce adolescent risky sexual behaviour [10] and that it is associated with positive SRH outcomes in general.

However, both parents and adolescents/young adults face difficulties in initiating effective communication about SRH, and it is more challenging in many African countries [5–13]. In the absence of support, listening and attention from parents, the family environment can become less conducive for adolescents, particularly due to the prevailing sociocultural barriers in sub-Saharan Africa [14, 15]. Other barriers, such as parents’ level of education, their profession, sensitivity to the topic and social and religious norms, further limit the ability of many African parents to play their role and to engage effectively in an open discussion with their children about their SRH. Studies illustrate parents in many African societies’ difficulties in discussing SRH issues, particularly sexuality, with their adolescent children [5–18, 18–20]. A recent systematic review of parent-adolescent communication on sexuality in the African context concludes that communication is characterised by threats, warnings and moralistic and religious views without providing essential information about sexuality [13].

Even though parent-adolescent communication is a protective fact, less than 2% of adolescent SRH interventions specifically address parent-adolescent communication [21]. Moreover, an in-depth analysis of the state of parent-adolescent SRH communication in Senegal is lacking, as are strategies and opportunities to foster or improve it.

This study adds to our understanding of the issue by exploring and assessing parent-adolescent communication in three departments of Senegal’s Koalack region. The study is part of a larger action research project looking at several components of the SRH of young people aged 10–24 years in three regions (including Koalack) in Senegal.

The study employed a qualitative methodology and used the socio-ecological model as a conceptual framework [22], which allows the analysis of a given issue or problem from a broad perspective, taking into account the different layers of influence in which it occurs. The holistic nature of this model helps understand the extent to which, and at what level, environments at the micro, meso, and macro levels influence human behaviour.

The findings of the study are intended to inform the development of a service model tailored to adolescents and young adults in the regions and through this to improve parents’ involvement in adolescent SRH.

Methods

Study design and setting

This qualitative case study included focus group discussions and in-depth interviews.

Study sites

The study took place in three departments of the Kaolack region (Kaolack, Nioro and Guinguinéo) where Belgian-Senegalese bilateral cooperation programmes are implemented. The department of Kaolack is an urban area that serves as a crossroads city offering greater opportunities and economic dynamism. The departments of Nioro and Guinguinéo, on the other hand, are semi-rural and offer few socio-economic opportunities and services for adolescents and young adults.

Different SRH services are offered for adolescents in these three departments. SRH services included the ‘Centre-conseils ados’ (CCA), ‘Centre départemental d’éducation populaire et sportif’ (CDEPS) and ‘espaces ados/jeunes’. Three ministries provided these services. The Ministry of Health and Social Action (MSAS) recommends the establishment of spaces for adolescents and young adults (‘espace ados/jeunes’) at healthcare facilities. The Ministry of Youth ensures that every Departmental Center for Popular Education and Sports (CDEPS) is equipped with an Adolescent Counselling Center (CCA) or an advisory office for adolescents and young adults (BCA). The Ministry of Education is responsible for promoting health in schools (Education on Family Life), including SRH messages.

Study population

The study participants include a variety of actors: in-depth interviews and focus group discussions (FGDs) with adolescents aged 10–19 and young adults aged 20–24 (users and non-users of SRH services), FGDs with mothers and fathers of adolescents and young adults of 10–24 age, and (KIIs) with SRH service providers and community and religious leaders living in the three departments. The research compared adolescents’ and young adults’ experiences and perspectives as users and non-users of SRH services.

Among adolescents and young adults, focus groups were divided according to participants’ sex (boys/young men; girls/young women) and age (10–14 years; 15–24 years), bringing together out-of-school, in-school and unschooled young people. To explore the quality of SRH service provision, in-depth interviews were conducted with adolescents who had used them at least once. When talking about people aged 10–24, we sometimes use the term ‘young people’ instead of ‘adolescents and young adults’ to shorten the sentences.

Focus groups were held with parents, split by sex (mothers and fathers). The parents were selected from various socio-economic strata of the population in the three departments to understand their perceptions and experiences and to document their needs to better involve them. All six focus groups consisted of parents with children aged between 10 and 24 years.

Sampling and data

We used a purposive sampling procedure to select participants to reflect the diversity of adolescents’ and young adults’ experiences according to their sociodemographic profile (sex, age range, place of residence, education) and parents’ knowledge, attitudes, perceptions and experiences about their children’s SRH, taking particular care to respect the heterogeneity of the actors interviewed as well as the range of the information to be collected. In all, 32 focus groups with a total of 224 participants, 14 in-depth interviews with adolescent users of SRH services and 17 key informant interviews were conducted in the three departments (Table 1).

Data collection and quality control

The data were collected in two phases following the grounded theory principles [23]. After the half-first FGDs, IDIs and KIIs in the three departments, the research team jointly assessed the data quality and conducted preliminary analyses, which informed the revision of the data collection tools followed by the second phase of data collection in the three departments.

Interviewers and their supervisors, who had at least a Master’s degree, were recruited in Kaolack based on their local knowledge and extensive experience in qualitative research. They received four days of training in adolescent SRH, interview techniques, consent procedures and anonymity. Several simulations were also conducted to test the tools and make any necessary adjustments.

The tools were designed in French by the lead researcher, and the data collectors reviewed each question to ensure understanding, discuss translation possibilities in Wolof, and agree on key local terms to ensure consistency among the data collectors. The interviews and focus groups were conducted and recorded mainly in Wolof, the region’s official local language. Recordings were transcribed directly into French by selected bilingual data collectors (Wolof/French). Senior researchers quality controlled the transcriptions by comparing a sample of Wolof recordings with their transcriptions. The transcriptions were progressively shared with the lead researcher for analysis using NVivo, along with regular meetings with other experts in the team for updates and reflection. The data analysis and the writing of this article were conducted in French to allow all team experts to review and contribute. The final version of the article was translated into English by the Enabel translation department.

Data collection tools

We developed six semi-structured interview and FGDs guides (for parents, adolescents/young adults aged 10–14 and 15–24 (users and non-users of SRH services, SRH services providers and local leaders) based on the research objectives, a literature review and local researchers’ field experience.

Parent-adolescent communication questions were included in all FGDs and IDIs guides to explore and understand the state and nature of communication such as what was discussed, the level of sensitivity, barriers and facilitators of parent-adolescent communication, strategies for improving this communication and parents’, adolescents’ and young adults’ knowledge, attitudes and practices. Key informants shared their views on parent-adolescent communication within the communities.

Data analysis

The transcripts were analysed by the lead qualitative researcher using Nvivo 12 and following the inductive thematic analysis approach [24, 25] and a deductive approach using themes in the interview guides. Memos were developed throughout the coding phases to document and reflect on the analysis process. There were no inter-coder reliability issues, as the lead researcher was the sole coder for the entire dataset using NVivo. The emerging data themes/codes from the data across settings were grouped under main codes. For example, quotations linked to parent-adolescent communication were grouped and labelled as a main code. The themes were revised iteratively with the whole research team, which consists of experts from Senegal, Enabel and three Belgian academic institutions, to ensure accurate understanding, and interpretation, and to address any biases or contextual factors affecting data analysis. Finally, the codes were categorised into themes and subthemes. The data analysis followed an iterative process: initial coding by the lead researcher led to team discussions on themes and sub-themes. In subsequent rounds, data analysis refined these themes, reaching saturation when no new themes emerged. Both deductive and inductive approaches were used to develop themes and sub-themes from the research instruments and the transcripts. Results

The research revealed several similarities between the responses of parents and adolescents/young adults relating to the nature of communication, the topics discussed, the sensitive topics, parent-adolescent communication barriers and potential strategies for improving this communication.

Limited family communication

The variety of sources adolescents and young adults used to find out about their SRH was linked to their sociodemographic profile. The sources identified were school (life and earth sciences courses, teachers), internet/social networks, friends/peers, socio-educational facilities (CDEPS, CCA, teen/youth centre, health centre), parents, neighbourhood godmothers called “Bajenu Gox” in Wolof, radio, television, etc.

Adolescents and young adults cited their parents as one of their preferred information sources. They attributed this to their parents’ relationship proximity and their primary responsibility for their education. Unfortunately, the lack of or poor communication and the sensitivity of some SRH topics often make this channel inaccessible and neglected by adolescents and young adults in favour of their peers, the Internet or teachers.

Parent-adolescent communication is mainly characterised by a lack of adequate communication on SRH in the three departments. Where communication exists, it is gendered (mother-daughter), restricted to certain SRH topics, marked by warnings or prohibitions, or completely absent on the issue. For example, during the FGDs with mothers and fathers, it was only the ‘neighbourhood godmothers’, called locally “Bajenu Gox” (a Wolof term for godmother) and SCA presidents who felt comfortable discussing adolescent SRH issues, unlike the rest of the parents. They are local resource people who are well informed and trained on the subject.

Both ‘Neighborhood Godmothers’ and ‘ASC Presidents’ can serve as confidants for some adolescents and young adults. ‘Neighborhood Godmothers’ are active members of their communities and are involved in promoting the health and well-being of women, children, adolescents and young adults. ASC Presidents are the Presidents of sports and cultural associations within the neighbourhoods. They supervise and mentor adolescents and young adults who are part of the ASCs and act as tutors.

In general, parent-adolescent communication on SRH is rather “general” or even “superficial”, as it does not delve deeply into the topics. Some parents admit having difficulties discussing these issues in-depth and finding the appropriate words to do so. They struggle to discuss SRH-related topics with their children and limit themselves to giving them advice or warnings on how to behave.

Parent-adolescent communication practices

In families that do communicate about SRH, the topics discussed are mainly related to menstruation, sexual abstinence, HIV/AIDS and unwanted pregnancies. Parents talk about certain topics in a general or superficial way but do not go into them in depth.

Generally, it is the parents who initiate such communication around or during puberty. The communication focuses mainly on giving advice (particularly on menstruation), stating prohibitions and even threats to respect social norms and religious customs prevailing in Senegalese society, particularly the preservation of virginity until marriage. For girls, the recurring message is to “stay away from boys”, “learn to be faithful” and “hold back”. For boys, they are generally advised to “watch out for girls” and avoid “associating with girls who use the birth control pill”. The following extracts from the FGDs of parents and teenagers on the three sites bear witness to this:

“When a girl comes of age and starts menstruating, we have to know and master her menstrual cycle to control her and impose certain conditions on our daughter.” Mothers of 10–24 age, FGD Nioro. “Imposing certain conditions” actually means setting rules that the parent wants the adolescent girl a adhere to so they can control her behaviour.

“I’m always trying to advise them, considering they are boys and physically grown up, and to put them on the right path, that they have to abstain from a lot of things […]. I control the girls too. When you come downstairs, I watch the way you walk and talk, and when I notice you have your period, I talk with you and tell you how to protect yourself. Because at every stage of life, we have to prepare her for respect.” Mothers of 10–24 age, FGD Guinguinéo.

These expressions or forms of communication reflect parents’ concerns about their children’s sexual health, in particular the fear of pregnancy among unmarried girls or the loss of their virginity, without the topic being discussed openly and without providing any real information about SRH. The advice or commands given within the family are not sufficiently substantiated to give adolescents/young adults a better understanding of the risks involved and how to avoid them.

Parent-child gendered communication

Communication between parents and adolescents/young adults mainly focused on advice and control, is strongly influenced by gender, with mothers tending to talk more with their daughters (rather than their sons), although some fathers also claim to do so. Adolescent girls said that mothers in society are seen as a source of security and trust, particularly regarding menstrual topics. Mother-daughter communication focuses mainly on menstruation, abstinence and virginity, with alarming messages to frighten young girls by highlighting the risks of pregnancy during what is considered to be a very critical period.

“My mother told me not to have relations with men because once you see your period, you are no longer a child. She also tells me that I’m a woman and I’m going to get married, so I have to preserve my virginity.” Girl aged 15–19, FGD Guinguinéo.

“My mum has already talked to me about puberty, marriage, early pregnancies, keeping up the body, how to protect yourself. She usually takes advantage of an event that is happening in the neighbourhood to put me on my guard, for example when a girl is pregnant or has been raped.” Girls aged 15–24, FGD Kaolack.

In the FGD, several fathers also pointed out that daughters talk more with their mothers. Some fathers of a focus group in Nioro complained during the discussion saying that they are not aware of their daughters’ “movements and attitudes because their wives refuse to share this information with them”.

Although most of the participants said that it was up to the father to discuss sensitive issues with the son, stating that “the son must be supervised by his father and the daughter by her mother”, some boys also find it easier to confide in their mothers than in their fathers. Some mothers say they talk about topics regarding puberty with their sons, even though this is perceived as embarrassing. Some boys also confide in their mothers. The nature of communication between parent and child is determined by the level of trust established between them, as illustrated by the separate comments of two fathers in Nioro.

“In any case, in my house, the boys only trust their mother. […] How am I going to talk to my sons, who refuse to consult me?” Fathers of 10–24 age, FGD Nioro.

“I have been very lucky with the girls. They are very close to me, and they are my friends. They respect my advice and we share everything.” Fathers of 10–24, FGD Kaolack.

In some situations, family communication extends to members of both the nuclear and extended family. Particularly when the relationships with the parent (father or mother) are not good, some girls said they talk to their grandmother, aunts, sisters or even older school-educated brothers. “Sometimes, we can get information from a member of our family (grandmother, aunt or other),” said a 17-year-old girl from Guinguinéo during a one-on-one interview.

Lack of communication on sexuality and contraception

The topics of sexuality and the use of contraception are rarely discussed within families. They are considered to be the most sensitive and difficult to discuss by both parents and adolescents and young adults.

On the subject of sexuality, the participants pointed out that in today’s Senegalese society, it is rare for a parent to call his or her child to openly discuss this topic. Even in families where parents have advanced education, communication on this subject is not systematic because of the shame and taboo associated with sexuality for both parents and adolescents/young adults. Below are some of the comments made by parents on this subject:

“I am ashamed to discuss these topics with my son; it is a taboo subject for me.” Mothers of 10–24 age, FGD Guinguinéo.

“At the moment, it is very difficult to talk to children about sexuality. […] At a certain age, both boys and girls need to talk about it.” Fathers of 10–24 age, FGD Nioro.

“I advise them [her daughters] in general terms, but I find it hard to name things… I’m not comfortable talking about sexuality. I do not know, but maybe it is because of my upbringing. […] I do not know enough about why I am blocked.” Mothers of 10–24 age, FGD Kaolack.

Adolescents and young adults are also subject to taboos and embarrassment that prevent them from broaching the subject of sexuality with their parents. They fear being perceived as disrespectful or rude if they initiate such a discussion, which can hinder their communication with their parents on these topics. Here is what adolescents and young adults think about the subject:

“Children are sometimes ashamed to discuss these topics with their parents.” Boy aged 24, individual interview Kaolack.

“You will be accused of being impolite if you broach certain topics when, in your opinion, you were just looking for knowledge.” Boys aged 20–24, FGD Guinguinéo.

According to most participants, the main reason for this situation is the taboo as well as the social and religious norms that advocate abstinence and virginity, especially for girls. This taboo is reinforced by the fear that discussing sexuality with children would have negative consequences. For some, this attitude is rooted in tradition, while for others, who are more progressive, it is linked more to ignorance. “As you notice, we cannot talk with our children about SRH. That is because we have some complex and are unaware,” said a father during an FGD in Kaolack. However, adolescents and young adults would like to discuss these issues, and some parents are aware of this, knowing that puberty is a period of many physical and emotional transformations for adolescents and young adults. To facilitate communication, some parents feel it is necessary to establish a climate of trust and accessibility with their children. However, all the parents in the focus group discussions admitted that they found it difficult to talk about sexuality, either because they felt uncomfortable or because they did not have the right words.

Even though there is no communication about sexuality, there is a different perception of girls’ and boys’ sexuality. There is a certain tolerance for boys, who are often perceived as “warriors” and whose sexuality is a sign of virility or masculinity, whereas there are social control and negative perceptions around sexually active girls. Unmarried girls who have sex before marriage are socially frowned upon. When asked what is said about a girl or a boy who has premarital sex, the responses of all the participants converged.

“It is frowned upon and unhealthy. This is especially true for girls because the penalties are heavier for girls than for boys. Usually, the boy is called a gentleman while the girl is called a whore, and so on. Society is more discriminating towards girls than boys.” Key informant, Guinguinéo.

“If a boy has impregnated a girl, he is nicknamed the warrior, he does not feel any pressure; on the other hand, the girl will be called a prostitute and the girl is often reproached as if the boy had done nothing.” II 21-year-old girl, individual interview Kaolack.

Actions to improve SRH for adolescents and young adults must take into account the additional pressures that girls face. Many participants, including boys and girls, felt that both deserved equal treatment and that the social sanction, such as in the case of unwanted pregnancy, should also apply to the person responsible for the pregnancy.

Contraception is the second topic rarely discussed, as it is closely linked to the issue of sexuality, which is itself little discussed. The social value placed on virginity (repeatedly mentioned by all the participants) imposes abstinence until marriage, particularly for girls, which makes the use of contraceptive methods inappropriate in this context. Sex before marriage is widely frowned upon socially and viewed negatively by parents, especially concerning girls.

“[…] At a certain age, girls have to distance themselves from boys. […] A girl who is not married and allows herself to have sex is a scandal, just like a boy.” Fathers of 10–24 age, FGD Kaolack.

Nevertheless, our study showed a high frequency of early pregnancies, demonstrating a challenge in the face of messages advocating abstinence. The reality of sexually active unmarried adolescents/young adults and the resulting early pregnancies highlight a conflict between social norms and religious prescriptions on the one hand and the individual desires of some adolescents to experiment with their sexuality before marriage on the other hand.

In general, there are two opposing camps among participants regarding the use of contraception: those who support it as an alternative to abstinence and those who are vehemently opposed to it, demanding that adolescents and young adults respect the abstinence norm. Parents and adolescents/young adults defend one position or the other, although socially, abstinence is encouraged, promoted and valued. However, given the prevalence of early pregnancy, it is essential to find ways to help parents openly and constructively discuss SRH with their children, rather than simply warning or frightening them.

The lack of communication between parents and adolescents/young adults on these two sensitive topics has led adolescents and young adults to turn to the Internet or their friends or peers, regardless of whether they are attending school or not. They say they feel “at ease” with their peers because they “speak the same language”, “discuss everything” and can “confide their secrets”. For the Internet, young people mainly use it to find information about sexuality, sexual relationships and menstruation. They highly value the discretion, confidentiality and availability of online information. However, the viewing of pornographic videos is cited and criticised by adolescents/young adults and parents. Many parents criticised mobile phones and feel powerless because they often lack the means or the time to monitor how adolescents and young adults use them. “Children are connected to Wi-Fi, especially at night, watching pornographic movies. This has contributed more to the drift of children.” Fathers of 10–24 age, FGD Nioro.

Some adolescents and adolescents/young adults also complained about certain parents for their excessive use of cell phones and the Internet or that some parents also consult pornographic videos.

Their children realise the bad behaviours of their parents because some people post indecent videos, and their children will be looked down upon” Boys aged 10–14, FGD Kaolack:

“When some parents give you their phone, you see a lot of things, and you are surprised by what you see. As my friend said, sometimes you see in the women’s WhatsApp groups a woman sending these kinds of porn, and then she says: ‘It is a mistake, my son had the phone and sent it’. Now, if her son sees that and if he has a certain age, he will research the subject. In addition, there are a lot of things about health, and it affects us” Boys aged 15–24, FGD Kaolack.

Finally, adolescents and young adults who attend school have an advantage over their peers who are not because they are taught about sexual and reproductive health (SRH) at school.

Barriers to parent-adolescent communication

The majority of parents feel uncomfortable discussing certain adolescent SRH topics, partly due to what they describe as taboo and shame. Adolescents/young adults and parents mentioned additional barriers to communication, such as parents’ lack or limited knowledge of SRH issues, their low level of education, the lack of time or willingness, particularly among mothers who are often busy with daily activities and family affairs as well as the severity or resignation of some parents. There are discrepancies in what they reported as additional barriers. Under these conditions, children may feel powerless, ashamed or afraid to discuss their worries with their parents.

“In my neighbourhood, she [the mother] does not even have time for her children or her husband. She is a shopkeeper, and she is always busy with her business […].” FGD Mothers, Kaolack.

“Some parents turn away; they need to be open with their children […]. If parents do not open to their children, they will not know anything about them.” FGD Mothers, Nioro.

Adolescents and young adults also express the view that some parents disengage from their parental responsibilities for various reasons, one being that some parents are often focused on their phones and do not have time to talk to them.

“There are often parents who have no communication line with their children, and that is not good. I think it is up to the parent to take responsibility for supervising their child during their adolescent years to avoid certain things happening.” Girls aged 15–24, FGD Guinguinéo.

Finally, the participants cited the attitudes and misbehaviours of some adolescents and young adults towards their parents, which some parents attributed to “excessive freedom granted to adolescents/young adults” resulting from the “prohibition of hitting children”.

Parents recognise that they should get closer to their children and communicate more with them because communication is essential, and there should be complicity between the parent (mother and child or father) and his/her child. Young people also expect their parents to be role models for their children and to demonstrate behaviours accordingly. However, the difficult living conditions of some families can also be an obstacle to building a trustful parent-adolescent relationship.

Ways of improving parent-adolescent communication

The majority of parents feel uncomfortable discussing certain adolescent SRH topics, partly due to what they describe as taboo and shame. The results of this study open up opportunities for developing interventions to improve parents’ role in promoting adolescent SRH. Indeed, both parents and adolescents/young adults advocate for the implementation of strategies aimed at raising awareness and training parents to help them establish open communication with their children about reproductive health. In all the focus groups, parents expressed the desire to have their capacity strengthened to initiate and confidently convey accurate information about SRH and to be able to “put words” to sensitive topics. Both fathers and mothers expressed their desire to be better informed and prepared in this respect through awareness-raising and training. These parents requested in these terms:

“Some parents want to communicate but do not have the tools to do it […] I would have liked to be given the tools to discuss RH with our children It is a real handicap.” Fathers of 10–24 age, FGD Guinguineo.

“I’m ashamed to discuss these topics with my son. It is truly a taboo subject for me. […] Parents need to be supported with techniques so that discussions can continue with their children.” Mothers of 10–24 age, FGD Nioro.

“[Training] is a necessity. We have difficulty talking to our children. […] It is the parents who should be trained first and then the young people. Not all parents know about this issue. If someone comes to help you educate your children, you have to join in.” Fathers of 10–24 age, FGD Kaolack.

Adolescents and young adults also recommend sensitising and training parents through communication, information and awareness-raising activities that can reach a wider audience through channels such as community dialogues, home visits, talks, theatres and community radio. They also emphasised the importance of encouraging parents to get closer to their children from a certain age to create a relationship of complicity between them.

“We need to raise parents’ awareness and educate them because most of our mothers are ignorant. Many of them find it challenging to have discussions with their children […] To eradicate this problem, we need to encourage mothers to get closer to their children when they reach a certain age to establish a bond.” Girls aged 20–24, FGD Kaolack.

Training parents on SRH issues should equip them with the skills they need to communicate openly on SRH issues, including sensitive ones. In this way, parents can gain accurate and reliable information on various aspects of SRH and learn the tools enabling them to effectively communicate with their children on these topics and overcome their feelings of shame and taboo. Parents feel that such training should be tailored to the sociocultural context of Senegal, and they suggest complementary actions such as raising awareness in homes and through existing neighbourhood women’s groups and tontines. For sensitive topics such as sexuality, external resources (schools, SRH centres or health centres) can be valuable sources of information complementing the information provided by parents.

Gender inequality in the social treatment

The findings also highlight gender differences in the social treatment of boys and girls by parents. While parental and social control affects both genders, it is marked by gender inequalities that disadvantage girls. Girls face greater risks, particularly of unwanted pregnancies and loss of virginity, which bring dishonour to both the family and the girl herself. Consequently, control is primarily focused on sexual experience and the use of contraceptive methods. Although sexual activity is prohibited for adolescents and young adults of both sexes, there is sometimes a certain ‘tolerance’ towards boys, whereas girls are expected to fully adhere to prevailing social rules. Unmarried girls who engage in sexual relations before marriage are often stigmatized and labelled as ‘girls with too much freedom,’ ‘prostitutes,’ or ‘girls who know all the streetlights of Kaolack.’ In contrast, sexuality among unmarried boys is either difficult to control socially or even perceived as a sign of masculinity and virility: ‘a warrior.’ The narratives about unequal treatment between boys and girls were consistent across all study participants.

Researcher: How is a girl or a boy who engages in sexual relations before marriage perceived?

“Badly seen and unhealthy, especially for the girl because the penalties are harsher for her than for boys. Usually, the boy is called a gentleman, while the girl is called a slut, etc. Society is more judgmental towards the girl than towards the boy.” Biology Teacher, Guinguinéo.

“If a boy gets a girl pregnant, they nickname him ‘the warrior,’ he faces no pressure; on the other hand, the girl will be labelled a prostitute, etc.” A 21-year-old girl, services user, Kaolack.

“ For the boy, no one cares. It’s only the girl who needs to be managed. If both are involved, people often point fingers at the girl as if the boy did nothing. However, a girl will no longer have peace in her family.” Bajenou Gox, Kaolack.

A girl’s virginity at the time of marriage is seen as an indicator of both the family’s and the girl’s honour, placing additional pressure on adolescent girls who already face significant familial and societal control. The onset of menstruation further adds to this control over adolescent girls. The absence or delay of menstruation is also a source of concern for girls, as they have to navigate not only their worries but also parental control and suspicion.

Discussion

In this study, we examined the perspectives and reactions of parents and adolescents regarding family communication about SRH in three departments in the Kaolack region of Senegal.

Our results show that a variety of parental approaches are used to communicate with adolescents. Parent-child communication is generally characterised by advice, factual information or prohibitions, mainly focused on less sensitive topics such as menstruation. Sexuality and contraception are rarely discussed. With sexuality in particular, parents tend to use imprecise terms or messages to instil fear in adolescents. Parents face barriers such as their lack of information about SRH or embarrassment or shame about broaching certain subjects. Additionally, adolescents and young adults face barriers such as family or social pressure, embarrassment or shame and lack of or inaccessibility of knowledge on SRH. This pattern seems to be common to several cultures, and our results converge with other studies showing the difficulty for parents, in many African societies, to discuss SRH issues with their adolescent children, particularly regarding sexuality [5–20]. A recent systematic review of parent-adolescent communication about sexuality in the African context concludes that communication is characterised by threats, warnings and moralistic and religious views without providing essential information about sexuality [13]. A study conducted in Ghana concluded that parents talk to their children about sexuality, but their conversations focus mainly on abstinence and rarely on condoms and contraception [26]. A study conducted in Senegal confirms that the persistence of “taboo” and “silence” around sexuality makes it difficult for parents and adolescents to discuss the subject [1].

Our results show that parents, especially mothers, communicate more with girls than with boys and that communication strategies based on commands or fear present gender inequalities that disadvantage girls. While the ban of sexuality before marriage applies to adolescents/young adults of both sexes, this control is marked by gender inequalities that disadvantage girls – there is a degree of tolerance towards boys, whereas girls are expected to obey the applicable social rules of abstinence and preserving virginity. There is additional pressure on adolescent girls, especially after they start menstruating, as they run the greatest risk of unwanted pregnancy and loss of virginity, which brings dishonour to the family and the girl herself. Family and social pressure on girls go hand in hand with fear-based communication about sexuality and the use of contraceptive methods. Our study did not document the effectiveness of the fear-based communication approach. However, research conducted in different African contexts has shown that this strategy does not have a positive influence on the sexual behaviour of adolescents [13–28].

The accounts from the IDIs and FGDs with adolescents and young adults show that they perceive parents as a valuable source of information for them, but the reality is characterised by little or no appropriate parental communication. Our results also indicate that parents are aware of adolescents’ and young adults’ needs and recognise that they should play a key role in this regard. However, many parents are unaware of the best approach to take or are held back by the sensitivity of the subject, taboos, social norms and lack of knowledge. All these factors limit the ability of many parents to properly play this key role and engage in open discussion with their children on SRH issues. These findings are consistent with the literature [11–13, 29, 30].

Conclusion

Our study highlights the crucial importance of parent-child communication and describes current practices in the Kaolack region. It identifies significant barriers, including cultural taboos, gendered communication practices, and discomfort among both parents and adolescents/young adults. These obstacles often limit discussions, leading adolescents and young adults to rely on peers or the internet. Communication tends to be gender-specific, restricting comprehensive dialogue, and societal expectations impose stricter standards on girls’ sexuality while being more tolerant of boys’ sexual experiences.

To address these challenges, the study proposed several courses of action. Both parents and adolescents/young adults recommended that interventions focus on training and equipping parents with culturally sensitive and gender-inclusive communication skills to facilitate open and age-appropriate discussions about sexual and reproductive health (SRH) with their children. Recommendations expressed by parents and young people in our study have informed the development of a locally adapted intervention, now being implemented by Enabel in the Kaolack region. This intervention aims to train parents in effective communication to enhance their children’s sexual and reproductive health (SRH). Specific tools are used in these community-based trainings, taking into account the country’s sociocultural context and involving a variety of parents with different backgrounds. In addition, the training will equip parents to initiate age-appropriate communication. Although our study focused on the 10–24 age group, some young people wished their parents had addressed certain topics at an early age, such as menstruation, to better prepare them for the physical and emotional changes of adolescence. A planned evaluation of these trainings should assess their effectiveness and impact, providing valuable insights for similar settings across sub-Saharan Africa.

This type of intervention still seems to be uncommon in sub-Saharan Africa, although systematic reviews have shown that interventions with parents to promote parent-adolescent communication have significant positive effects on adolescents’ knowledge and behaviour regarding SRH [30–33]. Improving communication between parents and their children on SRH can lead to better health outcomes and support the overall well-being of adolescents and young adults.
