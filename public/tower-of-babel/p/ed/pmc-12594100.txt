Polypharmacy, anticholinergic burden and oral microbiome among U.S. middle-aged and older adults: a representative national survey
Authors: Kai Wei, Yanping Yang, Chun Chen, Yong Yang, Kui Hu, Qi Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPolypharmacy has been linked to alterations in gut microbiota, but its effects on the oral microbiome remain underexplored. This study aimed to examine the association of polypharmacy and anticholinergic burden with oral microbiome diversity and composition.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis using data from NHANES 2009–2012, including participants aged 55–69 years who reported at least one prescription medication. Polypharmacy was defined as the concurrent use of five or more medications. Anticholinergic burden was quantified by the Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden scale and the Anticholinergic Drug Scale. Oral microbiome profiling comprised alpha diversity, beta diversity, and taxonomic composition at multiple phylogenetic levels. Associations were analyzed via weighted multivariable linear regression, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), and multivariate analysis of variance.ResultsAmong 1,596 participants, 29.2% reported polypharmacy, which correlated with higher anticholinergic burden. Both polypharmacy and anticholinergic burden were inversely associated with alpha diversity across multiple measures. Taxonomic analyses showed heterogeneous associations, with Porphyromonadaceae negatively linked to both exposures. PCoA indicated significant differences in community structure by polypharmacy status (Bray-Curtis: R² = 0.35%, P < .001).ConclusionsAmong middle-aged and older U.S. adults, both polypharmacy and anticholinergic burden were inversely associated with oral microbiome diversity and linked to distinct microbiome composition.Keywords: Polypharmacy, anticholinergic burden, oral microbiome, diversity

Background

The issue of polypharmacy has emerged as a significant public health concern, particularly in relation to older adults [1]. With the global population aging rapidly, there has been a substantial rise in the proportion of older individuals who take five or more medications concurrently, which is commonly defined as polypharmacy [2,3]. This increase is largely driven by the high prevalence of multiple chronic conditions and by clinical practice guidelines that recommend the use of multiple therapeutic agents for disease management. Polypharmacy is usually accompanied by an elevated anticholinergic burden [4], which has been associated with a wide range of adverse health outcomes, including impaired cognitive function, increased risk of falls, and poor oral health [5]. Despite growing attention to these systemic effects, the potential influence of polypharmacy on the human microbiome, particularly the oral microbiome, remains poorly characterized.

In older adults, the composition of the oral microbiome undergoes specific changes that may reflect underlying biological aging and cumulative health exposures [6–8]. These age-related shifts have been linked to the development and progression of chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and cancer [9–11]. Recent research has increasingly focused on the interactions between prescription medications and the gut microbiota, revealing complex bidirectional relationships that may affect both drug metabolism and microbial balance [12–14]. Polypharmacy, in particular, has been associated with microbial diversity and alterations in community structure in the gut, possibly due to antimicrobial properties of drugs [15–17].

Emerging evidence also suggests that medication use can affect the integrity of microbial barriers. For example, a prospective interventional study by Xiao et al. [18] demonstrated that the use of proton pump inhibitors led to translocation of oral-origin Streptococcus into the gut microbiota. This finding highlights the potential role of medications in disturbing the ecological boundaries between the oral and intestinal microbiomes. However, compared to the gut microbiome, the relationship between polypharmacy or anticholinergic burden and the oral microbiome has received relatively little attention, representing an important gap in current research.

In 2022, Emily Vogtmann and colleagues developed a comprehensive oral microbiome reference using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), which provides a representative sample of the U.S. population [19,20]. Using this publicly available dataset, the present study aimed to investigate the associations between polypharmacy, anticholinergic burden and the composition of the oral microbiome, contributing to a deeper understanding of how complex medication regimens may affect microbial health in older adults.

Methods

Data source and study population

The NHANES, designed to be nationally representative, surveys the noninstitutionalized civilian population in the United States. In the 2009–2010 and 2011–2012 cycles of the NHANES, a total of 20,293 participants were selected through a complex, probability-based sampling design incorporating stratification and multistage clustering. Oral microbiome profiles were generated from oral rinse specimens collected from participants between the ages of 14 and 69. For this cross-sectional study, we restricted the analysis to 1,596 adults aged 55 to 69 years who had undergone oral microbiome testing and reported taking at least one prescription medication. The participant inclusion process is illustrated in the flow diagram presented in Figure 1. Ethical approval for NHANES was granted by the Research Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. The present analysis was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [21].

Definition of polypharmacy and anticholinergic burden

During the household interview, participants were asked: ‘In the past 30 days, have you used or taken medication for which a prescription is needed?’. If the answer is ‘Yes’, the medicine container was inspected by the interviewer and the generic name and quantity of the drug will be recorded. And then, participants were asked about number of days taken medicine by the question: ‘For how long have you been using or taking [PRODUCT NAME]?’. Given the lack of consensus on the definition of polypharmacy [1,22], we have adopted the definition commonly used in most studies [3,23]. Consequently, the concurrent use of five or more medications was defined as polypharmacy.

We assessed participants’ anticholinergic burden using two well-established algorithms: the Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden (ACB) scale [24] and the Anticholinergic Drug Scale (ADS) [25]. In both scales, each medication is assigned a score according to its anticholinergic potency: 0 (no potency), 1 (mild), 2 (moderate), or 3 (high) (Supplementary Table S1). For each participant, the total anticholinergic burden was calculated as the sum of scores for all medications reported during the assessment period, with higher total scores indicate a greater anticholinergic burden [26,27]. We also evaluated the concordance between the two scales, and participants’ total ACB and ADS scores were moderately correlated (Spearman's ρ = 0.684, Supplementary Figure S1).

In addition, we checked the use of xerogenic medications (Supplementary Table S1). Based on the systematic review from the World Workshop on Oral Medicine VI (28), 56 medications were previously classified as having strong evidence for causing xerostomia, and participants were considered exposed if they reported the use of at least one of these medications.

Oral microbiome data processing

Oral microbiome testing was performed using oral rinse samples collected from NHANES participants in two consecutive NHANES cycles, 2009−2010 and 2011−2012 [19]. Further details regarding sample collection, laboratory protocols, and bioinformatics procedures are available in the NHANES Oral Microbiome Data Documentation (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Nhanes/omp/). In brief, the V4 region of the 16S ribosomal RNA gene was sequenced from DNA extracted from oral rinse samples. Sequencing data were processed using the NHANES-provided DADA2 pipeline with taxonomy assignment based on the SILVA v123 database. Quality control steps included removing a non-bacterial ASV (SV1032) and taxa lacking phylum-level identification. The public-use dataset does not include per-sample sequencing depth statistics for any participant.

Alpha diversity is a measure of the diversity within a sample. The alpha diversity in this study was quantified using four metrics: observed ASVs, Faith's Phylogenetic Diversity [28], Shannon-Weiner index [29] and Inverse Simpson index [30]. In NHANES, all measures of alpha diversity were calculated using post-rarefaction data (random subsampling without replacement) of 2,000 to 10,000 reads per sample, each replicated ten times. Using the NHANES-provided rarefied tables at 10,000 reads, we calculated the final value for each metric as the mean of the 10 replicates in this study.

Beta diversity is another term for sample dissimilarity, which quantifies the differences in the overall taxonomic composition between two samples. In this study, the ASV feature tables were rarefied at 10,000 reads/sample using the QIIME command beta diversity, which included the unweighted UniFrac distance [31], weighted UniFrac distance [31], and Bray-Curtis dissimilarity [32].

Taxonomic profiling was performed on unrarefied datasets, limited to samples with at least 10,000 sequence reads. Bacterial taxa were identified at the phylum, class, order, family, and genus levels using QIIME, and summarized as relative abundance (proportion of taxon-specific reads in a sample).

Covariates

Sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, educational level, and family income), lifestyle variables (smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity), and disease information were self-reported via computer-assisted questionnaires. Physical measurements, including height and weight, were taken at the mobile examination center by trained personnel. Marital status was classified into three categories: never married, married or cohabiting, and divorced, widowed, or separated. Educational level was grouped based on total years of schooling into: less than 9 years, 9 to 12 years, and more than 12 years. Annual family income was dichotomized according to whether it exceeded $20,000. Physical activity (PA) was categorized based on whether respondents met the national guidelines [33]: at least 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity, 75 minutes per week of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination thereof. Body mass index (BMI) was computed as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared and categorized as follows, per guidelines from the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association [34]: normal (<25 kg/m²), overweight (≥25 and <30 kg/m²), and obese (≥30 kg/m²). Participants were asked whether they had ever been diagnosed by a physician with certain chronic conditions, including hypercholesterolemia, hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases (including congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina, myocardial infarction, or stroke), cancer, arthritis, or airway diseases (including emphysema, asthma, or bronchitis). Comorbidity was operationalized as the total number of these self-reported conditions. Based on prior literature and our conceptual framework, comorbidity was treated as a key potential confounder [35,36]. Individuals' ratings of their teeth and gums health were categorized into three groups: those who rated themselves as ‘excellent’ or ‘very good’ were classified as excellent, those who rated themselves as ‘good’ or ‘fair’ were classified as general, and those who rated themselves as ‘poor’ were classified as poor. For variables with missing data, multiple imputation was applied to minimize potential bias.

Statistical analysis

To generate estimates generalizable to the U.S. civilian population, sample weights, clustering, and stratification were incorporated into the overall analyzes according to NHANES analytical guidelines. Participants were stratified into two groups based on the number of participants’ prescription medication. Weighted means (standard deviations, SDs), weighted medians (interquartile ranges, IQRs), and numbers (weighted percentages) were used to present the baseline characteristics of the study participants where appropriate. Differences between the groups were tested using ANOVA, the Kruskal-Wallis test, or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate.

The weighted multivariate regression analysis was conducted using the svyglm function from ‘survey’ package to examine the independent associations of polypharmacy and anticholinergic burden with four alpha diversity metrics and the relative abundance of taxa. For taxonomic analyzes, we retained only taxa detected in more than 10% of participants. Given the left-skewed distribution of taxa abundances, a small pseudocount (1 × 10–6) was added to each value prior to applying a natural log transformation. To account for multiple testing, p-values were adjusted for false discovery rate (FDR) separately within each taxonomic level (phylum, class, order, family, genus), and taxa with an FDR-adjusted p-value <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, and race/ethnicity. Model 2 was based on Model 1, with additional adjustment for education levels, marital status, family income, smoking status, alcohol consumption, PA, and BMI. Model 3 was adjusted based on Model 2, with additional adjustments for hypercholesterolemia, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, cancer, arthritis, airway diseases, and oral health condition. Principal coordinate analyzes (PCoA) were conducted on each beta diversity matrix using the wcmdscale function, incorporating survey weights, to visualize the distribution of the oral microbiome community according to polypharmacy status. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was performed using the adonis2 function to evaluate the variability (R2) and significant differences in the beta diversity metrics by polypharmacy status. Both wcmdscale and adonis2 functions are from the ‘vegan’ package.

We performed several sensitivity analyzes to make our results more robust. First, we repeated the main analysis of the data without any missing covariates. Second, to exclude bias due to short-term use of multiple medications for acute illness, we repeated the main analysis after excluding respondents who used prescribed medications for less than 30 days. Third, in model 3, the number of comorbidities was used as a key covariate instead of individual age-related diseases, to further minimize potential confounding of comorbidity on the association between polypharmacy and the oral microbiome. Fourth, we repeated the analysis after excluding participants who reported using systemic anti-infective medications. Fifth, we excluded participants who reported using dentures, partial dentures, or dental plates, as these dental prostheses can alter the oral environment and substantially influence the composition of the oral microbiome. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the statistical analyzes were performed with R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

The study included 1,596 adults with a weighted median age of 61 years (interquartile range: 58−65 years), of whom 51.2% were women. Overall, 29.2% of the participants reported polypharmacy (≥5 medications), and they had a higher anticholinergic burden, consistently quantified by both the ACB and ADS (both P < .001). Additionally, 28.6% of participants used at least one xerogenic medication, with a significantly higher prevalence among those with polypharmacy (P < .001). Our analysis revealed inverse associations of both polypharmacy and higher anticholinergic burden with oral microbiome alpha diversity, after adjusting for multiple covariates (Table 2). In the fully adjusted model, polypharmacy was significantly associated with lower Observed ASVs (β = –9.686; 95% CI, –15.649 to –3.723), Faith’s Phylogenetic Diversity (β = –0.592; 95% CI, –1.121 to –0.064), and Shannon–Weiner index (β = –0.123; 95% CI, –0.221 to –0.026), but not with the Inverse Simpson index. Both ACB and ADS showed similar inverse associations, most notably with the Shannon–Weiner index. For ACB, significant associations persisted for Observed ASVs (β = –2.374; 95% CI, –4.390 to –0.357) and Shannon–Weiner index (β = –0.044; 95% CI, –0.078 to –0.010), but were attenuated for Faith’s Phylogenetic Diversity after full adjustment. In contrast, ADS showed robust inverse associations with all four alpha diversity metrics (all P < .001). Similar findings were observed in all sensitive analyzes (Supplementary Tables S2−S6).

Discussion

In this nationally representative sample of middle-aged and older adults, we found that both polypharmacy and anticholinergic burden were negatively associated with oral microbiome diversity, as measured by the observed ASVs, Faith’s Phylogenetic Diversity, and the Shannon–Weiner index, independent of demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and comorbidities. Taxonomic analyzes further revealed that several microbial taxa were associated with polypharmacy and anticholinergic burden. Additionally, polypharmacy was strongly related to inter-individual variation in overall microbiome.

Prior research has focused on the impact of particular therapeutic agents on the structure and function of the gut microbiome [12–14], with several studies suggesting that polypharmacy is linked to gut microbiome diversity [15–17]. For example, an analysis of 76 elderly hospitalized patients found a significant negative correlation between the number of drugs and the Chao1 index of alpha-diversity in gut microbiota [15]. Additionally, a population-level metagenomic analysis revealed that the number of drugs had a slight but significant negative correlation with gut microbiome composition, as well as gut microbial function [16]. In a longitudinal aging mouse study, researchers observed that chronic administration of polypharmacy with high drug burden index mitigated the aging-related reduction in alpha diversity [17]. Our findings in the oral microbiome are directionally consistent with this literature, underscoring that medication burden may broadly be linked to host-associated microbial communities.

In addition to evidence from the gut, a few studies have reported associations between particular therapeutic agents and the oral microbiome [20,37,38]. Yet, only a limited number of investigations have addressed the overall influence of polypharmacy, an area that is just beginning to be explored. For instance, Schwartz et al. [39], examined a small sample comprising 85 older dental patients and founded medication count was associated with several specific taxa, including Catonellamorbi and Porphyromonas pasteri within the Porphyromonadaceae family, although no overall association was observed with salivary microbiome beta diversity. More recently, Shahin et al. [40] extended this line of work by analyzing two independent cohorts of older adults and observed that polypharmacy was associated with lower alpha diversity as well as differences in selected taxa. Together, these studies suggest that polypharmacy can be linked to shifts in the oral microbiome, although sample sizes have thus far been relatively modest. In this context, our study builds on prior findings by providing evidence from a large, nationally representative cohort, showing that both polypharmacy and anticholinergic burden were associated with differences in the abundance of the Porphyromonadaceae and other taxa.

Consistent with prior evidence, our results also show that polypharmacy is accompanied by multimorbidity (supplementary Figure S2). Therefore, it is particularly important to account for multimorbidity when discussing the association between complex medication exposures and health outcomes [35,36]. To address this, we used two approaches: first, adjusting for several common age-related conditions in multivariate models (Table 2), and second, using the number of self-reported conditions as a proxy for comorbidity, given the lack of information to calculate the Carlson comorbidity index (supplementary Table S4). In both models, polypharmacy and anticholinergic burden remained inverse association with the oral microbiome alpha diversity. Polypharmacy also explained a significant proportion of between-individual variation in oral microbiome composition, independent of comorbidity, and this association persisted regardless of the modeling sequence (Figure 3, supplementary Figure S4). Furthermore, taxonomic analyzes revealed distinct and non-overlapping patterns linked to polypharmacy, anticholinergic burden, and comorbidity. These findings parallel previous observations in gut microbiota by Ticinesi et al. [15] and Nagata et al. [16], who reported that polypharmacy and comorbidity were linked to specific microbial taxa. They suggested that the polypharmacy-microbiome associations were mostly independent of the number of diseases. Nevertheless, given the strong correlation between polypharmacy and multimorbidity, complete disentanglement of their effects is challenging, and residual confounding cannot be fully excluded.

The mechanisms underlying medication burden associated with shifts in the oral microbiota remain incompletely understood. Given that sequencing samples were collected from oral rinse [19], medication-induced xerostomia may be a contributing factor [39]. A wide range of drugs are known to contribute to xerostomia [41], with anticholinergic agents being the most prominent due to their direct inhibition of salivary secretion. In our study, nearly one-third of participants reported use of at least one xerogenic medication, with substantially higher prevalence among those with polypharmacy, suggesting the potential role of xerostomia. Reduced salivary flow could plausibly alter local pH and oxygen tension and change microbial adherence, thereby promoting shifts in community composition. Consistent with this hypothesis, we observed that higher anticholinergic burden was significantly associated with alterations in the oral microbiome. Previous studies have reported less salivary volume [42] or higher xerostomia prevalence [43] among older adults taking multiple medications, particularly those including xerogenic medications. Weng et al. [44] further documented major phylum-level shifts of oral microorganisms in patients with xerostomia compared to healthy controls. Taken together, these observations suggest that anticholinergic effects on salivary function may represent a mechanistic link between polypharmacy, anticholinergic burden, and changes in the oral microbiome. It should be noted that this xerostomia-mediated pathway remains hypothetical and cannot be inferred from our cross-sectional data. Beyond salivary effects, polypharmacy and anticholinergic burden have been associated with elevated systemic inflammatory markers [45,46], which may further perturb oral microbial communities and amplify health risks. These pathways—xerostomia, inflammation, and direct microbial perturbation—may act synergistically to mediate the impact of complex medication regimens on the oral microbiome composition, potentially contributing to adverse outcomes such as cognitive decline [47,48] and mortality [49,50]. Future longitudinal and mechanistic studies that integrate medication exposures, salivary function, inflammation, and oral microbiome dynamics will be essential to disentangle these complex relationships and identify potential intervention targets.

The strengths of our study include the use of a large sample of participants who are representative of the U.S. population and the use of several measures of alpha and beta diversity. However, this study has several limitations. One is that the cross-sectional design of NHANES precludes the establishment of a causal relationship. Second, although we performed taxonomic analyzes, the restriction to 16S rRNA gene sequencing constrained our resolution to genus-level classifications, precluding species-level discrimination and functional characterization. Third, oral rinse specimens capture a pooled microbial signal from multiple oral niches, and therefore preclude site-specific conclusions (e.g. subgingival vs. supragingival plaque, tongue, or mucosa). Fourth, despite comprehensive adjustment for documented confounders including comorbidities, residual confounding may persist due to unmeasured variables. These include self-report bias in medical history, undocumented disease severity, undiagnosed conditions, and the limitation that self-rated scores may not capture clinically assessed oral health status. Fifth, NHANES did not collect oral microbiome data for adults aged 70 years and older, an age group that typically has greater medication burden, which limits the generalizability of our findings. Finally, the absence of data on medication dosage and over-the-counter drugs in the NHANES dataset precluded a more accurate assessment of the medication burden, including the cumulative effects.

In conclusion, this analysis of a large, nationally representative sample provides evidence linking polypharmacy and anticholinergic burden with the oral microbiome composition. Both polypharmacy and higher anticholinergic burden were inversely associated with oral microbiome diversity. Moreover, specific microbial taxa were differentially associated with polypharmacy and anticholinergic burden. Clinically, our results highlight the importance of considering oral health when managing medication use in aging populations. These findings may have implications for integrating oral microbiome considerations into geriatric pharmacotherapy. Further prospective observational and interventional studies are warranted to clarify the relationships between polypharmacy, anticholinergic burden, and the composition and function of the oral microbiome and its related metabolites.
