Molecular survey of Borrelia spp. in bats and soft tick infesting bats in Thailand
Authors: Siwaporn Tuangpermsub, Apinya Arnuphapprasert, Elizabeth Riana, Thongchai Ngamprasertwong, Morakot Kaewthamasorn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBats are recognized reservoirs for numerous pathogens, including zoonotic agents. They harbor a variety of ectoparasites, such as ticks, which pose a potential risk for cross-species pathogen spillover. Borrelia, a genus of spirochete bacteria responsible for several vector-borne diseases, represents a significant public health concern due to its global distribution. However, its genetic diversity and circulation in Southeast Asia remain poorly understood. To address this knowledge gap, this study investigated the occurrence and genetic diversity of Borrelia spp. in Thai bats and the ticks associated with them based on PCR and sequencing, without bacterial isolation or clinical case data. A total of 1031 bats were sampled across nine provinces in Thailand between February 2018 and February 2023. Morphological and molecular analysis-based methods identified and confirmed 96 Reticulinasus sp. ticks infesting Craseonycteris thonglongyai and Eonycteris spelaea. Molecular screening of 48 pooled tick samples (from 96 individual ticks) detected four Borrelia-positive pools originating from three individual C. thonglongyai bats. PCR screening of 814 bats targeting the flaB gene revealed 13 positive individuals (1.6%; 95% CI: 0.9–2.7%), comprising Taphozous melanopogon, Hipposideros gentilis, and C. thonglongyai. Multilocus sequence analysis of the 16S rRNA, flaB, and glpQ genes revealed three distinct Borrelia lineages. Two clustered within the relapsing fever Borrelia group, highlighting the need for further investigation of their transmission potential and epidemiological significance in other animal hosts. The third lineage formed a highly divergent, bat-associated clade, suggesting possible cryptic speciation based on sequence divergence across the three loci; however, formal taxonomic classification will require broader sampling and additional genetic markers. This study presents the first molecular-level evidence of Borrelia in Thai bats and bat-infesting ticks.Keywords: Bats, Borrelia, Ticks, Tick-borne disease, Thailand, Zoonosis

Introduction

Bats (Order Chiroptera) represent one of the most diverse and ecologically significant groups of mammals; the only mammals capable of sustained powered flight, with at least 1500 species worldwide (Simmons and Cirranello, 2026). Recent reports indicate that certain species migrate over long distances from their roosting sites (Hurme et al., 2025). These mammals are widely recognized as reservoirs of numerous pathogens, including zoonotic agents (Szentivanyi et al., 2024). They also host a diverse assemblage of temporary or intermittent ectoparasites, such as bat flies and ticks. These factors create potential pathways for pathogen spillover to other animal species, particularly through bat movements into new habitats and the blood-feeding activities of associated ectoparasites (Szentivanyi et al., 2024). Furthermore, migratory bats may facilitate the geographical expansion of ectoparasites by transporting them to new locations, thereby disseminating both vectors and the pathogens they harbor. Despite their ecological importance, research on bats remains challenging due to their nocturnal behavior and protected status, which limit opportunities for capture and systematic investigation.

Ticks (Acari: Ixodida) are obligate blood-feeding ectoparasites and rank among the most crucial vectors of animal and human pathogens. The order Ixodida comprises ≥ 960 species worldwide and is classified into three families: Ixodidae (hard ticks), Argasidae (soft ticks), and Nuttalliellidae (Socolovschi et al., 2009; Dantas-Torres, 2018; Guglielmone et al., 2020). These vectors have maintained long-standing ecological associations with bats, exhibiting diverse behavioral adaptations that allow them to inhabit nests, burrows, cave crevices, and, sometimes, human dwellings. Furthermore, several tick species commonly associated with bats and humans harbor and transmit pathogens, including zoonotic agents of significant global public health concerns (Lourenço et al., 2023).

Borrelia is a genus of Gram-negative spirochete bacteria comprising at least three major phylogenetic groups. Of these, two are responsible for several acute tick-borne diseases; one is Lyme disease, and the other is a relapsing fever. These represent a substantial public health burden due to their wide distribution and potential for severe clinical outcomes. A third phylogenetic group of Borrelia is associated with reptiles and echidnas. Although it shares an evolutionary ancestor with the members of the relapsing fever-causing clade, no pathogenicity has been reported in vertebrate hosts (Takano et al., 2010; Loh et al., 2017).

The Lyme disease group, referred to as Borrelia burgdorferi (sensu lato), is primarily transmitted by hard ticks, particularly those of the genus Ixodes. Recently, Margos et al. (2025) proposed classifying this group within the subgenus Borreliella. Lyme borreliosis is the most common tick-borne illness globally, with the highest prevalence reported in North America, Europe, and parts of Asia (Kurtenbach et al., 2006). The disease typically manifests as erythema migrans accompanied by a range of systemic symptoms (Wormser, 2006). In contrast, the relapsing fever Borrelia group comprises agents for which soft ticks of the genera Ornithodoros and Argas serve as the primary vectors (Hoogstraal, 1985; Kleinerman et al., 2021). Infections are characterized by recurrent episodes of fever. While relapsing fever remains a serious public health concern in Africa, it is also endemic to several other regions, including southern Europe, the Middle East, Central Asia, and North America (Cutler, 2006; Qiu et al., 2019).

Numerous studies have detected Borrelia spp. in bats and bat-associated ticks across multiple regions, including North America (Gill et al., 2008; Michalik et al., 2020; Colunga-Salas et al., 2021; Occi et al., 2023), South America (Jorge et al., 2023; López et al., 2023), Europe, e.g. Poland, Romania, and Sweden (Michalik et al., 2020; Jaenson and Wilhelmsson, 2021), and Australia (Verrett et al., 2025). In France, Borrelia spp. have been detected in ticks from human dwellings with bat roosting (Socolovschi et al., 2012). Although most reported lineages appear to circulate primarily within wildlife, some strains show close phylogenetic relationships to known human pathogens. More recently, genetically similar Borrelia strains were identified in bats and bat-associated ticks in Zambia, demonstrating close relatedness to isolates obtained from a human patient (Qiu et al., 2019). Collectively, these findings suggest that bat-associated ticks may serve as potential vectors of Borrelia, underscoring the need for further investigation into their zoonotic potential. However, many studies are limited by small sample sizes or examined either bats or ticks alone, rather than simultaneously (Evans et al., 2009; Schwan et al., 2009; Socolovschi et al., 2012; Piksa et al., 2016; Michalik et al., 2020; Colunga-Salas et al., 2021; Jaenson and Wilhelmsson, 2021). Moreover, unlike Borrelia infections in hard ticks, studies focusing on soft ticks parasitizing bats remain scarce. In Asia, only two studies, both from China, have examined Borrelia spp. in bats and associated ticks (Han et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021). Notably, although bat-associated soft ticks have been reported in Thailand (Tuangpermsub et al., 2025) and other Southeast Asian countries (Kazim et al., 2021), investigations of Borrelia spp. in bats and bat-infesting ticks in this region remain largely limited.

Thailand, a tropical biodiversity hotspot, supports at least 142 bat species nationwide, with estimates reaching 146 species (Karnpun et al., 2023). Despite this vast diversity, information on bat-infesting ticks and their potential role in Borrelia transmission in Thailand remains limited. While Borrelia species have been documented in rodents, dogs, and reptiles in the country, no data are currently available regarding their occurrence in bat populations or their associated ticks. Given reports of bat-associated ticks and Borrelia-infected ticks in other regions, we hypothesized that (i) bat-infesting soft ticks in Thailand may harbor relapsing fever group Borrelia, and (ii) bats may host distinct, potentially undescribed Borrelia lineages. However, the present study does not aim to establish clinical disease or transmission to humans. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to molecularly detect and genetically characterize Borrelia spp. in bat-infesting ticks and their bat hosts.

Materials and methods

Bat capture, species identification, and blood collection

This study was conducted as a part of a nationwide bat pathogen surveillance project described by Arnuphapprasert et al. (2020), Poofery et al. (2022), Riana et al. (2022), and Novianto et al. (2025). Briefly, bats were captured using mist nets, tunnel nets, and harp traps placed at cave entrances or along flight paths. Sampling was conducted from before sunset until early morning, with at least one trap deployed per site for three consecutive nights. Hand nets were additionally used to capture bats roosting inside caves. Bats were trapped from at least nine provinces across five geographical regions of Thailand between February 2018 and February 2023. Each captured bat was individually placed in a sterile cloth bag and identified to the species-level using standard morphological keys (Francis, 2008); if inconclusive, molecular identification was applied. Echolocation call analysis was employed when necessary. The geographical coordinates of each capture location were recorded. Blood samples and ectoparasites were collected from each bat before release. Blood samples were obtained by puncturing the uropatagial vein with a sterile 23–26G needle. Blood was collected into a sterile capillary tube coated with acid citrate dextrose anticoagulant solution (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and transferred onsite into sterile microcentrifuge tubes. A minimum of 20 μl was collected from each bat, except for individuals weighing < 2.5 g, to ensure animal welfare. Thin blood smears were prepared when sufficient blood remained in the capillary tube.

Tick collection and identification

Ticks were manually removed from the bats’ skin using forceps and preserved in sterile microcentrifuge tubes containing either 70% or absolute ethanol. Tick specimens were identified using a combination of morphological and molecular approaches. Morphological identification was performed using stereomicroscopy, light microscopy, and scanning electron microscopy, following standard taxonomic keys (Sonenshine et al., 1966; Dhanda and Rajagopalan, 1971; Keirans and Durden, 2005; Barros-Battesti et al., 2013). Molecular identification was conducted by targeting partial sequences of the 16S rRNA and cox1 genes, as well as whole mitochondrial genomes, as described by Tuangpermsub et al. (2025).

DNA extraction and detection of Borrelia spp. in blood and tick samples

Genomic DNA was extracted from blood samples using the NucleoSpin® Blood Kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions, but with a minor modification to the final elution volume (25 μl). DNA from individual ticks was also extracted with the NucleoSpin® Tissue Kit (Macherey-Nagel). The quality of bat-derived DNA was assessed in a representative subset of samples by PCR amplification of an approximately 651-bp fragment using the vertebrate-specific primers VF1d and VR1d (Ivanova et al., 2007; Kusuminda et al., 2022). Tick DNA quality was similarly evaluated by PCR targeting the mitochondrial 16S rRNA gene (Tuangpermsub et al., 2025).

After molecular identification of species at the larval stage, DNA samples from the same tick species were pooled by host depending on specimen abundance and availability, with 1–3 ticks per pool. Borrelia spp. was initially screened using nested PCR targeting the flagellin B (flaB) gene with primers described by Sato et al. (1997) and Takano et al. (2010), yielding an expected amplicon size of 345 bp. A Borrelia-positive control kindly provided by Dr Yongjin Qiu (Hokkaido University, Japan) was included, while nuclease-free water (Thermo Fisher Scientific, São Paulo, Brazil) served as the negative control. Samples testing positive were subsequently amplified for the glycerophosphodiester phosphodiesterase (glpQ) and 16S rRNA genes. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

The first-round PCR was performed in a total reaction volume of 12.5 μl containing 6.25 μl of 2× KOD Fx Neo Buffer, 2.5 μl of dNTPs (0.4 mM each), 0.375 μl of each primer (final concentration, 0.3 μM), 0.25 μl of KOD Fx Neo polymerase (Toyobo Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan), 1 μl of genomic DNA, and sterile distilled water to make up the volume. Amplicons were diluted 10-fold and served as templates for the second-round PCR. It was carried out in a 12.5 μl reaction mixture containing 1.25 μl of 10× Ex Taq Buffer, 1 μl of dNTPs (2.5 mM each), 0.25 μl of each primer (final concentration, 0.3 μM), 0.125 μl of TaKaRa Ex Taq™ polymerase (5 U/μl; Takara Bio Inc., Osaka, Japan), 2 μl of the diluted first-round amplicon, and sterile distilled water to make up the volume. Amplification was performed on a MiniAmp™ Thermal Cycler (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Amplicons (3 μl) were mixed with loading dye and separated by electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel at 100 V and 400 mA for 40 min. The bands were visualized under UV, and amplicons of the expected size were purified using the NucleoSpin® Gel and PCR Clean-Up Kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. They were Sanger sequenced at U2Bio Co., Ltd., Seoul, Republic of Korea.

For Borrelia-positive bats, thin blood smears were prepared and stained with 10% Giemsa solution (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) for 40 min. Slides were examined at 1000× magnification using an Eclipse Si RS upright microscope (Nikon Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an HD1210W-N digital camera (Lanoptik Technologies Ltd., Guangzhou, China).

Genetic diversity, pairwise genetic divergence, phylogeny, and statistical analysis

The forward and reverse chromatograms of all Borrelia sequences were visually inspected for quality control. Low-quality bases and primer-derived sequences at either end were trimmed using BioEdit version 7.0.5.3 (Hall, 1999). All protein-coding sequences were translated into peptides to confirm the absence of any internal stop codons. Preliminary species identification involved querying the edited nucleotide sequences against the NCBI database employing the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLASTn).

Sequences of the 16S rRNA, flaB, and glpQ genes generated in this study were analyzed using DNA Sequence Polymorphism version 6.12.03 (DnaSP; Rozas et al., 2017) to determine nucleotide sequence types (ntSTs) and assess genetic polymorphism. Genetic diversity indices: nucleotide diversity (π), the number of nucleotide sequence types (ntSTs), nucleotide sequence type diversity (Hd), the number of variable sites (VS), and the average number of nucleotide differences (κ) were calculated. The sequences were subsequently trimmed to ensure uniform lengths across all loci, resulting in final alignment lengths of 297 bp for flaB, 933 bp for 16S rRNA, and 670 bp for glpQ. Intra- and interspecific variations in the flaB and 16S rRNA fragments were recognized by estimating pairwise the genetic distances using the Kimura 2-parameter (K2P) substitution model implemented in MEGA version 11 (Tamura et al., 2021). The resulting distance matrices were visualized as heatmaps using the Morpheus platform.

Phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method implemented in IQ-TREE multicore version 1.6.12 (Trifinopoulos et al., 2016). Nodal support was evaluated with 1000 bootstrap replicates, and the software was selected because it employs a fast and efficient stochastic algorithm for ML phylogenetic reconstruction (Nguyen et al., 2015). The best-fitting nucleotide substitution models were selected employing ModelFinder. Specifically, TPM3+F+I+G4, TIM3e+I+G4, and TIM+F+G4 were identified as the optimal models for 16S rRNA, flaB, and glpQ, respectively. For analyzing the concatenated dataset, an edge-equal partition model was applied, allowing all partitions to share a common set of branch lengths. Borrelia infection prevalence in each bat species was calculated using the Clopper-Pearson exact binomial method implemented in SPSS Statistics version 30 (IBM Corp., NY, USA).

Results

Outcomes of bat and tick sampling and species identification

A total of 1031 bats were captured across nine provinces of Thailand between February 2018 and February 2023, representing 28 species, 11 genera, and 7 families. Sampling intensity varied every year, with the highest number of bats captured in 2020 (n = 327), followed by 2022 (n = 239), 2019 (n = 234), 2021 (n = 130), 2023 (n = 60), and 2018 (n = 41). The distribution of sampling sites and sample sizes by province was as follows: Nan (n = 5), Kanchanaburi (n = 850), Ratchaburi (n = 26), Phetchaburi (n = 4), Saraburi (n = 24), Nakhon Si Thammarat (n = 13), Songkhla (n = 7), Phang Nga (n = 33), and Trat (n = 69). A majority were captured at eight sampling sites in Kanchanaburi. Bats belonging to the genus Hipposideros comprised the largest proportion (n = 491, 47.6%). Tick infestation was detected in only two species: C. thonglongyai (33/139, 23.7%) and E. spelaea (1/2, 50%), with intensities ranging from 1 to 22 ticks per bat. All 96 soft tick larvae collected were identified as Reticulinasus sp.

Molecular detection and characterization of Borrelia spp. in bat blood samples

A total of 814 bats were screened for Borrelia by PCR targeting flaB; blood samples from the remaining 217 were unavailable. The distribution of screened samples by province was as follows: Nan (n = 5), Kanchanaburi (n = 660), Ratchaburi (n = 21), Phetchaburi (n = 4), Saraburi (n = 24), Nakhon Si Thammarat (n = 13), Songkhla (n = 7), Phang Nga (n = 33), and Trat (n = 47). In total, 13 individuals tested positive, yielding an overall detection rate of 1.6% (95% CI: 0.9–2.7%). Positive individuals belonged to three species: T. melanopogon (3/121, 2.5%; 95% CI: 0.5–7.1%), H. gentilis (7/225, 3.1%; 95% CI: 1.1–5.6%), and C. thonglongyai (3/76, 3.9%; 95% CI: 0.8–11.1%), representing the families Emballonuridae, Hipposideridae, and Craseonycteridae, respectively. The maximum detection rate was recorded in C. thonglongyai. Geographically, all infected bats belonged to Kanchanaburi. Only one Borrelia-positive C. thonglongyai was captured in the Dhepnimitra Cave, whereas the remaining infected individuals were collected from the Phra Cave (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Regarding seasonality, most positive detections occurred during the dry season; however, in 2020, infections were detected during the dry and rainy seasons. Detection rates stratified by bat species and sampling period are summarized in Table 2 and Supplementary Table S1. Notably, one Borrelia-positive C. thonglongyai individual was concurrently infested with Borrelia-positive ticks. In addition, putative spirochete-like organisms consistent with Borrelia spp. were identified by microscopic examination of Giemsa-stained blood smears from this host (Supplementary Fig. S2).

All 13 Borrelia-positive bat samples were successfully amplified and sequenced for the flaB gene. Of these, five samples were also successfully sequenced for the 16S rRNA gene (933 bp), while four yielded sequences for the glpQ gene (670 bp). Consequently, eight samples in this study were represented solely by flaB sequences. BLASTn analysis of the 294-bp flaB fragment classified the 13 positive samples into three distinct groups. The first group comprised a single sequence sharing 98.61% identity with B. hispanica detected in the tick Ornithodoros erraticus from Spain (GenBank: GU357612). The second group was most similar to “Candidatus Borrelia fainii” from Zambia (GenBank: AP027070), with homology values ranging from 99.31% to 99.65%. The third group exhibited lower similarity (80.41–84.07%) to uncultured Borrelia spp. detected in Uroderma sp. and Carollia perspicillata from Colombia (GenBank: ON650714 and MT154617) and in Pteropus alecto from Australia (GenBank: PQ492350 and PQ492351).

B. fainii” (GenBank: AP027070) and the other with 95.71–96.03% similarity with an unculturable Borrelia sp. detected in P. alecto from Australia (GenBank: PQ488732). Molecular detection and characterization of Borrelia spp. in bat-infesting ticks

Of the 48 pooled tick samples analyzed, four pools originating from three C. thonglongyai bats tested PCR-positive for Borrelia spp. flaB gene, corresponding to a minimum infection rate of 3.1% (95% CI: 0.7–8.9%). All pools of Borrelia-positive ticks were also positive for the three genetic markers, 16S rRNA (931 bp), flaB (294 bp), and glpQ (670 bp). Sequence comparison revealed that the 16S rRNA and flaB sequences shared the highest homology with “Ca. B. fainii” (GenBank: AP027070), with sequence identities ranging from 99.79% to 99.89% and 99.31% to 99.65%, respectively. Meanwhile, the glpQ sequences exhibited 99.40–99.50% similarity to Borrelia sp. from China (GenBank: MG921625), which was designated as “Ca. B. fainii”. Discussion

Bats provide essential ecosystem services and play a critical ecological role; however, they are recognized reservoirs of diverse pathogens, including Borrelia spp., a relationship documented more than a century ago (Verrett et al., 2025). In Thailand, molecular investigations into Borrelia have thus far been limited to ticks associated with rodents (Takhampunya et al., 2019, 2021, 2023), dogs (Sthitmatee et al., 2016), and reptiles (Trinachartvanit et al., 2016; Kaenkan et al., 2020). Therefore, this study provides the first molecular evidence of Borrelia spp. infection in bats and bat-infesting ticks in Thailand, expanding the known host range of these pathogens in the region. Nested PCR was employed as the primary screening method for Borrelia. Because only small volumes of blood could be collected from bats, blood smears were examined only for samples that tested positive by molecular screening to assess the presence of Borrelia spp. However, blood smear microscopy has substantially lower sensitivity than molecular diagnostic methods and is generally insufficient for detecting Borrelia spp. during periods of low bacteremia. In addition, these organisms can be difficult to reliably distinguish from staining artifacts, further limiting the diagnostic utility of conventional light microscopy alone.

Strict laws governing bat conservation in Thailand necessitated non-lethal sampling, with only minimal blood volumes collected before release. Moreover, due to the technical difficulty of handling extremely small tick larvae (0.8–1.5 mm in size) and the lack of prior data on Borrelia prevalence, tick specimens originating from the same host were pooled prior to DNA extraction to maximize overall DNA yield. Despite the small blood volumes and the use of pooled tick samples, nested PCR, which has been reported to be up to 1000 times more sensitive than conventional PCR (Picken et al., 1996; Rodrigues et al., 2013), was employed to increase the sensitivity of Borrelia detection. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that these constraints may have contributed to a lower observed prevalence compared with studies utilizing larger blood volumes, internal organs, or individually processed ticks. The Borrelia spp. detection rate observed in this study was lower than those reported in studies using larger blood volumes or internal organs in regions such as in Australia (Verrett et al., 2025), South America (Jorge et al., 2023), and other regions of the Americas, including Mexico (Colunga-Salas et al., 2021), Colombia (López et al., 2023), and the USA (Occi et al., 2023). However, it was slightly higher than that reported in studies in China that were conducted with relatively small sample sizes (Han et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021), suggesting potential geographical or ecological influences on Borrelia spp. circulation in bat populations. It should also be noted that this study investigated 28 bat species using a convenience sampling approach, representing approximately 20% of the currently recognized bat fauna of Thailand. Due to logistical constraints associated with capturing diverse species across multiple habitats, many taxa remain unsampled. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the overall diversity and distribution of Borrelia spp. in bats in Thailand.

Among the 28 bat species surveyed across nine provinces in Thailand, Borrelia was detected in only three species: T. melanopogon, C. thonglongyai, and H. gentilis. However, the small sample sizes for several species (n ≤ 5) resulted in limited statistical power and wide confidence intervals. Therefore, zero prevalence observed in these subsets does not exclude the presence of Borrelia in the broader population, and these findings should be interpreted as preliminary rather than evidence of pathogen absence. Notably, C. thonglongyai was frequently infested with larval ticks of the genus Reticulinasus. In this study, only the larval stage of ticks was collected, which posed a significant challenge for species-level identification. As previously discussed by Tuangpermsub et al. (2025), the absence of other life stages is likely related to the feeding behavior of soft ticks. While nymphs and adults typically feed for only 15–60 min before detaching, larvae may remain attached to the host for several hours to days. This observation is consistent with the known ecology of many nidicolous soft ticks, including members of the genus Reticulinasus, in which different life stages exhibit distinct host-seeking behaviors. Larvae commonly parasitize bats during roosting, whereas nymphs and adults typically remain within the roost environment (e.g. cave walls, crevices, or guano substrates) and feed intermittently on available hosts. Therefore, the absence of nymphal and adult stages on captured bats likely reflects the biology and feeding behavior of nidicolous soft ticks rather than a sampling limitation. Consequently, the inability to identify these ticks at the species level represents a limitation in definitively linking the detected Borrelia lineages to a specific vector species. Further taxonomic work, including the establishment of voucher specimens representing all life stages, is required to clarify vector-host-pathogen associations within this system. With respect to other bat species with tick infestations, our previous study identified a host preference of Reticulinasus sp. ticks for E. spelaea. The ecological behavior of E. spelaea, which inhabits forests and agricultural edge habitats and typically roosts near cave entrances, likely increases its exposure to crawling soft ticks. Nevertheless, Borrelia was not detected in E. spelaea in this study, which may be attributable to the limited sample size examined for this species. Finally, the apparent geographical clustering of positive cases in Kanchanaburi Province should be interpreted with caution. The bat species found to be infected were sampled only in this region, preventing robust statistical comparisons among geographical locations.

The Borrelia sequences obtained were classified into three distinct genetic groups, each likely representing infections derived from a single host species. Borrelia hispanica infects bats experimentally; Angulo (1945) demonstrated infection in four bat species following inoculation with B. hispanica-positive guinea pig blood. Subsequently, B. hispanica has been sporadically detected in humans and in O. erraticus ticks from Spain (Toledo et al., 2010). “Ca. B. fainii” was first identified in Zambia in a patient, three bat species (Rousettus aegyptiacus, Hipposideros sp., and Miniopterus sp.) and the tick Reticulinasus fainii (Qiu et al., 2019). This species was later detected in Rhinolophus pusillus and Myotis davidii in China (Han et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021), indicating a broader geographical distribution across at least two continents.

Both “Ca. B. fainii” and B. hispanica cluster within the relapsing fever Borrelia group. Consistent with this finding, our phylogenetic analyses showed that Group G1, detected in T. melanopogon, formed a sister lineage to B. hispanica, whereas Group G2, obtained from C. thonglongyai and Reticulinasus sp. ticks infesting this host, formed a sister clade to “Ca. B. fainii”, exhibiting modest genetic divergence across the three loci investigated. These findings support the hypothesis that bats may serve as reservoir hosts for potentially zoonotic Borrelia species. Moreover, Reticulinasus spp. ticks may play a vital role as vectors, facilitating transmission among bats and potentially to other animals and humans. However, vector competence was not experimentally evaluated in this study. In contrast, Group G3, which was detected in T. melanopogon and H. gentilis, formed a distinct and well-supported clade separate from bat-associated Borrelia groups reported in Australia (Verrett et al., 2025), Brazil (Jorge et al., 2023; Polli et al., 2024), and Colombia (Muñoz-Leal et al., 2021). Notably, flaB sequences within this clade exhibited substantial intraspecific divergence (0–18.01%) and pronounced genetic distances (16.23–23.04%) relative to other bat-associated Borrelia lineages.

Notably, nucleotide polymorphism analysis of 17 flaB sequences derived from bats (n = 13) and bat-infesting ticks (n = 4) revealed a relatively high overall nucleotide diversity (π = 0.138) across groups G1-G3. This elevated diversity likely reflects the presence of three clearly distinct phylogenetic groups, as well as the additional variability introduced by sequences obtained from pooled tick samples. Currently, no universally accepted genetic distance threshold exists for species delineation within the genus Borrelia. However, Pritt et al. (2016) reported that intraspecific genetic divergence of flaB in B. burgdorferi typically does not exceed 3%. Furthermore, Picken (1992) demonstrated that even distinct species from different groups, such as B. burgdorferi and B. hermsii, exhibit a genetic distance of approximately 15% within a 276-bp fragment of the flaB gene. Therefore, the high genetic distances observed within lineage G3 suggest that these isolates represent highly divergent lineages, potentially comprising more than one distinct species. This observation is consistent with the findings of Muñoz-Leal et al. (2021), who described a “Ca. B. perspicillata”-associated Borrelia lineage from Macaregua Cave in Colombia. Although that lineage is phylogenetically associated with the Lyme disease Borrelia group, it exhibited notable nucleotide divergence (1.94–9.81%), supporting its recognition as a novel taxon. Despite these findings, the zoonotic potential and transmission dynamics of bat-associated Borrelia spp. remain to be investigated. Nevertheless, the taxonomic status of lineage G3 should be interpreted with caution. The dataset in the present study is limited, and the analyses are based on relatively short sequence fragments. Consequently, formal species designation would be premature without additional evidence, such as multilocus sequence typing (MLST), bacterial isolation, or whole-genome characterization. In this context, lineage G3 is best regarded as a highly divergent or potentially cryptic lineage within the bat-associated Borrelia clade rather than a formally defined species. Furthermore, the zoonotic potential and transmission dynamics of bat-associated Borrelia remain largely unknown and require further investigation. In the present study, no ticks were detected on T. melanopogon or H. gentilis. Although ticks and lice are recognized vectors of Borrelia spp., other potential vectors remain largely unexplored. For example, the tick I. collaris has been reported to parasitize Hipposideros pomona in Vietnam (Hornok et al., 2016). Additionally, the three bat species identified as Borrelia-positive in this study have been reported to host bat flies (Novianto et al., 2025). This observation suggests that other blood-feeding ectoparasites may also play a role in Borrelia transmission among bats and warrant further investigation.

To date, only a single study from Korea has investigated bat flies as potential vectors of Borrelia spp., reporting entirely negative results (Lee et al., 2021). Furthermore, there are currently no confirmed reports of Borrelia spp. infection in other blood-feeding ectoparasites associated with bats. Consequently, the vector competence of ectoparasites such as bat flies remains unresolved and warrants further investigation through both epidemiological surveillance and experimental studies. From an ecological perspective, the prevalence of Borrelia spp., as vector-borne bacteria, is strongly influenced by the presence and abundance of competent vectors, such as bat-associated ticks. However, in the present study, the highly uneven sample sizes across bat species, ranging from a single individual to more than 100 individuals, combined with the overall low prevalence detected, limit the ability to characterize the ecological dynamics between Borrelia spp. and its bat hosts. Therefore, additional studies with more balanced sampling across host species are required to better understand these host-vector-pathogen relationships.

The genetic markers most widely used for Borrelia spp. identification include the 16S rRNA gene, rrs (particularly V3 and/or V4 regions), and flaB. In this study, pathogens were initially screened using flaB, followed by 16S rRNA and glpQ. Phylogenetic and pairwise distance analyses suggested that flaB exhibits greater genetic variability, whereas 16S rRNA is highly conserved. These findings are consistent with reports indicating that flaB provides superior species-level discriminatory power within the relapsing fever Borrelia group compared with 16S rRNA (Scott, 2005; Taylor et al., 2025). glpQ serves as a molecular marker for the relapsing fever Borrelia group, as it is absent in Lyme disease-associated Borrelia spp. (Schwan et al., 2003). Moreover, glpQ may provide additional resolution for species discrimination when results from 16S rRNA and flaB analyses are ambiguous. Nevertheless, the genetic markers used in this study consisted of relatively short partial sequences, which provide limited resolution for definitive species delineation. Although concatenated gene sequences can enhance phylogenetic signal and improve discriminatory power, they may still be insufficient to unequivocally confirm species identity in highly divergent lineages, thereby necessitating additional genetic markers. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) have emerged as robust approaches for species and strain differentiation within the genus Borrelia, particularly given the challenges associated with its species’ slow growth and the difficulty of primary isolation. The MLST scheme for Borrelia spp. typically includes eight housekeeping genes, offering high discriminatory power for strains while maintaining a reliable framework for inferring deep evolutionary relationships and stable genetic lineages (Margos et al., 2008). Nevertheless, genotypic data are insufficient to assess bacterial virulence and zoonotic potential. To date, no confirmed clinical cases of human Borrelia infection have been reported in Thailand. Consequently, further epidemiological investigations, including bacterial isolation and experimental characterization, are required. Additionally, a comprehensive understanding of transmission dynamics and ecological interactions among Borrelia spp., bats, and their associated vectors is essential for determining the potential risk of zoonotic emergence.

Conclusions

This study provides the first molecular evidence of Borrelia spp. circulating in bats and bat-infesting ticks in Thailand, identifying three distinct genetic lineages. Our findings highlight two important observations: (i) the detection of two Borrelia lineages phylogenetically related to the zoonotic relapsing fever Borrelia group, suggesting potential public health relevance; and (ii) the identification of a highly divergent bat-associated Borrelia lineage, which may indicate cryptic speciation or previously underestimated biodiversity in this region. However, the use of relatively short sequence fragments limits the resolution required for definitive species delineation. Furthermore, because no clinical cases of human Borrelia spp. infection have yet been reported in Thailand, the virulence, host range, and vector competence of these lineages remain unknown. Nevertheless, these findings provide an important foundation for future investigations aimed at confirming species taxonomy and evaluating the potential risk of pathogen spillover. Future research should prioritize bacterial isolation, MLST or WGS, and experimental infection studies, together with integrated surveillance encompassing bat, tick, and human populations.
