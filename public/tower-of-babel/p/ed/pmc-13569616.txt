Endoscopic Transnasal Apicoectomy for Odontogenic Maxillary Sinusitis
Authors: Kenta Fukui, Hiromitsu Hatakeyama, Riku Ando, Akito Ichikawa, Yamato Oki, Yu Matsumoto, Hikaru Takasu, Nobuhiko Oridate
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Endoscopic transnasal apicoectomy (ETA) is a novel multidisciplinary technique that combines endoscopic sinus surgery with apicoectomy via a transnasal approach. ETA enables direct access to periapical lesions while preserving the causative tooth. Our initial experience suggests that this minimally invasive approach is safe, feasible, and effective for selected cases of odontogenic maxillary sinusitis.

Introduction

No standardized international guidelines currently exist for the management of odontogenic maxillary sinusitis (OMS). According to a recent comprehensive review by Craig et al. [1], OMS should be managed through a multidisciplinary approach that starts with appropriate dental treatment of the responsible tooth, followed—when necessary—by endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS) to improve ventilation and drainage of the maxillary sinus. In clinical practice, this stepwise approach is widely used, although the optimal timing and combination of dental and sinonasal treatments are still debated.

Recent Japanese reports have shown that almost 90% of OMS cases arise from teeth that have already undergone root canal treatment [2]. This trend is also seen in international studies, where failed or previously treated root canal therapy is the most common dental source of OMS, although the exact percentage differs among reports [1]. Persistent apical periodontitis after root canal treatment is an important factor worldwide, and such recurrent lesions often lead clinicians to choose extraction rather than nonsurgical retreatment, especially when anatomical or restorative problems exist [1].

However, tooth extraction can negatively affect a patient's quality of life and should be selected carefully.

For this reason, we developed a new surgical technique, Endoscopic Transnasal Apicoectomy (ETA), in collaboration with dentists. This technique allows transnasal endoscopic dental treatment to be performed together with ESS for the management of OMS.

Methods

This report describes five treated sides and focuses on the technical aspects and feasibility of a newly developed surgical technique, Endoscopic Transnasal Apicoectomy (ETA), for odontogenic maxillary sinusitis.

This study included four patients (five sides) with OMS who underwent ETA at our hospital between October 2024 and September 2025.

The indications for ETA were diagnosis of OMS, the causative tooth having undergone previous root canal treatment, no absolute indication for extraction, and strong patient preference for tooth preservation.

OMS was diagnosed based on maxillary sinus shadow on CT and a periapical lesion of maxillary tooth (Figure 1).

Absolute indications for extraction included severe dental caries (C4), advanced periodontitis with Grade 3 mobility, root fracture, severe alveolar bone loss, or periapical radiolucency involving multiple teeth, as determined by our oral and maxillofacial surgery team.

Before surgery, a three‐dimensional model was prepared by the dental team, and a joint conference was held to plan the drilling site.

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia. After palpating the bony edge of the piriform aperture, a vertical mucosal incision is made at the anterior edge of the inferior turbinate using needle cautery (Figure 2a). The mucosa of the inferior meatus is then elevated along the lateral nasal wall, swinging the nasolacrimal duct laterally. The lateral wall of the inferior meatus is then drilled to access the maxillary sinus (Figure 2b).

(a) An incision is made at the anterior edge of the inferior turbinate using an electric scalpel. (b) The lateral wall of the inferior meatus is removed to access the maxillary sinus. (c) The position of the causative tooth is confirmed using a navigation system. (d) The maxillary sinus mucosa is elevated, and the periapical lesion of the causative tooth is identified (blue arrow). (e) The periapical lesion and root apex are drilled and removed. (f) Root canal filling material in the mesial buccal root is confirmed (blue arrowhead). (g) Additional drilling is performed to identify the distal buccal root and ensure adequate resection (red arrowhead). (h) The mucosal incision is sutured to complete the procedure.

Using the navigation system, the exact position of the periapical lesion of the causative tooth is confirmed (Figure 2c). The maxillary sinus mucosa is elevated to expose the lesion (Figure 2d). As drilling continues, yellowish dentin gradually becomes visible, followed by the appearance of the root canal filling material (Figure 2e–g).

The periapical lesion is removed, and the root apex is resected to a depth of 3 mm beyond the tip. Finally, the mucosal incision is sutured with 5–0 Vicryl to complete the procedure (Figure 2h).

All patients were followed postoperatively according to the standard protocol for ESS at our institution. Outpatient visits were scheduled at 2 weeks, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months after surgery, during which nasal endoscopic examinations and routine postoperative nasal care were performed. Depending on clinical findings, antihistamines and intranasal corticosteroids were prescribed as needed.

The following items were evaluated: patient background, operative time, blood loss, postoperative complications, and clinical course. Clinical evaluation included both preoperative and postoperative assessments. SNOT‐22 and the Geriatric Oral Health Assessment Index (GOHAI) were obtained preoperatively and at 1, 3, and 6 months after surgery. Computed tomography (CT) imaging was performed preoperatively for diagnosis and again at 6 months postoperatively to evaluate sinus aeration and the surgical site.

This study was approved by the institutional review board of our institution, and informed consent was obtained from all patients.

The causative teeth ranged from the second premolar to the second molar. All sites were accessible with drills angled 15°–70°, and both buccal and palatal roots were reached through the transnasal route.

The additional operative time required for ETA beyond standard ESS was about 57 min, with no increase in blood loss.

All patients were discharged within the same postoperative period typically required for standard ESS, with no delays in recovery.

The follow‐up period ranged from 5 to 6 months. Three patients completed 6 months of follow‐up, while one patient had reached 5 months at the time of manuscript preparation. During the available follow‐up period, CT imaging demonstrated improvement of maxillary sinus opacification, and no recurrence or need for extraction was observed (Figure 3).

Postoperative complications were specifically assessed, including facial swelling, nasal bleeding, sensory disturbances, tooth mobility, and persistent pain. No cases of significant postoperative bleeding, facial swelling requiring intervention, sensory disturbance, or tooth mobility were observed. One patient experienced mild tooth pain, which resolved spontaneously within 1 month without additional treatment.

Discussion

Although the standard treatment strategy for OMS is dental treatment followed by ESS if needed [1], this approach has limitations. Recent studies show that most OMS cases arise from teeth that have already undergone root canal treatment, reducing the chance of successful nonsurgical retreatment [1, 2]. This situation highlights the need for alternatives that can treat periapical lesions while preserving the tooth.

Recurrent disease after root canal treatment may be managed by retreatment, apicoectomy, or extraction.

Nonsurgical retreatment is minimally invasive, but success decreases in apical periodontitis, particularly for large lesions [3]. It may also be unsuitable when restorations cannot be removed or when root fracture risk exists [3].

Apicoectomy removes the root apex and periapical lesion through a gingival approach, but it is relatively invasive. Also, conventional apicoectomy gives limited access to the maxillary molars, especially the palatal root. For these reasons, as well as the risk of persistent disease, extraction is often chosen [4].

Tooth extraction is invasive and may cause bleeding, dry socket, neuropathic symptoms, and reduced quality of life. Loss of teeth is also associated with impaired mastication and swallowing, particularly in older patients.

In recent years, several reports propose ESS before dental intervention to preserve the causative tooth. Sato et al. reported tooth preservation in approximately 98% using ESS with postoperative antibiotics [2].

Similar findings have been reported internationally, suggesting that restoration of sinus ventilation and drainage through ESS may support infection control and contribute to tooth preservation in selected patients [1].

However, numerous studies have shown that periodontal disease and oral infection are associated with various systemic conditions, particularly diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cerebrovascular events [5]. These systemic associations underscore the importance of providing appropriate dental management in addition to treating odontogenic maxillary sinusitis.

To our knowledge, transnasal endoscopic apicoectomy has not been clearly described as a unified technique for OMS. This report provides a technical description of this approach.

The ETA technique allows preservation of the tooth by performing apicoectomy endoscopically through the nasal cavity during ESS. Performing the procedure under general anesthesia reduces the burden compared with apicoectomy or extraction under local anesthesia. As noted earlier, conventional apicoectomy has limited access to maxillary molars, but ETA enables direct access.

The steps are based on ESS, making ETA easy to learn for surgeons familiar with sinonasal surgery. Navigation also helps identify the affected root.

Conventional apicoectomy may cause swelling, pain, sensory disturbance, and tooth mobility [4]. In this study, only mild transient tooth pain occurred.

These preliminary findings suggest that ETA may represent a technically feasible alternative in selected patients.

However, the long‐term outcomes of ETA remain unclear. Because at least 1 year of follow‐up is recommended after apicoectomy [4], longer observation is necessary.

This initial series has important limitations, including the small number of cases, short follow‐up (5–6 months), and absence of retrograde obturation. In conventional endodontic surgery, retrograde filling is critical for sealing the root canal system; however, it was technically challenging via the transnasal approach due to anatomical and instrumental constraints.

Accordingly, ETA should be interpreted as an early technical feasibility report rather than a definitive alternative to established endodontic surgery. Further technical refinement, additional cases, and longer‐term follow‐up are warranted.

Conclusion

ETA is a new, minimally invasive technique developed through collaboration between otolaryngology and dentistry. It enables transnasal access to the root apex during ESS and may contribute to tooth preservation in selected patients. Further clinical experience and long‐term follow‐up are required to define its role in the management of OMS.
