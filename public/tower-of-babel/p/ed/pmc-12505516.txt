DeepRNA-Reg: a deep-learning based approach for comparative analysis of CLIP experiments
Authors: Harshaan Sekhon, Robin Kageyama, Neil T. Sprenkle, Hannah C. Happ, Eric J. Wigton, Heather H. Pua, K. Mark Ansel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDeepRNA-Reg employs advances in deep learning to enable high-fidelity comparative analysis of paired datasets of high-throughput sequencing of RNA isolated by crosslinking immunoprecipitation (HITS-CLIP). In a HITS-CLIP experimental paradigm where Ago2 targeting is selectively perturbed via gene knock-out of a microRNA cluster, DeepRNA-Reg offers a superior prediction set when compared with the current best prescription for differential HITS-CLIP analysis. Furthermore, DeepRNA-Reg predictions adhered better to the ground-truth of RNA primary and secondary structural motifs that enable miRNA-mediated targeting of RNA. In the tested data sets, DeepRNA-Reg uncovered novel mediators in the mechanism of microRNA-mediated restraint of type-2 immunity in T-Helper 2 cells. In a comparative analysis, DeepRNA-Reg predictions show greater translatability across distinct biological milieux, offering prediction sets with wide applicability for investigators.

Background

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) have a large impact on the behaviour of coding and non-coding RNAs, directing complex networks of post-transcriptional gene regulation [1–3]. For example, post-transcriptional regulation shapes a large proportion of the gene expression changes that occur upon T cell activation, helping to reprogram these cells as they enter an immune response [4]. Technologies that measure specific or global RBP occupancy transcriptome-wide have been used effectively and systematically to identify biologically important post-transcriptional gene regulatory networks [5–7]. High-throughput sequencing of RNA isolated by crosslinking immunoprecipitation (HITS-CLIP) enables the identification of RNA interactions for a specific RBP at nucleotide-level resolution [8–10]. Paired CLIP experiments can reveal the differential behaviour of RBPs under varying experimental conditions [11]. Despite the need for robust algorithms to call and quantify differential RBP binding, few computational tools have been created for the comparative analysis of HITS-CLIP experiments [12,13]. The first computational approach designed to solve this problem relied on a qualitative procedure for the determination of differential enrichment, entailing first calling peaks in HITS-CLIP data and then designating uniquely called peaks as differentially bound [12]. A more quantitative approach was provided by dCLIP, employing a hidden Markov model and Viterbi algorithm to call differential enrichment [13]. Since its inception, dCLIP has provided a valuable modality for differential HITS-CLIP analysis with high reliability and performance. Yet opportunities remained to improve the sensitivity and precision of detecting differential enrichment. Here, we present DeepRNA-Reg, an algorithm that takes advantage of deep learning technology to offer a robust, orthologous approach to comparative HITS-CLIP analysis.

Results

Benchmarking design

The DeepRNA-Reg algorithm utilizes deep learning to predict differentially enriched sites in paired HITS-CLIP data sets, employing a recurrent neural network to infer differential enrichment status across a given region of the transcriptome (Figure 1(A)). To probe the efficacy of DeepRNA-Reg, we tested its ability to detect and localize microRNA (miRNA) target sites using AGO2 HITS-CLIP (AHC) data from T cells lacking specific miRNAs. We performed comparative AHC on in vitro differentiated mouse wildtype (WT) T helper type 2 (Th2) cells and ‘miR-23, 24, 27 KO’ Th2 cells bearing targeted deletions of both Mirc11 (miR-23a/27a/24–2) and Mirc22 (miR-23b/27b/24–1), the genomic miRNA clusters that encode all members of the miR-23, miR-24, and miR-27 families. These clusters and their corresponding mature miRNAs are both strongly expressed in T cells, and miR-23, 24, 27 KO T cells exhibit enhanced Th2 differentiation and cytokine production [14,15]. Both DeepRNA-Reg and dCLIP 1.7 were executed on the paired AHC data generated using WT and miR-23, 24, 27 KO Th2 cells (Supplementary Tables S1-4). This benchmarking analysis uses the set of genomic loci consisting of all defined 3’UTR of genes in the UCSC mouse database. Overlapping 3’UTR annotations were collapsed to singly defined 3’UTRs to prevent multiple entries for alternatively spliced or alternatively polyadneylated regions of the transcriptome. Benchmarking analyses were further replicated using comparative AHC data from in vitro differentiated WT Th17 cells and ‘miR-29ab1 KO’ Th17 cells bearing a conditional mutant allele of Mirc33, which encodes miR-29a and miR-29b-1, sharply reducing the expression of miR-29 [16,17] (Supplementary Tables S5-8). Benchmarking I: sensitivity

MicroRNA binding is strongly influenced by Watson-Crick base pairing between the miRNA ‘seed’ region (nucleotides 2–8) and complementary seed binding sequences in target sites within mRNAs [18]. As a first metric to gauge the efficacy of DeepRNA-Reg and dCLIP to identify Ago2 binding at miRNA target sites, we compared the number of miRNA seed binding sequences that fall within regions of increased binding enrichment within the WT condition, relative to the corresponding miRNA KO condition. We reasoned that an algorithm that selectively captures more such sequences correctly identifies more miRNA-bound sites of potential biological relevance. DeepRNA-Reg identified a significantly larger set of predicted target sites containing seed binding sequences than dCLIP. The DeepRNA-Reg set of targets with enriched Ago2 binding in WT Th2 cells – as compared with miR-23,24,27 KO Th2 cells – contained > 80% more canonical (6 nt. − 8 nt. match) seed binding sequences complementary to miR-23, miR-24, or miR-27 than did the dCLIP prediction set (Figure 1(B)). Similarly, the DeepRNA-Reg prediction set of differential AHC enrichment in WT Th17 cells as compared with miR-29ab1 KO Th17 cells contained more canonical miR-29 seed binding sequences than the prediction set generated by dCLIP (Figure 1(C)). For all four miRNAs, DeepRNA-Reg also captured a higher number of perfect 8-mer seed binding target sequences, which offer the strongest sequence-based prediction for miRNA regulation of target gene expression [18].

To further assess the accuracy of target site detection, we analysed the distribution of predicted miRNA target sites in the Targetscan 7.2 database among the binding predictions of DeepRNA-Reg and dCLIP. In addition to classifying target sites based on the number of matching seed nucleotides, the Targetscan 7.2 database of likely miRNA targets incorporates additional sequence-based information including supplementary base pairing, local A/U richness, and phylogenetic conservation [19]. For both the Th2 and Th17 differential AHC analyses, DeepRNA-Reg predictions of increased binding enrichment in the WT condition identified the vast majority of dCLIP captured TargetScan sites; furthermore, the DeepRNA-Reg prediction set significantly expanded the number of captured TargetScan sites. Within the set of sites called to be enriched in the WT condition relative to the miR-23, 24, 27 KO condition in Th2 cells, the DeepRNA-Reg prediction set overlapped 306 out of 341 TargetScan predictions captured by dCLIP, while significantly expanding beyond the latter set by capturing an additional 293 Targetscan predictions (Figure 1(D)). Similarly, within the set of sites called to be enriched in the WT condition relative to the miR-29ab1 KO condition in Th17 cells, DeepRNA-Reg predictions overlapped 65 out of 78 Targetscan-predicted miR-29 targets captured in the dCLIP prediction set, adding an additional 119 Targetscan predictions not captured by dCLIP (Figure 1(E)). Thus, DeepRNA-Reg expanded upon the Ago2 binding predictions of dCLIP, offering more sites of potential biological relevance while maintaining the integrity of the dCLIP prediction set.

Benchmarking II: precision

Thus far, the benchmarking analysis treated all differential enrichment predictions equally. However, predictions that localize smaller genomic regions offer more precision than predictions localizing larger regions. Furthermore, since larger annotations have a higher probability of capturing miRNA seed binding sequences, an algorithm that predicts very large regions may appear to contain more biologically relevant annotations by chance alone. For the set of sites predicted to be enriched in WT Th2 cells – relative to the miR-23,24,27 KO Th2 cells – DeepRNA-Reg predictions are, on average, smaller than those of dCLIP (Figure 2(A)). These findings held in our replicate differential AHC analysis for miR-29ab1 KO and WT Th17 cells (Figure 2(B)). Furthermore, for both analyses, the size distribution for the set of DeepRNA-Reg predictions shows significantly less variance than the size distribution for the set of dCLIP predictions (Figure 2(A,B); miR-23,24,27 paradigm: F15219, 44,517 = 2.41, p < 2.2e-16; miR-29 paradigm: F11446, 47,168 = 2.56, p < 2.2e-16). Within the set of sites called to be enriched in the WT condition relative to the miRNA KO condition, DeepRNA-Reg predictions also showed significantly greater centring of canonical and 8-mer seed binding sequences for miR-23 and miR-27 in Th2 cells (Figure 2C), and for miR-29 in Th17 cells (Figure 2(D)) as measured by the Mann Whitney U Test. Together, the DeepRNA-Reg prediction set localized smaller regions with less size variability and greater centring of prediction sites, increasing the precision of target site predictions. Benchmarking III: experimental validation

Abundant miRNAs bind and regulate dozens or even hundreds of target mRNAs [20]. Nevertheless, the differential Ago2 binding predictions returned by both algorithms certainly include some false positive predictions. To assess each algorithm’s ability to provide a prediction set with minimal false positives, we ordered predictions based on a measure of differential enrichment. The measure provided by DeepRNA-Reg will hereinafter be referred to as ‘DBE’ (Differential Binding Enrichment). A similar measure provided by dCLIP reports for each site the normalized difference of the tag intensities between the two conditions, which will be referred to as the ‘DBE’ as well.

A critical test of each algorithm’s quantification of differential binding enrichment is their concordance with miRNA-dependent changes in mRNA expression. Ideally, genes bearing loci in 3’ UTR that show greater AHC read enrichment in the WT condition relative to the KO condition should also show greater inhibition of expression upon exposure to the miRNA(s) of interest. To test this expectation and benchmark both algorithms’ performance, we utilized gene profiling data from miRNA-deficient (Dgcr8Δ/Δ Tbx21-/-) Th2 cells transfected with oligonucleotide miRNA mimics for miR-23a, miR-24, miR-27a, or a control mimic [14]. For this benchmarking analysis, genes were ascribed a DBE sum, defined as the sum of the DBE for all loci called to be differentially enriched within their 3’UTRs. Next, genes were partitioned by the percentile rank of this DBE measure. As expected, gene expression changes induced by each miRNA correlated with the DBE sum. For each miRNA, the top 10% (DBE percentile 90–100) of differentially bound genes predicted by DeepRNA-Reg and dCLIP showed similar miRNA-induced reduction in expression (Figure 3(A–C)). Notably, however, DeepRNA-Reg offers almost twice as many predictions at this confidence level for each miRNA. Benchmarking the efficacy of measures of differential binding enrichment.

As another approach to interrogate the efficacy of each algorithm’s quantification of differential binding enrichment, we probed the enrichment of seed sequence motifs in successively more restrictive subsets of each algorithm’s prediction set, defined by a cut-off for DBE. In the Th2 comparative AHC analysis, for successively more restrictive subsets of the DeepRNA-Reg prediction set based on a cut-off for the DBE percentile, there is a strong trend of greater enrichment of seed binding sequence motifs for miRNAs 23, 24, and 27 (Figure 3(D)). This trend was less clear for similar subsets of the dCLIP prediction set (Figure 3(E)). These findings hold in a comparison of both algorithm’s prediction sets of the WT Th17 vs. miR-29 KO comparative AHC analysis (Figure 3(F)). To analyse this trend from another perspective, we next probed whether differentially bound regions containing one or more relevant miRNA seed binding sequence motifs also tend to achieve higher DBE measures. DeepRNA-Reg returned predictions with successively higher distributions of DBE measures for regions containing one canonical miR-23, miR-24, or miR-27 binding motif, one perfect 8-mer motif, more than one canonical motif, and more than one 8-mer motif. This trend was present but less pronounced in the distribution of DBE measures defined in the dCLIP prediction set (Figure 3(G)). The same pattern was also observed for miR-29 target sites revealed in the differential AHC analysis in Th17 cells (Figure 3(H)). In conclusion, DBE defined by DeepRNA-Reg showed greater concordance with the biological outcome of miRNA targeting events and the presence of biologically relevant features in the primary RNA sequence, than did the definition of DBE by dCLIP.

MicroRNA regulation of Th2 cells

Prior research uncovered a regulatory network connecting the co-expressed miRNAs, miR-24 and miR-27, with the differentiation of Th2 cells and their production of lineage-defining cytokines, including Interleukin-4 (IL-4) [14,15]. Functionally relevant direct target genes identified without the advantage of comparative AHC included Gata3 and Ikzf1, which encode transcription factors that regulate the Il4 locus, the Il4 gene itself, and several other genes previously unconnected to Th2 differentiation or cytokine production. We used DeepRNA-Reg to further investigate this regulatory network to validate endogenous miR-24 and miR-27-dependent Ago2 binding to known target transcripts and to uncover novel relevant target genes acted upon by these miRNAs.

DeepRNA-Reg readily detected differential Ago2 binding at miR-27 canonical binding sites in the 3’ UTRs of previously established target mRNAs encoding Ikzf1, Gata3, Aff4 and Gpr174 (Figure 4(A–D)). Similarly, differential Ago2 binding was detected at canonical miR-24 binding sites in Ikzf1 (Figure 4(A)), Cnot6, and Clcn3 (Figure 4(E,F)). Two additional previously characterized targets, Ccnk and Galnt3, appeared to contain differentially enriched sites with canonical seed binding motifs by DeepRNA-Reg, but insufficient signal due to low mRNA counts led to low DBE measures, thereby excluding these genes from the set of high confidence predictions. For Gpr174, we observed a differentially enriched site at the miR-27 canonical binding motif, but not at the canonical miR-24 binding motif (Figure 4(B)). Ago2 binding was also observed at the miR-24 seed-binding sequence immediately following the translation stop codon of Il4, but this binding was equally detected in miR-23, 24, 27 KO Th2 cells, suggesting that another miRNA can attract Ago2 to this site. Overall, DeepRNA-Reg analysis of comparative AHC in primary Th2 cells verified several functionally relevant direct targets of miR-24 and miR-27 and identified specific binding sequences occupied by these miRNAs. To determine whether DeepRNA-Reg could identify new direct miRNA targets, we next compiled a set of probable miR-24 and miR-27 differentially bound targets within the DeepRNA-Reg analysis of WT compared to miR-23,24,27 KO Th2 cells, filtering the prediction set for 3’UTR regions having a DBE measure above the 50th percentile and a canonical motif for either miR-24 or miR-27. We then cross-referenced this prediction set with the aforementioned RNA-sequencing gene expression data from Dgcr8Δ/Δ Tbx21-/- Th2 cells transfected with miR-23a, miR-24, miR-27a, or a control mimic (Supplementary Tables S9-11). This intersection yielded a subset of high-confidence target genes that showed a decrease in expression of 15% or greater in cells transfected with the miR-24 or miR-27 mimic relative to control mimic.

The list of high-confidence DeepRNA-Reg predicted miR-24 and miR-27 targets was then intersected with the Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) knowledgebase of IL-4 regulators to reveal those genes regulated by miR-24 and/or miR-27 that are documented upstream regulators of Il4 expression (Figure 5(A)). This analysis uncovered a suite of transcriptional regulators that can affect IL-4 expression, including Gata3 and Ikzf1 but also Rora, Tcf7, Runx1, Bcl11b, Foxo1, Nfat5, Tsc22d3 and Klf11 (Figure 5(A,B)). In addition, the analysis uncovered the cell surface costimulatory receptor CD28 as a novel target of miR-24 and miR-27. CD28 is a key regulator of T cell activation, differentiation and cytokine production [21]. The Cd28 mRNA contains evolutionarily conserved canonical miR-27 and miR-24 binding motifs, both of which displayed differential enrichment for Ago2 binding in the DeepRNA-Reg comparison of WT and miR-23,24,27 KO Th2 cells (Figure 5(C)). Flow cytometric analysis of CD28 protein expression confirmed that miR-23,24,27 KO Th2 cells express a larger quantity of CD28 on their cell surface, consistent with direct targeting by miR-24 and/or miR-27 (Figure 5(D)). These findings demonstrate that DeepRNA-Reg analysis of comparative Ago2 HITS-CLIP data can both confirm prior knowledge and uncover novel nodes in post-transcriptional regulatory networks. Investigation of RNA-secondary structure at miRNA-target sites

Local A/U richness plays an important role in facilitating the recognition of miRNA seed binding sequences in mRNA by the RISC complex [22]. The TargetScan database uses a context++ score to predict the likelihood of miRNA targeting a particular site within the transcriptome; this scoring method includes the presence of local A/U richness as a positive marker for the presence of miRNA targeting [19]. We independently validated the presence of two discrete A/U rich regions flanking Ago2 bound sites within 3’UTRs (irrespective of miRNA) within our own dataset of HITS-CLIP performed in mouse Th2 cells (Figure 6(A)), and within a recently developed High-Resolution HITS-CLIP (HEAP) technique in mESC cells [23] (Fig. S1A). Within the Th2 differential AHC analysis for miR-23, 24, 27 targets, we verified the presence of two distinct A/U rich regions immediately surrounding the set of DeepRNA-Reg predictions (Figure 6A). This same analysis performed on the set of dCLIP predictions did not reflect a clear consensus signal surrounding the target sites. Secondary structure of Ago2 binding sites.

We reasoned that A/U rich sequences flanking miRNA target sites should lead to decreased RNA secondary structure, possibly enabling greater accessibility of the seed binding sequence. To test this idea, we used icSHAPE reactivity, a metric of RNA accessibility that was determined transcriptome-wide in mESC cells (in vivo) and in RNA isolated from the same cells (in vitro) [24]. We calculated the average icSHAPE reactivity for miRNA target sites in both the DeepRNA-Reg and dCLIP prediction sets (Figure 6(B,C)). The DeepRNA-Reg prediction set showed two distinct unstructured regions of high icSHAPE reactivity immediately surrounding miRNA target sites. This signal was present but less clear for the dCLIP prediction set.

To more explicitly test the hypothesis that A/U richness around the seed binding sequence allows it to be more accessible to the miRISC, we used the RNAfold package from viennaRNA to simulate the free energy change of dimerization of the central 10 bp within each site and a rolling 10 bp window around the central site. We calculated the average free energy change signal of dimerization for all enriched Ago2 sites in 3’UTRs, and for differentially enriched sites determined by DeepRNA-reg or dCLIP (Figure 6(D–F)). Immediately surrounding the central 10 nucleotides of miRNA target sites, there exists a more positive free energy change of dimerization, providing support for the assumption that A/U richness around the seed binding sequence allows it to be more accessible to the protein complexes that target it in the RISC pathway. Again, this sequence-encoded hallmark of the regions flanking miRNA binding sites is far clearer for the aggregate sum curve of dimerization energy calculated for the DeepRNA-Reg prediction set than that of the dCLIP prediction set.

Translation of predicted miRNA-targets to diverse biological milieux

One of the central goals of DeepRNA-Reg is to predict regions of differential enrichment that hold validity in different biological milieux. This would allow researchers to catalogue miRNA targets and variable RBP activity that can readily be applied to diverse research interests. Therefore, we tested the ability of DeepRNA-Reg and dCLIP predictions to remain valid between different mouse tissues. Kobayashi et al. have shown that there exists a robust downregulation in the activity of miR-29 family members following ischaemia and reperfusion (I/R) injury [25]. This study used an in vitro system for cerebral ischaemic injury where cultured mouse cortical cells were exposed to oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD). After oxygen-glucose deprivation, the cultures were replenished with full growth media and reoxygenated to simulate reperfusion. RNA-Seq on this OGD model then revealed that miR-29 targets were indeed upregulated in response to OGD and reperfusion, a model of (I/R) injury. The differentially enriched genes called by DeepRNA-Reg and dCLIP were again partitioned by the DBE sum for all differentially enriched sites in their 3’UTRs. The targets of miR-29 defined by DeepRNA-Reg in Th17 cells were also upregulated after oxygen-glucose deprivation in cortical cells, consistent with reduced miR-29 activity (Figure 7(A)). The DeepRNA-Reg partitions show greater separation, and, within the most restrictive partition, the DeepRNA-Reg prediction set outperforms dCLIP in its shift while including a set of genes that is twice as large. Importantly, downregulation of these targets was restored by retroviral overexpression of miR-29 (Figure 7(B)). To further assess the translatability of DeepRNA-Reg predictions in an independent dataset unrelated to T cell differentiation and to assess the tool’s broader applicability, we examined data from Hsin et al., who performed differential iCLIP and RNA-Seq in activated macrophages from WT and miR-155 KO mice, revealing miR-155-dependent regulation of gene expression [26]. The differentially enriched genes called by DeepRNA-Reg and dCLIP were again partitioned by the DBE sum for all differentially enriched sites in their 3’UTRs. As expected, gene expression changes induced by each miRNA correlated with the DBE sum. The top 10% (DBE percentile 90–100) of differentially bound genes predicted by DeepRNA-Reg exhibited a significantly greater difference in expression between WT and miR-155 KO conditions (Figure 8; two‑sample Kolmogorov – Smirnov test: D = 0.03, p = 0.02). Discussion

The RNA-protein interactome enables post-transcriptional regulation of RNA, and thus greatly shapes the physiology of mammalian cells. The use of comparative HITS-CLIP allows the large breadth of all protein-RNA interactions captured by a particular HITS-CLIP paradigm to be condensed to just those transcriptomic loci that show significant changes between two experimental conditions. Here, we present DeepRNA-Reg, a new algorithm for analysing comparative HITS-CLIP data. DeepRNA-Reg joins a growing stable of tools for analysing HITS-CLIP data [27,28]. Several of these tools focus on single-sample peak calling, while others use comparative analysis only for background subtraction [29,30], or only for PAR-CLIP data [31]. dCLIP is the primary and most widely used tool specifically designed to use comparative analysis to detect differential binding between experimental conditions [13]. Like dCLIP, DeepRNA-Reg is compatible with a wide range of CLIP assays (i.e. HITS-CLIP, eCLIP, GCLiPP, iCLIP, PAR-CLIP, etc.). Both tools compare sequence read density at defined binding peaks, while dCLIP can further integrate analysis of characteristic sequence mutations or truncations induced at crosslinking sites in PAR-CLIP and iCLIP, respectively.

Benchmarking analyses presented here demonstrate that DeepRNA-Reg provides efficacious comparative HITS-CLIP analyses, exceeding the performance of dCLIP in two key performance metrics: sensitivity and precision. DeepRNA-Reg exhibited greater sensitivity across a range of differential binding enrichment scores, returning larger sets of differential binding regions whose regulatory function could be experimentally validated by gene expression analyses equally well as the smaller sets of regions returned by dCLIP. The regions returned by both DeepRNA-Reg and dCLIP analysis of Ago2 HITS-CLIP data showed a high degree of agreement with the TargetScan database of predicted miRNA-directed Ago2 binding sites. DeepRNA-Reg regions were of shorter average length, with predicted miRNA binding sites centred within the region, indicating greater precision in localizing binding sites. DeepRNA-Reg also recapitulated the characteristic RNA secondary structure patterns near Ago2 binding sites as determined by icSHAPE [22,24]. Thus, DeepRNA-Reg may enable the use of many nodes of information to yield insights into how variability within the RNA-protein interactome leads to the emergence of important physiological changes within mammalian cells.

An interesting finding of our extrapolation study was that DeepRNA-Reg predictions determined in one cell type could be translated to a different cell type with remarkable success. The original study of miR-29 regulation of cortical neurons lacked HITS-CLIP data [25]. However, our analysis revealed that a DeepRNA-Reg analysis performed in an unrelated tissue (T cells) successfully predicted miRNA binding that correlated with gene expression changes in mouse cortical cells in the OGD model. Thus, it may be effective to construct databases of miRNA target sites empirically defined through DeepRNA-Reg analysis of paired HITS-CLIP experiments in experimentally tractable cells, and then apply them across different cell types in the same way researchers currently use databases based solely on sequence-based predictions. Such a collection of data may naturally emerge as researchers complete comparative HITS-CLIP experiments in various biological milieux. Ultimately, an empirically based dataset of miRNA targets would prove invaluable to studying the post-transcriptional regulatory networks that shape gene expression in mammalian cells.

The advent of high-throughput sequencing has paved the way for the generation of very large data sets describing biological systems. With big data comes the need for better tools to extract useful information. DeepRNA-Reg provides functionally relevant genomic annotations about differential RBP binding activity under variable conditions. This useful, validated tool will accelerate functional annotation of the RNA interactome, advancing our understanding of the RNA circuitry that governs gene expression and cell behaviour in health and disease.

Methods

Algorithm schema

For input, the DeepRNA-Reg algorithm requires two processed (BAM-formatted) sequencing files, and 1 (BED-formatted) file containing genomic loci of interest. For each BAM file given as input in this study, reads mapping to the same genomic coordinates are collapsed to a single unique read to remove PCR duplicates. This is an optional step left to the user and not included in the software package contained herein. For a given genomic locus within the BED-formatted file, the following processing steps are conducted: MA Normalization: The algorithm reads in coverage over the genomic region given as input. An MA plot and regression are applied to the read depths to capture the true scaling relationship between coverage of the two files over a genomic region. This linear relationship is removed from each M value to arrive at an ‘Adjusted M’ value, a metric of differential binding strength. This method of normalization was originally used in microarray data, then used in ChIP-Seq analysis, and later borrowed by dCLIP [13,32,33].Denoising: A Savitzky-Golay algorithm is applied to the data set of Adjusted M values, defined for each discrete genomic region given as input. Model design and training

The deep learning model assessed in this study has an architecture that consists of three LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) layers and one Dense layer. The three LSTM layers have 150 units, 150 units, and 50 units, respectively. The dense layer is the final layer with 1 unit, producing a single output. The total number of parameters in the model is 312,051. The optimizer used is an implementation of the Adam algorithm, and the loss function used is mean absolute error. With three LSTM layers, the model has the capacity to capture complex temporal dependencies in the input sequences required for decoding differential binding enrichment in paired HITS-CLIP experiments. This model architecture was gradually arrived at by iteratively training multiple models and an assessment of prediction quality for each trained model. The training dataset used in this study was derived form a WT vs. miR-155 KO paired HITS-CLIP experiment presented in Loeb et al. 2012 [11]. The training strategy of this model was stochastic gradient descent. The number of training epochs were 10 and the batch size was 1. Initially, the model was trained on a large dataset that led to overfitting. Ergo, to prevent overfitting, in the sequential training strategy, multiple versions of the model – with sequentially increasing training set size – were assessed for performance via an assessment of prediction. The final model utilized in this study was trained on 1,400 discrete transcriptomic loci. The method of assessing model performance in the various iterations and evolutions of the model developed in this study included both a qualitative and quantitative strategy to assessing model performance. Models were assessed on a separate set of loci held for testing, representing a simple holdout validation strategy. The method of assessing model performance was as follows: (1) an examination of model predictions in the context of well-expressed genes in the aforementioned testing dataset, and (2) a quantitative performance of the number of biologically relevant miR-155 microRNA seeds captured in predictions.

Mice

All animal studies were performed after obtaining approval from the UCSF or Vanderbilt Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Mice were housed and bred in specific pathogen-free conditions in the Animal Barrier Facility at UCSF or Vanderbilt. C57BL/6 male and female age and sex matched mice between 5 to 12 weeks of age were used for all experiments. To generate ‘miR-23, 24, 27 KO’ T cells lacking expression of all Mirc11 cluster miRNAs (miR-23a, miR-24–2, and miR-27a) and Mirc22 cluster miRNAs (miR-23b, miR-24–1, and miR-27b), we intercrossed previously generated conditionally mutant Mirc22tm1.1Mtm mice [14] and Mirc11tm1.1Mtm mice [34] and with Cd4-Cre transgenic mice (Tg(CD4-cre)1Cwi; 4196, Taconic) to induce Cre-mediated deletion in T cells.

T cell activation, culture, and flow cytometry

T cells from mouse spleens and lymph nodes were enriched by Dynabead-positive selection (Invitrogen). Enriched CD4+ T cells were plated into non-tissue culture-treated dishes (Fisher) precoated with goat anti-hamster IgG (1 μg/mL, Thermo). Cells were activated with anti-CD3 (1 μg/mL, clone 2C11, BioXCell) and anti-CD28 (1 μg/mL, clone 37.51, BioXCell) in the presence of IL-4 (10 ng/mL, Peprotech) and anti-IFN-γ (10 μg/mL, clone XMG1.2, eBioscience) for 3 days to promote Th2 cell differentiation. Cells were cultured in high glucose DMEM with pyruvate and L-glutamine (Gibco) containing 1x MEM non-essential amino acids (Sigma), 1x MEM vitamin solution (Gibco), 1x aspartate (Sigma) – arginine (Fisher) -folic acid (Sigma) mixture, 1x penicillin and streptomycin (Gibco), 1x HEPES (Gibco), 1x L-glutamine (Gibco), 2-mercapoethanol (Sigma), and 10% foetal bovine serum (Omega). Cells were then split into new dishes containing fresh media supplemented with recombinant IL-2 (20 U/mL, NCI Biological Resources Branch), IL-4, and anti-IFN-γ for each of the next 2 days. Following culture, cells were stained with eFluro780 viability dye (Invitrogen) then the following fluorescent antibodies: APC anti-mouse CD4 (Tonbo Biosciences), violetFluor 450 anti-mouse CD8a (Tonbo Biosciences), FITC anti-mouse CD28 (Biolegend), and PE-Cy7 anti-mouse TCRβ (eBioscience). Stained samples were run through an 8-colour FACSCANTO II flow cytometer and data were processed using FlowJo software.

Ago2 HITS-CLIP

CD4+ T cells were isolated from spleen and lymph nodes and cultured under Th2 conditions as described above. Ago2 HITS-CLIP was performed using an anti-Ago2 monoclonal antibody (Wako; clone 2D4) as described [16].
