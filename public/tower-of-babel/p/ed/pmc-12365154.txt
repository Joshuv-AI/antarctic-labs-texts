Acute effects of BFR intra-conditioning on torque and muscle activity of the rectus femoris muscle during isokinetic knee extensions
Authors: Krzysztof Fostiak, Marta Bichowska-Paweska, Robert Trybulski, Dan Iulian Alexe, Michal Wilk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study aimed to evaluate the impact of blood flow restriction (BFR) used only before sets of isokinetic knee extensions on torque and muscle activity of the Rectus Femoris muscle. Twenty healthy physically active males (age = 22.7 ± 6.7 years; body mass = 83.0 ± 15.9 kg; body height = 180.8 ± 8.3 cm) performed three experimental sessions in a random crossover design (BFR - with blood flow restriction; control - without BFR, Sham). In the BFR and Sham conditions cuffs were applied for 5 min and released 1 min prior to each set of isokinetic knee extensions. The pressure of the cuffs was 80% AOP (arterial occlusion pressure) for BFR conditions and 20 mmHg for Sham conditions. For Control conditions no restriction was applied. Experimental sessions consisted of 5 sets of 3 isokinetic knee extensions (180°/s) with 6.5-min rest intervals between sets. The repetitions were performed with maximal effort. During the concentric phase of the exercise peak torques and peak electromyographic activity of Rectus Femoris muscle were gathered. The eccentric phase was passive. The two-way repeated measures ANOVA did not show statistically significant condition × set interaction for peak torque and muscle activity. There was also no main effect of condition as well as no main effect of set for peak torque and muscle activity. The results of this study indicate that BFR intra-conditioning (5 min; 80% AOP; 1 min reperfusion) did not significantly affect strength performance or Rectus Femoris EMG activity. However, the lack of performance decline suggests it does not impair neuromuscular output, which may be beneficial in high-load training with long rest intervals. Previous research shows that BFR may induce adaptive changes not examined here. Further studies should explore these adaptations and the effects of varying BFR parameters, intensities, and work to rest ratios.Keywords: Blood flow restriction, Ischemia, Resistance exercises, Sports performance, OcclusionSubject terms: Quality of life, Skeletal muscle

Introduction

BFR methods and its limitations

Blood Flow Restriction (BFR) is a technique that involves the use of specialized compression cuffs to limit or block the blood flow to a specific muscle area. There are different methods of BFR: pre-conditioning (applied before exercise)1–3, continuous (BFR during exertion and rest intervals)4–6, intermittent (restriction only during exercise)4. Despite their efficacy, the practical implementation of these methods may be constrained by factors such as pain, discomfort, and the interference of the cuff with proper exercise technique (in continuous and intermittent BFR), as well as the additional time needed for ischemic pre-conditioning, can be limiting factors4,7,8. Therefore, a new method of BFR has been introduced (intra-conditioning), where blood flow is restricted only during rest intervals between bouts of exercise9–11.

Effect of BFR intra-conditioning on strength performance

There are only few studies analyzing the effect of blood flow restriction intra-conditioning on acute resistance exercise performance. Wilk et al.12 reported that BFR intra-conditioning caused increased peak and mean bar velocity, during the bench press exercise (5 sets; 3 repetitions; 60% 1RM (one-repetition maximum; 5 min rest interval; 5 min BFR; 80% AOP) compared to a control condition. However, Trybulski et al.11 showed that BFR intra-conditioning does not increase peak power performance but reduces the decrease in power output during consecutive sets of squats (5 sets; 2 repetitions; 60% 6RM; 5 min rest interval; 5 min BFR; 60% AOP) compared to control condition. On the other hand, Jarosz et al.13 observed that intra-conditioning BFR (3 min; 80% AOP) increased only peak bar velocity (only at 20% 1RM and 50% 1RM), however such changes were not observed in mean velocity. Even though the above-mentioned studies have shown that BFR intra-conditioning can increase physical performance, not all studies support its positive effect in various exercise tests10,14,15.

BFR intra-conditioning research gaps

Previous studies by Jarosz et al.13, Trybulski et al.11, and Wilk et al.12 used non-laboratory measurement devices in their experimental protocols, which could have led to varying results. Given that BFR intra-conditioning is a novel approach, its evaluation requires high-precision measurement device, such as isokinetic dynamometer (such as Biodex). Isokinetic testing allows to eliminate potential confounding factors, such as inaccuracies of measurement devices (Tendo, Keiser Squat machine) and possible changes in exercise technique. Biodex testing provides standardized, precise, and objective assessment conditions by isolating specific muscle groups and controlling angular velocity during movement, thus more accurately examining direct effect of BFR intra- conditioning on strength performance. According to the authors’ knowledge no publications utilizing BFR intra-conditioning and isokinetic dynamometry exist. However, the results of experiments utilizing other BFR methods show that it might cause changes in isokinetic tests16–18. Fujita et al.16 indicated that continuous BFR (200 mmHg) caused greater decline in torque during successive repetitions of a resistance exercise (30 repetitions; 180°/s) compared to a control group. On the other hand, ischemia pre-conditioning (3 sets; 5 min BFR; 5 min reperfusion; 220 mm Hg) increased torque of isokinetic knee extensions (5 sets; 5 repetitions; 20°/s) in comparison to placebo17. However, Huang et al.18 showed that BFR before exercise (3 sets; 5 min BFR; 5 min reperfusion; 170 mm Hg) did not cause any changes in torque during isokinetic knee extensions during different angular velocities and durations of exercise (3 repetitions; 30°/s; 3 repetitions 150°/s; 3 repetitions 270°/s.; 30 repetitions 180°/s).

What is more, previous studies on BFR intra-conditioning and short-term resistance exercise11–13 have not investigated the physiological mechanisms underlying performance changes. Assessing variables like muscle excitation or activity via surface electromyography (sEMG)19 can offer insights into motor unit recruitment, which directly affects strength capability. Incorporating such measurements in future research could enhance understanding of how BFR intra-conditioning affects neuromuscular function.

Effect of BFR on electromyographic activity of muscles

Current evidence indicates that BFR can affect electromyographic activity of muscles. Specifically, restricting blood flow during low load resistance exercises can lead to increased muscle activity20,21 which indicates the involvement of a larger pool of motor units to overcome external resistance associated with high metabolite accumulation. Whereas, when BFR was applied during high-load resistance exercises (70% 1RM; 70% MVC), no significant increase in muscle activity was observed22–24. However, the above-mentioned dependencies apply to the continuous and intermittent BFR methods. To the authors’ knowledge, only one study assessed muscle activity changes caused by BFR intra-conditioning. Teixeira et al.24 showed that BFR applied during rest intervals caused a greater decline in muscle activity during knee extensions (3 sets; 8 repetitions; 70% 1RM; 1-minute rest interval; 80% AOP) compared to control conditions and intermittent BFR. However, Teixeira’s et al.24 tests were strength-endurance focused (knee extensions lasting 16 s). To the authors’ knowledge, there are no studies examining BFR intra-conditioning effects on muscle activity during short-term resistance exercises.

Aim of the study and hypotheses

Taking into account the available literature on BFR and existing gaps in knowledge regarding BFR intra-conditioning, the aim of this study was to evaluate whether BFR intra-conditioning and sham interventions affect strength performance (torque) and electromyographic activity of the Rectus Femoris muscle during isokinetic knee extensions (180°/s), using high-quality measurement tools (Biodex 4 Pro, Noraxon DTS). Based on previous findings showing improved performance with BFR intra-conditioning11–13, we hypothesized that BFR would increase torque compared to Control and Sham conditions. Given earlier reports of both increases and decreases in muscle activity following BFR20,22,24, we also hypothesized that BFR would alter muscle activity of the Rectus Femoris. Furthermore, following results by Trybulski et al.11 and Wilk et al.12, it was hypothesized that performance outcomes would change across consecutive sets, particularly in later sets (3–5). Lastly, it was hypothesized that the sham condition would not significantly affect torque or muscle activity compared to control or BFR.

Materials and methods

The experiment was performed following a randomized crossover design, where each subject performed three testing sessions in a random and counterbalanced order, in > 72-h time interval: (a) BFR with a 80% AOP applied before every set of isokinetic knee extensions (b) Sham with a 20 mmHg applied before every set of isokinetic knee extensions (c) Control conditions. During each session, participants performed 5 sets of 3 repetitions of isokinetic knee extensions at angular velocity of 180°/s with 6,5 min rest intervals. The exercise was performed with a dominant extremity. In ischemic and SHAM conditions a 10,5-cm wide pneumatic cuff was applied on a proximal part of the dominant leg and then pressurized to either 80% AOP or 20 mmHg for 5 min and removed 1 min before each set of exercise to allow for reperfusion. Preparing and pressurizing the cuff lasted 30 s. All of the tests were performed in the Physical Exercise Laboratory at the University of Physical Education and Sport in Gdansk, Poland.

Subjects

Prior to experiment the sample size was determined using G*Power software version 3.1.9.2 (Düsseldorf, Germany), based on the following statistical test parameters: “ANOVA: repeated measures within-factors” (3 groups, 5 measurements). The statistical power was set at 0.8, the significance level at 0.05, the effect size was established at 0.27, a correlation coefficient at 0.5 and non-sphericity correction at 1. The analysis indicated that the minimum required sample size was 18 participants.

Previous studies on BFR have reported sex-specific differences, with some findings indicating no effect or even a decrease in performance among females compared to males. Therefore, to eliminate potential gender-related variability, only male participants were included in the present study25–27. The inclusion criteria were: male, recreational physical activity 3 times a week for min. 1 h, for minimum 3 months before the experiment. To ensure safety and eliminate the risk of undesired interference due to impaired limb function, the exclusion criteria were: cardiovascular diseases (including atrial fibrillation, hypertension, thrombosis, heart failure) and musculoskeletal injuries for at least 6 months before the start of the study (personal declaration).

Following sample size calculations and inclusion and inclusion criteria 20 subjects (gender: male, age: 22.7 ± 6.7, weight: 83.0 ± 15.9, height: 180.8 ± 8.3) were tested. Before the beginning of the experimental sessions, the subjects were informed about the potential risks and benefits of participating in the research and they signed a written informed consent. The participants were allowed to withdraw from the study at any time. The subjects were asked to follow their standard diet and not to use additional supplements or drugs throughout the entire duration of the experiment, however it was not strictly controlled. The experimental project was approved by the Bioethics Committee at the District Medical Chamber in Gdańsk (KB − 11/ 22) and adhered to the ethical standards specified in the Helsinki Declaration.

Procedures

Familiarization session

Participants underwent a familiarization session one week before the main experiment. During the familiarization session participants, performed a warm-up consisting of: 5-minute ride on a cycle ergometer (60 revolutions per minute, external load of 60 W), the quadriceps femoris muscle stretches (3 sets, 10 repetitions, 2 s each), squats (2 sets, 10 repetitions, body weight), half-squat jumps (4 repetitions, body weight) and three isometric knee extensions (5 s, performed with 25%, 50%, 75% of perceived maximum exertion28. After warming up, the subjects rested for 5 min and then performed two sets of one 5-s isometric knee extension of a dominant leg (MVC) with a 30-s rest interval. After exercise, participants rested for 10 min (5 min in a sitting position). Then, the subjects began an exercise test consisting of 3 sets of 3 repetitions of isokinetic knee extensions of the dominant leg at angular velocity of 180°/s. Between each exercise test, participants rested for 6.5 min (5 min in a sitting position). During the first, second and third rest breaks, a 5-minute blood flow restriction of 100 mm Hg was applied to the dominant lower limb.

Experimental sessions

At the beginning of the first experimental session, participants were weighted and measured. Before the tests, the subjects performed a 10-minute standardized warm-up, the same as during the familiarization session. After warming up, subjects performed two sets of one 5-s isometric knee extensions of the dominant limb (MVC - Maximum Voluntary Contraction) with a 30-s rest interval to measure muscle activity of the Rectus Femoris. The MVC results were used to normalize the electrical signal acquired during isokinetic knee extensions of the dominant limb. Participants then rested for 10 min (5 min in a sitting position). Afterwards, the subjects performed the main part of the experimental procedure, which consisted of 5 sets of 3 repetitions of isokinetic knee extensions at angular velocity of 180°/s with a 6.5-minute rest interval (5 min in a sitting position) between sets.

Each participant was subjected to three interventions in random order:

BFR – blood flow restriction intra-conditioning (80% AOP),

BFR – blood flow restriction intra-conditioning (80% AOP),

Sham - sham blood flow restriction (20 mm Hg),

Sham - sham blood flow restriction (20 mm Hg),

Control – no blood flow restriction.

Control – no blood flow restriction.

Blood flow restriction

Blood flow restriction was applied with a 10,5-cm wide pneumatic cuff (FitCuffs Leg Cuffs V3.1; Denmark) only on a dominant lower extremity in a seated position. Before the first session, either BFR or Sham, the AOP was assessed. AOP measurement was performed before the warmup. During AOP assessment participants were in a seated position with a cuff applied on a proximal part of the dominant thigh. The cuff was inflated to 70 mmHg and then the pressure was increased by 5 mmHg increments until the pulse stopped29. Pulse measurement was performed on the Posterior Tibial Artery near the Medial Malleolus using hand-held Doppler with an 8 MHz probe (Edan SD3, Sonoline C, Contec, China)30.

The pressure used in BFR procedure was 80% of the AOP (163.2 ± 24.0 mm Hg) and 20 mm Hg in Sham conditions. Putting on and inflating the cuff after each set of exercise took 30 s. The cuff in BFR and Sham procedures was pressurized for 5 min12,31, followed by 1 min of reperfusion (for cuff depressurization, removal, and secure positioning of the participant in the Biodex) before each set of isokinetic knee extensions. The pressure of the cuff restricting blood flow was constantly monitored using a manometer.

Electromyography

A TeleMyo DTS surface electromyograph from Noraxon (Scottsdale, AZ, USA) was used to measure the electrical activity of the Rectus Femoris muscle of the dominant leg. In line with the guidelines of the International Society of Electromyography and Kinesiology32, two electrodes (45 × 42 mm, Sorimex EK-S 45 PSG, Poland, Toruń) according to SENIAM (Surface ElectroMyoGraphy for the Non- Invasive Assessment of Muscles) were placed over the belly of the muscle (Rectus Femoris) along with the expected muscle fiber orientation. Inter-electrode distance was 20 mm. Prior to electrode application the skin was shaved and rubbed with alcohol. The electrodes were attached after completing the warm-up and remained in the same place until the end of the test session. sEMG (electromyography) data from each concentric contraction of the muscle were gathered. The sEMG signal was recorded with 16-bit resolution for analog-to-digital conversion and a sampling frequency of 1500 Hz. A band-pass filter was applied within the frequency range of 10–500 Hz. Obtained data were processed and analyzed using MyoResearch 1.08 software from Noraxon. The sEMG results were normalized to MVC performed before the main part of each experimental session. Peak sEMG values were presented as means.

Torque measurements

The Biodex System 4 Pro isokinetic dynamometer (Biodex Shirley Company, USA) was used to measure the torque of knee extensions. The seat of the device was set so that the angle at the hip and knee joints were 90°. The torso, hips and thigh of the dominant leg were attached to the chair with straps to avoid additional body movements, while the lower leg was attached to the dynamometer’s arm. The axis of rotation of the dynamometer was in line with the axis of rotation of the knee joint of the tested limb. Participants performed knee extensions in isometric (MVC) and isokinetic conditions. The MVC test was performed to obtain EMG data for further analysis of muscle activity. During the exercise in isometric conditions, the knee joint angle was 90°. However, during tests in isokinetic conditions, the movement was conducted from a 90° angle in the knee joint to full extension with a constant angular velocity of 180°/s. Peak torque values were gathered only during concentric phase of the isokinetic knee extensions and presented as means. The eccentric phase of the exercise remained passive (rest).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis were performed using Statistica 9.1. Results for peak torque obtained in isokinetic measurements and muscle activity (% MVC) were presented as means with standard deviations. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of data distribution, and the sphericity of variance was analyzed using the Mauchly test. Statistical analysis for torque values ​​and muscle activity levels between individual interventions was examined using a two-way ANOVA [3 conditions (Control vs. Sham vs. BFR) x 5 sets]. Additionally, one-way ANOVA was used to assess differences between sets (sets 1–5), independently within individual interventions (Control; Sham; BFR). Effect sizes (ES) for main effects and interactions were determined by partial eta squared (η2). Partial eta squared values were classified as small (0.01–0.059), moderate (0.06–0.137) and large (> 0.137). Post hoc comparisons using Tukey’s test were conducted to locate the differences between mean values when the main effect or an interaction was found. For pairwise comparisons, ESs were determined by Cohen’s d. Percent changes with 95% confidence intervals (95CI) were also calculated. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

The main finding of the study was that BFR intra-conditioning protocol used in this study did not alter the torque and muscle activity during isokinetic knee extensions (5 sets; 3 repetitions; 180°/s) compared to other conditions, which is contrary to our hypothesis. No changes in the conditions were found, nor were any significant differences found between particular sets.

Neuromuscular mechanisms

To the best of authors’ knowledge, only Teixeira et al.24 studied the effect of BFR intra-conditioning combined with knee extensions (70% 1RM) on changes in Rectus Femoris muscular activity. Teixeira et al.24 observed that BFR intra-conditioning did not lead to increased muscle activity compared to control conditions, which aligns with our findings. The lack of changes in EMG activity is also consistent with previous research employing continuous or intermittent BFR methods20–22,24. All of the mentioned studies utilized high external loads (70% MVC or 70% 1RM). Muscle contraction during exercise with high external load causes intramuscular blood flow restriction, which potentially mitigated the effect of the cuff (increase of muscle activity). What’s more, it is necessary to pay attention to the fact that there is a dependence between muscle activity and external load used. Gołaś et al.33 showed a direct proportionality resulting from the increased recruitment of motor units with increasing external load. Given these facts, the isokinetic knee extensions in our experiment engaged high motor units from the start, leading to strong activation, which may have limited any further effects from BFR intra-conditioning. Teixeira et al.24 also, noted a decline in muscle activity in sets 2 and 3 compared to the first set of knee extensions, accompanied by a lower repetition count in the group using BFR during rest intervals. This contrasts with our results, as we did not observe any decrease in torque or EMG during subsequent sets. In Teixeira’s et al.24 study one set of exercise lasted 16 s and ATP resynthesis primarily relied on glycolytic pathways. In addition, short rest intervals led to high metabolite accumulation. The decrease in muscle activity may have resulted from type III and IV muscle afferents detecting metabolite buildup (H+, lactate, ATP), inhibiting α-motor neurons and/or descending neural drive, thereby attenuating motor unit recruitment and reducing muscle activity24. However, the knee extensions in our protocol lasted only ~ 3 s and ATP resynthesis was primarily driven by the phosphagen system which fully establishes after depletion in 5 min34. The 6.5-minute rest interval, even with 5 min BFR at 80% AOP allowed for sufficient energy replenishment. Thus, we did not note any decrease in either torque or EMG levels in our study.

Performance changes across consecutive sets

Comparing our findings with other BFR intra-conditioning and short-term resistance exercise studies reveals both discrepancies and similarities. Wilk et al.12 observed an increase in the examined variables, but only in the 3rd to 5th sets, with no significant changes in the first two sets of the exercise. Similarly, Trybulski et al.11 demonstrated that positive effect of intra-conditioning BFR appeared only in sets 3 to 5. This contradicts the present study’s findings, as no significant changes in torque were observed in sets 3 to 5. However, similarly to the studies by Wilk et al.12 and Trybulski et al.11, torque during the first set of knee extensions remained at the same level as in the control or placebo conditions. Therefore, when analyzing the obtained results, it is necessary to consider the impact of BFR on the results obtained in the first set of the exercise as well as in sets 3–5.

Cuff pressure effects

When analyzing the first set of the resistance exercise, it is important to consider studies that used pre-conditioning consisting of a single bout of BFR before resistance exercise. Bichowska-Paweska et al.35 showed that BFR with 80% AOP did not increase peak or mean bar velocity during the bench press exercise, which aligns with our results. However, there are studies indicating that a single cycle of BFR (100% AOP) increased bench press performance compared to control conditions31, which is in contrary to our results. Considering only the effect of BFR on the first set, the main difference between the BFR protocol used in our, Bichowska-Paweska et al.35 Trybulski et al.11, Wilk et al.12 and Salagas et al.31 studies was the cuff pressure applied. Salagas et al.31 used higher percentage of AOP to restrict the blood flow (100% in contrary to 80% in Bichowska-Paweska’s et al.35, Wilk’s et al.12 or 60% AOP in Trybulski’s et al.11. The available literature indicates that cuff pressure is a key factor influencing the magnitude of acute changes induced by BFR, with even a 10% or 20% AOP difference significantly affecting the outcomes22,36. Therefore, it can be suggested that higher (than 80% AOP) cuff pressure should be applied to induce an acute increase in performance due to BFR in the first set of exercise.

Taking into consideration consecutive sets of knee extensions (especially 3–5 sets) we did not register any increase or decrease in torque between conditions (Control; Sham; BFR). We also didn’t notice any changes of tested variables within the conditions. Those results are inconsistent with Wilk et al.12, who noted an increase in bar velocity in set 3–5 due to BFR intra-conditioning with 80% AOP compared to control conditions. A study conducted by Wilk et al.12, unlike our and Trybuski et al.11 experiment, involved the use of upper limbs during exercise. Gepfert et al.37 strongly emphasized in their work (especially in the field of muscle power changes) the need to apply higher cuff pressure in the lower limbs compared to the upper limbs, in order to induce beneficial changes as a result of the use of BFR. This statement reinforces the assumption that pressure applied in our study might be a limiting factor to induce the desired adaptive changes and thus an increase in torque.

Work to rest ratio

The lack of torque decline across sets in any condition in this research contrasts with Trybulski et al.11, who reported power decreases in consecutive sets (3–5) of Keiser squats (60% 6RM; 2 repetitions; 5sets; 5 min rest interval), with BFR during rest intervals reducing the extent of these losses (compared to control conditions). Even though in our studies subjects did 5 sets of 3 repetitions of isokinetic knee extensions (which involves maximum strength output), we did not see any signs of decrease in torque in any of the conditions (BFR, Sham, Control). The lack of fatigue can be associated with the number of muscles involved in an exercise, duration of the exercise and rest interval. Isokinetic knee extensions engage less muscles than the Keiser squats. Also, the duration of effort in one set of exercise in Trybulski et al.11 protocol was longer than the effort during knee extensions conducted in our research. What’s more, the rest intervals between Keiser squats were shorter (5 min) compared to our (6,5 min). Shorter duration of the exercise in our study might have caused lower ATP consumption and the longer rest interval allowed for its greater replenishment. Phosphocreatine mainly used during short-time high intensity exercise mostly regenerates in 5–8 min, thus no fatigue occurred during consecutive sets in Control conditions. Also, Harris et al.38 showed that ischemia causes attenuation of phosphocreatine regeneration, however BFR with 80% AOP used in our study, was not sufficient to disturb regeneration, affecting the torque levels compared to the Control group and causing fatigue. No decrease in torque under the control conditions did not allow for the assessment of effectiveness of the applied BFR protocol in regard to strength endurance.

Despite the lack of statistically significant differences in torque and muscle activity (between conditions), this study contributes to the understanding of BFR intra-conditioning under isokinetic conditions.

Conclusion

Blood flow restriction intra-conditioning is a novel method that has only recently been studied, so there are no precise guidelines and scientific data regarding the relationship between particular variables of BFR used and their potential impact on direct post-exercise reactions and adaptive changes. To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to examine the acute neuromuscular response under isokinetic conditions to BFR intra-conditioning using high-precision laboratory devices (Biodex System 4 Pro, Noraxon EMG), thereby enabling a more reliable assessment of the effects of this intervention. This study indicates that BFR intra-conditioning protocol used (80% AOP; 5 min BFR; 1 min reperfusion) does not affect strength performance as well as muscle activity during isokinetic knee extensions (180°/s; compared to Control and Sham conditions). The lack of statistically significant differences in torque and sEMG (compared to the Control and Sham conditions) may suggest potential limitations of the BFR protocol used in this study (cuff pressure, work to rest ratio), which should be carefully considered in future research, for which the present results may serve as a valuable foundation. What is also important is that the BFR intra-conditioning protocol applied in this study did not impair neuromuscular output, which may be considered advantageous from a practical perspective. This method may hold particular value in high-load resistance training regimens that incorporate prolonged rest intervals, where sustaining performance across sets is essential. What is more, it cannot be excluded that BFR intra-conditioning may have contributed to adaptive changes that were not assessed in the present study, especially considering that previous research has demonstrated its ability to upregulated gene expression pathways involved in muscle regeneration11,12,39. However, further research on its possible acute and chronic adaptations is needed.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. The sample consisted exclusively of physically active men without professional sports experience, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Future research should include female participants, elite athletes, and larger sample sizes to improve generalizability and allow subgroup analyses. Additionally, sEMG electrodes may have been positioned over the innervation zone, potentially affecting comparisons between sessions, although this likely did not influence within-session results. The experimental protocol was restricted to short-duration, high-intensity resistance exercise (five sets of three repetitions) with long rest intervals (6.5 min), which may be relevant for sports that rely primarily on phosphagen energy systems. Therefore, future studies should explore varying durations of effort, intensities, and rest intervals to reflect other metabolic demands. Furthermore, only one level of blood flow restriction (BFR) pressure (80% AOP) was applied, aside from the sham condition (20 mmHg). To allow for more comprehensive comparisons and insights into performance outcomes, future research should investigate a range of BFR pressures, including full arterial occlusion (100% AOP), as well as the effects of different BFR durations.
