Taiwanese consensus statement on the use of diabetes‐specific formula: Scientific evidence
Authors: Chih‐Hsun Chu, Hui‐Yu Peng, Chun‐Chuan Lee, Chih‐Yuan Wang, Yuh‐Jane Ko, Wen‐Hui Chen, Pi‐Hui Hsu, Chien‐Ning Huang, Yi‐Sun Yang, Kuei‐Mei Yeh, Chung‐Mei Ouyang, Horng‐Yih Ou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionMedical Nutrition Therapy remains the cornerstone of diabetes management. Clinical evidence shows that early intervention with medical nutrition therapy can significantly improve cardiometabolic markers, thereby delaying or preventing diabetes‐related complications. As part of medical nutrition therapy, diabetes‐specific formula has proven to be an effective tool in clinical practice and a viable support alternative in special circumstances where consuming whole foods is challenging. However, Taiwan currently lacks consensus on diabetes‐specific formula products, and many patients with diabetes, as well as some healthcare professionals, still hold misconceptions about these products.MethodsTo address these needs, the Taiwanese Association of Diabetes Educators and the Diabetes Association of the Republic of China, Taiwan, collaborated to form an expert panel to formulate consensus statements on the appropriate use of diabetes‐specific formula.ResultsFollowing a comprehensive review of international guidelines and clinical evidence, the panel formulated five consensus statements covering indications, clinical benefits, nutritional composition, and practical applications of diabetes‐specific formula.ConclusionThis consensus provides evidence‐based recommendations for integrating diabetes‐specific formula into individualized diabetes care, particularly in patients with poor glycemic control, malnutrition, sarcopenia, gestational diabetes, or diabetic kidney disease. These statements are tailored to Taiwan's healthcare system but may serve as a reference for other regions.Keywords: Diabetes, Diabetes nutritional care, Diabetes‐specific formula

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes mellitus or simply diabetes, which encompasses both type 1 diabetes (T1D) and type 2 diabetes (T2D), including gestational diabetes (GD), is a global health concern 1 . According to the International Diabetes Federation, 589 million or 1 out of 9 adults (aged 20–79 years) are living with diabetes, and this number is expected to increase to 853 million by 2050 1 . In Taiwan, the latest data show that 2.6 million people have diabetes, and this figure is projected to reach 3.1 million by 2050 1 . Diabetes healthcare costs substantially impact Taiwan's healthcare system, with renal complications accounting for the highest share of the diabetes‐related economic burden 2 .

Guidelines from the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and the European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) recommend that all patients with diabetes should receive Medical Nutrition Therapy (MNT) as part of their comprehensive treatment plan 3 . MNT is based on a diet of whole foods, comprised of vegetables, fruits, legumes, and low‐glycemic index (GI) carbohydrates 4 . A diet consisting of low‐GI carbohydrates has shown benefits in lowering fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and blood lipids 5 . Additionally, a whole foods diet rich in monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) and fiber improved blood glucose and other metabolic markers 6 , 7 . Despite the evidence supporting the benefits of dietary interventions focused on whole foods, low‐GI foods, and controlled calorie intake, always maintaining a healthy diet may be challenging for many patients with diabetes.

Considering these challenges, diabetes‐specific formula (DSF) may serve as an alternative option in special circumstances where maintaining a healthy diet of whole foods is difficult or inconvenient. Studies have demonstrated the benefits of DSF in managing diabetes when used as a meal replacement or when used as a snack 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 . Furthermore, the Australian Diabetes Society (ADS) formulated a consensus statement on the appropriate use of DSF to support the protein and nutritional requirements of individuals with diabetes 13 . Regardless of the growing body of evidence supporting the use of DSF in patients with diabetes, consensus on its use in Taiwan is currently lacking, and misconceptions continue to persist among patients and healthcare professionals.

In response, the Taiwanese Association of Diabetes Educators (TADE) and the Diabetes Association of the Republic of China (ROC), Taiwan (DAROC), collaborated to formulate consensus statements on the use of DSF in patients with diabetes. Although these consensus statements were specifically tailored to Taiwan's healthcare landscape, they may also be applied to other countries or regions.

METHODS

TADE and DAROC established a collaborative 12‐member expert panel comprising six diabetologists, five registered dietitians, and one registered nurse. These experts, who have extensive clinical experience in diabetes care and nutrition, were specifically endorsed by their respective associations.

The expert panel conducted two formal meetings during which each consensus statement was proposed and supporting clinical evidence was presented. To identify relevant studies on the use of DSF in supporting glucose control and nutritional management of patients with diabetes, we searched the literature published between 2015 and 2024. Database searches included PubMed, Airiti Library, and the National Digital Library of Theses and Dissertations in Taiwan. The search utilized key terms including “diabetes‐specific formulas,” “diabetes‐specific nutrition formulas,” “diabetes‐specific enteral formula,” “liquid formula for diabetes,” “diabetes nutrition,” “enteral nutritional support,” “oral nutrition supplement for diabetes,” “liquid diets for diabetes,” “liquid meal replacements for diabetes,” and “enteral nutrition for diabetes.” Studies were included if they involved human subjects and excluded if they exhibited poor statistical analyses, substandard reporting, or poor organization.

Resulting from the search strategy, the primary reference identified and used for the discussion was the ADS Consensus Statement: The Use of Diabetes‐Specific Formulas in Type 2 Diabetes (2024) 13 . The panel also extensively reviewed the latest published research articles on the use of DSF in supporting glucose control and nutritional management of patients with diabetes. The literature cited in the ADS consensus statement and other key references were also examined to identify other relevant evidence. We also verified the formula brands, dosages, and sugar ratios reported in the literature to compare with existing DSFs in Taiwan. During the meetings, each expert contributed their insights, and the consensus statements developed were voted on and revised accordingly until full consensus (100% agreement) was reached through nominal voting. This systematic process ensured that each statement is grounded in evidence and validated by unanimous agreement.

Following the experts' comprehensive review of the existing literature on DSF, particularly referencing the ADS consensus statement on the use of DSF in T2D 13 , five consensus statements were formulated, reflecting both the strength of supporting evidence and the clinical insights contributed by the experts. Consensus statement 1

For individuals with diabetes, MNT is the primary care approach for improving blood glucose control and overall health. Patients are advised to follow a healthy, evidence‐based dietary pattern for blood glucose control. In special circumstances where a diet based on whole foods is difficult to implement, DSF may serve as an alternative option, supporting nutrition, blood glucose control, and overall health in patients with diabetes. DSF should be used under the guidance of a physician or registered dietitian.

Evidence

MNT is an evidence‐based intervention, supervised by a qualified dietitian, to treat a disease or condition 4 , 14 . For patients with diabetes, the goal of MNT is to promote and encourage healthy eating patterns using a variety of whole, nutrient‐dense foods, such as vegetables, fruits, and legumes, in appropriate portions 4 . This healthy eating pattern aims to improve HbA1c, blood pressure (BP), cholesterol level, and body weight, and delay or, if possible, prevent the complications of diabetes 4 . MNT has been clinically proven to benefit patients with diabetes and remains a cornerstone of diabetes care 4 , 5 , 6 , 15 . However, its implementation may be challenging in certain circumstances. Therefore, alternative options such as DSF may be necessary.

The 2024 ADS consensus statement on DSF states that although MNT is the primary strategy for improving glycemic control and overall health of patients with T2D, DSF can be a valuable alternative in special circumstances, including situations where a healthy diet is not always possible, when taking whole foods do not achieve the desired outcome, or if the dietary intake is not adequate to meet the recommended nutritional requirements 13 . The 2018 UK evidence‐based diabetes nutrition guidelines also endorsed DSF as one of the nutritional strategies in diabetes care 16 . The Diabetes Canada Clinical Practice Guidelines Expert Working Group has also recommended the use of meal replacement products to aid in weight reduction, glycemic control, and reduce the need for anti‐diabetic medications in patients with T2D 17 .

Consensus statement 2

DSF should not be used alone but should be incorporated into a holistic diabetes management plan, which includes regular blood glucose monitoring, a healthy diet, appropriate physical activity, adequate sleep, maintaining healthy blood lipid levels, BP control, and medication adherence. It is recommended to choose DSF products supported by clinical evidence.

Evidence

The 2024 ADS consensus on DSF highlighted that DSF should not be used alone, but rather as part of a broader T2D plan 13 . The 2018 International Special Dietary Foods (ISDI) recognizes DSF as a nutritional strategy for individuals with diabetes, particularly for hospitalized patients with poor glycemic control or those at high risk of dysglycemia 18 . In addition, the 2017 European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) guidelines, based on the latest clinical evidence, also recommend the use of DSF as a nutritional supplement in patients who are obese or have diabetes 19 . In these patients, the risk of hyperglycemia can be minimized with the use of a DSF containing reduced carbohydrates and slowly digested complex carbohydrates 19 .

Consensus statement 3

Evidence demonstrates that replacing regular meals or snacks with DSF provides multiple benefits for patients with diabetes, including improved glycemic control, BP management, body weight and body fat reduction, increased time‐in‐range (TIR), reduced cardiovascular risk, mitigation of sarcopenia, prevention of hypoglycemia, decreased insulin requirements, as well as promotion of balanced nutrition and overall health.

Evidence

Multiple systematic reviews and meta‐analyses support the clinical benefits of DSF in glycemic and metabolic control. A meta‐analysis that included products such as Glucerna® SR (Abbott Laboratories, Illinois, USA), SlimFast® (Glanbia, Florida, USA) Medifast® (Medifast Inc., Baltimore, USA), Probiotec® Formula WL (Probiotec Limited, Victoria, Australia), and MicroDiet® (Downlee Systems Ltd., Derbyshire, UK) showed that in overweight patients or obese patients with T2D, partial meal replacement with DSF or liquid meal replacements under dietitian supervision significantly reduced body weight and systolic BP, and marginal reductions were also observed in other metabolic parameters (e.g., HbA1c, FBG, and fasting insulin) 10 . Another meta‐analysis that included products like Diben® (Fresenius Kabi, Hesse, Germany), Glucerna®, Diasip® (Nutricia, Amsterdam, Netherlands), and Nutrison® Advanced Diason (Nutricia, Amsterdam, Netherlands) demonstrated that in patients with diabetes, oral or tube‐fed DSF improved blood glucose parameters, and particularly reduced FBG and HbA1c while increasing high‐density lipoprotein‐cholesterol (HDL‐C) compared to standard formulas (SF) 20 . Furthermore, a study demonstrated that long‐term use of DSF may reduce cardiovascular risk 8 .

Several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) also offer compelling evidence of the efficacy of DSF in supporting diabetes management. In one study, patients with T2D supplementing with Glucerna® (228 kcal/serving) 1–2 times daily under dietitian guidance, either alongside or as a meal replacement, experienced significant improvements in blood glucose markers, body weight, visceral fat, BP, as well as increased fat‐free mass (FFM) 21 . In newly diagnosed T2D patients, lifestyle intervention combined with daily Glucerna® SR powder (221 kcal/serving) as a breakfast replacement for 4 weeks resulted in better glycemic variability (GV) and TIR compared to lifestyle intervention alone 11 . For intensive care unit (ICU) patients with hyperglycemia, Glucerna® (33% carbohydrates, tube feeding) combined with subcutaneous insulin (4 times daily) significantly reduced hypoglycemia incidence compared to continuous intravenous insulin infusion 22 .

In malnourished or sarcopenia‐prone patients with prediabetes or diabetes, a prospective study showed that daily supplementation with 1–2 bottles of Nutavant® Plus Diabetica (Persan Farma, Argentona, Spain) (40% carbohydrates, 300 kcal/bottle) plus 3 months of dietitian‐led nutrition education significantly reduced malnutrition rates and improved muscle strength in sarcopenic patients 23 . Patients with T1D, consuming half a serving of Glucerna® SR (205 kcal/serving) at 10:30 PM and during high hypoglycemia‐risk periods, experienced reduced frequency and severity of hypoglycemia within 48 h 12 .

Randomized trials have shown that certain DSF significantly lowered postprandial glucose (PPG) and improved other blood glucose parameters. Nutren® (Nestlé Health Science, Lausanne, Switzerland; 250 kcal/serving) reduced PPG area‐under‐the‐curve (AUC) and insulin levels compared to isocaloric regular meals in patients with T2D 9 . A double‐blind crossover trial showed that Glucerna® SR (47.7% carbohydrates, 214 kcal/serving) increased glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) concentrations vs SF in overnight‐fasted subjects with T2D 24 . Glucerna® Select (33% carbohydrates, tube feeding) reduced GV and insulin requirements for 48 h in ICU patients 25 . In long‐term care patients, split‐dose administration of Fortimel® DiaCare (Nutricia, Amsterdam, Netherlands; 41.2% carbohydrates, 302 kcal/200 mL/serving) achieved lower PPG AUC than single‐dose consumption 26 . A Taiwanese study demonstrated that in healthy young adults, replacing a regular breakfast with Glucerna® SR (50% carbohydrates, 480 kcal) resulted in lower PPG and insulin levels 27 .

Consensus statement 4

For individuals with prediabetes and specific diabetic conditions, or conditions related to diabetes, including poor glycemic control, GD, newly diagnosed diabetes, malnutrition, postbariatric surgery, high cardiovascular disease risk, and those at risk of muscle loss, DSF is an option to improve glycemic control and overall health.

Evidence

A prospective study on malnourished prediabetic patients demonstrated that nutrition education by dietitians, combined with daily supplementation of 1–2 bottles of Nutavant® Plus Diabetica (40% carbohydrates, 300 kcal/bottle) for 3 months, significantly reduced malnutrition rates and improved muscle strength in sarcopenic patients 23 . An open‐label parallel trial involving healthy individuals with above‐average weight, blood glucose, and waist circumference showed that replacing breakfast with Glucerna® SR (180 kcal) under a dietitian's supervision, together with dietary counseling, led to better weight and glycemic control after 12 weeks compared to dietary counseling alone 28 .

Multiple studies have indicated that DSF improves glycemic control and clinical outcomes of hospitalized patients with diabetes. An RCT found that mechanically ventilated patients with diabetes or hyperglycemia receiving a DSF (Diaba® HP [IQMED, Larnaca, Cyprus] or Glucerna® Select via tube feeding) for ≥5 days until ICU discharge had significantly lower insulin requirements, GV, and infection rates than those on high‐protein SFs 29 . Another RCT showed that patients using Novasource® Diabet Plus (Nestlé Health Science, Lausanne, Switzerland; 40% carbohydrates, tube feeding) required less insulin and had better glucose control than the high‐protein SF group 30 . A blinded pilot trial revealed that hyperglycemic ICU patients with diabetes fed exclusively with Glucerna® Select (33% carbohydrates, tube feeding) for 48 h experienced reduced GV and needed less insulin 25 . A retrospective study from National Taiwan University Hospital showed similar results 31 . A small study on hospitalized patients with diabetes reported that exclusive use of Glucerna®‐Ex for 3 months reduced pre‐ and postmeal GV, insulin requirements, and HbA1c 15 .

DSF also benefits patients with newly diagnosed diabetes. The DiRECT trial enrolled obese patients with T2D (diagnosed ≤6 years, average 3 years) and placed them exclusively under a nutrition formula (59% carbohydrates, 825–853 kcal/day) for 3–5 months, followed by gradual dietary reintroduction. At 12 months, almost half achieved diabetes remission, and in 24 months, one‐third achieved remission 32 , 33 . Another RCT in patients with newly diagnosed T2D showed that replacing breakfast with Glucerna® SR powder (221 kcal) for 4 weeks alongside lifestyle counseling improved GV and TIR more than lifestyle changes alone 11 . Short‐term (2–3 months) use of Glucerna® (190 kcal/serving, 4 servings/day) in patients diagnosed with T2D (≤6 years) as the sole nutrition source led to significant weight loss, better metabolic improvements, and diabetes remission in 60% of subjects at week 8 34 .

Evidence also supports the use of DSF in obese individuals with diabetes, for whom weight loss is an important cardiometabolic goal. A systematic review and meta‐analysis found that liquid meal replacements (e.g., Glucerna® SR, SlimFast®, Medifast®) as part of weight‐loss diets improved cardiometabolic risk factors in patients with T2D (e.g., weight, systolic BP, body fat, HbA1c, FBG, fasting insulin) better than traditional low‐calorie diets, although the difference was marginal 10 .

DSF has also demonstrated benefits in patients with GD. A 2024 RCT in patients with GD showed that replacing milk with a DSF (44.6% carbohydrates, 150 kcal/serving) at breakfast and postdinner snacks, alongside 6‐meal/day diets and exercise, significantly lowered PPG, delayed insulin initiation, and shortened insulin use duration vs diet and exercise alone 35 . Earlier research reported that patients with GD following a 6‐meal/day plan with Glucerna® at breakfast for 2 weeks had reduced FBG and PPG; similar benefits were seen in glucose‐intolerant pregnant women 36 .

Diabetes and sarcopenia share a bidirectional relationship: aging‐related muscle loss and fat gain elevate diabetes risk, whereas diabetes exacerbates muscle decline 37 . RCTs demonstrated that overweight or obese patients with T2D who replaced 1–2 daily meals with Glucerna® (228 kcal/serving) for 45–90 days increased their FFM21. Another study found that patients with sarcopenia using Nutavant® Plus Diabetica (300 kcal/bottle, 1–2 bottles/day) for 3 months experienced reduced malnutrition and improved their muscle strength 23 .

The use of DSF may also be warranted in patients receiving medications or undergoing procedures for weight loss. For example, GLP‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs), primarily used for glycemic control in patients with T2D and also for weight management, may cause muscle loss and gastrointestinal side effects, leading to impaired nutrient absorption 38 , 39 . Thus, adequate protein intake and micronutrient supplementation are recommended for these patients 40 . Additionally, ADS consensus guidelines endorse DSF for those on GLP‐1 RAs or postbariatric surgery to mitigate muscle loss 13 . The American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery's guidelines recommend the use of DSFs (e.g., Glucerna®, Diabetisource® [Nestlé Health Science, Lausanne, Switzerland]) to improve glycemic control postbariatric surgery in patients with diabetes, though clinical evidence is limited 41 . Likewise, the ADS consensus supports the use of DSF in patients undergoing bariatric surgery or using a GLP‐1 RA for weight loss to support protein and nutrient intake.

In patients at high risk for cardiovascular disease, meta‐analyses show DSF improves cardiometabolic markers, including reduced HbA1c, FBG, fasting insulin, and BP, while raising HDL‐C 8 , 10 , 20 . Formulas rich in MUFAs also lower triglycerides and boost HDL‐C 42 .

DSF can also effectively address malnutrition in patients with diabetes. An RCT involving elderly malnourished patients with T2D found that Diben® DRINK Vanilla (300 kcal/serving, 2 times daily for 12 weeks) improved PPG vs isocaloric SFs 43 . Nutavant® Plus Diabetica (1–2 bottles/day for 3 months) reduced malnutrition and enhanced muscle strength 23 . A retrospective study demonstrated that Glucerna® 1.5 (330 kcal/serving, 2 times daily for 1 year) increased calorie intake, stabilized blood glucose, and raised albumin (+10.6%) and hemoglobin (+6.4%) 44 . Studies on Taiwanese residing in home care and long‐term facilities reported weight gain, improved albumin, mid‐arm circumference, and reduced triglycerides with DSF supplementation 45 , 46 .

Patients with diabetes having diabetic kidney disease (DKD) need nutritional support to address both hyperglycemia and renal decline. As such, the 2020 Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative guidelines recommend kidney‐specific formulas (high‐calorie, low‐electrolyte, adjusted protein) for chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients 47 . Taiwanese trials in CKD/DKD patients (stages 3–5) showed that renal formulas (Nutritec®‐Enjoy [Nutritec‐Enjoy, Taipei, Taiwan], Suplena® [Abbott Laboratories, Illinois, USA]) improved dietary compliance, reduced proteinuria, slowed renal decline, and increased weight/grip strength 48 , 49 . For hemodialysis patients, Nepro® HP (Abbott Laboratories, Illinois, USA; 396 kcal/serving) led to a lower PPG AUC vs SFs 50 .

Consensus statement 5

DSFs are characterized by their unique nutritional profile, which includes slow‐digesting carbohydrates (low‐GI), high‐quality fats (e.g., MUFAs), protein, dietary fiber, and essential vitamins and minerals. Furthermore, DSFs should be easy to prepare, convenient to use, and have standardized portion sizes.

Evidence

A systematic review and meta‐analysis incorporating 54 trials demonstrated that low‐GI diets significantly reduced HbA1c, FBG, body mass index (BMI), total cholesterol, and low‐density lipoprotein‐cholesterol (LDL‐C) 5 . Another meta‐analysis corroborated these findings, showing that low‐GI diets were more effective than high‐GI diets in improving Homeostasis Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA‐IR) and fasting glucose control in patients with T2D 51 .

Additionally, systematic reviews and meta‐analyses have indicated that MUFA‐rich diets supervised by a dietitian significantly improved several cardiometabolic parameters (e.g., FBG and triglycerides) 44 , 52 . On the other hand, RCTs revealed that MUFA‐rich diets significantly reduced liver fat accumulation and improved insulin sensitivity 53 , 54 . A crossover trial involving patients with noninsulin dependent diabetes found that a MUFA‐rich diet administered for 3 weeks under dietitian supervision significantly reduced blood glucose and BP without adversely affecting lipid profiles, compared to a higher‐carbohydrate diet 6 . A case–control study in women with metabolic syndrome showed that a 14‐week MUFA‐rich high‐fat diet with fiber supplement reduced waist circumference and triglycerides more than a MUFA‐rich diet alone 7 .

In patients with T2D, soluble or insoluble dietary fiber plus prebiotics can reduce total cholesterol levels and BMI, with higher fiber intake linked to lower mortality risk 55 , 56 . Additionally, a review of RCTs highlighted that synbiotics (specific probiotics with dietary fiber) improved metabolic and antioxidant profiles in patients with T2D 57 . Daily supplementation with 225–1,500 mg of myo‐inositol in individuals with prediabetes, diabetes, or GD lowered HOMA‐IR and fasting insulin levels 58 .

Vitamin D has shown potential benefits in insulin resistance and in providing renal protection. Systematic reviews and meta‐analyses have suggested that vitamin D supplementation (20 IU daily or up to a maximum single dose of 300,000 IU) can mitigate insulin resistance in patients with T2D who have vitamin D deficiency 59 . A separate review of studies conducted in Taiwan noted that vitamin D deficiency in patients with diabetes increased the risk of DKD progression, though further research is needed to validate the efficacy of vitamin D interventions 60 .

Although studies demonstrate the benefits of magnesium, chromium, and herbal compounds in diabetes, the ADA 2025 guidelines maintain that evidence remains insufficient to recommend high‐dose supplementation 14 . Regarding chromium, the Taiwan Food and Drug Administration recommends that the total daily intake of chromium from general food products should not exceed 200 μg, and for Foods for Special Medical Purposes, if the chromium content exceeds this limit, safety evidence must be provided 61 . Furthermore, the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) suggests caution should be exercised when taking chromium in the presence of kidney or liver disease 62 . Regarding herbal ingredients, the ADA guidelines note that there is insufficient evidence to support the use of cinnamon, turmeric, curcumin, or aloe vera for glycemic control 14 . Similarly, common Taiwanese herbal supplements such as bitter melon peptides, Gymnema sylvestre, guava leaf extract, and brown algae extracts require further large‐scale clinical studies to confirm their optimal dosage and safety.

CONCLUSION

MNT provides the foundation for achieving glycemic targets in diabetes; however, in situations where adhering to a whole‐foods diet is not possible or inconvenient, DSFs supported by clinical evidence are viable options that can help improve metabolic parameters in patients with diabetes. DSFs are specially formulated to contain low‐GI carbohydrates, MUFAs, fiber, adequate protein, and essential micronutrients, which are beneficial in improving clinical outcomes of patients with diabetes. Under the supervision of a physician or a dietitian, DSF supplementation provides benefits for patients with diabetes, including improved glycemic control and weight and BP management. Additionally, they have shown benefits for other diabetic conditions or conditions related to diabetes, including poor glycemic control, GD, newly diagnosed diabetes, malnutrition, postbariatric surgery, high cardiovascular disease risk, and those at risk of muscle loss. Nonetheless, the effectiveness of DSF is contingent upon its integration with holistic diabetes management. This consensus statement provides guidance to clinicians and dietitians in integrating DSF into the care plan of patients with diabetes.
