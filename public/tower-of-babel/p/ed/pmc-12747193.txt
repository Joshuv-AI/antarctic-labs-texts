Molecular detection and characterization of Anaplasma phagocytophilum and Neoehrlichia mikurensis in wild rodents and their ectoparasites in South Korea
Authors: Yujin Kim, Min Gyou Lee, Hyang Hee Lee, So-Jin Yang, Ji-Eun Lee, Eunji Kim, Yi Deun Ha, Eun Ju Kim, Jung Mi Seo, Sun-Hee Kim, Changjong Moon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWild rodents act as crucial reservoir hosts for various tick-borne pathogens, such as Anaplasma phagocytophilum and Neoehrlichia mikurensis, which are responsible for the emergence of zoonotic diseases in humans. While tick-borne pathogens have been examined in various animal species, the genetic diversities present in wild rodents and their ectoparasites remain poorly understood. This study examined the prevalence and genetic characteristics of A. phagocytophilum and N. mikurensis in wild rodents, mites, and ticks from South Korea. PCR amplification and sequencing of the 16S rRNA, msp4, and groEL genes were performed to genotype A. phagocytophilum (16S rRNA and msp4) and N. mikurensis (16S rRNA and groEL). A. phagocytophilum was identified in 25.8 % of rodents and in ixodid ticks collected from rodents, with a minimum infection rate (MIR) of 2.8 %. A. phagocytophilum was detected in mites (MIR: 0.4 %) from rodents, indicating their potential role in pathogen circulation. Of the 461 wild rodents included in this study, five (1.1 %) tested positive for N. mikurensis. Furthermore, one positive pool was identified in Ixodes nipponensis nymphs (MIR: 0.1 %), representing the first documented occurrence of N. mikurensis in ticks in South Korea. Phylogenetic analysis indicated that the A. phagocytophilum sequences obtained in this study cluster with sequences from South Korea and China associated with rodents or I. nipponensis, while remaining distinct from those of European origin. The N. mikurensis sequences clustered with East Asian strains, forming two distinct groups separate from European lineages. These findings corroborate the hypothesis that wild rodents and their ectoparasites play a role in the natural maintenance and transmission of A. phagocytophilum and N. mikurensis in South Korea. Given the growing acknowledgment of these pathogens as emerging threats to human health, continued surveillance and molecular characterization are essential to understand their regional distribution and implications for public health.Keywords: Anaplasma phagocytophilum, Neoehrlichia mikurensis, Prevalence, Genetic characteristics, South Korea

Introduction

Vertebrate hosts are essential for the maintenance and circulation of tick-borne pathogens in enzootic foci. Small rodents and wild ruminants serve as primary reservoirs for various pathogens (Alberdi et al., 2000; Bown et al., 2008; Nicholson et al., 1998; Petrovec et al., 2003; Scharf et al., 2011). Particularly, rodents play a crucial role in the tick life cycle, acting as primary feeding and maintenance hosts for the developmental stages of several tick species (Silaghi et al., 2012). Anaplasmosis and ehrlichiosis are significant diseases that are transmitted to humans by arthropod vectors, such as ticks, with rodents constituting an essential component in the ecological cycles sustaining these pathogens (Rar et al., 2020). Human encroachment on undisturbed habitats has led to more frequent interactions with animals that act as important reservoirs, particularly those in the order Rodentia (Panthawong et al., 2020).

The prevalence of Anaplasma phagocytophilum infections has been increasing due to accelerating global climate change (Bouchard et al., 2019; Karshima et al., 2023). A wide range of animal species are recognized as hosts for A. phagocytophilum, with humans serving as incidental, dead-end hosts (Dumler et al., 2005). In Europe, rodents serve as reservoir hosts for a specific genetic lineage of A. phagocytophilum, which is transmitted by Ixodes trianguliceps (Huhn et al., 2014; Jaarsma et al., 2019; Jahfari et al., 2014; Langenwalder et al., 2020; Rar et al., 2021). Previous studies indicate that A. phagocytophilum can be transmitted by I. ricinus ticks, affecting both humans and domestic animals (Beninati et al., 2006; Liz et al., 2000; Spitalska et al., 2008; Stefancikova et al., 2008). In the United States, I. scapularis (blacklegged tick or deer tick) is the primary vector of A. phagocytophilum, with rodents serving as established reservoir hosts (Massung et al., 2003). Transovarial transmission in Ixodes ticks remains unconfirmed, indicating that the persistence of A. phagocytophilum in natural ecosystems depends on reservoir vertebrate hosts, which exhibit host-specific circulation patterns (Jahfari et al., 2014; Svitalkova et al., 2015).

Recent studies indicate that specific ecotypes of A. phagocytophilum are primarily associated with certain host species, and these ecotypes differ in zoonotic potential (Jaarsma et al., 2019; Jahfari et al., 2014). Furthermore, multilocus sequence typing (MLST) analyses have revealed differences in infection potential based on the clonal complexes of A. phagocytophilum (Huhn et al., 2014). This pathogen has been identified in small mammals, including rodents and shrews (Crocidura lasiura), in South Korea (Chae et al., 2008; Kim et al., 2006). In addition, A. phagocytophilum has been detected in multiple rodent species in South Korea, with prevalence rates ranging from 13.5 % to 21.4 % (Choi et al., 2024; Jha et al., 2018).

Strain variations of A. phagocytophilum may arise from geographic location, host origin, and the species of tick vectors (Jaarsma et al., 2019). The 16S rRNA gene is deemed excessively conserved for phylogenetic analyses; consequently, alternative genetic markers, such as groESL (the operon comprising the groES and groEL genes), ankA (ankyrin repeat protein A), msp2 (major surface protein 2), and msp4, have been frequently used to investigate host preferences among various A. phagocytophilum strains (Stuen et al., 2013). Analyses using these genetic markers, such as 16S rRNA, groEL, ankA, and msp, have documented intraspecific heterogeneity in A. phagocytophilum. This complex of closely related strains exhibits variations in vector and host preferences, geographic distribution, pathogenicity, and the severity of clinical manifestations (Carpi et al., 2009; Massung et al., 2006; Stuen, 2007; Stuen et al., 2002). Significant disparities in the epidemiology of granulocytic anaplasmosis have been observed between Europe and North America. In the United States, granulocytic anaplasmosis is among the most prevalent tick-borne diseases, whereas in Europe, it is relatively uncommon (Strle, 2004). Consequently, it is essential to conduct analyses using diverse genetic markers for A. phagocytophilum in South Korea.

Neoehrlichia mikurensis was first described in endothelial cells of rats (Rattus norvegicus) spleens and in I. ovatus ticks on Mikura Island, Japan, in 2004 (Kawahara et al., 2004). Meanwhile, N. mikurensis has since been documented in ticks and mammals throughout Europe and Asia (Cheng et al., 2025; Jahfari et al., 2012). In 2010, N. mikurensis was recognized as a human pathogen in Sweden (Welinder-Olsson et al., 2010). Subsequently, several case series and individual instances of N. mikurensis infections have been reported, predominantly among immunosuppressed individuals owing to hematologic neoplasms, splenectomies, or immunosuppressive therapy (Andreasson et al., 2015; Boyer et al., 2021; Grankvist et al., 2014; Lenart et al., 2021; Maurer et al., 2013; Pekova et al., 2011; von Loewenich et al., 2010). Furthermore, N. mikurensis can also cause disease (neoehrlichiosis) in immunocompetent individuals or lead to asymptomatic infections (Li et al., 2012). The disease presents with nonspecific symptoms such as joint pain, prolonged fever, migrating pain, chills, nocturnal sweating, and an increased risk of thromboembolic events, which are characteristic of N. mikurensis infection. The reported prevalence of N. mikurensis in questing I. ricinus ticks varies across 18 European countries, ranging from 0.1 % in Denmark to 24.3 % in Hungary (Portillo et al., 2018). In South Korea, N. mikurensis was first detected in 2018 in small mammals, with a prevalence rate of 28.6 % (Jha et al., 2018).

Despite current extensive research on pathogens circulating between animals and ticks, exploration into the genetic diversity of these pathogens in wild rodents and any respective ectoparasites remains limited. This study aimed to investigate the epidemiological status of A. phagocytophilum and N. mikurensis in wild rodents as reservoirs in South Korea and to identify the vertebrate host species and arthropod vectors, including ticks and mites, involved in the circulation of these pathogens. DNA sequences of A. phagocytophilum and N. mikurensis, sourced from wild rodents and their ectoparasites, were analyzed in comparison with those from ticks and mites. This study aims to further our understanding of the distribution of A. phagocytophilum and N. mikurensis in South Korea through phylogenetic analysis, contributing valuable data to their epidemiology.

Materials and methods

Animal and sample collection

A total of 461 live male and female wild rodents were captured in the southwestern region of the Korean Peninsula between April 2023 to March 2025 using 3” × 3.5” × 9” folding Sherman traps (Sherman Traps Inc., Tallahassee, FL, USA). The traps, intended for small rodents, were assigned sequential numbers. Captured rodents were transported to the laboratory in the same traps, morphologically identified, and subsequently euthanized using CO2 and cervical dislocation when deemed appropriate. Subsequently, blood and organ samples were then collected from each animal. DNA extraction was conducted using whole blood and spleen samples.

Surveillance of ticks and mites

To collect external ectoparasites, including ticks and mites, from each euthanized rodent, the animal was suspended upside down by the tail on a rod positioned above a bowl of water, which was placed inside a beaker. This setup facilitated the collection of external parasites that dislodged and fell into the beaker over a 24-h period. The dislodged ticks and mites were collected from the water surface using a fine brush. Mites were preserved in 70 % ethanol for pathogen analysis, and the remaining collected ticks were stored at −80 °C for further processing. Ticks were identified using an Axio Zoom.V16 microscope, following the guidelines established by Yamaguti et al. (1971). The total number of ticks and mites collected from each rodent was visually counted. Due to the high number of mites, a pooled sample of 1–20 individuals was selected for DNA extraction and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis. Depending on the tick species and developmental stage, 1 to 30 nymphs and 1 to 50 larvae were collected from each rodent to assess pathogen prevalence. The remaining mites were mounted on slides for species identification; however, those results are not included in this study.

Screening for Anaplasma and Ehrlichia spp. using quantitative real-time PCR

Approximately 500 mg of spleen from each rodent and tick pool was transferred to a 2 mL hard tissue grinding tube. Subsequently, 700 μL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 4 °C) was added, and the mixture was ground for 30 s at 5000 rpm, repeated twice, using a Precellys® 2000 homogenizer (Bertin Technologies, Montigny-le-Bretonneux, France). The samples were centrifuged at 4 °C for 5 min at 13,000 rpm. The supernatants were subjected to DNA/RNA extraction using a Maxwell® RSC viral total nucleic acid purification kit (Promega, Fitchburg, WI, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions. DNA and RNA were extracted from blood samples using the Maxwell® RSC Whole Blood DNA kit (Promega), following the manufacturer's instructions. Mites were rinsed with sterile PBS prior to DNA extraction. The mite pool was subsequently ground using a manual tip. The supernatants were subjected to DNA/RNA extraction using a Maxwell® RSC viral total nucleic acid purification kit (Promega), according to the manufacturer's guidelines.

Nucleic acids were analyzed using an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio™ 5 quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) system (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). Remaining samples were stored at 4 °C for further analysis. Detection of Anaplasma and Ehrlichia spp. was conducted by targeting the 16S rRNA gene through qRT-PCR using POPGEN® pathogen detection kits (PostBio, Namyangju, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea), as described previously (Kim et al., 2024). PCR amplification was performed using 20 μL reaction mixtures composed of 15 μL of the POPGEN® qPCR reaction mix and 5 μL of template DNA. The thermal cycling program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s.

Molecular characterization of Anaplasma and Ehrlichia spp.

Positive samples identified via qRT-PCR were subjected to amplification of the 16S rRNA and msp4 gene fragments for A. phagocytophilum using nested PCR on a ProFlex™ PCR system (Applied Biosystems). Similarly, amplification of the 16S rRNA and groEL genes for N. mikurensis was performed, followed by sequencing in both cases. The amplification products of the 16S rRNA, msp4, and groEL genes, along with the primer sets used for the identification of A. phagocytophilum and N. mikurensis, are presented in Table 1 (Barlough et al., 1996; Bown et al., 2007; de la Fuente et al., 2005; Jha et al., 2018; Oh et al., 2009). The PCR products were analyzed and visualized utilizing an automated capillary electrophoresis system FSv2-CE2 (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The amplified PCR products were sequenced using Sanger methodology at Macrogen (Seoul, South Korea), using an ABI 3730XL DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems). Nucleotide sequences were aligned using Clustal W within the MEGA-X software (version 10.2.6; http://www.megasoftware.net) and compared to reference sequences in the GenBank database via NCBI BLAST.

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic trees for A. phagocytophilum and N. mikurensis were constructed using maximum-likelihood estimations and neighbor-joining algorithms implemented in MEGA-X software version 10.2.6 (http://www.megasoftware.net). Phylogenetic estimations for A. phagocytophilum were based on partial 16S rRNA (825 unambiguously aligned base pairs, best-fit model: K2+G) and msp4 sequences (360 bp, model: T92+G). For N. mikurensis, estimations were based on partial 16S rRNA (467 bp, model: K2+G) and groEL sequences (346 bp, model: T92+G). Bootstrap analysis was performed with 1000 replicates to evaluate confidence levels. A total of 28 Anaplasma and 6 Neoehrlichia nucleotide sequences (16S rRNA gene) were submitted to GenBank (accession numbers PV618371–PV618398 and PV616888–PV616893, respectively). A total of 13 Anaplasma and 6 Neoehrlichia nucleotide sequences, specifically partial msp4 and groEL genes, respectively, were submitted to GenBank (accession numbers: PV646526–PV646544).

Results

Collection and classification of wild rodents and ticks

A total of 461 small rodents were captured in the southwestern region of the Korean Peninsula between April 2023 and March 2025 (Fig. 1). The specimens were morphologically classified into the following species: Apodemus agrarius (A. agrarius; 407, 88.3 %), Crocidura spp. (30, 6.5 %), Craseomys regulus (C. regulus; 23, 5.0 %), and Micromys minutus (1, 0.2 %). A total of 1804 ticks were collected and grouped into 279 pools according to developmental stage (nymph and larva) and species (Table 2). Additionally, 16,406 mites were collected and arranged into 800 pools for pathogenicity testing. Ixodes nipponensis accounted for 17.3 % of the total ticks collected from wild rodents, totaling 312 nymphs. Haemaphysalis longicornis represented 0.7 % of the total, with 12 nymphs. Haemaphysalis flava constituted 0.3 % of the total tick population, with five nymphs. Amblyomma testudinarium represented 0.1 % of the tick population, with a single nymph observed. Furthermore, 1474 larvae, representing 81.7 % of the total ticks collected, could not be classified to the species level. No adult ticks were identified in the samples collected from wild rodents, whereas the predominant nymphal species was Ixodes spp., particularly I. nipponensis (Table 2). Ticks were collected throughout the year, with a notable increase in numbers observed from summer to autumn (Fig. 2). Larvae were collected in significant numbers during this period, constituting a substantial proportion of the total ticks collected. Nymphs were also collected throughout the year, except from January to March.

Prevalence of A. phagocytophilum and N. mikurensis

The qRT-PCR analysis was conducted on blood and spleen samples from 461 live and captured wild rodents to examine the presence of A. phagocytophilum and N. mikurensis. In total, 119 of the 461 tested rodents were positive for Anaplasma spp. and Ehrlichia spp. Meanwhile, the following positive rates were revealed by a species-specific analysis: A. agrarius, 106 out of 407 (26.0 %); Crocidura spp., 11 out of 30 (36.7 %); C. regulus, 2 out of 23 (8.7 %). One individual of Micromys minutus was captured, although the test was negative for A. phagocytophilum. A total of 279 tick pools were analyzed, consisting of 146 pools of I. nipponensis, 4 pools of H. longicornis, 4 pools of H. flava, 1 pool of A. testudinarium, and 124 pools for larvae. Nymphs and larvae were grouped by species and life stage to assess pathogen prevalence. The minimum infection rate (MIR) for ticks testing positive for A. phagocytophilum was 2.8 %, with I. nipponensis at 11.9 %, H. longicornis at 16.7 %, H. flava at 20.0 %, A. testudinarium at 0 %, and larvae at 0.7 %, respectively (Table 2). In addition to the positivity rate of tick pools, the MIR was calculated to estimate pathogen prevalence at the individual tick level. While the pool positivity rate represents the proportion of positive pools among all those tested, the MIR provides a conservative estimate by assuming that at least one tick in each positive pool is infected. Since the MIR allows comparisons across tick species and studies on an individual basis, this metric was emphasized in the presentation of our results. Among the 800 mite pools analyzed, 59 tested positive for A. phagocytophilum, yielding a MIR of 0.4 %. N. mikurensis was detected in 5 of the 461 wild rodents, all of which were A. agrarius. A positive pool was identified in I. nipponensis ticks, with a MIR of 0.1 %; however, it was not detected in mites (Table 3).

A total of 119 samples tested positive for A. phagocytophilum using qRT-PCR. Nucleotide sequencing was conducted on all positive samples, and high-quality sequences, selected according to rodent species and tick origin, were utilized for phylogenetic analysis. A total of 41 sequences sourced from wild rodents, ticks, and mites were included, which comprised 28 and 13 sequences that were positive for 16S rRNA and msp4, respectively (Fig. 4). BLAST analysis of the 16S rRNA gene revealed 17 sequences from A. agrarius, two from Crocidura spp., five from I. nipponensis, and four from mite pools (Fig. 4A), all exhibiting 98.67–100 % similarity. A further BLAST analysis of the msp4 gene identified 13 sequences: five from A. agrarius, six from I. nipponensis, and two from mite pools. A phylogenetic analysis indicated that these sequences clustered together, forming a distinct group that included A. agrarius, I. nipponensis, and mite-derived isolates. These were clearly separated from the clusters formed by I. persulcatus, I. ricinus, and I. trianguliceps, as reported in a previous study (Rar et al., 2014). Our sequences within this cluster exhibited complete identity (100 %) with a strain isolated from I. nipponensis in South Korea (OM294663) (Fig. 4B).

For N. mikurensis, 6 out of 119 positive samples in the qRT-PCR analysis were sequenced, with all six confirming the presence of both the 16S rRNA and groEL genes. N. mikurensis was distinctly separated from other genera in the family Anaplasmataceae, forming a robust sister clade to the genus Ehrlichia (Fig. 5A). Phylogenetic analysis using groEL, along with geographical and host/vector data, revealed four distinct clusters: northeastern China and the Asian region of Russia, Europe, southwestern China and Japan, and southeastern China and Japan (Li et al., 2013). Three strains (PV646526, PV646528, and PV646529) among the isolates were found to cluster with the northeastern China group, whereas a further three (PV646527, PV646530, and PV646531) were associated with the southeastern China cluster. PV646528 (A. agrarius, South Korea) demonstrated 98.2 % identity with JQ359073 (A. agrarius, China); meanwhile, PV646530 (I. nipponensis) and PV646531 (A. agrarius) displayed 98.2 % and 98.8 % identity, respectively, with JQ359064 (A. agrarius, China) (Fig. 5B). All sequences examined in this study were categorized as the single species N. mikurensis.

Discussion

This study investigated wild rodents, ticks, and mites to determine the presence of A. phagocytophilum and N. mikurensis in South Korea. The findings corroborate previous reports indicating that wild rodents play an important role in the maintenance of A. phagocytophilum and N. mikurensis, as evidenced by infections detected in multiple ectoparasite species collected from these rodents, which exhibited a high degree of genetic similarity. (Beninati et al., 2006; Liz et al., 2000; Panthawong et al., 2020; Spitalska et al., 2008; Stefancikova et al., 2008). The prevalence of A. phagocytophilum in wild rodents was 25.8 %, exceeding the previously reported range of 13.5 %–21.4 % in wild rodents in South Korea (Choi et al., 2024; Jha et al., 2018). A. phagocytophilum infection was identified in ixodid ticks collected from wild rodents, with an MIR of 2.8 %. Additionally, mites obtained from rodents tested positive for A. phagocytophilum, with an MIR of 0.4 %. The detection of A. phagocytophilum in multiple ectoparasite species suggests a possible association with its circulation; however, infections may reflect acquisition from infected hosts rather than independent maintenance within the ectoparasite population. Furthermore, the high prevalence of A. phagocytophilum in wild rodents, along with its detection in multiple tick species associated with these rodents, provides valuable baseline data for future research. In particular, these findings may contribute to a better understanding of ecotype-specific variations of A. phagocytophilum and their potential implications for human infection (Jahfari et al., 2014). The first documented case of human A. phagocytophilum infection in South Korea was reported in 2013 (Kim et al., 2014). Additionally, the seroprevalence of A. phagocytophilum, as determined by immunofluorescence testing, has been estimated at 1.8 % among Korean patients with acute fever (Heo et al., 2002). These findings suggest that Anaplasma infection may be a potential etiological agent in patients with febrile illness.

This study conducted a phylogenetic analysis of A. phagocytophilum using both the 16S rRNA and msp4 genes. Previous phylogenetic studies of A. phagocytophilum have primarily utilized ribosomal and housekeeping genes. While ribosomal genes are highly conserved and may lack sufficient resolution to differentiate closely related species or strains (Dumler et al., 2001), combining housekeeping genes, such as groEL, with major surface proteins (MSPs) has proven effective for distinguishing A. phagocytophilum isolates. Among these markers, particular attention has been given to MSPs, especially msp4, owing to their potential role in host-pathogen interactions and their utility in phylogenetic analyses. A prominent protein antigen is located on the outer membrane of A. phagocytophilum, with several immunodominant MSPs demonstrating sequence homology to A. marginale msp2 and msp4 (Lin et al., 2004), as well as Ehrlichia ruminantium msp1 (van Vliet et al., 1994). The precise biological role of msp4 remains unclear; however, it is likely involved in host-pathogen interactions and may experience accelerated evolution relative to other nuclear-encoded proteins, influenced by selective pressure from host immune responses (de la Fuente et al., 2001, 2002, 2003a, 2003b). Therefore, we incorporated msp4 in conjunction with 16S rRNA in our analysis, as it offers enhanced resolution for delineating the phylogenetic and phylogeographic characteristics of A. phagocytophilum strains. In this study, the 16S rRNA gene successfully differentiated A. phagocytophilum from other taxa within the Anaplasmataceae family, such as Ehrlichia and Anaplasma species. However, it lacked the resolution necessary to elucidate phylogeographic differentiation among A. phagocytophilum strains. In contrast, the msp4-based analysis identified a distinct clade encompassing the strains examined in this study. Despite utilizing relatively short sequences, the phylogenetic analysis revealed that the A. phagocytophilum sequences clustered more closely with the reference sequence from I. nipponensis in South Korea (OM294663), while remaining distinct from those derived from I. persulcatus (KF745731, KF745733, and OM294664) and I. trianguliceps (KF420109, KF745729, and KF745730). Meanwhile, strains isolated from I. ricinus (KM205441 and MZ348261) also formed a distinct cluster. Within this clade, the sequences obtained in this study showed 100 % identity with OM294663 (I. nipponensis, South Korea), indicating a close genetic relationship among strains that share both a geographical origin and a common vector. Reference strains from the same region occasionally form distinct clades based on tick species or host origin. However, our findings indicate that sequences from rodents, ticks, and mites exhibit clustering, demonstrating a significant level of genetic similarity. Notably, 94.5 % (312/330) of the non-larval ticks collected from wild rodents were identified as I. nipponensis, thereby supporting the role of I. nipponensis as a potential vector for the genovariant of Anaplasma spp. circulating in this region.

N. mikurensis was initially identified in 2018 from organ tissues of wild rodents (A. agrarius) in the southwestern region of the Korean Peninsula, showing a prevalence of 28.6 % as determined by nested PCR targeting the 16S rRNA and groEL genes (Jha et al., 2018). In this study, N. mikurensis was identified in 5 out of 461 wild rodents (1.1 %), all classified as A. agrarius. In Europe, N. mikurensis has been identified in seven rodent species (A. agararius, A. flavicollis, A. saprophyticus, A. sylvaticus, Myodes glareolus, Microtus agrestis, and Microtus arvalis) and in ten species across Asia, with infection rates varying significantly among species (8.3–52.7 %) (Silaghi et al., 2016). This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in sampling scope or methodology. Additional research with larger and more representative sample sizes is required to clarify the factors contributing to this variation. In South Korea, previous studies have confirmed the presence of N. mikurensis in rodent reservoirs; however, its occurrence in tick vectors has not been reported. Given its detection of N. mikurensis in Ixodes ticks in neighboring countries such as China and Japan (Kawahara et al., 2004; Li et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2024), additional surveillance is necessary to assess its potential circulation in tick populations on the Korean Peninsula. Interestingly, this study identified one positive pool of nymphal I. nipponensis among ticks collected from wild rodents, resulting in a MIR of 0.1 %. This report represents the first detection of N. mikurensis in ticks in South Korea, highlighting the epidemiological significance of this finding.

Phylogenetic analysis of N. mikurensis was conducted using the 16S rRNA and groEL genes. The groEL gene, which encodes a heat shock protein, is frequently employed in phylogenetic studies because it provides an optimal balance between conservation and variability, facilitating improved resolution for distinguishing closely related bacterial species and strains (Hu et al., 2017; Portillo et al., 2018). The 16S rRNA gene effectively differentiated N. mikurensis from other taxa within the Anaplasmataceae family, including Ehrlichia and Anaplasma species. However, it lacked sufficient resolution to elucidate phylogeographic differentiation among N. mikurensis strains. The phylogenetic analysis based on the groEL sequences in this study revealed that several obtained sequences constituted a distinct clade, separate from European reference strains, including those from France (JX392803) and Germany (EU810407), while clustering with strains from northeastern China and Russia. A further collection of three sequences categorized with strains from southeastern China and Japan. A previous study identified four major clades of N. mikurensis in the East Asian strains (Li et al., 2013). Our findings indicate that the sequences acquired in this study belong to two of these clades, suggesting that N. mikurensis infection is present in rodents and ticks within geographically proximate areas.

This study has some limitations. First, the detection rate of N. mikurensis was comparatively low. To efficiently analyze a large number of samples, commercial kits were used for the initial identification of Anaplasma and Ehrlichia spp., followed by sequencing using particular primers. If a detection technique targeting N. mikurensis exclusively had been used, the observed prevalence might have differed. Second, as the study aimed to examine intraspecific variation and basic grouping of infections, the 16S rRNA and msp4 genes were used for A. phagocytophilum. Future studies incorporating more advanced methods, such as groEL sequencing or MLST, would further enhance understanding of A. phagocytophilum diversity and epidemiology. Third, the geographical scope of this study was limited, which may constrain the generalizability of the findings. Expanding the sampling to include a broader range of locations and host species would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the ecological dynamics underlying host-vector-pathogen interactions. Fourth, pathogen detection in ticks may represent acquisition from the host rather than inherent infection, as this study analyzed ticks collected from wild rodents during blood feeding. Although data from engorged ticks provide valuable insights into natural infection patterns, the inclusion of control samples of unfed ticks could offer additional information. Despite these limitations, our study provides essential data on the circulation of infections by simultaneously analyzing infections in wild rodents and their associated ectoparasites in the study area. These findings contribute to the growing epidemiological knowledge necessary for understanding the transmission of emerging zoonotic diseases and for informing public health strategies.

Conclusion

With the global increase in zoonotic infections, further research is required to investigate the host–vector relationships of various tick-borne pathogens. This study confirmed that wild rodents and the associated ticks, which exist in close proximity to humans, can harbor A. phagocytophilum and N. mikurensis, and that some strains form identical genetic clades, indicating the role of rodents and ticks as reservoirs or vectors. Notably, to our knowledge, this is the first report of N. mikurensis in Ixodes ticks in South Korea. Furthermore, the results of this study suggest that A. phagocytophilum infection in wild rodents is likely influenced by the tick species involved, whereas N. mikurensis appears to circulate in South Korea in two distinct phylogenetic clades. These findings provide valuable insights into potential transmission pathways and enhance our understanding of the epidemiology and public health significance of these pathogens.
