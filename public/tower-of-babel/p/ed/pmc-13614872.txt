Statistical quality control practices in a sample of Spanish Blood Establishments: a mixed-methods exploratory study
Authors: Héctor Sarmiento Palao, María I. Ortiz de Salazar Martín, Luis R. Larrea González, Ana M. Arruga Manzano, María L. Ayape Puyales, Aida I. Azcarate Gonzalez, Teresa Díaz Rueda, Marta López Melchor, Pascual Marco Vera, María del Carmen Martín Alonso, María Mercadillo González, Ana I. Perez Aliaga, Magdalena Prohens-Batle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe European Directorate for the Quality of Medicines & HealthCare (EDQM) provides guidance for statistical quality control in blood component manufacturing, including recommendations for sampling and statistical process control. However, the extent to which these recommendations are implemented in routine practice remains unclear.Materials and methodsWe conducted a sequential explanatory mixed-methods study among professionals working in blood establishments, recruited through the Spanish Society of Blood Transfusion blood processing working group. Phase 1 was a cross-sectional survey (10 responding centers across 10 autonomous communities; response rate 77%). Phase 2 comprised semi-structured interviews with a purposive sample of survey respondents (No.=7). Survey findings informed the interview topic guide. Thematic analysis followed the Braun and Clarke approach. Findings from both phases were integrated using a joint display.ResultsRespondents represented centers with varied production volumes, and most had more than 6 years of experience (80%). Use of statistical quality control methods was limited: 90% reported using descriptive statistics only and 10% used control charts. For sample size determination, 80% applied fixed production percentages without formal statistical justification. Spreadsheet-based workflows with custom formulas predominated (90%). Key challenges included determining sample size and laborious report generation (both 80%). Interviews highlighted uncertainty about sampling adequacy, potential for earlier trend detection, perceived complexity of guidance, and the need for interpretive decision support. All participants expressed interest in automated approaches.DiscussionAmong participating centers, variation in the implementation of statistical quality control practices relative to EDQM recommendations suggests opportunities for improved harmonization. Automated tools that embed recommended sampling and monitoring methods may support implementation, although larger studies are needed to estimate prevalence at national level.

INTRODUCTION

Globally, Blood Establishments (BEs) –organizations that collect, process, test, store, and distribute blood components per European regulatory and European Directorate for the Quality of Medicines & HealthCare (EDQM) definitions–handle millions of blood components each year, requiring strict quality control for patient safety and regulatory compliance1. The EDQM, through its Guide to the Preparation, Use and Quality Assurance of Blood Components, establishes quality specifications for blood components including red blood cell concentrates, plasma, and platelet products2.

Beyond product specifications, EDQM guidelines recommend statistical process control (SPC) methodologies for continuous quality monitoring, including control charts for trend detection and statistically-justified sampling strategies based on production volume, presented as supportive guidance in Appendix 3 (Statistical Process Control) of the EDQM Blood Guide2. The UK Joint Professional Advisory Committee (JPAC) guidelines similarly specify, for several component quality parameters, testing of 1% or as determined by statistical process control3.

Despite these recommendations, implementation in routine practice may vary considerably between establishments. A survey of Spanish hospital transfusion services identified variability in transfusion quality indicator adoption4. However, specific data on statistical methodology implementation for blood component manufacturing remains limited. The recent publication of EU Regulation 2024/1938 on Substances of Human Origin (SoHO), applicable from 7 August 2027, reinforces the importance of robust quality systems in BEs, although it does not prescribe specific statistical methodologies5. Understanding current practices is essential for identifying opportunities to support BEs in aligning with international guidelines.

In Spain, the voluntary Fundación CAT certification framework, Estándares en Hemoterapia, provides an additional avenue for quality standardization, with its latest edition aligned with EU Regulation 2024/19385,6. This study aimed to explore current statistical quality control practices in a sample of Spanish BEs and identify opportunities for standardization aligned with EDQM recommendations. The present work forms part of a larger doctoral research project aimed at developing and validating an automated quality control system for BEs. This phase sought to characterise current practices and identify user requirements to inform evidence-based system development, rather than to confirm pre-existing assumptions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

A sequential explanatory mixed-methods design was employed7. Phase 1 consisted of a quantitative cross-sectional survey to establish baseline practices. Phase 2 involved qualitative semi-structured interviews to explore survey findings in depth and understand contextual factors influencing practice. Survey results informed the development of the interview topic guide, with specific probes designed to explore quantitative findings (e.g., reasons for fixed-percentage sampling, barriers to SPC adoption, Excel-related challenges). The study was conducted between October and December 2025.

Ethical considerations and data governance

This study involved anonymous surveys and interviews with healthcare professionals regarding their routine work practices; no patients, biological samples, or patient data were involved. The study protocol was reviewed by the Quality Department of the Centro de Transfusión de la Comunitat Valenciana and was deemed not to require formal ethics committee review.

All participants provided informed consent prior to participation, including consent for audio recording. Interviews were conducted in Spanish; quotes were translated by the first author with back-translation verification. Audio recordings and transcripts were stored on password-protected institutional servers (GDPR-compliant) and will be deleted after study completion. Only aggregated, non-identifiable operational totals were used for contextualization; no patient data and no center-level operational metrics were disclosed. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Phase 1: quantitative survey

Sampling frame and recruitment

Professionals responsible for quality control in BEs were recruited through the Spanish Society of Blood Transfusion and Cellular Therapy (SETS) blood processing working group network. This network comprises quality control professionals from regional blood establishments across Spain, representing approximately 15 of the 17 autonomous communities with dedicated BE infrastructure. Of 13 professionals contacted (representing the accessible membership actively participating in working group activities during the study period), 10 completed the survey (response rate 77%). In the absence of an official national registry of professionals specifically responsible for blood component quality control, this working group represents the most comprehensive sampling frame available. Assuming approximately one quality control lead per autonomous community blood establishment, the national target population is estimated at approximately 17 professionals; under this assumption, our 10 respondents represent approximately 59% of the estimated national population and 77% of the accessible working group membership (10 of 13 contacted). To characterise the coverage of participating centers, aggregated national whole blood collection totals for 2024 were provided by the SETS blood processing working group. Only aggregated figures were used for contextualization; no center-level collection volumes were collected or reported.

Inclusion criteria were: current responsibility for statistical quality control activities and minimum one year of experience in blood component processing quality control.

Survey instrument

A structured questionnaire was developed based on EDQM guidelines and previous literature on BE quality practices. The instrument assessed: professional and operational characteristics (professional experience, center type, production volume); current methodologies (statistical methods employed, sample size determination approaches); tools and resources (software and instruments used for analysis); challenges (main difficulties in current practice, ranked selection); requirements (desired characteristics for quality control tools, open-ended); and interest in automation (Likert scale).

Content validity was established through expert review by three senior BE quality professionals. The questionnaire was piloted with two quality professionals (not included in final sample) to assess clarity and completion time; minor wording adjustments were made based on feedback. Respondents selected the single option that best described their primary practice for each question. Centers using partial or informal trend analysis without formal control rules or documented out-of-control procedures were classified according to the primary tool selected–typically “descriptive statistics only”– reflecting the absence of a systematic SPC framework rather than the total absence of any longitudinal review of data. No incomplete responses were received (Online Supplementary Content, Table SI).

Data collection

The survey was distributed electronically via Microsoft Forms (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) through institutional email with two reminder messages over a two-week period (October 2025). Responses were anonymous with optional contact provision for Phase 2 participation. Participating BEs typically process the panel of components routinely produced at Spanish blood establishments: whole blood, red blood cell concentrates, fresh frozen plasma, apheresis plasma, pooled platelet concentrates, and apheresis platelet concentrates. Many centers additionally apply post-processing treatments–such as irradiation, washing, cryopreservation, and pathogen inactivation of plasma and platelet products–to one or more of these base components. EDQM quality control parameters routinely measured across the panel include unit volume, hemoglobin content, hematocrit, hemolysis, total platelet content, residual leukocytes, residual red cells and residual platelets in plasma products, factor VIII activity, total protein content, and fibrinogen content in pathogen-inactivated plasma, applied per component and per EDQM specification.

Phase 2: qualitative interviews

Participant selection

Purposive sampling was employed to ensure diversity in: center size (small [<50,000 whole blood units collected/year], medium [50,000–100,000], large [>100,000]); geographic distribution; professional experience (novice, intermediate, expert); and organizational complexity (single-site vs multi-site).

All survey respondents were invited to participate in interviews; seven agreed. In qualitative exploratory research, sample size adequacy is assessed through informational sufficiency rather than statistical power calculations8.

Interview protocol

Semi-structured interviews were conducted using Microsoft Teams (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) (35–70 minutes) by the first author (HSP), a quality control professional with no prior relationship with participants beyond professional network contact. Sessions were recorded and transcribed using Teams’ integrated transcription functionality, with manual verification for accuracy. The interviewer maintained reflexive awareness of his dual role as researcher and practitioner, documented in field notes.

The topic guide (available in Online Supplementary Content, Table SII) was developed based on Phase 1 findings and explored: current workflow for quality control activities; decision-making processes for sampling and analysis (probing specifically on fixed-percentage approaches identified in survey); challenges and workarounds in daily practice (with probes on Excel complexity); interpretation and communication of results; and perspectives on potential improvements.

Identifying information was removed during transcript verification. Production volume was obtained from official 2024 annual whole blood collection data for each participating center. The completed COREQ checklist is provided in Online Supplementary Content, Table SIII.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach9: familiarization, initial coding, theme searching, theme review, theme definition, and report production. Two researchers independently coded transcripts with discrepancies resolved through discussion. Sampling continued until informational sufficiency was achieved, with the final two interviews yielding no substantively new themes. A coding framework and theme development audit trail are available in Online Supplementary Content, Table SIV.

Member checking was not performed due to anonymity constraints and feasibility considerations; this is acknowledged as a limitation.

Integration of findings

Quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated using a joint display approach10. The integration process involved: (1) mapping survey findings to interview topics; (2) identifying convergence and divergence between data sources; and (3) developing meta-inferences that synthesized insights from both phases. For example, the survey finding that 80% of centers used fixed-percentage sampling was explored qualitatively to understand underlying reasons (uncertainty about statistical methods, institutional tradition), yielding the meta-inference that sampling practices reflect a combination of knowledge, operational, and organizational factors rather than statistical knowledge alone. Additional methodological documentation, including the mapping of survey items to EDQM/JPAC recommendations and definitions of key terms, is provided in Online Supplementary Content, Tables SV and SVI, respectively.

RESULTS

Phase 1: survey results

Participant characteristics

Ten responses were received from professionals representing 10 autonomous communities (Table I). The 10 participating blood establishments included high-volume regions and collectively accounted for approximately 65% of national whole blood collection in 2024 (967,143 of 1,482,509 units), based on aggregated internal data provided by the SETS blood processing working group. These figures were used solely to contextualize the sample; no center-level collection volumes are reported. Most respondents (8/10, 80%) had more than 6 years of experience in quality control, with 4/10 (40%) exceeding 10 years. Regarding center profiles, participants represented a range of production volumes: 4/10 (40%) from small centers (<50,000 WB units collected/year), 3/10 (30%) from medium centers (50,000–100,000 WB units collected/year), and 3/10 (30%) from large centers (>100,000 WB units collected/year).

Current statistical methodologies

Most respondents (9/10, 90%) used only descriptive statistics, such as mean and standard deviation, while 1/10 (10%) employed control charts (Figure 1A). No respondent reported using formal statistical sample-size calculations; 8/10 (80%) applied fixed percentages of production (1%, 5%, or 10%), while 2/10 (20%) consulted normative tables (EDQM or AABB) (Figure 1B).

Tools and time investment

Microsoft Excel with custom formulas was the predominant tool (9/10, 90%), with one respondent supplementing Excel with specialized statistical software (Figure 1C). Overall, 7/10 (70%) dedicated more than 30 minutes per analysis cycle. In this context, an “analysis cycle” comprised one complete iteration of quality control activities for a defined production batch or reporting period, encompassing data collation from measurement records, statistical calculation, preparation of charts or summary reports, and drafting of the corresponding internal quality report.

Identified challenges

Respondents ranked their top three challenges (Table II). Difficulty determining optimal sample size and laborious report generation were most frequently cited (8/10, 80% each).

Interest in automation

Interest in automated solutions was unanimous: 5/10 (50%) expressed “high interest” and 5/10 (50%) “considerable interest”; no respondent selected moderate, low, or no interest (categories collapsed as “Low/No interest” in Figure 1D).

Phase 2: qualitative findings

Seven in-depth interviews were conducted with professionals from diverse center profiles (Table III). Four main themes emerged.

Theme 1: uncertainty about sampling adequacy

A pervasive sense of uncertainty regarding sampling adequacy emerged across interviews, regardless of professional experience level. Participants consistently described tension between normative recommendations and practical implementation.

Participants described situations in which trends or shifts in process indicators became apparent only during retrospective review, rather than in real time. These accounts illustrate the potential for earlier detection of process trends with proactive monitoring, without implying that existing quality systems failed to protect product safety.

Beyond data collection and calculation, participants identified a significant gap in decision support. Current systems provided numerical outputs but offered no guidance on interpretation.

Table IV presents integrated findings. The integration revealed that heterogeneity in practices (survey) reflects underlying knowledge gaps and institutional constraints (interviews). For example, the predominance of fixed-percentage sampling reflects uncertainty about statistical justification alongside locally established practice, rather than a single deliberate methodological choice-a finding that emerged only through qualitative exploration. DISCUSSION

Principal findings

This exploratory study identified variation in implementation approaches relative to EDQM recommendations among participating Spanish BEs, with opportunities for improved harmonization. Key findings include: predominant use of descriptive statistics without SPC methodologies (9/10, 90%), sample size determination based on fixed percentages rather than statistical calculation (8/10, 80%), and universal reliance on Excel-based manual processes. Qualitative findings suggest that these practices reflect a combination of factors–knowledge gaps regarding specific statistical methodologies, legacy systems inherited across staff transitions, operational and resource constraints, proportionality considerations, and locally validated standardization practices–rather than a single explanatory factor. For example, the use of fixed-percentage sampling in some centers may represent pragmatic local standardization rather than lack of understanding of alternative approaches. The integration of quantitative and qualitative data yielded insights that neither approach alone could provide-specifically, that the demand for automation reflects a need for interpretive support, not merely calculation efficiency. The observed time investment (7/10 respondents reporting more than 30 minutes per analysis cycle) should be interpreted as a description of current workload rather than evidence of inefficiency; given the diversity of blood components routinely produced by Spanish blood establishments, this level of effort may be entirely appropriate.

Interpretation relative to european guidance and regulatory context

EDQM guidelines recommend statistical process control for production monitoring, including control charts for trend detection and statistically-justified sampling based on production volume, presented as supportive guidance in Appendix 3 (Statistical Process Control) of the EDQM Blood Guide2. The UK JPAC Red Book similarly specifies, for several component quality parameters, testing of 1% or as determined by statistical process control3. Our findings suggest limited use of certain recommended statistical methodologies in participating centers, with only 1/10 (10%) employing control charts and none reporting a formal SPC system.

This observation acquires particular relevance given the forthcoming EU Regulation 2024/1938 on Substances of Human Origin (SoHO), applicable from 7 August 20275. Although the Regulation does not prescribe specific SPC methodologies, its emphasis on robust quality systems provides a transitional window in which BEs may choose to strengthen their statistical monitoring practices.

Interpretation of EDQM recommendations

The EDQM Blood Guide provides guidance intended to support harmonization and good practice across European Blood Establishments. The sampling guidance in Appendix 3 is illustrative rather than prescriptive: it offers a statistically-informed reference for centers seeking to size their quality control programmes, but does not preclude alternative validated approaches when justified by operational context, scale of processing, or risk-based considerations. For the purposes of this study, we defined “harmonization with EDQM Recommendations” operationally as the systematic use of methodologies consistent with those described in the Guide–including control charts with defined decision rules, statistically-informed sampling, and documented review procedures–regardless of whether the specific values reproduced the worked examples in Appendix 3. Locally validated approaches equivalent in rigor to those described in the Guide were considered acceptable within the study framework. We did not evaluate compliance against a specific regulatory requirement, and the observations reported here should not be read as indicating that any participating center operates outside acceptable quality practice.

Comparison with previous research

Our findings align with studies from other healthcare contexts showing heterogeneity in quality control practices and variable adoption of SPC methodologies11. The Spanish study by Romon et al.4 on quality indicators in hospital transfusion services documented similar variability. The recent ISBT Quality Management Working Party recommendations12 provide a framework for standardized quality indicators. The universal interest in automation observed in our study echoes findings from laboratory medicine, where automated quality systems have demonstrated efficiency improvements13. Previous work has demonstrated the feasibility of SPC implementation in blood component production14,15. More broadly, a recent review by Vuk16 highlighted the need for adaptable monitoring systems and additional staff education and training in response to evolving quality-management requirements under the new SoHO regulatory framework; our findings provide complementary empirical evidence from the Spanish blood establishment setting.

Implications for practice

The identified practice gaps suggest several opportunities: standardization of sampling methodology through tools that automatically calculate sample sizes based on production volume and EDQM tables; implementation of statistical process control through automated generation of control charts with interpretive guidance; and decision support that translates statistical findings into practical recommendations. The finding that automation interest reflects desire for interpretive support–not just calculation efficiency–should inform tool development priorities.

Contextual factors reported by participants

Beyond the coded thematic findings, participants reported additional contextual factors during interviews. Because each was raised in a single center, these are reported as observations rather than as consolidated themes, and are described in narrative form to preserve center anonymity. In one center, coincident staff absences and a period of instrumentation unavailability were described as having temporarily reduced routine quality control coverage. In another center, the transition to a substitute technician for sample handling was described as having introduced a systematic pre-analytical variation identified only after investigation of unexpected hemoglobin values. These accounts suggest that even where methodological guidance and appropriate tools exist, sustained SPC implementation depends on organizational continuity, equipment reliability, and standardization of pre-analytical procedures. Systematic assessment of these contextual dimensions was beyond the scope of the present study but is proposed as a priority for a larger follow-up survey with instruments designed to capture them. In addition, product portfolio complexity was not raised spontaneously as a barrier by any participant, although it remains a plausible constraint for small centers with wide product ranges and warrants dedicated assessment.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include the mixed-methods design enabling both quantification of practices and in-depth understanding of contextual factors, geographic diversity across autonomous communities, and inclusion of varied center profiles and experience levels. The integration of findings yielded insights beyond what either method alone could provide.

Limitations require careful consideration. While participating centers collectively represent 65% of national whole blood collection and an estimated 59% of the national population of blood establishment quality control professionals (a necessarily approximate denominator, given the absence of an official registry), the modest sample size (No.=10 survey, No.=7 interviews) precludes national prevalence estimation; findings should be interpreted as exploratory insights rather than representative statistics. Recruitment through a professional network may have introduced selection bias in either direction: centers with more advanced practices may be overrepresented (as engaged professionals are more likely to participate in working groups), or alternatively, centers experiencing more challenges may have been more motivated to contribute, the direction of this potential bias cannot be determined from available data. Self-reported practices may not fully reflect actual behavior. Member checking was not performed due to anonymity constraints. The survey was designed to characterise statistical methodologies, workflow, tools, challenges, and interest in automation; other potentially relevant dimensions–including educational background, formal statistical training, dedicated staffing, and organizational constraints–were not captured as structured items and are identified as priorities for future research. We also acknowledge that both the survey and the interview topic guide included explicit references to automation and tool improvements, which may have primed respondents toward reporting greater interest in automated approaches than they might have expressed in a differently framed instrument; the universal interest observed (10/10) should therefore be interpreted with caution and viewed as an upper-bound estimate of demand.

Whether automated tools can improve the understanding and interpretation of statistical process control is best regarded as an empirical question rather than an established assumption. Automated systems may support statistical reasoning by embedding EDQM sampling guidance, formal decision rules, and standardized graphical outputs within the evaluation process, thereby facilitating consistent interpretation without replacing professional judgement. Whether these mechanisms translate into measurable improvements in interpretive performance will be evaluated during the prospective pilot phase of the parent doctoral project through an inter-rater agreement (kappa) study comparing conformity judgements made with and without automated support. Accordingly, the present study should be regarded solely as an exploratory needs assessment and not as evidence that automation improves SPC interpretation.

Future directions

Future research should include larger surveys to establish national prevalence estimates and evaluate interventions to support guideline implementation, including automated tools for statistical quality control.

CONCLUSIONS

This exploratory study of BE professionals from 10 Spanish autonomous communities revealed variation in the implementation of statistical quality control practices, with predominant use of descriptive statistics and manual Excel-based processes. While participants demonstrated awareness of EDQM guidance, systematic use of the SPC methodologies described in Appendix 3 remained limited among participating centers. The unanimous interest in automated solutions–driven by desire for interpretive support rather than mere efficiency–combined with the mix of knowledge, operational and pragmatic factors identified, suggests that standardized tools facilitating guidance-consistent approaches could support both harmonization and operational objectives. Larger studies are needed to establish national prevalence of these practices.
