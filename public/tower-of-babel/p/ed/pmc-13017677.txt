Regulation of chloroplast biogenesis and differentiation
Authors: Tamara Hernández-Verdeja, Karl-Josef Dietz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChloroplasts are semi-autonomous organelles essential for photosynthetic organisms. They derived from ancestral cyanobacteria through an endosymbiotic event. In plants, these organelles are inherited as non-photosynthetic plastids, the proplastids, which can differentiate into chloroplasts or other specialized types of plastids in response to external and internal signals and cues, and following precise developmental programmes. Transcriptional regulation of chloroplast biogenesis and, to a lesser extent chromoplast differentiation, has been a central focus of interest, leading to the identification of several key factors. This review highlights recent research on transcription factors and epigenetic modifiers that regulate chloroplast biogenesis, the evolution of transcriptional regulation in land plants, and factors regulating chromoplast differentiation. However, significant knowledge gaps remain regarding plastid differentiation in specific tissues and species, as well as the biogenesis of other plastid types. Thus, the review highlights the complexity of chloroplast biogenesis, and open questions on spatial and temporal regulation, lineage- and species-specific mechanisms, and biogenesis of diverse plastid types. Understanding this process will advance basic plant biology but also holds biotechnological potential to address present and future challenges.Keywords: Chloroplast biogenesis, chloroplast compartment, chloroplasts, chromoplast, epigenetic regulation, histone acetylases, plastids, transcription factors, transcriptional regulation

Introduction

Chloroplasts are complex plastids that host photosynthetic reactions and are essential not only for algae and plants, but for life on Earth, as they convert sunlight into chemical energy that supports other organisms. Like mitochondria, plastids are semi-autonomous organelles, they have their own genome, and they must divide to be transmitted to daughter cells. All plastids originated from an endosymbiotic event, in which an ancestral cyanobacterium was acquired by a eukaryotic cell ∼1.5 to 1 billion years ago (Maréchal, 2024). The engulfed photosynthetic organism gradually reduced its genome through gene loss or transfer to the post-endosymbiotic host nuclear genome. This compartmentalization of genetic information between the nuclear and plastidic genomes led to the loss of the endosymbiont autonomy and gain of nuclear control, since the plastidic chromosome no longer encodes all the components required for plastid biogenesis, function, and maintenance. During evolution, systems for metabolic exchange and for targeting and translocation of nuclear-encoded proteins to the plastids were incorporated (Füssy and Oborník, 2024). Additionally, complex signalling networks evolved, from the nucleus to the plastids (anterograde) and from plastids to the nucleus (retrograde), to coordinate gene expression between the two compartments in response to developmental and environmental conditions (Hernández-Verdeja et al., 2020).

Beyond the well-known chloroplasts, plant cells contain an extraordinary diversity of plastid types with specialized structures and functions that are present in specific cells or species (reviewed in Sierra et al., 2023). Plastids are inherited as small, non-photosynthetic proplastids that can differentiate into other forms following precise programmes during organogenesis and in response to environmental signals. Plastids are also incredibly dynamic and can interconvert between forms depending on developmental stage and environmental cues, following pathways that are tissue and species specific (reviewed in Jarvis and López-Juez, 2013; Liebers et al., 2017; Choi et al., 2021; Sierra et al., 2023). Due to their central role, chloroplast biogenesis from proplastids has been the most studied pathway and has provided insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying plastid development.

Chloroplast biogenesis varies between plant lineages but typically begins in germinating seedlings, where proplastids differentiate into chloroplasts upon exposure to light (Fig. 1). In darkness, for example for seeds germinating buried in the soil, proplastids develop into an intermediary form, the etioplast, in tissues that will later become photosynthetic (Pogson et al., 2015). Etioplasts are characterized by a lattice-like membranous structure, the prolamellar body (PLB), that contains the chlorophyll precursor protochlorophyllide, light-dependent protochlorophyllide oxidoreductase (LPOR), and prothylakoids (Floris and Kühlbrandt, 2021). During this stage, seedlings establish mechanisms to prevent excessive accumulation of photosynthetic components that could damage the cell when exposed to light (reviewed in Hernández-Verdeja et al., 2020). Upon light exposure, etioplasts rapidly transform into chloroplasts. While chlorophyll biosynthesis proceeds from the precursor and levels increase linearly with time in light, the PLBs disperse within minutes, and thylakoid lamellae appear within 4 h. Thylakoid density grows, by incrementing lamellae length and number, and stacking of lamellae to form grana. After 14 h in light, the photosynthetic machinery is assembled and it is fully functional, and, after 24 h, the characteristic mature chloroplast structures are present (Pipitone et al., 2021).

In dicotyledonous plants, such as Arabidopsis, transition through the etioplast phase occurs in dark-grown cotyledons. In contrast, in true leaves developing from the shoot apical meristem, the fate of proplastids depends on the tissue layer and cell lineage. Subepidermal meristematic cells, which will give rise to most photosynthetic tissue in the leaves, contain proplastids that begin to gain thylakoids near the leaf primordia, while cells that will form the epidermis contain plastids with thylakoid networks that are largely lost during organogenesis (Charuvi et al., 2012). In monocot leaves, chloroplast biogenesis exhibits a spatial gradient, with proplastids in leaf primordia at the leaf base differentiating chloroplasts a few millimetres before young leaf emergence from the enveloping coleoptile, and fully mature chloroplasts towards the leaf tip (Loudya et al., 2021).

Besides chloroplasts, other specialized plastids are essential for plant physiology, but their biogenesis remains poorly understood. Chromoplasts synthesize and store large amounts of carotenoids and are present in fruits, flowers, and some tubers and roots. Due to the economic value of these products, chromoplasts are the second most studied plastid type after chloroplasts. Depending on the tissue, they can differentiate from proplastids, leucoplasts, or chloroplasts (reviewed in Li and Yuan, 2013; Sadali et al., 2019). Chromoplast differentiation is dependent on environmental factors, such as light and temperature, and is under hormonal regulation; for example, in climacteric fruits, ethylene induces differentiation of chromoplasts and ripening (Sadali et al., 2019). Despite chromoplast variation in different species, organs, and tissues, their transition from chloroplasts generally involves down-regulation of chlorophyll biosynthesis, degradation of chlorophyll and thylakoids, and an increase in carotenoid biosynthesis and accumulation (Fig. 2) (Sadali et al., 2019).

Plastids not only differentiate into specialized forms, but also grow in size and divide, processes that are tightly coordinated with plant organogenesis (Loudya et al., 2021; Pipitone et al., 2021). Plastid number and size determine the plastid compartment—the cellular volume allocated to chloroplasts—which is precisely regulated, cell type specific, and largely independent of chloroplast division (Larkin, 2024). For example, while in mature mesophyll cells chloroplasts occupy all the available cellular space, in other cell types, such as epidermal and guard cells, chloroplast content remains low, with numbers varying across lineages and photosynthetic type (Jarvis and López-Juez, 2013; Loudya et al., 2021).

This review focuses on recent advances in nuclear transcriptional regulation of chloroplast development, including the identification of novel transcriptional and epigenetic regulators, of factors determining cell-specific plastid differentiation, and new insights into well-known factors highlighting their evolutionary significance and contributions to plastid specialization.

Usual suspects in nuclear transcriptional regulation

When discussing chloroplast biogenesis and photosynthetic development, several transcription factors (TFs) have been in the spotlight for years, but they still hold interest, and research keeps revealing new roles and details of their function and regulatory mechanisms (Fig. 1).

The well-known bZIP TF ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL5 (HY5) is a master regulator of development in light (reviewed in Gangappa and Botto, 2016; Xiao et al., 2022). HY5 plays an antagonistic role with PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTORs (PIFs) by activating transcription of light-regulated and photosynthesis-associated genes, thus allowing the transition from dark-to-light developmental programmes and chloroplast development (Toledo-Ortiz et al., 2014). HY5 originated in chlorophytes alongside other light perception components. Recent work in the model bryophyte Marchantia polymorpha suggests that HY5 regulation of photosynthetic gene transcription was established first in response to changing temperatures, a role still present in Arabidopsis (Toledo-Ortiz et al., 2014; Han et al., 2019; Yelina et al., 2024).

The GOLDEN2-LIKE (GLK) family has also been a major focus of research since the discovery of GOLDEN2 as the factor responsible for bundle sheath chloroplast development in C4 maize (reviewed in Hernández-Verdeja and Lundgren, 2024; Zheng et al., 2024). These TFs belong to the GARP superfamily, and regulate chloroplast biogenesis by directly controlling the expression of chlorophyll biosynthesis genes and genes encoding light-harvesting and photosystem proteins (Waters et al., 2009; Tu et al., 2022; Yelina et al., 2024). Recent studies and phylogenetic analyses indicate a single origin for GLKs in the last common ancestor of embryophytes, and a redundant role in promoting chloroplast development (Hernández-Muñoz et al., 2024; Hernández-Verdeja and Lundgren, 2024; Yelina et al., 2024 ). The evolutionary importance of the GLKs for chloroplast biogenesis was revealed in two recent works in Marchantia. Marchantia GLK has a pseudo-response regulator (PRR) domain, characteristic of other members of the GARP superfamily, but not present in GLKs of the Spermatophyta lineage (Hernández-Muñoz et al., 2024). Results revealed that Marchantia GLK has a conserved function in chloroplast biogenesis in terms of chlorophyll levels, chloroplast size, and thylakoid structure (Hernández-Muñoz et al., 2024; Yelina et al., 2024). Although not a canonical domain of the GLKs, truncated versions of Marchantia GLK without the PRR domain revealed that it was important for chloroplast biogenesis (Hernández-Muñoz et al., 2024). Moreover, identification and characterization of Marchantia homologues of TFs known to be involved in chloroplast biogenesis revealed that only GLK has a role in chloroplast biogenesis (Yelina et al., 2024). A genome-wide study of GLK binding in five species, Arabidopsis, tomato, tobacco, maize, and rice, and data from Marchantia confirmed the core motif RGATTYY as the binding motif for the GLKs (Tu et al., 2022; Yelina et al., 2024). These works revealed a core set of conserved GLK-regulated genes with photosynthetic function in all five species, but also a majority of species-specific genes, reflecting the maintenance of the core function of GLKs in chloroplast development and unveiling a divergence in the cistrome during evolution (Tu et al., 2022; Yelina et al., 2024). Transcriptomic results in Marchantia Mpglk mutants showed a higher number of genes under GLK control than in flowering plants, including genes coding for plastid proteins that are not GLK regulated in the other species (Yelina et al., 2024). This divergence of the biding motifs may explain positive or negative results in heterologous complementation of GLKs of close or distant species, respectively (Yasumura et al., 2005; Bravo-Garcia et al., 2009; Chen et al., 2018; Yelina et al., 2024).

Another surprising result regarding GLK functions goes back to the initial characterization of GOLDEN2 in maize. Recent work with maize and another C4 grass, Setaria viridis, revealed the prominent role of GLK2 in chloroplast development not only in bundle sheath but in mesophyll cells too (Lambret-Frotte et al., 2024). This contradicts the initial hypothesis where GLK1 and GLK2 controlled chloroplast biogenesis in mesophyll and bundle sheath cells in C4 maize, respectively, based on the golden2 maize mutant variegated phenotype and high expression of GLK1 in mesophyll cells (Langdale and Kidner, 1994). Surprisingly, Setaria and maize GLK1 loss-of-function plants showed normal chloroplast in both cell types, while Setaria Svglk2 mutants presented severely disturbed chloroplasts in mesophyll and bundle sheath cells that SvGLK1 could not complement. These results indicate that dimorphic chloroplasts in mesophyll and bundle sheath cells of these C4 species are not a result of subfunctionalization of GLK1 and GLK2 activity (Lambret-Frotte et al., 2024). In addition, results of gene expression in Setaria mutants indicated that SvGLK2 positively regulates its own expression and that of SvGLK1, suggesting that in C4 grasses chloroplast biogenesis is mainly regulated by monocotyledonous GLK2 orthologues (Lambret-Frotte et al., 2024).

Another family of TFs long-known to be involved in chloroplast biogenesis is the LLM-domain B-GATA factors, comprised of GATA NITRATE-INDUCIBLE CARBON-METABOLISM INVOLVED (GNC) and CYTOKININ-RESPONSIVE GATA1 (CGA1), as well as GATA15, GATA16, GATA17, and GATA17L. GNC and CGA1 were first identified as positive regulators of chlorophyll accumulation with partially overlapping roles (Bi et al., 2005; Mara and Irish, 2008). Later studies, with mutants and overexpressors, revealed their role in chloroplast development and division (Hudson et al., 2011; Chiang et al., 2012). Although single mutants of the other LLM-GATAs do not result in chlorophyll accumulation defects, quadruple and quintuple mutants showed lower chlorophyll content than the double gnc cga1 mutant, suggesting that they could have additional roles in chloroplast biogenesis (Ranftl et al., 2016). It is unclear when these GATA TFs acquired the role in chloroplast biogenesis, but according to lack of chloroplast-related phenotypes in Marchantia gata mutants and overexpressors, this function could be a later addition during streptophyte evolution (Yelina et al., 2024).

Chloroplast biogenesis is heavily dependent on environmental and developmental signals and factors, such as light, temperature, and phytohormones (reviewed in Cackett et al., 2022; Mahapatra et al., 2024 ). Accordingly, all the TFs described above are integrated in regulatory networks that control their expression and activity (reviewed in Cackett et al., 2022). Moreover, the acquisition of new factors during evolution, and the requirement to adapt chloroplast biogenesis to different organs, tissues, and environmental conditions, led to a higher complexity of these regulatory networks. While in Marchantia only GLK has a role in chloroplast biogenesis, and HY5 has a temperature-dependent role, in Arabidopsis HY5, GLK, CGN, and CGA1 are major regulators and are interconnected (Fig. 1). HY5 directly binds and activates expression of GLK genes, CGN, and CGA1 in light-grown seedlings (Zhang et al., 2024). Moreover, HY5 and GLKs interact, although the functional role of this interaction is unclear (Zhang et al., 2024 ). While the functions of GLKs and CGA1 and GNC in chloroplast biogenesis are partially overlapping, GLKs seem to play a more pronounced role in this process, but of these TFs only GNC and CGA1 affect chloroplast division and number (Zubo et al., 2018). Only in specific conditions, such as short days, is there an additive effect in the quadruple glk1 glk2 gnc cga1 Arabidopsis mutant in terms of chlorophyll, thylakoid density, and chloroplast size (Zubo et al., 2018). Additionally, they might be regulating each other’s expression levels (Zubo et al., 2018).

Brassinosteroids negatively regulate chloroplast biogenesis in dark-grown Arabidopsis seedlings through the action of the TF BRASSINAZOLE RESISTANT1 (BZR1) and its homologue BZR2/BRI1-EMS-SUPPRESSOR1 (BES1; Fig. 1). In darkness, BZR1 interacts with PIF4 and forms a network that represses chloroplast development by repressing gene expression of GLK, GATA, and photosynthesis-associated genes (Oh et al., 2012). Light signals, mediated by HY5, inactivate BZR1 and derepress chloroplast biogenesis (reviewed in Cackett et al., 2022; Mahapatra et al., 2024). Results from rice support the central role of BZR1 in mediating brassinosteroid regulation of gene expression and repression of chloroplast biogenesis, but in contrast to Arabidopsis BZR1 it might not be mediated through GLKs, or the GATA TFs CGA1 and GNC, suggesting evolutionary divergence of this regulatory module in plant lineages (Cackett et al., 2025).

Expanding the transcriptional regulatory network of chloroplast biogenesis

Knowledge on transcriptional regulation of chloroplast biogenesis has incremented considerably, with additional factors being identified and integrated into the growing network (Fig. 1). Recently, two new elements had been linked to the regulation of GLKs. DEEP GREEN PANICLE (DGP) was first identified in rice, as a repressor of GLK transcriptional activity (Zhang et al., 2020). The Arabidopsis DGP orthologue, named REPRESSOR OF PHOTOSYNTHETIC GENES2 (RPGE2)/PSEUDO-ETIOLATION IN LIGHT1 (PEL1)/BRZ-INSENSITIVE-PALE GREEN4 (BPG4), also interacts with GLKs, regulating chloroplast development and homeostasis. PEL1/BPG4 suppressed GLK activity to modulate and fine-tune transcription of photosynthesis-associated nuclear genes by integrating light and brassinosteroid signalling (Han et al., 2024; Tachibana et al., 2024). In turn the GLKs activate BGP4 expression in light, increasing the control over gene expression during early chloroplast biogenesis (Tachibana et al., 2025). Another member of the PEL family, the dark-inducible BPG4 paralogue BPG4 HOMOLOGOUS GENE2 (BGH2), interacted with GLKs in etiolated seedlings to prevent excessive expression of photosynthetic genes (Tachibana et al., 2025). Results in rice had revealed the potential mechanism used by PELs to suppress GLK activity. Direct binding of PEL and GLKs in the cytoplasm might inhibit the nuclear localization of the GLKs and their protein levels, hence reducing their transcriptional activity (Choi et al., 2025). G-BOX BINDING FACTORS (GBFs), and other members of the G-group of bZIP TFs, were known for their role in light signalling and photomorphogenesis. Recently they have been characterized for their involvement in chloroplast biogenesis. GBF1, 2, and 3 interact with GLKs to modulate carotenoid and chlorophyll biosynthesis in leaves, although it seems that the GBF–GLK module does not regulate these pathways during Arabidopsis etioplast-to-chloroplasts transition (Sun et al., 2025). However, GBF1, bZIP16, and bZIP68 had been described to act redundantly in chloroplast development during de-etiolation, probably integrating light signals with gene expression to ensure proper timing of chloroplast development. After 24 h in light, chloroplasts in the triple lack-of-function mutant, bzip triple, showed thylakoid membranes but lacked grana staking (Norén Lindbäck et al., 2023). The large number of members in this family, redundancy, combined with their ability to form heterodimers with changing DNA binding activity is probably masking potential roles of other G-group bZIPs in chloroplast biogenesis and development in light.

An additional relevant family of TFs is the B-BOX (BBX), which are involved in photomorphogenic development of seedlings. Importantly, some BBXs are regulated by plastid retrograde signals and are part of the GLK regulatory network, suggesting participation in chloroplast biogenesis. Work by Veciana et al. (2022) on the antagonistic regulation of photomorphogenesis by light and plastid retrograde signals revealed BBX16 as a downstream target of the GLKs. BBX16 regulated a subset of GLK-dependent photosynthetic genes and, together with GLK1, is part of a network that integrates both signalling pathways (Veciana et al., 2022). In addition, comparative analyses of GLK binding data in Marchantia, two monocots, and three dicots further confirmed that GLK binds BBX genes in all species, suggesting that GLK regulation of BBX is part of an ancient regulatory module in photomorphogenesis and possibly in chloroplast biogenesis (Tu et al., 2022; Yelina et al., 2024). Another BBX, BBX32, is involved in modulating etioplast formation and early etioplast-to-chloroplast transition in light by interacting with and regulating the activity and degradation of the ETHYLENE-INSENSITIVE3/PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTOR3 (EIN3/PIF3) module, one of the major mechanisms repressing etioplast-to-chloroplast development in the dark (Liu et al., 2017; K. Wang et al., 2025). Additionally, BBX14 and BBX15 were identified with a machine learning approach for calculation of gene regulatory networks (Halpape et al., 2023, Preprint). In this same work, transcriptomic analysis of inducible BBX14 overexpression plants showed opposite regulation of photosynthetic targets, with lower expression of light-harvesting and photosystem components and higher expression of photorespiration and Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle-related genes (Halpape et al., 2023, Preprint). More work is required to determine whether these BBXs and other members of the family have a role in chloroplast biogenesis, or if they modulate photosynthetic activity and chloroplast function.

Although the role of GLKs, GNC, and CGA1 as major regulators of chloroplast biogenesis is established, lack-of-function quadruple mutants in Arabidopsis or the Marchantia Mpglk mutant still present a certain degree of chlorophyll accumulation and chloroplast development, suggesting the involvement of additional factors (Zubo et al., 2018; Yelina et al., 2024). This hypothesis was supported by the identification of several TFs with the machine learning approach mentioned above (Halpape et al., 2023, Preprint). Besides known factors, such as GLKs, GNC, CGA1, and HY5, the analysis revealed new factors such as BBX14 and 15, Nuclear Factor Y subunit B3 (NF-Y B3), and MYBS1 (Halpape et al., 2023, Preprint).

An elegant work in Marchantia and Arabidopsis characterizing two TFs of the RR-MYB/CCA1-like subfamily of MYB-related TFs—Arabidopsis MYBS1 and MYBS2 and Marchantia MpRR-MYB2 and MpRR-MYB5—alongside the GLKs further supports the role of MYBS1 in chloroplast biogenesis (Frangedakis et al., 2024). Expression of these RR-MYB genes was induced during seedling de-etiolation in Arabidopsis and germination in Marchantia, when chloroplast biogenesis takes place in both organisms, like GLKs, the GATA TFs CGA1 and GNC, and HY5 in Arabidopsis (Frangedakis et al., 2024). RR-MYBs are functionally redundant, and the absence of both genes resulted in low levels of chlorophyll and smaller chloroplasts with underdeveloped thylakoids in both species. Transcriptomic analyses confirmed that these RR-MYB factors regulate expression of photosynthesis genes. Comparison with GLK data indicated that they share targets among chlorophyll biosynthesis-, photosystem-, and light-harvesting-coding genes, and could cooperate in their regulation. Additionally, RR-MYBs controlled an extended set of genes coding for enzymes of the Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle, photorespiration, and assembly and repair of Rubisco (Halpape et al., 2023, Preprint; Frangedakis et al., 2024). The overlapping and specific functions were confirmed in Marchantia deficient in GLK and RR-MYB5 by even smaller chloroplasts and lower chlorophyll levels, and the inability to generate the triple null mutants for the RR-MYB genes and GLK, indicating an essential role in chloroplast development (Frangedakis et al., 2024). This function in plastid biogenesis was not conserved for GATA TFs and it was conditional for HY5 in Marchantia, suggesting that the function of GLKs and RR-MYBs was established early in land plant evolution (Yelina et al., 2024). Gene expression results also revealed that GLKs and RR-MYBs can regulate each other in Arabidopsis and partially in Marchantia (Frangedakis et al., 2024). These findings in two distant evolutionary organisms place GLKs and RR-MYBs at the core of a regulatory network that has grown during evolution, becoming more complex, to coordinate chloroplast biogenesis in parallel with plant development during the transition to autotrophic growth.

The requirement for coordinating two genomes

Compartmentalization of genetic information between the nuclear and plastidic genomes requires a tight coordination to achieve proper chloroplast biogenesis and function. This coordination could be achieved by factors localized in both the nucleus and plastids (Krupinska et al., 2020). Several proteins with a role in plastid transcription and dual plastid–nuclear localization, and essential for chloroplast biogenesis have been characterized (reviewed in Krupinska et al., 2020). Analyses in Arabidopsis predicted that between 48 and 78 nuclear TFs could be dually localized to the nucleus and plastids, making them good candidates to coordinate gene expression in both compartments (Wagner and Pfannschmidt, 2006; Schwacke et al., 2007). However, only a few nuclear TFs involved in nuclear transcriptional regulation of chloroplast biogenesis have been characterized to date. Barley ALBOSTRIANS/CCT Motif Family gene 7 (HvCMF7) and its Arabidopsis homologue CHLOROPLAST IMPORT APPARATUS 2 (CIA2) are conserved CONSTANS, CO-like, and TOC1 (CCT) TFs with described dual localization. Mutations in ALBOSTRIANS caused leaf variegation in barley, with white leaf sectors containing undifferentiated plastids without thylakoid lamellae (Li et al., 2019). Meanwhile, in Arabidopsis, lack of CIA2 resulted in pale plants with smaller chloroplasts with less complex thylakoid structures (Li et al., 2021). Interestingly, the CIA2 close homologue CIA2-LIKE (CIL) had not been detected in plastids, and CIA2 chloroplast localization remains unconfirmed. Nevertheless, cia2 cil double mutants had even less pigments (chlorophyll and carotenoids), smaller chloroplast size, with fewer thylakoids than the cia2 single mutant, indicating that they are required for chloroplast development although not essential (Gawroński et al., 2021; Li et al., 2021). Whether CIA2 and ALBOSTRIANS are aiding coordination of gene expression during chloroplast development in both cellular compartments remains to be established. BAI1, for albino in Chinese, is another TF localized to both the nucleus and plastids that is essential for chloroplast biogenesis. bai1 mutants were albino, with round plastids that failed to develop thylakoid membranes (Qin et al., 2025). In plastids, BAI1 interacted with plastid-encoded RNA polymerase-associated proteins and positively regulated expression of plastid genes, while nuclear-localized BAI1 acted as a TF activating photosynthesis genes. Thus, BAI1 probably coordinates transcription in both compartments throughout chloroplast development, a role that could have evolved early during Streptophyte evolution (Qin et al., 2025).

Cell specificity and the dream of photosynthetic bundle sheath cells

Cell type, developmental, and environmental signals and cues determine plastid type, size, and division. Importantly, allocation of cellular space depends heavily on cell type. For example, although leaf tissues and cells are exposed to highly similar environmental conditions, mesophyll cells in C3 plants are packed with well-developed chloroplasts, whereas chloroplasts in guard cells and epidermal cells occupy little cellular space and are relatively smaller with less developed photosynthetic structures. Although these cell-type-specific differences are key for plant development, the mechanisms regulating plastid type conversion and plastid content remain mostly unknown, with the exception of C4 bundle sheath cells (reviewed in Jarvis and López-Juez, 2013; Larkin, 2024).

Driven by the goal of increasing photosynthetic efficiency by engineering the more efficient C4 in C3 crops, a lot of effort has been invested in unveiling the mechanisms behind the C4 photosynthetically active bundle sheath cells. In C4 and C2 photosynthetic species, bundle sheath cells are characterized by large well-developed photosynthetically active chloroplasts, that occupy more cellular volume than in C3 species, a trait known as functionalization of the bundle sheath (reviewed in Sage et al., 2014). One of the most significant outcomes of the C4 projects was the identification of GOLDEN2 in C4 maize, the founding member of the GLK TF family, described in the sections above, and considered as major regulators of chloroplast biogenesis (Langdale and Kidner, 1994). All these efforts are starting to pay off, revealing potential mechanisms of chloroplasts development and establishment of the chloroplast compartment in bundle sheath cells of C4 and C3 species. BUNDLE SHEATH DEFECTIVE2 (BSD2), a chloroplast-localized DnaJ-type zinc finger protein, was identified in maize as a positive regulator of bundle sheath chloroplast size and coverage (Salesse et al., 2017). Comparative analysis of single-nucleus gene expression and chromatin accessibility atlases in C3 rice and C4 sorghum revealed that gene expression in sorghum bundle sheath cells was more similar to that of rice mesophyll cells, with chromatin of photosynthesis-associated genes similarly accessible. These findings in gene expression suggest rewiring of existing cis-motifs, by acquisition of motifs of bundle sheath-specific TFs, such as DNA-binding with one finger (DOF), by photosynthesis-related genes to control chloroplast development and C4 biochemistry in bundle sheath cells (Swift et al., 2024). Results from another C4 monocot, S. viridis, suggest that SHORT ROOT (SHR), a TF linked to the establishment of the characteristic C4 leaf anatomy, could be involved in chloroplast biogenesis in bundle sheath cells potentially through the regulation of GLKs (Camo-Escobar et al., 2024, Preprint). In rice, bundle sheath cell chloroplast content is dependent on cell area, similar to mesophyll cells, but chloroplast development does not keep up with cell expansion, resulting in lower content and suggesting an as yet unidentified repressive mechanism operating specifically in bundle sheath cells (Plackett and Hibberd, 2024). Recent results in rice suggest that GLK activity repression by PELs could be one of these mechanisms in bundle sheath cells (Choi et al., 2025). Although the relevance for bundle sheath chloroplast development is still not known, hormones can affect chloroplast content. Cytokinins and gibberellins were able to enhance chloroplast content, while inhibition of brassinosteroids enhanced chloroplast number in rice bundle sheaths (Plackett and Hibberd, 2024; Cackett et al., 2025). Similarly, manipulation of rice BZR1 or HEME-ASSOCIATED PROTEIN 3H (OsHAP3H), a repressor of chloroplast development, affected the chloroplast compartment in the bundle sheath, suggesting that these pathways and factors could be involved in the functionalization and also could be good candidates for biotechnological approaches (Cackett et al., 2025; Lambret-Frotte et al., 2025). However, these manipulations confirmed the limit to chloroplast occupancy in bundle sheaths, highlighting it as a key knowledge gap and a limiting factor for C4 and C2 engineering efforts.

Beyond transcription factors: chromatin-regulatory mechanisms during chloroplast biogenesis

Epigenetic modifications and nuclear structure dynamics have been reported to be involved in plant light responses and the control of gene expression during de-etiolation. However, their connection with chloroplast biogenesis was indirect, and reports mainly involved changes in epigenetic modifications and the expression of genes encoding proteins related to chlorophyll biosynthesis and light harvesting (reviewed in Patitaki et al., 2022). Recent works have revealed more significant and direct roles for specific epigenetic modifiers in the transition to functional chloroplasts. Histone acetylation, typically associated with transcriptional activation, has been revealed as an important factor in the process. The histone acetyltransferase HOOKLESS1 (HLS1) mediated the deposition of H3K9ac and H3K27ac in genes related to de-etiolation, promoting their activation. Moreover, confocal microscopy of etiolated cotyledons of lack-of-function hls1 mutants showed a diffuse fluorescence pattern of the chlorophyll precursor protochlorophyllide, instead of the strong fluorescence localized in a distinct structure that most probably was associated with the PLB. After light exposure, chlorophyll fluorescence in the hls1 mutants revealed smaller plastids and weaker florescence. All these results suggested defects in the PLBs of etioplasts and chloroplast development (Peng et al., 2025). Another acetyltransferase involved in chloroplast development is the protein complex Nucleosomal acetyltransferase of H4 (NuA4). NuA4 subunits ESA1-ASSOCIATED FACTOR1 (EAF1)—important for the formation of the complex—and ENHANCER OF POLYCOMB-LIKE1 (EPL1) have been shown to be essential for chloroplast biogenesis in Arabidopsis. Both EAF1 and EPL1 are encoded by two genes that are redundant, EAF1A and B, and EPL1A and B. Lack of EAF1 or EPL1 NuA4 subunits, by mutating both copies, results in severe phenotypes resembling mutants defective in essential plastid functions (Barrero-Gil et al., 2022; Bieluszewski et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2022). Double eaf1a eaf1b mutants resulted in small pale plants with small chloroplasts due to lower expression of chlorophyll biosynthesis, light-harvesting, and plastid ribosomal genes (Bieluszewski et al., 2022). Similarly, the epl1a epl1b double mutant produced even smaller plants that are sterile, almost albino, and with small, misshaped chloroplasts that seemed to lack regular thylakoid structures (Barrero-Gil et al., 2022). Interestingly, results showed that EPL1 was also required for the transcription of nuclear genes coding for components of the plastid transcription machinery, both the nuclear-encoded RNA polymerase (NEP)- and the plastid-encoded RNA polymerase (PEP)-associated proteins, probably explaining the severe phenotype of the epl1a epl1b mutant (Liebers et al., 2017; Barrero-Gil et al., 2022). In addition, based on examination of published microscopy images, it seems that the double mutants contained a reduced number of chloroplasts, suggesting a role in plastid division that has not been explored by the authors (Barrero-Gil et al., 2022; Bieluszewski et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2022). Transcriptomic data indicated only a partial overlap between NuA4 targets and genes regulated by the main TFs involved in chloroplast biogenesis known at the time of the publications (GLKs and HY5). These analyses suggested that recruitment of NuA4 to the promoters of target genes is dependent on other unidentified TFs or factors (Barrero-Gil et al., 2022; Bieluszewski et al., 2022). Taken together, these studies clearly show that epigenetic modifiers, specifically histone acetylases, are essential for chloroplast biogenesis. It is noteworthy that the only epigenetic modifiers clearly associated with regulation of this process are histone acetylases. Future works should address the role of additional epigenetic modifiers, as well as epitranscriptomic modifications, the spatio-temporal specificity of the regulation, and their potential involvement in differentiation of other plastid types.

Regulation of chromoplast differentiation

Due to their interest for agriculture, the conversion of chloroplasts into chromoplasts during ripening of red, yellow, or orange fruits such as tomato or bell pepper has been studied in some detail (Fig. 2). During chloroplast-to-chromoplast differentiation, thylakoid membranes and chlorophylls degrade and carotenoids accumulate. Carotenoids can be stored in membrane or crystal structures, plastoglobuli, or lipoprotein complexes forming fibrils. During the transition, plastoglobuli change in shape and size to accommodate the carotenoids (reviewed in Torres-Montilla and Rodriguez-Concepcion, 2021; Zhu et al., 2024). Moreover, the plastoglobuli proteome of pepper and tomato chromoplasts revealed the presence of carotenoid biosynthetic enzymes, indicating not only a role in storage but also a role in carotenogenesis (Ytterberg et al., 2006; Zita et al., 2022). Results suggest that gene expression and enzyme activities of carotenoid biosynthesis and differentiation of chromoplasts are coordinated and influence each other (Rodriguez-Concepcion and Stange, 2013). ORANGE (OR) proteins, which are chaperones, are the main factors known to induce chromoplast biogenesis. Members of the OR family regulate this transition at the post-translational level, for example by interacting with phytoene synthase (PSY) in plastids to regulate carotenoid biosynthesis. However, the specific mechanisms are still unknown (reviewed in Zhu et al., 2024; Wrightstone et al., 2025). Although OR proteins do no regulate transcription directly, research has unveiled their role interacting with TFs in the nucleus (Zhou et al., 2011; Sun et al., 2019). OR interacted with and repressed the activity of the TF TEOSINTE BRANCHED1/CYCLOIDEA/PROLIFERATING CELL FACTOR14 (TCP14) during chloroplast biogenesis in Arabidopsis cotyledons (Fig. 1) (Sun et al., 2019). It remains to be determined if OR interaction with TCPs or other TFs could be involved in transcriptional regulation of chromoplast differentiation.

Regarding transcriptional regulation, some of the TFs involved in chloroplast biogenesis also affect mature fruit quality and carotenoid composition by regulating the development of chloroplasts during the green immature phase, before chromoplast differentiation and ripening (Fig. 2). The best examples are, again, the GLK family. In tomato, GLK2 was identified as the locus responsible for the ‘uniform ripening’ phenotype. The uniform ripening mutation in GLK2 was bred commercially for its uniformly light green tomatoes and even ripening that facilitates harvest (Powell et al., 2012; Nguyen et al., 2014). When GLKs were overexpressed in tomato plants, they produced dark green tomatoes, with higher sugar and carotenoid content in the ripe tomatoes (Powell et al., 2012; Nguyen et al., 2014; Lupi et al., 2019). Although GLK genetic manipulations did not alter ripening per se, the carotenoid content and sugar accumulation in ripe tomatoes was affected as a consequence of their influence in chloroplast development during the immature phase (Nguyen et al., 2014). In addition, GLKs are responsible for variations in chlorophyll content in immature pepper fruit and peach fruit skin during ripening, supporting their involvement in the transition from chloroplast to chromoplast (Brand et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2018). PSEUDO RESPONSE REGULATOR2-LIKE2 (PRR2), another member of the GARP superfamily of TFs that includes the GLKs, positively regulated accumulation of chlorophyll and carotenoids in unripe and ripe tomato and pepper fruits, respectively (Pan et al., 2013; Jeong et al., 2020). Importantly, microscopy analyses revealed that PRR2 overexpression increased chloroplast and chromoplast number and compartments in tomato pericarp cells (Pan et al., 2013). Recent results in melon suggest that PRR2 in fruits could be subjected to similar regulation to the GLKs by RPGE/PEL proteins, as indicated by CmRPGE1 regulation of chloroplast development in melon flesh and direct interaction with PRR2 (Valverde et al., 2025, Preprint). Similarly, Capsicum chinense LSD ONE LIKE1 (CcLOL1) controlled chloroplast development in pepper and tomato fruits that resulted in higher carotenoid content in ripe fruits (Borovsky et al., 2019).

Phytoene synthesis and carotenoid accumulation down-regulate photosynthesis, and promote both thylakoid disassembly and accumulation of plastoglobules (Llorente et al., 2020; Morelli et al., 2023). These findings suggest that factors controlling carotenoid synthesis could be regulating chloroplast-to-chromoplast transition (Fig. 2). Accordingly, increasing carotenoid levels by overexpressing a bacterial PSY revealed that accumulation of carotenoids promoted the transition from chloroplast to chromoplasts and induced artificial chromoplast formation in leaves (Llorente et al., 2020). In tomato, HY5 promotes carotenoid biosynthesis, regulating ripening and fruit quality (J. Wang et al., 2025). However, HY5 direct involvement in transcriptional regulation of chromoplast biogenesis beyond carotenoid and sugar accumulation has not been established. Several members of the MCM1, AGAMOUS, DEFICIENS, and SRF, serum response factor (MADS) family of TFs have been implicated in the regulation of fruit ripening and carotenoid biosynthesis in different species (Tadiello et al., 2009; Martel et al., 2011; Dong et al., 2013; Lu et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2023). Results of ectopic expression of sweet orange CsMADS6 in tomato revealed that transcriptional reprogramming and regulation of carotenoid biosynthesis could lead to chromoplast differentiation. Tomato fruit pericarp in the CsMADS6 transgenic plants was similar to that of the wild type, but sepals developed characteristics of fruit pericarp with high levels of carotenoids and chromoplasts instead of chlorophyll and thylakoid-rich chloroplasts (Lu et al., 2018). More recent work overexpressing navel orange CsMADS3 has shown not only increased carotenoid content but also an increment in plastoglobuli and larger chromoplasts in citrus calli and tomato fruit, supporting the relevance of this family of TFs in chromoplast biogenesis (Zhu et al., 2023).

Approaches to identify transcriptional regulators of carotenoid biosynthesis in Mimulus flowers have led to the characterization of REDUCED CAROTENOID PIGMENTATION2 (RCP2), a tetratricopeptide repeat (TRP) protein homologous to Arabidopsis REDUCED CHLOROPLAST COVERAGE (REC) proteins that are involved in chloroplast compartment establishment (Fig. 2). Mimulus RCP2 was involved not only in carotenoid biosynthesis but also in chromoplast development in flowers. Analysis of other Mimulus mutants affected in carotenoid biosynthesis revealed that the role of RCP2 in promoting chromoplast development is independent of carotenoid biosynthesis (Stanley et al., 2020). Although not a transcriptional regulator, the study that identified RCP2 is of high interest since it involves chromoplast development in flowers. Furthermore, it also identified two RCP2-like proteins that specifically regulated the chloroplast compartment, and chlorophyll and carotenoid levels in Mimulus leaves (Stanley et al., 2020). The role of REC proteins in the regulation of chromoplast development was recently supported with results from tomato and watermelon (Hu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). Watermelon REC2 expression was positively correlated with chromoplast number and accumulation of carotenoids in deep (scarlet) red versus paler (coral) red flesh varieties (Li et al., 2025). In tomato, REC proteins, particularly REC2, regulated carotenoid accumulation in fruit and flowers, and chloroplast and chromoplast development and the cell space which they occupy in pericarp cells of green and red fruit, respectively. Interestingly, tomato REC proteins negatively regulate plastid division specifically during the transition to chromoplasts (Hu et al., 2024).

Although transcriptional changes have been widely studied, chromoplast differentiation involves massive changes in the plastid proteome, and, consequently, plastid protein import and degradation play an important role in the process. Arabidopsis plastid envelope-localized ubiquitin E3 ligase SUPPRESSOR OF PPI1 LOCUS1 (SP1) is involved in leaf senescence, and tomato SP1 and its homologue SPL2 promote the transition to chromoplasts in tomato fruit (Ling et al., 2021). Tomato SP1 knockdown plants showed delayed fruit ripening, and plastids with abundant thylakoid membranes still present at day 8, when wild-type fruit plastids had already transitioned to immature chromoplasts. These results suggest that tomato SP1 and SPL2 regulate plastid import to control protein levels during plastid transitions (Ling et al., 2021 ).

Recent research is shedding light on regulatory mechanisms of chromoplast biogenesis, but current knowledge is limited to fruit ripening and the chloroplasts-to-chromoplast transition mainly in tomato. Chromoplasts are of high interest for the agrifood sector, and research into the pathways regulating their development is still in its infancy.

Conclusion and future perspectives

Plastids are a very diverse group of semi-autonomous organelles with the same phylogenetic and ontogenetic origin. Despite recent advances, the mechanisms underlying biogenesis and differentiation of plastids other than chloroplasts remain limited or unknown.

Chloroplast biogenesis is regulated by a complex regulatory network that shares common elements but also differs in specific cells and organs. Some components of the transcriptional network evolved early and could be common to all lineages, such as GLKs and the recently characterized RR-MYBs, which are the only TFs currently known regulating chloroplast development in both Arabidopsis and Marchantia (Frangedakis et al., 2024; Hernández-Muñoz et al., 2024; Yelina et al., 2024). Other factors of the network appear to have been recruited or evolved in some lineages and branches, such as HY5 and GATAs, to accommodate increased tissue complexity or due to the requirement to adapt to challenging environments (Yelina et al., 2024).

Cell- and tissue-specific regulation of plastid development is becoming more relevant. Although some factors have been identified that could be regulating chloroplast development in specific tissues, such as C4 bundle sheath (Salesse et al., 2017; Camo-Escobar et al., 2024, Preprint), it is more clear now that this cellular specificity might have appeared through the rewiring of existing cis-motifs of cell- and tissue-specific TFs (Swift et al., 2024). This hypothesis of motif evolution is gaining increasing relevance as a mechanism to adapt and diversify gene regulatory networks to control chloroplast biogenesis at the species, tissular, or cellular level, as shown by the comparative analysis in C3 and C4 plants and the divergence of GLK-binding sites across several species (Tu et al., 2022; Swift et al., 2024; Yelina et al., 2024). Besides transcriptional control of chloroplast development, recent works with the histone acetyltransferases HLS1 and NuA4 complex show that, as expected, epigenetic modifications are involved in the process (Barrero-Gil et al., 2022; Bieluszewski et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2022; Peng et al., 2025). However, the details and range of this epigenetic regulation, and the contribution of other epigenetic modifications, remain largely underexplored.

By contrast, mechanisms regulating differentiation of other plastids remain mostly unknown and restricted to chromoplasts in fruits. Although research into fruit ripening is extensive, many of the works do not go further than analysing carotenoids or sugar levels due to their impact on organoleptic properties and commercial value, leaving the link to chromoplast development and ultrastructure unknown. Despite this, several factors that promote chromoplast development have been identified (Fig. 2), such as OR and REC family proteins (Stanley et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Wrightstone et al., 2025). TFs of the MADS family seem to be regulating exclusively chromoplast differentiation (Lu et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2023). In addition, other TFs, such as the GLKs, PRR2, and CsLOL1, regulate chloroplast development and indirectly affect later chromoplast differentiation (Powell et al., 2012; Pan et al., 2013; Brand et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2018; Borovsky et al., 2019; Jeong et al., 2020). Further research is required to understand the regulation of chromoplast development, and the links between chloroplasts and onset of chromoplast differentiation, as well as to identify additional regulatory factors. Additionally, research work in other tissues that develop chromoplasts, such as flowers, roots, and tubers, could improve our understanding of the pathways by highlighting general and specific components.

Plastid research is a very active field, but further work is needed to gain a better understanding of the molecular mechanisms behind the biogenesis of the different plastids. Even in the more active chloroplast development area there are still some outstanding questions that remain unanswered (Box 1). As described above, adoption of new model organisms with less complex structures and genomes could facilitate the identification of novel regulators controlling chloroplast development. Research including diverse plants and photosynthetic organisms together with comparative genomics, transcriptomics, and machine learning approaches could uncover information on rewiring of regulatory networks and recruitment of existing factors. Detailed spatio-temporal analysis of plastid development and identification of developmental milestones during the process in different photosynthetic types along with single-cell and spatial omics will enable the identification of cell-specific regulatory factors and networks that promote or repress the development of chloroplasts or other plastids in specific tissues and cells. Advancing this knowledge is crucial not only for understanding plastid biology and chloroplast functions, but also for future efforts to engineer climate-smart crops with enhanced productivity and resilience.

Outstanding questions

Plastid biogenesis is a highly complex process that depends on both developmental and environmental signals and cues. Over the course of evolution, and with increasing organismal complexity, a diversity of plastids emerged beyond the photosynthetic chloroplast. This diversity required the addition of molecular mechanisms to regulate differentiation and interconversion in specific tissues and cells, at specific developmental stages. Although significant progress has been made in the identification of factors controlling chloroplast biogenesis, many questions remain for future research.
