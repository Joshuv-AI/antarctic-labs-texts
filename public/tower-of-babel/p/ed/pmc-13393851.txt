Morphological changes of the cervical spine in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis and correlation analysis
Authors: Feipeng Qin, Jiajun Ma, Yi Shen, Hapulile Ndalyolusha Tileinge, Xiaoming Ying
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate the morphological changes of the cervical spine in patients with adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) and to analyze their correlation with other coronal and sagittal plane parameters of the spine.MethodsFull-length standing spinal radiographs were retrospectively collected from 199 patients with AIS and 77 adolescents without scoliosis who underwent evaluation at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University between January 2019 and February 2025. Patients with AIS were classified into two groups based on the physiological curvature of their cervical spine: a non-kyphotic group and a cervical kyphosis (CK) group. The following radiographic parameters were measured: coronal main curve Cobb angle, C2–C7 angle, T1 slope (T1S), T1 coronal tilt, apical vertebra translation (AVT), thoracic kyphosis (TK), lumbar lordosis (LL), sacral slope (SS), pelvic tilt (PT), pelvic incidence (PI), cervical sagittal vertical axis (CSVA), and sagittal vertical axis (SVA). Intergroup comparisons and correlation analyses of these parameters were subsequently performed.ResultsThe incidence of CK among patients with AIS was 60.3%, which was significantly higher than the 46.8% observed in the control group. Compared to patients with mild scoliosis, those with moderate scoliosis exhibited a significant reduction in the C2–C7 angle (P < 0.05), while the CSVA showed a significant increase (P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed in the C2–C7 angle, CSVA, or CK rates among the lumbar, thoracolumbar, and thoracic main curve groups (P > 0.05). In the thoracic main curve group, patients with a left convex curve had a significantly lower rate of CK compared to those with a right convex curve (P = 0.039). Correlation analysis revealed that C2–C7 angle was positively correlated with T1S (r = 0.628, P < 0.001) and TK (r = 0.552, P < 0.001), and negatively correlated with Cobb angle (r = -0.232, P < 0.001) and PT(r = -0.170, P = 0.016), CK was positively correlated with the Cobb angle (r = 0.289, P < 0.001), PT (r = 0.173, P = 0.015), while it showed a negative correlation with TK (r = -0.460, P < 0.001) and T1S (r = -0.518, P < 0.001).ConclusionCervical kyphosis is a prevalent morphological change observed in patients with AIS, and is correlated with spinopelvic parameters including the Cobb angle, PT, TK, and T1S. Logistic regression analysis identified Cobb angle and T1S as independent predictors of CK.

Introduction

Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis (AIS) is a three-dimensional spinal deformity of unknown etiology and is one of the major conditions affecting the spinal health of adolescents, with a global prevalence of approximately 2%–3% [1]. Although the coronal Cobb angle has long been relied upon as the gold standard for diagnosis and severity grading in clinical practice, increasing evidence suggests that the pathological changes in AIS cannot be adequately summarized by a single plane; structural alterations in both the sagittal and axial planes are equally significant and should not be overlooked [2–4]. In the field of sagittal plane studies, a core theory is the concept of “spinal-pelvic sagittal balance” [5], which describes the intricate mechanical compensatory relationships among various segments from the cranial base to the pelvis. The cervical spine, as the apex of this compensatory chain, plays a crucial role in maintaining head positioning and overall balance. In recent years, researchers have begun to focus on the cervical spine of patients with AIS, with preliminary studies revealing significant changes in sagittal plane morphology. However, existing research primarily centers on the associations of parameters within the sagittal plane [6], and investigations into how coronal plane deformities affect the sagittal alignment of the cervical spine—a cross-plane issue—are still relatively scarce. Understanding this “coronal-sagittal” relationship is crucial for elucidating the three-dimensional compensatory mechanisms underlying AIS. Based on this rationale, the present study employs a retrospective case analysis approach, systematically collecting full-length spinal X-ray data of AIS patients from the Third Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University over the past five years. The aim of this research is to analyze the intrinsic relationships between coronal plane scoliosis characteristics and sagittal parameters of the cervical spine, with the hope of filling the current research gap and providing theoretical support for comprehensive three-dimensional assessments and the development of individualized treatment strategies for AIS.

Materials and methods

This retrospective case study analyzed patients who underwent full-length standing anteroposterior and lateral spinal radiographs at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University from January 2019 to February 2025. The study included 199 patients with AIS and 77 adolescents without scoliosis (control group). Radiological parameters of the coronal and sagittal planes were measured on the full-length spinal radiographs. All included subjects were categorized into two groups based on the physiological curvature of their cervical spine: (1) a non-kyphotic group, defined as a C2–C7 angle ≥ 0°; and (2) a CK group, defined as a C2–C7 angle < 0°.

Inclusion criteria for the AIS group were as follows

(1) age 10–18 years; (2) initial presentation with untreated AIS; (3) a Cobb angle of 10°–45° on standing full-spine radiographs; (4) absence of other spinal disorders or prior spinal surgery.

Exclusion criteria for the AIS group were as follows:

(1) congenital, neuromuscular, or syndromic scoliosis; (2) history of spinal surgery; (3) history of cervical trauma, infection, or tumor; (4) inadequate radiographic quality.

Inclusion criteria for the control group were as follows

(1) age 10–18 years; (2) a Cobb angle < 10° on full-spine radiographs with no structural spinal abnormalities; (3) no history of scoliosis, kyphosis, or cervical symptoms.

Exclusion criteria for the control group were identical to criteria (2)–(4) of the AIS group

All included subjects underwent full-length standing anteroposterior and lateral spinal radiographs. All measurements were performed using Surgimap software.

Measurement reliability

All radiographic parameters were independently measured by two investigators who had undergone standardized training. In cases of disagreement, the final value was adjudicated by a third physician.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 25.0. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (for normally distributed data) or as a median and interquartile range (for non-normally distributed data). Intergroup comparisons were conducted using the independent samples t-test for normally distributed data and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and were analyzed using the chi-square (χ²) test. The correlation between cervical curvature and other parameters was assessed using two approaches: (1) Spearman correlation analysis with the C2–C7 angle as a continuous variable; and (2) point-biserial correlation and between-group comparisons with CK (C2–C7 angle < 0°) as a dichotomous variable. Sensitivity analysis was additionally performed using a more conservative threshold for CK (C2–C7 angle < − 5°). The strength of correlation was interpreted as negligible (|r| < 0.1), weak (0.1 ≤ |r| < 0.39), moderate (0.4 ≤ |r| < 0.69), strong (0.7 ≤ |r| < 0.89), or very strong (|r| ≥ 0.9). Logistic regression was performed to identify independent risk factors of CK. A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Correlation analysis of C2–C7 angle and cervical kyphosis with various spinal parameters

Correlation analysis between C2–C7 angle and various coronal and sagittal parameters revealed that C2–C7 angle was weakly positively correlated with SVA; moderately positively correlated with T1S and TK; and weakly negatively correlated with PT, Cobb angle, and CSVA, and significant correlations were observed with the remaining parameters. Discussion

AIS is one of the principal spinal disorders affecting adolescent health, characterized by a three-dimensional structural deformity of the spine involving the coronal, sagittal, and axial planes. As an integral component of the spinopelvic complex, the cervical spine plays a key role in maintaining global sagittal balance through its alignment [10]. However, the relationship between cervical sagittal morphology and main coronal curve characteristics in patients with mild to moderate AIS remains insufficiently explored. Abnormal cervical curvature in AIS has been investigated for decades. As early as 1983, Cochran et al. [11] reported that approximately 47% of patients with AIS exhibited loss of cervical lordosis or overt cervical kyphosis. Subsequent studies have corroborated this finding: Canavese et al. [12] observed a CK incidence of 34.4% in a cohort of 32 patients, while Hiyama et al. [13] and Tang et al. [14] reported rates of 59.5% and 60.7%, respectively. These studies uniformly adopted a C2–C7 angle < 0° as the radiographic criterion for CK. Applying the same threshold, the present study identified a CK prevalence of 60.3% in the AIS group, which was significantly higher than that in the non-scoliosis control group; the C2–C7 angle was also significantly lower in the AIS group. Furthermore, TK was found to be significantly different between the AIS and control groups, consistent with previous findings reported by Zhang et al. [15] and Yong et al. [16]. These observations further support the notion that cervical sagittal malalignment constitutes an integral component of the three-dimensional deformity in AIS. Cervical morphological alterations in AIS are generally considered to be associated with global spinal realignment, particularly with changes in sagittal profile such as TK. In the context of nonoperative management of scoliosis, increasing emphasis has been placed on the role of sagittal alignment. Accordingly, we postulate that cervical sagittal morphology constitutes an essential part of the comprehensive evaluation of AIS

Tang et al. [14] reported that the prevalence of CK increased with increasing Cobb angle, with patients demonstrating a Cobb angle > 40° exhibiting a significantly higher rate of CK compared to those with Cobb angle ≤ 40°. Hu et al. [17] and Han et al. [18] respectively reported that the prevalence of CK increased when the coronal thoracic Cobb angle reached ≥ 20°, and that the C2–C7 angle was negatively correlated with the Cobb angle when the latter was < 45°. In the present study, CK was also positively correlated with the Cobb angle, lending further support to the notion that coronal plane deformity influences cervical sagittal alignment. Moreover, CK was negatively correlated with both T1S and TK. Taken together with prior evidence that the Cobb angle is negatively correlated with TK, these findings suggest that coronal thoracic curvature may indirectly compromise cervical lordosis through a reduction in thoracic kyphosis

Comparison of cervical sagittal parameters among the three curve pattern groups (main thoracic, thoracolumbar, and main lumbar) revealed no significant differences in C2–C7 angle, CSVA, or rate of CK. The location of the main curve did not influence the prevalence of CK. These findings suggest that the coronal position of the main curve may not directly dictate cervical sagittal alignment, nor does it independently contribute to the risk of developing CK. Cervical sagittal imbalance appears to be more closely associated with global truncal alignment, spinopelvic relationships, or compensatory mechanisms, rather than being determined solely by the level of the main curve.

Subgroup analysis of patients with main thoracic AIS, stratified by curve direction, revealed that the CK rate was significantly lower in those with left-convex curves than in those with right-convex curves. This finding suggests that curve direction may influence the prevalence of CK in patients with main thoracic AIS

The present study demonstrated significant differences between the CK group and the non-kyphotic group in multiple parameters, including C2–C7 angle, T1S, SVA, TK, PT, and Cobb angle. No significant differences were observed in AVT or coronal T1 tilt. Hilibrand et al. [19], Akbar et al. [3], and Hiyama et al. [13] have previously reported a significant association between diminished thoracic kyphosis and the presence of CK. Wang et al. [20] further observed that the T5-T12 Cobb angle was significantly lower in the CK group compared to the non-kyphotic group. Han et al. [18] also identified a positive correlation between C2–C7 angle and TK, indicating that reduced thoracic kyphosis is accompanied by diminished cervical lordosis. The present findings corroborate this compensatory relationship: TK was significantly lower in the CK group than in the non-kyphotic group, and correlation analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between CK and TK, whereas no significant correlation was found between CK and LL

Jiang et al. [21] reported a positive correlation between cervical tilt and AVT in the main thoracic curve. La Maida et al. [22] demonstrated that apical vertebral derotation-translation techniques aimed at correcting AVT could simultaneously improve sagittal balance and CL. Tang et al. [14] also identified an association between AVT and CK, suggesting that AVT may serve as a predictor of cervical morphological changes. However, the present study did not observe such associations. Among patients with AIS and a Cobb angle < 45°, no significant difference in AVT was found between the CK and non-kyphotic groups, and correlation analysis revealed no significant relationship between CK and AVT. The absence of a significant association between cervical kyphosis and AVT in our study, which differs from the findings reported by Tang et al. [14] and Jiang et al. [21], warrants further discussion. This discrepancy cannot be fully explained by differences in curve severity alone. From a biomechanical perspective, the influence of AVT on the cervical spine may be mediated by the compensatory capacity of the thoracic and lumbar spine. In our cohort, which consisted predominantly of patients with mild-to-moderate AIS (mean Cobb angle 20.39°), effective regional compensation may have shielded the cervical spine from coronal plane imbalance. In contrast, in severe curves (Cobb angle > 40°), this compensatory reserve may be exhausted, thereby unmasking a direct correlation. Furthermore, Zhang et al. [23] reported no significant correlation between coronal and sagittal parameters in patients with mild-to-moderate thoracic AIS, which indirectly corroborates our observations. Methodological differences, such as variations in AVT measurement techniques or the inclusion of different curve patterns, may also have contributed to the discrepant findings. Future studies should stratify analyses by both curve severity and curve type to elucidate these biomechanical pathways.

No significant association was observed between CK and T1 coronal tilt in the present study, which contrasts with the findings of Tang et al. [14], who reported a significantly greater T1 coronal tilt in patients with CK. The discrepancy between our findings and those of Tang et al. [14] may be attributable to differences in the nature of T1 coronal tilt. In the study by Tang et al., T1 coronal tilt was identified as an independent risk factor for CK, suggesting that T1 coronal tilt in their cohort may have largely reflected involvement in the primary structural deformity. In contrast, the present study predominantly comprised patients with main thoracic curves, in whom T1 was typically not included within the instrumented segments of the curve. Under these circumstances, the observed T1 coronal tilt does not represent a primary structural alteration but rather a secondary compensatory deviation of the cranial end of the spine aimed at maintaining global trunk balance. Such compensatory deviations are generally of limited magnitude, possess low mechanical transmission efficiency, and are insufficient to generate the biomechanical effects required to induce measurable changes in local osseous morphology. Consequently, no statistically significant association was observed between T1 coronal tilt and cervical kyphosis in this study.

Spinopelvic parameters related to the T1 vertebra have been proposed as predictors of global spinal alignment [24–27]. Knott et al. [28] demonstrated a significant correlation between T1S and SVA, suggesting that T1S may serve as a useful parameter for assessing sagittal balance. Pesenti et al. [24] further reported that T1S and sagittal tilt effectively predict postoperative changes in regional parameters such as cervical lordosis and TK. Logistic regression analysis further demonstrated that both Cobb angle and T1S were independent predictors of CK. A larger Cobb angle emerged as an independent risk factor for CK, confirming that greater curve severity is associated with an increased risk of cervical kyphosis, consistent with the univariate and sensitivity analyses. Conversely, a larger T1S was identified as a protective factor, indicating that a greater T1 slope is associated with a lower risk of CK. Biomechanically, a larger T1S affords greater compensatory capacity for cervical lordosis, thereby reducing the likelihood of kyphotic malalignment. Specifically, coronal deformity in AIS is frequently accompanied by a reduction in TK, which in turn causes the T1 vertebra to adopt a more vertical orientation, thereby decreasing T1S. As the foundation for cervical lordosis, a reduced T1S compels the cervical spine into compensatory hyperextension to maintain horizontal gaze; once this compensatory mechanism is exhausted, physiological lordosis may progress to cervical kyphosis

Sensitivity analysis provided further support for the above inference. When a more stringent criterion for CK (C2–C7 angle < − 5°) was applied, the associations of CK with Cobb angle, TK, and T1S remained robust, whereas its associations with coronal displacement parameters such as AVT disappeared. This suggests that changes in sagittal curvature represent a more direct contributor to CK in mild-to-moderate AIS. Notably, T1S was significantly lower in the CK group than in the non-kyphotic group in our study, which contrasts with the findings of Tang et al. [14], who reported a larger T1S in patients with CK. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in the study populations: an increased T1S in patients with severe or postoperative curves may represent an end-stage compensatory manifestation, whereas the reduced T1S observed in our mild-to-moderate AIS cohort may reflect an earlier phase in the progression from compensatory cervical hyperlordosis to decompensated kyphosis. This suggests that T1S may carry distinct pathophysiological significance at different stages of the disease.

Based on these observational findings, clinicians may consider sagittal alignment, particularly thoracic kyphosis and the cervicothoracic junction, when comprehensively evaluating patients with AIS. In conservative management, attention to maintaining thoracic sagittal contour during bracing and incorporating exercises aimed at thoracic extension and deep cervical flexor endurance may be clinically relevant. However, these implications should be interpreted cautiously, as the present cross-sectional study cannot establish whether modifying cervical or thoracic sagittal alignment improves scoliosis outcomes

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective cross-sectional design precluded dynamic observation of disease progression. Second, the sample sizes were unevenly distributed across AIS subtypes; in particular, the number of right-sided curves in the thoracolumbar and lumbar groups was too small, limiting the validity of subgroup comparisons. Third, coronal curve patterns were not further stratified, making it difficult to exclude the potential influence of combined curve patterns on the results. Finally, although baseline demographic characteristics were matched, several confounding factors that may independently affect the C2–C7 angle were not controlled for, including (1) habitual posture (e.g., prolonged smartphone use with a forward head posture in adolescents) and (2) participation in specific sports (e.g., swimming, gymnastics). The absence of these variables may have led to an overestimation of the association between cervical kyphosis and AIS. Future studies should enroll larger samples, adopt more refined subgroup designs, and systematically collect relevant confounding variables to yield more robust evidence.

Conclusion

Cervical kyphosis is among the most prevalent morphological alterations in patients with AIS and is closely associated with curve severity. The significant correlations of CK with Cobb angle, PT, TK, and T1S suggest that cervical sagittal alignment constitutes an integral component of the comprehensive evaluation of AIS. Logistic regression analysis further demonstrated that Cobb angle and T1S are independent predictors of cervical kyphosis in AIS patients
