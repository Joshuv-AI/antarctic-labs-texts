Next-generation sequencing, assembly, annotation, and phylogenomic analysis of the complete mitochondrial genome of the painted lady butterfly Vanessa cardui (Linnaeus, 1758) (Insecta: Lepidoptera: Nymphalidae)
Authors: Roohollah Abbasi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVanessa butterflies, with 22 known species with local and cosmopolitan distributions, are good model organisms to study factors that contribute to the distribution and adaptation in the animal kingdom. Here, the complete mitochondrial genome of Vanessa cardui, a cosmopolitan member of Vanessa butterflies in Nymphalidae, was sequenced, assembled, annotated, and characterized. ResultsThe mitogenome was a midsize circular molecule with 15,299 bps containing regular 37 elements, including 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 2 rRNA genes, 22 tRNA genes, and an A + T-rich control region. The genes were organized by 11 intergenic and 13 overlapping spaces. The mitogenome showed a GC content of 19 % and a GC skew of -0.191724138, which was the lowest among the other sequenced Vanessa species. Out of 13 PCGs, only 7 start with ATG, and only 9 end with a TAA stop codon. Analysis of Relative Synonymous Codon Usage (RSCU) demonstrated that out of 66 codons, including stop codons, 25 codons were overrepresented, 7 were unbiased, and 34 were underrepresented. The tRNAs with the size range of 60 bps (tRNASer(AGN)) to 74 bps (tRNATrp) all showed typical cloverleaf secondary structures except for tRNASer(AGN), in which the D-loop was not seen. A Bayesian phylogenomic analysis of V. cardui's mitogenome and 42 other butterfly mitogenomes showed a well-resolved tree with 7 major clades.ConclusionsStudying Vanessa butterflies with different distribution ranges and their mitogenomes may provide insights into genetic factors influencing species distribution and adaptation. The complete mitogenome data provided better resolution in phylogenetic reconstructions for exploring the evolutionary history of the species. Background

The mitochondrion is the powerhouse of eukaryotic cells, and the number of them in each cell varies enormously by cell type, metabolic state, and organism. This tiny organelle is believed to be the result of a permanent endosymbiosis between alphaproteobacterium (purple non-sulphur bacterium) and an ancestral eukaryote some two billion years ago [1, 2]. Along with energy production, cell aging, apoptosis, production of biosynthetic precursors for macromolecules, metabolic waste management, and redox homeostasis are other important processes in which mitochondria are involved [1, 3].

Mitochondria have their own genome, and the structure and size of the genome can be diverse among organisms. For example, in Metazoans, the banded tube-dwelling anemone Isarachnanthus nocturnus (Hartog, 1977) in the phylum Cnidaria has the largest mitochondrial genome with linear structure and a size of 80,923 bp [4] and creeping comb jelly Vallicula multiformis (Rankin, 1956) in the phylum Ctenophora has the smallest mitochondrial genome with a circular structure and size of 9,961 bp [5]. A few lineages of lice (Insecta: Psocodea: Phthiraptera) have replaced their single circular mitochondrial DNA with multiple minicircular chromosomes with fewer genes [6]. The typical structure and size of the mitochondrial genome in the animal kingdom is circular and around 16 kb [7, 8].

Typically, the animal mitochondrial genome contains 37 genes, including 2 for rRNAs, 22 for tRNAs, and 13 for proteins [7, 8]. There is also a single large non-coding region that contains the origin of replication and transcription and is called the control region, A + T-rich region, or D-loop [7, 9–11]. With a few exceptions, these 37 genes and their arrangements within the mitochondrial genome are conserved across metazoans with bilateral symmetry [11].

In recent years, the importance of the mitochondrial genome in evolutionary and systematic studies has become clear [11–13]. Exploring the organelle genomes database, which is part of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Reference Sequence project, for the statistics of complete mitogenomes [14, 15] resulted in 18,207 complete mitochondrial genomes belonging to Eukaryotes to the date of submission of the manuscript. Among these mitochondrial genomes, 7862 mitogenomes belong to chordates, and the remaining belong to non-chordate Eukaryotes. Phylum Arthropoda, with 6080 mitogenomes, is the most studied phylum of non-chordate Eukaryotes but given the vast diversity of the group which includes about 80% of the known species of the Animal Kingdom, it is still underrepresented in the database.

Butterflies are among the well-studied group of insects in terms of their mitochondrial genomes in the Phylum Arthropoda. The painted lady butterfly, Vanessa cardui (Linnaeus, 1758) (Insecta: Lepidoptera: Nymphalidae: Nymphalini), is a cosmopolitan species living in most of the planet except for most of South America and Oceania [16]. It is a migratory butterfly with a powerful and fast flight, which makes it a long-distance, multi-generational migrant [17, 18]. The species has been recorded from all provinces in Iran [19, 20]. It has also emerged as an excellent model organism for studying color pattern evolution and development, and can be reared in the laboratory [21–24]. The adult butterfly feeds usually on nectar from Taraxacum, Rubus, Tamarix, Mentha, Sambucus, Thymus, Acantholimon, and other plants. The larvae also feed on plants from the Astraceae, Brassicaceae, Lamiaceae, Chenopodiaceae, Apiaceae, Rutaceae, Leguminosae, and Ulmaceae [17, 25].

Here I sequenced, assembled, and annotated the complete mitochondrial genome (mitogenome) of the painted lady butterfly Vanessa cardui (Linnaeus, 1758) (Insecta: Lepidoptera: Nymphalidae: Nymphalinae: Nymphalini) collected from Iran. I also did phylogenomic analysis of the mitogenome of the species. There is no publication to specifically discuss the painted lady butterfly mitogenome features, but there are two Vanessa cardui mitogenomes in GenBank (LR999956.2 and MN013030.1) that both lack annotations. The mitogenome LR999956.2 has been sequenced using the PacBio platform with the sample collected from the United Kingdom as part of the Darwin Tree of Life Project (https://www.darwintreeoflife.org/). The mitogenome MN013030.1 has been sequenced using the Illumina Hiseq 4000 platform with the sample collected from China [26].

Results

Exploring the organelle genomes database of NCBI

List of complete mitogenomes for Arthropoda, Insecta, Lepidoptera, Nymphalidae and Nymphalinae have been provided in a supplemental excel file. In the Phylum Arthropoda, the mitogenome size varied between 12,984 bp to 42,736 bp. Also, the Class Insecta with 4955 mitogenomes was the most studied class, with the mitogenome size ranging from 13,010 to 27,450 bp. The four largest insect orders, Coleoptera, Lepidoptera, Diptera, and Hymenoptera, had 702, 1081, 784, and 256 mitogenomes deposited in the database, respectively. The mitogenome size in Lepidoptera varied between 14,489 bp and 17,050 bp. From the Lepidopteran insects, the family Nymphalidae had 272 mitogenomes ranging in size from 14,957 to 16,348 bp. Also, the subfamily Nymphaline had 69 mitogenomes ranging from 14,957 to 15,921 bp in size.

Assembly and general features of the Vanessa Cardui mitochondrial genome

A total of 68,301,479 Illumina NovaSeq 6000 reads were produced from the indexed library of V. cardui. After quality trimming the total reads, the V. cardui circular mitogenome was assembled from 279,630 paired reads (Fig. 1). The minimum and maximum base coverages of the mitochondrial genome were 19-fold and 4067-fold, respectively, and the mean base coverage was 2739.8-fold, and the standard deviation was 747.2. The nucleotide composition of the mitogenome was 41.4% A, 7.7% C, 11.3% G, and 39.6% T, and the GC content was 19.0%. The typical mitochondrial gene composition and arrangement in butterflies is seen in the V. cardui mitogenome [27]. The circular V. cardui mitogenome is 15,299 bp in length and composed of 37 genes, including 13 protein-coding genes, 2 rRNA genes, and 22 tRNA genes. Based on the information from the database, the V. cardui mitogenome is among the average-size mitogenomes in Nymphalid butterflies. Comparing the assembled mitogenome with LR999956.2, only 4 SNPs and 8 insertions, and 8 deletions were detected.

Protein coding genes

The Vanessa cardui mitogenome contained 13 protein-coding genes with a total of 3731 codons. The study of amino acids used in the structure of these 13 proteins showed that Leucine, with a frequency of 539, and Cysteine, with a frequency of 35, were the most and the least used amino acids in the V. cardui mitochondrial genome. Also, of these 13 genes, 9 were encoded by the heavy strand and 4 were encoded by the light strand of the mitochondrion. The start codons for V. cardui protein coding gene included: ATG (COII, ATP6, COIII, ND4, ND4L, Cytb and ND1), ATT (ND2, ATP8, ND3 and ND5), ATC (ND6), and CGA (COI) [28]. The stop codons for V. cardui protein coding gene included: TAA (ND2, ATP8, ATP6, COIII, ND3, ND4L, ND6, Cytb, and ND1) and a single T (COI, COII, ND5, and ND4), completed by post-transcriptional addition of 30 A residues (Table 1). Codon counts were provided by Geneious Prime, and the RSCU values for codons of protein-coding genes were calculated in a formulated Microsoft Excel sheet (supplemental data) based on the formula provided in the methods section, and the results were provided in Fig. 2. As seen in the figure, the codon CTT for Leucine, with the value of 3.29, is the most overrepresented, and 5 codons with the RSCU values of 0 are the most underrepresented. Out of 66 codons, including stop codons, 25 codons were overrepresented, 7 codons were unbiased or randomly used, and 34 codons were underrepresented.

tRNAs and rRNAs, and control region

The Vanessa cardui mitogenome contained 22 tRNA genes similar to other butterflies. Of these 22 tRNAs, 8 were encoded by the light strand and 14 were encoded by the heavy strand. All tRNA structures were verified using ARWEN v.1.2 [29]. The tRNAs all had typical cloverleaf secondary structures except for tRNASer (AGN), in which the D-loop was not seen (supplemental data). In terms of size, tRNA genes ranged from 60 bp (tRNASer(AGN)) to 74 bp (tRNATrp). The V. cardui mitogenome also contained 2 rRNA genes encoded by the light strand, namely the 16 S rRNA with a length of 1333 bp and the 12 S rRNA with a length of 779 bp, and had a similar GC content of 14.9%. It also had a control region with a length of 335 bp and GC content of 6.2%.

Gene number and arrangement comparison

Geneious Prime was used to examine mitogenomes for the number, arrangement, and orientation/position of genes and compare them to the ancestral insect and typical lepidopteran arrangements [27] (Table 3). The Vanessa cardui, along with the other 42 studied butterflies, had the typical 37 genes in their mitogenome, and no complete gene duplications or deletions were observed. In terms of the number and order of protein-coding genes, no differences were seen among the studied butterflies. Regarding the 16 s and 12 s rRNA genes, only Stichophthalma louisa had both of them in the Heavy strand, in contrast to the rest of the studied butterflies. Also, about the number of tRNA genes, no difference was seen among the 43 taxa; however, the position of a few tRNAs was different as follows: In Stichophthalma louisa, the tRNACys, tRNAPhe, and tRNAHis were observed on the Heavy strand. In Junonia iphita, the tRNAPhe and tRNAPro were observed in the Heavy strand.

Phylogenomic analysis of V. cardui

Three Bayesian phylogenetic trees were reconstructed with the complete mitogenome alignment (Fig. 3), the Gblocks curated alignment (Fig. 4), and the 13 protein-coding genes alignment (Fig. 5). All the trees were reconstructed using the mitogenome of V. cardui and 40 other mitogenomes from the family Nymphalidae, a mitogenome from Lycaenidae, and a mitogenome from Riodinidae. The first tree (Fig. 3) was well resolved with strong grouping supports, while the second (Fig. 4) and third (Fig. 5) trees showed polytomy and weak grouping supports in parts of the trees, respectively. The concept of clade demonstrates the monophyletic groups within the tree and introduces sister taxa and groups. The first phylogenetic tree from complete mitogenomes was well resolved, and 7 major clades were seen with high posterior probability (PP) support (Fig. 3). A clade for subfamily Danainae (PP = 100), a clade composed of Calinaginae, Charaxinae, Morphinae, and Satyrinae (PP = 100), a clade for Libytheinae (PP = 100), a clade including Heliconiinae and Limenitidinae (PP = 100), a clade for Pseudergolinae (PP = 100), a clade composed of Apaturinae, Biblidinae, Cyrestinae, and the tribe Coeini from Nymphalinae (PP = 86), and finally a clade including the rest of the studied species of Nymphalinae (PP = 100). In the second and third trees, the major change in groupings compared to the first tree happened with the species of Libytheinae that became the basal group instead of the species of Danainae on the studied Nymphalidae butterflies (Figs. 4 and 5).

Divergence dating

The time tree was inferred by the RelTime method [31, 32]. For this analysis, the Bayesian phylogeny from the complete mitogenome dataset and the General Time Reversible model of nucleotide substitutions were used. Branch lengths were estimated using the Maximum Likelihood method [33]. The estimated log likelihood of the tree was − 175,409.35. The evolutionary rate differences among sites were modeled using a discrete Gamma distribution across 5 categories (+ G, parameter = 0.6667), with 40.27% of sites deemed evolutionarily invariant (+ I). The RelTime analysis incorporated 2 calibration constraints that were used to derive minimum and/or maximum bounds at nodes with calibration densities [34]. Divergence time estimates were shown next to the nodes in the tree (Fig. 6). Notably, times were not estimated for nodes in the outgroup because the RelTime method does not assume that evolutionary rates are the same for ingroup and outgroup lineages [32]. The time tree showed that Vanessa, Nymphalinae, and Nymphalidae diverged some 22.8, 46.3, and 69.9 million years ago (MYA), respectively.

Discussion

The complete mitochondrial genome of V. cardui was sequenced and studied. Exploration of the organelle genomes database of the NCBI for complete mitogenome features revealed that the V. cardui mitogenome was among the midsize mitogenomes deposited for Lepidoptera, Nymphalidae, and Nymphalinae. To date of submission, the mitogenome of four Vanessa species has been studied, including V. indica, V. atalanta, V. cardui and V. tameamea, the order is from the smallest to the largest mitogenome size. Mitochondrial genomes contain a set of genes, including tRNAs, rRNAs, and proteins involved in the electron transport chain and ATP synthesis, that are conserved among animals [7, 35], and size variation in mitogenomes can indicate differences in the number and arrangement of these genes. The presence of gene duplications and repetitive sequences in large mitochondrial genomes can affect mutation rates and genomic stability [35]. The size of V. cardui mitogenome is among the average-sized mitogenomes in Lepidopteran insects, and it is among the most widespread species of butterflies that lives in all continents except Antarctica.

With a GC content of 19%, the V. cardui mitogenome had the lowest GC content among the Vanessa mitogenomes. It also had below-average GC content (19.35%) among the 40 Nymphalid butterflies studied, with GC content ranging from 18.4 to 20.5%. In general, higher GC content contributes to the higher thermal stability of the genome in prokaryotes due to the three hydrogen bonds between the nucleotide pairs [36]. Variations in GC content may indicate adaptations to different environmental conditions [37]. It is also proposed that higher GC content is a thermal adaptation of homeothermic animals [38]. Without conclusive evidence, assuming a similar role for GC content in butterflies is a mere speculation.

The concept of skewness discusses the amount of asymmetry in the distribution of any given dataset. The GC and AT skew describes the relative excess of nucleotides G over C and A over T in the genome, respectively, which shows a deviation from Chargaff’s second parity rule [39–41]. Negative AT and GC skews indicate a lower prevalence of A over T and G over C, respectively. In terms of AT and GC skew, the V. cardui mitogenome had skewness values of −0.022017905 and − 0.191724138, respectively. Also, the average AT and GC skew in the studied 41 Nymphalid taxa, excluding Lycaenid and Riodinid outgroups, were − 0.021130046 and − 0.21008569, respectively. Except for a few cases, these data indicate a consistent strand asymmetry in the studied Nymphalid butterfly mitogenomes, characterized by an excess of T over A and C over G on the reference strand (resulting in negative AT and GC skews).

The gene number and arrangement can give insights into evolutionary relationships among taxa [42, 43]. Changes in the number and order of these genes can reflect adaptation to a particular lifestyle or environment and may lead to speciation [43, 44]. The number and arrangement of the genes on the V. cardui mitogenome were the same as studied Nymphalid butterflies. Also, the arrangement of the 37 genes in Vanessa cardui and other examined butterflies was the same as the typical Lepidopteran arrangement [27].

With 7200 identified species, the Nymphalidae is the largest group of butterflies and a monophyletic group distributed around the world [45–48]. The relationships observed between the subfamilies on the complete mitogenome tree were similar to the earlier studies [46, 49] which emphasizes on the usefulness of the complete mitogenome and all 37 genes in the mitogenome for phylogenetic analysis. All of the trees demonstrated that the Nymphalinae is a monophyletic group with strong support, in line with previous studies [47, 50, 51], provided that Baeotus beotus from Coeini is excluded [52]. The Coeini is a controversial tribe, as it includes genera Baeotus and Historis that are not closely related to other genera in the tribe, making Coeini a polyphyletic group. They also do not appear to be within Nymphalinae either [52, 53]. The difference in the topology between the three phylogenetic trees and the occurrence of polytomy and weak grouping support in the trees based on the decreased datasets suggested that the complete mitochondrial genome with a bigger dataset preferably provides better resolution and grouping support. The basal branch in Nymphalid phylogenetic studies appears to be unstable and interchangeably occupied by Danainae [46, 49, 54] or Libytheinae [47, 55]. This is also the case in this study, and Danainae is the basal branch in the complete mitogenome dataset phylogeny, and Libytheinae is the basal branch in the Gblocks curated and PCG dataset phylogenies. The polytomy in the Gblocks curated dataset occurred within the Nymphalinae, where the grouping supports were also weak in the PCG tree. The relationship between the tribes of the subfamily Nymphalinae was provided with stronger support in the complete mitogenome tree, but the taxon sampling of the study is limited. The Nymphalini was a sister group to the rest of the tribes within the Nymphalinae in all trees, similar to previous studies [46, 52, 53, 55]. The four species of the genus Vanessa also grouped well with each other in all trees.

It has been suggested that the Nymphalid family diversified in the Late Cretaceous, around 90 million years ago, using mitochondrial, nuclear, and morphological data with a large taxon sampling [55]. The divergence timing using the complete mitochondrial data in this study suggested the divergence time of Nymphalidae in the late Cretaceous, around 70 million years ago. The difference in the timing might be due to the limited taxon sampling of this study, relying solely on the mitochondrial data, as well as a different time-calibration system.

Conclusions

Here I provided the first annotated mitogenome of the painted lady butterfly Vanessa cardui. Genus Vanessa contains 22 species, and only four of them have had their mitochondrial genomes sequenced. These butterflies included from the cosmopolitan species of V. cardui and a widely distributed V. atalanta on four continents, to the V. indica with a distribution limited to the eastern Palearctic and the V. tameamea with a local distribution in the Hawaiian Islands. No difference is seen in terms of the number and orientation of the genes on the mitochondrial genomes in the four species. In terms of GC content and the GC skew, V. cardui had the lowest GC content and skew among the other Vanessa species. The mitochondria are the powerhouse of the cells, and studying the mitogenomes of this small genus of butterflies with different distribution patterns may provide insights into the factors that contribute to the distribution of species.

Materials and methods

Database exploration and sample collection

The organelle genomes database of NCBI was explored for the number and composition of the complete mitogenomes deposited in the Phylum Arthropoda for butterflies and moths [14, 15]. Also, complete mitogenomes for 13 subfamilies of Nymphalidae, an outgroup from Lycaenidae, and an outgroup from Riodinidae were downloaded from GenBank. The Lycaenidae and Riodinidae are sister families of Nymphalidae [45].

Vanessa cardui specimen RA2022.1 was collected from Zanjan Province, Iran (Latitude 36°49’22” N, Longitude 47°21’43” E, and Altitude 2214 m) on July 23, 2022, using a sweep net. The sample was identified based on morphology and pinned, spread, and deposited in the Wallis Roughley Museum of Entomology, University of Manitoba (http://www.wallisroughley.ca/, Jason Gibbs, Jason.Gibbs@umanitoba.ca) with the voucher WRME0507746.

DNA extraction and library Preparation for next-generation sequencing

Genomic DNA extraction step: A leg was removed from the sample, and the specimen was pinned and spread. The DNA was extracted using a DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen, Dusseldorf, Germany) using standard protocol for animal tissue extraction with slight modifications as follows: The leg was ground using ceramic mortar and pestle with tissue lysis buffer (Buffer ATL), also to increase the final concentration, the DNA was suspended in 100 ul of TE (Tris EDTA) buffer [22].

DNA Fragmentation step: The DNA sample was fragmented by sonication with an S220 Focused-Ultrasonicator (Covaris, Woburn, Massachusetts, USA) to an average fragment size of 845 bp. Then the fragment sizes were analyzed using a High Sensitivity DNA chip for the Bioanalyzer 2100 electrophoresis system (Agilent, Santa Clara, California, USA) following the standard manufacturer’s protocol.

Adding next-generation sequencing and multiplexing adaptors: For multiplexing and preparing the library for next-generation sequencing, DNA multiplexing barcodes and sequencing indexes were added to the fragmented DNA samples using a NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, Massachusetts) for loading onto a NovaSeq6000 NextGen Sequencing Instrument (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA) [12, 56].

Mitogenome assembly and bioinformatics analysis

Geneious Prime 2021.1.1 [57] was used for quality trimming, assembly, and annotation of the SRA library of 68,301,479 paired 150 bp reads (GenBank SRR31052250) of V. cardui (GenBank PQ540662). The V. cardui reference mitogenome (GenBank: LR999956.2) was used for the assembly of V. cardui mitogenome. The assembled consensus sequence was double-checked for discrepancies, and base calls were edited based on the quality of reads and the reference sequence. Since the reference mitogenome did not have an annotation, a database with some closely related annotated mitogenomes from blast search results was made in Geneious Prime and used for annotation with a criterion of 90% or higher similarity of the sequences. The best match happened to be V. indica mitogenome annotation (GenBank: NC_038157) [58]. The annotated mitogenome was reversed and rotated so that it starts with tRNAMet and goes clockwise. The amino acid frequencies of the 13 proteins were calculated by Geneious Prime 2021.1.1. The graphical image of the mitogenome was made by the Proksee web application [59]. The overlapping and intergenic spacer regions were calculated based on gene coordinates in Table 1 by taking the end position of the first gene and the start position of the next gene into account and counting the nucleotides for distance/overlap. Skewness for AT and GC was calculated by the following formulas: AT skew = (A% − T%)/(A% + T%); GC skew = (G% − C%)/(G% + C%) [60]. The secondary structure of tRNAs was tested using the ARWEN web server [29]. Codon counts were provided by Geneious Prime, and the relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values for protein-coding genes were determined by taking the ratio of the observed frequency of codon usage to the expected frequency, assuming that all codons corresponding to the same amino acid were utilized with equal probabilities [61]. Codons with RSCU values higher than 1.6 and lower than 0.6 were considered overrepresented and underrepresented, respectively, and codons with RSCU values in between these numbers were considered unbiased or randomly used [62]. Mitogenomes were examined and compared for the number, arrangement, and orientation/position of genes using Geneious Prime.

Phylogenomic analysis of V. cardui

Representatives for 13 subfamilies of family Nymphalidae were selected for the analysis based on the published phylogenetic trees and classifications [45, 46, 63]. Three separate alignments were used to reconstruct three Bayesian phylogenetic trees, including the complete mitogenome alignment, the Gblocks [64–66] curated alignment, and the concatenated protein coding genes alignment. In the first approach, the complete mitochondrial genomes were used to reconstruct a phylogenetic tree [67–69]. The collected sequences were aligned using built-in ClustalW [70, 71] aligner in MEGA12 [72] with its default settings for gap opening and gap extension penalties. The best nucleotide substitution model based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC) [73] and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) [74] was determined to be a General Time Reversible model with a proportion of invariable sites and a gamma-shaped distribution of rate variation across sites (GTR + I + G) using MEGA12. For the model selection, all three options of dealing with the gaps/missing data (using all sites, partial deletion, and complete deletion) in MEGA12 resulted in the same GTR + I + G model. Two species from two sister families of Nymphalidae [26], Lycaena phlaeas from Lycaenidae and Dodona eugenes from Riodinidae, were selected as outgroups to root the tree and sort the lineages. Bayesian phylogenetic analysis was carried out using MrBayes v3.2.7 [75] in which two simultaneous runs were allowed for 10,000,000 generations with sampling every 1,000 generations. Large sequence insertions in a single species in the alignment were excluded from the analysis in the following positions (from-to): 361–389, 1374–1860, 7376–7393, 10,629–11,207, 14,450–14,513, 15,609–15,646, 16,248–16,275, 17,300–17,334 and 17,626–18,041 for a total of 1694 trimmed sites, decreasing the analyzed positions from 18,041 to 16,347 bps. The Bayesian analysis was continued until the average standard deviation of split frequencies became lower than 0.01 (The actual number after 10,000,000 generations was 0.000797). Then in the burnin process, the first 25% of the sampled trees (2,500,000) were discarded, and the remaining trees were used to make the consensus tree and posterior probability values. The convergence of parameters was assessed to be beyond the average standard deviation of split frequencies (Effective Sample Size >200) using Tracer v1.7.2 software package [76]. The resulting trees (cladogram and phylogram) were visualized using FigTree v1.4.5 [77].

In the second approach, the complete mitogenome alignment was curated with Gblocks 0.91b online server [64–66] to eliminate the divergent and poorly aligned regions of the alignment, decreasing the size of the dataset from 18,041 to 13,406 bps. The data were then realigned and processed for model selection as described above, and the model was determined to be GTR + I + G. The Bayesian phylogenetic analysis on the 13,406-bp alignment was carried out as described above for 10,000,000 generations.

In the third approach, the approximate position of the 13 protein-coding genes was determined by searching the V. cardui sequences on the alignment. The sequences for each gene were copied from the complete mitogenome alignment using the Bioedit [78] software package and pasted into the duplicate file with totally removed sequences, in the MEGA12 sequence alignment tool, and saved. The genes ND5, ND4, ND4L, and ND1 were copied as their reverse complements. Then the 13 files were double-checked for incomplete sequences and fixed, then realigned separately, and finally concatenated to make a single file containing all 13 protein-coding genes with a total size of 11,363 bps in MEGA12. The order of the genes in the alignment has been presented in the supplemental data Excel file. The total alignment and the alignments for each gene were then used separately for model selection in MEGA12. The model for the total alignment was determined to be GTR + I + G. The models for most of the genes were also determined to be GTR + I + G, except for ATP8 (TN93 + G + I), COII (TN93 + G + I), and ND4L (HKY + G + I). The total alignment in the nexus format was then partitioned based on the models for Bayesian phylogenetic analysis. The Bayesian phylogenetic analyses were then carried out using the partitioned 11,363-bp alignment as described above for 12,000,000 generations.

Divergence dating

The divergence dating analysis carried out in MEGA12 to estimate the divergence of the species studied in the project using the Bayesian phylogenetic tree from the complete mitogenome dataset [79] and two time-calibration points between Danaus Plexippus and Kallima inachus, as well as Kallima inachus and Kallima paralekta [80, 81]. The analytical procedure encompassed 43 nucleotide sequences with 18,041 positions in the final dataset.
