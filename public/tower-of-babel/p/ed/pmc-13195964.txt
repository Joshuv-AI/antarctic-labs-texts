Diabetes-related differences in body composition and their prognostic value among patients with heart failure and preserved ejection fraction: a cardiac-MRI-based study
Authors: Si-Yu Xiao, Yuan Li, Chen-Yan Min, Han Fang, Jia-Ke Li, Ke Shi, Zhi-Gang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart failure and diabetes mellitus share common risk factors and can independently regulate the body composition profile of patients. Approximately one-third of patients with heart failure and preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) have concomitant diabetes mellitus, but the additional impact of diabetes on the body composition of HFpEF patients and its associations with prognosis have not been explored.MethodsThis study included 281 HFpEF patients, among whom 87 had type 2 diabetes (T2DM). On the basis of their cardiac magnetic resonance (MR) images, regional adipose tissue (including subcutaneous, visceral and paracardial adipose tissue) as well as thoracic skeletal muscle were measured and compared between the diabetic and non-diabetic groups. Clinical endpoints were recorded and the predictive performance of body composition parameters and body mass index (BMI) for adverse events was assessed using multivariable Cox regression analysis in the overall HFpEF population and subgroups stratified by diabetes.ResultsHFpEF patients with concomitant diabetes were significantly older (65.06 ± 12.92 vs. 57.98 ± 14.44 years, p < 0.001). The LVEDV index and LVSV index were significantly lower in the T2DM group. The paracardial adipose tissue (ParaAT index) was noticeably greater in the T2DM group than in the non-T2DM group (76.21 vs. 65.60 ml/m2, p = 0.005). In the overall HFpEF population, the increase in the skeletal muscle index (SMI) corresponded to a linear decrease of prognostic risks. In contrast, the increase in the ParaAT-VAT ratio and ParaAT-muscle ratio corresponded to linearly increasing risks of poor outcomes. BMI demonstrated a non-linear correlation with survival risks (p for nonlinearity: 0.031) and the lowest tertile compared with the middle tertile increased survival risks significantly. Subgroup analysis revealed that the prognostic risks associated with the ParaAT-VAT ratio and ParaAT-muscle ratio were greater in diabetic HFpEF patients (adjusted HR: 1.069 [1.022, 1.117] and 1.948 [1.391, 2.729], respectively) than in nondiabetic patients (adjusted HR: 1.053 [1.006–1.103] and 1.597 [1.018, 2.505], respectively). Protective role from increased SMI persisted among the non-T2DM patients (adjusted HR: 0.968 [0.951, 0.985], p < 0.001) but not among the T2DM patients (adjusted HR: 0.990 [0.968, 1.012], p = 0.380). The lowest tertile group of BMI for non-T2DM patients or instead, the highest tertile group of BMI for T2DM patients led to an unfavorable prognosis.ConclusionsConcomitant diabetes exacerbated paracardial adipose excess among HFpEF patients and worsened the association between body composition and adverse outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03176-2.

Introduction

Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) accounts for approximately half of all heart failure cases, and its prevalence continues to increase [1]. Recent data from the Spanish DIABET-IC study showed a high burden of HF among outpatients with type 2 diabetes [2]. In addition, the more recent DIABET-IC analysis reported that HFpEF was the most frequent HF phenotype in patients with type 2 diabetes, and that outcomes were broadly comparable to HFrEF, despite HFpEF being more frequently under recognition [3]. Despite being heterogeneous diseases, diabetes and heart failure share common risk factors such as insulin resistance, systemic inflammation and neurohormonal activation.

Body composition abnormalities, as a specific manifestation of diabetes-related influence on HF patients, could in turn affect the progression of heart failure and further determine the clinical outcomes of HF patients. Skeletal muscle loss and visceral adipose tissue excess were validated to indicate an undesirable phenotype of HFpEF patients. Skeletal muscle, the site where the majority of insulin-stimulated glucose and fatty acid metabolism occurred, plays a predominant role in insulin resistance development and its loss is both a cause and a consequence of diabetes [4, 5]. For HF patients, severe loss of skeletal muscle (i.e., sarcopenia) has been widely validated to predict a poor prognosis [6–10]. A refined classification was reported recently by Navin Suthahar that stratified heart failure patients into groups with diverse prognoses using the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) combined with skeletal muscle mass [11]. Along with muscle wasting, some studies have reported that muscular function might also be impaired in the context of metabolic disturbance, followed by aggravated insulin resistance in a vicious cycle, exacerbating the limited exercise capacity and poor life quality in HF patients [4, 12]. Adiposity, the derangements of lipid metabolism associated closely with glucose utilization defects, played a crucial role in heart failure deterioration [13, 14]. Adipose abnormal deposition from other places caused visceral and ectopic (such as hepatic, perinephric and intra- or extrapericardial) adipose excess, hampering adipose tissue’s supportive function. Paracardial adipose tissue, for example, affected the myocardium directly or indirectly through paracrine and vascular secretion, myocardial malnutrition and coronary microvascular dysfunction, ultimately correlating with cardiovascular adverse events [15] .

To our knowledge, the additional effects of diabetes on the body composition of HFpEF patients and in turn, the prognosis of HFpEF patients have not been fully explored. Through critical insight into body composition analysis, the mechanism through which diabetes aggravates the progression of HFpEF is expected to be further elucidated, which is the aim of this study. We postulated that diabetes would induce a different body composition profile and modify the association between body composition and clinical outcomes in HFpEF patients.

Methods

Study population

The diagnostic criteria for HFpEF were based on the guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology (2021) [16]. Patients were enrolled between January 2022 and December 2024 with symptoms and/or signs of HF, elevated amino-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF > 50%). Patients who met any of the following criteria were excluded: (1) age younger than 18 years; (2) a history of decompensated HF within 3 months of enrollment; (3) acute coronary syndrome; (4) severe arrhythmia; (5) incomplete clinical information; or (6) loss to follow-up. Finally, a total of 281 patients were enrolled in this study. In accordance with the current guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology (2019) [17], 87 of the enrolled patients were diagnosed with diabetes mellitus and all of them had type 2 diabetes (T2DM).

Demographics, clinical characteristics, and laboratory measurements of these patients at baseline were collected from electronic clinical records. This study was approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committees of West China Hospital and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of this study. All medical data were protected with full confidentiality and used only for the purpose of the present study.

Cardiac MRI protocol

All patients underwent cardiac magnetic resonance imaging at the time of HF diagnosis on a 3-Tesla scanner (MAGNETOM Skyra/Tim Trio; Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) for the assessment of cardiac structure and function. The routine imaging protocol included an axial stack of steady-state free precession (SSFP).

images acquired with the following parameters: temporal resolution = 224.16 ms; echo time (TE) = 1.23 ms; slice thickness = 6.0 mm; flip angle (FA) = 60°; acquisition matrix = 126 × 256 pixels; and field of view (FOV) = 340 × 255 mm2. Cine imaging was performed using a balanced SSFP sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2.81 ms; TE = 1.22 ms; slice thickness = 8.0 mm; FA = 40°/50°; acquisition matrix = 166 × 208 pixels; and FOV = 340 × 284 mm2. For each breath-hold acquisition, 25 frames were reconstructed to generate cine images.

Cardiac function and remodeling analysis

All the images were analyzed using commercially available CVI42 software (Circle Cardiovascular Imaging, Inc., Calgary, Alberta, Canada). For LV volumetric analyses, endocardial and epicardial borders were traced semiautomatically at the LV end-diastolic and end-systolic phases on the short-axis stacks and manually corrected if needed. LV volumetric and functional parameters, including end-diastolic volume (LVEDV), end-systolic volume (LVESV), stroke volume (LVSV) and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) were automatically calculated with this software. LV papillary muscles were included in the LV mass (LVM) measurements but not in the LV volume measurements. Volumetric and mass measurements were then indexed by body surface area (BSA). Left ventricular remodeling, represented by the LV remodeling index (LVRI), was calculated as the ratio of the LVM to the LVEDV.

Body composition measurement

Paracardial adipose tissue (ParaAT) combines adipose tissue from two regions, epicardial adipose tissue (located between the myocardial surface and the visceral layer of the pericardium) and pericardial adipose tissue (located outside this layer). One experienced radiologist delineated ParaAT manually per slice from the most basal slice around the atria toward the most apical slice around the ventricles on end-diastolic short-axis images and subsequently ParaAT volume was calculated automatically by the summation of related volume per slice on CVI42 software (Fig. 1). Abdominal subcutaneous and visceral adipose tissue was measured using thickness instead of volume at the upper abdomen on mid-sagittal images because of the limited coverage of cardiac MR imaging [18, 19]. The maximal antero-posterior thickness anterior to the left lobe of the liver separated by the linea alba represented visceral adipose tissue (VAT) and subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), respectively.

Thoracic skeletal muscle was measured as the area (cm2) of the main muscle groups at the level of the carina using methods adopted in previous studies [19–21]. The main muscle groups included the bilateral pectoralis major, bilateral pectoralis minor, bilateral serratus anterior, bilateral periscapular, bilateral paraspinal and bilateral trapezius muscle. The borders of these muscle groups were traced manually to obtain the relevant areas, and the areas of all muscle groups were summed up eventually.

Body composition measurements were standardized by body surface area (BSA), thus producing the SAT index (mm/m2), VAT index (mm/m2), ParaAT index (ml/m2) and skeletal muscle index (SMI, cm2/m2). In addition, the ratios of paracardial adiposity to abdominal visceral adiposity and to skeletal muscle size were calculated and recorded as the ParaAT-VAT ratio (ParaAT divided by VAT, ml/mm) and the ParaAT-muscle ratio (ParaAT divided by skeletal muscle area, ml/cm2), respectively.

Intraobserver agreement

To evaluate the intraobserver reproducibility, 50 cases were selected randomly and analyzed one month after the initial body composition measurement by the same experienced radiologist. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were used to assess reproducibility, which ranged from 0.85 to 0.91 with good reproducibility (defined as ICCs > 0.80).

Follow-up and outcomes

Follow-up data were obtained from electronic medical records or standardized telephone interviews with patients or family members by a trained researcher utilizing a structured questionnaire when oral consent was obtained. To minimize possible bias, we limited the telephone visit period within a month and, whenever possible, verified reported clinical events against electronic medical records. Patients who could not be reached after three attempts on separate days were considered lost to follow-up. The composite endpoint included heart failure rehospitalization and all-cause mortality whichever occurred first. HF rehospitalization was defined as an unplanned hospitalization or an urgent hospital visit for worsening heart failure. The causes of death included cardiac and noncardiac causes. The follow-up duration was calculated as the interval between the initial cardiac MRI examination and either the occurrence of any endpoint or December 2025 (the end of follow-up).

Statistical analysis

Two software programs, SPSS (IBM SPSS Inc., Armonk, New York, USA) and R (R, version 3.6.3), were used for statistical analysis. The Shapiro‒Wilk test was used to determine the normality of the clinical, laboratory and radiological data. NT-proBNP was logarithmically converted and analyzed in a continuous manner. Continuous data were recorded as the mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range, IQR), and categorical data were recorded as the number (frequency). Unpaired t tests or Mann‒Whitney U tests for quantitative variables and chi‒square tests for categorical variables were used to evaluate the intergroup differences between HFpEF patients with and without diabetes. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was used to evaluate long-term adverse outcomes, and the log-rank test was used to compare the differences in survival between tertiles of body composition parameters. Uni- and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted to determine the associations of body composition parameters and body mass index (BMI) as either continuous or categorical variables before and after adjusting for potential confounding factors with adverse outcomes. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to eliminate the potential impact of medication use on prognosis. p value < 0.05 was used to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Diabetes-related baseline differences

This study enrolled 281 HFpEF patients, among whom 87 were diagnosed with diabetes. Compared with nondiabetic patients, the diabetic patients were significantly older (65.06 ± 12.92 years vs. 57.98 ± 14.44 years, p < 0.001) and had faster heart rates (74.9 ± 15.1 vs. 70.1 ± 13.9 beats/min, p = 0.018) (Table 1). The prevalence of hypertension (70.11% vs. 44.84%, p < 0.001), coronary heart disease (43.67% vs. 24.74%, p = 0.001) and chronic kidney disease (31.03% vs. 16.49%, p = 0.006) was significantly greater in the diabetic group. Laboratory examination revealed that the level of NT-proBNP in the diabetic group was higher (1178 (655, 3135) vs. 949 (453, 1826) pg/mL, p = 0.031) than that in nondiabetic patients. In addition to significantly elevated glycemic levels (fasting blood glucose: 6.52 (5.69, 7.68) vs. 4.96 (4.64, 5.35) mmol/L, p < 0.001; HbA1c: 6.81 ± 1.14% vs. 5.57 ± 0.36%, p < 0.001), the estimated glomerular filtration rate was also lower (68.58 (52.20, 89.99) vs. 82.57 (65.58, 92.67) mL/min/1.73 m2, p = 0.003) in diabetic patients. In terms of medications, calcium channel blockers (32.18% vs. 18.56%, p = 0.012), statins (55.17% vs. 32.99%, p < 0.001) and SGLT-2i (29.89% vs. 9.79%, p < 0.001) were more frequently used in diabetic patients.

Body composition and cardiac function parameters between patients with and without diabetes

There was no significant difference in the subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT index, 7.95 vs. 8.70 mm/m2, p = 0.409), visceral adipose tissue (VAT index, 5.70 vs. 5.75 mm/m2, p = 0.722) or skeletal muscle size (SMI, 49.94 vs. 47.82 cm2/m2, p = 0.201) between the T2DM and non-T2DM groups (Table 2). However, compared with the non-T2DM group, the T2DM group had significantly greater paracardial adipose tissue (ParaAT index, 65.60 vs. 76.21 ml/m2, p = 0.005), which was also the case in terms of paracardial adipose tissue relative to skeletal muscle size (ParaAT-muscle ratio, 1.19 vs. 1.48 ml/cm2, p = 0.008). Paracardial adipose tissue relative to visceral adipose tissue didn’t show a significant difference between the two groups (ParaAT-VAT ratio, 11.35 vs. 13.58 ml/mm, p = 0.111). The detailed muscle group measurements were demonstrated in Supplemental Table 1. The comparison of cardiac function and remodeling between the T2DM and non-T2DM group showed a significantly lower LVEDV index (81.44 ± 21.81 vs. 73.44 ± 18.43 mL/m2, p = 0.005) and lower LVSV index (49.60 ± 13.68 vs. 43.58 ± 11.83 mL/m2, p = 0.001) in the T2DM group (Supplemental Table 2).

Clinical outcomes predicted by body composition and BMI in the overall population

After a median follow-up period of 736 days, 93 patients reached the composite endpoint, among whom 70 patients experienced HF rehospitalization and 23 patients experienced all-cause mortality. The performance of six body composition parameters plus body mass index (BMI) for prediction of clinical outcomes was evaluated (Supplemental Table 3). The results showed that SMI (AUC: 0.68 [0.61, 0.75]), ParaAT-VAT ratio (AUC: 0.66 [0.58, 0.73]) and ParaAT-muscle ratio (AUC: 0.67 [0.60, 0.74]) displayed strong predictive values for composite endpoints. SMI (AUC: 0.66 [0.58, 0.74]) and ParaAT-muscle ratio (AUC: 0.66 [0.57, 0.74]) had the highest predictive values for HF rehospitalization, while ParaAT-VAT ratio (AUC: 0.71 [0.60, 0.82]) demonstrated the most reliable prediction for all-cause death. These parameters significantly improved prediction for adverse outcomes compared with BMI according to DeLong tests (Fig. 2).

Diabetes-related differences in the prognostic implication of body composition and BMI

Compared with in non-T2DM patients, positive association between ParaAT-muscle ratio and the risk of composite endpoint was more pronounced in T2DM patients (T2DM: adjusted HR = 1.948 [1.391–2.729], p < 0.001 versus non-T2DM: adjusted HR = 1.597 [1.018–2.505], p = 0.042), and the difference was also observed in terms of ParaAT-VAT ratio (T2DM: adjusted HR = 1.069 [1.022–1.117], p = 0.003 versus non-T2DM: adjusted HR = 1.053 [1.006–1.103], p = 0.027). The association between ParaAT-muscle ratio and HF rehospitalization (T2DM: adjusted HR = 1.874 [1.269–2.767], p = 0.002 versus non-T2DM: adjusted HR = 1.416 [0.863–2.325], p = 0.169) or between ParaAT-VAT ratio and all-cause death (T2DM: adjusted HR = 1.140 [1.058–1.228], p = 0.001 versus non-T2DM: adjusted HR = 0.031 [0.000–36.319], p = 0.336) differed evidently between the T2DM and non-T2DM patients as the corresponding associations became insignificant in non-T2DM patients (Fig. 5). The protective role of increased SMI for overall HFpEF population persisted among the non-T2DM patients against composite endpoints (adjusted HR: 0.968 [0.951–0.985], p < 0.001) and HF rehospitalization (adjusted HR: 0.966 [0.945–0.987], p = 0.001) but not among the T2DM patients (all p values > 0.05). BMI was obtained into subgroup analysis in their tertiles exclusively because of its nonlinear correlation with prognostic risks, and the results showed that for the non-T2DM patients, the lowest tertile group of BMI was still associated with increased risks of adverse events (adjusted HR: 2.157 [1.096–4.244], p = 0.026) while for the T2DM patients, the highest tertile group, instead, increased prognostic risks significantly (adjusted HR: 3.098 [1.156–8.302], p = 0.025) (Supplementary Table 5). Despite significantly lower LVEDV index and LVSV index at baseline MRI examination in the diabetic group, these two indices didn’t show significant prognostic values.

Discussion

In this study, regional adiposity, skeletal muscle mass and BMI were comprehensively compared between HFpEF patients with and without T2DM. Compared with the non-T2DM group, the T2DM group had significantly greater paracardial adiposity, with a similar profile of thoracic skeletal muscle mass, abdominal adiposity (visceral and subcutaneous) and relative deposition of visceral fat (evaluated by the ratio of paracardial to abdominal visceral fat, ParaAT-VAT ratio). Vital differences in the associations between these parameters and clinical prognosis induced by diabetes were discovered. For the overall HFpEF population, the decrease of SMI and the increase of ParaAT-VAT ratio as well as ParaAT-muscle ratio were linearly associated with the increase of prognostic risks. The increase of BMI, however, showed a nonlinear relationship with prognostic risks. The further subgroup analysis revealed that increased ParaAT-VAT ratio as well as ParaAT-muscle ratio brought about higher risks of adverse events in T2DM group than in non-T2DM group. The protective role of increased SMI against poor outcomes was applicable in non-T2DM patients but lost its efficacy in T2DM patients. The lowest tertile of BMI for non-T2DM patients or instead, the highest tertile of BMI for T2DM patients increased prognostic risks significantly.

Different effects of body composition on prognosis induced by diabetes

Consistent with previous findings concerning the impact of diabetes on fat accumulation, the results of this study revealed that compared with HFpEF patients without T2DM, the degree of paracardial adiposity was greater in those with T2DM [22–25], and the detrimental effect of paracardial fat on the prognosis of HFpEF patients was further increased by diabetes. In diabetes, metabolically activated paracardial adipose tissue secretes pro-inflammatory adipokines, driving coronary microvascular inflammation and myocardial fibrosis via paracrine pathways, exacerbating the effects of paracardial fat. Visceral fat excess was positively associated with the severity of muscle wasting among general population, and Zhou et al. [26] reported the association resulted to be more pronounced in the diabetic group after subgroup analysis, proving an improved ability of visceral fat to accelerate skeletal muscle wasting in the context of diabetes. In this study, a noticeable decrease of muscle mass wasn’t observed in diabetic patients, which we presumed to be attributed to the defects of muscle mass evaluation by area.Besides the muscle mass decrease, Liu et al. [27] reported muscular strength and physical capacity were also weakened in diabetic patients. Through comparing sarcopenia under different conditions, researchers reported that the pathological status of skeletal muscle associated with diabetes differed from muscle atrophy caused by aging or disuse, which was characterized by insulin resistance and the loss mainly of type I fibers [28]. This caused a shift of muscle fiber phenotype from slow-twitch to fast-twitch which further aggravated energy metabolism disturbance, insulin-mediated glucose utilization defects and ultimately, muscle weakness [29]. Supporting this inflammatory framework, a recent study by Mohammed et al. [30] demonstrated that the systemic immune-inflammation index independently predicted adverse outcomes in diabetic HFpEF patients but not in non-diabetic patients (p-interaction = 0.038). This finding, concordant with our results, suggests that diabetes-amplified systemic inflammation serves as a shared mechanistic substrate linking adverse body composition to worse prognosis. Under this inflammatory framework, protective effects of skeletal muscle might be inapplicable to diabetic patients through a mere increase in muscle size. To our knowledge, this is the first article to explore the differences in HFpEF patients’ body composition profiles according to diabetes status and the way diabetes modified the prognostic implications of body composition.

The obesity paradox differs according to diabetic status

Increased BMI demonstrated a unique reversed-J-shape increase of survival risks in this study. Risks of adverse events were increased significantly among lean (the lowest tertile group of BMI) patients rather than their obese (the highest tertile group of BMI) counterparts as the overall HFpEF population were concerned, which was consistent with the “obesity paradox” reported in cardiovascular events [18]. The inherent limits of BMI in discriminating between adipose and muscular tissue might depict a body composition profile with contrasting prognoses and management plans. The “obesity paradox”, however, after a subgroup analysis according to diabetes status, was shown to persist among nondiabetic patients but disappeared among diabetic patients. The modified effect of fat and muscles on adverse outcomes as reported explained the absence of “obesity paradox” in diabetic patients to some extent, as these patients were greatly harmed by visceral fat excess with unnoticeable protection from muscular mass preservation and thus, the prognosis of diabetic patients with obesity was poor.

Special managements in diabetic HFpEF patients

We proposed that the ParaAT-VAT ratio and ParaAT-muscle ratio enabled a more comprehensive depiction of the metabolic profile for HFpEF patients as it evaluated adiposity in different regions or compared two critical body components. The association within body composition and between body composition and clinical prognosis differed according to diabetes status and was proved to be more detrimental for diabetic patients. Acknowledgements of diabetes-associated differences in body composition and its implication thus helped in an earlier detection of patients at increased risk of adverse prognosis and in the development of targeted and effective therapeutic interventions in time. A recent study suggested that the origin of the cascade interaction associated with diabetes in heart failure patients was excessive visceral adiposity rather than insulin resistance and that the key to interference in these patients was good control of visceral adiposity [31]. Consistently, we also highlighted the importance of visceral adiposity management, which we proposed to be even greater than that of skeletal muscle improvement especially for diabetic patients. We postulated that measures to increase skeletal muscles be effective after diabetes-associated metabolic disturbance was quenched, which needs to be validated by future studies.

With the mechanism by which medications improved clinical outcomes through the change of body composition profile gradually elucidated, therapeutic implications of body composition may make a difference in HF management. Reports have shown that GLP-1 receptors are expressed in epicardial adipose tissue, and the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists catalyzed reductions in epicardial fat, suggesting a plausible mechanistic link with the adiposity-associated findings of this study [32, 33]. In parallel, semaglutide has demonstrated clinical benefit in obesity-related HFpEF, particularly through improvements in symptoms, physical limitations, exercise capacity, and weight loss [34]. However, these benefits were mainly functional without clear evidence of mortality reduction and thus shouldn’t be overinterpreted. Meanwhile, given the prognostic relevance of skeletal muscle observed in this study, concerns about the untargeted loss of skeletal muscle and adipose tissue under weight-loss therapies were raised. Recent data suggested that part of the weight reduction associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists may involve lean mass loss, a condition characterized by sarcopenia and reduced exercise capacity and of particular relevance in HFpEF population [35]. The balance between reducing pathological adiposity and preserving skeletal muscle mass and function may serve as a key to treatment efficacy.

Limitations

Our study had several limitations. First, this was a retrospective study with inherent selection bias. Second, ParaAT consists of two different components, epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) and pericardial adipose tissue (PAT), which had been reported to have various effects on the prognosis of HFpEF patients but were not discussed separately in this study because of the difficulties in differentiation between these two components as reported [19]. In addition, the increase of per unit (ml/mm) might constrain the interpretability of ParaAT-VAT ratio. This was supposed to be resolved through an identical measurement of visceral fat by volume instead of thickness, which could better present the relative distribution of paracardial fat to visceral fat. Third, the quantification of skeletal muscle was not adequate for evaluating patients’ muscular health. The implementation of other factors, such as fat infiltration [4] and muscle strength and performance [36], is expected to contribute to a more comprehensive assessment. Finally, interaction analysis wasn’t practical based on the present sample size [37], and results from the subgroup analysis for all-cause death were exploratory due to the relatively small number of occurrence. These above necessitated validation by a future study with a substantially large sample size.

Conclusion

Concomitant diabetes exacerbated paracardial adipose excess in HFpEF patients and increased its detrimental effect on clinical outcomes. It also impaired the protective effect of skeletal muscle increase on HFpEF patients’ prognosis. Management plans targeted at body composition should be made according to diabetes status for better efficacy.
