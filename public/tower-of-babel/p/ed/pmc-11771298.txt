Driving into Diabetes: Risk Assessment among Interstate Truck Drivers in West Bengal
Authors: Aditi A. Jaiswal, Bobby Paul, Lina Bandyopadhyay
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Diabetes mellitus has led to a huge increase in its number over recent decades. Due to their occupation, truck drivers are predisposed to higher risk of lifestyle disorders. Hence, this study intended to assess their risk of developing diabetes mellitus with respect to IDRS (Indian Diabetes Risk Score). A cross-sectional study was conducted in a selected logistic company for 3 months, where 160 interstate truck drivers were selected by simple random sampling. The collected data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics using SPSS 16 version. The mean age of study participants was 38.4 ± 10.7 years. Of the total, 49.5% of the participants had a high IDRS. A significant increase in IDRS was associated with the occupational variables. Strict monitoring of government guidelines on fair working hours for truck drivers is required. They, being a high-risk group due to their occupation, require periodic health checkups, and appropriate measures should be taken to minimize the detrimental effects on their health.

INTRODUCTION

Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), which affect people worldwide, are a combined result of genetic, physiological, environmental, and behavioral factors. The World Health Organization (WHO) states that it kills 41 million people each year, which is equivalent to 71% of all deaths globally.[1] Out of the total deaths caused by it, 77% are in low- and middle-income countries.[1] Cardiovascular diseases, cancers, chronic respiratory diseases, and diabetes are some of the main types of NCDs.

A recent article published in 2019 on the diabetes scenario reported India to be the second largest country harbouring diabetics next to China.[2] As diabetes has an arena of health implications, its screening would help in lowering the burden considerably.

The natural professional activity of drivers makes them fall under a higher risk of obesity, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and carbohydrate metabolism disorders such as diabetes mellitus,[3] for which they do not tend to seek medical advice, due to their ever changing location. Thus, being a high-risk group for NCDs, they need to be frequently evaluated for the risk factors pertaining to the disease.

Though studies on truck drivers with respect to diabetes have been barely done in India, a study on morbidity profiles of long-distance truck drivers in Hyderabad city showed that 30.51% had a high-risk body mass index, which is a known contributing factor for diabetes.[4] Therefore, research in this area needs to be escalated as these individuals often go unscreened due to their occupation and might be forming a great chunk in the diabetes burden of the country. The Indian Diabetes Risk Score (IDRS) tool, which screens the population for diabetes, was developed using four simple parameters, namely, age, abdominal obesity, family history of diabetes, and physical activity. It has a sensitivity of 72.5% and a specificity of 60.1% and is derived based on the largest population-based study on diabetes in India CURES (Chennai Urban Rural Epidemiology Study).[5] Its main advantages are that it is highly cost effective, simple and can be easily used for mass screening. For a resource-poor country like India, it can prove to be of considerable benefit.

Hence, this study tends to evaluate the inertstate truck drivers on their risk of developing diabetes mellitus, with respect to IDRS.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A cross-sectional study was conducted among interstate truck drivers of West Bengal, working under a designated logistic company, operating from North 24 parganas, West Bengal, in a span of 3 months (September 2021 to February 2022). Truck drivers who were a known case of diabetes mellitus were excluded from the study.

Sampling

The proportion of people at high risk of diabetes was 36.55% in a study on assessment of risk of type 2 diabetes using the IDRS in an urban slum of Pune, Maharashtra, conducted in 2016 by Patil RS et al.[3] With that, considering P = 0.37 and an absolute error of precision of 7.5%; the minimum sample size estimated using Chocran’s formula was 159.19 ⁓ 160.

A list of 620 interstate truck drivers was obtained from the logistic company, out of which 160 were selected by simple random sampling (without replacement) through random number generation. On an average, seven of them were interviewed each day.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed by Microsoft Excel 2019 and SPSS software (16 versions). Descriptive statistics was used to report the dependent variable. The differences in variables by classification of IDRS (low risk/medium risk/high risk) were analyzed with Jonckheere-Terpstra test for occupational variables and measured parameters.

Ethical approval

The Institutional Ethics Committee approved the study protocol. The designated authority of the logistic company was informed about the study in details, and due written permission was obtained. Subject information sheets in local language and written informed consent forms were provided to each of the participants. Anonymity was maintained throughout the study in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

RESULTS

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants

The mean age of the study participants was 38.4 ± 10.7 years, and 55 (34.4%) of them belonged to socioeconomic class 3 according to BG Prasad Scale 2021.

IDRS

Out of the total, 79 (49.5%), 70 (43.7%), and 11 (6.8%) participants had high-risk, medium-risk, and low-risk IDRS, respectively.

Occupational variables

The mean years spent driving was 15.8 ± 9.2 years. The majority of the participants, that is, 140 (87.5%), were driving above the permissible limit of 48 hours per week. The mean hours of break taken while driving was 1.4 ± 0.9 hours. In the last month, most of the participants, that is, 71 (44.4%), spent less than 6 hours on sleeping and a total 30 (18.8%) participants had been having night shifts of more than 48 hours per week in the last month. DISCUSSION

The burden of NCDs has been haunting mankind since the millennial era, with diabetes mellitus being one of them.[9] A number of studies conducted internationally have shown alcohol, older age, dyslipidemia, higher body mass index, and larger waist circumference to be major contributing factors in the risk of developing diabetes;[1011] similar were the findings of our study where amount of alcohol consumed, BMI, SBP, and DBP were significantly associated with the high risk score of IDRS.

In context to truck drivers, studies in India showed 38.46% of them to be prediabetic and diabetic;[12] congruent to that, our study showed 93.2% of the participants with a medium and high-risk score of IDRS, thus demanding accelerated research in this arena, with making occupation the cornerstone for its conception.

Statistically significant association between the occupational variables and IDRS score was noted. It was seen that 87.5% of the participants were driving beyond 48 hours per week and most of them spent less than 6 hours on sleeping. It was seen that more than half of the participants had been taking breaks of only 1 hour or less while driving for a trip. They had also been subjugated to long hours of night shifts, which could be playing a major role in increasing the risk of diabetes as we can see from the study itself, where the risk score of IDRS has shown an increasing trend with an increase in the hours of night shifts. An article titled “Driver shortage for trucking industry – an India perspective” published in 2018[13] pointed toward the abnormally high working hours of truck drivers in India. It stated that about half (48%) of the drivers on an average undertook more than 12 trips a month, with the average duration of a trip for 50% of the drivers being more than 12 hours. It also highlighted that 23% of the truck drivers in the study battled lack of sleep, about 18% of them faced physical stress, and 12% faced mental stress. Identical findings were noted in our study with respect to occupational variables as discussed above, and a majority of the participants scored higher on the PSS4 scale.

The measured parameters of BMI, SBP, and DBP also showed statistically significant association with IDRS scores, thus indicating the vicious chain of noncommunicable diseases.

As deciphered from the study, truck drivers face an array of issues due to their poor working condition and this study has highlighted the same. Health of truck drivers, which otherwise remains ignored because of their constant displacement, needs to be brought into light as most of the contributing risk factors to diabetes can be dealt with if their working conditions are addressed.

CONCLUSION

The occupational variables proved to be a contributing factor toward the increased risk of developing diabetes as almost half of the participants had a high risk of IDRS. A co-ordinated approach toward screening and raising awareness among the employers and employees can prove to be beneficial in limiting the disease. Along with this, stringent monitoring of government guidelines on the working hours of truck drivers and organized health screening programs can help in preventing the disease.

Limitations of the study

Since one logistic company was chosen due to feasibility, generalizability might be an issue.
