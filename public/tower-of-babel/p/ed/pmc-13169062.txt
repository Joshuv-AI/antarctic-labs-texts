Sleep-disordered breathing in children with neurodisabilities
Authors: Kristien Vanhaverbeke, Merve Selçuk, Refika Ersu, Hui-Leng Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The prominence of sleep-disordered breathing (SDB) in children with neurodisabilities is increasingly becoming apparent. Patients can manifest not only obstructive sleep apnoea but also central sleep apnoea, nocturnal hypoxaemia and hypoventilation or often a combination thereof. The impact of SDB on quality-of-life measures has been increasingly demonstrated. This in turn is powering a paradigm shift, where investigation of SDB and subsequent treatment with surgical procedures or respiratory interventions, such as noninvasive ventilation, are increasingly considered in this cohort of patients, whereas historically they may not have been, given perceived barriers to successful treatment. In this review, we aim to summarise the literature on SDB in children with neurodisabilities published in the past two decades. We have concentrated on three main groups of children with neurodisabilities, namely those with cerebral palsy, those with inborn errors of metabolism where disease-modifying therapies are available for selected conditions and those with other genetic conditions resulting in significant neurodisability, such as Rett syndrome.

Introduction

Neurodisability is defined as a group of congenital or acquired long-term conditions attributed to impairment of the brain and/or neuromuscular system, characterised by functional limitations and associated support needs rather than by a single diagnostic entity [1].

Sleep-disordered breathing (SDB) represents a spectrum of conditions characterised by abnormal respiration during sleep and includes obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), central sleep apnoea (CSA), sleep-related hypoventilation and nocturnal hypoxaemia, which may coexist in the same patient [2]. The elevated risk of SDB in children with neurodisabilities has become increasingly apparent. There are several factors in play, encompassing upper airway instability, impairment of central respiratory drive, abnormal facial or upper airway anatomy, laryngeal dystonia and increased respiratory load, the latter seen in scoliosis, aspiration or recurrent chest infections. These mechanisms may be further compounded by comorbid epilepsy and the use of commonly prescribed medications, including benzodiazepines, muscle relaxants and antiseizure drugs, which may alter arousal thresholds and ventilatory control. Epilepsy-related brainstem dysfunction and sedative or gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-mediated medications have been shown to blunt ventilatory responses to hypoxia and hypercapnia, thereby increasing vulnerability to both obstructive and central respiratory events during sleep [3–5].

Often, factors combine, with the impact of this interplay dependent on the underlying condition and the individual child. Importantly, children with neurodisabilities may present with atypical or subtle features of SDB; classical symptoms such as snoring or witnessed apnoeas are not always evident and presentation may be limited to nonspecific manifestations such as behavioural changes, daytime irritability or fragmented sleep.

Untreated SDB in this population is associated with significant adverse consequences, including impaired neurocognitive functioning, behavioural dysregulation, reduced quality of life, increased caregiver burden, recurrent hospitalisations and, in severe cases, progression to chronic respiratory failure or pulmonary hypertension. Early identification and treatment of SDB therefore represents a potentially modifiable factor with important implications for both short- and long-term outcomes in children with neurodisabilities.

The underlying aetiologies of neurodisability are legion. In this review, we focus on three main groups, as follows: children with cerebral palsy (CP), those with inborn errors of metabolism and those with other genetic disorders. For each group, the literature on SDB is summarised with particular reference to possible presentations, impact on quality of life, treatment options and impact of treatment.

Materials and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included articles that investigated the prevalence, incidence and treatment of SDB in children with neurodisabilities. For this review, we focused on CP, inborn errors of metabolism and other genetic disorders that cause neurodisabilities. We did not include neurodisabilities such as narcolepsy, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, autism, neuromuscular diseases (unless in the context of genetic neurodisability with neuromuscular weakness), trisomy 21, Prader–Willi syndrome and congenital central hypoventilation syndrome as their pathophysiology and complications with regards to SDB are very distinct and have been described elsewhere in the literature.

We included original research articles, case series and reviews written in English. We defined “paediatric” as 0–18 years old for this review. We limited the search to papers with a publication date from January 2008 until April 2025.

Search strategy

Three different searches were conducted to reflect the three patient groups described above. All searches were conducted via MeSH (medical subject heading) terms in PubMed in March and April 2025. For each search, we filtered the results to only include papers in English and including patients younger than 18 years old.

With regards to CP, we used the keywords “sleep disordered breathing” and “cerebral palsy”. For metabolic diseases, we searched via the keywords “sleep disordered breathing” and “metabolic disease”. Similarly, “sleep disordered breathing” and “genetic condition” were searched for the third patient group. For each search, the respective MeSH terms were combined via the “AND” function in PubMed to obtain all publications combining the term “sleep disordered breathing” and the respective disease group.

Study selection

Search results were centralised and reviewed per group (i.e. CP, inborn errors of metabolism and genetic disorders associated with neurodisability). Relevant studies were selected in three stages. First, duplicates were removed. Then, publications were screened by title and abstract. Finally, the full texts of the remaining studies were screened. If there was doubt about the eligibility of a publication for inclusion, a second reviewer was involved.

Data extraction

We extracted data focusing on the following parameters: 1) investigated disease(s) and control groups (if any), 2) aim of the study, 3) study design, 4) inclusion and exclusion criteria, 5) assessed outcome parameters, and 6) main results. Given the heterogeneity of the study methods, a statistical meta-analysis was not possible.

Results

Study selection

We conducted a systematic literature search in PubMed. In total, 32 records were retrieved for the search regarding SDB in CP, 64 records for inborn errors of metabolism and SDB, and 118 records for genetic conditions associated with neurodisability (figure 1). After screening of title, abstract and full text, a total of 67 articles were included in the current review (13 articles on SDB in CP, 23 on SDB in metabolic diseases and 30 on SDB in genetic conditions). The remaining 147 records were excluded because 1) the study did not include the target population (n=44), 2) the study did not meet the scope of the review iterated above in the aims (n=42) or 3) the study was published before 2007 (n=60).

Cerebral palsy

Several authors investigated the prevalence of SDB in CP and the value of systematic screening for SDB in this population using standardised questionnaires. Koyuncu et al. [6] used the sleep-related breathing disorders (SRBD) scale of the Paediatric Sleep Questionnaire (PSQ) to investigate the prevalence of SDB and found that there was a prevalence of 18.1% in the CP group versus 7.4% in the control group of typically developing children (p=0.029). In this cohort, there were no significant correlations between SDB, type of CP, motor dysfunction levels, epilepsy and spasticity levels. Garcia et al. [7] also found an increased prevalence of SDB based on PSQ scores in children with CP with or without comorbid epilepsy compared to a control group of children with non-CP neurodisabilities. Using the PSQ questionnaire allowed identification of SDB in 44% of the study cohort, compared to only 8% with a standard clinical assessment. In this cohort, there was a significant positive correlation between PSQ scores and Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) scores.

In a study in which questionnaire performance was evaluated against an objective reference standard, Kalyoncu et al. [8] evaluated the correlation between the PSQ and full polysomnography (PSG) and assessed the sensitivity and specificity of PSQ in children with underlying conditions presenting with symptoms of SDB, including 80 children with CP, as well as patients with genetic and metabolic diseases and neuromuscular disorders. When compared with PSG, this revealed that in the CP group, the PSQ had a sensitivity of 88.8%, specificity of 30.7%, a positive predictive value of 22.8% and negative predictive value of 92.3% (with the low specificity likely due to the heterogeneity of the study group). Furthermore, the apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI) was significantly higher in the children with genetic and metabolic diseases compared to children with neuromuscular diseases (median AHI 5.95 events·h–1 versus 1.2 events·h–1, p>0.001). Grychtol et al. [9] described a prevalence of sleep disturbances in children with CP of 80% compared to 20–40% in the general population and found that, in their cohort, SDB is present in 10–14% of patients with CP. Sandella et al. [10] assessed whether the presence of sleep problems is associated with impaired quality of life in children with CP, using the parent-proxy Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) 4.0 Generic Core scales. They found that patients with CP had significantly higher SRBD scores and lower quality of life (particularly in the physical and psychosocial domains of the PedsQL score. In addition, they showed that psychosocial PedsQL scores could be predicted in a multiple regression model including excessive daytime sleepiness, insomnia and snoring. A model including the GMFCS, excessive daytime sleepiness, insomnia and snoring was also significant. Insomnia, excessive daytime sleepiness and functional motor abilities were associated with lower quality of life in children with CP.

Inborn errors of metabolism

Mucopolysaccharidosis

The high prevalence of OSA in mucopolysaccharidosis (MPS) is thought to reflect a combination of progressive glycosaminoglycan deposition in the upper airway, macroglossia, adenotonsillar hypertrophy, midface hypoplasia and skeletal abnormalities, all of which contribute to airway narrowing and collapsibility. Several metabolic conditions associated with neurodisability have been studied in the literature. MPS was the most extensively studied inborn error of metabolism in terms of SDB (n=12) [11–21], followed by Pompe disease (n=3) [22–24], mitochondrial diseases (n=3) [25–27], mucolipidosis (n=2) [28, 29], pycnodysostosis (n=2) [30, 31] and Leigh syndrome (n=1) [32].

Several authors have aimed to characterise SDB in cohorts of treatment-naïve children with MPS and children with MPS who received enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) or haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). The main results are summarised in table 2. In a cohort of treatment-naïve MPS patients, Nashed et al. [21] found evidence of OSA in 64%, of whom 87% had moderate or severe OSA despite previous adenotonsillectomy in over half of them. The incidence of OSA was highest in the MPS type 1 patients. Similar results were found by John et al. [20] in patients with MPS type 6, with very common clinical signs of SDB, macroglossia and pectus carinatum, with a prevalence of at least mild OSA of 85%, and 52% of the cohort showing severe OSA. Interestingly, there was no correlation between clinical signs of SDB, urinary glucosaminoglycan levels, anaerobic sulfite reductase subunit B (ASRB) enzyme activity and PSG findings. Although SDB in MPS is most commonly characterised by obstructive events, there have been cases of combined central and obstructive OSA, e.g. in the case described by Solaiman et al. [15], where an infant with MPS type 1 had developed severe SDB with an overall AHI of 72 events·h–1 (of which central AHI 42 events·h–1, obstructive AHI 24 events·h–1 and mixed AHI 1 event·h–1) secondary to hydrocephalus; which resolved after insertion of a ventriculoperitoneal shunt.

Data on SDB in children with MPS who received treatment (ERT or HSCT) are also emerging. Despite treatment, rates of SDB remain high in these cohorts, ranging from 68 to 86% [12, 17]. Moreau et al. [18] described a cohort of patients who underwent HSCT in infancy with a follow-up into adolescence. They showed a linear correlation between the time elapsed since HSCT and the AHI, with an AHI significantly higher in patients older than 10 years old; but they did not find a significant correlation between AHI and enzymatic activity. Pal et al. [17] conducted a similar cohort study in children with MPS type 1, showing significant SDB in 68% of their cohort, but they did find a significant correlation between metabolic correction (expressed as the dermatan sulfate:chondroitin sulfate (DS:CS) ratio) and clinical regression of airway disease following treatment. Furthermore, patients with significant levels of inhibitory antibodies showed significantly elevated SDB over ERT-treated patients without inhibitory antibodies. Similarly, Koehne et al. [13] showed that MPS type 1 patients who underwent HSCT and had lower donor chimerism (with high chimerism being a measure of successful HSCT) presented with more severe OSA. As expected, they also found that MPS type 1 patients without OSA had less coarse facial features and less pronounced midface hypoplasia. Finally, Ademhan Tural et al. [12] studied MPS type 4 and 6 patients who received ERT and found a similar prevalence of SDB in MPS type 4 and 6, with a sleep apnoea prevalence of 86%. From a parental point of view, SDB in MPS is also a major cause for concern [16]. Taken together, these studies provide evidence for a high prevalence of SDB in MPS, with or without HSCT or ERT, and support standardised screening for SDB in children with MPS using routine sleep studies. The use of home sleep studies as a screening tool for SDB has also been proposed, but it has a high technical failure rate of 32%. In the technically successful studies, a high rate of OSA was shown (90% of patients had a degree of OSA; 26% had mild OSA, 10% had moderate OSA, 58% had severe OSA) [19].

Pompe disease

We identified two studies that described the prevalence of SDB in infantile Pompe disease [22, 23]. A retrospective cohort study including 17 patients with infantile Pompe who underwent PSG at a mean age of 8 months old, showed that six patients had mild OSA and one had severe OSA [23]. Six patients had nocturnal hypoventilation (defined as spending >25% of study time with transcutaneous carbon dioxide tension >50 mmHg), which was, as expected, more pronounced in children who received oxygen as part of their chronic treatment. After treatment with ERT, there was a tendency towards improvement in AHI, although the results were not statistically significant [22].

Nabatame et al. [24] described a cohort of children with childhood-onset Pompe disease and found that all conducted PSGs were abnormal with AHIs ranging from 6.7 events·h–1 to 25.9 events·h–1. Interestingly, they noted a significant correlation between AHI and drop in vital capacity. After noninvasive ventilation (NIV) initiation, SDB symptoms resolved and PSG normalised. As for MPS, the evidence suggests that routine screening for SDB via PSGs is warranted in Pompe disease.

Mitochondrial deficiencies

Mitochondrial deficiencies have scarcely been described in the literature [25–27]. Available evidence shows that OSA is common, despite previous adenotonsillectomy in some cases [26, 27]. Mosquera et al. [27] also described hypoxaemia and nocturnal hypoventilation in 40% and 20% of their study cohort respectively. Abnormal muscle tone was a common clinical feature and Barcia et al. [25] further assessed this by measuring oesogastric and transdiaphragmatic pressures, allowing a quantitative assessment of respiratory muscle strength. All three patients in their cohort showed signs of diaphragmatic dysfunction.

A retrospective study compared patients with Leigh syndrome to matched controls with OSA [32]. This study revealed OSA in four of six patients and CSA (defined as central AHI ≥5 events·h–1) in three of six patients with Leigh syndrome. Sleep stage distribution was similar between the patients and matched OSA controls, but children with Leigh syndrome had lower mean total sleep time and sleep efficiency.

Other genetic conditions associated with neurodisability

Trisomy 13 and 18

Several studies have highlighted the occurrence of SDB in this population. In a chart review of 66 children (47 with trisomy 18 and 19 with trisomy 13), Kettler et al. [33] identified symptoms suggestive of SDB in 21 of 47 (44.7%) and 10 of 19 (52.6%) patients, respectively. Only seven children (10%) successfully underwent diagnostic PSG (six with trisomy 18 and one with trisomy 13). Among those studied, the mean±sd AHI was 3.82±3.46 events·h–1 in trisomy 18 and 0.15 events·h–1 in trisomy 13, with mean oxygen nadirs of 87% and 82%, respectively. The average peak end-tidal carbon dioxide tension (PETCO2) was 48.6 mmHg in children with trisomy 18 and 43 mmHg in children with trisomy 13, indicating mild obstructive events with limited nocturnal hypoventilation. Reported clinical findings included micrognathia, midface hypoplasia, small oral cavity, hypotonia, gastro-oesophageal reflux and laryngomalacia.

In a retrospective cohort of 47 children (18 with trisomy 13 and 29 with trisomy 18), Benson et al. [34] reported that OSA was confirmed by PSG in 48% of patients with trisomy 18 and 11% with trisomy 13. The most frequent otolaryngologic procedures included adenotonsillectomy in 23% of cases (11% in trisomy 13, 31% in trisomy 18) and tracheostomy in 9%, though not all were directly related to SDB. Ear, nose and throat (ENT) comorbidities such as gastro-oesophageal reflux, airway malacia and hearing loss were also common but not necessarily specific to SDB.

Goff and Soares [35] described severe CSA in a child with trisomy 13, diagnosed clinically rather than by PSG. Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed corpus callosum hypoplasia, cerebellar and vermian dysplasia, and enlarged cisterna magna, suggesting a potential neuroanatomical substrate for central respiratory dysregulation.

22q11.2 deletion/duplication

Across seven studies, SDB was consistently reported as a frequent comorbidity in children with 22q11.2 deletion or duplication syndromes. Ingram et al. [36] analysed 89 PSG studies from 60 children and found a high overall prevalence of SDB (76%), including 25.8% with moderate to severe OSA, 5.6% with clinically significant CSA (central apnoea index (CAI) >5 events·h–1) and 7.9% with nocturnal hypoventilation (PETCO2 >50 mmHg for >25% total sleep time (TST)). Sleep-related hypoxaemia (peripheral oxygen saturation <90% for >1% TST) occurred in 25.8% of patients. There were no significant differences between deletion and duplication groups regarding OSA, CSA, hypoventilation or hypoxaemia severity. Notably, parent-reported symptoms did not correlate with PSG-confirmed SDB, underscoring the need for objective assessment [36]. Arganbright et al. [37] further showed that 97% of children with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome had clinically significant sleep problems on the Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire, yet only 30% had ever undergone a sleep study and 20% had an OSA diagnosis.

In 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, upper airway anomalies such as velopharyngeal insufficiency (VPI), palatal hypotonia and craniofacial dysmorphism often require surgical correction to improve speech resonance rather than to treat airway obstruction. However, these procedures, including pharyngeal flap and velopharyngeal reconstruction, can secondarily impact airway patency and increase the risk of postoperative OSA even in nonsyndromic children [38]. Kennedy et al. [39] reported persistent OSA in 44–53% of patients after adenotonsillectomy or VPI repair, while Arganbright et al. [40] similarly found residual OSA in 69% of children following tonsillectomy. Filip et al. [41] observed postoperative OSA in one of 12 patients (8%) after superiorly based pharyngeal flap surgery, indicating a low but present risk, warranting vigilance in symptomatic cases.

Rett syndrome and MECP2-related disorders

In Rett syndrome, central apnoeas and desaturations typically occur during wakefulness. PSG is useful to distinguish pathological SDB from wake-related events and to guide appropriate clinical diagnosis and management. In a PSG-based study by Amaddeo et al. [42] involving 17 children with Rett syndrome, OSA was identified in 23.5% and severe CSA in 11.8%, with CAI up to 132 events·h–1. Central apnoeas frequently occurred both during sleep and wakefulness, often following hyperventilation episodes, a hallmark of brainstem dysregulation [43, 44].

Hagebeuk et al. [44] evaluated four children with CDKL5-related atypical Rett syndrome, central apnoeas during wakefulness (28–41 events·h–1) were observed in half of the cohort, but the highest central AHI in sleep was only 3.4 events·h–1. Similarly, Wagner et al. [45] described prolonged central apnoeas lasting up to 72 s during wakefulness in a child with Rett syndrome, despite a near-normal sleep AHI.

MECP2 (methyl-CpG-binding protein 2) duplication syndrome is a rare X-linked neurodevelopmental disorder that primarily affects male infants and is characterised by infantile hypotonia, epilepsy and recurrent respiratory infections. Children with MECP2 duplication syndrome are at high risk for SDB, including severe OSA and hypoventilation [46, 47]. Multimodal treatment (continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), NIV, surgery) can improve respiratory outcomes, but ongoing susceptibility to infections necessitates a multidisciplinary approach.

Findings for SDB in CHARGE (coloboma, heart disease, atresia of the choanae, retarded growth and mental development, genital anomalies, and ear malformations and hearing loss) syndrome and other rare genetic conditions are discussed in the supplementary material.

Treatment of SDB in children with neurodisability

Multiple overarching treatment strategies for SDB in children with neurodisability have been described and mainly consist of surgical approaches (such as adenotonsillectomy, but also multilevel surgical approaches and in extremis tracheostomy), the use of NIV via CPAP or bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP), combinations of both, or for some diseases, disease-modifying treatments.

Cerebral palsy

For CP, as reviewed by Grychtol et al. [9], there is significant evidence to support and target surgical treatment. Adenotonsillectomy seems to significantly improve obstructive AHI (from 12.4 events·h–1 preoperatively to 3.1 events·h–1 postoperatively) as well as quality of life, although the treatment group in the latter study comprised both surgical and nonsurgical treatment strategies via NIV [48, 49]. There is a higher risk of surgical complications, with frequent need for postoperative monitoring in an intensive care setting: Tweedie et al. [50] showed that CP patients accounted for 17% of unplanned paediatric intensive care unit admissions, despite only representing 3% of the study population. Consistently, a recent systematic review by End et al. [51] reported frequent postoperative respiratory complications, prolonged hospital stays and increased unplanned intensive care unit admission following adenotonsillectomy in children with CP, despite improvements in OSA severity. In addition, a contemporary cohort study demonstrated that although obstructive AHI improved in all children with CP after adenotonsillectomy, postoperative respiratory complications were common, particularly in those with severe motor and swallowing impairment [52].

It has been suggested in the literature that more extensive surgery including a multilevel approach after initial adenotonsillectomy is more likely to be needed in children with a complex background, such as CP, but this also applies to metabolic and genetic diseases that are often characterised by changes on multiple levels in the (upper) airways. Hartzell et al. [53] investigated the value of tongue base suspension surgery in comparison to other surgical measures for OSA in CP and found significant improvements in AHI and arousal index in both groups after surgery, but there was no difference between the two surgical groups in terms of AHI reduction or complications. Supraglottoplasty for CP was investigated by Durvasula et al. [54] in a cohort of neurologically affected and syndromic children, including 11 CP patients. They showed that the success rate of supraglottoplasty in CP was significantly lower compared to other neurologically affected/syndromic children, reaching only 64% (p=0.002) [54]. There was a significantly higher tracheostomy rate in the CP group (18%, p=0.049). A different study found that only a minority of children had sufficient control of SDB with noninvasive treatment (i.e. oxygen, CPAP or BiPAP) [55]. 73% of their CP cohort required adenotonsillectomy, 20% required supraglottoplasty and 54% required tracheostomy, with 63% of children who initially underwent adenotonsillectomy requiring a tracheostomy, on average 1.9 years later [55].

In contrast to the surgical methods, there are limited data on NIV in children with CP, although they account for more than 10% of the total paediatric NIV population [9]. Theoretically, there is physiological evidence to warrant the use of NIV, with CPAP often constituting a good treatment option in case of OSA, which is common in the earlier disease stages. However, with disease progression, respiratory failure may develop, warranting more extensive NIV support, often related to the combined effects of progressive scoliosis, restrictive chest wall mechanics, recurrent or chronic respiratory infections, and impaired ventilatory control associated with neuromuscular dysfunction. In extremis, tracheostomy with or without ventilation may be needed. In each case, treatment options should be discussed within the family and multidisciplinary team taking care of the patient; weighing possible risks, benefits and taking into account treatment goals tailored to the individual patient and quality of life for the patient and the caregivers [9]. These decisions often raise ethical considerations, including proportionality of treatment, potential caregiver burden and the child's best interests, particularly when long-term invasive ventilation or tracheostomy is considered, given the risk of chronic complications, prolonged dependency and impact on quality of life. As for other NIV indications, as well as with oxygen as treatment alternative, effectiveness and adherence should be monitored, whilst being careful to not attempt very aggressive correction of longstanding hypoventilation in view of chemosensor desensitisation due to hypoxaemia. In the CP population specifically, NIV may be more challenging than in the general population, with up to 75% suffering minor side-effects and failed therapy establishment in 55% of cases [9]. In clinical practice, failure rates and lack of sustained long-term adherence may be even higher and NIV can also exacerbate gastro-oesophageal reflux, further limiting tolerance in some children.

Mohon et al. [56] studied the impact of intrathecal baclofen on sleep-related symptoms and PSG in a multicentre study. There was a statistically significant reduction in OSA after initiation of intrathecal baclofen treatment in one of the study centres (pre-treatment 78% of patients had OSA, post-treatment 51%, p=0.04), but not in the other centre (pre-treatment 91% of patients had OSA, post-treatment 79%, p=0.17). Furthermore, the role and impact of botulinum toxin type A (Botox) on SDB has been investigated. Although only anecdotal evidence based on a single case report, the use of Botox injections in the agonist and antagonist muscles of the oromandibular complex has also been described; and allowed a significant reduction in required CPAP pressures and delay of further surgical intervention for micro-retrognathia [57].

A case report by Tamura et al. [58] highlighted a potential role for orthodontic treatment in a child with CP, severe teeth malalignment and OSA. In this case, AHI was reduced from 32 events·h–1 to 6 events·h–1 pre- versus post-orthodontic intervention.

Interventions for sleep problems other than SDB, such as fragmented sleep and changes in the sleep-wake cycle, have been reviewed previously in the literature and suggest a paucity of evidence-based interventions in this area, but possible benefits of cognitive behavioural therapies, melatonin, but also seizure and pain control where relevant have been put forward [59, 60].

Inborn errors of metabolism

As iterated above, overarching treatment concepts relating to surgical interventions in the upper airway and/or NIV via CPAP or BiPAP can also be applied to children with neurodisability caused by metabolic diseases. In general, OSA is the most common form of SDB in these children and adenotonsillectomy is the first line surgical treatment. However, a combined approach with initiation of CPAP for residual OSA is commonly needed, despite some initial improvement after ERT or HSCT [16–18]. Indeed, Pal et al. [17] showed that 16% of the population required 13 episodes of therapeutic airway intervention following initiation of ERT or HSCT. A later age at start of treatment significantly correlated with the need for intervention.

In children with MPS, adenotonsillectomy is commonly performed and, as reviewed by Galluzzi et al. [11], more than half of the patients in the respective study cohorts underwent adenotonsillectomy. One study showed a significant reduction in AHI to mild or moderate OSA in five of 18 patients and in a second cohort there was a decrease in AHI of more than 50% in four of 15 patients [61, 62]. Interestingly, it was also shown that adenotonsillectomy resulted in an improvement in OSA-related symptoms reported by parents [14]. By contrast, Ademhan Tural et al. [12] did not find significant differences in frequency and severity of sleep apnoea between MPS patients who did versus did not have upper airway surgery, likely reflecting the multifactorial pathophysiology of OSA in MPS. The study by Elwell et al. [63] showed that children with MPS undergoing adenotonsillectomy were younger and had a longer and more complicated hospital stay with higher rates of postoperative haemorrhage compared to a control group of children undergoing adenotonsillectomy. Harrison et al. [14] proposed transnasal endoscopic adenoidectomy as an alternative to “classical” coblation adenoidectomy for patients with MPS who may have restricted airway access due to extensive deposition of glucosaminoglycans intrinsic to MPS. This technique was safe and effective, albeit in a limited number of patients. Finally, Koehne et al. [13] noted a role for maxillary expansion in addition to adenoidectomy in patients with maxillary constriction.

Specifically for Pompe disease, Kansagra et al. [22] described a cohort of 17 children with infantile Pompe, of whom five patients began ERT prior to 6 months of age and had an average baseline of 2.3 events·h–1 and a decrease in overall AHI of 1.3 events·h–1. The remaining patients began ERT after the age of 6 months and had an average baseline AHI of 3 events·h–1, with a decrease in AHI of 2.1 events·h–1. The decrease in AHI between these two groups was not statistically significant.

Other genetic conditions associated with neurodisability

Trisomy 13 and 18

Children with trisomy frequently present with multilevel airway obstruction, hypotonia and craniofacial anomalies, making SDB management particularly challenging. In the largest series, Benson et al. [34] reported that 74% of subjects required ENT interventions, mainly adenotonsillectomy (23%) and tracheostomy (9%), mostly targeting airway obstruction rather than SDB. Kettler et al. [33] similarly described frequent respiratory procedures, including intubation in 19–36% and tracheostomy in 3–11% of patients, with difficult airways reported in up to 70%. Postoperative instability and poor tolerance of NIV were common.

In a palliative context, Ariagno et al. [64] documented successful management of central apnoea with caffeine in an infant receiving palliative care.

22q11.2 deletion/duplication

Tonsillectomy is frequently performed in children with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, often for speech optimisation or SDB management. Adenoid tissue may contribute to velopharyngeal closure and adenoidectomy can unmask or exacerbate VPI. Surgical strategies such as tonsillectomy alone or partial (superior) adenoidectomy have been proposed in selected patients, alongside careful preoperative counselling and individualised risk and benefit assessment [40]. Filip et al. [41] successfully treated one patient with postoperative OSA using CPAP therapy. Jon et al. [65] described a child with 22q11.2 deletion (DiGeorge) syndrome and suprastomal airway collapse, in whom laryngotracheal reconstruction with anterior tracheal wall suspension effectively restored airway patency and resolved OSA and hypoventilation, allowing safe tracheostomy decannulation.

Rett and MECP2-related disorders

Management of SDB in Rett and MECP2-related disorders requires a multimodal approach. NIV therapy may improve nocturnal hypoventilation and severe OSA in selected patients, while supplemental oxygen may be used in early stages; however, treatment success is variable and often limited by poor tolerance, autonomic instability, and neurobehavioral factors [42, 47]. Ohno et al. [66] reported that combining NIV with serotonergic modulation, specifically a 5-HT1A agonist (tandospirone) and a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (fluvoxamine), led to improved AHI (from 33.5 events·h–1 to near-normal levels) and stabilisation of behavioural symptoms, even after weaning from NIV.

Discussion

In this review, we investigated the prevalence, presentation and management of SDB in children with neurodisabilities. We focused on CP, inherited errors of metabolism and other genetic disorders that cause neurodisabilities. Despite the fact that SDB is highly prevalent in patients with neurodisabilities, with a significant impact on quality of life, and that they represent a large proportion of the NIV population, data on optimal diagnosis and management are limited [9, 67]. Furthermore, data on the impact of SDB and treatment on quality-of-life remain rather scarce.

Diagnosis of SDB in neurodisability

As evident from the results section, the clinical presentation of SDB may be atypical in children with neurodisabilities and the use of standardised questionnaires such as the PSQ may help to increase clinical detection of SDB [6, 8]. The data also suggest that routine sleep studies (ideally with the gold standard, i.e. PSG) should be considered in patients with neurodisabilities even if symptoms of SDB are very mild. In diseases such as MPS, standardised annual screening with PSG should be considered given the high prevalence and progression of SDB despite ERT or HSCT.

Surgical interventions

Adenotonsillectomy is by far the most described surgical intervention for SDB in children with neurodisabilities. However, the success rate of adentonsillectomy, in terms of complete resolution of OSA/SDB, is much lower than in otherwise healthy children; reflecting the underlying complex and multifactorial origins of SDB in this population. A multilevel surgical approach is often required, especially if adenotonsillectomy fails. Orthodontic and maxillofacial interventions have also been described, tailored to the individual patient's needs.

Noninvasive ventilation

NIV is an effective treatment for SDB and is either delivered in the form of CPAP or BiPAP. The number of children with neurodisability, especially with CP, on long-term NIV has increased significantly over the past decade. A profound alteration in attitude towards which patients are offered home long-term ventilation has driven this change, inspired by the increasing realisation that treatment of the SDB can potentially improve quality of life. The impact on practice is reflected by the European Respiratory Society statement on paediatric long-term noninvasive respiratory support, specifically having a section for those with severe neurodisability [68]. This section emphasises the paucity of data on health outcome changes attributable to NIV use in neurodisability. In children with developmental delay, CPAP has been shown to be associated with improvement in the Epworth Sleepiness Scale score, total behaviour score, OSA specific score and quality of life [69]. However, the rate of NIV failure is higher in children with neurodisability, with prolific oral secretions, gastro-oesophageal reflux, aspiration and epilepsy increasing the rate of NIV failure [9, 68]. Despite this higher risk of NIV failure, for those who tolerate, it has been associated with improvements in OSA symptoms and quality of life [49].

This potential for improvement is in line with our experience. As part of a quality improvement project for our home ventilation service, we obtained feedback from families of children with neurodisabilities who had been started on NIV. While there is the potential for a skewed population, as only the children for whom there was perceived benefit on NIV would have continued on it, the overwhelming opinion of NIV was a positive one. Although these patients took a few weeks to get used to using NIV, once established, it was tolerated well, with excellent adherence. Parents reported a perceived benefit to sleep quality, with more restful sleep, improved concentration and behaviour, less tiredness, and less emotional dysregulation after initiation.

Rajkumar et al. [70] performed a retrospective study of the health outcomes of patients with CP before and after starting CPAP. Of the 76 patients who underwent sleep studies, 21 were diagnosed with SDB and 10 started on CPAP. Not only did their AHI improve as one would expect, what was more important was there was also a statistically significant improvement in the number and duration of hospital admissions the year after starting CPAP compared with the year prior to starting it. This improvement was achieved purely utilising CPAP without recourse to bilevel positive pressure ventilation. There was also an apparent decrease in the number and duration of antibiotic courses, however, this did not reach statistical significance. This potentially could be due to the limited cohort size, with a larger prospective study planned.

There is a significant proportion of patients who do not tolerate PAP therapy, as described by Grychtol et al. [9]. Over half of their patients with CP failed NIV either due to intolerance of the mask and/or ventilation pressure or subsequent poor adherence. Decisions to undertake NIV must thus be individualised, with a nuanced balance of the potential benefits outweighing the risks and burden of the treatment.

Collaborative decision-making on treatment is crucial in patients with neurodisability, with a stepwise, multidisciplinary approach advocated. The main aim is to improve the quality of life of the child. A trial of NIV may be appropriate, but prior to its initiation, it is important to agree with the family what would success look like, conversely what would failure look like and what are the indicators to stop the trial. Thus, discussions regarding burden of therapy, comfort, safety with secretions/vomiting and the expectations apropos gains in reduction of hospital admissions should be addressed as part of any NIV trial. Overall, this is true for therapeutic decision-making regarding SDB in neurodisability, whether the proposed intervention is surgical, consists of orthodontic procedures or considering NIV. Furthermore, other interventions such as positioning, weight control and optimising general respiratory management should also be considered in the approach to SDB in children with neurodisabilities [71]. Finally, in some cases, the outcome of the decision-making may be to not pursue surgical treatment or NIV. Conclusion

In summary, there is a high prevalence of SDB in children with neurodisabilities. This can manifest as OSA, CSA, nocturnal hypoxaemia and hypoventilation or a combination thereof and can potentially be quite severe. Aetiology is often multifactorial and therefore treatment can be challenging; not only are these patients often at higher anaesthetic/surgical risk, but also standard treatment such as adenotonsillectomy has a lower rate of success as it only targets one of the causes of the SDB. Therapies such as NIV can be considered but can have a higher failure rate in some patients and the benefits need to be weighed against risks and burden of treatment. The ultimate aim of diagnosing and treating SDB in these patients is to improve quality of life. Collaborative decision-making with the family, with a multidisciplinary approach is strongly recommended.
