Histone Lactylation Enhances Th17 Cell Differentiation Through DPP4 to Promote Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition in Asthma
Authors: Xinxin Zhong, Yamei Luo, Jiale Su, Yaxi Liang, Kai Ding, Xiaowen He, Bo Xiao, Lixia Hou, Feiqian Xue, Guiming Zhou, Feixiang Ling, Yi Gou, Libing Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe airway remodeling in asthma is closely associated with the abnormal differentiation of Th17 cells and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) of bronchial epithelial cells. This study aims to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms of histone lactylation in Th17 cell differentiation and the EMT of bronchial epithelial cells.MethodsAn asthma mouse model was constructed, pathological changes, immune cell subsets, and histone lactylation levels were analyzed. CD4⁺ T cells from normal and asthmatic mice were treated with a glycolysis activator (Nala) or inhibitor (2-deoxy-glucose (2-DG)). ChIP-qPCR and dual-luciferase assay were performed to verify the regulation of DPP4 promoter by H3K18la. DPP4 inhibitor (K579) was used to intervene in Th17 cell differentiation. Finally, bronchial epithelial cells were induced to undergo EMT by TGF-β1, and co-cultured with CD4+ T cells to evaluate EMT markers.ResultsThe asthma mouse model showed lung inflammation, airway remodeling, and imbalance in immune cell subsets. H3K18la and DPP4 levels were upregulated, correlating positively with IL-17. Inhibiting glycolysis reduced H3K18la, inhibited Th17 cell differentiation, and decreased IL-17 secretion. H3K18la activated DPP4 transcription by enriching in DPP4 promoter region. K579 blocked Th17 cell differentiation mediated by H3K18la. Additionally, DPP4 significantly promoted the EMT of bronchial epithelial cells by promoting Th17 cell differentiation, as evidenced by downregulation of E-cadherin, upregulation of α-SMA, and changes in cell morphology. This process was partially inhibited by 2-DG treatment.ConclusionsH3K18la promoted Th17 cell differentiation by activating DPP4, thereby driving the EMT of bronchial epithelial cells. Introduction

Asthma is a prevalent chronic inflammatory condition of the airways, distinguished mainly by airway hyperresponsiveness and progressive airway remodeling [1]. The pathogenesis of asthma involves the complex interplay of immune cell imbalances, epithelial cell dysfunction, and extracellular matrix remodeling [2]. Airway remodeling encompasses a variety of structural changes, among which epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is one of the core events. EMT leads to the loss of normal polarity and connections of airway epithelial cells, endowing them with mesenchymal cell properties, thereby promoting tissue fibrosis and functional impairment [3]. CD4+ T helper (Th) cells are important participants in asthma pathogenesis [4]. IL-17 secreted by Th17 cells and type 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) has the capacity to induce neutrophilic airway inflammation [5], which is associated with increased airway hyperresponsiveness and thus contributes to the pathogenesis of asthma [6–8]. Abnormal differentiation of Th17 cells and the inflammatory cytokines they secrete play a key role in airway remodeling in asthma, making the Th17-EMT axis a hot topic in asthma research [9]. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms driving Th17 cell differentiation and EMT have not yet been fully elucidated, which hindering a comprehensive grasp of asthma pathogenesis and the development of effective therapeutic strategies.

The rise of metabolic immunology and its “metabolism-epigenetics coupling” theory offer a fresh lens for elucidating immune-epithelial cell interactions [10]. As the end product of glycolytic metabolism, lactate is significantly enriched in the inflammatory microenvironment and has been demonstrated to influence the functions of several immune cells [11]. In experimental autoimmune uveitis (EAU) model, inhibition of lactylation significantly reduced Th17 cell proportions and attenuated inflammatory responses [12], suggesting that lactylation may be a key regulatory node in Th17 cell differentiation. Lactylation, as a histone post-translational modification, contributes to the regulation of gene transcription [13]. Histone lactylation, by adding lactyl groups to histone lysine residues, regulates chromatin structure and gene expression, and has shown significant regulatory potential in immune cell differentiation (such as Th17 cells) and tissue remodeling [14]. In particular, in inflammatory diseases, changes in histone lactylation levels are intricately linked to the intensity and duration of inflammatory responses [12]. However, its role in Th17 cell differentiation and subsequent EMT process in asthma remains unclear.

Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP4) is a membrane-bound protein involved in the regulation of inflammation and metabolism [15]. Given its clinical association with a range of diseases, the possible involvement of DPP4 in asthma has also attracted the interest of researchers. Studies have shown that, as a potential regulator of Th17 cell differentiation, serum levels of DPP4 are positively correlated with asthma severity and indicators of airway remodeling [16]. DPP4 promoted ovalbumin (OVA)-induced asthma in female C57BL/6J mice via regulating Th17/IL-17 axis and accelerating airway EMT [17], indicating that DPP4 is a therapeutic target for asthma. Moreover, DPP4 can enhance TGF-β1-induced EMT in bronchial epithelial cells and has a synergistic effect with IL-17 [18]. However, it remains unclear whether DPP4 is involved in regulating Th17 cell differentiation and EMT through epigenetic mechanisms, particularly histone lactylation. This uncertainty highlights the importance of further investigating the role of histone lactylation in asthma.

Based on the above background, this study, for the first time, proposes the scientific hypothesis that histone lactylation may promote Th17 cell differentiation through transcriptionally regulating the expression of DPP4, thereby driving the EMT of bronchial epithelial cells. To test this hypothesis, we integrated an asthma mouse model with an in vitro cell co-culture system, aiming to elucidate the “metabolism-epigenetics-immunity-remodeling” cascade regulatory network and provide a possible perspective for targeted asthma therapy.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Female C57BL/6J mice, 6–8 weeks old, were purchased from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. Mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 5 per group): the Sham group (untreated controls, no OVA sensitization or challenge) and the Asthma group (OVA‑induced asthma airway remodeling model). Randomization was performed using a random number table, and all animal experiments and outcome assessments were conducted in a blinded manner to avoid selection bias and observer bias. The asthma airway remodeling mouse model was induced as follows: Mice were sensitized by intraperitoneal injection of Ovalbumin (OVA) and aluminum hydroxide gel (each mouse received 10 µg of OVA dissolved in 0.2 mL of aluminum hydroxide gel) on days 0 and 12. After sensitization, the mice were exposed to a 5% OVA solution for 30 min daily from days 18 to 23. This acute phase was followed by chronic airway remodeling induction, with continuous OVA exposure (three times per week, 30 min each time) from day 24 to day 55. On day 56, mice were processed for subsequent analyses [19, 20]. After the experiment, spleens were aseptically removed to isolate CD4+ T cells. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), lung tissue, and para-tracheal lymph node tissues were collected for further detection.

Isolation and Identification of Splenic CD4+ T Cells

CD4+ T cells from the spleen were identified in Sham and Asthma group mice using CD4-positive labeling antibodies and flow cytometry. The spleens were aseptically excised and processed into single-cell suspensions, which were subsequently enumerated. The splenic single-cell suspensions underwent positive selection for CD4+ T cells utilizing immunomagnetic bead technology [21]. After the positive selection of CD4+ T cells with immunomagnetic beads, cells were identified using flow cytometry with CD4-positive labeling antibodies.

Culturing for the Directed Differentiation of Splenic CD4+ T Cells into Th17 Cells

Murine splenic CD4 + T cells were isolated and plated at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL. To mimic the cytokine milieu necessary for Th17 cell differentiation, the following components were included: anti-CD3 (2 µg/ml), anti-CD28 (2 µg/ml), anti-IFN-γ (10 µg/ml), anti-IL-4 (10 µg/ml), TGF-β1 (5 ng/ml), IL-6 (20 ng/ml), and IL-23 (50 ng/ml). Following a 5-day culture period, cells from each group were collected [21].

Isolation and Identification of Mouse Bronchial Epithelial Cells

After Sham group mice were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of 2% pentobarbital under anesthesia, the chest and abdomen were disinfected with alcohol, and the mice were fixed on the operating table (taking care to protect the oral cavity and nasal passages from alcohol irritation). Under aseptic conditions, an incision was made along the midline of the abdomen to fully reveal the abdominal aorta. Blood was drained by cutting open the abdominal aorta to minimize the contamination of red blood cells during the isolation of airway epithelial cells. The trachea and bronchi were then exposed layer by layer and completely freed. As long and pure a segment of the bronchus as possible was taken. A small amount of 0.05% Streptomyces protease pre-chilled at 4 ℃ was injected to lavage the inner wall of trachea until it was clean. The free end of the trachea was ligated, and after confirming that the ligatures at both ends were secure, the entire trachea was filled with the digestive enzyme. The trachea was then folded and clamped with an arterial clamp, and the entire segment was fixed and immersed in DMEM/F12 at 4 ℃ overnight (12 h). On the following day, the digestive fluid was gathered, and the trachea was rinsed with DMEM/F12 using a 5 mL syringe to collect the rinse. The digestive fluid and rinse were combined and transferred to a 15 mL centrifuge tube, where red blood cell lysis buffer was added. The mixture was then centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 7 min. After removing the supernatant, cells were resuspended in a suitable volume of DMEM/F12 and plated into a tissue-free culture dish containing complete medium. After 1 h of incubation, fibroblasts, which have a stronger attachment ability, adhered to the dish earlier than airway epithelial cells and were thus removed, similar to the principle of differential adhesion (using a tissue-free culture dish is more conducive to cell separation and purification).

Cell Culture and Treatment

Previously, we isolated CD4+ T cells from mice in the Sham and Asthma groups. Here, we used these two groups of cells as the Control and Model groups, respectively, and simulated the cytokine microenvironment required for Th17 cell differentiation. First, we screened the intervention concentrations of the glycolysis activator Nala and the glycolysis inhibitor 2-deoxy-glucose (2-DG). Control group cells were cultured in 5, 10, 20, and 25 mM of the glycolysis activator Nala for 24 h to detect PanKla levels [22]. Additionally, Control group cells were cultured in 0, 5, 10, and 20 mM of the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG (HY-13966, MCE) for 24 h to detect PanKla levels [23]. Cells were further divided into the following groups: Control group (cells isolated from Sham group mice cultured normally for 24 h), Control + Nala group (cells isolated from Sham group mice cultured in 20 mM glycolysis activator Nala for 24 h), Model group (cells isolated from Asthma group mice cultured for 24 h), and Model + 2-DG group (cells isolated from Asthma group mice cultured in 10 mM glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG for 24 h). All four groups were simultaneously simulated with the cytokine microenvironment required for Th17 cell differentiation. Furthermore, cells isolated from Sham group mice were treated with 24.5 ng/mL DPP 4 inhibitor K579 for 24 h [24], and were divided into following groups: Control (simulated Th17 cell differentiation microenvironment for 24 h), Nala (simulated Th17 cell differentiation microenvironment and treated with glycolysis activator Nala for 24 h), and Nala + K579 (simulated Th17 cell differentiation microenvironment and treated with glycolysis activator Nala and K579 for 24 h). In addition, we isolated and cultured primary mouse bronchial epithelial cells. Co-culture and treatment of mouse bronchial epithelial cells with Th-17 cells were as follows: Bronchial epithelial cells were treated with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 to induce EMT. On the basis of TGF-β1 treatment, bronchial epithelial cells were co-cultured with Th-17 cells at a ratio of 1:10, which was determined based on preliminary optimization experiments and previous studies [25]. The groups were as follows: Normal (bronchial epithelial cells seeded at a density of 1 × 105/mL as negative control culture), TGF-β1 (10 ng/mL TGF-β1 added and cultured for 72 h to induce EMT in bronchial epithelial cells), TGF-β1 + Model (TGF-β1-treated bronchial epithelial cells were co-cultured with CD4+ T cells induced to differentiate into Th17), and TGF-β1 + Model + 2-DG (TGF-β1-treated bronchial epithelial cells were co-cultured with CD4+ T cells induced to differentiate into Th17 and treated with 2-DG). Photos were taken at 72 h to observe the morphological changes of bronchial epithelial cells.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) Staining

Lung tissue morphology and inflammatory cell infiltration were assessed using HE staining. Sections were baked at 60 ℃ for 12 h, then deparaffinized in xylene (3 × 20 min) and rehydrated through graded ethanol (100, 100, 95, 85, 75%; 5 min each). They were washed in distilled water for 5 min, stained with hematoxylin (1–10 min, AWI0001a, Abiowell), rinsed, and blued in PBS, followed by eosin staining (1–5 min, AWI0029a, Abiowell) and distilled water rinse. Sections were dehydrated with graded ethanol (95–100%; 5 min each), cleared in xylene (2 × 10 min), and mounted with neutral gum for microscopic observation.

Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) Staining

The PAS staining kit (AWI0536a, Abiowell) was employed to evaluate goblet cell hyperplasia, mucus secretion, subepithelial collagen deposition, and basement membrane thickness. Sections were baked at 60 °C for 12 h, deparaffinized in xylene (3 × 20 min), and rehydrated through graded ethanol (100, 100, 95, 85, 75%; 5 min each). After washing in distilled water for 5 min, sections were treated with 50 µL periodic acid for 5–7 min, rinsed under running tap water for 5–7 min, and stained with Schiff’s reagent for 3–5 min. Nuclei were lightly stained with hematoxylin for 20 s, washed, and blued in PBS. Sections were air-dried, dehydrated with graded ethanol, cleared in xylene (2 × 10 min), mounted with neutral gum, and examined under a microscope.

Biochemical Assays

IL-17, TGF-β1, and MMP9 in BALF and IL-17 levels in BALF and cell supernatants were detected using ELISA kits. In this study, we used multiple ELISA kits provided by Wuhan Huamei Bioengineering Co., Ltd. for the quantitative analysis of the relevant cytokines. Specifically, these included ELISA kit for IL-17 detection (CSB-E04608m), MMP9 detection (CSB-E08007m), and TGF-β1 detection (CSB-E04726m).

Flow Cytometry

Flow cytometry was applied to measure the changes in the number of Th17 cells (CD4+IL-17+ T cells) in the para-tracheal lymph node tissues of mice. Lymph nodes were placed in cold PBS, cleaned of fat and connective tissue, and washed thrice with PBS. They were homogenized with a 10 mL syringe plunger (with 1 mL PBS), passed through a 70 μm strainer, and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min at 4 ℃. The pellet was resuspended in PBS, treated with red blood cell lysis buffer for 5 min, washed, and resuspended in RPMI 1640 with 10% FBS. Cells were stimulated with Cell Stimulation Cocktail (plus protein transport inhibitors) (00-4975-93, eBioscience) for 4 h at 37 ℃, washed, and stained with CD4 antibody (11-0041-82, eBioscience) and IL-17 antibody (12-7177-81, eBioscience). After fixation and permeabilization, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (A00-1-1102, Beckman). Additionally, flow cytometry was conducted to assess the changes in the number of Treg cells (CD4+CD25+Foxp3+) in the para-tracheal lymph node tissues of mice. Cells were stained with CD4 antibody (11-0041-82, eBioscience) and CD25 antibody (12-0251-82, eBioscience), fixed, permeabilized, and stained with Foxp3 antibody (00-5523-00, eBioscience). Cells were washed and resuspended in 0.5% BSA-PBS for flow cytometry analysis. Moreover, flow cytometry was utilized to detect the number of IL-17+CD4+ T cells. Purified CD4 + T cells were stimulated with Cell Stimulation Cocktail for 4 h at 37℃, washed, stained with CD4 antibody (11-0041-82, eBioscience) and IL-17 antibody (12-7177-81, eBioscience), fixed, permeabilized, and analyzed by flow cytometry. For gating strategy, single cells were first gated by FSC/SSC, and dead cells were excluded. For Th17 and IL-17 + CD4+ T cells, CD4 + populations were gated first, followed by analysis of IL-17 + subsets. For Treg cells, CD4 + cells were gated first, followed by analysis of CD25 + Foxp3+ subsets. All gates were set based on corresponding isotype controls.

Real-Time Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR)

RT-qPCR was employed to assess DPP4, E-cadherin, and α-SMA levels in cells. Total RNA was extracted with the Trizol Total RNA Extraction Kit (15596026, Thermo), and its concentration and purity were determined. mRNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA using mRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (CW2569, CWBIO). Gene expression was analyzed with Ultra SYBR Mixture (CW2601, CWBIO) on the ABI 7900 system, normalized to β-actin, and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primers are as follows: M-DPP4-F: TCATTCCGCTGTGAAGTTGG, M-DPP4-R: GGGAGAGACTGAACCATCACA; M-E-cadherin-F: AGCCATTGCCAAGTACATCCTC, M-E-cadherin-R: CGCCTTCTGCAACGAATCCC; M-α-SMA-F: GCCCCTGAAGAGCATCCGAC, M-α-SMA-R: CCAGAGTCCAGCACAATACCAGT; M-β-actin-F: ACATCCGTAAAGACCTCTATGCC, M-β-actin-R: TACTCCTGCTTGCTGATCCAC.

Western Blot

Western blot was conducted to evaluate PanKla, H3K18la, H3K23la, H4K5la, H4K8la, H4K12la, LDHB, E-cadherin, and α-SMA levels in cells. Total protein was extracted using RIPA (AWB0136, Abiowell), separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. After blocking with 5% skim milk for 1.5 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4℃, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1.5 h at room temperature. The primary antibodies used were: PanKla (PTM-1401RM, 1:1000, PTM BIO), H3K18la (PTM-1427RM, 1:1000, PTM BIO), H3K23la (PTM-1413RM, 1:2000, PTM BIO), H4K5la (PTM-1407RM, 1:1000, PTM BIO), H4K8la (PTM-1415RM, 1:1000, PTM BIO), H4K12la (ab177793, 1:10000, Abcam), LDHB (14824-1-AP, 1:10000, Proteintech), DPP4 (32397-1-AP, 1:4000, Proteintech), α-SMA (14395-1-AP, 1:5000, Proteintech), E-cadherin (20874-1-AP, 1:20000, Proteintech), H3 (ab1791, 1:1000, Abcam), H4 (PTM-1009, 1:1000, PTM Biolabs), and β-actin (66009-1-Ig, 1:5000, Proteintech). The proteins were detected using ECL reagent (AWB0005, Abiowell) and analyzed with an imaging system. H3, H4, and β-actin served as the reference protein, and the expression levels were quantified using Quantity One 4.6.6.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

IF staining was utilized to detect PanKla and H3K18la expressions in CD4⁺ T cells and Cytokeratin 8 expressions in primary mouse bronchial epithelial cells. Slides were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, and blocked with 0.3% Triton X-100 and 5% BSA. Primary antibodies PanKla (PTM-1401RM, 1:50, PTM BIO), H3K18la (PTM-1427RM, 1:50, PTM BIO), and CK8 (ab53280, 1:50, Abcam) were applied overnight at 4℃. After washing, slides were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (AWS0005, 1:200, Abiowell) for 90 min at 37℃, stained with DAPI for 10 min, and mounted with buffered glycerol for microscopy.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR

The enrichment of H3K18la in the DPP4 promoter region was confirmed by ChIP. Cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min, and DNA was fragmented to 200–800 bp by sonication. DNA was immunoprecipitated using an antibody specific to H3K18la. ChIP DNA was purified and eluted in 100 µL of water, and 2.5 µL was used for RT-qPCR analysis with specific primers targeting DPP4 promoter. Primer sequences are as follows: DPP4-ChIP1-F: CCCTGGTCTCACTCGGTAG, DPP4-ChIP1-R: GGTGAAGTGGGCTCTTAC; DPP4-ChIP2-F: AATACCTGTGCTCGGCCTCC, DPP4-ChIP2-R: CTCCATAAAGCCACTGAA. For ChIP quality control, input DNA was used as a positive control, and non-specific normal IgG was used as a negative control for immunoprecipitation.

Dual-Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay

The activity of DPP4 promoter was evaluated with Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (E1910, Promega). CD4+ T cells were cultured in 24-well plates and transfected with the respective plasmids in four groups, as detailed below: Control group (Cells (CD4+ T cells isolated from Sham group mice) were co-transfected with DPP4 (WT) and pRL-TK plasmids for 24 h, and then cells were cultured normally for another 24 h), Control + Nala group (Cells (CD4+ T cells isolated from Sham group mice) were co-transfected with DPP4 (WT) and pRL-TK plasmids for 24 h, and then cells were cultured in 20 mM glycolysis activator Nala for 24 h), Model group (Cells (CD4+ T cells isolated from Asthma group mice) were co-transfected with DPP4 (WT) and pRL-TK plasmids for 24 h, and then cells were cultured for 24 h), and Model + 2-DG group (Cells (CD4+ T cells isolated from Asthma group mice) were co-transfected with DPP4 (WT) and pRL-TK plasmids for 24 h, and then cells were cultured in 10 mM glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG for 24 h). The pRL-TK plasmid expressing Renilla luciferase was used as an internal control to normalize transfection efficiency, and empty vector transfection was used as a baseline control.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between two groups were made using Student’s t-test, while multiple-group comparisons were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Pearson correlation analysis was applied to assess the relationships between DPP4, H3K18la, and IL-17. P < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Increased H3K18la Levels in an Asthma Mouse Model

First, we established an asthma mouse model and conducted a series of tests on mice in the Sham and Asthma groups. HE staining revealed that lung tissue structure of mice in Sham group was relatively normal, with thin alveolar walls, clear alveolar cavities, and minimal inflammatory cell infiltration, presenting an overall well-organized tissue structure. In contrast, lung tissue of mice in Asthma group exhibited significant inflammatory responses, with thickened alveolar walls, a large number of inflammatory cells infiltrating, disordered alveolar structure, and some areas showing narrowed or disappeared alveolar cavities, indicating that asthma had caused significant pathological changes in the lung tissue (Fig. 1A). PAS staining revealed that the Sham group mice had fewer goblet cells in the lung tissue, with no obvious mucus secretion, low subepithelial collagen deposition, and normal basement membrane thickness, presenting relatively healthy lung tissue characteristics. In the Asthma group mice, goblet cells were significantly proliferated, with increased mucus secretion, increased subepithelial collagen deposition, and thickened basement membrane. These pathological features suggested that asthma led to airway remodeling, goblet cell metaplasia, increased mucus secretion, collagen deposition, and thickening of the basement membrane, further confirming the damage of asthma to the structure and function of lung tissue (Fig. 1B). Cytokine detection in BALF revealed that, in contrast with Sham group, IL-17, MMP9, and TGF-β1 levels in Asthma group were notably elevated (Fig. 1C), indicating intensified inflammatory responses. Flow cytometry of para-tracheal lymph node tissue revealed that, in comparison with Sham group, the number of Th17 cells (CD4+IL-17+ T cells) in Asthma group increased, while the number of Treg cells (CD4+CD25+Foxp3+) decreased, reflecting an imbalance of immune cell subsets (Fig. 1D and E). Next, we isolated CD4+ T cells from mice in the Sham and Asthma groups and identified them using flow cytometry with CD4-positive labeling antibodies. Figure 1F confirmed that we successfully isolated CD4+ T cells from mice in Sham and Asthma groups. Here, we used these two groups of cells as the Control and Model groups, respectively. Western blot analysis revealed that, in comparison with Control group, PanKla, H3K18la, H3K23la, H4K5la, H4K8la, and H4K12la levels in CD4+ T cells of Model group were markedly upregulated, with the most significant increase observed in H3K18la (Fig. 1G). IF detection also confirmed that PanKla and H3K18la levels in CD4+T cells of Model group were elevated compared to Control group (Fig. 1H and I). In addition, RT-qPCR detection found that DPP4 levels in CD4+ T cells of Model group were upregulated compared to Control group (Fig. 1J). Pearson correlation analysis further revealed positive correlations between DPP4 and both H3K18la and IL-17 (Fig. 1K). These results indicated that, in the asthma mouse model, H3K18la levels increased and was closely related to inflammatory responses, immune cell subset imbalance, and specific gene expression changes, providing important clues for in-depth research on the pathogenesis of asthma.

Inhibition of Histone Lactylation Suppresses Th17 Cell Differentiation

Previously, we isolated CD4+ T cells from mice in the Sham and Asthma groups. Here, we used these two groups of cells as the Control and Model groups, respectively, and simulated the cytokine microenvironment required for Th17 cell differentiation. First, we screened the intervention concentrations of the glycolysis activator Nala and the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG. Control group cells were cultured in 5, 10, 20, and 25 mM of the glycolysis activator Nala for 24 h, and PanKla levels were evaluated. As the concentration of Nala increased, the PanKla levels in cells showed an upward trend. PanKla levels increased at 10 mM, significantly rose at 20 mM, and reached the highest value at 25 mM, with a slower increase compared to 20 mM. Based on the trend of PanKla level changes, 20 mM was initially determined as the effective intervention concentration of Nala (Figure S1A). Furthermore, control group cells were cultured in 0, 5, 10, and 20 mM of the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG for 24 h, and PanKla levels were measured. Compared with the 0 mM 2-DG group, PanKla levels began to decline in the 5 mM 2-DG group; they further decreased at 10 mM, with a more significant drop compared to the 5 mM group; and reached the lowest level at 10 mM, indicating effective inhibition of glycolysis; while at 20 mM, PanKla levels increased compared to 10 mM. Therefore, based on the inhibitory effect on PanKla levels, 10 mM was determined as the effective intervention concentration of 2-DG (Figure S1B). Next, we explored the effects of glycolysis activator Nala and glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG on Th17 cell differentiation. The findings indicated that, relative to Control group, LDHB, PanKla, and H3K18la levels as well as the number of IL-17+CD4+ T cells and IL-17 levels in supernatant, were significantly higher in both the Control+Nala and Model groups; whereas compared to Model group, these indicators were notably reduced in Model + 2-DG group (Fig. 2A and E), indicating that inhibiting histone lactylation could effectively suppress Th17 cell differentiation.

H3K18la Activated DPP4 Transcription

To further explore the impact of H3K18la on DPP4 transcription, we first detected the enrichment of H3K18la in DPP4 promoter region using ChIP-qPCR. Compared to Control group, the enrichment of H3K18la in the DPP4 promoter region was notably elevated in Control+Nala and Model groups, while it was markedly decreased in Model + 2-DG group compared to Model group. This indicated that H3K18la promoted the enrichment in the DPP4 promoter region in the asthma model, and the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG could reduce this enrichment (Fig. 3A). In addition, in comparison with Control group, DPP4 mRNA and protein levels were notably elevated in Control+Nala and Model groups, while they were markedly reduced in Model + 2-DG group compared to Model group, suggesting that H3K18la could activate the transcription and expression of DPP4, and the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG could inhibit this process (Fig. 3B). Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay results showed that, in comparison with Control group, the activity of DPP4 promoter was markedly enhanced in Control+Nala and Model groups, while it was notably reduced in Model + 2-DG group compared to Model group (Fig. 3C). This further confirmed the activating effect of H3K18la on DPP4 promoter activity and the inhibitory effect of the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG on this process. These results indicated that H3K18la could activate DPP4 transcription, promote the enrichment in the DPP4 promoter region, DPP4 expression, and the enhancement of DPP4 promoter activity, while the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG could inhibit this process.

Histone lactylation of DPP4 promoted Th17 cell differentiation

To further investigate the impact of histone lactylation of DPP4 on Th17 cell differentiation, we treated cells in Control group with DPP4 inhibitor K579. In comparison with Control group LDHB, PanKla, and H3K18la levels were notably elevated in both Nala and Nala+K579 groups, with no significant differences between the Nala and Nala+K579 groups (Fig. 4A and B). Additionally, in comparison with Control group, the number of IL-17+CD4+ T cells and IL-17 levels in supernatant were markedly increased in both the Nala and Nala+K579 groups. However, compared to Nala group, the number of IL-17+CD4+ T cells and IL-17 levels in supernatant were notably decreased in Nala+K579 group (Fig. 4C and D). These results suggested that histone lactylation promoted Th17 cell differentiation by activating DPP4, and the DPP4 inhibitor K579 could effectively inhibit this process, revealing the key role of the histone lactylation-DPP4 pathway in Th17 cell differentiation.

DPP4 Mediated Bronchial Epithelial Cell EMT by Promoting Th17 Cell Differentiation

Finally, we isolated bronchial epithelial cells from mice in the Sham group. IF identification showed that bronchial epithelial cells from Sham group mice were positive for Cytokeratin 8 expression (Fig. 5A). Next, bronchial epithelial cells were treated with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 72 h to induce EMT. On this basis, bronchial epithelial cells were co-cultured with CD4+ T cells. Among them, CD4+ T cells were induced to differentiate into Th17 cells and treated with 2-DG. We found that, in comparison with Normal group, the morphology of bronchial epithelial cells in TGF-β1 group changed markedly. Cell shape changed from polygonal to spindle-shaped, and the intercellular contact was weakened, showing typical EMT changes. After co-culture of bronchial epithelial cells with Th17-differentiated CD4+ T cells, EMT changes were further enhanced. However, 2-DG treatment partially inhibited this process (Fig. 5B). RT-qPCR and Western blot detection results revealed that, in comparison with Normal group, epithelial cell marker E-cadherin levels in TGF-β1 group were significantly reduced, while mesenchymal cell marker α-SMA levels were notably elevated. After co-culture of bronchial epithelial cells with Th17-differentiated CD4+ T cells, E-cadherin levels were further reduced, while α-SMA levels were further increased. However, 2-DG treatment partially inhibited this change (Fig. 5C). These results demonstrated that DPP4 significantly promoted EMT in bronchial epithelial cells by promoting Th17 cell differentiation, and 2-DG treatment could partially inhibit this process, revealing the important regulatory role of the DPP4-Th17 cell axis in EMT of bronchial epithelial cells.

Discussion

To investigate the role of histone lactylation in asthma pathogenesis, this study employed an asthma mouse model in conjunction with in vitro cellular assays. Focusing on the core pathological interaction between immune and epithelial cells in asthma, this study provides evidence that H3K18la promoted Th17 cell differentiation by activating DPP4, thereby driving the EMT of bronchial epithelial cells. This finding offers new insights into the epigenetic mechanisms underlying airway remodeling in the pathogenesis of asthma.

Recent studies have revealed that lactate, produced by metabolic reprogramming (such as glycolysis activation), not only serves as an energy metabolite but also regulates gene transcription through histone lactylation, contributing to the development of inflammation-related diseases [26, 27]. It has been reported that key glycolytic enzymes significantly co-localize with F4/80⁺ macrophages in the lung tissues of asthmatic mice, and Dexamethasone could significantly downregulate the activation of the OVA-induced HIF-1α-glycolysis axis, leading to reduced lactate levels [28], which implies that there is abnormal lactylation driven by metabolic reprogramming in asthma pathogenesis. Our study results indicated that in the asthma mouse model, H3K18la levels were notably elevated, which was closely related to inflammatory responses, immune cell subset imbalances, and changes in specific gene expression. This finding is highly consistent with the theory of metabolic immunology—inflammatory microenvironments enhance glycolysis in immune cells, leading to lactate accumulation, which provides substrates for histone lactylation and thereby regulates gene expression through epigenetic modifications [29]. In vitro experiments have shown that the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG could reduce the level of H3K18la and inhibit Th17 differentiation, supporting the involvement of a “metabolic reprogramming-histone lactylation-immune cell differentiation” axis. Notably, the specific upregulation of H3K18la in Th17 cells raises the possibility that it may serve as an epigenetic marker for Th17 differentiation. This is in line with reports on the regulation of Th17 by lactylation in experimental autoimmune uveitis [12], suggesting a potentially conserved regulatory function in Th17 cell differentiation in inflammatory diseases across different disease models. However, correlation does not imply causation; our pharmacological inhibitors (2-DG, Nala) may have off-target effects, and genetic manipulation of the lactylation machinery is needed to confirm the causal role of H3K18la in Th17 differentiation.

DPP4, as a multifunctional enzyme, plays a significant role in asthma [17, 30]. Histone lactylation involves the addition of lactyl groups to the lysine residues of histones, a modification that can alter chromatin structure and thereby affect the transcriptional activity of genes [31]. Mechanistic evidence from this study suggests that H3K18la activated the transcription of DPP4 by enriching in its promoter region, which represents a key finding. As a key molecule at the intersection of inflammation and metabolism, serum levels of DPP4 are positively correlated with asthma severity and indicators of airway remodeling [32], yet the upstream regulatory mechanisms of DPP4 have long been unclear. Through ChIP-qPCR, dual-luciferase assay, and experiments with the DPP4 inhibitor K579, this study indicates that DPP4 is an essential mediator of histone lactylation in promoting Th17 differentiation. Inhibition of DPP4 could block Th17 cell proliferation and IL-17 secretion induced by overexpression of H3K18la, while the promoting effect of the glycolysis activator Nala on Th17 cells appears to be dependent on DPP4 expression. This finding places DPP4 into the “metabolism-epigenetics” regulatory network, suggesting that DPP4 is not only a functional executor of Th17 differentiation but also a potential node in the signal transduction of histone lactylation. These results extend the application of the metabolism-epigenetics coupling theory in asthma research, linking the function of the metabolite lactate from energy metabolism to the realm of immune regulation.

At the level of immune-epithelial interactions, this study observed that DPP4 was associated with promoting EMT in bronchial epithelial cells by regulating Th17 cell differentiation, characterized by significant downregulation of E-cadherin, upregulation of α-SMA, and transformation of cell morphology into a spindle shape. Inhibition of H3K18la or DPP4 could partially block this process. This finding is consistent with previous reports that “Th17-derived IL-17 promotes EMT” [33, 34] and suggests a potential synergistic effect between DPP4 and IL-17 have a synergistic effect in the regulation of EMT, possibly forming the “Th17-DPP4-EMT” signaling axis. Notably, co-culture experiments of epithelial cells and Th17 cells during the EMT process have shown that treatment with 2-DG could inhibit the exacerbated EMT caused by enhanced Th17 differentiation. These results imply that targeting the glycolysis-lactylation pathway might simultaneously interfere with immune cell differentiation and epithelial cell remodeling, providing a dual-target approach for asthma therapy.

This study introduces histone lactylation into the pathogenesis of asthma, proposing a complete regulatory chain of “metabolic reprogramming-epigenetic modification-immune cell differentiation-EMT.” Compared with traditional Th17 differentiation regulatory factors (such as IL-6 and TGF-β), histone lactylation, as a reversible epigenetic modification, may have greater potential for pharmacological intervention. Moreover, DPP4 is already a clinical target (e.g., DPP4 inhibitors for diabetes) [35], elucidating its epigenetic regulatory mechanisms in asthma may provide a rationale for drug repurposing in asthma treatment.

Despite the meaningful findings of this study, there are also some limitations. First, this research is primarily based on animal models and cellular experiments, and the results need to be further validated in human asthma patients. Second, the regulatory mechanisms of histone lactylation are very complex and may involve multiple enzymes and signaling pathways, which have not been fully elucidated in this study. Third, we acknowledge that the metabolic modulators Nala and 2-DG may have off-target effects, and definitive causal proof will require genetic approaches in future studies. In addition, our epigenetic analysis was restricted to locus-specific investigation of H3K18la at the DPP4 promoter, which limits the mechanistic depth. Genome-wide profiling such as ChIP‑seq and ATAC‑seq would help to reveal the global epigenetic regulatory network in future research. Further investigation is also needed to clarify the specific mechanisms of DPP4 in asthma and promote clinical translation.

In summary, this study provides evidence for a mechanism by which histone lactylation promoted Th17 cell differentiation through DPP4, thereby driving the EMT of bronchial epithelial cells. This finding extends the understanding of the “Th17-EMT axis” and suggests that targeting H3K18la-DPP4-Th17 pathway could represent a dual therapeutic strategy to block both immune inflammation and structural remodeling in asthma. These results offer a theoretical basis for developing new asthma drugs based on metabolic-epigenetic regulation, although further mechanistic and translational studies are needed.
