Healthy habits, better searches: behavioral predictors of cancer information access
Authors: Nicholas Acuna, Katelyn M. Atkins, Shaowli Kabir, Jane C. Figueiredo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study examined the association of cancer-preventive lifestyle behaviors with cancer information-seeking experiences among United States adults without a history of cancer.MethodsWe analyzed data from the Health Information National Trends Survey (HINTS) 7 from 2412 participants between March 2024 and September 2024. A healthy behavior index was constructed based on lifestyle behaviors and categorized into quintiles (Q1-Q5), where Q5 reflects a higher healthy lifestyle score. The Information Seeking Experience scale was computed from four questions that capture participants' experiences for cancer-related information (i.e., search required a lot of effort, was frustrating, raised concerns about information quality, or was hard to understand) and was categorized into quartiles (Q1-Q4, where Q4 reflects the most positive experiences).ResultsCompared with the lowest healthy behavior index quintile, the quintile was associated with higher relative risk ratios (RRRs) for Information Seeking Experience Q3 (RRR = 2.3; 95% CI: 1.2,4.5) and Q4 (RRR = 2.7; 95% CI: 1.5,5.0). Maintaining a healthy weight (RRR Information Seeking Experience Q4 = 1.9, 95% CI = 1.2,3.1) and not consuming alcohol (RRR Information Seeking Experience Q3 = 2.4; 95% CI = 1.1,5.3) were also associated with more positive experiences.ConclusionsAdults with lower healthy behavior profiles reported more negative cancer information-seeking experiences.

Introduction

In an era of readily accessible information on the internet, social media, and other digital platforms, it is important to understand how individuals seek health information. This understanding is pivotal for enhancing public health awareness and ensuring that people have access to accurate, reliable, and relevant information. Access to comprehensive and accurate health information empowers patients to participate in their health decisions (Herrera et al., 2023; Fraenkel and McGraw, 2007), fostering a collaborative relationship with their health care providers. By being well-informed, people can engage in meaningful discussions about their preferences and potential outcomes. Previous studies have found that individuals who sought health information, either online or from their healthcare provider, were more likely to follow healthy behaviors and cancer screening guidelines (Swoboda et al., 2021; Zhang and Jiang, 2021). A systematic review also found that patients who seek health information online showed improved patient-provider relationships (Tan and Goonawardene, 2017).

Many patients report a desire to be more actively involved in their health care, especially women, those with a higher education, and those who self-report being healthier (Levinson et al., 2005). Although patients want to be more involved in their health care decisions, prior studies suggest that adults in the United States often have negative experiences when searching for cancer information such as concerns about information quality, the effort required to find information, frustration during the search process and difficulties understanding information (Arora et al., 2008). These experiences are likely shaped by individual and systemic factors, including prior health care interactions, digital access and literacy and the ability to locate trustworthy information (Anker et al., 2011). By identifying those who face negative experiences when searching for cancer-related information, we can refine public health messaging and develop better targeted interventions to support those in need.

Reaching high-risk individuals, such as those who use tobacco, consume alcohol, experience obesity, or lack physical activity, is critically important, as those individuals are at the greatest risk for developing cancer (Islami et al., 2024). Therefore, ensuring that cancer prevention information reaches individuals who do not meet healthy behavior guidelines should be a key public health priority. By examining how cancer-preventive behaviors are associated with information-seeking experiences, we can identify groups that may need more accessible information. However, few studies to date have examined how an individual's lifestyle behaviors are associated with their experiences when searching for cancer-related information.

The current study aims to address this gap in the literature by assessing the associations of a healthy behavior index and individual cancer risk factors with cancer information-seeking experiences using a national survey focused on health communication and information. We hypothesized that healthier behaviors would be associated with positive experiences in seeking cancer-related information.

Methods

Study design and population

The Health Information National Trends Survey (HINTS), funded by the National Cancer Institute, is a United States representative survey that has collected data biennially on how adults access and use information across the cancer continuum since 2003 (National Cancer Institute. Health Information National Trends Survey, 2026; Nelson et al., 2004). The purpose of HINTS is to track trends in health communication technologies and the public's knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors while engaging with health communications (Nelson et al., 2004). The current study uses HINTS 7, which sampled non-institutionalized adults aged 18 or older between March 25th, 2024, and September 16th, 2024. Individuals were invited to participate in the self-administered survey by mail or could complete it online. A total of 7278 participants completed the survey, with a response rate of 27.3%. We excluded individuals who reported not seeking cancer information because HINTS did not administer the information-seeking experience items to these respondents (n = 3548). We also excluded respondents who reported ever being diagnosed with cancer (n = 1019), because experiences among cancer survivors may reflect diagnosis and treatment-related information needs rather than primary prevention information seeking and individuals missing information-seeking variables (n = 64). We then conducted a complete-case analysis by excluding participants with missing data on a priori selected covariates to be adjusted in the multivariable model (n = 235). Therefore, the final analytic sample for the current analysis included 2412 participants. The HINTS data is based on publicly available anonymized data and is exempt from ethical compliance.

Measures

Demographic information, including age (continuous), sex (male, female), annual household income groups (less than $20,000, $20,000 to <$35,000, $35,000 to <$50,000, $50,000 to <$75,000, $75,000 to $100,000 and $100,000 or more), education level (less than high school, high school graduate, some college, college graduate or more), self-reported race and ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black or African American, Hispanic, non-Hispanic Asian, and non-Hispanic Other), and health insurance status (yes, no), were examined using the categories defined in HINTS 7.

The Information Seeking Experience scale was based on four items describing participant's most recent search for cancer-related information: “Based on the results of your most recent search for information about cancer, how much do you agree or disagree with each of the following statements: ‘It took a lot of effort to get the information you needed...’, ‘You felt frustrated during your search for the information...’, ‘You were concerned about the quality of the information...’, and ‘ The information you found was hard to understand...’”. Participants responded to each item using the following scale: “1 = Strongly agree”, “2 = Somewhat agree”, “3 = Somewhat disagree”, and “4 = Strongly disagree”. A composite Information Seeking Experience scale variable was computed using the mean of the four variables and transformed into a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 100. A higher score indicated more positive experiences, reflecting less effort, frustration, concern about quality, and difficulty understanding cancer-related information. For clinical interpretability, the Information Seeking Experience score was categorized into sample quartiles (Q1-Q4), where Q1 reflected the least positive experiences, and Q4 reflected the most positive experiences. The four-item Information Seeking Experience scale is a valid and reliable scale that captures an individual's experience of seeking cancer-related information (Murray et al., 2025) and is an updated version of a previous three-item scale (Arora et al., 2002).

A healthy behavior index was created and adapted based on the 2018 World Cancer Research Fund and American Institute for Cancer Research recommendations (World Cancer Research Fund/American Institute for Cancer Research, 2018). We applied a similar scoring approach to that used in the National Institute of Health-AARP Diet and Health Study (Korn et al., 2022). We used all available questions that aligned best with the recommendations, such as body weight, physical activity, plant-based intake, and alcohol consumption. Supplemental Table 1 reports the scoring for each healthy behavior index component. Body mass index (kg/m2) was calculated based on a participant's height and weight. Physical activity from at least moderate intensity exercise was derived from two items: “In a typical week, how many days do you do any physical activity or exercise of at least moderate intensity, such as brisk walking, bicycling at a regular pace, and swimming at a regular pace?” and “On the days that you do any physical activity or exercise of at least moderate intensity, how long do you typically do these activities?”. Plant-based intake was derived from two questions in the HINTS 7 survey using the following items: “About how many cups of fruit (including 100% pure fruit juice) do you eat or drink each day?” and “About how many cups of vegetables (including 100% pure vegetable juice) do you eat or drink each day?”. Participants responded to each item using the following categories: “None”, “1/2 cup or less”, “1/2 cup to less than 1 cup”, “1 to less than 2 cups”, “2 to less than 3 cups”, “3 to less than 4 cups”, and “4 or more cups”. Responses for fruit and vegetable intake were combined into a single plant-based intake variable. Alcohol consumption was derived from the average drinks per week variable. Sex-specific cut-points were used based on the U.S. Dietary Guidelines, 2020–2025 (U.S. Department of Agriculture and U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2020). Tobacco use was included in the scoring, despite it not being in the 2018 World Cancer Research Fund/American Institute for Cancer Research score, since it is the leading modifiable risk factor contributing to cancer cases (Islami et al., 2024). Scores for the five equally weighted components were summed to create a total healthy behavior index score ranging from 0 to 5, and total scores were divided into sample-based quintiles. A higher quintile reflects healthier behaviors.

Statistical analysis

Categorical and continuous variables are presented using descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, count, and weighted percentages to account for the HINTS 7 complex survey design. The Taylor series linearization variance estimation method was used to account for the complex survey design, and the weighted percentage, along with a 95% confidence interval (CI), was computed using the demographic information age, sex, race, ethnicity, and health insurance (Williams, 2008). Bivariate analyses, including chi-square and ANOVA, were used to explore differences among the categorical and continuous variables, with Information Seeking Experience as the target variable of interest. For the primary analysis examining the association between healthy behavior index and Information Seeking Experience, we used multinomial logistic regression instead of ordinal logistic regression because the proportional odds assumption was violated. We also adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, health insurance status and annual household income. Results from the multinomial logistic regression model are presented with relative risk ratios (RRR) and the 95% CI. For secondary analyses, each individual healthy behavior index component was evaluated in a separate model. To address multiplicity across the five individual component models, we used the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate applied to omnibus Type III tests. Results from applying the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery did not meaningfully change the overall interpretation and thus are not reported. All analyses were completed using SAS software version 9.4 (Cary, North Carolina).

Results

The weighted analytic population had a mean age of 46.3 (SD = 16.0) years. The majority were female (52.0%), non-Hispanic White (66.1%), with a college graduate education or more (44.4%), had an income level of over $100,000 (41.3%), and had health insurance (93.0%, Supplemental Table 2). There were no differences between sex (p = 0.26), race and ethnicity (p = 0.85), education level (p = 0.72), annual household income (p = 0.24), or insurance status (p = 0.76) across Information Seeking Experience quartiles. However, younger individuals reported more negative experiences than older individuals (mean age = 43.9 in Q1 v. mean age range = 46.3–48.3 in Q2-Q4, p = 0.01). We also observed that those with an annual household income greater than $100,000 were more likely to be in the highest healthy behavior index quintile than in the lowest (56.4% vs. 21.4%, p < 0.01, Supplemental Table 3). In addition, those with a college degree or higher were more likely to be in the highest quintile than in the lowest (63.0% v. 26.4%, p < 0.01). As expected, individual risk factors (i.e., body weight, physical activity, alcohol use, and smoking) varied significantly across healthy behavior index quintiles (p-values<0.01).

The weighted distribution of Information Seeking Experiences varied significantly by certain individual risk factors and by composite HBI scores (Table 1). The weighted population in the highest healthy behavior index quintile was more likely to be in Information Seeking Experience Q4 than Q1 (21.8% v. 14.7%, p = 0.03). The weighted population consuming alcohol beyond recommendations was more likely to be in Information Seeking Experience Q1 than Q4 (47.7% v. 41.4%, p = 0.03). Discussion

In this nationally representative survey conducted in the United States, we observed that individuals who engaged in healthier behaviors tended to report more positive experiences when seeking cancer information. In contrast, adults at a higher risk for developing cancer—due to lifestyle factors such as higher body weight and alcohol consumption—reported greater negative experiences accessing cancer-related information. Low adherence to recommended lifestyle guidelines is associated with an increased risk for developing cancer (Khong et al., 2025) as well as a higher likelihood of cancer-related mortality (Ford et al., 2011). Therefore, understanding disparities in information-seeking experiences can help inform more effective public health communication interventions.

Seeking cancer-related information has several potential benefits, including healthier behaviors and cancer screening, compared to not seeking information (Wigfall and Friedman, 2016; Shim et al., 2006). Our results extend this literature by showing heterogeneity among those who do seek cancer-related information: adults with lower adherence to healthy behaviors appeared to have less favorable cancer-seeking experiences. The Information Seeking Experiences domains (i.e., effort to get information, frustration in searching information, concerns about the quality of information, and difficulty in finding information) may limit access to actionable prevention information. Future public health messaging should prioritize this high-risk group with more accessible materials.

Individuals who maintain healthier lifestyle behaviors might have a stronger sense of self-efficacy (Kalantzi et al., 2024), the belief in their ability to achieve goals. This heightened self-efficacy could affect both how they search for information and their experiences in finding and using that information (Ma et al., 2023). Previous studies have shown differences in a few lifestyle behaviors and information-seeking experiences, including smoking (Rutten et al., 2009), and physical activity (Lee et al., 2022). However, evidence on overall lifestyle behaviors is limited. When we examined individual cancer risk factors, we did not find a statistically significant association between Information Seeking Experience and physical activity, plant-based intake, or tobacco use. Small cell counts may have reduced our ability to detect statistically significant differences, given that the effect estimates are in the expected direction. We also only observed significant associations for healthy behavior index quintiles 2 and 5 and Information Seeking Experience, while the estimates for the remaining healthy behavior index quintiles were non-significant, but generally in the same direction, potentially due to limited cell counts. This pattern suggests that the overall association between a healthy behavior index and Information Seeking Experience may be positive. Additional studies are needed to confirm the patterns observed in this study.

We also observed some evidence that younger individuals reported more negative experiences when searching for health information than older individuals did. Younger individuals are often more comfortable with digital platforms and have access to vast amounts of information online (Jiao et al., 2023). Exposure to extensive amounts of health information has been associated with information overload (Khaleel et al., 2020). Although we did not directly assess cancer information overload, cancer information overload may represent one potential mechanism underlying these findings among younger individuals. Prior work suggests that information overload can make it more difficult for individuals to process, evaluate, and use health information effectively (Khaleel et al., 2020). Consequently, younger adults who engage more frequently with digital health information may be particularly vulnerable to information overload when navigating cancer-related information. When young adults seek health information, they often look for content that is credible, user-friendly, respectful, interactive, private, and inclusive (Stifjell et al., 2025). Designing health information platforms with these priorities may help improve experiences for younger populations.

This study has several strengths, including the use of a large, representative survey that is broadly generalizable to the United States population. Additionally, we used a validated instrument that captures the experiences of individuals who have searched for cancer-related information (Murray et al., 2025). Limitations include smaller cell counts in some healthy behavior index quintiles, which may have reduced precision. We also lacked sufficient dietary information to recreate the full 2018 World Cancer Research Fund and American Institute for Cancer Research score, including red and processed meat, sugar-sweetened beverages and whole grains. This may have contributed to non-differential misclassification and attenuated the estimates, although the included behaviors follow an approach used in the UK Biobank (Elwood et al., 2018). The questionnaire also counted 100% fruit or vegetable juice as part of fruit and vegetable intake, which could misclassify dietary quality. We included only one wave of HINTS data because other waves did not include all measures needed to construct the healthy behavior index, including fruit and vegetable intake. Selection bias remains possible despite probability-based sampling and weighting. Respondents may differ from nonrespondents. Because adults who did not seek cancer information were not asked Information Seeking Experience items and cancer survivors were excluded, findings apply to cancer-free information seekers and should not be generalized to nonseekers or survivors.

Conclusions

In summary, individuals with low healthy behavior profiles reported more negative cancer information-seeking experiences. These findings suggest that current information platforms may be insufficiently accessible or actionable for these individuals. Intentionally designed communication approaches may reduce informational barriers and support cancer prevention among underserved and at-risk populations.
