Dual syntax aware graph attention networks with prompt for aspect-based sentiment analysis
Authors: Ao Feng, Tao Liu, Xiaojie Li, Ke Jia, Zhengjie Gao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aspect-based sentiment analysis (ABSA) is a challenging task due to the presence of multiple aspect words with different sentiment polarities in a sentence. Recently, pre-trained language models like BERT have been widely used as context encoders in ABSA. Graph neural networks have also been employed to extract syntactic and semantic information from sentence parsing trees, resulting in superior results. However, dependency trees may establish irrelevant dependencies for sentences with irregular syntax and complex structures. Additionally, previous methods have not fully utilized recent developments in pre-trained language models. Therefore, we propose a Dual Syntax aware Graph attention networks with Prompt (DSGP) model to address these issues. Our model utilizes prompt templates to maximize the potential of pre-trained models and masked vector outputs of templates as supplementary aspect feature representations. We also leverage both dependency trees and constituent trees with graph attention networks to extract different types of syntactic information. The dependency tree captures syntactic correlation between words, while the constituent tree provides a high-level formation of the sentence. Finally, the output from the prompt and parsing trees is fused and fed into a standard classifier. Experimental results on four public datasets demonstrate the competitive performance of our model.

Introduction

With the rapid development of social media and E-commerce, sentiment analysis has become one of the major natural language processing (NLP) applications. Traditional sentiment analysis research mainly works at the sentence or document level1–3, focusing on the overall sentiment polarity of the text. Since the introduction of BERT4, coarse-grained sentiment analysis has almost become a solved problem. Aspect-based sentiment analysis (ABSA) deals with a more interesting case, trying to make the judgment for each individual aspect in a (usually) multiple-aspect sentence. With the overlapping context and complex interaction among various aspects, it becomes a more challenging problem. A short sentence “The price is reasonable although the service and the environment are poor.” contains three aspect terms (price, service, environment), and the task of ABSA is to predict the sentiment polarity for each of them.Fig. 1Example sentence with dependency tree (a), adjacency matrix (b) and dependecy types index table (c), with aspect terms in red. The general approach to solve the ABSA task is to build connections between aspect words and their relevant contexts (e.g., opinion words) to better detect sentiment for a given aspect. In the difficult cases, there are multiple aspects in a sentence, and the subject’s opinion may not be consistent among them. Some previous works5–11 employ neural networks with attention mechanism to address this problem and achieve compelling results. However, the attention mechanism is easily interfered with by irrelevant words in a sentence, resulting in inability to correctly establish the connection between aspect words and the right opinion words. Some recent works adopt the syntactic structure of sentences to model aspect words and related contexts to obtain more accurate dependencies. Some methods12–16 treat the dependency tree as an adjacency matrix, using graph convolutional networks (GCN17) or graph attention networks (GAT18) to encode the input sentence into the matrix. As shown in Fig. 1a,b, the dependency tree can establish correlation between aspect words and relevant contexts, ignoring the influence of irrelevant words to obtain better aspect feature representation. But these methods basically only consider whether the nodes in the dependency tree are connected, ignoring their dependency types that may be useful for sentiment disambiguation19. In addition, all word-to-word relations in the dependency tree are treated equally, so unimportant relations may not get effectively filtered out, introducing unnecessary noise20. Therefore, some methods also count the dependency types(such as in Fig. 1c) and incorporate them into the graph network to filter out some unimportant relationships. However, syntactic structure of online reviews may not be standardized and some of the review sentence structures are complex, which makes it difficult for a syntactic parser to build a good dependency tree, reducing the ability of a dependency tree to capture the aspect-related contexts21. Finally, most approaches use BERT as a contextual encoder, using hidden vectors from [CLS] as a complement for aspect word feature representation. In reality, a sentence may have multiple aspects, and it is difficult for [CLS] to express a specific aspect even when aspect words are individually added to the input.Fig. 2Example sentence with constituent tree, with aspect terms in red.

Example sentence with constituent tree, with aspect terms in red.

To address the problems above, we propose a Dual Syntax aware Graph attention networks with Prompt (DSGP) framework. First, DSGP uses a BERT encoder to capture contextual word representations. Unlike previous work19,22–24, we add a masked prompt template to the input in order to retain feature representations closer to the specified aspect and capture richer semantic information. Second, to exploit the variance of types in the dependency tree, we adopt the method of Bai et al.19 and incorporate label features into the attention mechanism to better capture the relationship between words. Meanwhile, we also form the constituent tree of a sentence, as shown in Fig. 2. The constituent tree mainly performs phrase segmentation and hierarchical division of the sentence, which helps to establish the connections between different aspect words. It correctly aligns the sentiment words that are closely related to the aspect words. The constituent tree can obtain syntactic information from a higher-level perspective, thus making up for the deficiency of the dependency tree. Finally, inspired by the work of DGEDT14, we adopt the BiAffine module to bridge the relevant information between two syntax trees for better aspect feature representation.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:We propose a DSGP model for the ABSA task, which simultaneously considers two types of syntactic information in a sentence. Specifically, our model integrates syntax information from the constituent tree and the dependency tree through a mutual BiAffine module.Text templates with cloze-type hard prompt are proposed to fully exploit the ability of pre-training language models. Output from the [MASK] position is combined to the output of the syntax module, forming a better aspect representation.Extensive experiments on four datasets show that our proposed model achieves competitive results, when compared to other methods with similar backbone and utilization of similar information.

Related work

Traditional methods in ABSA

Early methods before the deep learning era25,26 are usually based on hand-crafted features to predict aspect-specific sentiment polarity. They fail to make good use of dependencies between the given aspects and their context, leading to unsatisfactory performance.

With the development of deep learning in recent years, many use neural network models to deal with ABSA tasks and bring great performance improvements27,28. Most use LSTM neural networks to process words in a sequential manner, while some also apply attention mechanism29 to model the interactions between an aspect and its corresponding context5–7,30–32. For instance, Wang et al.6 propose an attention-based LSTM which can concentrate on different parts of a sentence when different aspects are taken as input. Tang et al.32 introduce target-dependent LSTM (TD-LSTM) to capture the aspect information when modeling sentences. Fan et al.7 build a multi-grained attention network with both fine-grained and coarse-grained attentions to capture word-level interaction between aspects and their context. In addition to LSTM, others have explored different network structures to support the attention mechanism, including CNN-based networks33,34, memory networks5,8 and gated networks34,35. More recently, pretrained language models such as BERT4, GPT36 and ELECTRA37 have shown their great value in many ABSA tasks. Sun et al.22 construct an auxiliary sentence from the aspect term and convert ABSA to a sentence pair classification task, which can better utilize the sentence pair modeling ability of BERT. Gao et al.23 propose a BERT variant that extracts aspect-related sentiment information from the aspect terms instead of the default [CLS] symbol.

Syntax-based methods

Following the tradition of the NLP community, a different approach takes the syntactic structure of sentences to model and make predictions. Through the syntactic structure tree, the connection between aspect words and opinion words can be established to learn the syntax-aware feature representation of aspects. Some recent works have used graph neural networks and their variants to encode dependency tree and constituent tree with promising results. In particular, Zhang et al.12 build a GCN over the dependency tree of a sentence to exploit syntactical information and word dependencies. Tang et al.14 propose a dependency graph enhanced dual-transformer network (DGEDT) by jointly considering representations from Transformers and the corresponding dependency graph. Li et al.38 propose a dual graph convolutional networks (DualGCN) model that simultaneously considers the complementarity of syntactic structure and semantic relevance. Zhang et al.21 propose an aspect-aware attention mechanism combined with self-attention to learn aspect-related semantic relevance and global semantics of sentences. Meanwhile, a syntactic mask matrix is constructed to supplement semantic information. However, all these methods consider only binary relations within the dependency tree, ignoring dependency types between nodes, which is intuitively useful. Wang et al.39 define a unified aspect-oriented dependency tree structure rooted at the target aspect, and propose a relational graph attention networks (R-GAT) to encode the new tree structure for sentiment prediction. Li et al.19 demonstrate the effectiveness of type-dependent structures in target sentiment classification, by extending a graph attention networks encoder with relational features. Tian et al.20 propose a type-aware graph convolutional networks (T-GCN) with multiple layers to enhance ABSA by combining word relations and their dependency types to learn comprehensively from dependency parsing results. Yuan et al.40,41 also propose a syntactic graph attention network (SGAN) and a syntax-aware transformer (SA-Transformer) to make full use of dependency types in the dependency tree to solve ABSA tasks. In addition to dependency trees, Liang et al.24 propose a bi-syntax aware graph attention networks (BiSyn-GAT+) framework to fully leverage syntax information. They use both the constituent tree and the dependency tree by modeling the sentiment-aware context of each single aspect and the sentiment relations across aspects.

Prompts

In order to better utilize the knowledge learned by pre-trained large language models, more and more people start using prompts to guide the model to perform different tasks. By adding some descriptive sentences before and after model input, the potential of the pre-trained model can be better utilized. The LAMA dataset42 provides manually created cloze templates to explore knowledge in language models. Brown et al.43 create manually crafted prefix prompts to tackle a variety of tasks. Schick and Schütze44 define pairs of cloze question patterns and verbalizers to help leverage the knowledge contained within pretrained language models for downstream tasks. Seoh et al.45 incorporate target word into prompt templates, turning the ABSA task into next/masked word prediction. Our model also contains manually customized prompt templates. Unlike the common practice, we directly use masked hidden vectors for classification instead of verbalizers to convert label words into a sentiment polarity.

Dual syntax aware module

The dual syntax aware module includes three main parts: Dependency GAT (DepGAT), Constituent GAT (ConGAT) and the BiAffine module (BiAffine). Among them, DepGAT obtains the relationship between words in the dependency tree, and ConGAT uses the hierarchical structure of the constituent tree to obtain higher-dimensional syntactic information and complements DepGAT. BiAffine Module is used to exchange information between the different perspectives of two trees.

We conduct experiments on four English standard datasets, including restaurant and laptop reviews from SemEval 2014 task 446, Twitter47, and MAMS48. For the dual syntax aware module, we use 4 attention heads in each layer of GAT with an embedding dimension of 384. Since the constituent tree has a multi-layer structure, we use 2–3 layers in it to extract information, referring to the work of BiSyn-GAT24.

Baselines

To demonstrate the effectiveness of our DSGP method, we compare its performance with the following baseline models.

AEN49 adopts an attentional encoder network to establish semantic interaction between an aspect and its context.

TNet50 proposes a target-specific transformation component to generate target-dependent embeddings, and uses a CNN for encoding.

MGAN7 proposes a fine-grained attention mechanism to capture word-level interactions, and combines it with a coarse-grained mechanism to obtain more aspect-related information.

BERT-SPC49 inputs “[CLS]” + sentence + “[SEP]” + target word + “[SEP]” into the pre-trained BERT model, and then uses pooled embeddings for classification.

AEN-BERT49 is AEN with BERT as its context encoder.

CDT13 utilizes bidirectional LSTM to learn the representation of sentence features, and obtains more syntactic information from the dependency tree of sentences through GCN.

DGEDT14 proposes a dependency graph enhanced dual-transformer network by jointly considering the flat representations from Transformer and graph-based representations from the dependency graph.

R-GAT39 takes the aspect words as its root, reshapes and prunes common dependency trees, and proposes a graph attention networks to encode them.

RGAT19 studies a novel relational graph attention network that integrates typed syntactic dependency information.

BERT4GCN51 integrates BERT’s positional features and syntactic knowledge of dependency graphs.

DGEDT-BERT is DGEDT with BERT as the context encoder.

R-GAT-BERT is R-GAT with BERT as the context encoder.

T-GCN20 explicitly exploits ABSA’s dependency type via type-aware graph convolutional networks (T-GCN), and uses attention to distinguish between different edge types (relationships) in the network. For a fair comparison, we use its BERT-base version.

RGAT-BERT is RGAT with BERT as its context encoder.

To evaluate the performance of our DSGP model, accuracy and macro-averaged F1 (percentage as the unit) are used as the evaluation metrics. Experiment results of our model and previous work are shown in Table 2. Experimental results show that our DSGP model achieves competitive performance on four datasets. DSGP has the best accuracy and Macro-F1 on Laptop and MAMS. For Restaurant and Twitter, it gets the highest score on one of the two metrics, and falls behind with a small margin on the other. At the same time, we notice that methods using syntax trees (w/ Syn. in Table 2) tend to achieve better results than methods without syntax trees (w/o Syn. in Table 2), and adding syntax information usually achieve moderate performance improvement. From the results, it can be assumed that the syntax tree provides correlation information to find the context associated with the aspect words, in addition to the attention mechanism. In addition, methods using dependency types such as RGAT can achieve better results compared to methods that do not use dependency types (such as CDT, DGENT), which indicates that dependency types also contain useful information. After introducing constituent trees and prompts, our model shows improvements over models using only dependency information, implying that constituent trees can compensate for the deficiency of dependency trees, while prompts can induce pre-train models to provide richer semantic information. By combining syntax trees and prompts, our model achieves better performance, and we will further analyze the value of these modules in the next section.

To further investigate the contribution of each module in the DSGP model, we conduct extensive ablation studies, and the results are shown in Table 3. BERT-SPC is taken as the baseline without adding any syntactic information or hint templates. DSGP w/o syn does not use any syntactic information, but only uses prompt templates, and its performance is better than BERT-SPC, with Laptop as an exception. This shows that the hint template can better exploit the ability of the pre-trained model. In addition, DSGP w/o con and DSGP w/o dep both use prompt but only one type of syntax information, and their performance is better than DSGP w/o syn. This suggests that rich syntactic knowledge can provide more useful information. We notice that dependency trees perform better on Laptop and Restaurant datasets with more formal reviews. In contrast, in the Twitter dataset where user-generated comment are more informal, constituent trees perform better. DSGP w/o BiAffine means that we remove the BiAffine module, cutting off information exchange between the two syntax trees. Therefore, its performance drops compared to the full DSGP model. Finally, we experiment with hint templates and BERT pooling vectors separately, and the results were worse than combined. The masking vector provided by prompt combined with the BERT pooling vector can get a feature expression closer to the aspect word, removing any part hurts the model performance.

To better understand how our model works, we selected several samples with complex sentence structures from different fields in four datasets for case studies, as shown in Table 4. The sentence structures of the first three samples are relatively complex, and there are other noise words (such as love, okay, good) that are not related to the given aspect. The interferences increase difficulty for a good parsing tree, which easily leads to errors in model prediction. But DSGP successfully alleviates these problems with prompt templates, and utilizes the inherent knowledge and blank-filling ability of pre-trained models to generate feature expressions related to aspect words. The structure of other samples are simpler, with relative independence between the first half and the second half, and there is no interference from other irrelevant sentiment words. However, there are conflicting (one positive and one negative) sentiment polarities for multiply aspects, and the traditional BERT method has difficulty making a good prediction. Syntax trees in the DSGP model help establish the correct dependency and make correct predictions.

Number of DSGP layers

Fig. 4Effect of the number of DSGP layers.

Effect of the number of DSGP layers.

In order to study the impact of the number of DSGP layers, we conduct experiments on two datasets, Laptop and Restaurant, and evaluate the performance of the model with 1-7 layers. As shown in Fig. 4, the model has the best performance when the number of layers is 2-3, and the performance drops slightly as the number of layers increases. Our assumption is that interaction on too many layers between the syntax trees causes the original information to be lost and more redundant information generated, which hurts performance of the model.

We conduct experiments to study the effects of parsing accuracy on the classification performance. Specifically, we use Stanford Parser52 and Biaffine Parser53 in SuPar as dependency parsers, and use the neural CRF constituency parser54 in SuPar to model the constituent tree. We use BERT-SPC as the base model and compare the performance of the DSGP model when using different parsers. Table 5 shows the performance of the two dependency parsers in UAS and LAS metrics, as well as the experimental results. From the experimental results, our model can also achieve good performance using Stanford Parser. In addition, Biaffine Parser performs slightly better than Stanford Parser, which shows that the model classification performance is positively correlated with parsing accuracy.

Conclusion and future work

As the more challenging branch of sentiment analysis, ABSA has gained most of the attention in this NLP field in recent years. Since context of multiple aspects are hard to disentangle, previous research either focuses on the syntactic structure of a sentence, or calculates similarity in embedded semantic space to locate relevant context for each given aspect. We argue that each of the syntactic and semantic approaches only retains part of the whole picture from original text, and an appropriate merger keeps the complementary benefits on both sides. Therefore, we propose a DSGP model that takes both the dependency tree and constituent tree as part of the feature extractor, and utilizes a BiAffine module to try aligning two sides of the same input. In addition, we use the cloze prompt template from the language modeling field, taking the vector output of [MASK], and combine the BERT pooling vector to obtain a more comprehensive feature of the aspect word. Experiments on multiple datasets prove stable performance improvement over multiple baseline models, including those with BERT backbone and syntactic information, and detailed ablation study displays the value for each of the design options.

Considering training efficiency and return on investment, this model does not involve a state-of-the-art (also more expensive to experiment on) backbone to try pushing for the best performance. However, its network design can also be applied to more sophisticated Encoder models, e.g., RoBERTa, DeBERTa, etc. Causal language models like GPT-x embeds all information into its large-scale pretraining, which makes them harder to disentangle into an interpretable state. However, it can be assumed that they are learning the syntactic and semantic information at the same time, as the probabilistic generative approach in a black box shows excellent performance in most NLP applications, no matter which one focuses more on language understanding or generation. In addition, we have only tried the most intuitive hard prompt without testing more variants. Design of hard prompt templates has huge impact on the performance of a model, with soft prompt as another option. There may be valuable information in the syntax trees that we haven’t tried. It is possible to use hypergraphs55 to model global and local dependencies in syntax trees, which may bring more performance boost to the current framework.
