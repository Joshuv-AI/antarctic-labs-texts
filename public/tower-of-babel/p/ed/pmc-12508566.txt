Self-Perceived Psychological, Social, Academic and Personal Impact of the COVID-19 Pandemic on Chiropractic Students in South Africa
Authors: Danica Strydom, Desiree Varatharajullu, Kirsten Van Heerdan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe purpose of this observational study was to determine the social, personal, psychological, and academic self-perceived impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on Chiropractic students in South Africa.MethodsThis study used a quantitative, descriptive, cross-sectional design. A self-reported, Likert-scale survey of 35 online questions, administered through QuestionPro, was sent to Chiropractic students registered at 2 South African institutions from November 2022 until January 2023. The responses were anonymous. The survey was validated using a pilot study and focus group.ResultsA 51.65% (n = 97) response rate was recorded. A total of 60.00% (n = 113) of respondents expressed discomfort if individuals did not wear masks and 73.40% (n = 138) of respondents reported to have experienced conflicts due to prolonged time spent with household members in limited personal space. The respondents also experienced personal self-perceived impacts, such as 69.00% (n = 130) reported having reduced productivity, 42.00% (n = 79) increased financial difficulties, 50.00% (n = 94) decreased physical activity, and 80.85% (n = 152) spent more time on social media. Participants self-reported that these challenges had detrimental effects on their mental health. These included increased anxiety (92.02%; n = 171), difficulties in thought control (75.50%; n = 142), withdrawal from social interactions (80.32%; n = 151), decreased motivation (90.96%; n = 171), and concentration issues (89.89%; n = 169). A portion of the students also reported a decline in academic marks (43.62%; n = 82) and practical skills (76.60%; n = 144).ConclusionMost Chiropractic student respondents perceived that the COVID-19 pandemic negatively impacted their social and personal functioning, self-reported mental health and overall academic progress.Key Indexing Terms: COVID-19 pandemic, Chiropractic, Education 1

Introduction

In 2019, a new virus named SARS-Co-V-2, was introduced into the world.1 On the 3rd of March 2020, the first South African COVID-19 patient was detected in Johannesburg. The Department of Health in South Africa then announced various levels of lockdown in line with World Health Organization2 regulations that would be implemented to reduce the spread of this virus, recommending individuals to remain within their house. Each level allowed only a certain number of students at a tertiary education institution at one time, with level five, the highest level, allowing no students on campus. The lockdown caused by the pandemic had various effects on the student populations around the world.

The COVID-19 pandemic introduced significant challenges across various sectors, particularly in education. Online learning platforms were encouraged to accommodate the levels of lockdown experienced.3 Existing challenges with online platforms were exacerbated for both students and lecturers. Specifically, in South Africa, students faced challenges, particularly in rural settings. Students living in rural areas were found to have poorer computer skills, a shortage of electronic devices and had to contend with expensive and limited internet access.4 The transition to online learning also posed limitations on the practical components essential for students to apply their theoretical knowledge.5 The lack of theoretical application also limited the opportunity for students to problem-solve and build on their communication skills, which is necessary for patient interaction in a clinical setting.5,6

Uddin and Uddin7 found that the psychological strain of students was greatly increased by the pandemic. The professional careers of university students were significantly disrupted as a result of interruptions in their assessments and teaching, leading to delays in the scheduling of their examinations and graduation ceremonies.7,8

In addition to logistical challenges, the pandemic adversely affected students’ mental health and academic performance. Studies conducted at tertiary institutions in Spain and Turkey found that students reported heightened levels of anxiety and depression due to the uncertainty of their academic future during the pandemic and lack of hands-on practical exposure.6,9 Online learning also seemed to create a heightened level of stress for students as they needed to adjust to never experienced virtual classes, manage technical difficulties with online platforms and needed to stay self-motivated and disciplined.10

The emotional self-perceived impact of the pandemic extended further into the personal lives of the students.11 Participants in a Canadian study noted the lack of separation between their work and personal environment while working from home.11 Also, additional responsibility of attending to family members in the household during working hours was reported leading to an interrupted routine.11 Undergraduate healthcare students described a varied response of either no self-perceived impact or a negative self-perceived impact on their financial situation, due to loss of part-time job opportunities.12

Social interactions were restricted during higher lockdown levels due to travel limitations, confining socializing to online interactions.11 This hindered the students’ ability to form and maintain relationships.12 Participants from this study also admitted to feeling overwhelmed from continuous social media and news reports of COVID-19.11 Undergraduate healthcare students in Northern Ireland reported that a lack of informal social interactions with peers hindered valuable opportunities for learning and support.12 These students were also concerned about transmitting the virus home to their families after clinical rotations began.12

It is important to investigate the self-perceived impact of the COVID-19 pandemic in the South African Chiropractic student population to help understand what support could be offered to students postpandemic and to help universities in South Africa be better prepared if a similar situation arises in the future. It is also important to study the actual experience of Chiropractic students to better understand the self-perceived impact lockdown restrictions/social isolation has had on students.

The purpose of this observational study was to determine the social, personal, psychological, and academic self-perceived impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on Chiropractic students in South Africa.

Methods

Study Design

This study used a quantitative, descriptive, cross-sectional questionnaire design. The study population included registered Chiropractic students at the University of Johannesburg (UJ) and the Durban University of Technology (DUT) as these are the only 2 institutions in SA offering Chiropractic studies.

Setting

The questionnaire was distributed via an online QuestionPro (QuestionPro Inc, Seattle, WA, USA) survey link. Settings were activated to prevent a student from completing the survey more than once and to forbid the participants from taking multiple surveys; however, if the participants did not complete the survey; they were still able to access the same link and continue with the questionnaire. All participants' answers were anonymous.

Participants

The study included second to fourth year Bachelor of Health Sciences in Chiropractic (BHSc) students and first to second year Master of Health Sciences in Chiropractic (MHSc) students. The UJ had a total number of 163 students, of which 105 were registered under the Bachelor of Health Sciences in Chiropractic qualification and 58 were registered under the Master of Health Sciences in Chiropractic. The DUT had a total number of 201 students, of which 138 were registered under the Bachelor of Health Sciences in Chiropractic, 34 were registered under the Master of Health Sciences in Chiropractic (the new program), and 29 were registered under the Master of Technology in Chiropractic (the existing program being phased out). Purposeful sampling was used to select the participants. All data were recorded. Responses from questionnaires that were not fully completed were excluded from the study due to the established inclusion criteria.

The Heads of Department of both the UJ and the DUT were approached via email to distribute the documentation to the registered Chiropractic students. The researcher then emailed the pre- survey notification and the QuestionPro survey link to the class representatives for bulk distribution to the students via a single email.

Questionnaire Instrument

The questionnaire included questions related to the students’ mental health and how the COVID-19 pandemic affected their mental health and various aspects of their life. The questionnaire was adapted from a study that analyzed the personal, social, and psychological impact of COVID-1913. Permission was obtained from the author of the study and a focus group was held to adapt the questions to be more specific towards Chiropractic students and their studies, as compared to the questions aimed at a general university student. Furthermore, South-African specific demographic questions were included, such as residing provinces and South African lockdown levels. A focus group and pilot study were held to ensure validity of the questionnaire. The focus group consisted of 3 Chiropractic master’s degree students who have knowledge in quantitative studies, 2 qualified chiropractors who lecture at the DUT and a qualified PhD clinical psychologist. Feedback from the focus group helped identify unclear questions, leading to their rephrasing for better understanding and provided valuable insights that guided the addition of new questions to address overlooked topics or removal of questions that were not considered relevant to the topic. The pilot study consisted of 3 Chiropractic master students, 1 from the UJ and 2 from the DUT, who were not included in the study. Suggestions from the pilot study were incorporated to enhance the questionnaire’s overall flow and coherence.

The final questionnaire consisted of 35 questions. The first section included demographic data, and the remaining sections, personal, social, psychological, and academic, consisted of 5-scale Likert questions. Only complete questionnaires were included in the study.

Study Size

The survey was distributed in November 2022 and reached a 51.65% response rate by the end of January 2023. After achieving this response rate, it was decided to close the survey. The statistician had determined that a 50.00% response rate would be sufficient to obtain statistically viable results.

Statistical Methods

Statistical analysis was achieved using Microsoft Excel Spreadsheet Software (Microsoft Corp, Redmond, WA, USA). The demographics of the participants and the self-perceived social, personal, psychological, and academic impact were described using frequency tables, bar graphs and pie charts.

Multiple linear regression was used to determine the strength and direction of the relationship between the variables measured in the questionnaire. Multiple linear regression can incorporate categorical independent variables by using dummy coding techniques, which ensures that the analysis maintains its validity. This statistical technique was chosen because it can be used on continuous or categorical data and it models the relationship between 2 or more variables. A standard regression was used and, to further examine the strength and direction of the relationship between questions, a Pearson correlation was performed. In the regression analysis, the dependent variable was anxiety while the independent variables included periods of low mood and the control of thoughts. Another dependent variable was concentration while the independent variable was motivation. In this study, several key assumptions were tested to ensure the validity of the regression analysis. First, linearity was assessed, confirming that the relationships between independent and dependent variables were linear. Independence of observations was verified to ensure that the residuals were uncorrelated. Homoscedasticity was also examined, checking that the variance of residuals remained consistent across all levels of the independent variables. Additionally, the normality of residuals was evaluated, ensuring they were approximately normally distributed, which is particularly important for smaller sample sizes. To address potential multicollinearity, the correlations among independent variables were investigated to confirm that they were not highly correlated. Furthermore, autocorrelation in the residuals were assessed for particularly relevant, time series data. Lastly, we ensured the accuracy of measurements for all variables to minimize bias.

Ethical Considerations

This study adhered to stringent ethical considerations to ensure integrity and responsibility in research. It received approval from DUT’s Institutional Research Ethics Committee (IREC 217/22). Similarly, ethical clearance was received from both universities via the Heads of Department and Institutional Ethics committees prior to the study commencing. Validity of the questionnaire was achieved through the use of a pilot study and focus group, with informed consent obtained from all participants.

Self-Perceived Impact Analysis

Regression Analysis on Anxiety

Multiple linear regression analysis was performed on whether participants were more anxious than usual. Overall, the regression was significant (R2 = 0.49, F (9, 178) = 19.29, P < .001). A relatively strong, positive correlation was found between being more anxious and periods of low mood (r = 0.65; P < .001; β = 0.65), as well as being more anxious than usual and control of thoughts (r = 0.52; P = .23; β = 0.52). A confidence level of 95% was used.

Regression Analysis on Periods of Low Mood

Multiple linear regression analysis was performed on periods of low mood. Overall, the regression was significant (R2 = 0.53, F (14, 173) = 14.07, P < .001). A relatively strong, positive correlation was also found between question periods of low mood and control of thoughts (r = 0.60; P < .001; β = 0.60). A confidence level of 95% was used.

Regression Analysis on Concentration

Multiple linear regression analysis was performed on difficulty concentrating. Overall, the regression was significant (R2 = 0.46, F (10, 177) = 15.17, P < .001). A relatively strong, positive correlation was found between question difficulty concentrating and levels of motivation (r = 0.61; P < .001; B = 0.61). A confidence level of 95% was used.

Effects of Hours on Academic Performance

Table 6 shows that students who spent less than 3 hours on university work were more likely to have their self-reported marks worsen. Only 20.00% of students who spent less than 1 hour a day on work had their marks improved, whereas 40.00% of those students who spent less than 1 hour a day on work had their marks worsened. Similarly, 60.00% of students who spent between 1-2 hours on work a day had their marks worsen. A confidence level of 95% was used.

Discussion

The range of students varied between 19 to 37 years old, an expected finding attributable to students pursuing a second degree or needing additional time to complete their studies.9 Additionally, female participation in this study was higher (59.57%) than male participation (39.36%). This corresponds with data from O’Connor and Yelverton,13 who also found a higher female response rate (71.90%) compared to male responses (25.00%) among Chiropractic students in South Africa during the pandemic. This trend is further supported by university enrolment data, which shows a higher percentage of registered female students.13

Moreover, the study sample represented diverse ethnic backgrounds, inclusive of Asian, Black, Mixed Race, and White participants, reflecting South Africa’s multicultural demographics. This is unsurprising given the country’s rich ethnic diversity.14 Most students resided in urban areas, reflecting the urban population growth trend in South Africa.14

The COVID-19 pandemic had a notable social observation on students in the current study. The self-reported social impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on students was multifaceted, revealing significant shifts in social interactions, an increase in conflict within domestic spaces, and a complex relationship with social media usage.

Many students reported feeling uneasy when in close proximity to people not wearing masks. This apprehension could be attributed to the World Health Organization15 guidelines that emphasized mask-wearing as a crucial measure for limiting COVID-19 transmission.

Furthermore, most students reported experiencing increased conflict during the lockdown period. The necessity of social distancing led to household members spending extended periods together in limited personal space, potentially creating a conflict-prone environment. Family members found themselves navigating a stressful situation in close proximity, which likely exacerbated tension and conflict within the home.16 Additionally, an environment with high levels of family conflict has been shown to act as a stressor that amplifies the negative effects of major stressful life events.17 Another significant observation was the increased social media usage among students during the pandemic. Research confirms that there was a general rise in social media engagement as students sought stress relief, information, and social support.9 However, while social media provided various benefits, such as coping strategies and pandemic updates, excessive use was also linked to adverse mental health outcomes, particularly an increase in depression.18,19

Additionally, most students from both universities felt that appropriate social distancing rules were in place. The South African government’s stringent guidelines to promote social distancing and curb the virus spread—mandating mask-wearing, physical distancing, maximum venue capacities, and the use of alcohol sanitizers—were deemed adequate.20

The COVID-19 pandemic had a profound disruption on various aspects of personal functioning, notably reducing overall productivity among students. The COVID-19 pandemic significantly disrupted students’ productivity and personal functioning through a range of interconnected factors.21 During the pandemic, many students reported increased personal household responsibilities, such as meal preparation and cleaning, which interfered with their academic responsibilities. Students who lived with or moved back in with their parents contended with unique challenges such as varying parental education levels, crowded conditions, limited access to functional devices, and inadequate internet connectivity.22 They also had to manage academic responsibilities alongside home responsibilities, which included dealing with interpersonal conflicts and financial troubles.23

Students’ sleep patterns were also affected, with many reporting an increase in sleeping hours during the pandemic. Various studies confirmed this trend, noting that sleep time generally increased during lockdown periods.24,25,26 Trakada et al26 highlighted that individuals younger than 25 years old reported longer sleeping hours, attributed to higher anxiety levels.27 Interestingly, while some studies reported increased sleep, findings in Brazil showed a decline in sleep duration linked to heightened fear and anxiety due to the high number of COVID-19 cases and fatalities.

Additionally, a considerable percentage of students had to cope with the death of someone close to them during the pandemic. Qualitative studies revealed that those who lost loved ones were more prone to depression and anxiety.28 There were also slightly higher rates of suicide among younger individuals compared to prepandemic times.29

Financial difficulties were another significant concern, with 43% of students’ families facing monetary challenges during the pandemic. Research in South Africa highlighted how the COVID-19 pandemic worsened financial positions, with reductions in both domestic leisure and business travel impacting local businesses significantly.30,31.

Physical activity levels were also affected, with nearly half of the participants noting decreased activity and a quarter reporting an increase. Reasons for a sedentary lifestyle included disruptions to daily routines, constraints on outdoor activities, and the closure of gyms and parks, coupled with mental fatigue and diminished social interaction.32,33,34 Increased levels of activity may be attributed to trying to alleviate boredom during lockdown, reducing the stress related to the pandemic.35

The initial psychological disruption of the COVID-19 pandemic on university students can be understood through the reported increase in anxiety, decreased motivation, and difficulty in concentrating during the lockdown period. University students were already identified as vulnerable to mental health issues even before the pandemic, and their susceptibility intensified during this period.36,37,38,39 The heightened level of self-reported anxiety observed in this study, where 92.02% of Chiropractic respondents reported experiencing anxiety, significantly surpasses prepandemic levels found in South African students, which stood at 20.80%. 40

Various studies have echoed these findings, indicating that the psychological ramifications on students were not geographically isolated. For example, in Pretoria, South Africa, a study revealed that a third of students faced psychological challenges during lockdown, with substantial portions experiencing anxiety and depression.41 Similar patterns were observed in Bangladesh,42 where 97.00% of students reported anxiety, and comparable psychological symptoms were recorded among students in countries such as Pakistan, Malaysia, Egypt, and China.43,44,45,46 The stressors contributing to this anxiety included economic strain, academic delays, and disruptions to daily life, while increased social support was shown to help mitigate these effects.46

Despite the psychological toll, the majority of students did not turn to increased substance use, although 36.70% reported higher alcohol consumption, and 18.08% reported smoking more cigarettes. The lockdown government-imposed bans on alcohol and cigarettes in South Africa created a black-market scenario where students managed to procure these substances, correlating with studies showing escalated substance use and severe mental health issues.47 The dependency on such substances as coping mechanisms only aggravated overall mental well- being, impacting daily functionality and exacerbating depressive symptoms.48

The participants in the current study who reported experiencing heightened anxiety during the lockdown period was also more inclined to indicate increased occurrences of low mood and difficulties in controlling their thoughts compared to their usual state. Moreover, those who experienced more frequent episodes of low mood were also more likely to admit struggling with thought control and lacking motivation to perform tasks compared to their typical levels. Additionally, the study revealed a correlation between students who faced challenges in concentration and a decrease in motivation for task completion. This can be accounted for by the close correlation between anxiety and depression.49

According to the findings, a significant number of students experienced disruptions in their academic progress while in isolation. This disruption is comparable to the decline in productivity observed among employees working from home during the pandemic.50,51 Birmingham et al52 noted that 90.50% of students found it challenging to focus on their schoolwork and 91.00% struggled with motivation to study. While a certain level of stress can enhance learning ability and motivation, excessive stress negatively affects students’ academic performance, leading to decreased marks.53

The Department of Higher Education urged all South African universities to assist students with electronic devices and data during the pandemic. However, studies indicated that not all universities complied, leaving some students without necessary resources.54 Despite this, the majority of students in this study expressed satisfaction with the data provided by their universities and reported having adequate internet connectivity for participating in online classes and assessments.

One significant area disturbed by the pandemic was the development of practical skills. Approximately three-quarters of the students felt that their practical skills were adversely affected. This finding aligns with Lollobrigida et al,13 which concluded that distance learning was an inadequate substitute for hands-on clinical training, essential in medical education.55 To offset these disadvantages, universities such as the UJ and the DUT reduced clinical requirements needed for graduation.

Furthermore, most students experienced a decline in their perception and self-reported academic performance during the pandemic, although about a third felt unaffected. This trend could be attributed to a preference for traditional face-to-face learning over online education.56,57 Decreased motivation also played a significant role. Mahdavi et al58 found that students with better mental health were more motivated, which in turn enhanced their academic performance. On the contrary, recent studies reflect that students may prefer remote classes; however, there is some concern that the increase in remote learning in higher education environments will promote social isolation and stress.59

The return to face-to-face learning on campus brought varying degrees of academic success among students, largely depending on the amount of time they dedicated to their studies during the pandemic. The data suggest that those students who dedicated fewer than 3 hours per day to academic work during the pandemic experienced a decline in their grades upon their return to campus. Conversely, students who engaged in academic activities for more than 5 hours daily were more likely to see their marks either improve or remain stable. This phenomenon can be attributed to empirical evidence demonstrating that individual variations in self-regulatory capacities consistently serve as reliable predictors of academic achievement.60

To contextualize these findings, it is essential to compare these hours to the prepandemic academic commitments required by similar programs. For instance, Chiropractic students at the DUT and the UJ typically spend about 6 hours per day on academic work under normal circumstances. This standard indicates that many students fell short of the expected study time during the pandemic. This shortfall in adherence could explain the difficulty these students had in adjusting back to a more rigorous academic schedule upon their return to campus. Kapasia et al61 supports this notion, reporting that a considerable percentage of students (54.30%) reduced their study time during the pandemic compared to their typical routine.

Moreover, psychological stress induced by the pandemic compounded these issues, further affecting self-perceived academic performance as mentioned above. The self-perceived decline in academic performance among students who spent less than the recommended amount of time studying during the pandemic underscores the importance of adhering to structured academic schedules even in nontraditional learning environments. These findings highlight the necessity for ongoing research and support to address and improve students’ well-being as they continue to deal with the aftermath of the pandemic. It is essential to consider future strategies for improving student support considering the findings from this study. To address psychological distress and bolster academic performance, institutions should actively promote available resources on campus, including counselling services, academic tutoring, and peer support programs. Collaborations with community organizations can also provide students with access to external resources, such as mental health workshops and stress management seminars. To ensure that students can effectively navigate any ongoing challenges, institutions should implement poststudy follow-up initiatives, offering tailored resources and support options based on individual needs. By fostering a supportive environment and ensuring that students are informed of the resources available to them, universities can better equip students to manage the complex challenges they face.

Limitations

Due to the small sample size and predominance of respondents from the DUT, generalizing the results to all Chiropractic students may be challenging. Furthermore, since the study was conducted a few months after COVID-19, it may have impacted memory recall.

Conclusion

This observational study has given insight on the multifaceted self-perceived impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on Chiropractic students in South Africa, encompassing social, personal, psychological and academic components. Students reported heightened levels of anxiety, lowered motivation, and difficulties in managing their academic responsibilities. The study emphasized the importance of addressing not only academic concerns but also the broader psychological and social challenges that students faced during this unprecedented time. This study demonstrates that the COVID-19 pandemic significantly disrupted the academic and psychological well-being of Chiropractic students in South Africa, providing specific insights into areas needing further exploration.
