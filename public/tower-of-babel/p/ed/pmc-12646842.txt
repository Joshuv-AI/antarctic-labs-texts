Oxidative modification of methionine residue in α-synuclein by dopamine induces cellular vulnerability, oligomerization and secretion of α-synuclein
Authors: Yugo Kato, Masahiro Kanatani, Tadashi Adachi, Hidetoshi Nagamine, Yosuke Horikoshi, Kazuhiro Nakaso
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the selective loss of dopamine (DA) neurons and presence of Lewy bodies, with prion-like propagation of α-synuclein (α-syn) also attracting attention recently. However, the specific causes for PD-related pathogenesis, including cell vulnerability and α-syn propagation, occurring only in selective neurons remain unclear. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the interactions between DA and α-syn protein to clarify its effects on α-syn degradation, secretion, and toxicity. We generated PC12 cells expressing human α-syn and M127A mutant in a tetracycline-inducible manner. In these cells, intracellular α-syn levels were controlled via autophagic/lysosomal degradation and secretion to extracellular space. Notably, M127A mutation decreased the intracellular degradation and secretion of α-syn. Using the generated cells, we investigated the association between cell viability and oxidized methionine [Met(O)] in α-syn. We also investigated the effects of Met(O) on α-syn toxicity and stability upon DA induction. Wildtype α-syn overexpression decreased the cell viability, and inhibition of methionine sulfoxide reductase, a methionine sulfoxide-reducing enzyme, further amplified this effect, suggesting that α-syn cytotoxicity is associated with methionine oxidation. Notably, vulnerabilities of M127A mutant cells were lower than those of wildtype α-syn-expressing cells. Overall, our results suggest M127 as the major target for oxidative modification by DA and that this modification is associated with both cell vulnerability and α-syn intracellular stability and secretion in PD pathogenesis.Keywords: oxidative modification, methionine sulfoxide reductase, dopamine, α-synuclein, Parkinson’s disease

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a major neurodegenerative disease characterized by the selective degeneration of dopamine (DA) neurons, formation of α-synuclein (α-syn)-containing Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra, and subsequent loss of terminals in the striatum.(1–3) Further loss of DA causes the most debilitating motor disturbances associated with PD. In addition to DA deficits, peripheral catecholaminergic neurons are disturbed, resulting in severe autonomic disorders.(4–6)

Several genetic risk factors induce and/or enhance PD onset. α-Syn is among the most important molecules associated with the pathogenesis of familial and sporadic PD.(1,7–10) α-Syn, a 140-aa protein, is a major Lewy body component in the brains of patients with PD and dementia with Lewy bodies.(3,11) Pathogenic accumulation of α-syn is observed in the neuropils of the central nerve system, peripheral catecholaminergic neurons, and glial cytoplasmic inclusions in the brains of patients with multiple system atrophy.(3,10–12) Collectively, these α-syn-related pathologies are known as “synucleinopathies”. α-Syn, which is mainly localized in the presynaptic terminals, is a causative molecule for the PARK1 and PARK4 forms of familial PD.(13–17) It plays an important cooperative role with cysteine-string protein-α in protecting the synaptic functions and serves as a protective molecule in non-DA/catecholaminergic cells.(18) However, α-syn acts as a toxic molecule in DA cells.(19) For example, α-syn overexpression due to multiplication of the α-syn gene induces the loss of DA neurons and typical parkinsonism in patients with PARK4 form of PD, and α-syn overexpression with pathogenic mutations causes DA-related cell death in patients with PARK1 form of PD.(15–17) We previously reported that α-syn overexpression in catecholaminergic PC12 cells leads to DA-related vulnerabilities under several toxic conditions, including endoplasmic reticulum stress.(20) However, the exact reason for the disturbance of DA neurons by α-syn overexpression remains unclear. It is possibly because oxidized forms of DA, such as dopamine quinone, interact with α-syn, inducing its oligomerization.(21)

Several post-translational modifications, including phosphorylation, nitration, and glycosylation, have been reported.(22–25) However, these modifications are not specific to DA neurons. Oxidation of methionine residues in α-syn is necessary for its oligomerization in the presence of DA in vitro, and C-terminal region of α-syn loosely interacts with dopachrome, initiating α-syn oligomerization.(26,27) Moreover, we previously reported that the M127 of α-syn is easily oxidized in the presence of DA.(28) Therefore, oxidation of α-syn methionine residues is possibly a key modification for DA neuron-specific vulnerabilities in PD pathogenesis. To investigate the DA-related modifications of α-syn and DA neuron-specific pathogenesis of PD, we established several cell lines expressing mutant forms of α-syn and examined the roles of oxidized methionine [Met(O)] in α-syn oligomerization, cytotoxicity, and secretion.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and antibodies

3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl) 2,5-diphenyl tetrasodium bromide (MTT) were obtained from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Co. (Osaka, Japan). α-methyltyrosine (αMT) was purchased from Pfaltz & Bauer (Waterbury, CT). Nerve growth factor (NGF) was purchased from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA). Tet System Approved fetal bovine serum (FBS) and doxycycline (Dox) were obtained from Clontech Laboratories, Inc. (Palo Alto, CA). Mouse monoclonal antibody against human α-syn was purchased from BD Transduction Laboratories (Lexington, KY). Goat polyclonal antibody against methionine sulfoxide reductase A (MsrA) antibody and Rabbit polyclonal antibodies against β-actin were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX) and Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA), respectively. HRP-linked anti-mouse IgG and anti-rabbit IgG were from Amersham Biosciences (Buckinghamshire, UK).

Establishment of α-syn-expressing cells and cell culture

PC12 cells expressing human α-syn controlled by the Tet OFF system (PC12-TetOFF-α-syn) have been previously established.(20,21) Briefly, cDNA of human α-syn was amplified from the cDNAs raised from human brain tissues using the primer set, 5'-ATGGATGTATTCATGAAAGGACTTTCA-3' and 5'-TTAGGCTTCAGGTTCGTACTCTTG-3', via reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction. The polymerase chain reaction products were subcloned into the pGEM easy vector (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI), and the sequences were confirmed. Human α-syn cDNA was subsequently inserted into the pTRE2 vector (Clontech Laboratories, Inc.). Mutagenesis of M127A mutant cell lines was performed using the pTRE2-wildtype α-syn construct as a template and Site Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Stratagene, La Jolla, CA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The following primer set was used for mutagenesis: 5'-AATGAGGCTTATGAAGCGCCTTCTGAGGAAGGG-3' and 5'-CCCTTCCTCAGAAGGCGCTTCATAAGCCTCATT-3'. During amplification, PC12-TetOff-α-syn cells were maintained in the Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium/F12 supplemented with 5% FBS, 10,000 ‍U/ml penicillin G, 100 ‍mg/ml streptomycin, and 2 ‍ng/ml doxorubicin (DOX) at 37°C and 5% CO2. The culture medium was changed to a DOX-free medium (Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium/F12 with 2.5% Tet System Approved FBS and penicillin G/streptomycin) to induce α-syn expression. Then, 50 ‍ng/ml NGF was added during culture to induce neural differentiation. The culture medium was replaced with a fresh medium to completely remove DOX. The cells were cultured for 1–8 days under DOX-free conditions for subsequent experiments.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was evaluated using the 3-(4,5-dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay following a previously described protocol,(29) with some modifications. For the MTT assay, cells (1 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in a 96-well plate, cultured for 1, 4, or 8 days, and incubated with MTT for 2 ‍h at 37°C. After adding 100 ‍μl of 0.05 ‍N HCl in 2-propanol and mixing thoroughly to dissolve the dark blue crystals, MTT reduction was measured at 570 ‍nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). The data are represented as the percentage of post-treatment recovery (percentage of live cells), and the absorbance of the control and non-treated cells was considered to indicate 100% live cells. To analyze the effects of catecholamines on α-syn cytotoxicity, PC12 cells were cultured with or without αMT, a specific tyrosine hydroxylase inhibitor.

Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, Coomassie brilliant blue (CBB) staining, and immunoblotting

For immunoblotting, cultured cells were harvested from a 6-well culture plate and lysed using the SDS sample buffer (50 ‍mM Tris-HCl, pH ‍6.8, 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 1 ‍mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and 2 ‍mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid). Aliquots (20 ‍μg) were separated by molecular size on 12% polyacrylamide gels, transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Hybond-P; GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL), and hybridized with the required antibodies in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing Tween 20 at room temperature for 1 ‍h. Immunoreactive signals were detected using horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG and ECL detection reagent (GE Healthcare). Subsequently, the polyacrylamide gel was stained with CBB, as previously described.(30) Densitometrical quantification in gels stained by CBB was performed using an ImageJ software system.(31,32)

Immunoprecipitation

Cells for immunoprecipitation were harvested and lysed in IP-lysis buffer (20 ‍mM HEPES, pH ‍7.5, 150 ‍mM NaCl, 1 ‍mM EDTA, 10 ‍μg/ml leupeptin, 1 ‍mM PMSF, 1% Triton-X100, 0.1% SDS). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 ‍min, and the supernatant was transferred to another tube containing prepared Agarose-Protein G (Santa Cruz)-antibody conjugate. The mixture was incubated at 4°C for 12 ‍h with gentle rotation, centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 30 ‍s, and the supernatant was discarded. The Protein A/G-agarose was washed 5 times with IP-lysis buffer and, finally, with IP-final washing buffer (20 ‍mM HEPES, pH ‍7.5, 150 ‍mM NaCl). The washed preparation was analyzed by Western blotting using SDS sample buffer.

Gene silencing

MsrA gene silencing was performed via RNA interference. MsrA and control small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were purchased from Santa Cruz. siRNA transfection was performed using the siRNA Reagent System (Santa Cruz), according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Immunofluorescent and immunochemical staining

For immunofluorescence staining, 4% paraformaldehyde-fixed PC12-TetOFF-α-syn cells were washed with PBS and incubated with an ice-cold solution containing 95% ethyl alcohol with 1% acetic acid for 30 ‍min at 4°C for permeabilization. After washing with PBS twice, the cells were incubated with antibodies in PBS containing 0.02% Tween 20 overnight at 4°C. Then, the cells were washed twice with PBS containing 0.02% Tween 20 and incubated with fluorescein isothiocyanate or Texas red-conjugated secondary antibodies (Santa Cruz) at room temperature. After 4 ‍h, the cells were washed twice with PBS containing 0.02% Tween 20 and covered using the Antifade Mounting Medium (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA). Immunohistochemical signals were detected via fluorescence microscopy.

Neuropathological examination of the brain tissues of patients with PD was performed as previously described.(33) Brain autopsies were conducted with consent from the next of kin.

Exosome isolation

After inducing α-syn using PC12-TetOFF-α-syn or PC12-TetOFF-α-syn (M127A) under Tet-OFF conditions for eight days, the medium was collected after 24 ‍h without any replacement. After removing the debris via centrifugation at 1,000 × g for 5 ‍min, the medium was passed through a 0.22-μm filter to obtain the crude exosome fraction. The medium was centrifuged again at 100,000 × g for 90 ‍min, and the crude exosome fraction was collected for analysis.

Statistical analyses

Quantitative data were assessed via Student’s t test or one-way analysis of variance, followed by the Tukey’s honest significant difference test, using the EZR software. Statistical significance was set at p<0.01 for the cell viability data and p<0.05 for the semi-quantitative immunoblotting data. All values are expressed as the mean ± SE of the mean.

Results

α-Syn causes cellular vulnerability under DA metabolism

We cultured PC12-TetOFF-α-syn and PC12-TetOFF-α-syn (M127A) cells under Tet-OFF conditions to induce α-syn expression. Inducible α-syn expression was observed after three days under Tet-OFF conditions, with considerable α-syn expression observed on day 7 (Fig. 1A).

Catecholamine-associated cytotoxicity of α-synuclein (α-syn) and methionine mutant α-syn in PC12 cells. (A) Western blotting of wildtype (wild) and mutant (M127A) α-syn expression levels in TetOff α-syn cells. Removal of doxorubicin (DOX) from the medium induced α-syn expression after 3–7 days. β-Actin expression was used as an internal control. (B, C) Cell viability was assessed using the 3-(4,5-dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay (B: wildtype α-syn; C: M127A α-syn). (B) Wildtype α-syn expression after DOX removal (OFF) reduced the cell viability compared to that under α-syn-suppressed conditions (ON); however, inhibition of catecholamine metabolism by α-methyl-p-tyrosine (αMT; 100 ‍μM) markedly blocked this effect. *p<0.01, one-way ANOVA followed Tukey’s HSD test. (C) M127A mutant α-syn expression did not reduce the cell viability compared to that under α-syn-suppressed conditions (ON), and no impact of αMT (100 ‍μM) was detected. N.S., not significant.

Upon wildtype α-syn induction, cell viability started to decrease on day 4 and dropped to nearly 50% on day 8. However, even under wildtype α-syn expression, no decrease in cell viability was observed when DA metabolism was suppressed by αMT (Fig. 1B). We performed the same experiment using M127A mutant cells and observed no decrease in cell viability upon α-syn induction, irrespective of DA metabolism (Fig. 1C).

MsrA protects PC12 cells against DA-associated α-syn toxicity

We confirmed MsrA localization in PC12-TetOFF-α-syn cells via immunofluorescence staining. MsrA was co-localized with α-syn in both the intracellular aggregates and cytosolic area (Fig. 2A).

Methionine sulfoxide reductase (MsrA) protects against dopamine-associated α-syn toxicity in PC12 cells. (A) α-syn and MsrA were co-localized in PC12-TetOFF-α-syn cells under dopamine metabolism. (B, C) Immunoprecipitation using anti-MsrA antibodies. (B) Coomassie brilliant blue (CBB) staining. Several bands were observed (arrow head) under α-syn expression; however, these bands were faint under α-syn expression and catecholamine (CA) inhibition (with αMT). The bands indicated by the arrowheads were positive for α-syn. *Non specific band. (C) CBB staining. M127A mutant cells showed fewer precipitated bands than the wildtype cells. *Non specific band. (D) MsrA knockdown enhanced α-syn toxicity under catecholamine metabolism. *p<0.05 (one-way ANOVA followed Tukey’s HSD test). (E) In M127A mutant cells, vulnerability was not enhanced by MsrA knockdown. See color figure in the on-line version.

Next, we performed immunoprecipitation using anti-MsrA antibodies to confirm the direct interactions between MsrA and α-syn. Although α-syn co-precipitated with MsrA under catecholamine metabolism, it did not co-precipitate with MsrA when catecholamine metabolism was inhibited by αMT. In SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis with CBB staining, co-precipitated α-syn was observed mainly as a trimer, with dimers or monomers also observed. Precipitated α-syn was also detected using anti-α-syn antibodies (Fig. 2B). We also examined the interactions of α-syn with M127A mutant and wildtype α-syn using PC12-TetOFF-α-syn (M127A) cells. Immunoprecipitation using anti-MsrA antibodies mainly revealed co-precipitation of wildtype α-syn with MsrA, whereas the precipitation of M127A mutant α-syn was quantitatively low. The signal of M127A α-syn trimer was 34.8% more faint than that of wildtype α-syn trimer. When catecholamine metabolism was suppressed by αMT, neither the wildtype nor the M127A mutant co-precipitated with MsrA (Fig. 2C).

We hypothesized that MsrA exerts cytoprotective effects by inhibiting methionine oxidation of α-syn. We used RNA interference to confirm the effect of MsrA on catecholamine-associated α-syn vulnerability. PC12-TetOFF-α-syn cells showed vulnerability upon α-syn induction (OFF) and treatment with CTL siRNA under catecholamine metabolism; however, cellular vulnerability was not observed under catecholamine inhibition by αMT. Therefore, α-syn induction (OFF) and RNA interference with MsrA enhanced the catecholamine-associated α-syn vulnerability (Fig. 2D). In contrast, PC12-TetOFF-α-syn (M127A) cells did not show vulnerability, even in the presence of catecholamines, and RNA interference with MsrA did not affect their viability (Fig. 2E).

Intracellular α-syn level are controlled via autophagic/lysosomal degradation of DA cells

As PC12-TetOFF-α-syn cells control the de novo expression of α-syn under Tet-OFF/ON conditions, once protein expression is induced sufficiently under Tet-OFF conditions and subsequently switched to ON conditions, the de novo expression of α-syn stops, and its degradation can be tracked. After expressing α-syn under Tet-OFF conditions for seven days, the cells were switched to ON conditions to examine the α-syn degradation mechanisms (Fig. 3A).

Intracellular α-syn levels are controlled via autophagic/lysosomal degradation under dopamine metabolism. (A) Schematic diagram of the experimental protocol. Red line indicates the Tet-ON conditions (suppressed), and blue line indicates the Tet-OFF conditions (induced). After α-syn induction for seven days, the culture was switched to Tet-ON conditions using several compounds. Lacta (1 ‍μM), lactacystin (proteasome inhibitor); 3MA (5 ‍mM), 3-methyladenine [autophagy and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) inhibitor]; Rapa (200 nM), rapamycin (autophagy activator). (B) In PC12-TetOFF-α-syn cells under dopamine metabolism, lactacystin did not inhibit α-syn degradation. In contrast, 3MA inhibited α-syn degradation, whereas rapamycin activated autophagic α-syn degradation. (C) In PC12-TetOFF-α-syn cells under dopamine inhibition, both lactacystin and 3MA did not inhibit α-syn degradation. (D) In PC12-TetOFF-α-syn (M127A) cells under DA metabolism, neither lactacystin nor 3MA inhibited α-syn degradation. See color figure in the on-line version.

After two days under ON conditions, the amount of α-syn protein decreased; however, proteasome inhibitor lactacystin did not inhibit α-syn degradation. In contrast, autophagy inhibitor 3-methyladenine (3MA) inhibited, whereas autophagy promoter rapamycin enhanced α-syn degradation (Fig. 3B).

When the same experiment was repeated under catecholamine inhibition by αMT, the difference between the proteasome system and autophagy in terms of α-syn degradation was not conspicuous (Fig. 3C). Similarly, no difference between the proteasome system and autophagy was observed when M127A mutant α-syn was degraded in PC12-TetOFF-α-syn (M127A) cells under the same experimental conditions (Fig. 3D).

Extracellular α-syn secretion is enhanced under DA metabolism

Next, we examined extracellular α-syn secretion in the presence and absence of catecholamines (Fig. 4). In the presence of catecholamines, a large amount of α-syn was detected in the culture medium. In contrast, α-syn secretion was suppressed under catecholamine inhibition by αMT, even with high intracellular α-syn expression. To examine the relationship between Met127 and extracellular secretion of α-syn in the presence of catecholamines, we examined α-syn secretion using PC12-TetOFF-α-syn (M127A). Even in the presence of catecholamines, only a small amount of α-syn was detected, which was further decreased upon the addition of αMT (Fig. 4A).

Inhibition of dopamine metabolism by αMT suppressed α-syn secretion, even under high α-syn expression in cells. In the culture medium of PC12-TetOFF-α-syn (M127A) cells, a small amount of α-syn was detected, even under high α-syn expression in cells. We also examined whether α-syn secretion occurred via exosomes. The amount of α-syn in exosomes was small, and it was further reduced upon the addition of αMT. In contrast, a large amount of α-syn was detected in the culture medium without exosomes, which was reduced upon the addition of αMT (Fig. 4B).

MsrA is a Lewy body component, especially in DA neurons

As PC12-TetOFF-α-syn cells showed co-localization of α-syn and MsrA under DA metabolism, we examined whether MsrA is a Lewy body component (Fig. 5). MsrA was densely localized in the Lewy bodies of DA neurons in the substantia nigra (Fig. 5A). MsrA was also observed in the cortical Lewy bodies of non-DA neurons in the cerebral cortex, with a relatively low density compared to that in DA neurons (Fig. 5B).

MsrA is a Lewy body component, especially in dopaminergic neuron. (A) In dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, MsrA was densely localized in the Lewy bodies. (B) In non-dopaminergic neurons in the cerebral cortex, only faint MsrA signals were observed in the cortical Lewy bodies.

Discussion

PD is classically characterized by the pathological loss of DA neurons and appearance of Lewy bodies, the main component of which is α-syn.(1–3) Interestingly, DA neuronal loss is also observed in familial parkinsonism caused by genetic α-syn gene duplication,(34,35) suggesting a pathological connection between DA and α-syn.

We previously demonstrated that α-syn overexpression induces neuronal cell death in a DA metabolism-dependent manner using α-syn-overexpressing cells under TetOFF conditions.(20,21,28) Specifically, we suggested the importance of the oxidation of the methionine residue at position 127 near the C-terminus of α-syn in a DA-dependent manner to form methionine sulfoxide.(28) Additionally, we showed that the tyrosine residue at position 125 and serine residue at position 129 in the vicinity promote the oxidative modification of methionine at position 127.(28)

In this study, we found that the cytotoxicity, intracellular degradation, and extracellular secretion of α-syn were all affected by DA metabolism. Oxidation of the sulfur atom of methionine residue forms methionine sulfoxide, and this is an important post-translational modification for α-syn cytotoxicity; however, four types of methionine sulfoxide reductases act as a defense system in the human body.(36,37) MsrA is distributed in many cell types but mainly present in the cytoplasm.(38) It is abundant in neurons.(39) Here, we focused on MsrA and found that it protected against α-syn cytotoxicity. MsrA bound to α-syn in a DA metabolism-dependent manner in PC12 cells; however, its binding to the M127A mutant was limited even in the presence of DA metabolism (Fig. 2B and C). These findings strongly suggest that Met127 oxidation of α-syn in the presence of DA causes cytotoxicity and that MsrA protects against this cytotoxicity. Mammals have three other types of methionine sulfoxide reductase B (MsrBs) with different distributions, and possibility that they might be partially involved in this phenomenon can not be ruled out.(37–39)

Next, we investigated the relationship between α-syn degradation and DA metabolism. In our previous study, when DA metabolism was inhibited in PC12 cells, degradation of various proteins shifted depending on the ubiquitin–proteasome or autophagy/lysosome system.(30) Our established PC12-TetOFF-α-syn cells are useful for investigating α-syn degradation systems as de novo expression of α-syn is abolished by inducing α-syn expression under Tet-OFF conditions and subsequently restoring it by switching to Tet-ON conditions. Using these cells, we investigated the effects of proteasome and autophagy inhibitors on α-syn. α-Syn was mainly degraded via autophagy under DA metabolism (Fig. 3B). However, inhibition of DA metabolism by αMT and M127A mutant reduced the dependence of α-syn degradation on autophagy (Fig. 3C and D). This highlights the importance of the oxidative modification of Met127 for α-syn degradation via autophagy. The difference of protein stability depending on whether or not M127 is oxidized in the presence of DA may also be related to the difference in vulnerability due to differences in α-syn expression levels.

We also investigated the relationship between extracellular α-syn secretion and DA metabolism/methionine oxidation modification. As per Braak’s hypothesis, α-syn-related lesions spread throughout the brain; specifically, α-syn spreads among cells in a prion-like manner.(40) We assessed extracellular α-syn secretion with and without DA metabolism using our established cell lines. A large amount of wildtype α-syn was detected in the cell culture medium; however, α-syn secretion into the medium was drastically reduced when DA metabolism was inhibited by αMT (Fig. 4A). Similarly, when the M127A mutant α-syn was used, mutant α-syn secretion into the medium was low, even in the presence of DA, and was further reduced by DA inhibition. We further investigated whether the DA-dependent extracellular secretion of α-syn is mediated by exosomes (Fig. 4B). Notably, the majority of extracellular α-syn secretion associated with DA metabolism occurred in an exosome-independent manner.

α-Syn is the most important pathological molecule in PD, and various modifications, such as phosphorylation, nitration, and L-dopa substitution of tyrosine, are related to increased PD pathogenesis.(22–25,41) Although oxidative modification of methionine is also caused by mitochondria-derived reactive oxygen species, it is increased in the presence of DA, which explains the high prevalence of PD lesions in DA neurons (Fig. 5).
