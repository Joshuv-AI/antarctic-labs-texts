Identification of multifunctional T-cell peptide epitopes for the development of DNA vaccines against dengue virus
Authors: Ningze Sun, Zhiwei Su, Chenghong Yin, Xiaoyan Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDengue virus (DENV) is an important arthropod-borne virus that poses a global health threat, with half of the world’s population at risk of infection. Currently, there is a lack of safe and effective vaccines for its prevention. Antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) occurs when cross-reactive antibodies fail to neutralize heterologous DENV serotypes effectively, facilitating viral entry into Fc receptor-bearing cells and leading to more severe disease. It is reported that multifunctional T cells are closely related to the protective effects of the vaccine. We selected 25 peptide candidates based on predictions from the IEDB database and relevant literature. These peptides were validated to stimulate the production of multifunctional T cells. The DNA sequences of the corresponding peptides were cloned into the pVAX1 vector, and monovalent DNA vaccines for DENV 1–4 were constructed. We analyzed cellular immunity, symptom scores, body weight changes, and survival rates after DENV challenge with the identical immunizing strain.Our studies confirmed that DNA vaccines can protect mice against DENV challenge. Compared with control groups, mice immunized with our DNA vaccines demonstrated better immune protection after being challenged with the corresponding strain of DENV. Our studies provide a basis for the development of new DENV tetravalent vaccines.KEYWORDS: Dengue virus, multifunctional T cells, DNA vaccine, immunoprotection, cellular immunity

Introduction

Dengue fever, a mosquito-borne infection caused by dengue virus (DENV), poses a significant public health challenge worldwide.1 DENV is primarily transmitted by Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus2 and comprises four different serotypes (DENV1–4),3 all of which cause dengue fever ranging from mild to severe dengue hemorrhagic fever and dengue shock syndrome,4 and their co-circulation complicates vaccine development. In addition, a potential fifth serotype (DENV-5) has been reported, which may further complicate vaccine design, although its epidemiological significance remains under investigation.5 According to a recent WHO report, more than 7.6 million dengue cases and over 3,000 deaths had been reported as of April 2024.6 In comparison, the total number of cases in 2023 reached nearly 5 million, with over 5,000 associated deaths.7 In China, both imported and indigenous dengue cases were documented between 2005 and 2020.8

Currently, due to the absence of widely recognized effective anti-dengue virus (DENV) drugs, vaccine prevention strategies are crucial for controlling and preventing DENV infections.9 Many dengue vaccines have passed the initial evaluation phases, but only two have been approved for public use.10 CYD-TDV (Dengvaxia®) was the first dengue vaccine to be marketed, marking a new milestone in the fight against dengue infection.11 However, its ineffective protection against DENV2 and the risk of exacerbation in specific age groups limited its use.12 The WHO recommended that the CYD-TDV vaccine be used only in individuals with a history of preexisting dengue infection, based on these risks.13 Comparatively, the vaccine TAK-003, developed by Takeda Pharmaceutical Company in Japan, demonstrated broad protection against different dengue virus serotypes and showed good safety and efficacy regardless of previous DENV exposure.14–16 Both of them are live attenuated vaccines, and most vaccines against dengue viruses currently in late-stage development are based on live-attenuated viruses, which consist of mutant chimeric forms of dengue virus serotypes.17–20 Additionally, there are inactivated vaccines, recombinant subunit vaccines, and viral vector vaccines.21 The primary function of these vaccines is to provide immunoprotection by stimulating a neutralizing antibody response.22 However, immunization strategies that rely on neutralizing antibodies are not without risks, particularly the issue of ADE, which usually occurs when an individual previously infected with DENV is re-exposed to a virus of a different serotype, and may exacerbate the disease or even raise the risk of death due to the inability of antibodies to effectively neutralize a different viral serotype despite cross-reactivity, which may be caused by low binding affinity or interaction with epitopes that are not critical for viral entry.23 Therefore, the ADE issue is crucial during vaccine development.

Vaccine developers are now focusing on designing vaccines that circumvent the risk of ADE. This strategy focuses on reducing reliance on neutralizing antibodies while enhancing the vaccine-induced T-cell immune response to provide safer and more comprehensive immunoprotection.24,25

Dengue viruses have four distinct serotypes, adding complexity to vaccine development. Therefore,to be effective against all serotypes, a vaccine must provide broad protection. However, existing vaccine candidates face challenges in ensuring full protection and safety, particularly concerning ADE risk. Thus, the development of a novel vaccine capable of avoiding ADE and providing a solid protective barrier is urgent.

The present study draws on significant advances in the field of T-cell immunology,26–28 particularly in understanding the critical role of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells in DENV infection. Studies have shown that these T cells play a dual role in providing immune protection: they prevent virus transmission by directly destroying infected cells and secrete pro-inflammatory factors that modulate the body’s immune response.27 Based on these findings, vaccine design has begun to focus on T-cell epitopes, with DNA vaccines showing great potential due to their unique advantages. DNA vaccines, being a simple and effective technology with advantages such as low cost, ease of production, and storage stability, can overcome the barrier of ADE through the balanced and prolonged expression of immunity to all four DENV serotypes.29

DNA vaccines trigger a cellular immune response by directing the intracellular production of pathogen-specific proteins, thereby more efficiently mimicking the way antigens are presented in natural infections.30 They not only promote T-cell activation but also lead to the formation of antibodies, resulting in more comprehensive immune protection. To realize the full potential of DNA vaccines, it is critical to accurately identify T-cell epitopes that trigger a strong and long-lasting immune memory, a step essential for successful vaccine development.

In this study, we developed an innovative DNA vaccine that integrates key T-cell epitopes from all four DENV serotypes. The design of this vaccine focuses on optimizing the presentation of T-cell epitopes to enhance their immunoreactivity and stimulate a cellular immune response, achieving protection against multiple serotypes. We selected T-cell epitopes based on the IEDB database and published literature,31 and validated them using ELISPOT assays, flow cytometry, and preclinical studies to evaluate their protective effects.This study provides key insights for dengue vaccine development and a valuable scientific asset for understanding the role of T-cell immunity in viral diseases and how these immune mechanisms can be more effectively utilized to design interventions. Our strategy aims to circumvent ADE and directly promote T-cell-mediated immune protection mechanisms while promising broad serotype protection and durable immune memory. By evaluating this multifunctional DNA-based T-cell epitope vaccine design and its immunoprotective efficacy, we explored novel vaccine strategies against the global dengue virus threat.

Methods

Database search and peptide synthesis

The selected T-cell epitopes were primarily identified from published literature,31 with corresponding entries available in the IEDB database, ensuring consistency and verifiability. Peptides capable of eliciting T-cell responses against DENV1–4 were synthesized: six DENV1 peptides (two CD4+, four CD8+), five DENV2 peptides (three CD4+, two CD8+), six DENV3 peptides (three CD4+, three CD8+), and eight DENV4 peptides (three CD4+, five CD8+) (Table 1).

Epitope prediction and peptide selection were conducted using the IEDB Analysis Resource (https://www.iedb.org).32 The selected peptides were synthesized and further validated using IFN-γ ELISPOT and intracellular cytokine staining (ICS) to confirm T cell responsiveness.

Animals and ethics statement

Six-week-old female BALB/c mice (Vital River Laboratories, Beijing, China) were maintained in a specific pathogen-free environment. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University, and the Institute of Biophysics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (approval numbers: 20–2023, ABSL-2-2022015).

Humane endpoints were strictly observed: mice exhibiting severe neurological symptoms, weight loss exceeding 20%, or moribund condition were humanely euthanized via carbon dioxide (CO₂) inhalation followed by cervical dislocation.

Viruses and cells

The DENV-1 (Hawaii strain) (Accession Number: NC_001477); DENV-2 (New Guinea strain) (Accession Number: NC_001474); DENV-3 (H87 strain) (Accession Number: NC_001475), and DENV-4 (H241 strain) (Accession Number: NC_002640) were propagated in Aedes albopictus C6/36 cells (ATCC CRL-1660). Viral titers were determined by plaque assay on Vero cells (ATCC CRL-1586). Vero cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂, using Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. C6/36 cells were maintained at 28°C in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS.

ELISPOT assay

The cytokine IFN-γ secreted by splenocytes was captured by antibodies and visualized as spots via a colorimetric reaction, following the manufacturer’s (BD, USA) recommendations. 96-well filtration plates (Millipore, USA) were coated with capture antibodies at 4°C overnight, then blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin at 37°C for 2 hr. Splenocytes from immunized mice were aliquoted at 5 × 105 cells/well and stimulated with purified DENV1–4 particles (5 μg/well) and specific peptides (10 µg/mL), or concanavalin A (ConA, Sigma-Aldrich; 0.5 μg/well) as a positive control, at 37°C for 48 hr. RPMI 1640 medium alone was used as a negative control. After incubation with a biotinylated detection antibody, spots were visualized using streptavidin-HRP and AEC substrate and counted with an ELISPOT analyzer (CTL, USA).

Flow cytometry analysis

All antibodies for flow cytometry were obtained from BD Biosciences, USA. 2 × 10^6 splenic lymphocytes were blocked with rat anti-mouse CD16/CD32 monoclonal antibody for 30 minutes. Cells were then surface-stained with anti-CD3-BV650, anti-CD4-BV510, anti-CD8-perCP-Cy5.5, anti-IL-2-BV785, anti-TNF-α-PE, anti-IFN-γ-FITC, anti-MIP-1β-APC, and anti-CD107a-BV421. For intracellular cytokine staining, splenocytes were stimulated with 1 μg of purified DENV1–4 particles for 24 hr, followed by brefeldin A (10 μg/mL) for the last 6 hr. Cells were then fixed and permeabilized using the BD Cytofix/Cytoperm kit and stained for intracellular markers. For analysis, splenocytes were stimulated with 5 µg/mL synthetic peptides and purified DENV particles (MOI = 1). Stained cells were analyzed on a DxFLEX flow cytometer using FlowJo software (v10.8.1).

Immunization and electro-transfection

The pVAX1 vector (Invitrogen, USA) was used as the backbone plasmid for cloning synthetic DNA sequences encoding multiple T-cell epitopes specific to each dengue virus serotype. This vector is widely used in DNA vaccine development due to its FDA compliance and low immunogenicity backbone design.29,33 The resulting monovalent vaccine plasmids – pVAX1-DV1, pVAX1-DV2, pVAX1-DV3, and pVAX1-DV4—were used for immunization.

Six-week-old female BALB/c mice were immunized with one of the four vaccine plasmids, and mice receiving the empty pVAX1 vector served as the negative control. Immunization was via intramuscular injection followed by electroporation (EP) using two needle electrodes spaced 6 mm apart delivering six electric pulses. Immunizations were administered on days 0, 21, and 42 with 100 μg of plasmid per dose in sterile saline. Two weeks after the final immunization, mice were challenged intracerebrally with lethal doses of DENV: 1 × 105 PFU of DENV1 (Hawaii strain), 200 PFU of DENV2 (Tr1751 strain), 1 × 107 PFU of DENV3 (H87 strain), or 1 × 105 PFU of DENV4 (H241 strain)34 (Figure 1).

Immunization and challenge timeline.

Each group of mice was challenged with the same DENV serotype used for immunization. For example, DENV1-immunized mice were challenged with the DENV1 (Hawaii strain), DENV2-immunized mice with DENV2 (NGC strain), and so forth. No mixed-serotype challenge was performed. The term “DENV1–4” refers to four parallel vaccine groups, each challenged with their corresponding homologous serotype.

Mice received the initial immunization at week 0, followed by booster immunizations at weeks 3 and 6. Spleen cells were harvested one week after the final booster immunization (at week 7) to evaluate the immune response induced by the vaccine. Subsequently, at week 8, the mice were challenged with the virus to assess the protective efficacy of the vaccine.

Evaluation of protective effect

To assess the immunoprotective effect of vaccination, mice were intracranially challenged with a lethal dose of the corresponding DENV serotype two weeks after the final immunization. Body weight, clinical signs, and mortality were monitored daily for 30 days. Clinical signs – including activity level, posture (e.g., hunching), fur texture (e.g., ruffling), and neurological symptoms such as hind-limb paralysis – were evaluated once daily by two blinded observers. Each parameter was scored on a 0–3 scale (0 = none, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = severe), and the total clinical score per mouse was calculated by summing all parameter scores. For graphical comparison (e.g., in Figure 7), a simplified 0–5 scale was also used: 0 = asymptomatic; 1 = ruffled fur; 2 = hunched back or listlessness; 3 = reduced mobility or hind-limb paralysis; 4 = quadriplegia or moribund state; 5 = death. Mice consistently receiving a score of 0 across all evaluated parameters throughout the observation period were classified as asymptomatic. These animals exhibited no detectable signs of reduced activity, hunching, fur ruffling, or neurological symptoms. This classification was based exclusively on clinical observation and does not account for viral load or seroconversion, which may be addressed in future studies.

Data analysis and statistics

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), as indicated in the figure legends or text. Statistical significance (P values) was calculated using SPSS 26.0, with body weight changes analyzed by repeated multivariate analysis of variance. Survival rates were compared using the log-rank test, and group differences were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. Significance levels were set at p < .05 (*), p < .01 (**), and p < .001 (***).

Results

Screening and synthesis of multifunctional T-cell epitope peptides

We conducted comprehensive screening and synthesis of T-cell epitope peptides targeting all four DENV serotypes. Using IEDB and relevant literature,31 we identified six peptides for DENV1, five for DENV2, six for DENV3, and eight for DENV4. We synthesized these peptides and evaluated their immunogenic properties. ELISPOT assay revealed that all synthesized peptides elicited robust IFN-γ production by mouse splenocytes, indicating their immunogenic potential (Table 1, Figure 2).

The multifunctionality of T cell responses was investigated in six-week-old female BALB/c mice, with five mice per group, following the final immunization. The experimental groups were stimulated with six peptides and compared to corresponding control groups. CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses were assessed using Intracellular Cytokine Staining to evaluate the magnitude and diversity of the responses.

For CD4+ T cell responses, the experimental group exhibited a higher proportion of multifunctional T cells among cytokine-producing cells compared to the control group. Specifically, the proportion of CD4+ multifunctional T cells was increased from 31.06% to 43.06%, 33.21% to 42.11%, 33.07% to 43.72%, 31% to 51.78%, 32.03% to 35.1%, and 30.24% to 43.03% for each respective peptide when compared to their controls. This enhancement in CD4+ T cell multifunctionality indicates a stronger and more diverse response induced by the peptides.

Similarly, CD8+ T cell responses in the experimental groups showed an increased proportion of multifunctional T cells compared to the controls. The percentages of multifunctional CD8+ T cells rose from 37.65% to 47.08%, 37.98% to 44.89%, 36.76% to 45.74%, 35.95% to 51.6%, 36.65% to 39.47%, and 34.54% to 48.44% for each peptide relative to the corresponding control groups. These findings suggest that the peptides induced robust multifunctional CD8+ T cell responses, further contributing to the overall enhancement of T cell immunity.

Notably, the peptide-induced T-cell responses in both CD4+ and CD8+ experimental groups were higher than those in the control groups, resulting in significantly elevated specific multifunctional T-cell responses. These results indicate that the peptides effectively enhance the functionality and diversity of T cell responses, which may be beneficial for developing effective immunization strategies.

Preparation of monovalent DNA vaccine and evaluation of immune response

Peptide fragments capable of stimulating multifunctional T cell production for DENV 1–4 were identified (Table 1), and their corresponding DNA sequences were cloned into the pVAX1 vector to develop monovalent DNA vaccines for DENV 1–4. The resultant vaccine plasmid was transfected into 293T cells.

Six-week-old female BALB/c mice received three immunizations at three-week intervals. The expression of IL-2, TNF-α, IFN-γ, MIP-1β, and CD107a in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells was evaluated using flow cytometry with intracellular staining to determine the induction of multifunctional T cell responses.

All monovalent DNA vaccines targeting DENV 1–4 induced multifunctional T cells in immunized mice. As shown in Figure 4C, the proportion of CD4+ T cells that produced two cytokines (doublepositive) increased markedly after vaccination, whereas the frequency of triplecytokineproducing cells remained essentially unchanged. This pattern indicates that our vaccine preferentially expands an intermediatefunction Tcell subset rather than the highly polyfunctional population, suggesting an early or primary activation stage of the response. The vaccine group showed higher proportions of CD4+ multifunctional T cells compared to controls (25.82% in control vs. 41.58% in DENV1, 45.75% in DENV2, 36.1% in DENV3, 50.2% in DENV4) (Figure 4) and CD8+ multifunctional T cells (6.66% in control vs. 23.56% in DENV1, 27.73% in DENV2, 39% in DENV3, 20.7% in DENV4). Survival rate of mice

Survival was assessed two weeks after the final immunization with five mice per group. The DENV-1 and DENV-3 vaccine and control groups showed 100% survival, likely due to the low virulence of the DENV-1 Hawai and DENV-3 H87 strains. Vaccinated mice for DENV-1 and DENV-3 had less weight loss and lower symptom scores, indicating immune protection. In the DENV-2 vaccine group, one mouse died on day 11 post-challenge, resulting in an 80% survival rate, compared to 20% in the control group (statistically significant, p < .05). For DENV-4, the vaccine group had an 80% survival rate, while the control group had 40%, with no statistical significance (Figure 5).

Changes in body weight in mice

To assess vaccine effects, mice body weights were measured daily post-challenge and expressed as a percentage of their weight on day 0. The DENV1, DENV3, and DENV4 vaccine groups experienced mild to moderate initial weight loss, followed by recovery and weight gain, whereas the DENV2 vaccine group showed significant weight loss but partial recovery. Control groups experienced more severe weight loss and slower recovery. Overall, vaccinated mice exhibited significantly smaller changes in body weight post-challenge compared to controls (p < .001, n = 5) (Figure 6).

Symptom scoring of mice

Following viral challenge, mice were monitored for clinical signs over 21 days using a scoring system from 0 (asymptomatic) to 5 (death). Vaccinated groups exhibited significantly lower symptom scores compared to controls across all DENV groups. For DENV1, the vaccine group had a score of 0.2 versus 1.6 for controls; DENV2 had 1.6 versus 4; DENV3 had 0.4 versus 0.6; and DENV4 had 3 versus 3.8. Overall, vaccinated mice showed better clinical outcomes (p < .001, n = 5) (Figure 7).

Discussion

In this study, we identified a panel of T cell epitopes from all four dengue virus (DENV1–4) serotypes that demonstrated strong immunogenicity, as evidenced by significant induction of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) secretion in ELISPOT assays. Notably, the majority of these epitopes were derived from non-structural (NS) protein regions, which are relatively conserved across serotypes and are not directly implicated in antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE). These findings suggest their potential utility as vaccine antigens for eliciting T cell – mediated protective immunity.

The current challenges facing dengue vaccine development underscore the importance of alternative immunization strategies. Licensed vaccines such as Dengvaxia have demonstrated suboptimal efficacy and increased risk of severe disease in seronegative individuals,35,36 largely due to their reliance on humoral immunity and the associated ADE phenomenon. In contrast, T cell – based vaccine approaches offer several theoretical advantages, including broader cross-reactivity, improved viral clearance, and reduced enhancement risk.37,38 Our findings support the feasibility of this approach by demonstrating that epitope-driven T cell responses can be elicited robustly in vivo.

The predominance of epitopes located within NS proteins is consistent with prior studies highlighting the importance of non-structural antigens in shaping antiviral T cell responses.39–41 These regions tend to be more conserved than structural proteins and less likely to participate in antibody-mediated enhancement, which further supports their suitability for vaccine design. To mitigate the risk of antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE), we prioritized the selection of T-cell epitopes from conserved non-structural proteins, such as NS1 and NS3, rather than structural proteins like prM and E, which are more commonly associated with ADE. While we did not explicitly exclude all known ADE-associated epitopes, our strategy aimed to reduce ADE risk by selecting epitopes reported in the literature to originate from regions less associated with ADE. These peptides were not experimentally evaluated for ADE in this study. Although a comprehensive analysis of epitope conservation across all DENV serotypes was not performed in this study, certain sequence features imply potential cross-reactivity, particularly relevant in light of the emergence of a fifth DENV serotype (DENV-5).

Functionally, the identified peptides significantly activated IFN-γ secretion, indicative of a Th1-biased cellular immune response, which is critical for the control of viral infections. This is particularly relevant in the context of DNA vaccination, where antigen processing and presentation via the MHC class I and II pathways facilitate the activation of CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and CD4+ helper T cells, respectively.42,43 Preliminary data from our plasmid-based DNA vaccine platform suggest that inclusion of these epitopes, coupled with electroporation delivery, further enhances immunogenicity, providing a promising avenue for multivalent vaccine development.

Although a universally accepted threshold for defining biologically meaningful increases in multifunctional T cell responses is lacking, previous studies have suggested that increases of approximately 10–15% or more are generally considered significant in ex vivo immunological assays.44,45 In our dataset, the magnitude of response varied across the 25 peptides tested. In terms of individual peptides, we observed marked increases in 9 out of 25 peptides, moderate increases in 8 out of 25, and no change or a decrease in the remaining 8 peptides. This distribution highlights the heterogeneity of peptide-specific responses and provides a more nuanced perspective than group-level averages alone.

These peptide-level observations indicate that while the overall group-level trends were statistically significant, individual peptide contributions differed in magnitude. This supports the rationale for selecting a multi-epitope vaccine strategy and underscores the importance of balanced T cell epitope inclusion in vaccine design. Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, immunogenicity was assessed exclusively in BALB/c mice, which do not fully replicate the MHC diversity and immune complexity of the human population.46 Second, the definition of “polyfunctional T cells” in this study refers to the ability to secrete multiple cytokines upon primary stimulation, whereas classical polyfunctionality also includes the presence of memory phenotypes and sustained functional capacity.47 Longitudinal studies will be necessary to determine whether the induced responses transition into long-lived memory T cell populations.

While some epitopes appeared capable of eliciting CD8+ T cell responses, we did not perform cytotoxicity assays or granzyme B quantification to directly assess their killing potential. Furthermore, several peptides exceeded the canonical 8–10 amino acid length typically presented by MHC class I molecules, implying that intracellular processing may be required for optimal presentation.48 Future work should include epitope truncation and MHC-binding validation experiments to identify the minimal CD8+ core epitopes.

We also cannot exclude the possible contribution of natural killer (NK) cells to the observed cytotoxic responses. Dissecting the relative contributions of CD8+ T cells and NK cells will require immune cell depletion studies using CD8+ or NK1.1 antibodies, along with tetramer staining to confirm antigen specificity.49

Regarding in vivo efficacy, we performed only preliminary assessments using weight loss and clinical scoring, which provide limited insight into the pathophysiology of dengue infection. More comprehensive evaluations, including viral load quantification, vascular permeability assays (e.g., Evans blue dye extravasation or hematocrit changes), and cytokine profiling, will be essential for a thorough assessment of vaccine protection.50 Moreover, we have not yet evaluated cross-serotype protection through heterologous challenge models. Given the risk of ADE, it will be critical to assess protective efficacy in models such as DENV1 immunization followed by DENV2 or DENV3 challenge.51

Importantly, ADE is not unique to dengue virus; similar enhancement mechanisms have been observed in respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), HIV, and other viral infections.52,53 These experiences emphasize the need for vaccine designs that balance humoral and cellular immunity. Our T cell – centric approach to vaccine design may eventually provide a means to address this challenge by conferring genuine protective immunity without exacerbating disease severity.

In summary, our study identified a set of IFN-γ–inducing T cell epitopes from all four DENV serotypes, demonstrating their immunogenic potential in a murine model. While further investigation is required to validate their polyfunctionality, memory formation, cytotoxic capacity, and cross-protective efficacy, these findings offer a solid foundation for the development of a safer, more durable, and broadly protective T cell – based dengue vaccine.
