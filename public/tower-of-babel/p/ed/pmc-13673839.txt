Feasibility and preliminary agreement of a lower-volume, pool-based incremental swimming protocol in trained male university swimmers: a pilot study
Authors: Merve Senol Aydogan, Ani Agopyan, Erdem Uylas, Erkan Tortu, Erkan Gunay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLower-volume pool-based protocols may help coaches and sport scientists monitor selected swimming responses with less disruption to routine training. This pilot study examined the operational feasibility of the Distance and Speed Incremental Swimming Test (DSIST50–100–150–200 m) and its preliminary agreement with the 7 × 200 m incremental step test (IST7 × 200 m) in trained male university swimmers.MethodsTen trained male university swimmers completed pool-based field testing, including the IST7 × 200 m, DSIST50–100–150–200 m, and a supportive 200 m freestyle time trial. The main DSIST50–100–150–200 m –IST7 × 200 m comparison focused on corresponding 200 m efforts. Completion time, swimming velocity, stroke-mechanics variables, highest recorded post-exercise heart rate, post-exercise blood lactate concentration ([La⁻]post), lactate change (Δ[La⁻]), and rating of perceived exertion (RPE) were assessed. Paired comparisons used the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Holm adjustment. Preliminary agreement was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) and Bland–Altman analysis.ResultsMean swimming velocity was close between the DSIST50–100–150–200 m and IST7 × 200 m protocols (1.41 ± 0.12 vs. 1.39 ± 0.12 m·s⁻¹), with no Holm-adjusted evidence of a systematic difference. Swimming velocity showed good preliminary agreement (ICC (2,1) = 0.81) and a small mean bias (0.018 m·s⁻¹; limits of agreement: −0.124 to 0.160 m·s⁻¹). Completion time showed poor agreement (ICC (2,1) = 0.26) and wide limits of agreement (− 30.0 to 14.4 s). Kinematic outcomes showed stronger agreement estimates, with ICC values of 0.81–0.94. Highest recorded post-exercise HR was not significantly different between different between the DSIST50–100–150–200 m and IST7 × 200 m protocols (181.0 ± 3.36 vs. 184.8 ± 6.8 beats·min⁻¹; Holm-adjusted p = 1.00) but showed poor agreement. [La⁻] post and RPE showed moderate agreement, whereas Δ[La⁻] showed poor agreement.ConclusionThe DSIST50–100–150–200 m was feasible and showed preliminary agreement with the IST7 × 200 m for swimming velocity and selected segment-based stroke-mechanics outcomes. Its lower-volume structure may support practical monitoring of selected performance and technical indicators with reduced testing burden. However, weaker agreement for completion time, HRpeak, and lactate-change responses indicates that the DSIST50–100–150–200 m should not be considered a direct substitute for the IST7 × 200 m or a criterion measure of aerobic capacity. Larger studies with repeated trials, counterbalanced test orders, and physiological criterion measures are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-01890-8.Keywords: Swimming performance, Field testing, Incremental swimming test, Stroke mechanics, Bland–Altman analysis, Heart rate, Blood lactate

Introduction

Swimming performance reflects a multifactorial and event-specific interaction among aerobic and anaerobic energy systems, hydrodynamic conditions, anthropometric characteristics, and biomechanical factors. In sprint events, the estimated aerobic contribution increases from approximately 20% in the 50-m event to 40–50% in the 100-m event, while a well-developed aerobic system may also support recovery following maximal efforts [1]. Traditionally, physiological monitoring in competitive swimming has combined laboratory-based oxygen-uptake measurements with poolside blood lactate assessment, often supplemented by heart rate as a complementary indicator, particularly in applied settings where complex laboratory procedures and specialized equipment are less feasible [2].

Blood lactate concentration ([La⁻]) can be assessed poolside and incorporated into incremental swimming tests to monitor metabolic responses across successive stages [2]. Although lactate-threshold velocity can be derived from the velocity–lactate relationship, the resulting estimate depends on the analytical method used and should therefore be interpreted on a method-specific basis [3]. Blood lactate nevertheless represents only one component of a multidimensional assessment. When considered alongside heart rate, perceived exertion, swimming velocity, and stroke-mechanics variables, it can contribute to the characterization of exercise-intensity responses and interpretation of training-intensity responses in applied settings [2].

From a biomechanical perspective, swimming technique is commonly characterized using variables such as stroke rate [SR], stroke length [SL], stroke index [SI], and inter-limb coordination. These variables should not be regarded as fixed technical characteristics because they may change in response to swimming velocity, hydrodynamic drag, and fatigue [4]. Previous studies have examined physiological responses together with kinematic variables to characterize swimmers’ responses to increasing exercise intensity [2, 5, 6]. However, evidence remains limited regarding lower-volume field-based protocols that concurrently assess internal-load, perceptual, performance, and kinematic responses.

Several pool-based approaches are available for evaluating endurance-related swimming performance. Critical swimming speed, derived from the distance–time relationship, is frequently used as a practical field-based indicator [7]. However, it is a mathematically derived estimate rather than a direct physiological criterion, and its value may vary according to the selected distances and calculation method. The 7 × 200-m incremental test (IST7 × 200 m) has also been used to monitor internal-load and stroke-mechanics responses across progressively increasing swimming velocities [6]. Nevertheless, its total swimming distance of 1400 m may represent a greater testing burden when repeated monitoring is required. Shorter alternatives, such as the 400-m time trial, have been investigated; however, differences in swimming velocity indicate that these protocols should not be used interchangeably for training prescription [6].

To address this methodological gap, the present study developed the Distance and Speed Incremental Swimming Test (DSIST50–100–150–200 m) as a lower-volume, distance- and speed-progressive pool-based protocol. Its design was informed by established incremental swimming tests that use small velocity increments, interstage recovery, and external pacing to standardize swimming velocity and facilitate stage-specific measurements [6, 8]. The 50–100–150–200-m sequence, 5% relative velocity progression, and 1:1.5 work-to-recovery ratio were selected to provide a standardized progression in external exercise demand while allowing physiological, perceptual, performance, and kinematic responses to be assessed at each stage. These protocol characteristics should be regarded as pilot design choices rather than previously validated physiological thresholds.

This pilot study aimed to examine the operational feasibility of the DSIST50–100–150–200 m and to compare selected internal-load indicators— highest recorded heart rate (HRpeak) post-exercise blood lactate concentration ([La⁻]post), and lactate change (Δ[La⁻])—rating of perceived exertion (RPE), performance outcomes (swimming time and velocity), and kinematic variables (stroke count [SC], SR, SL, SI, and index of coordination [IdC]) with the corresponding outcomes obtained from the IST7 × 200 m. Systematic between-protocol differences and preliminary agreement were also quantified. It was hypothesized that corresponding outcomes obtained from the two protocols would be positively associated. Because direct criterion measures such as VO₂max, vVO₂max, maximal lactate steady state, and ventilatory thresholds were not assessed, the comparison was intended to examine preliminary agreement between two field-based protocols rather than to establish physiological validity, equivalence, or interchangeability.

Methods

Participants

Ten trained male competitive swimmers aged 19–23 years from a same university swimming team voluntarily participated in this pilot study. An a priori sample-size calculation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) [9]. Because the main comparisons involved paired observations obtained from the same swimmers, the a priori sample-size calculation was performed using a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test. With α = 0.05, 80% power, and a large standardized paired effect size of dz = 0.80, the required sample size was estimated as 15 participants.

However, recruitment was restricted to the eligible swimmers available within the university training group, and the final pilot sample comprised 10 participants. The achieved sample was therefore smaller than the a priori target. The sample-size calculation was intended to support the paired comparisons between protocols and was not designed to ensure precise estimation of intraclass correlation coefficients or Bland-Altman limits of agreement. Accordingly, the agreement analyses were considered exploratory, and the Bland-Altman results were interpreted cautiously in line with methodological recommendations for agreement analysis [10, 11].

All participants were members of the same university swimming team and had at least five years of competitive experience. Performance level was characterized using each swimmer’s best 200-m freestyle time recorded at the 2024 National Swimming Championships and the corresponding World Aquatics points. The mean 200-m freestyle time was 137.47 ± 7.24 s (range: 124.11–146.78 s), and the mean World Aquatics score was 415.02 ± 69.95 points (range: 335.58-555.11 points).

Eligibility criteria included being a male competitive swimmer, participating regularly in structured swimming training, and being free from health conditions that could affect test performance. Exclusion criteria included current musculoskeletal injury, cardiovascular or metabolic disorder, use of medication or recent medical treatment affecting exercise responses, interruption in regular training lasting longer than two weeks during the predefined period, or inability to complete any testing protocol.

Recruitment was limited to trained young adult male swimmers to reduce biological heterogeneity within this small pilot sample, as potential sex-related physiological variability could not be adequately controlled within the study timeframe.

This study was approved by the Marmara University Faculty of Medicine Clinical Research Ethics Committee (09.2022.838) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants included in the study.

Familiarization

One week before the first experimental test, participants completed a 30-min familiarization session covering both swimming protocols (DSIST50−100−150−200 m and IST7 × 200 m). Standardized instructions were provided regarding the test procedures, stage-specific velocity progression, prescribed recovery periods, external pacing procedures, and the Borg CR10 rating of perceived exertion (RPE) scale. Participants then completed brief, non-fatiguing in-water trials representative of both protocols to practice pacing, recovery procedures, and RPE reporting. They were also familiarized with the wireless swimmer-coach communication system (YuzmeFit, Turkiye) used to provide pacing instructions during the experimental tests. The positioning and functioning of all measurement equipment were verified under pool conditions. No experimental data were collected during the familiarization session.

Pre-test standardization

All swimming tests were conducted in the same 25-m indoor pool (25 × 17 m), under standardized environmental conditions. Ambient temperature was maintained at 26 °C, water temperature at 27 °C, and relative humidity at approximately 65%. All testing sessions were performed between 16:00 and 19:00 to minimize the potential influence of diurnal variation. Participants were instructed to avoid strenuous exercise for 24 h before each testing session and to refrain from caffeine, alcohol, and stimulant-containing drinks on testing days [12].

Warm-up and cool-down protocol

Before each swimming test, participants completed the same standardized 25-min warm-up protocol, consisting of approximately 10 min of land-based exercises followed by approximately 15 min of in-water warm-up, adapted from [13].

The land-based warm-up included eight exercises: 25 repetitions of jumping jacks, 20 repetitions of half-arm rotations, 20 repetitions of full-arm rotations, 40 repetitions of standing knee-to-chest pulls, 20 repetitions of squats, 20 repetitions of crunches, 15 repetitions of reverse crunches, and 10 repetitions of jump squats.

The in-water warm-up consisted of 400 m freestyle swimming, followed by 200 m freestyle swimming, and 4 × 50 m freestyle performed at a target pace corresponding to each swimmer’s individual 400-m personal-best pace routinely used in training.

After each testing session, participants completed a standardized 10-min cool-down protocol consisting of 200 m low-intensity freestyle swimming and 200 m low-intensity backstroke swimming.

Study design and testing schedule

This pilot study used a within-participant repeated-measures design. All participants completed the testing procedures in the same fixed order; the sequence was not randomized or counterbalanced. The fixed sequence was used to standardize protocol administration and recovery conditions across participants. All 10 participants completed the three testing sessions, including the post-DSIST50−100−150−200 m 200-m freestyle time trial (200 m TT), and were included in the analyses (Fig. 1).

During the first testing session, resting heart rate and anthropometric measurements were obtained. After the standardized warm-up, participants completed the Critical Swimming Speed Test (CSS50&400 m) [14].

During the second testing session, resting heart rate and baseline blood lactate concentration ([La−]; Lactate Scout+, Leipzig, Germany) were recorded. After the standardized warm-up and an 8-min passive recovery period [15], heart-rate monitors (Polar Electro, Kempele, Finland) were fitted, and participants completed the 7 × 200-m Incremental Step Test (IST7 × 200 m) using the wireless swimmer-coach communication system for external pacing [16].

During the third testing session, participants completed the standardized warm-up, after which individualized base swimming velocity was determined as described in the “Determination of Individual Base Velocity and Stage-Specific Target Times” subsection. The Distance and Speed Incremental Swimming Test (DSIST50−100−150−200 m) was then administered using the same external pacing system.

200 m TT was used as a supportive and exploratory field-based performance comparison and was performed 30 min after completion of the DSIST50−100−150−200 m following passive recovery (Fig. 1). The same-session sequence was used to provide a pragmatic repeated-effort condition rather than to reproduce a specific competition schedule. Because recovery duration and mode may influence blood lactate removal and subsequent swimming performance [17], the 200 m TT was not regarded as an independent criterion-validation test. Residual fatigue and incomplete metabolic recovery following the DSIST50−100−150−200 m were therefore considered potential confounding factors.

The procedure used to calculate individualized target velocities was developed specifically for the present pilot protocol. The general use of progressively increasing, externally paced swimming stages was informed by previously published incremental swimming tests [19, 20]. However, using the completion time obtained at approximately 50 stroke cycles·min− 1 as the individual base-velocity reference and applying a 5% stage-to-stage velocity increase were prespecified, study-specific design choices. Accordingly, these values should not be interpreted as established or previously validated physiological thresholds.

Pacing control and termination criteria

Before each DSIST50−100−150−200 m stage, the prescribed completion time and cumulative target split times for each 25-m interval were calculated. Actual cumulative split times were recorded at every turn and compared with the corresponding targets. Immediate verbal feedback to increase, decrease, or maintain pace was delivered through the wireless swimmer-coach communication system (YuzmeFit, Türkiye). This procedure was used to improve pace control during the protocol and was informed by previous incremental swimming studies highlighting the relevance of externally guided or monitored pacing in swimming tests [19].

Across all participants and stages, the difference between the prescribed and actual stage-completion times did not exceed 5 s. This value represents the pacing accuracy observed in the present study rather than a predefined or universally accepted tolerance threshold. Corrective feedback was provided whenever a deviation from the target split time was detected. The protocol specified that the test would be terminated, without repeating the stage, if the swimmer was unable to re-establish the prescribed pace following corrective feedback. All participants completed the protocol as prescribed; no stage repetition or pacing-related termination occurred.

The DSIST50−100−150−200 m was designed as a fixed-endpoint protocol and was completed after the 200-m stage rather than continued to volitional exhaustion. Participants were permitted to stop the test at any time. Testing was also to be terminated in the presence of acute pain, dizziness, nausea, chest discomfort, unusual dyspnoea, loss of swimming coordination, visible signs of distress, equipment malfunction affecting participant safety, or a decision by the supervising researcher that continuation was unsafe. No voluntary withdrawal or safety-related termination occurred.

Measurements

Anthropometric measurements

Body mass (kg) was measured using a Seca 700 mechanical column scale (Seca North America East, Hanover, MD, USA), and stature (cm) was measured barefoot using a wall-mounted Seca 206 stadiometer/measuring tape (Seca GmbH & Co. KG, Hamburg, Germany), with participants wearing swimsuits. All anthropometric measurements were performed in accordance with the International Society for the Advancement of Kinanthropometry (ISAK) protocol [21]. Body mass index (BMI, kg·m− 2) was calculated as body mass (kg) divided by stature squared (m²) [22]. Sitting height (cm) was measured with the participant seated upright, leg length (cm) was calculated as stature minus sitting height, and arm span (cm) was measured as the distance between the tips of the middle fingers with both arms fully extended horizontally [21]. All measurements were recorded under standardized conditions.

Heart rate, blood lactate, and perceived exertion

HR, blood lactate concentration, and RPE were assessed across the swimming protocols using standardized pre- and post-effort procedures. In the present pilot study, the main physiological and perceptual comparison focused on the corresponding 200-m efforts, namely the final 200-m stage of the IST7 × 200 m protocol and the 200-m stage of the DSIST50−100−150−200 m protocol.

Resting HR was recorded one minute before the commencement of each test under standardized pre-test measurement conditions [23]. HR was monitored using a Polar Verity Sense heart rate monitor (Polar Electro Oy, Kempele, Finland) attached to the swimmers’ goggles. Data were transmitted in real time via Bluetooth using Polar Beat software (v2.4.5, Android), in accordance with previous related HR monitoring procedures [24]. For the analyses, HR values recorded immediately after completion of the corresponding 200-m efforts were used. This timing is consistent with the 7 × 200 m lactate profiling protocol described by [16], in which HR and RPE were assessed immediately after each 200-m swim, followed by capillary blood lactate sampling. The highest value recorded by the device during the final part of the effort and the immediate post-effort period was retained as HRpeak/highest recorded HR rather than HRmax.

Blood lactate concentration was assessed 1 min before the warm-up ([La⁻]pre) and within the first 30 s after completion of each swimming protocol ([La⁻]post), while swimmers remained at the pool edge, in accordance with previous pool-based testing procedures [6, 16]. Capillary blood was obtained from the fingertip using a single-use lactate test strip requiring a 0.2-µL sample and analyzed with an electrochemical lactate-oxidase analyser (Lactate Scout+, SensLab GmbH, Leipzig, Germany). Lactate was not sampled between stages to preserve the prescribed recovery intervals and the field-based structure of the protocols. Therefore, [La⁻]post was interpreted as an immediate post-protocol metabolic response rather than as peak blood lactate concentration or within-exercise lactate kinetics.

Rating of perceived exertion (RPE) was assessed using the Borg Category-Ratio 10 scale (CR10), ranging from 0 (“nothing at all/no exertion”) to 10 (“maximal exertion”) [25, 26]. Swimmers were familiarized with the scale card before testing and, immediately after each relevant swimming effort, marked the value that best represented their overall perceived exertion. For the main DSIST50−100−150−200 m versus IST7 × 200 m comparison, the post-exercise RPE recorded immediately after the final 200 m effort of each protocol was used.

Stroke-mechanics variables included stroke length (SL), stroke count (SC), stroke rate (SR), stroke index (SI), and index of coordination (IdC). These variables were recorded using a surface video camera (GoPro HERO7 Black, San Mateo, CA, USA), following previously described swimming kinematic procedures [4, 27]. SR and SC were measured during the corresponding 200-m stages of the IST7 × 200 m and DSIST50–100–150–200 m protocols and during the last 50 m of the 200-m time trial [6, 16]. These segment-based measurements were interpreted as representative of the analysed segment only and not as whole-test technical behavior. All video-based kinematic analyses were performed by a senior swimming coach with Level 3 coaching certification and experience in competitive swimming performance analysis and stroke-mechanics assessment.

Pool-based test descriptions

Critical Swimming Speed Test (CSS50&400 m)

The Critical Swimming Speed (CSS) protocol was applied as a practical, non-invasive, low-cost field-based method to estimate swimmers’ aerobic endurance capacity. Test procedures, data recording, and mathematical modelling were performed according to Dekerle et al. (2002) [14, 30].

The 50–400 m critical swimming speed test (CSS50&400 m) was performed to determine CSS as a field-based performance indicator derived from two maximal freestyle swimming trials. After the standardized warm-up, swimmers completed a maximal 50-m freestyle sprint. Following a 30-min passive recovery period, they performed a maximal 400-m freestyle swim. HR and RPE were recorded according to the standardized procedures described above, and a standardized cool-down was completed at the end of the testing session.

Incremental Step Test (IST7 × 200 m)

To characterize swimmers’ field-based aerobic performance profile and physiological responses to progressive swimming intensity, a multi-stage incremental swimming protocol was used. The testing structure, pacing strategy, and physiological monitoring procedures were performed according to Pyne et al. [16].

The 7 × 200 m incremental step test was used as the established field-based comparator protocol. The test consisted of seven 200-m repetitions performed with progressive increases in swimming speed. Swimmers positioned themselves under the starting block, placed their left hand on it, and signaled readiness with the right hand. The test commenced following an audible start signal.

Each 200-m repetition was externally paced using the YuzmeFit swimmer-coach communication system, and target completion time was reduced progressively according to the established IST7 × 200 m procedure [16]. Kinematic variables, including SR and SC, were recorded during the corresponding 200-m stages. Physiological and perceptual responses were monitored according to the standardized procedures described above: HR was recorded immediately after the relevant effort, blood lactate concentration was measured at baseline ([La−]preIST) and immediately after the final repetition ([La−]postIST), and RPE was collected at the predefined measurement points.

In the present study, the IST7 × 200 m was used as an established field-based comparator for preliminary agreement analysis, rather than as a gold-standard physiological criterion for direct validation of aerobic capacity.

Distance and Speed Incremental Swimming Test (DSIST50−100−150−200 m)

The Distance and Speed Incremental Swimming Test (DSIST50−100−150−200 m) was developed as a lower-volume, distance- and speed-progressive pool-based protocol for the present pilot study. The test consisted of consecutive 50-, 100-, 150-, and 200-m front-crawl stages, with progressive increases in both stage distance and prescribed swimming velocity.

The 50-m stage was performed at the individualized base velocity calculated from the stroke-rate-controlled 50-m trial. The target velocities for the 100-, 150-, and 200-m stages were increased by 5% relative to the immediately preceding stage. Interstage recovery followed a 1:1.5 work-to-recovery ratio, and external auditory feedback was provided through the YuzmeFit system to help swimmers maintain the prescribed pace.

HR and RPE were recorded immediately after each stage according to the standardized measurement procedures. Blood lactate was measured before and immediately after completion of the complete DSIST50−100−150−200 m protocol. Kinematic variables were obtained from the corresponding analysed segments, as described above. The total duration of the DSIST50−100−150−200 m protocol, including only the active swimming stages and prescribed interstage recovery intervals, was approximately 10 min. This duration did not include the standardized warm-up, cool-down, or pre- and post-test blood lactate sampling procedures.

Supportive 200-m freestyle time-trial comparison (200 m TT)

The 200-m freestyle time-trial (200 m TT) was used as a field-based performance criterion representing high-intensity swimming performance with a substantial anaerobic glycolytic contribution. Given the maximal nature and duration of the effort, the 200 m TT is expected to elicit marked metabolic stress, including increased blood lactate accumulation and perceptual fatigue. Therefore, this trial was used to evaluate swimmers’ maximal 200-m performance and the associated acute physiological and perceptual responses under field conditions [31].

200 m TT was performed 30 min after completion of the DSIST50−100−150−200 m protocol. Because it was conducted within the same testing session after the DSIST50−100−150−200 m, it was treated as a supportive and exploratory field-based performance comparison rather than an independent validation test.

Before the time trial, baseline blood lactate concentration was measured according to the standardized procedures. At the audible signal, swimmers initiated the trial and swam 200 m freestyle at maximal effort. Split times and total 200-m performance time were recorded using the YuzmeFit system and handheld timing procedures. RPE was collected immediately after the trial, and swimmers then completed the standardized cool-down. Swimming velocity was calculated as total distance divided by completion time (m·s− 1).

The possible influence of residual fatigue from the preceding DSIST50−100−150−200 m was considered in the interpretation of the 200 m TT responses.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all study variables. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), standard error (SE), median, minimum–maximum values, and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), where appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The distributional characteristics of the data were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, together with inspection of skewness, kurtosis, histograms, and Q–Q plots. Because of the small pilot sample size and because normality assumptions were not consistently satisfied across variables, non-parametric procedures were used for the main paired protocol comparisons.

The primary comparison involved paired outcomes obtained from the same swimmers during the DSIST50–100–150–200 m and IST7 × 200 m protocols. The main variables compared between protocols were swimming time, swimming velocity, stroke rate (SR), stroke count (SC), stroke length (SL), stroke index (SI), index of coordination (IdC), post-test heart rate (HR), post-test blood lactate concentration ([La⁻]post), lactate change (Δ[La⁻]), and rating of perceived exertion (RPE).

Between-protocol paired comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. To reduce the risk of Type I error arising from multiple primary comparisons, Holm correction was applied, and adjusted p values are reported as pholm. Effect sizes for Wilcoxon comparisons were expressed as r and interpreted as small (≈ 0.10), moderate (≈ 0.30), or large (≥ 0.50) [32].

Associations between corresponding DSIST50–100–150–200 m and IST7 × 200 m outcomes were examined using Spearman’s rho correlation coefficients. Correlation coefficients were reported with their direction and magnitude, and the strength of association was interpreted descriptively using the absolute rho value as negligible (< 0.30), low (≥ 0.30 to < 0.50), moderate (≥ 0.50 to < 0.70), high (≥ 0.70 to < 0.90), and very high (≥ 0.90) [33].

Preliminary agreement between the DSIST50–100–150–200 m and IST7 × 200 m protocols was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), standard error of measurement (SEM), and Bland–Altman analysis. ICCs were calculated using a two-way random-effects, absolute-agreement, single-measure model [ICC(2,1)] and were reported with 95% CI. ICC values were interpreted descriptively as poor (< 0.50), moderate (0.50–0.75), good (0.75–0.90), or excellent (> 0.90), according to Koo and Li [34].

Because the present analysis compared two different field protocols rather than repeated measurements of the same protocol, these categories were used only to describe the magnitude of cross-protocol agreement estimates and not to infer test–retest reliability. Bland–Altman bias was calculated as the mean paired difference between the two protocols, and the limits of agreement were calculated as the mean bias ± 1.96 × SD of the paired differences. The 95% confidence intervals for the limits of agreement were reported where applicable, and the results were interpreted as preliminary agreement estimates rather than evidence of interchangeability [11].

200 m TT was treated as a supportive and exploratory field-based performance comparison. Because it was performed after the DSIST50–100–150–200 m within the same testing session, it was not interpreted as an independent criterion-validation test or as evidence of protocol equivalence or interchangeability.

In the absence of respiratory gas analysis, physiological outcomes were interpreted as indirect indicators of swimming-related metabolic demand and performance, rather than as direct evidence supporting VO₂max validity. Given the pilot nature of the study and the limited sample size, all inferential statistics, ICC, SEM, and Bland–Altman results were interpreted as preliminary estimates of feasibility, reliability, and agreement. All statistical analyses were performed using JASP v.0.19.2 (JASP Team, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) and Jamovi v.2.6 (The Jamovi Project, Sydney, Australia).

Exploratory associations with 200 m TT performance

Exploratory correlations with the 200 m TT are presented in Supplementary Table S1. After Holm correction, IST7 × 200 m showed significant positive associations with 200 m TT swimming velocity (rho = 0.86; p = 0.001), stroke rate (rho = 0.83; p = 0.021), stroke count (rho = 0.89; p = 0.006), stroke length (rho = 0.91; p = < 0.001), and stroke index (rho = 0.87; p = 0.011). In contrast, none of the DSIST50–100–150–200m associations with 200 m TT survived Holm correction, except for stroke index (rho = 0.81, Holm-adjusted p = 0.008). Several other performance and kinematic coefficients remained moderate to high in magnitude but did not reach statistical significance after adjustment. These associations were interpreted as exploratory because the 200 m TT was performed after DSIST50−100−150−200 m with a 30-min passive recovery period and therefore should not be treated as an independent criterion-validation test.

Discussion

This pilot study examined the operational feasibility and preliminary cross-protocol agreement of the DSIST50–100–150–200 m in trained male university swimmers. The main findings were that all swimmers completed the DSIST50–100–150–200 m as prescribed, supporting its field applicability under the present testing conditions, and that agreement with the IST7 × 200 m was outcome-specific. Preliminary agreement was strongest for swimming velocity and selected kinematic variables, whereas completion time, highest recorded post-exercise HR (HRpeak), and lactate-change responses showed weaker agreement. Post-exercise blood lactate concentration and RPE showed moderate ICC agreement estimates, although these internal-load and perceptual outcomes should still be interpreted cautiously within the small pilot sample. This outcome-specific interpretation was based on both ICC estimates and Bland–Altman bias and limits of agreement [11], because agreement between protocols cannot be inferred from non-significant paired comparisons alone. Therefore, the DSIST should be interpreted as a lower-volume field-based protocol that may provide preliminary information on selected performance and stroke-mechanics outcomes, rather than as a validated substitute for the IST7 × 200 m or a criterion measure of aerobic capacity.

Performance outcomes and practical interpretation

The DSIST50–100–150–200 m and IST7 × 200 m protocols differed substantially in total swimming volume and stage structure. The IST7 × 200 m comprises seven 200-m stages and has been used as an established field-based incremental swimming protocol [6, 16], whereas the DSIST50–100–150–200 m was designed in the present pilot study as a lower-volume, distance- and speed-progressive protocol. Therefore, the lower total volume of the DSIST50–100–150–200 m should be interpreted as a defining practical feature of the protocol for field-based monitoring when a reduced testing burden is required. In applied swimming settings, this reduced testing burden may be relevant when coaches aim to monitor selected velocity and stroke-mechanics indicators without substantially disrupting routine training organization [6, 8, 16].

In the present study, mean swimming velocity was descriptively close between the DSIST50–100–150–200 m and IST7 × 200 m protocols (1.41 ± 0.12 vs. 1.39 ± 0.12 m·s⁻¹), with no Holm-adjusted evidence of a systematic difference. Swimming velocity also showed good cross-protocol agreement (ICC (2,1) = 0.81), with a small mean bias of 0.018 m·s⁻¹ [34]. These findings suggest that the DSIST50–100–150–200 m may provide useful information on selected velocity-related responses under the present testing conditions. However, velocity-related findings should not be interpreted as evidence of protocol equivalence. Completion time showed poor agreement (ICC (2,1) = 0.26) and wide Bland–Altman limits of agreement (− 30.0 to 14.4 s), indicating that time-based outcomes should not be interpreted as interchangeable between protocols. This interpretation is consistent with method-comparison principles, which emphasize that agreement should be evaluated from the magnitude and dispersion of between-method differences rather than from statistical non-significance or correlation alone [11]. Accordingly, the DSIST50–100–150–200 m may be useful for obtaining selected swimming velocity and stroke-mechanics information with a reduced testing burden, but it should not be considered a direct substitute for the IST7 × 200 m or a criterion measure of aerobic capacity.

Physiological and perceptual responses

The physiological and perceptual responses showed a different pattern from the swimming-velocity and kinematic outcomes. Post-exercise blood lactate concentration was descriptively similar between the IST7 × 200 m and DSIST50–100–150–200 m protocols (12.8 ± 1.5 vs. 12.6 ± 2.0 mmol·L⁻¹), but the agreement estimate was only moderate. In contrast, HRpeak was lower after the DSIST50–100–150–200 m than after the IST7 × 200 m (181.0 ± 3.36 vs. 184.8 ± 6.8 beats·min⁻¹; mean difference: −3.8 beats·min⁻) and showed poor agreement. RPE was similarly high after the DSIST50–100–150–200 m and IST7 × 200 m protocols (9.9 ± 0.3 vs. 9.8 ± 0.4), with no Holm-adjusted evidence of a systematic difference. RPE showed moderate agreement, whereas Δ[La⁻] showed poor agreement. Bland–Altman analysis further supported this interpretation, with poor agreement for HRpeak and Δ[La⁻], and more moderate agreement patterns for post-exercise blood lactate concentration and RPE [11].

These findings suggest that the DSIST50–100–150–200 m may elicit high internal-load and perceptual responses within a shorter and more compact testing structure. However, the agreement results indicate that these physiological and perceptual responses were outcome-specific rather than uniformly comparable with those obtained from the longer IST7 × 200 m protocol. Although post-exercise blood lactate concentration and RPE showed moderate agreement estimates, HRpeak and Δ[La⁻] showed weaker agreement, suggesting that the two protocols may differ in their acute physiological response patterns. These differences may be partly related to the distinct structure of the DSIST50–100–150–200 m, which combines progressive increases in both distance and prescribed velocity within a shorter total duration. From an applied perspective, the DSIST50–100–150–200 m may be useful as a time-efficient field protocol for monitoring selected performance, stroke-mechanics, and internal-load responses when a reduced testing burden is required. Nevertheless, it should not be considered a substitute for the IST7 × 200 m or a method for determining metabolic thresholds without further validation using repeated trials and physiological criterion measures [6, 16, 24, 26].

Kinematic responses and stroke-mechanics interpretation

The kinematic outcomes showed a more consistent pattern of preliminary agreement than the physiological and perceptual responses. No Holm-adjusted evidence of systematic differences was observed for stroke rate, stroke count, stroke length, stroke index, or index of coordination between the DSIST50–100–150–200 m and IST7 × 200 m protocols. Descriptive values were also close between protocols, particularly for stroke length and index of coordination, whereas stroke rate and stroke count showed small between-protocol differences. The ICC estimates for the kinematic variables were comparatively strong, with good cross-protocol agreement for stroke rate and index of coordination (0.81 and 0.85, respectively) and excellent agreement estimates for stroke count, stroke length, and stroke index (0.91–0.94) [34]. The corresponding Bland–Altman findings should be interpreted alongside these ICC estimates; they support the overall pattern of stronger preliminary agreement for selected kinematic outcomes, while still indicating protocol-specific variation rather than complete technical interchangeability [11].

These findings suggest that the DSIST50–100–150–200 m may capture selected segment-based stroke-mechanics responses in a broadly similar pattern to the IST7 × 200 m under the present field-testing conditions. This is practically relevant because stroke rate, stroke length, stroke count, stroke index, and inter-limb coordination are commonly used to characterize changes in swimming technique across different intensities and fatigue states [4, 16, 27]. Because swimming performance is influenced by the interaction between energetic demands and biomechanical organization [35], obtaining selected velocity and stroke-mechanics information within a lower-volume field protocol may be useful for applied monitoring. However, these ICC values should be interpreted as preliminary cross-protocol agreement estimates rather than as evidence of test–retest reliability or full technical equivalence. The two protocols differ in stage distance, total volume, pacing exposure, and accumulated fatigue, all of which may influence swimmers’ stroke organization. In addition, the kinematic variables were derived from selected analysed segments rather than from the whole test. Therefore, the present findings support the potential use of the DSIST50–100–150–200 m for monitoring selected stroke-mechanics indicators, but not as a fully interchangeable technical assessment protocol.

Limitations

This pilot study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small and included only trained male university swimmers; therefore, the findings should not be generalized to female swimmers, adolescent swimmers, masters’ athletes, or swimmers from different competitive levels. The small sample may also have increased the uncertainty of the ICC estimates and Bland–Altman limits of agreement. Accordingly, the agreement results should be interpreted as preliminary estimates rather than evidence of protocol interchangeability.

Second, the testing sequence was fixed and was not randomized or counterbalanced. Although this approach standardized protocol administration across participants, possible order effects cannot be excluded. In addition, the 200 m TT was performed 30 min after the DSIST50–100–150–200 m within the same testing session. Because recovery duration and recovery mode may influence lactate removal and subsequent swimming performance [17], residual fatigue or incomplete metabolic recovery may have affected the 200 m TT responses. Therefore, the 200 m TT should be interpreted only as a supportive exploratory comparison and not as an independent criterion-validation test.

Third, direct cardiopulmonary exercise testing with respiratory gas analysis was not performed. Thus, VO₂max, vVO₂max, maximal lactate steady state, and ventilatory thresholds were not directly assessed. HRpeak, post-exercise blood lactate concentration, Δ[La⁻], and RPE should therefore be interpreted as indirect field-based indicators rather than evidence of aerobic-capacity validity. In addition, HRpeak may have been affected by the timing and technical constraints of heart-rate monitoring in the aquatic environment, blood lactate was measured only before and after each protocol, RPE was subjective, and kinematic variables were derived from selected analysed segments rather than the whole test.

Finally, the present study did not include a separate test–retest assessment of the DSIST50–100–150–200 m. Therefore, the ICC and Bland–Altman findings describe preliminary cross-protocol agreement with the IST7 × 200 m and should not be interpreted as within-protocol reliability. Future studies should include larger and more diverse samples, randomized or counterbalanced test orders, repeated DSIST50–100–150–200 m trials, and direct physiological criterion measures.

Conclusion

This pilot study supports the operational feasibility of the DSIST50–100–150–200 m as a lower-volume, field-based swimming protocol in trained male university swimmers. The protocol was completed by all swimmers as prescribed and provided integrated information on swimming velocity, selected stroke-mechanics variables, and indirect internal-load responses within a shorter testing structure than the IST7 × 200 m. Preliminary agreement with the IST7 × 200 m was strongest for swimming velocity and selected segment-based kinematic outcomes, whereas completion time, HRpeak, and lactate-change responses showed weaker agreement. Post-exercise blood lactate concentration and RPE showed moderate agreement estimates, but these responses should still be interpreted cautiously as indirect and protocol-specific internal-load or perceptual indicators. Therefore, the DSIST50–100–150–200 m should not be considered a direct substitute for the IST7 × 200 m or a criterion measure of aerobic capacity. Rather, it may be considered a practical lower-volume monitoring protocol for obtaining selected performance, stroke-mechanics, and internal-load information when reduced testing burden is required. Further studies with larger and more diverse samples, repeated DSIST50–100–150–200 m trials, randomized or counterbalanced test orders, and direct physiological criterion measures are required to examine its reliability, validity, and applied monitoring utility.

Practical applications

The DSIST50–100–150–200 m may be useful in applied swimming settings when coaches or sport scientists need a brief field-based protocol that can be incorporated into routine training with less disruption than longer incremental tests. Its main practical value is that it allows selected swimming velocity, stroke-mechanics, and internal-load indicators to be collected within a lower-volume structure. Swimming velocity and selected kinematic variables appear to be the most promising outcomes for practical monitoring. HRpeak and lactate-change responses should be interpreted as protocol-specific internal-load indicators rather than as evidence of equivalence with the IST7 × 200 m. Post-exercise blood lactate concentration and RPE may complement performance and kinematic monitoring, but they should be interpreted as protocol-specific responses. Accordingly, the DSIST50–100–150–200 m may support practical monitoring of selected performance and technical responses, but it should not replace established physiological testing when direct assessment of aerobic capacity is required.
