Prevalence, demographic characteristics and clinical profile of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease among adult patients attending tertiary hospitals in Dodoma, Tanzania. a cross-sectional study
Authors: Francis Msagati, Baraka Alphonce, Emmanuel Sindato
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionGlobally, metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) has become one of the leading causes of liver disease, attributed to the rapid increase in obesity. There is paucity of data on the burden of MAFLD in Tanzania. The study assessed MAFLD prevalence and associated factors among adult patients in Dodoma,MethodsFrom November 2023 to March 2024 adult patients was recruited at tertiary hospitals. MAFLD was diagnosed based on evidence of steatosis on imaging plus any one of five cardio metabolic risk factors, such as overweight or obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2), diabetes mellitus type 2, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and metabolic dysregulation. Demographic and clinical characteristics were collected and analyzed to identify risk factors of MAFLD. The chi-squared test was used to determine if there is a proportional between categorical data. Logistic regression analysis was used to check for associated factors; significance was defined as a p-value < 0.05. The Institutional Research Ethics Committee (IRREC) of University of Dodoma (UDOM) approved the study, and informed consent was obtained.ResultsA total of 351 patients were recruited, of whom 186(53%) were females, and the median age was 62 (IQR 52–70) years. The prevalence of MAFLD was 21.9%. Among patients with MAFLD 12(15.6%) were found to have advanced fibrosis. Compared to non MAFLD subjects, MAFLD patients had elevated liver enzymes with a P value (< 0.001),T2DM (OR: 3.9; 95% CI (2.1–7.2, p < 0.001), dyslipidemia (OR: 2.3; 95% CI 1.2–4.7, p = 0.013), obesity (OR: 2.0; 95% CI (1.1–3.7, p = 0.016),alcohol consumption(OR:2.2;95% CI(1.2–4.1, P = 0.007),smoking( OR 2.6 ;95% CI (1.4–4.8, p < 0.001), and sedentary lifestyle (OR: 2.3; 95% CI (1.2–4.2, p = 0.005) were associated with MAFLD.ConclusionThe high prevalence of MAFLD indicates its emergence as a significant health concern in daily clinical practice in our setting, potentially leading to increase morbidity and mortality.

Introduction

In 2020 non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) was changed to metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) and this new term is widely recognized worldwide. MAFLD is a common and increasingly recognized condition worldwide [1, 2]. MAFLD was proposed to cover a broader range of patients and be more universally applicable. MAFLD is an umbrella term that encompasses wide patients with fatty liver, it does not exclude the possibility of other liver diseases co exciting or being the primary causes of liver damage [3]. MAFLD includes a variety of liver-related disorders, ranging from simple steatosis to Metabolic associated steatohepatitis (MASH), fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [4]. Obesity, Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and metabolic syndrome are identified as the most important risk factors for MAFLD. Although MAFLD is typically associated with obesity there is accumulating evidence that not all overweight or obesity people develop MAFLD. The clinical profile natural history and pathophysiology of patients with so called lean MAFLD are not well characterized. On the other hand a considerable proportion of patients with MAFLD are of normal weight, indicating the importance of metabolic health in the pathogenesis of the disease regardless of body mass index(BMI) [5, 6]. Of note is that the diagnostic criteria for MAFLD often overlap with its risk factors demonstrating the intricate relationship between these elements, obesity, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic dysregulation are all key factors involved in both diagnosis and development of MAFLD. In conclusion these elements are integral to the definition of MAFLD, they can also be considered independent risk factors that contribute to the disease’s development and progression [5].

MAFLD has emerged as the most frequent chronic liver disease. MAFLD has a global frequency of 25.24%, with the highest prevalence in the Middle East (31%), high prevalence in the Middle East is largely due to rising obesity levels, poor dietary habits, and sedentary lifestyles associated with rapid urbanization. Additional factors such as genetic predispositions, cultural influences, and limited healthcare access further contribute to the region’s vulnerability to this condition) and South America (30%) followed by Asia (27%), North America (24%), and Europe (23%), while it is less frequent in Africa (13%) [7].The recent meta analysis on MAFLD done in sub Saharan Africa(SSA) has shown a prevalence of MAFLD was 29·21% [8], highlight the emerging burden in sub Saharan Africa. The global prevalence of the condition is increasing due to rapid lifestyle transitions, posing significant challenges in its control, and posing a significant economic burden on healthcare systems due to the high potential complications [9]. The disease is asymptomatic until when the condition reaches the late stage of fibrosis, cirrhosis, or HCC. Despite the high prevalence and potential complications, there is no definitive management of the condition so far.

Patients with MAFLD had prevalence rates of 51%, 22.5%, and 42.5% for obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus(T2DM), and metabolic syndrome, respectively [6]. It is predicted by 2030 liver related fatalities caused by MAFLD will reach around 178% [6]. The prevalence of MAFLD is also increasing in developing countries, including Tanzania, due to the rising prevalence of obesity, diabetes, and other metabolic disorders. Non communicable diseases (NCD) Alliance report from Tanzania shows the rising prevalence of diabetes from 2.8% in 2011 to 12.8% in 2020, Studies conducted in Tanzania show the prevalence of Obesity (68.9%), dyslipidemia 45.1% abdominal obesity(11.8%), and hypertension 58.5% among chronic kidney disease patients(CKD) [10–12].

Despite the growing incidence of MAFLD in developing nations, there is a scarcity of epidemiological data on its prevalence and related risk factors in Tanzania. It is crucial to understand the prevalence of MAFLD since most patients have no visible clinical symptoms, and long-term intrahepatic fat buildup can accelerate the development of liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, liver failure, and other extra hepatic problems associated with MAFLD. The purpose of this study was to explore MAFLD prevalence, demographic characteristics and clinical profile among adult patients in Dodoma, Tanzania, using current diagnosis criteria.

Methods

Population and settings for the study design

This was a five-month observational, cross-sectional, descriptive, and analytical study of 351 patients at the medical division of Tanzania’s capital city in two centers, Dodoma Regional Referral Hospital (DRRH) and Benjamin Mkapa Hospital (BMH). These hospitals have served as the primary hospitals in Dodoma and the surrounding regions, providing care to an estimated 2.9 million people (“Tanzania in Figs. 2018,” 2019). These hospitals act as consultative and teaching facilities for the University of Dodoma and other health allied colleges. Each of the hospitals has 21 wards with bed capacities of 400 and 480. Medical clinics for DRRH and BMH were established on Tuesdays and Wednesdays. The medical clinics at DRRH and BMH, has an average attendance of 30 to 40 patients per day. The BMH laboratory has the potential to provide laboratory services to 500 clients per day; currently, the overall number of customers receiving laboratory services at BMH ranges from 300 to 350 per day, while the DRRH laboratory has the capacity to provide laboratory services to 250 to 300 clients per day. Adult patients above 18 years of age who were attending to the medical department as outpatients DRRH and BMH individuals with metabolic conditions who provided informed consent were recruited [12]. Patients with a history of liver disease or tested positive for hepatitis B and C or liver transplantation and pregnant women were excluded from the study.

Sample size

The sample size for the determination of prevalence was estimated using the Cochran formula. The Minimum sample size was 183 based on the cross-sectional study done in Tanzania with the prevalence of fatty liver of 13.9% [12]. While the minimum sample size of 183 would have allowed us to address our primary research question, the increased sample size of 351 was a deliberate decision to enhance the statistical power, improve the precision of our estimates, enable more detailed subgroup analyses, and mitigate the potential impact of missing data, ultimately strengthening the overall rigor and in formativeness of our findings in this cross-sectional study.”

Data collection procedure

Participant interview

All data were collected prospectively at the time of the interview. Demographic data, such as age, gender, location of residence, were collected using a standardized questionnaire printed in Swahili and English. Patients were asked to provide a history of their alcohol consumption alcohol consumption was assessed by recall if the respondent was a lifetime alcohol abstainer, or had history of alcohol consumption. The questionnaire questioned about the frequency and quantity of alcoholic drinks ingested either now or in the past. They were questioned about how often they drank alcohol on a weekly basis, how much beer, cider, wine, and spirits/liquor they consumed in a day, and how long they had been drinking. Based on the interviews, it was determined how many units of alcohol were drank daily and weekly respectively. A 500 ml bottle of beer, a 500 ml bottle of cider, a 25 ml glass of spirit or liquor, and a 175 ml glass of wine were the standard units used to record this data. We determined the following ethanol concentrations using data from Stringer G. et al.‘s Alcohol guidelines 11th Report of Session 2010–12:Cider: 20 g (2.6 units)/bottle; wine: 16 g (2 units)/glass; spirits/liquor: 8 g (1 unit)/glass. We computed the total grams of ethanol consumed in a typical day and the total units of alcohol drank in a week using these estimations. Organizations like the World Health Organization (WHO) and the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA) have recommended specific daily and weekly limits for alcohol consumption to minimize health risks. These guidelines often distinguish between men and women due to differences in metabolism and tolerance. Numerous studies have consistently linked excessive alcohol intake to liver damage, including alcoholic fatty liver disease (AFLD) and alcoholic hepatitis. The threshold of 4 units per day is often used as a marker for potentially harmful levels of alcohol consumption.

Anthropometric measurements and clinical examination

Each individual’s kilogram weight was measured using an RGZ-120 Health weighing scale and rounded to the nearest decimal place. A stadiometer was used to ascertain each person’s height (RGZ- 120 Health scale). Their Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated by dividing the body weight by height in meters squared (kg/m2) and the results were ranked into four groups: ≤18.4 kg/m2, as underweight, 18.5–24.9 kg/m2 as normal weight, 25.0–29.9 kg/m2 as overweight, and ≥ 30 kg/m2 as obese.

Waist circumference measurements

Waist and hip circumferences were measured twice using a 203 cm Seca® measuring tape.The participant was advised to exhale while having their waist circumference measured in accordance with the International Diabetes Federation’s ethnic or national values. The average of the two measurements was used for each of these recorded values. Central obesity was defined as measures greater than 88 cm in women and 102 cm in men. All measurements were taken by considering the standard methods of anthropo- metric measurement.

Blood pressure measurements

An automated digital instrument Omron HEM 7120 OMRON (Healthcare, Kyoto, Japan) was used to take a blood pressure (BP) reading in compliance with the 2018 AHA/ACC Hypertension guideline for standard measurement of BP [13]. A patient was termed hypertensive if their blood pressure was greater than 130/85 mmHg or if they were using an antihypertensive treatment [13].

Physical activities

A modified WHO STEP-wise survey questionnaire including the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire was used to collect information about physical activities, our study independent self-report (compendium coding) of activities that the individual does on a regular basis were identified and recorded was used. The Global Physical Activity Questionnaire was used to estimate levels of physical activities based on intensity, duration, and frequency of physical activity. The data collected included number of days, hours and minutes of physical activity performed for at least 15 min or more continuously each day [10]. The duration, intensity, and frequency of physical activities performed in a typical week were used to determine the Metabolic Equivalent (MET) as the unit for measuring physical activity energy expenditure. Each individual was classified as having high, moderate, or low level of physical activity following a standard classification approach. Sedentary life sedentary lifestyle was defined as exercising less than 15 min per day on fewer than three days per week [10].

Laboratory technique

Biochemical analyses

After completing the questionnaire, respondents were asked to consent to a venipuncture, in which approximately ten millilitres of blood was drawn from each patient on either side of the cubital fossa using a 5 cc syringe and placed in a red and lavender top vacutainer tube. The blood sample for both study blood collection centres was transported to the authorized Benjamin Mkapa Hospital (BMH) laboratory in a refrigerated box with an ice pack for planned tests. Blood samples were processed according to the BMH laboratory’s Standard Operating Procedures for each test. The samples for the following test was collected (HDL and triglycerides), Full blood profile (FBP), Serum albumin, aspartate transaminase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), hepatitis panel(B &C) and HbA1c was taken to those who was non diabetic. To separate the serum sample from the whole blood, centrifugation at 300 rpm for 5 min was used, followed by two aliquots, one for each lipid profile. If it was predicted that the analysis would take more than two hours, a sample was kept at temperatures ranging from 2 to 8 degrees Celsius. If the analysis was expected to be completed within two hours of sample collection, blood samples must be maintained at room temperature. Blood chemistry measurements were analysed using An automated chemical analyser. Erba machine XL-180 serial number 160,239 was manufactured in Germany BMH, and DRRH has Erba machines XL-180 manufactured in Germany, Maglumi-800 made in Ireland, COBASS, and Abbot M2000 made in the United States of America. Dyslipidaemia was defined as having a high leve l of triglycerides above 170 mg/dl and a high-density lipoprotein cholesterol level below 50 mg/dL for females and 40 mg/dL for males Elevated liver enzymes was defined as AST and ALT when is above 40IU/L and Pre diabetic patient was defined when glycated haemoglobin range from (HbA1c 5.7%−6.4%) and serum albumin was defined as < 3.2 Hypoalbuminaemia and ≥ 3.2(normal) [14].

A finger prick was used to obtain a blood sample for glucose measurement, as described in the DRRH and BMH laboratory’s sample collection handbook SM1-03.3. The blood sample was taken directly from the equipment (ACCU-CHECK Active Roche glucometer). Hyperglycaemia, according to the American Diabetes Association, was defined as random blood sugar above 11.1mmol/l [15].

Fatty liver diagnosis

Abdominal ultrasound was performed by an experienced radiologist to ensure inter-observer consistency, using the MINDRAY DC-35PRO color Doppler ultrasound device at DRRH and SONOSCOPE P20 at BMH at 3.5 Hz to determine the presence of hepatic steatosis. Fatty liver was diagnosed based on the following abdominal ultrasonography findings: increased hepato-renal contrast, increased echogenicity of liver parenchyma, unclear visibility of the intrahepatic arteries, and/or impaired visualization of the diaphragm.

MAFLD diagnosis

Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Fatty Liver Disease (MAFLD) was defined as evidence of hepatic steatosis by sonographer and the presence of at least 1 of the following 3 criteria:

Assessment of degree of liver fibrosis by FIB 4

To assess the extent of liver injury and the degree of liver fibrosis, the patients underwent blood tests to determine AST, ALT and platelet count as most of the patients with severe forms of MAFLD have a higher risk of fibrosis. The fibrosis was determined by the blood fibrosis 4 score, which was calculated by age of the patients, serum levels of AST, ALT, and platelet count. Score was interpreted as follows < 1.45 No fibrosis (low probability),> 1.45–2.5 Mild fibrosis, > 2.5–3.25 Moderate fibrosis, > 3.25 Severe fibrosis.

Data processing and analysis

“Prior to data entry, the investigators conducted a thorough review of all questionnaires to ensure accuracy and completeness. The collected data was initially recorded on an Excel spreadsheet and subsequently uploaded to SPSS version 28 for statistical analysis. The characteristics of the study participants were analyzed based on the presence or absence of Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Fatty Liver Disease (MAFLD). Continuous variables with a normally distributed distribution are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD); otherwise, they are reported as the median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are presented as percentages. Differences in categorical variables between groups were assessed using the chi-squared (χ 2) test.

To explore the potential association between continuous factors and the odds of MAFLD, simple univariate binary logistic regression models were employed. All variables exhibiting statistical significance in the univariate analysis (p < 0.05) were subsequently examined using multivariable binary logistic regression models. The results of these models are presented as crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs), along with their corresponding p-values (< 0.05) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All statistical tests were two-sided. Furthermore, acknowledging the potential for interrelationships among metabolic risk factors (e.g., the clustering of obesity, dyslipidemia, and hypertension as metabolic syndrome), multicollinearity was assessed and found not to significantly distort the results of the regression analyses.”

Demographic characteristics and clinical profile of the study patients

A total of 351 patients met the eligibility criteria and were examined during the study period. The median age of the patients was 62 (IQR 52–70) years, with 186 (53.0%) being over 60 years old and 242 (68.9%) residing in urban areas. There were 187 (54.0%) females. The median body mass index was 27.54 (IQR 26.25–29.35) kg/m2, and the median random blood glucose was 8.9 (IQR 7.5–8.5) mmol/l. The median systolic blood pressure was 135.4 (IQR 123–149) mmHg, and the median diastolic blood pressure was 79.5 (IQR74.02-91.0) mmHg. The median platelet count was 234 (IQR 185–296), the median aspartate aminotransferase level was 19 (IQR 14-28.5), the median alanine aminotransferase level was 23 (IQR 16–35), the median total cholesterol level was 6.14 (IQR 5.2–6.9) mmol/l, the median HDL level was 1.00 (IQR 0.30–2.06) mmol/l, and the median triglyceride level was 3.1 (IQR 2.1–4.6 mmol/l) (Table 1).

Clinical profile of the study patients

Among 351 patients 21.9%(77/351) had Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Fatty Liver Disease (MAFLD), alcohol consumption, lack of exercise, smoking, ALT, AST, Lipid profile, obesity, and type 2 diabetic mellitus(T2DM), were found to be positively associated with ORs of MALFD, and there was no association of Age, gender, WBC, platelets and serum albumin with ORs of MALFD (Table 2). To test the potential relationship between continuous variables and the ORs of MAFLD, A chi-square test, and the univariate logistic regression analysis were performed. We further divided the continuous variables with statistical significance into category variables. Multivariable logistic regression model adjustment for all the variables and results showed that alcohol consumption, lack of exercise, smoking, elevated liver enzymes, Lipid profile, obesity, T2DM were risk factors of MAFLD, even after adjusting for confounding factors(Table 3).

Discussion

Our study aimed to investigate the prevalence, demographic characteristic and clinical profile of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) among adult’s patients attending tertiary hospital in Tanzania. The results indicate that diabetes, obesity, smoking, alcohol consumption, lack of exercise, dyslipidemia and elevated liver enzymes are risk factors that are associated with MAFLD.

“In comparison to prior research at Muhimbili, which reported a non-alcoholic Fatty liver disease (NAFLD) prevalence of 13.9%, this current study demonstrated a significantly higher prevalence of 21.9% among patients with MAFLD [16]. This discrepancy can be attributed to the broader definition of MAFLD, which encompasses a wider range of patients with fatty liver conditions. Consistent with our current findings, a study conducted in Côte d’Ivoire found a prevalence of 23% in the general population [17]. Furthermore, a similar study conducted in a province in China revealed a prevalence of 26%. This study established that, compared with individuals without MAFLD, those with MAFLD had significantly higher values for body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, total cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST). These findings are consistent with the observations in our study [18]. On the contrary, a majority of studies have reported relatively higher rates of MAFLD in various populations. For example, a study involving 21,885 patients in Taipei, Taiwan, showed a MAFLD prevalence of 66.95% in patients with diabetes mellitus (DM) and 65.07% in obese patients [19]. Another meta-analysis, which included 2,667,052 participants from the general population, estimated the global MAFLD prevalence to be 50.7% [20].

The varying prevalence of MAFLD across different populations is influenced by a complex interplay of environmental, socioeconomic, and genetic factors. Geographic factors, such as climate and disease burden, may further contribute to these disparities.”

“The clinical profile of MAFLD patients in this study revealed a spectrum of fibrosis stages, ranging from mild to advanced. Among patients diagnosed with MAFLD, 23% presented with mild fibrosis, 7% with moderate fibrosis, and 15% with advanced fibrosis. These findings align with previous observational studies by Sangro et al. and Sumida et al., which reported significant fibrosis among MAFLD patients using the fibrosis-4 score (FIB-4) [3, 21], his suggests that a substantial proportion of individuals with MAFLD have advanced liver disease, potentially leading to severe complications.

Elevated liver enzymes, specifically alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), are commonly used as indicators of liver injury. Our study demonstrated a significant association between elevated liver enzyme levels and metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD). This observation is consistent with findings from Wen et al., who also reported significantly elevated AST and ALT levels in the MAFLD group compared to controls [22]. Similarly, another study found that MAFLD patients exhibited higher ALT and AST levels than their non-MAFLD counterparts, further supporting this positive association [19]. The link between MAFLD and elevated liver enzymes underscores the importance of routine liver function tests for the early detection and ongoing monitoring of this disease.

Furthermore, a study involving 73,566 individuals in Beijing reported a strong association between dyslipidemia and MAFLD. Notably, lean/normal-weight MAFLD patients in that cohort had a higher prevalence of hypertension and diabetes, along with a greater degree of hepatic steatosis and deranged liver function enzyme parameters [23].

While some prior research has indicated a higher prevalence of MAFLD in specific age groups and genders, our analysis did not reveal significant differences based on these demographic factors. This may be attributable to the relatively heterogeneous population and sample size of our study. However, other demographic characteristics exhibited significant associations with MAFLD. Specifically, individuals with higher BMI, a diagnosis of diabetes mellitus, a self-reported history of alcohol consumption, a history of smoking, and a lack of regular exercise were significantly more likely to have MAFLD.

Our findings revealed a strong association between type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), with T2DM patients exhibiting a threefold increase in the odds of MAFLD compared to those without the condition. The interplay of insulin resistance and hyperglycemia promotes increased hepatic lipid metabolism and excessive fat storage, thereby potentially driving MAFLD development. The co-occurrence of T2DM is often associated with more severe MAFLD, poorer glycemic control, and an elevated risk of cardiovascular events. These results corroborate existing evidence indicating a robust association between T2DM and MAFLD, independent of body mass index (BMI) [24], a finding further supported by another study [25]. Interestingly, a large Korean study involving 283,088 subjects demonstrated a sex-stratified association between smoking and MAFLD. In men, smoking was linked to an increased incidence of both hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and cardiovascular disease (CVD), whereas in women, it was primarily associated with an increased risk of CVD. Overall, smoking significantly elevates the incidence of HCC and CVD in MAFLD patients. Research suggests that cigarette smoke exposure in obese individuals exacerbates oxidative stress, insulin resistance, hepatic steatosis, hepatocellular ballooning, and lobular inflammation [26]. Moreover, smoking is linked to adverse lipid profiles, including elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and plasma triglycerides, and decreased high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), all of which are implicated in MAFLD pathogenesis [26].

Notably, individuals who reported consuming ≥ 4 units of alcohol per day had significantly higher odds of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), consistent with existing literature highlighting the detrimental effects of excessive alcohol consumption on liver health and its potential contribution to MAFLD development [27, 28]. While some previous studies have suggested potential protective effects of moderate alcohol consumption against MAFLD, our findings indicate a potential adverse effect associated with exceeding a low threshold of alcohol intake [29]. This is echoed by a study in Japan that found a positive association between consuming more than 2 units of alcohol per day and the likelihood of developing MAFLD [30]. The cutoff of more than 4 units of alcohol per day in MAFLD studies aligns with established guidelines from organizations such as the World Health Organization(WHO) and National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA), and this threshold is widely used in research. Furthermore, the synergistic effects of alcohol consumption and other MAFLD risk factors on liver health warrant consideration, potentially influencing the observed alcohol-related results and suggesting the need for combined lifestyle interventions in affected individuals.

Consistent with a substantial body of literature, the present study found a strong positive correlation between obesity and MAFLD. This aligns with the established understanding that excessive adiposity, particularly visceral fat, plays a crucial role in the development of this hepatic condition. Prior research [31] has underscored this critical link, proposing mechanisms involving altered cytokine profiles, inflammatory processes, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia. Interestingly, this association attenuated after adjusting for BMI, waist, and hip circumference. Conversely, high adherence to the Mediterranean diet was associated with a significant 64% reduction in MAFLD odds.

Our study also indicated that patients who reported a lack of regular exercise had a twofold greater risk of MAFLD. This finding corroborates existing evidence establishing physical inactivity as a significant risk factor for MAFLD development. Studies have shown that increased sedentary behavior, such as prolonged television viewing, is associated with a higher risk of MAFLD, while increased vigorous physical activity is linked to a reduced risk [32]. Additionally, a population-based study in Western China [33] highlighted the independent and joint associations of physical inactivity and poor sleep quality with an increased prevalence of MAFLD, suggesting that physical activity may positively influence circadian rhythms and mood [34], thereby improving sleep quality and metabolic health.

An unexpected finding in our cohort was the lack of a significant association between age and sex with metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), contrasting with some larger studies that have reported a higher MAFLD risk in older individuals or females. This may be due to the older age and high metabolic risk profile of the majority of patients in our cohort, potentially diminishing the differences between subgroups.

While this study provides valuable insights into the prevalence and risk factors of MAFLD in our setting, it is important to acknowledge several limitations. The cross-sectional design limits our ability to establish causality. The sample size, while informative, may not be fully representative of the entire Tanzanian population, and the recruitment of patients from a tertiary hospital introduces potential selection bias, as these individuals may not reflect the general population. Furthermore, the use of ultrasound for diagnosing fatty liver has inherent limitations, including observer dependency and reduced sensitivity in cases of mild steatosis (< 20%) or in individuals with higher BMI, potentially leading to false negatives and an underestimation of the true prevalence. Similarly, the FIB-4 index used for fibrosis staging, while non-invasive, has limitations in sensitivity and specificity depending on patient age, potentially leading to misclassification in our cohort with a median age of 62. It is important to note that fibrosis assessment was indirect and not confirmed by more definitive methods like FibroScan or biopsy. Finally, the accuracy of lifestyle data relies on patient recall and honesty, and despite using standardized questionnaires, some degree of subjectivity in reporting (e.g., exact alcohol units or activity levels) remains.

Future research should aim for larger, population-based studies with longitudinal designs to better understand the natural history and long-term outcomes of MAFLD in Tanzania. To our knowledge, this study represents the first exploration of the prevalence, clinical profile, and risk factors of MAFLD in tertiary hospitals in central Tanzania, Dodoma, since the adoption of the new MAFLD definition.

Conclusion

The high prevalence of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) observed in our study underscores its increasing significance as a clinical concern. Our findings confirm the elevated likelihood of fatty liver in patients with metabolic syndrome components, highlighting the potential for progression to cirrhosis and its associated complications. The risk factors identified in our setting are largely consistent with those reported globally. However, given the cross-sectional nature of our study, further longitudinal research is warranted to provide a more comprehensive understanding of these factors and their impact in our specific population.

Recommendation

Early and time management of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) can significantly reduce the burden of the disease and its associated complications, such as liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. It is necessary to do longitudinal studies or follow-up of these patients in the near future to track liver outcomes.
