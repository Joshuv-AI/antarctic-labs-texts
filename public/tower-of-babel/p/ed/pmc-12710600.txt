HZO/HSO Superlattice ReFET Array Integrating Optical Sensing for Neuromorphic Vision Computing
Authors: Bingjie Dang, Kaixuan Sun, Hanxin Su, Jiaxin Chen, Pengyu Yao, Tao Zeng, Shu Shi, Guowei Zhou, Liang Liu, Xiaohong Xu, Jingsheng Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuromorphic vision systems require artificial synapses that integrate sensing, memory, and computation with high precision and stability. Conventional memristors face limitations including forming requirements, few multilevel states, low endurance, and poor integration density, while ferroelectric and flash‐based transistors suffer trade‐offs among endurance, retention, and switching ratio, and generally lack intrinsic photonic sensitivity, constraining in‐sensor computing. Here, a photonic resistive‐gate field‐effect transistor (ReFET) array is presented that combines optical sensing, forming‐free multilevel memory, and analog computation in a single device. The ReFET employs a Hf0.5Zr0.5O2/Hf0.95Sr0.05O2 (HZO/HSO) superlattice gate and an amorphous InGaZnO (IGZO) channel, achieving 272 stable conductance states (>8‐bit) in a 20 × 20 array, ON/OFF ratios >10⁶, endurance >1010 cycles, and retention >10⁶ s. The array functions as an in‐sensor optical convolutional layer, performing multiply–accumulate (MAC) operations with 94.45% accuracy on Fashion‐MNIST using 8‐bit quantized weights, while delivering high energy efficiency. This platform enables scalable, high‐precision, energy‐efficient photonic neuromorphic computing, integrating sensing, memory, and computation in one architecture.Keywords: in‐sensor computing, memristor, neuromorphic computing, ReFET, vision transformer

Introduction

The rapid growth of Internet‐of‐Things (IoT) applications, driven by advances in artificial intelligence, autonomous systems, and large‐scale foundation models, is generating a surge of data at the network edge, particularly from vision sensors.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] These sensors produce high‐dimensional and high‐throughput data that require real‐time processing. However, conventional von Neumann architectures, which separate sensing, memory, and computation, suffer from significant energy consumption, latency, and bandwidth bottlenecks.[ 1 , 2 , 4 ] These issues are especially critical in edge devices, where power and area constraints make frequent data transfer inefficient.

To overcome these challenges, neuromorphic hardware architectures have been developed to emulate the parallel and co‐located sensing and processing found in biological systems.[ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 ] Memristive synapses play a key role in these systems due to their analog tunability, nonvolatile memory function, and potential for highly parallel computing.[ 5 , 8 , 9 , 10 ] Yet, traditional memristors, such as those based on HfO2 and TaO x , face fundamental trade‐offs between precision, endurance, forming‐free, and multilevel switching.[ 11 , 12 ] Passive crossbar architectures also suffer from sneak‐path currents, while the integration of selector devices adds design complexity and restricts computational flexibility[ 13 , 14 ] (Figure 1a).

Emerging classes of nonvolatile transistors, including resistive‐gate field‐effect transistors (ReFETs),[ 15 , 16 ] 2D flash transistors,[ 17 , 18 ] and ferroelectric field‐effect transistors (FeFETs),[ 19 , 20 , 21 ] have attracted increasing interest as scalable alternatives to conventional memristive devices. These transistor‐based architectures offer key advantages such as effective suppression of sneak‐path currents, compact device footprints, and seamless integration with complementary logic circuits. Nevertheless, several intrinsic limitations remain. ReFETs often require an initial electrical forming process to activate resistive switching, which can compromise uniformity and scalability.[ 16 ] 2D flash transistors generally exhibit limited endurance under repeated program–erase cycling.[ 17 , 18 ] FeFETs, while promising for low‐power and high‐speed operation, face challenges related to polarization fatigue and thermal instability, which degrade device reliability over time.[ 19 ] In addition, the lack of intrinsic optical sensitivity in most of these platforms limits their applicability in in‐sensor visual computing systems, where co‐location of sensing, memory, and processing is essential for low‐latency and energy‐efficient neuromorphic vision. ReFETs address many of these challenges by offering several key advantages over conventional 1T1R arrays. Programming through the RS gate separates write and read operations, minimizing state disturbance (Figure 1a). The integrated gate avoids series impedance mismatches, simplifying design and improving operational uniformity. The HZO/HSO superlattice is forming‐free and self‐rectifying, ensuring high device consistency across the array. Furthermore, the ReFET architecture inherently supports scalable in‐memory computing with integrated optical sensing, enabling compact and efficient neuromorphic visual computing.

Here, we demonstrate a photonic ReFET array that monolithically integrates optical sensing, forming‐free, multilevel memory, and analog computation within a single device. The HZO–HSO superlattice gate enables oxygen‐vacancy‐mediated 8‐bit analog switching with robust phase stability (Figure 1b), while the IGZO channel provides photonic responsivity, allowing light‐dependent conductance modulation. A 20 × 20 ReFET array (Figure 1c–e), implemented as an in‐sensor optical convolutional layer, achieves 272 stable conductance states, ON/OFF ratios >10⁶, endurance >1010 cycles, and retention exceeding 10⁶ s, enabling direct multiply–accumulate operations. Under a convolutional vision transformer framework with 8‐bit quantized weights, the array attains 94.45% classification accuracy on Fashion‐MNIST, while delivering ultra‐high energy efficiency. These results establish a scalable, high‐precision, and energy‐efficient platform for photonic neuromorphic computing, addressing longstanding challenges in integrating sensing, memory, and computation at the device level.

Results and Discussion

Motivated by the aforementioned insights, we engineered a memristive device incorporating a superlattice structure composed of HZO and HSO, as schematically illustrated in Figure 2a. The device architecture, comprising LSMO (20 nm)/HZO–HSO (15 nm)/Pt (35 nm), functions as the resistive switching (RS) gate within a reconfigurable ReFET framework. The nanoscale structural characteristics of the HZO–HSO superlattice film were examined via high‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR‐TEM), as depicted in Figure 2b. To validate the periodic modulation inherent to the superlattice, X‐ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were conducted. As shown in Figure S1a (Supporting Information), the diffraction profile reveals a polycrystalline morphology comprising both monoclinic and orthorhombic phases, with reflections at ≈28.5° for the (−111) planes of the monoclinic phase and ≈30° for the (111) orientation of the orthorhombic phase. These phase identifications are corroborated by HR‐TEM imaging (Figure 2b; Figure S1b, Supporting Information), wherein discrete monoclinic nanocrystallites are embedded within a predominantly orthorhombic matrix, confirming the coexistence of polymorphic domains. We further conducted EELS mapping along the out‐of‐plane direction, which revealed Zr‐rich HZO layers and Zr‐deficient HSO layers, consistent with the designed superlattice (Figure S2a–c, Supporting Information). To confirm the presence of Sr in HSO, EDXS detected weak Sr peaks corresponding to the nominal ≈5% doping, thereby verifying the integrity of the HZO–HSO multilayer structure (Figure S2d, Supporting Information).

Electrical characteristics and HRTEM analysis of the LSMO/HZO–HSO/Pt memristor. a) Schematic of the LSMO/HZO–HSO/Pt memristor structure fabricated using HZO–HSO superlattice engineering. b) Cross‐sectional HRTEM image of the HZO–HSO superlattice. Scale bar: 5 nm. c) Typical I–V characteristics of the LSMO/HZO–HSO/Pt device, exhibiting analog switching behavior with a high ON/OFF ratio and self‐rectifying properties, without the need for an electroforming process. d) HRTEM image of the device in the pristine state. Scale bar: 5 nm. e) HRTEM image after a write operation (0–+5 V sweep), revealing the formation of conductive filaments within the HZO–HSO layer. f) Endurance performance of the device under consecutive ±3.5 /−3 V, 10 µs voltage pulses, demonstrating stable operation over 1010 cycles (read pulse: 0.1 V, 2 ms).

The RS behavior of the LSMO/HZO–HSO/Pt heterostructure was systematically investigated through cyclic current–voltage (I–V) measurements over 200 consecutive DC sweeps, as shown in Figure 2c. The device exhibits reproducible analog‐type switching behavior with inherent self‐rectification. A positive voltage bias (0 to +5 V) facilitates the transition from the high‐resistance state (HRS) to the low‐resistance state (LRS), whereas a negative bias (0 to −3.5 V) reinstates the HRS, achieving an ON/OFF resistance ratio exceeding 10⁵. The self‐rectifying behavior of the LSMO/Hf0.5Zr0.5O2–Hf0.95Sr0.05O2/Pt device arises from its asymmetric band structure and interfaces, where the p‐type LSMO and multilayer HZO/HSO facilitate carrier injection under forward bias, while the high Schottky barrier at Pt and interfacial potential barriers suppress reverse current (Figure S3, Supporting Information). The continuous and gradual modulation of conductance under successive voltage sweeps (0 to +3 V for set and 0 to −2.5 V for reset), as illustrated in Figure S4a,b (Supporting Information), highlights the analog resistive switching capability of the device. Furthermore, the device exhibits the retention performance exceeding 10⁶ s, as shown in Figure S4c (Supporting Information).

To investigate the microscopic origin of the resistive switching (RS) behavior, HR‐TEM was performed on the device both before and after electrical stimulation. Following a positive voltage sweep from 0 to +5 V (Figure 2c), a localized conductive filament was observed (Figure 2d–e). Fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis (Figure S5, Supporting Information) confirmed that the filament retained the same crystalline phase as the surrounding HZO–HSO superlattice, indicating that the RS behavior is not induced by a phase transition but is instead associated with the migration and redistribution of oxygen vacancies.[ 22 , 23 ] EELS analysis of the STO/LSMO/HZO–HSO/Pt memristor after a 0 to +5 V sweep shows that the conductive filament formed during the SET process originates from oxygen vacancies in the HZO–HSO superlattice, with dark regions indicating oxygen deficiency and red regions confirming the oxygen distribution (Figure S6, Supporting Information).To further clarify the roles of the individual components, control devices with only HSO or HZO as the gate dielectric were fabricated. To compare the electrical and ferroelectric properties of different interface structures, we systematically examined LSMO/HSO/Pt, LSMO/HZO/Pt, and LSMO/HZO–HSO/Pt devices (Figure S7, Supporting Information). The results show that the HSO and HZO–HSO structures are dominated by oxygen‐vacancy‐driven resistive switching, whereas only the HZO device exhibits clear ferroelectric switching. These results confirm that the engineered HZO–HSO superlattice contains a substantial concentration of oxygen vacancies,[ 24 , 25 ] which are the active species responsible for the analog RS behavior, and importantly, this behavior is achieved without the need for an initial forming process. Moreover, the current during the SET and RESET operations remains in the sub‐nanoampere range (≈1 nA, Figure 2c), suggesting a low‐power, non‐destructive switching mechanism. This stable and repeatable behavior is further supported by the presence of a thermodynamically stable monoclinic phase in the dielectric layer,[ 16 , 26 ] as identified by XRD in Figure S1 (Supporting Information). Together, these findings establish the LSMO/HZO–HSO/Pt heterostructure as a robust and energy‐efficient gate dielectric for advanced transistor designs with analog switching capabilities.

To realize a ReFET, we fabricated a gated device comprising an LSMO (20 nm)/HZO–HSO (15 nm)/Pt (10 nm)/HfO2 (3 nm) stack, as illustrated by the fabrication flowchart in Figure S8 (Supporting Information; see Experimental Section for details). To endow the device with optoelectronic sensory capabilities, IGZO was employed as the channel layer owing to its well‐known photoresponsive properties.[ 27 ] The optical properties of IGZO thin films deposited on quartz substrates were characterized using UV–Vis spectroscopy. The results revealed pronounced absorption in the blue wavelength range (Figure S9, Supporting Information), validating its potential for emulating synaptic photoresponses. The device architecture, engineered to replicate optoelectronic synaptic behavior, is schematically depicted in Figure 3a.

Figure 3b presents the top‐view micrograph of the completed device, exhibiting a channel length of 2.5 µm and a width of 25 µm. The electrical characteristics of the ReFET were examined via double‐sweep transfer (Ids–Vgs ) measurements under gate voltage sweeps from −3.5 to +6.5 V, repeated over 200 consecutive cycles (Figure 3c). The device demonstrates robust nonvolatile memory behavior with a high ON/OFF current ratio exceeding 10⁶. Simultaneously, gate leakage current (Igs ) measurements reveal characteristic resistive switching (RS) behavior within the gate stack (Figure 3d), corroborating that the nonvolatile memory effect in the IGZO channel arises from RS‐induced modulation of the gate field, attributed to the LSMO/HZO–HSO/Pt/HfO2 multilayer. Furthermore, the endurance performance of the ReFET device was evaluated under repetitive pulsed gate voltage conditions (+3 and −2 V, 10 µs pulse width). As shown in Figure 3d, the HZO–HSO superlattice ReFET exhibits excellent cycling stability, sustaining over 1010 switching/programming cycles without significant degradation (I ds), while maintaining minimal variation of the gate leakage current (I gs) that ensures negligible impact on power consumption, device lifetime, or performance. These results underscore the reliability and functional scalability of the HZO–HSO‐based RS gate for future integration into optoelectronic neuromorphic computing platforms.

In addition to exhibiting robust resistive switching behavior, the ReFETs also demonstrate essential synaptic functionalities, such as long‐term plasticity and multilevel conductance modulation, owing to their inherent analog switching characteristics. The long‐term potentiation (LTP) and depression (LTD) behaviors were evaluated by applying sequences of gate voltage pulses: 1000 consecutive positive pulses (2.5–4.5 V, 10 µs duration) for potentiation, and 1000 negative pulses (−1 to −3 V, 10 µs duration) for depression, under a constant drain voltage of V ds = 0.1 V and a readout interval of 2 ms. The corresponding synaptic weight evolution is depicted in Figure 3e,f.

To assess reliability and uniformity, both cycle‐to‐cycle (C2C) and device‐to‐device (D2D) variations of the synaptic plasticity were measured. C2C consistency was evaluated over 20 programming cycles, while D2D uniformity was extracted from measurements on an array of 400 distinct devices. The results reveal minimal variation, highlighting the excellent reproducibility of the analog switching process. We implemented a write–verify programming strategy in a 20 × 20 ReFET array with carefully controlled bit‐line and word‐line voltages to ensure reliable read/write operations of the selected device while keeping unselected devices in a high‐resistance state (see Figures S10 and S11, Supporting Information), and characterized multilevel resistive states across the full 400‐device array, achieving up to 8‐bit precision in nonvolatile conductance due to the large dynamic range and highly linear analog switching enabled by the HZO–HSO superlattice gate dielectric. To validate array‐level programming capability, we applied iterative write–verify operations to a single column of 20 ReFET devices within the 20 × 20 array, achieving 272 distinct and stable conductance states with over 8‐bit resolution, demonstrating that ReFET arrays can reliably support precise, reproducible, and fine‐grained multilevel programming (Figure S12, Supporting Information). The ReFET device demonstrates high write accuracy across 272 conductance states (>8‐bit), with write errors decreasing from ±1.8 to ±0.12 nA as the target current increases, indicating higher precision at larger conductance states. The state‐to‐state conductance resolution spans from 4.8 to 0.81 µA, with most resolutions concentrated near 0.81 µA, ensuring uniform and distinguishable increments across the 8‐bit range (Figures S13 and S14, Supporting Information). To study the effect of temperature on its multilevel states, the 272 ReFET conductance states were measured and found to exhibit stable retention up to 1 × 10⁶ s at room temperature, with moderate, temperature‐dependent decay at 85 and 125 °C; low‐resistance states proved more thermally robust than high‐resistance states, maintaining compatibility with in‐memory computing (Figure S15, Supporting Information). These features render the device architecture particularly promising for high‐accuracy in‐memory computing applications, where fine‐grained weight modulation is critical.[ 28 ] To evaluate the integration scale of arrays while maintaining a read margin above 50%, we analyzed the combined effects of IR drop and sneak paths, which reduce the sensing margin and shift the optimal resistance, demonstrating reliable operation up to a 315 × 315 array (Figure S16, Supporting Information).

To further emulate the functionality of optically responsive synapses, the photonic behavior of the ReFET device was systematically investigated under varying light intensities.[ 29 ] In this context, the illumination power serves as an independent stimulus to modulate the output current of the optoelectronic ReFETs. As illustrated in Figure 3h, transfer characteristics were recorded under laser illumination intensities ranging from 25 to 85 mW mm−2. A progressive shift in the threshold voltage is observed with increasing optical power, indicative of enhanced carrier generation and improved channel conductivity, which facilitates more efficient charge transport under photoexcitation.[ 30 ] Excitation with 450 nm light was chosen for IGZO because it optimally balances absorption by defect‐induced subgap states and efficient carrier generation, whereas longer wavelengths yield weaker photocurrent due to reduced excitation efficiency and increased recombination.[ 31 , 32 ]

The device exhibits a monotonic increase in photocurrent as a function of incident light intensity, attributable to the elevated density of photogenerated electron–hole pairs in the IGZO channel.[ 29 ] This behavior highlights the potential of the ReFET platform as an optical sensory synapse, capable of transducing external light stimuli into electrical signals with tunable response, a key feature for neuromorphic vision computing systems.[ 5 , 9 , 30 ] Importantly, the integration of optoelectronic responsiveness with analog resistive switching in a single device represents a major advance in the field. The HZO–HSO superlattice‐based ReFET array not only demonstrates 8‐bit multilevel memory states and ultrahigh endurance exceeding 1010 cycles, but also supports light intensity–dependent signal modulation. Besides, multilevel resistance states remained stable and fully recoverable under light illumination, confirming the reliability of IGZO/HZO–HSO devices against optical exposure (Figure S17, Supporting Information).

To realize a fully integrated neuromorphic vision computing hardware platform, we developed a 20 × 20 ReFETs array monolithically integrated with peripheral decoder circuitry and an optical input setup, as illustrated in Figure 4a. The architecture of the in‐sensor computing system comprises a photonic ReFET array serving as the core in‐sensor computing engine, decoder circuits for data acquisition, and a field‐programmable gate array (FPGA) to implement system‐level control logic. This integrated platform enables direct optical sensing and in‐memory computation within the same physical layer, effectively mimicking the functionality of a biological retina.

Conventional vision transformer (ViT) architectures typically rely on flattening non‐overlapping image patches and projecting them into high‐dimensional token embeddings via fully connected (FC) layers (Figure 5a).[ 34 , 35 , 36 ] While effective in software, such FC‐based projection lacks hierarchical feature extraction and demands pixel‐wise slicing operations, which pose challenges for efficient hardware implementation[ 35 ] To overcome these limitations, we propose replacing the FC layer with a photonic convolutional projection layer realized by the ReFET array, forming the basis of a convolutional vision transformer (Cvt),[ 37 ] as illustrated in Figure 5b. This modification enables direct in‐sensor convolution and optical feature encoding by leveraging the intrinsic MAC operation and light sensitivity of ReFET devices.

We implemented an in‐sensor vision transformer using a 20 × 20 ReFET array, configured to realize sixteen 5 × 5 convolutional kernels for spatial feature extraction from Fashion‐MNIST[ 38 ] images (input size: 20 × 20 pixels), as depicted in Figure 5c. The learned weights of each convolutional kernel were programmed into the ReFET conductance states via analog tuning. Figure 5d shows the structure of the convolutional layer, and Figure 5e presents an example of optical edge detection obtained using a 5 × 5 ReFET‐based kernel. The network was trained offline using 70 000 training images over 20 epochs, with the training loss convergence shown in Figure 5f. The trained weights were quantized into 8‐, 4‐, 3‐, and 2‐bit conductance states (Figure 5g) and implemented in hardware.

Experimental evaluations demonstrated that the 8‐bit ReFET‐based optic in‐sensor vision transformer achieved a classification accuracy of 94.45%, closely matching the results from software‐based simulations. Additionally, a 4 × 4 ReFET‐based optical convolutional network for letter recognition achieved stable kernel training and 86.75% accuracy, as shown in Figures S18–S20 (Supporting Information), validating online learning and demonstrating the capability of the array for in‐memory computing. This work achieves record conductance states, exceptional energy efficiency, and online training capability, enabling direct optical image processing and high‐accuracy neuromorphic inference with integrated memory and sensing, while comparative analysis (Table S1, Supporting Information) confirms clear advantages over state‐of‐the‐art devices in multifunctionality, retention, endurance, and optical adaptability.[ 9 , 10 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 20 ] Together, these results mark a significant step toward photonic in‐memory computing architectures for next‐generation, energy‐efficient neuromorphic vision systems.

Conclusion

We report a photonic ReFET array based on a HZO–HSO superlattice gate stack that integrates nonvolatile memory, optical sensing, and synaptic plasticity within a single device. High‐resolution transmission electron microscopy and EELS/EDXS analyses confirm that oxygen‐vacancy‐mediated conductive filament formation underlies the robust analog resistive switching in the superlattice gate. The ReFET exhibits 272 stable (>8‐bit) conductance states, ON/OFF ratios >10⁶, endurance >1010 cycles, and retention exceeding 10⁶ s, enabling high‐performance long‐term synaptic plasticity. The IGZO channel provides photonic sensitivity, allowing light‐dependent conductance modulation and optical sensory synaptic dynamics. A 20 × 20 ReFET array functions as an in‐sensor optical convolutional layer within a convolutional vision transformer framework, achieving 94.45% classification accuracy on Fashion‐MNIST with 8‐bit quantized weights, while delivering high energy efficiency (6.664 × 10−3 TOPS/W). This work establishes a scalable, high‐precision, and energy‐efficient platform for integrated photonic neuromorphic computing, enabling simultaneous sensing, memory, and computation at the device level.

Experimental Section

Thin Film Preparation

A 20 nm LSMO bottom electrode was deposited on (111)‐oriented SrTiO3 (STO) substrates by pulsed laser deposition (PLD) using a KrF excimer laser (λ = 248 nm, fluence: 1 J cm− 2, 5 Hz). Subsequently, alternating layers of HZO and HSO were deposited sequentially to form a 16 nm superlattice gate dielectric. The laser fluence for HZO and HSO deposition was 1.2 J cm− 2 at 3 Hz. All oxide layers were grown at 850 °C under an oxygen pressure of 100 mTorr, followed by cooling to room temperature at 10 °C min−1 in 1000 mTorr O2.

X‐Ray Diffraction (XRD)

Crystallinity of the films was characterized by high‐resolution XRD (Rigaku SmartLab) using Cu Kα1 radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). To accurately identify the diffraction peaks corresponding to the HSO/HZO superlattices and extract their crystal phases, X‐ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was first performed with reference to ICDD PDF cards 00‐034‐0104 (monoclinic HfO2), 04‐002‐0037 (cubic HfO2), and 04‐005‐5597 (orthorhombic HfO2).

ReFET Array Fabrication

A 20 × 20 array of ReFET devices was fabricated on LSMO/STO. After deposition of the HZO–HSO gate stack, a 10 nm Pt top electrode was deposited by DC magnetron sputtering. A 3 nm HfO2 interfacial layer was grown by atomic layer deposition (ALD) at 260 °C. Gate structures were patterned using maskless photolithography and dry etching. A 20 nm IGZO channel layer was deposited by RF sputtering in Ar (3 mTorr, 80 W). Source/drain contacts (35 nm Ni) were deposited by e‐beam evaporation and patterned via liftoff. A 1 µm SiO2 passivation layer was added using PECVD, and 10 nm Ti / 200 nm Au interconnects were formed by magnetron sputtering (see Figure S6, Supporting Information).

HRTEM Characterization

Cross‐sectional microstructure and interfaces of the LSMO/HZO–HSO/Pt stack were analyzed using aberration‐corrected STEM and HRTEM. TEM lamellae were prepared by focused ion beam (FIB). High‐angle annular dark‐field (HAADF) STEM imaging was performed with a 30 mrad probe angle and 45.9–200 mrad collection angles.

Electrical and Optical Characterization

DC and pulsed electrical measurements were performed using a Keysight B2912B source meter and a custom test board. Optical absorption of IGZO films was measured with a Shimadzu UV‐3600Plus spectrophotometer. Photonic ReFET behavior was evaluated using a custom optical setup incorporating a digital micromirror device (DMD) and lens system.
