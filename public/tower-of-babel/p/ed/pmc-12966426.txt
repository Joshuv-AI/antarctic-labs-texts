PRDM1 restricts bladder cancer progression and enhances chemosensitivity by suppressing OTUD6A-mediated deubiquitination of CDC6
Authors: Jianfeng Cui, Shouzhen Chen, Xiaochen Liu, Xuewen Jiang, Guangzhou Cheng, Zhifeng Liu, Hui Zhao, Yaofeng Zhu, Benkang Shi, Yongxin Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPR domain zinc finger protein 1 (PRDM1) functions as a critical transcriptional repressor. The role of PRDM1 in various tumors is controversial, and its specific mechanism in bladder cancer (BCa) remains unclear. In the present study, we demonstrated that PRDM1 expression is downregulated in both human BCa tissues and BBN-induced mouse models of BCa. Gain- and loss-of-function experiments revealed that PRDM1 delays cell cycle progression, suppresses BCa cell proliferation, and enhances chemosensitivity, whereas PRDM1 knockdown promotes cell proliferation and induces chemoresistance. Ovarian tumor deubiquitinase 6 A (OTUD6A) is a deubiquitinating enzyme that prevents the proteasomal degradation of CDC6. PRDM1 directly binds to the OTUD6A promoter and suppresses its transcription, thereby reducing CDC6 deubiquitination and promoting its degradation. Knockdown of CDC6 or OTUD6A abrogates the protective effects of PRDM1 both in vitro and in vivo. Consistently, PRDM1 expression is negatively correlated with CDC6 and OTUD6A expression in BCa tissues. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PRDM1 acts as a tumor suppressor in BCa by inhibiting OTUD6A transcription and promoting CDC6 degradation. We propose a PRDM1‒OTUD6A‒CDC6 axis model, providing novel insights into PRDM1 as a potential therapeutic target in BCa.Subject terms: Bladder cancer, Cell growth

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BCa) ranks as the tenth most commonly diagnosed malignancy worldwide, with approximately 573,000 new cases and 212,000 deaths annually [1]. BCa can be divided into two types, namely, non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) and muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC), with proportions of 75% and 25%, respectively. Approximately 30% of patients with NMIBC experience progression to MIBC [2]. Despite advances in surgery and immunotherapies, chemotherapy remains a cornerstone treatment for MIBC and metastatic disease. However, over 80% of patients exhibit intrinsic or acquired resistance to chemotherapy, leading to treatment failure and poor survival outcomes [3]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to comprehensively understand the molecular mechanism of tumor development and chemoresistance in BCa.

The PR domain zinc finger protein 1 (PRDM1) gene, which encodes the transcriptional repressor B-lymphocyte-induced maturation protein 1 (BLIMP1), was initially identified as a master regulator of plasma cell differentiation in B lymphocytes [4]. PRDM1 also governs T-cell exhaustion and maintains epithelial homeostasis [5]. Recent studies have shown that the role of PRDM1 in various tumors is dichotomous. On the one hand, PRDM1 acts as a tumor suppressor and inhibits MDR1 transcription to reverse doxorubicin resistance in diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL) [6]. On the other hand, PRDM1 acts as an oncogene in liver cancer via the USP7–PRDM1–FGL1 pathway to attenuate CD8+ T-cell activity [7]. Interestingly, PRDM1 plays a suppressive role by inhibiting hepatocellular carcinoma cell growth, and it also upregulates PD-L1 levels to induce cancer immune evasion [8]. The role of PRDM1 in BCa is unclear. To our knowledge, only one study has suggested that PRDM1 inhibits c-Fos expression and plays a tumor suppressive role in BCa [9]. However, the function of PRDM1 in chemoresistance and the detailed mechanism of PRDM1 in BCa remain unexplored. Elucidating the role of PRDM1 in BCa may reveal novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets to overcome treatment resistance.

In the present study, we demonstrate that PRDM1 is downregulated in BCa samples compared with normal samples. We further characterized the pivotal role of PRDM1 in inhibiting tumor growth and enhancing chemosensitivity by transcriptionally repressing OTUD6A, thus decreasing CDC6 protein levels, which identifies PRDM1 as the upstream factor of the OTUD6A-CDC6 axis that we previously reported [10]. Together, our findings reveal that PRDM1 constrains BCa progression and chemoresistance through suppressing of the OTUD6A‒CDC6 pathway in BCa.

Materials And Methods

Clinical tissue specimens

A total of 48 paired surgical fresh BCa tumor and paired adjacent normal tissues were obtained from Qilu Hospital of Shandong University. The inclusion criteria were as follows: [1] histologically confirmation of urothelial BCa by experienced pathologists [2] absence of concurrent primary malignancies and distant metastases [3] no treatment with neoadjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee, Shandong University of Clinical Medicine, and informed consent was obtained from all enrolled patients or their relatives before surgery.

Cell culture and reagents

HEK293T and UMUC3 cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in DMEM (Gibco, 11995065). T24 cell line were obtained from ATCC and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, 11875093). The medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, 10099141 C). The cells were maintained in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Mycoplasma contamination was routinely excluded using PCR-based detection kits.

To establish gemcitabine-resistant (GR) UMUC3 cells, wild-type UMUC3 cells were initially cultured in medium containing 10 μg/L gemcitabine, and increasing concentrations of gemcitabine over multiple passages. The treatment concentration is gradually escalated every 2–3 cell cycles whenever stable proliferation is observed, spanning over 6 months until reaching nearly 5 times the original half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50).

N-butyl-N-(4-hydroxybutyl) nitrosamine (BBN, B0938) was purchased from TCI. Gemcitabine (HY-17026), Cisplatin (HY-17394), MG132 (HY-13259), chloroquine (HY-17589A), and cycloheximide (HY-12320) were purchased from MedChemExpress. Thymidine (T1895) and nocodazole (M1404) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Plasmids and transfection

The human OTUD6A plasmid and the HA-Ub plasmids were provided by Pro. CJ Gao [11]. The Flag-PRDM1 and Myc-CDC6 plasmids were purchased from GeneCopoeia (Guangzhou, China). The lentiviral vectors carrying a shRNA targeting human CDC6 were purchased from GeneChem Inc. (Shanghai, China), and targeting human PRDM1 were purchased from Abiotech (Jinan, China). Cells at 70% confluence were transfected with the indicated plasmid DNA using LipofectamineTM 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, L3000015) following the manufacturer’s optimized ratio of 1:2 (DNA:reagent). The mixture between plasmid DNA and transfection reagent was carried out in serum-free Opti-MEM medium for 15 minutes at room temperature prior to cellular administration. The media was replaced after 6 h to mitigate cytotoxicity, and subsequent experimental analyses were conducted 24–48 h post-transfection.

Lentivirus production and transduction

Lentivirus particles were generated using a standardized three-plasmid system as previously reported [12]. Briefly, HEK293T cells were cotransfected with shRNA constructs (target-specific or negative control, shNC), packaging plasmid psPAX2, and envelope plasmid pMD2.G using LipofectamineTM 3000 transfection reagent. Viral supernatants were harvested 48 h post-transfection, clarified through 0.45 μm filters (Millipore).

For the construction of cells with stable PRDM1-, CDC6-, and OTUD6A- knockdown/overexpression, cells were transduced with the indicated lentiviruses. PRDM1 overexpression, CDC6 overexpression and OTUD6A knockdown/overexpression and indicated control lentiviruses were purchased from Genechem Inc. (Shanghai, China). Lentiviruses were used to transduct cells in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene for 48 h, followed by 5 day puromycin selection (1 μg/mL) to establish resistant populations. Stably overexpression/knockdown cells were identified and confirmed by RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis.

Generating CRISPR-Cas9 based PRDM1 knockout cell

CRISPR-Cas9 mediated ablation of the PRDM1 gene was achieved with CRISPR-Cas9 RNP (provided by Haixing Bioscience) containing expression cassettes for hSpCas9 and chimeric guide RNA. To target exon 5 and 7 of the PRDM1 gene, two guide RNA sequences of CTTCTAGAATGAGAGTGCGT TGG and GAATGGCCACAGGACTCGGT GGG were selected through the http://crispor.tefor.net. RNP complexes were electrotransfected into cells using Neon transfection system according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After two days, single colonies were transferred into 96-well plates. To determine the presence of deletions in PRDM1 targeted clones, genomic DNA was isolated using a Quick-DNA Miniprep kit and PCR amplification was achieved using 2 × Taq Master Mix (Dye Plus; Vazyme, P112) of primers flanking exon: Forward: 5’- CCCCACCCCAGTATTTTTCCA-3’; Reverse: 5’-TCCTGCACTACTGGACACAC-3’. Genomic DNA were retrieved from the single clones and sequenced by Sanger sequencing. Clones with mutations in both alleles were selected for downstream studies.

Total RNA extraction, reverse transcription PCR and qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using SteadyPure Quick RNA Extraction Kit (ACCURATE BIOTECHNOLOGY (HUNAN) CO., LTD, ChangSha, China, AG21023) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA integrity was verified by spectrophotometric A260/A280 ratios (1.8–2.0). Reverse transcription was performed with 1 μg total RNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (ACCURATE BIOTECHNOLOGY(HUNAN) CO., LTD, ChangSha, China, AG11706) under thermal cycling conditions: 37 °C for 15 min (cDNA synthesis) followed by 85 °C for 5 s (enzyme inactivation).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted using the LightCycler 96 system (Roche, Mannheim, Germany). Cycling parameters included initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, 55 °C for 30 s and 72 °C for 30 s. Melt curve analysis (65–95 °C, 0.5 °C increments) confirmed primer specificity. Relative mRNA levels were calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method using GAPDH as the endogenous control. Gene-specific primer pairs for qRT-PCR analysis were commercially synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and described in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blot

The protein lysates (30 μg/sample) were resolved on 10/12% SDS-PAGE gels and electrotransferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010) at 100 V for 60 min. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST (Tris-buffered saline, 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer at 4 °C overnight. After three 10 min TBST washes, membranes were probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Chemiluminescent signals were developed using BeyoECL Plus (Beyotime, P0018FM) and captured on an Amersham™ ImageQuant™ 800 instrument (GE Healthcare, Fairfield, USA). Band densitometry was performed using ImageJ software (v1.6.0, NIH, USA) with actin normalization. The primary antibodies are listed in Supplementary Table 2, and the original WB figures were shown in Figs. S9–11.

CCK-8, colony formation and EdU incorporation assays

CCK-8, colony formation, and EdU incorporation assays were performed as previously described [10]. Briefly, for the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay, indicated cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured for 1–5 days under experimental conditions. At each time point, 10 μL of CCK-8 (NCM Biotech, C6005) reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. More than triplicate wells were analyzed for each condition. The IC50 value was defined as the concentration causing a 50% decrease in cell viability. The IC50 values were estimated by nonlinear regression using a variable Hill slope model. GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2, GraphPad Software, CA, USA) software was used to calculate cell viability.

For the colony formation assay, 800 cells/well were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 7–14 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 minutes, stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbio, G1063) for 30 minutes, and manually counted using a threshold of ≥ 50 cells/colony. Three biological replicates were performed per experimental group.

The EdU (5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine) incorporation assay was performed using the Cell-Light™ EdU Apollo 567 In Vitro Kit (Ribobio, C10310-1, Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, indicated cells grown on coverslips were pulsed with 10 μM EdU for 30 min, fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and subjected to EdU staining solution for 1 h. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, F6057). Fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (NE910FL, Nexcope).

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP-fluorescein nick end labelling (TUNEL) assay

The TUNEL assay was performed using a one-step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (Beyotime, C1089) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were cultured on coverslips and exposed to gemcitabine for 48 h. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, incubated with TUNEL mixture for 1 h and subsequently stained with DAPI. Fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (NE910FL, Nexcope).

Immunofluorescence

Cells were seeded on cover slips, cultured under different conditions and harvested at the indicated times. The cover slips were washed with PBS three times and fixed with 4% PFA for 20 min. Fixed cells were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 20 min, followed by blocking in 5% goat serum diluted in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. The cover slips were incubated with anti-γH2A.X primary antibody overnight. The cover slips were probed with Alexa Fluor™ 594-conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam, ab150080) for 1 h protected from light. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (NE910FL, Nexcope).

Alkaline comet assay

DNA strand breaks were assessed using the Single Cell Gel Electrophoresis Assay Kit (Trevigen, 4250-050-K) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, indicated cells were harvested and resuspended in ice-cold PBS (Ca2+ and Mg2+ free). Cell suspensions were mixed with molten low-melting agarose at 1:10 (v/v) ratio, and immediately layered onto CometSlide™ (50 μL/well). Solidification was achieved by 4 °C incubation in darkness for 10 min followed by lysis in pre-cooled lysis buffer at 4 °C overnight. Slides were subsequently immersed in alkaline unwinding solution at 4 °C for 60 min. Electrophoresis was conducted in fresh alkaline buffer and DNA was stained with DAPI and imaged with a fluorescence microscope (NE910FL, Nexcope). Quantitative analysis was used CometScore software (version 1.5).

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq)

RNA sequencing was performed by Majorbio (Shanghai, China) and transcriptome library was prepared following Illumina® Stranded mRNA Prep Ligation from Illumina (San Diego, CA) using 1 μg of total RNA. Libraries were size selected for cDNA target fragments of 300 bp through 2% Low Range Ultra Agarose, followed by PCR amplified using Phusion DNA polymerase (NEB) for 15 PCR cycles. Quantified libraries were sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten/NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were processed using SeqPrep and Sickle for adapter trimming and quality filtering. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome in strand-specific mode using HISAT2, followed by reference-guided transcript assembly with StringTie. Transcript expression levels were quantified via transcripts per million (TPM) normalization. RSEM was employed to estimate transcript-level abundance. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2.

Flow cytometry

Cell cycle analysis was performed using propidium iodide (PI)-based DNA quantification. Briefly, indicated cells were harvested using trypsin-EDTA, centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at 4 °C rpm for 3 min, and washed once with cold PBS. Cells were fixed with 70% ethanol for 16 h at −20 °C and stained with PI solution in the dark for 30 min at room temperature. Sample acquisition was performed on a flow cytometer (Cytoflex S, Beckman Coulter, California, USA). Cell cycle phase distribution was quantified using ModFit LT 5.0.9 (Verity Software House).

Immunoprecipitation

Immunoprecipitation (IP) was conducted using an Immunoprecipitation System Kit (Proteintech, PK10007) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cell lysates were prepared by incubating cells in IP lysis buffer with protease inhibitors at 4 °C for 30 min. The lysate was subsequently clarified through centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. 2,000 μg of total protein was loaded onto the spin column with end cap in combination with 1–2 μg of the indicated antibody and 150–300 μL incubation buffer. The mixture was subjected to continuous agitation on a rotary platform at 4 °C overnight. Protein A/G beads slurry was added to the column and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C. The end cap was plucked off and the column was washed by washing buffer 4 times. The proteins were then eluted with 80 μL of elution buffer. Eluates were denatured by boiling in 10 uL alkali neutralization buffer and 23 uL 5× sample buffer at 99 °C for 5 min prior to western blot.

In vivo deubiquitination assay

HEK293T cells were co-transfected with indicated plasmids for 24 h. And the cells were treated with 20 μM MG132 for 6 h to stabilize ubiquitinated substrates. The cells were harvested and used for IP assay to analyse the ubiquitination levels of target protein.

Protein half-life assay

For protein stability analysis, indicated cells were exposed to cycloheximide (CHX, 20 μg/mL), and collected at indicated time-points. The samples were lysed and used for Western blot.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and ChIP-seq

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed by using an EpiQuik Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Kit (Epigentek, P-2002) following the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, cells were harvested and crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde. DNA and proteins were sheared into fragments by ultrasonication. Supernatants were then transferred into a 96-well plate that was precoated with an anti-PRDM1 antibody, followed by washing. DNA crosslinking was reversed, and DNA was purified and analysed by PCR and qPCR. The PCR primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

ChIP-seq was performed by BGI (ShenZhen, China). ChIP-derived DNA fragments undergo end-repair, and the blunt fragments. Adapter ligation is performed under optimized reaction conditions to attach sequencing adapters. The PCR reaction system and program are configured and set up to amplify the product. The library quality control protocol will be selected depending upon product requirements. Denaturation generates single-stranded PCR products, which are then circularized through precisely controlled enzymatic reactions. Residual linear DNA molecules are selectively digested to ensure purity of cyclized products. Single-stranded circle DNA molecules are replicated via rolling cycle amplification, and a DNA nanoball (DNB) which contain multiple copies of DNA is generated. Sufficient quality DNBs are then loaded into patterned nanoarrays using high-intensity DNA nanochip technique, followed by sequencing through combinatorial Probe-Anchor Synthesis (cPAS).

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

HEK293T cells were cultured in 24-well plates. Five hundred nanograms of OTUD6A promoter reporter plasmid and fifty nanograms of pRL-TK plasmid were cotransfected with five hundred nanograms of Flag vector or Flag-PRDM1 plasmid into HEK293T cells for 24 h. Luciferase activity was determined using the Luc-Pair™ Duo-Luciferase Assay Kit (GeneCopoeia, LF001) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and luminescence was measured with a Centro XS LB 960 luminometer (Berthold Technologies).

Mouse models

Four-week-old female BALB/c (nu/nu) mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (JiangSu, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with 12 h light/dark cycles and ad libitum access to irradiated feed.

5 × 10⁶ indicated cells in 100 μL PBS were subcutaneously implanted into the right dorsal flank. Tumor progression was monitored every 4 days using vernier caliper measurement, and mice were sacrificed at indicated time. Tumor volumes were calculated as (a × b2)/2, where a and b represent the longest and shortest diameters, respectively. For gemcitabine efficacy studies, mice bearing about 100 mm³ tumors (Day 10–14 post-implantation) were randomized into vehicle control (5% DMSO in saline) and treatment (50 mg/kg gemcitabine in vehicle) groups. Intraperitoneal injections were administered weekly for two cycles, and the mice were sacrificed at indicated time. The tumors were resected and fixed in 4% PFA for IHC staining.

For establishment of N-butyl-N-(4-hydroxybutyl) nitrosamine (BBN)-induced bladder carcinogenesis model, eight-week-old C57BL/6 J male mice (8 weeks old) received ad libitum administration of 0.05% BBN in sterile tap water for 12 consecutive weeks, followed by regular sterile water until experimental termination. Age-matched control groups received sterile tap water throughout the study period. To examine the change of PRDM1 protein expression during BCa progression, mice were systematically euthanized at predetermined intervals. The bladder specimens were surgically excised, paraffin-embedded, and processed for immunohistochemical analysis. All experimental protocols received formal approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shandong University and were conducted in strict accordance with international laboratory animal care standards.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections were sequentially dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol series, followed by distilled water rinsing. Antigen retrieval was performed via microwave irradiation in preheated EDTA buffer (Proteintech, PR30002) with subsequent cooling to room temperature. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H₂O₂ in methanol for 20 min. Non-specific binding sites were saturated with 5% BSA (Solarbio, SW3015) for 1 h. Sections were incubated with indicated primary antibodies in humidified chambers at 4 °C overnight. The sections were incubated with indicated HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h, and chromogenic development used DAB substrate (ZSGB-BIO, ZLI-9018) with hematoxylin counterstaining. Dehydration employed reverse ethanol gradient and xylene clearing prior to resin mounting. The sections were imaged with a microscope (BX43FC, Olympus Life Science). The protein intensity was defined as follows: 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), and 3 (strong). And the histochemical score (H-score) was calculated as: H-score = Σ(1 × % weak cells + 2 × % moderate cells + 3 × % strong cells) with a maximum potential score of 300.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry and Multichannel imaging

Deparaffinization of TMA was done through xylenes and rehydration through decreasing graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed in AR6 buffer (Akoya Biosciences) using microwave irradiation. Endogenous peroxidase was inactivated by incubation in 3% H2O2 for 10 min. Multiplex IHC was performed by several rounds of staining, each cycle comprised a protein block with 1% BSA followed by primary antibody and corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Akoya Biosciences). The slides were then incubated in different Opal fluorophore (1:100) diluted in 1× Plus Amplification Diluent (Akoya Biosciences). Antibody stripping between cycles was achieved through microwave-mediated antigen retrieval. The sequence of primary antibody and Opal fluorophore was anti-PRDM1/Opal 520, anti-CDC6/Opal 620, anti-OTUD6A/Opal 570, and anti-CK/Opal 480. All slides were counterstained with spectral DAPI (Akoya Biosciences) and mounted with Anti-fade fluorescence mounting medium (Abcam, ab104135). Whole-slide multichannel imaging was performed on a PANNORAMIC SCAN II Imaging System (3Dhistech, Hungary).

Statistical analysis

All quantitative analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2, GraphPad Software, CA, USA). The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) values. Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used to compare independent group comparisons. Two-tailed paired Student’s t-test was used to compare matched BCa tissue analyses. Categorical variables were analyzed through chi-square test. Bivariate correlations between continuous variables employed Spearman correlation analysis. The P-values in RNA-seq and pathway analyses were adjusted by Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) test. P-values < 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

results

PRDM1 expression is downregulated in BCa

We first investigated the expression level of PRDM1 in 48 paired specimens of human BCa tissues and adjacent normal tissues. WB and IHC analyses revealed markedly lower PRDM1 protein levels in BCa tissues than in matched normal tissues (Fig. 1A–C). Analysis of TCGA and GEO (GSE13507) datasets also revealed that PRDM1 mRNA levels were significantly lower in BCa tissues than in normal tissues (Fig. 1D, E). To further explore the dynamic regulation of PRDM1 expression during bladder carcinogenesis, we employed a BBN-induced BCa murine model. The PRDM1 protein levels progressively decreased with prolonged BBN exposure (Fig. 1F), suggesting that PRDM1 expression is suppressed during BCa development.

PRDM1 suppresses the cell cycle progression and tumor growth of BCa cells

Given the downregulated expression of PRDM1 in BCa tissues, we hypothesized that PRDM1 has a potential tumor-suppressive function. To test this hypothesis, we generated stable PRDM1-knockdown BCa cell lines from T24 cells (Fig. S1A) and performed RNA-seq analysis on PRDM1-knockdown and control T24 cells. RT‒qPCR validation of the RNA-seq results demonstrated 90% concordance among randomly selected targets (18 out of 20 genes; Fig. S1B). Transcriptomic profiling revealed 2,373 differentially expressed genes (853 upregulated genes and 1,520 downregulated genes; FDR < 0.05; Fig. S1C). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed that PRDM1 was associated with the “cell cycle” and “DNA replication” processes (Fig. 2A). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed the following enriched GO terms: the biological process (BP) terms included “cell cycle process”, “mitotic cell cycle progress” and “DNA damage response”; the cellular component (CC) terms included “membrane-bound organelle”, “site of DNA damage” and “protein‒DNA complex”; and the molecular function (MF) terms included “protein binding”, “DNA binding” and “chromatin binding” (Fig. 2B and Fig. S1D, E). Additionally, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further confirmed that PRDM1 was involved in the cell cycle pathway (Fig. 2C). KEGG and GO enrichment analyses were performed using the RNA-seq data from GEO datasets (GSE31684 and GSE48276). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that PRDM1 was associated with the cell cycle pathway, and the enriched GO terms included “mitotic spindle organization”, “positive regulation of growth”, “replication fork” and “mitotic cell cycle phase transition” (Figure. S1F–I). On the basis of these analyses, we next evaluated the cell cycle distribution by flow cytometry. We constructed stable PRDM1-overexpressing T24 cells (Fig. S1J). Neither the knockdown nor the overexpression of PRDM1 affected the cell cycle distribution of asynchronous T24 cells (Fig. S1K, L). However, synchronized cell cycle analysis revealed that PRDM1 overexpression significantly delayed cell cycle progression in T24 cells following thymidine-nocodazole treatment (Fig. 2D). We further constructed stable PRDM1-knockdown and PRDM1-overexpressing UMUC3 cells (Fig. S1J, M), and neither the knockdown nor the overexpression of PRDM1 affected the cell cycle distribution of asynchronous UMUC3 cells (Fig. S1K, L).

We further investigated the effect of PRDM1 on the growth of BCa tumors. Knockdown of PRDM1 markedly enhanced the proliferation of T24 and UMUC3 cells (Fig. 2E, F and Fig. S2A–C). Conversely, PRDM1 overexpression significantly suppressed cell proliferation, as demonstrated by CCK-8, colony formation, and EdU incorporation assays in both T24 and UMUC3 cells (Fig. 2G, H and Fig. S2D–F), indicating that PRDM1 functions as a negative regulator of BCa cell proliferation. To extend our findings, we established PRDM1-knockout UMUC3 cells using CRISPR/Cas9 system (Fig. S2G, H). Consistent with the results in PRDM1-knockdown UMUC3 cells, knockout of PRDM1 also markedly enhanced the proliferation of UMUC3 cells (Fig. 2I and Fig. S2I, J). To confirm the suppressive effects of PRDM1 on BCa cell proliferation in vivo, we utilized a subcutaneous xenograft model in BALB/c-nude mice. PRDM1-knockdown tumors exhibited accelerated growth (Fig. 2J–L and Fig. S2K). The protein levels of Ki-67, a classic marker of cell proliferation, were significantly elevated in the PRDM1-knockdown tumors (Fig. 2M). Conversely, ectopic PRDM1 expression in T24 and UMUC3 cells resulted in a marked reduction in both tumor volume and tumor weight, accompanied by reduced Ki-67 expression levels (Fig. 2N–Q and Fig. S2L). Collectively, these results demonstrated that PRDM1 functions as a tumor suppressor in BCa both in vitro and in vivo.

PRDM1 potentiates chemosensitivity to gemcitabine in BCa cells

KEGG and GO enrichment analyses of the RNA-seq and GEO datasets revealed that PRDM1 was associated with “DNA damage response”, “damaged DNA binding”, “apoptosis-multiple species” and “drug metabolism” (Fig. 2B and Fig. S1E–G). Moreover, GSEA confirmed that PRDM1 was involved in the apoptosis pathway (Fig. S3A). Because chemotherapy plays a vital role in BCa treatment, we investigated the role of PRDM1 in the regulation of chemosensitivity to gemcitabine. The CCK-8 assays revealed that the knockdown and knockout of PRDM1 significantly elevated the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of gemcitabine in T24 and UMUC3 cells (Fig. 3A and Fig. S3B, C). Conversely, PRDM1 overexpression increased the chemosensitivity of BCa cells to gemcitabine (Fig. 3B and Fig. S3D). Cisplatin is another widely used chemotherapeutic agent in BCa treatment. As shown in Fig. S3E–I, both knockdown and knockout of PRDM1 significantly elevated the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of cisplatin in T24 and UMUC3 cell lines. Conversely, PRDM1 overexpression markedly increased the sensitivity of BCa cells to cisplatin. Dose‒response clonogenic assays further confirmed the role of PRDM1 in enhancing the chemosensitivity of BCa cells to gemcitabine (Fig. 3C and Fig. S3J, K). TUNEL assays were used to evaluate the antiapoptotic function of PRDM1. Knockdown and knockout of PRDM1 decreased apoptosis in gemcitabine-treated cells, while the overexpression of PRDM1 increased apoptosis in gemcitabine-treated cells; however, these changes were not observed in untreated cells (Fig. 3D and Fig. S3L-P). Consistently, the levels of cleaved caspase-3, an executor of apoptosis, were significantly decreased in the PRDM1-knockdown cells but increased in the PRDM1-overexpressing cells (Fig. 3E and Fig. S3Q).

We then analyzed the role of PRDM1 in the DNA damage caused by gemcitabine treatment in BCa cells. γH2A.X staining revealed that PRDM1 knockdown and knockout reduced DNA damage, whereas PRDM1 overexpression enhanced gemcitabine-induced DNA damage (Fig. 3F and Fig. S3R–U). Alkaline comet assays also demonstrated that knockdown and knockout of PRDM1 decreased comet tail formation in gemcitabine-treated cells, whereas PRDM1 overexpression increased comet tailing (Fig. 3G, H and Fig. S3V, W). Given the important role of Ataxia telangiectasia and the Rad3-related protein (ATR)-Chk1 pathway in the DNA damage response (DDR) and in the regulation of chemosensitivity to gemcitabine [13, 14], we investigated whether PRDM1 regulates chemosensitivity through this pathway. PRDM1 knockdown promoted gemcitabine-induced ATR and Chk1 activation, whereas the overexpression of PRDM1 reduced the ATR and Chk1 phosphorylation levels (Fig. 3I and Fig. S3X). Moreover, the ATR inhibitor VE-821 reversed the decreased gemcitabine sensitivity induced by PRDM1 knockdown in T24 and UMUC3 cells (Fig. 3J and Fig. S3Y). Tumor xenograft models were used to confirm the role of PRDM1 in regulating chemosensitivity in vivo. The knockdown of PRDM1 reduced the degree of chemosensitivity to gemcitabine and decreased the levels of cleaved caspase-3 and γH2A.X in the T24 and UMUC3 tumors (Fig. 3K, L and Fig. S4A–C). Conversely, the overexpression of PRDM1 significantly enhanced chemosensitivity to gemcitabine (Fig. S4D–H).

We established UMUC3 gemcitabine-resistant (GR) cells, which presented a > 5-fold higher IC50 value and downregulated PRDM1 expression compared with wild-type UMUC3 cells (Fig. 3M and Fig. S4I). We then established stable PRDM1-overexpressing UMUC3 GR cells (Fig. S4J). PRDM1 overexpression significantly inhibited tumor growth and restored gemcitabine cytotoxicity in UMUC3 GR cells (Fig. 3N, O and Fig. S4K). Together, these findings indicated that PRDM1 increases BCa tumor chemosensitivity and reverses gemcitabine resistance.

PRDM1 suppresses tumor growth and enhances chemosensitivity by downregulating CDC6 protein levels

Given the role of PRDM1 in cell cycle progression, we examined the levels of several important proteins involved in regulating cell cycle progression. Western blot analysis revealed that PRDM1 overexpression significantly decreased CDC6 protein levels and that PRDM1 knockdown increased CDC6 protein levels in both T24 and UMUC3 cells (Fig. S5A–D). Consistent with the results of PRDM1-knockdown cells, knockout of PRDM1 significantly increased CDC6 protein levels (Fig. S5E). Meanwhile, transient transfection with the Flag-PRDM1 plasmid also resulted in reduced CDC6 protein levels (Fig. S5F).

We subsequently investigated the biological role of CDC6 in PRDM1-regulated tumor growth. As evidenced by CCK-8, EdU incorporation, and colony formation analyses, CDC6 knockdown effectively suppressed cell proliferation, and it effectively attenuated the enhanced proliferative effects caused by PRDM1 knockdown in T24 and UMUC3 cells (Fig. 4A–C and Fig. S5G–I). Consistently, in vivo xenograft experiments confirmed that CDC6 knockdown inhibited tumor growth and reversed the PRDM1 knockdown-mediated promotion of tumor growth (Fig. 4D–F and Fig. S5J).

Because CDC6 regulates DDR activation and chemosensitivity [10, 15], we evaluated whether PRDM1 enhances chemosensitivity to gemcitabine through regulating CDC6 expression. CDC6 knockdown reduced the IC50 value of gemcitabine and restored gemcitabine sensitivity caused by PRDM1 knockdown in T24 cells (Fig. 4G). In addition, CDC6 knockdown increased apoptosis and attenuated the decrease in apoptosis caused by PRDM1 knockdown (Fig. 4H and Fig. S5K–N). Alkaline comet assays revealed similar results, indicating that CDC6 knockdown enhanced DNA damage and attenuated the decreased DNA damage induced by PRDM1 knockdown in gemcitabine-treated cells (Fig. 4I and Fig. S5O). Furthermore, CDC6 knockdown repressed the phosphorylation of ATR and Chk1, and it effectively attenuated PRDM1 knockdown-induced ATR-Chk1 pathway activation (Fig. 4J). We employed tumor xenograft models to further investigate the role of CDC6 in PRDM1-mediated chemosensitivity to gemcitabine in vivo. Consistent with the in vitro findings, CDC6 knockdown enhanced gemcitabine cytotoxicity and restored gemcitabine responsiveness caused by PRDM1 knockdown, as evidenced by increased apoptosis markers in gemcitabine-treated T24 tumors (Fig. 4K–M and Fig. S5P).

We next investigated the role of CDC6 in PRDM1-mediated chemoresistance. Compared with those in wild-type cells, the CDC6 levels were increased in UMUC3 GR (Fig. 4N). The overexpression of PRDM1 reversed chemoresistance to gemcitabine, whereas the ectopic expression of CDC6 abrogated the increased gemcitabine sensitivity induced by PRDM1 overexpression in UMUC3 GR cells (Fig. 4O, P and Fig. S5Q, R). Consistent with the in vitro findings, the overexpression of PRDM1 reversed chemoresistance to gemcitabine, whereas ectopic CDC6 expression significantly attenuated these effects in vivo (Fig. 4Q, R, and Fig. S5S). Together, these results indicated that CDC6 functions as a critical downstream effector that mediates the tumor-suppressive functions of PRDM1.

Neither PRDM1 knockdown nor PRDM1 overexpression altered the CDC6 mRNA expression level, indicating that PRDM1 does not directly regulate CDC6 at the transcriptional level (Fig. 5A, B).

We utilized cycloheximide (CHX), a eukaryotic protein translation inhibitor, to assess protein stability [16]. Compared with that in control cells, the half-life of the CDC6 protein in PRDM1-overexpressing T24 cells was significantly shorter, whereas the half-life of CDC6 was prolonged in PRDM1-knockdown T24 cells (Fig. 5C, D). Consistent with the results of stable PRDM1-overexpressing or PRDM1-knockdown cells, transient transfection with the Flag-PRDM1 plasmid also resulted in increased CDC6 protein instability (Fig. 5E). Protein degradation is a key regulatory mechanism of protein expression in eukaryotic cells and is mediated primarily through two major proteolytic systems—the ubiquitin–proteasome system and the autophagy–lysosomal pathway—which collectively maintain intracellular protein homeostasis [17]. The present results revealed that MG132 (a proteasome inhibitor), but not chloroquine (a lysosome inhibitor), reversed PRDM1-mediated CDC6 downregulation, indicating that PRDM1 regulates CDC6 expression through the proteasome degradation pathway (Fig. 5F, G). Furthermore, ectopic expression of PRDM1 enhanced CDC6 polyubiquitination, confirming that PRDM1 regulates CDC6 expression through the ubiquitin‒proteasome pathway (Fig. 5H). To delineate the specific ubiquitination linkage involved, K48 and K63 ubiquitin mutants were co-transfected with PRDM1 and CDC6 into cells, and PRDM1 overexpression selectively promotes K48-linked polyubiquitination of CDC6, while exerting no discernible effect on K63-linked ubiquitin chains (Fig. 5I). In addition, GSEA enrichment also revealed that PRDM1 was involved in the ubiquitin process (Fig. 5J). These findings collectively indicated that PRDM1 posttranscriptionally modulates CDC6 protein stability by promoting the ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of CDC6.

OTUD6A abrogates PRDM1-mediated tumor suppressive function

To further elucidate the mechanism by which PRDM1 downregulates CDC6 protein expression, we investigated the role of specific E3 ubiquitin ligases and deubiquitinating enzymes that regulate CDC6 protein degradation [10, 18–20]. Among the proteins detected, the levels of the OTUD6A protein, which binds to and deubiquitinates CDC6, were decreased in the PRDM1-overexpressing cells but were increased in the PRDM1-knockdown cells (Fig. 6A and Fig. S6A). Meanwhile, PRDM1 knockout also elevated OTUD6A protein levels (Fig. S6B). Furthermore, ectopic expression of OTUD6A markedly prolonged the CDC6 half-life and reduced CDC6 polyubiquitination in cells, and it reversed the reduced half-life and polyubiquitination of CDC6 caused by PRDM1 overexpression (Fig. 6B and Fig. S6C). Consistently, OTUD6A knockdown significantly downregulated CDC6 expression and reversed the upregulated CDC6 expression caused by PRDM1 knockdown (Fig. 6C). In order to determine whether the deubiquitinase activity of OTUD6A is required for mediating PRDM1 effect, we generated T24 cells stably overexpressing a catalytically inactive mutant of OTUD6A (C152A, where the 152nd cysteine residue was substituted with alanine) (Fig. S6D). Consistent with expectations, transient transfection of the mutant OTUD6A did not extend the half-life of CDC6 protein (Fig. S6E). Importantly, overexpression of the mutant OTUD6A failed to rescue the reduction in CDC6 levels induced by PRDM1 overexpression (Fig. S6D). Together, these results suggested that PRDM1 downregulates OTUD6A expression, which in turn promotes CDC6 ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation.

We conducted functional studies to investigate the role of OTUD6A in the regulation of PRDM1-mediated cell proliferation. Similar to the effects observed in CDC6-knockdown cells, OTUD6A knockdown significantly suppressed cell proliferation and effectively counteracted the accelerated proliferation caused by PRDM1 knockdown in T24 and UMUC3 cells (Fig. 6D, E and Fig. S6F–I). Consistent with the in vitro findings, OTUD6A knockdown inhibited tumor growth and abrogated the PRDM1 knockdown-induced increase in T24 tumor growth in tumor xenograft models (Fig. 6F, G and Fig. S6J, K). Moreover, CCK-8, colony formation, and EdU incorporation assays consistently demonstrated that the mutant OTUD6A was unable to counteract the PRDM1-induced suppression of cell proliferation in T24 cells (Fig. S6L-N).

We further determined the role of OTUD6A in PRDM1-mediated chemoresistance to gemcitabine. Similar to CDC6 knockdown, OTUD6A knockdown enhanced chemosensitivity and inhibited gemcitabine-induced ATR and Chk1 activation (Fig. 6H–J and Fig. S7A–F). Importantly, OTUD6A knockdown reversed the chemoresistance and activated the ATR-Chk1 pathway caused by PRDM1 knockdown (Fig. 6H–J and Fig. S7A–F). To confirm the in vitro results, OTUD6A knockdown increased chemosensitivity in gemcitabine-treated T24 tumors and reversed the impaired chemosensitivity to gemcitabine caused by PRDM1 knockdown (Fig. 6K, L and Fig. S7G). Furthermore, overexpression of the mutant OTUD6A did not reverse the increased chemosensitivity to gemcitabine induced by PRDM1 overexpression (Fig. S7H–K). Collectively, these findings indicate that the deubiquitinase activity of OTUD6A is essential for PRDM1-mediated regulation of CDC6 expression and its tumor-suppressive functions.

To further evaluate the ability of OTUD6A to regulate chemoresistance, we evaluated the expression level of OTUD6A in GR cells. Similar to the upregulation of CDC6 expression, OTUD6A protein levels were elevated in UMUC3 GR cells compared with those in wild-type cells (Fig. 6M). OTUD6A overexpression abrogated the restored chemosensitivity mediated by PRDM1 overexpression in UMUC3 GR cells (Fig. S7L–N). Similar results were observed in tumor xenograft experiments in which UMUC3 GR cells were used (Fig. 6N, O and Fig. S7O). Taken together, these data indicated that PRDM1 exerts its tumor-suppressive effects by downregulating OTUD6A expression.

PRDM1 binds to the OTUD6A promoter and inhibits its transcriptional activity

To further explore the molecular mechanism by which PRDM1 regulates OTUD6A expression, we examined the mRNA expression of OTUD6A in the PRDM1-overexpressing and PRDM1-knockdown T24 and UMUC3 cells. The overexpression of PRDM1 suppressed OTUD6A mRNA expression, whereas the knockdown of PRDM1 increased OTUD6A mRNA levels (Fig. 7A, B). The negative regulatory effect was also confirmed in 293 T cells transiently overexpressing the PRDM1 plasmid (Fig. 7C). Bioinformatics analysis using the JASPAR database revealed a putative PRDM1-binding site in the human OTUD6A promoter region (Fig. S8). ChIP confirmed that PRDM1 directly binds to the OTUD6A promoter (Fig. 7D), and the ChIP-seq data further supported this interaction (Fig. 7E). Additionally, PRDM1 overexpression enhanced the enrichment of PRDM1 at the OTUD6A promoter region in 293 T cells (Fig. 7F). Dual-luciferase reporter assays revealed that PRDM1 overexpression reduced the transcriptional activity of the OTUD6A promoter in T24 and UMUC3 cells (Fig. 7G). To determine whether PRDM1-mediated transcriptional repression is dependent on PRDM1 binding to the OTUD6A promoter, we generated luciferase reporter constructs containing a mutated PRDM1-binding site in the OTUD6A promoter (Fig. 7H). Overexpression of PRDM1 decreased the transcriptional activity of the wild-type OTUD6A promoter (Fig. 7I). However, mutation of the PRDM1-binding site abrogated the decrease in luciferase activity caused by PRDM1 overexpression (Fig. 7I). Collectively, these results demonstrated that PRDM1 directly binds to the OTUD6A promoter and represses its transcriptional activity.

We next analyzed the expression of PRDM1, CDC6 and OTUD6A in BCa tissues and adjacent normal tissues. Both CDC6 and OTUD6A protein expression levels were increased in BCa tissues compared with matched adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 8A–C). Importantly, the CDC6 and OTUD6A protein levels were negatively correlated with PRDM1 protein expression, whereas the CDC6 and OTUD6A protein levels were positively correlated with PRDM1 protein expression (Fig. 8D–F). Discussion

PRDM1, which encodes the BLIMP-1 protein, is an important transcriptional repressor. PRDM1 has garnered increasing attention in immunology research because of its indispensable role in regulating the development and function of various immune cells, particularly in driving plasma cell development and maintaining B-cell identity [21, 22]. However, few studies have focused on the function of PRDM1 in solid tumors. In the present study, we revealed the tumor suppressive role of PRDM1 and revealed its underlying mechanism. The present results demonstrated that PRDM1 is downregulated in BCa samples compared with matched normal bladder epithelial samples. Furthermore, PRDM1 expression consistently decreases during BCa tumorigenesis induced by BBN in mice. Functionally, PRDM1 inhibits tumor proliferation and enhances chemosensitivity both in vitro and in vivo. The present study revealed a novel mechanism of PRDM1 in BCa, demonstrating that PRDM1 binds to the promoter region of OTUD6A, thereby downregulating the transcriptional activity of OTUD6A, which subsequently decreases the CDC6 protein level. Thus, PRDM1 exerts its tumor suppressive effects and enhances chemosensitivity by suppressing the OTUD6A‒CDC6 axis.

PRDM1 expression dysregulation or functional loss has been implicated in hematological malignancies, such as lymphomas and leukemia, where normal differentiation pathways are disrupted. Genomic analyses have revealed that approximately 25% of patients with the activated B-cell-like (ABC) subtype of DLBCL exhibit PRDM1 deletions and mutations, suggesting that PRDM1 is a tumor-suppressor gene [23]. PRDM1 is downregulated in NK cell neoplasms, and re-expression of PRDM1 in NK cell lines has been shown to suppress cell proliferation [24]. Additionally, PRDM1 has been linked to chemoresistance in B-cell lymphoma and is susceptible to downregulation by bortezomib, while PRDM1 inhibits MDR1 transcription to reverse doxorubicin resistance in non-germinal center B-cell-like (non-GCB) DLBCL cells, indicating the role of PRDM1 in regulating chemosensitivity is context-dependent across different hematological malignancies [6, 25]. Consistently, the role of PRDM1 in solid tumors remains controversial. Elevated PRDM1 expression is detected in ER alpha-negative breast cancer cells and primary breast tumors, where PRDM1 binds to the ESR1 gene promoter region and inhibits its transcription to enhance tumor metastasis [26]. PRDM1 promotes cell proliferation and inhibits apoptosis by repressing p53 transcription in HCT116 cells, and PRDM1 induced by stress-driven ribosomal dysfunction led to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) resistance in the colon cancer cells [27, 28]. However, forced expression of PRDM1 represses MYC-responsive and stem cell-related genes, inhibiting the growth of primary colon tumor organoids, and low PRDM1 expression is associated with poor survival in colon cancer patients [29]. Furthermore, PRDM1 expression is negatively regulated by CBX8 and attenuates the progression of BCa by repressing c-FOS in BCa cells [9]. Here, we demonstrated PRDM1 suppresses BCa tumor growth and is a key determinant of chemosensitivity through ATR-Chk1 pathway. Knockout and knockdown of PRDM1 induces chemoresistance to both gemcitabine and cisplatin, while overexpression of PRDM1 enhances chemosensitivity, highlighting PRDM1 as a promising therapeutic target in BCa. Importantly, the present results revealed that PRDM1 overexpression delays the M/G1 cell cycle transition and that CDC6 is a key downstream regulator of PRDM1.

The mitosis phase, the final phase of the cell cycle, ensures the accurate segregation of genetic material into daughter cells [30, 31]. The G1 phase ensures proper DNA replication and genomic stability, and dysregulation of cell cycle progression is a hallmark of cancer [32, 33]. RNA-seq revealed that PRDM1 is associated with the cell cycle and DNA replication. Although PRDM1 did not alter the cell cycle distribution in asynchronous cells, the overexpression of PRDM1 caused a delay in the M/G1 transition. Further investigation of key cell cycle regulators identified CDC6 as a downstream effector of PRDM1. The differential effects of PRDM1 observed in synchronized versus asynchronous cell populations can be interpreted by the following aspects. First, CDC6 is likely only one of several targets of PRDM1, and it is also regulated by other pathways. For instance, CDC6 protein levels can be still detected in PRDM1-overexpression cells. Therefore, overexpression of PRDM1 just delayed but did not arrest M/G1 transition progression, and overexpression of PRDM1 only slowed down but did not stop cell proliferation. Second, we used stable but not transient transfected cells. Thus, it is reasonable that no significant changes in cell cycle were observed in unsynchronized PRDM1 stably overexpressed or knockdown cells. Previous studies have demonstrated that overexpression of CDC6 caused similar results. Overexpression of CDC6 led to premature entry into S phase in synchronized cells, but did not affect cell cycle distribution in unsynchronized cells [18, 34, 35].

CDC6 is classically associated with the initiation of DNA replication by recruiting minichromosome maintenance proteins to replication origins, and it plays a significant role in mitosis. CDC6 interacts with the centrosome during the G2 phase [36, 37]. Moreover, CDC6 binding to the centrosome impedes the accumulation of pericentriolar material proteins, including γ-tubulin, pericentrin, CDK5RAP2, and Cep192, depending on its ATPase activity [38, 39]. Phosphorylation of Plk4 modulates the binding of CDC6 to Sas-6, preventing excessive centrosome overreplication [34]. The present study revealed that PRDM1 inhibits CDC6 expression mainly during the M phase to regulate cell cycle progression. CDC6 is overexpressed and functions as an oncogene in various tumors [10, 40, 41]. The present results showed that the knockdown of CDC6 suppressed the tumor growth and chemoresistance caused by PRDM1 knockdown both in vivo and in vitro. Furthermore, CDC6 is associated with DDR pathways and promotes gemcitabine-induced activation of the ATR-Chk1 pathway [10, 42, 43]. Similar to CDC6 overexpression, PRDM1 knockdown activated ATR and Chk1 phosphorylation. Knockdown of CDC6 rescued the increase in phosphorylated ATR and Chk1 expression, highlighting the importance of CDC6 in PRDM1-mediated chemosensitivity.

OTUD6A is an ovarian tumor protease (OTU) family member, and several studies have reported the function of OTUD6A in tumor progression. OTUD6A deubiquitinates various oncogenic proteins, including androgen receptor (AR), AURKA, c-Myc, Drp1, TopBP1, and nucleolin [44–49]. In addition to its tumorigenic effect, OTUD6A also enhances the stability of NLRP3 in macrophages, increases IL-1β expression, and promotes intestinal inflammation and colitis [50]. OTUD6A is upregulated in hypertrophic heart tissues, and STING has been identified as a direct substrate protein of OTUD6A. The inhibition of STING eliminates the hypertrophic response in cardiomyocytes caused by OTUD6A overexpression [51]. Moreover, OTUD6A knockout protects against angiotensin II-induced kidney injury and fibrosis in mice, and OTUD6A binds to signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) and promotes its nuclear translocation [52]. Our previous study demonstrated that OTUD6A upregulates CDC6 expression by maintaining its protein stability [10]. The present study revealed that PRDM1 reduces CDC6 protein expression without affecting its mRNA expression. Thus, OTUD6A is a critical molecular bridge connecting PRDM1 and CDC6 within the PRDM1–OTUD6A–CDC6 pathway.

Previous studies have reported PRDM1 inhibits target gene expression by recruiting corepressors, including G9a lysine methyltransferase, histone deacetylases 1 and 2 (HDAC1/2), protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5), and lysine-specific demethylase 1 (LSD1) [53]. Meanwhile, PRDM1 coordinates with FOXA to recruit Nucleosome Remodeling and Deacetylation (NuRD) complexes and Polycomb Repressive Complexes (PRC) to repress gene transcription [54]. Thus, we will investigate the potential epigenetic mechanisms that regulate the PRDM1-OTUD6A pathway in further study. Despite the mechanism of transcriptional repression remains unclear, our findings reveal that PRDM1 functions as an upstream regulator of the OTUD6A-CDC6 axis, thereby providing a more complete picture of this oncogenic pathway.

In conclusion, the present finding suggested that PRDM1 exerts a suppressive effect on tumor growth and enhances chemosensitivity in BCa by inhibiting the transcriptional activity of OTUD6A, which in turn reduces the stability of the CDC6 protein. Thus, PRDM1 may function as a potential tumor suppressor gene in BCa and may represent a promising therapeutic target for BCa patients.
