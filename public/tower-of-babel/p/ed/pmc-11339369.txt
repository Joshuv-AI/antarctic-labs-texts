Minimal dose resistance training enhances strength without affecting cardiac autonomic modulation in menopausal women: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Rayra Khalinka Neves Dias, Eduardo Macedo Penna, Ádria Samara Negrão Noronha, Octávio Barbosa Neto, Elren Passos Monteiro, Victor Silveira Coswig
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe proposition of a minimal dose of resistance training (RT) to elicit health benefits, encompassing physiological and psychological aspects, has garnered attention. While empirical investigations have demonstrated the efficacy of low-volume RT protocols in inducing adaptations such as enhanced strength and functional capacity, further exploration of the effects of this paradigm across a broader spectrum of variables is warranted. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the effects of minimal dose RT on strength and functional capacity, cardiac autonomic modulation, and hemodynamic parameters in menopausal women. Twenty-six women were randomly assigned to the training (TG: 63.2 ± 9.3 years) or control group (CG: 59.3 ± 7.6 years). Anthropometric measurements, strength and functional performance tests, cardiac autonomic assessment, and hemodynamic parameters were performed before and after four weeks of intervention. The TG performed the minimum dose RT twice weekly for four weeks (2 sets of 8–12 repetitions in three dynamic exercises, plus three 1-min isometric planks), and the CG had a weekly meeting with lectures and stretching. Two-way ANOVA with repeated measures was applied to each variable. Regarding time comparisons, there was a significant increase for LniRR (F = 4.78; ω2 = 0.046; p = 0.04), one repetition maximum (1RM) bench press (F = 8.06; ω2 = 0,013; p = 0.01), and 1RM leg press (F = 17.3; ω2 = 0,098; p < 0.01). There was a group*time interaction only for the index LnRMSSD (F = 5.11; ω2 = 0.042; p = 0.03), and 1RM bench press (F = 9.52; ω2 = 0,016; p = 0.01). No between-group main effect for any variable was found. The minimal dose RT protocol improved muscle strength, while cardiac autonomic and hemodynamic variables, as well as functional capacity, remained stable over 4 weeks in menopausal women.Subject terms: Rehabilitation, Neurophysiology

Introduction

The autonomic nervous system modulates cardiovascular parameters such as heart rate (HR), blood pressure (BP), and baroreceptor reflex activity, influenced by environmental and physiological stimuli1. Heart rate variability (HRV) is a non-invasive indicator to infer cardiac oscillations between the R-R consecutive intervals related to fluctuations in sympathetic and parasympathetic nerve flow over time2. Overall, women had lower HRV values (from 8 to 11%) in the time domain compared to men, which suggests lower global autonomic modulation capacity3. Also, women showed significantly worsened autonomic control after menopause, possibly due to decreased estrogen, which increases sympathetic activity, causing sympathovagal imbalance4. However, age and physical activity levels were not detailed3, although previous research has reported age-related HRV dysfunctions, increasing cardiovascular risks1,5.

Positive associations between physical exercise and HRV have been evidenced in older adults5,6. However, the effects of resistance training (RT) on autonomic control are controversial7. Increases in HRV after RT were observed in young but not in middle-aged adults, which suggests an aging effect in cardiac autonomic sensitivity to training8. Also, a meta-analysis found positive effects of RT on older adults, HRV for those with previous chronic and metabolic diseases7. This evidence suggests that age and sex need to be considered while investigating cardiac autonomic adaptations to RT.

Despite muscular strength gains, HRV remained unchanged after 12 weeks of RT in menopausal women (≥ 60 years), with three sessions per week (eight exercises, two sets of 10–15 repetitions, 25–30 min/session)9. Similar findings were also found after shorter periods of RT (4 weeks) in young women10. In contrast, two sessions per week (eight exercises, three sets of 8 maximum repetitions) performed during 12 weeks induced positive responses on HRV time and frequency domains in healthy older women6. Also, 12 weeks of a low-volume RT, performed three times a week (four exercises, three sets of 18–22 repetitions), improved cardiac autonomic control of obese menopausal women11. Another study found improvements in HRV of women with fibromyalgia (50 ± 10 years old) after 16 weeks of low-volume RT performed twice a week (nine exercises, one set of 8–12 repetitions)12. Collectively, these studies suggest that both volume and duration may influence cardiac autonomic adaptations to RT. However, the question of how low the RT dose can be to achieve these adaptations in menopausal women remains unanswered.

The minimal dose approach was proposed to produce significant physiological adaptations, such as increased strength, functional capacity, and psychological benefits with a lower training volume13. The main idea is to counteract specific barriers to exercise adherence, since long workouts are time-consuming and may induce lower motivation among older people14. This protocol is composed of moderate to high-intensity loads (50–90% of 1 repetition maximum; 1RM), performed twice weekly, with single sets of 8 to 12 repetitions13. However, further exploration of the effects of this paradigm across a broader spectrum of variables is warranted to respond if this “minimal” can be understood as universal or limited to some specific adaptations.

Thus, this study aimed to investigate the effects of minimal dose RT on muscle strength, functional capacity, cardiac autonomic modulation, and hemodynamic parameters, in menopausal women. Since training volume can influence autonomic responses and age seems to reduce HRV responsiveness to training, we hypothesize that a minimal dose of RT can increase muscle strength without compromising cardiac autonomic control in menopausal women.

Results

The recruitment procedures are described in Fig. 1, as suggested by CONSORT (http://www.consort-statement.org/). Due to the risk of dropout, 58 women were enrolled. Of these, one was excluded because she had not been menopausal for more than a year, nine did not attend the initial evaluations, and three participants underwent hormone replacement. Therefore, 45 women were included and randomized into the training and control groups. In the TG, four participants dropped out due to health problems unrelated to the protocol, such as "depression," "knee injury" from a fall, "stroke," and "surgical adherence.” In addition, five more women did not complete the evaluations or did not complete the eight meetings during the 6-week protocol period due to "lack of motivation" or "adherence to the method." In the CG, there were also sample losses, one due to "walking difficulties," one due to "knee injury" from a fall, one due to "travel," and seven did not complete the evaluations due to "lack of motivation" or "adherence to the method." At the end of the protocol, 26 participants were included in the final analyses.

In the results referring to functional capacity, none of the variables presented a group*time interaction (p > 0.05) nor between groups' main effects (p > 0.05). Regarding the time main effect, significant improvements for the timed up and go (F = 17.98; ω2 = 0.106; p < 0.001; TG = −11.6% vs CG = -14.3%) and sit-to-stand (F = 7.58; ω2 = 0.078; p = 0.01; TG = −19.0% vs CG = −12.6%) were found. In summary, functional capacity remains stable, and eventual changes do not derive from training.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the effects of a minimal dose RT program on strength, functional capacity, cardiac autonomic modulation, and hemodynamic parameters, in menopausal women. The main findings suggest that low-volume RT protocol improved muscle strength but did not alter autonomic modulation, hemodynamic parameters, or functional capacity.

The minimal dose RT protocol significantly improved muscle strength. A previous study analyzed the effects of RT for 12 weeks (3 times per week, 8 exercises, 2 sets of 10–15 repetitions) in sedentary postmenopausal women (n = 15), the protocol was able to promote increases in muscle strength (10.1% for bench press and 12.6% leg extension) without affecting HRV9. Our results also showed that is possible to achieve similar results (17.2% for bench press and 22% for leg press) with a significantly lower weekly volume and training program duration. Previous findings also found improvements in muscle strength, muscular endurance, and functional tests in older women from low-volume RT (5 exercises, one set each) after 24 sessions16. This evidence reinforces the suggestion that minimal-dose RT programs may be interesting for providing significant neuromuscular improvements, even in the short term. However, it is noteworthy that we failed to find significant changes in functional capacity, which may have occurred due to the good participants' initial functional capacity15 or the short duration of the training program17.

No effects of the RT protocol were found on autonomic function in menopausal women, even after orthostatic stress with active postural change. Contrary to our findings, a previous study showed a significant improvement in HRV in sedentary older women (65 ± 3 years) after 12 weeks of a RT protocol composed of two weekly sessions (8 exercises, 3 sets of 8 maximum repetitions)6. A possible reason for different results may be related to the training volume applied, since a high training volume can lead to cardiovascular imbalances, altering the plasma volume and changing the cardiac effort to maintain cardiac output18, which can generate high blood pressure peaks during exercise and provide sufficient stimuli for baroreflex adaptation9. Alternatively, low-volume RT (4 exercises, 3 sets of ~ 20 repetitions) was effective for improving the sympathovagal balance of older women, the authors suggested that the high repetition was chosen due to acute effects in increasing sympathetic activity11.

Additionally, total exposure to training stimuli appears to affect autonomic responses, since long-term interventions (> 12 weeks) and high-frequency training were associated with larger effect sizes in increasing parasympathetic activity in older people5. Indeed, a short-term (4 weeks) high-intensity low-volume RT program (3 exercises, 3–4 sets of 5–6 repetitions) was unable to induce changes in cardiac autonomic control, which the authors related to the resilience of the autonomous nervous system of young women10. Thus, it seems that questions regarding the effects of manipulating training variables (i.e. volume, frequency, duration, intensity, etc.) on cardiac autonomic control in older women remain of interest, and mechanisms for these effects still require more clarity. Specifically, our findings suggest that the minimal dose RT approach may be insufficient to provide such benefits, at least in the short term. Further research using low-volume RT programs lasting longer may yield different results.

Higher changes in HRV variables are expected when the training protocol predominates in aerobic energy production9,19. A study compared HRV and baroreflex sensitivity of prehypertensive people after four weeks of RT (3 times/week, 9 exercises, 3 sets of 10 repetitions) and aerobic training (3 times/week, 30 min on treadmill, 65% of VO2peak)20. Aerobic training decreased sympathetic modulation and increased vagal tone, while RT reduced sympathetic modulation to peripheral blood vessels and decreased cardiac parasympathetic modulation. Thus, combining aerobic training sessions with RT programs may be beneficial and low-volume approaches can help avoid excessively long training sessions1.

The cardiac autonomic effect is also related to aging, where older adults typically exhibit heightened sympathetic modulation and decreased baroreflex sensitivity21. Existing evidence showed that women aged 27 to 60 years (n = 10) had significant improvements in HRV (total power of HRV and RMSSD) after performing a RT protocol of two sessions per week (9 exercises, single set, 8 to 12 repetitions) for 16 weeks12. The wide age range can be a confusing factor, since autonomic regulation is affected by the decline in estrogen hormone levels during the menopausal period22 which appears to reduce the responsiveness of HRV to training, especially in comparison with women at reproductive age8.

Regarding hemodynamic parameters, the minimal dose RT did not significantly alter the resting cardiovascular function of BP, HR, and DP. These responses may be evidenced by the absence of changes in autonomic regulation because, although investigating these mechanisms was not the aim of this study, there is the possibility of BP decline after decreased sympathetic nerve action on total vascular resistance23, which also explains the maintenance of HR and DP. Furthermore, the BP reductions after exercise are related to the initial BP (SBP: 120 to 129 mm Hg; DBP: 80 to 84 mm Hg)24.

Although not objectively measured in the present investigation, the adherence to the used approach must be considered. The reports received by the researchers and the dropout rate that occurred due to “non-adherence to the method” suggest that this is a factor that deserves attention. The attempt to reduce RT volume is proposed as a strategy to overcome the “lack of time” barrier to engage in physical exercise programs14. However, our experience might alert that sessions lasting an excessively low duration (< 30 min, including warm-up) may also negatively impact adherence. Despite the lack of time, motivating factors (i.e. health benefits, training instructors, social interaction, etc.) may increase interest in performing exercise programs by influencing the net of individual, interpersonal, and organizational domains25.

This study has limitations. We evaluated only short-term HRV, while longer durations, such as 24 h, might provide more comprehensive insights2. The respiratory rate was standardized according to the commands provided by the Elite HRV application. Although not as ideal as using a respiratory plethysmography belt26, such respiratory normalization is not necessary when the objective is the variation of HRV, especially on HF27. In this study, 11 women were diagnosed with hypertension, which could cause greater reductions in BP compared to normotensives after exercise9. However, our findings did not show significant differences in responses to RT. Another possible limitation is the lack of control of food intake, although the participants were asked to maintain their usual diet, except caffeinated foods, before the autonomic and hemodynamic evaluations.

The present study’s findings show that, in menopausal women, the low-volume training proposed by the minimal dose RT was efficient in improving muscle strength with no effects on autonomic and hemodynamic variables, and functional capacity was evidenced after four weeks of intervention. These findings suggest that the dose applied may not be universally minimal and some outcomes may require higher doses or longer training periods.

Methods

Study design

This study is a randomized clinical trial submitted to the Brazilian Registry of Clinical Trials under the code RBR-2p4gpvk (11/09/2022) and approved by the local IRB (# 4.922.776/2021), conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Recreationally active menopausal women participated in a twice-weekly minimal dose RT protocol over four weeks. Participants were randomized into a training group (TG: n = 13) and a control group (CG: n = 13). Both groups were evaluated for anthropometric measurements (waist circumference, height, and body mass), hemodynamic parameters (HR and BP), cardiac autonomic modulation (RMSSD, SDNN, pNN50, HF, LF, LF/HF), functional performance (6 min walk test, 4-m walk, sit-to-stand, time up and go and balance) and muscle strength [1 repetition maximum test (1 RM)] before and after the intervention period. These evaluations were performed in the same order, all in the morning, and in the same laboratory. The participants were advised to maintain their eating and medication habits during these four weeks. The post-intervention tests were applied 48 h after the last day of training to minimize possible acute effects.

Participants

The participants were recruited through community outreach on a local radio program, at a social assistance reference center (CRAS), and on social media to participate in the research conducted at the Federal University of Pará, Castanhal Campus. Initially, 58 recreationally active women agreed to participate in the study. Participants were informed about the procedures, benefits, and risks involved in the investigation and all participants signed the Informed Consent Form. To be included, recreationally active women had to self-report being menopausal for > 1 year, not present any chronic or acute condition that would compromise performance during the research, be able to practice RT, not be on hormone replacement treatment, and not have participated in any RT program in the last 6 months. As exclusion criteria, not completing, for any reason, the evaluations, or not completing the eight meetings during the 6-week protocol period.

Participants were described through an anamnesis, including personal information, disease history, and medications. The IPAQ questionnaire (short version) was applied to identify whether the participants have a physically active behavior or not, involving information about the time spent per week in the sitting position, the walks performed, and the low, moderate, and vigorous-intensity physical activities performed during the week28. In addition, the PAR-Q is an instrument designed to identify whether people should undergo a medical evaluation before the exercise protocol. It is a self-screening consisting of seven 'YES' or 'NO' questions. A positive response indicates the need for a medical evaluation to avoid possible health risks29.

Interventions

The four-week intervention protocol was adapted from the minimal dose approach proposed by13. The exercises chosen for intervention were leg press 180°, bench press, seated row, and plank (Table 6). The choice of these exercises arose to target the major muscle groups30. These sessions were held twice a week with a 72-h interval between them. Before each session, a joint warm-up was performed. Repetition range was used for load progression to ensure moderate to high training intensity. Thus, when the participant could perform all the sets at the upper limit of repetitions (12 repetitions), the load for the next session was increased (5% to 10%). The speed of execution of the exercises was established as 2 s in the concentric phase and 2 s in the eccentric phase. In the plank exercise, three sets were maintained with a duration of 1 min in isometry. The CG attended lectures on health education (first and third meetings) and stretching classes (second and fourth). The sessions were held once a week and lasted 60 min. Participants of the CG were invited to engage in the exercise program at the end of the four weeks.

Outcomes

Anthropometry

Body mass was assessed with a digital scale (G-TECH, Glass 7FW, 150 kg, Accumed, Brazil). To measure height, a manual tape measure (Western®) was used. The Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated using body mass (kg) and height (m) and classified as normal weight (18.5 to < 25 kg/m2), overweight (25 to < 30 kg/m2), obesity (≥ 30 kg/m2), obesity I (30 to < 35 kg/m2), and obesity II and III (≥ 35 kg/m2)31. Waist circumference was measured using an inextensible tape (Balmak, FM-150) at the midpoint between the anterior superior iliac crest and the last rib, with an accuracy of 0.1 cm32.

Hemodynamic assessment

BP and HR assessments were performed using an automatic oscillometric monitor (Omron, 7122, Brazil) and a cardio frequency monitor (Polar H10, Polar Electro Ou, Kempele, Finland), respectively. The participants followed Brazilian Hypertension Guidelines recommendations for the evaluations33. Two measurements were taken at rest on non-consecutive days and at the same time. Participants was recommended to rest for 5 min before the measurement, and during the evaluation, the participant sat with her feet flat on the floor and her back relaxed on the chair. The measurements were performed on the left arm. With the data of systolic (SBP) and diastolic BP (DBP), it was possible to calculate the mean BP (MBP), being the average pressure exerted by the blood to the tissues during the cardiac cycle, MBP = (2 (DBP) + SBP)/3)34. To estimate ventricular function with myocardial oxygen consumption during rest and physical exertion, the doubled product (DP) was calculated, obtained by multiplying SBP by HR35.

Heart rate variability measurements

HRV analyses were performed using the Elite HRV Wellness mobile application (version 5.5.4), which showed an ICC between 0.70 and 0.9536. The app is available for Android and iOS platforms and was connected to an elastic chest strap with transmitter Blutooth® (Polar H10, Polar Electro Ou, Kempele, Finlândia). All HRV files were exported from the Elite HRV application via e-mail to a computer for further analysis in Kubios HRV software (version 2.2). Data were corrected for ectopic beats and artifacts according to the standard procedure by removing the first minute of all recordings37. The control of respiratory incursions was made with the help of the application with the objective of better standardization.

Before the recordings, the participants were instructed not to perform physical activity or drink alcoholic and caffeinated beverages in the previous 24 h. On the day of the measurements2, the protocol was to empty the bladder and remain seated at rest for at least five minutes before the measurements. Before each recording, a 3–5-min stabilization period was performed. For the measure at rest, the participants performed 5-min recordings in the sitting position, with two sessions on a non-consecutive day and simultaneously for baseline values. The active tilt test was applied to diagnose autonomic nervous system dysfunction or syncope caused by postural changes38. After a one-minute stabilization period, the measurement of HRV was held in the supine position for 5 min, after which a quick and independent change to the vertical position was requested, remaining in this position for another 5 min38. No participant experienced syncope or presyncope symptoms during the recordings. These analyses were performed using linear models of HRV in the time (iRR, RMSSD, SDNN, and pNN50) and frequency domains (LF, HF, and LF/HF)2.

Functional capacity assessment

The 6-min walk test involves walking approximately 30 m back and forth. At the signal from the evaluator, the participants walk at their own pace for the longest possible distance during a timed period of 6 min. The value of the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of testing and retesting is reported between 0.78 and 0.9439. For gait and balance, the Timed Up and Go test was performed. The initially seated participant must stand up and walk as fast as possible for three meters, turn around, return to the chair, and sit down again. Time was measured from the beginning of the movement to the final position. In this test, the best time of two attempts was evaluated. The faster, the better the functional performance40. The 4-m walk test assessed walking speed. From a standing position at their usual walking speed, the participants were asked to walk four meters, with a one-meter deceleration zone. The best time of two attempts was computed41. The sit-and-stand test was used to analyze lower limb strength and endurance. The participants were asked to complete five cycles of the sit-up-and-stand-up movement as quickly as possible with arms crossed over the chest15.

The Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) was used for presenting a performance scale on the gait speed test, sitting and standing up from a chair performance, and the balance test, with three different positions, evaluating the ability to stand with the feet side by side, semi-tandem, and tandem. In this sense, each component is scored from 0 to 4 points, where 4 refers to the best performance. The total SPPB scores (range 0–12) are calculated by adding the three tests applied, with higher total scores indicating higher lower limb functional strength15.

Familiarization and load estimation

Familiarization was performed 48 h before the beginning of the interventions in a single session to learn the correct techniques of the exercises. At the end of the familiarization session, the participants could perform the multiple repetition test, using the following equation: 1RM = load/(1.0278–0.0278*repetitions)42. After 48 h of this first contact, the participants performed the 1RM test.

One repetition maximum test (1 RM)

Participants should attempt to reach their maximum weight, appropriately lifted in one repetition in the full range of motion. For this, three to five maximal tries with 2 to 3 min intervals between attempts and 5 to 10 min between exercises. The leg press 180°, bench press, and seated row exercises were applied, all performed on RT machines. The load addition for each trial was between 5 and 10% for bench press and seated row and 10–20% for leg press 180°. The 1RM test for older people presents high reliability (ICC ≥ 0.90)43. All three exercises were performed in the TG and CG groups on the same day, with alternating exercises for the upper and lower limbs to reduce the fatiguing effect.

Sample size

An a priori sample size analysis was performed in the G*Power® software version 3.1.9.744. Thus, with an effect size of 0.25, considering the study based on ANOVA for repeated measures (group x time) and probability α of 5%, 24 participants are needed to obtain statistical power of 95%.

Randomization and blinding

The TG and CG groups were randomly formed using an electronically generated block randomization sequence (https://www.randomizer.org/). The size of the blocks was determined by the responsible researcher. A second researcher assigned the interventions to the participants. With the inability of this protocol to blind the participants and the test assessors, the researcher responsible for analyzing the data was blinded during the research.

Statistical analysis

After testing the normality of the data distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test, the HRV indices that did not show a normal distribution, such as RR, RMSSD, SDNN, LF, and HF, were logarithmically transformed (Ln). The t-independent test verified the differences between groups. To compare the frequencies of the categorical variables, the Chi-square test was performed (X2). Two-factor ANOVA (group*time) with repeated measures was performed for intra- and inter-group comparisons at pre- and post-four weeks and, when necessary, Bonferroni post hoc test was applied45. Additionally, the variation deltas (Δ%) are presented to analyze the modifications of the pre-and post-protocol moments of the measured parameters in percentage terms, according to the equation (Δ% = [(post – pre)/pre*100)]. Spearman's test was used to test correlations between HRV and muscle strength. The effect sizes were presented by omega squared (ω2), and Cohen's dz were considered trivial (< 0.50), small (0.50 to 1.25), moderate (1.25 to 1.9), and large (> 2.0), as suggested earlier46. All analyses were performed in JASP software (JASP Version 16.4.0, University of Amsterdam, The Netherlands) with a significance level set at p < 0.05.
