Similarities in Brazilian dietary diversity: a literature review of ethnobotanical studies on regional, vegetation, and cultural contexts
Authors: Luan Victor Brandão dos Santos, Daniel Pereira Monteiro, Lucilene Lima dos Santos Vieira, André Luiz Borba do Nascimento, Roseli Farias Melo de Barros
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEdible flora is one of the most relevant groups for the survival and food security of socioeconomically vulnerable communities in Brazil. We aimed to provide an overview of research advances in the country, record the known edible biodiversity, national consumption patterns across different communities (rural, traditional, and urban), and the geographic and ethnic distribution of the articles, while identifying gaps and possibilities for future studies. To this end, a literature review was conducted in the SciELO, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar databases for the indexing of ethnobiological research. Following detailed analyses, 163 articles were included in our database. The research was concentrated in the Northeast and North regions, primarily in rural communities. A total of 1,079 botanical species were cited (754 native, 325 exotic), with the most diverse families being Myrtaceae Juss., Arecaceae Bercht. & J. Presl., and Fabaceae Lindl. The chosen predictors (urbanity gradient, phytogeographic domains, and types of traditional communities) could not distinguish the analyzed groups according to their similarity in food diversity, although some detectable statistical differences exist, especially when considering the type of traditional community and native edible species. Gaps in Brazilian local and traditional ecological knowledge regarding species diversity for food security and its cultural relationship, in addition to the low scientific representation of traditional groups, influenced these results. Studies on the internal variability of edible species composition are important as they allow for an understanding of whether the characteristics that differentiate communities, regarding the richness of utilized edible species, are related to ethnic, cultural, and geographical delimitations and which groups overlap these barriers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13002-026-00881-w.Keywords: Food Security, Plant Use, Traditional Ecological Knowledge

Introduction

Ethnobotany is an interdisciplinary field aimed at understanding the relationship between human societies and plants of local interest [1]. Brazil has an extensive diversity of significant botanical species, whether for medicinal, religious, or food purposes, among others [2–4]. Furthermore, the Brazilian territory is composed of an immense heterogeneity of peoples who rely on these plants for survival, with food use being one of the most relevant categories [3, 5–7].

Edible plants are significant agents in promoting food security, health, and the economy of various communities, especially those with moderate to high degrees of financial and social vulnerability, serving as essential sources of carbohydrates, vitamins, and proteins [7–9].

Among the plant organs provided, fruits are the most frequently cited and sought after by the studied communities [9–11]. In this regard, the literature survey by Santos et al. [11] reports a total of 557 fruit species cataloged for Brazil, demonstrating the potential and high diversity of edible fruits. Seeds, leaves, stems, and roots exhibit lower richness; however, they present equivalent importance regarding the nutritional resources necessary for food security [3, 9, 12].

Currently, like the rest of the world’s biodiversity, the Brazilian flora faces the effects of environmental and climatic changes caused by anthropogenic activities [13–15]. Increased deforestation of natural areas, wildfires, rising average temperatures, habitat fragmentation, and the reduction of the hydrological regime in the country are factors responsible for local and regional extinctions of botanical species of nutritional importance [13–15]. This directly and indirectly impacts the food security of communities that depend on these resources for subsistence and the continuity of cultural elements, as well as the knowledge of the country’s biocultural heritage [2, 5, 9, 10].

Given this scenario, the continuity of ethnobotanical research aimed at recording biocultural knowledge regarding plants of local food use is essential. Although the number of publications on the subject has increased significantly in the country [11, 16], objectives are primarily concentrated on recording useful plant species cited by communities, without deepening issues related to the mode of use, ecological management, consumption sustainability, and the impact of social, economic, and cultural changes on local biodiversity [11, 12, 16].

In this context, significant gaps are observed, particularly the lack of systematic assessments concerning the national overview of these studies and the similarity of useful species cited by different communities across distinct phytogeographic domains. Such analyses are vital for diagnosing vulnerabilities and guiding future investigations. Systematic reviews are useful tools to contribute to this problem, mainly by indicating potential species of nutritional and cultural importance, widely recognized use patterns, and modes of knowledge transmission.

Therefore, we sought to understand the landscape of ethnobotanical research on edible plants in Brazil, with emphasis on the recorded species (native and exotic) and the similarity of diversity in transethnic contexts (considering the type of community: non-traditional rural, traditional, and urban) and transgeographic contexts, associating them with the country’s phytogeographic domains.

The guiding questions of this review were: (1) What is the territorial distribution of ethnobotanical studies on plants for food use in Brazil? (2) What is the diversity of native and exotic edible species in the Brazilian territory? (3) Is there similarity in the species cited among community types (rural, traditional, and urban), among Brazilian phytogeographic domains (Caatinga, Cerrado, Amazon Forest, Atlantic Forest, Pampa, and Pantanal), and among the different types of traditional, rural, and urban communities?

Materials and methods

The literature search followed the PRISMA protocol for systematic reviews [17]. The following databases were accessed: Web of Science, Scopus, SciELO, and Google Scholar. For the latter, the results were evaluated up to the tenth page, thereby standardizing the sampling effort across all employed combinations and avoiding gray literature, which is common in Google Scholar results [18]. The databases were searched using the descriptors: (“Food Plants” AND “Brazil”); (PANCs AND Brazil); (“Wild food Plants” AND Brazil); and (“Unconventional Food Plants” AND Brazil), which were also translated into Portuguese and Spanish.

This research was conducted in accordance with Brazilian legislation safeguarding associated traditional knowledge under Law No. 13.123/15 and is duly registered in the National System for the Management of Genetic Heritage and Associated Traditional Knowledge (SISGen) under registration number A32DDC6. For traditional communities, the definition and recognition followed the criteria established by Decree No. 8.750/2016, which institutes the National Council of Traditional Peoples and Communities in Brazil.

Data collection

The results were initially screened by reading the titles, abstracts, and keywords. In this study, we selected only peer-reviewed articles on food ethnobotany, excluding gray literature to ensure high academic standards. A formal risk of bias assessment was not conducted, as the primary objective was to build a comprehensive database, prioritizing the representativeness of the scientific literature available in the field. Therefore, the inclusion criteria were limited to ethnobotanical scientific articles reporting plant use for human consumption, indexed in the databases with primary data, written in Portuguese, English, or Spanish, published through 2023, and containing a list of properly identified species.

Articles that did not meet one or more of the inclusion criteria mentioned above were excluded from the analysis, as were those containing the same dataset as another study previously indexed in the set of accepted articles, when identified by one or more authors of this review. A cross-referencing process was performed exclusively with the selected articles, following the same inclusion and exclusion criteria mentioned above to complement the database. Since the objective of this research was to survey the diversity of edible species, no risk of bias assessment was conducted, as it was necessary to collect the maximum number of articles published in the field.

Data extraction and processing

With the aid of Excel spreadsheets, information regarding authors, journals, research methods, study areas, locations, participating communities, and phytogeographic domains was recorded. Regarding the associated knowledge, data was extracted on species, utilized plant parts, and ways of use.

The spelling, botanical classification, habit, and origin were corrected and updated according to the Flora and Funga of Brazil [19]. Species that were not fully identified were excluded from the database. Data spreadsheets were constructed using Excel software, version 2503.

The distribution of the studied areas was mapped using the geographic coordinates provided by the included studies and processed using Google Earth Pro and QGIS 3.32.3 software. In cases where the article did not provide the geographic location of the participating community, the geographic coordinates of the seat of the municipality to which the community belongs were used as a reference.

Only records with botanical identification to the species level and complete information regarding the phytogeographic domain, state, and community type were included in the analyses. Species were classified according to their origin (native or exotic, according to the Flora and Funga of Brazil), and the data were organized into a presence/absence matrix.

Data analysis

For the analysis of species similarity, articles were screened based on the type of participating community and classified into three groups: rural (any non-traditional community located in rural areas, as indicated by the assessed article), traditional (communities defined according to the norms of Decree No. 6,040/07, which institutes and defines Brazilian traditional communities), and urban (non-traditional communities located in urban areas). Regarding the Brazilian phytogeographic domains (Caatinga, Cerrado, Amazon Forest, Atlantic Forest, Pampa, and Pantanal) and the specific type of traditional community. Articles that did not provide this information or presented it ambiguously were excluded from this analysis.

Patterns in the composition of edible plants among communities were assessed using a multivariate approach based on the Jaccard distance, utilizing functions from the vegan package [20]. Initially, non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) with two dimensions (k = 2) was applied as an exploratory tool to visualize relationships between communities. Solution stability was assessed by stress value. In cases where NMDS did not converge, particularly due to a reduced number of samples in certain groups, Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) was used as an alternative graphical representation.

Floristic differences among groups were tested using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with 999 permutations. Homogeneity of variance among groups was evaluated with the multivariate dispersion test (betadisper). When this assumption was violated (p < 0.05), analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) was applied as a complementary method to assess dissimilarity between groups. The analyses were performed on three datasets: (i) total species, (ii) native species, and (iii) exotic species, to evaluate similarity by domain and by community type (traditional or non-traditional).

Cluster analysis

To complement the understanding of the floristic relationships among the groups, a hierarchical cluster analysis using the UPGMA (Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean) method was performed based on the Jaccard distance. This analysis was conducted separately for each data subset to identify general similarity patterns among the analyzed groups (phytogeographic domain, community type, and traditional community type) and to verify the degree of cohesion among communities in terms of the richness of edible plants cited in the studies. The groupings were visualized through dendrograms developed using the dendextend package [21]. The faithfulness of the clusters in representing the original distances was evaluated using the cophenetic correlation coefficient.

Bibliometric results

Initially, the database query yielded 2,169 records. After the screening stage to eliminate duplicates and the application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, 96 eligible scientific articles remained. Subsequently, cross-referencing allowed for the incorporation of an additional 79 studies, bringing the total to 175 in the original database. However, 12 articles were excluded for not providing information regarding the type of participating community (Fig. 1).

Results

Scientific production about edible ethnobotany in Brazil

The earliest publications identified in this review date back to the 1970s, characterized by a stable pace with sporadic records throughout the subsequent decades until the late 1990s (Additional file 1). From the 2000s onwards, there was a significant increase in publications regarding edible plants, reaching a peak in the 2010 s, with 2012 notably accounting for 15 publications (Fig. 2).

Overview of publication years in Ethnobotany highlighting the use of plants for human food in Brazil through 2023; the red curve indicates the cumulative total of articles at the identified dates

Research on the use of plants for human food is heterogeneously distributed in Brazil. Although it has been conducted across all geographic regions of the country, there is a clear concentration in the Northeast (52 studies; 31.9%) and North (38 studies; 23.3%) regions, which together account for 55.1% of the total retrieved in this review.

Among the states, Pará, Pernambuco, and Mato Grosso exhibit the highest number of published studies. Pará stands out, individually representing 52.6% of the entire scientific production of the North region which comprises seven states; of these, Amapá and Rondônia lack records regarding the use of flora for human food (Fig. 3).

Distribution of published articles on food plants by region and participating community in Brazil, up to 2023

Regarding the participating research communities, most studies were conducted in non-traditional rural communities, representing 54% of the total (n = 91). Traditional communities represented 31% of the studies, encompassing nine distinct sociocultural types: Quilombolas (Afro-Brazilian Maroon descendants) (8%; n = 14), Indigenous peoples (7.4%; n = 13), Ribeirinhos (riparian dwellers) (6.2%; n = 11), Fishers (4.5%; n = 8), Caiçaras (Traditional coastal populations) (2.8%; n = 5), Caboclo populations (1.1%; n = 2), and Azoreans (Azorean-Brazilians), Geraizeiros (Traditional Cerrado populations of the “Gerais” region) and Seringueiros (rubber tappers) (0.5%; n = 1 each). Specifically, regarding community types, urban communities were more extensively explored in the Southeastern region, generally associated with the Atlantic Forest biome (Fig. 4G-H). The mean number of participants per study was 86 per scientific article, with a minimum of one and a maximum of 1,061. The mean duration was 17 months, with a range varying from one-third of a month to 84 months; however, 58 articles did not report the research execution time or the frequency of field trips, elements that are fundamental to understanding data collection dynamics in Ethnobotany.

Brazilian food biodiversity

In total, 1,079 botanical species were cataloged (754 native and 325 exotic). These are used either directly, where the product is the plant itself, consumed in natura or through culinary processing; or indirectly, where the food is produced from previously processed plant resources, such as in fermented preparations or when transformed into powders, seasonings, or sugar crystals, leading to a de-characterization of the product’s botanical origin (Additional file 2). Although native plants exhibit greater richness, exotic species play an essential role in Brazilian cuisine, such as rice (Oryza sativa L.), common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.), maize (Zea mays L.), and wheat (Triticum aestivum L.).

Arecaceae was the family with the highest species diversity (92/8.5%), followed by Myrtaceae (89/8.2%), Fabaceae (76/7.04%), and Asteraceae (53/4.9%), while the genera with the highest diversity were Eugenia L. (27/2.5%), Solanum L. (25/2.3%), Annona L. and Inga Mill. (20/1.8% each).

Regarding the most frequently cited species, at least one-quarter of the analyzed articles mentioned one of the following: Psidium guajava L. (68/41.7%), Carica papaya L. (66/40.4%), Anacardium occidentale L. (65/39.8%), Mangifera indica L. (61/37.4%), Manihot esculenta Crantz (58/35.4%), Cocos nucifera L. and Eugenia uniflora L. (53/32.5% each), Persea americana Mill. (52/31.9%), Ipomoea batatas (L.) Lam. (45/27.6%), Passiflora edulis Sims (44/26.9%), Ananas comosus (L.) Merr. (43/26.3%), and Zea mays L. (42/25.7%).

The Atlantic Forest harbors the greatest diversity of food plants in Brazil, with 573 recorded species (53.1%), followed by the Amazon Rainforest (418 spp.; 38.7%) and the Cerrado (312 spp.; 28.9%). Regarding the number of exclusive native species, the predominantly forested phytogeographical domains (Atlantic Forest and Amazon Rainforest) led with 130 and 123 species, respectively, followed by the Caatinga with 94 species (Table 2). Fruits (49.1%) represent the plant organs with the highest diversity of cited species, followed by leaves (18.1%), seeds (10.2%), flowers (6.1%), stems (5.1%), roots (4.7%), and others (6.7%).

Floristic similarity patterns

Ordination (NMDS) and clustering (UPGMA) analyses revealed an expressive overlap in food species composition across all evaluated scales (Phytogeographical Domains, Communities, and Traditional Community Types) (Figs. 5, 6 and 7), a pattern corroborated by the low R-values in the ANOSIM test. Although PERMANOVA indicated statistically significant differences between groups (p < 0.05), the low proportion of explained variance (R2 < 0.25) and the significant results of the multivariate dispersion test (betadisper) suggest that such distinctions derive more from internal heterogeneity of variance than from floristic separation.

The dendrograms corroborated this trend, showing diffuse partitions with no evident cohesion, which reflects the absence of a clearly compartmentalized floristic structure at these scales. The consistency of these representations was ensured by appropriate stress values in the NMDS and high cophenetic correlation coefficients in the dendrograms (Table 3), confirming that the data’s similarity structure is characterized by extensive species sharing among the studied vegetational domains and communities. It is noteworthy that, for the traditional communities, the dendrograms showed trends of partially cohesive subgroups, especially for exotic species, although without evident floristic divisions. Despite these incipient groupings, the results reinforce that the sharing of plant resources remains the predominant characteristic among the different traditional populations analyzed. Discussion

Ethnobotany is a growing field in Brazil, this popularity can be measured both by the results found in this research and by other ethnobotanical reviews, particularly during the first decades of the 2000s [22, 23].

The most explored botanical families are those with the highest number of species associated with specific uses, notably Anacardiaceae, Arecaceae, Myrtaceae, and Passifloraceae. Furthermore, the fact that fruit tree diversity surpasses other types of food plants is a constant finding across different regions of Brazil and the world [9, 24, 25]. This phenomenon may be closely associated with selection characteristics [26].

Plant species that provide structures other than commercial fruits, that is, those where only the pericarp, mesocarp, and endocarp are edible, may exhibit lower diversity and be underestimated regarding their socioeconomic relevance due to low representation in general checklists. However, they play a vital role in Brazil’s food security, especially those producing edible seeds, such as cereals (rice, wheat, and corn) and legumes (beans, peas, and soybeans), as well as starch-rich roots (potatoes and cassava) [27, 28].

Caution is warranted in the interpretation of such data, as they may virtually mask the importance of agricultural species with broad consumption and dietary significance. In these cases, low taxonomic richness should not be confused with irrelevance, as the hegemony of these foods in the national diet exerts a socioeconomic and cultural influence that biological diversity metrics, in isolation, fail to represent.

Since, if diet were driven solely by environmental availability, our dietary patterns would proportionally reflect the local flora. However, data from China and Brazil demonstrate the opposite [26, 29]. In China, out of 39,174 species, only 28% are utilized, with a clear preference for families such as Rosaceae and Lamiaceae for medicinal and food purposes [26]. In contrast, in Brazil, the selection focuses on Arecaceae, Cactaceae, and Myrtaceae [29].

Curiously, from this perspective, the Poaceae family, which includes global food staples, is underutilized within the context of Brazilian wild plants [26]. This suggests that although modern agriculture relies heavily on grasses, in local wild harvesting, the Brazilian cultural filter rejects these plants in favor of more sensorially attractive resources, such as fruits containing edible epi, meso, and endocarps.

This same cultural selectivity, which favors certain groups over wild abundance, helps explain the consolidation of exotic species in the national diet. The relevance of exotic plants in the Brazilian diet, such as the widely consumed rice (O. sativa) and beans (P. vulgaris), exemplifies this homogenization trend driven by global food staples, as discussed by Rodrigues et al. [28].

Such a scenario of homogenization and preference for introduced species is inseparable from the process of invasion and colonization of the Brazilian territory. This historical period not only enabled the introduction and spread of various exotic food plants but also established a constant flow of people of different nationalities, which fostered the diffusion of traditional ecological knowledge and plants of regional interest [3, 30–32].

This flow of people, knowledge, and experiences, encompassing both immigrants and rural migrants from vulnerable regions in the North and Northeast to urban areas in the South and Southeast [30, 31] was responsible for breaking down geographical, phytogeographical, and cultural barriers. This facilitated the spread of traditional knowledge from isolated communities to major urban centers, establishing a significant network of exchange regarding species with potential as food resources [8, 12, 32, 33].

The fact that the Traditional Community Type explains species composition more effectively (R2 up to 24%) than the Phytogeographical Domain (R2 < 10%) suggests that human management and tradition are stronger selective forces than environmental constraints. Culture, therefore, transports and maintains species regardless of the phytogeographical domain, reinforcing the concept of anthropogenic landscapes, which we shape based on our needs and memories, thereby challenging geographical determinism.

The human migration and colonization of the planet occurred in parallel with the dissemination of plants and animals essential to our survival [34, 35]. Cereals, vegetables, fruit trees, and tubers were extensively favored in this process, at local and regional scales as well as globally [34–37].

For migrants, geographical and cultural barriers drive dietary adaptation, where preparation methods are preserved at the expense of substituting ingredients, or consumed species, with local or processed options, representing a nutritional transition and a loss of dietary diversity [38]. This may partially justify the homogenization of dietary patterns within these communities.

Furthermore, local management has been an important mechanism for food enhancement, reflected in the domestication and genetic crossing of various species, such as Cupuaçu (Theobroma grandiflorum (Willd. ex-Spreng.) K. Schum.). This species originated from genetic management promoted through variety selection and crossbreeding carried out by generations of Indigenous peoples in the North of the country, starting prior to the Portuguese invasion and colonization of the region, and is generally consumed in Amazon Rainforest areas [39].

Culture, therefore, is a substantial force in understanding the dietary patterns of a human population [38]. Traditional knowledge plays a fundamental and multifaceted role in food security, acting not only as a cultural guide but also as a survival mechanism that encompasses the production, processing, preservation, and distribution of food [38, 40–42].

The patterns observed in this review may directly reflect human influence and the utilitarian nature of these species rather than the ecological and biogeographical processes that shape wild flora. Human activity plays a significant role in the intentional or accidental dispersal of plants across the planet through cultivation and trade, which has enabled the wide range and reach of various plant species globally [34, 35, 43].

Regarding the similarity results found among the subgroups within Traditional Communities, even though the data demonstrated a comparatively stronger influence with a higher R2, indicating the role of sociocultural factors and human management in shaping floristic composition, this influence did not result in clearly distinct floristic groups.

A separation between resource types is observed: while native species remain tied to environmental specificities and traditional management practices, exotic species exhibit a homogeneous distribution across traditional communities, reflecting the influence of globalization and capitalist consumption models.

There is evidence that the introduction of industrialized foods into brazilian iIndigenous villages has reduced dietary diversity and increased the incidence of chronic diseases, such as obesity and diabetes, among indigenous populations [44, 45]. This nutritional transition, accelerated by interethnic contact, consolidates a dependency on ultra-processed products at the expense of natural resources [45, 46], culminating in the erosion of traditional knowledge and the disuse of ancestral plants.

Finally, it is important to emphasize that the results and the interpretation of the data found in this research warrant caution and oversight, as there are significant limitations to be considered. Our survey was conducted specifically using scientific articles, which limited our reach regarding the complex network of studies published in books, theses, and dissertations.

There is a clear discrepancy in the conduct of ethnobotanical research among rural community groups in Brazil, with a concentration in the states of Pernambuco, Pará, Paraíba, and São Paulo; however, there is a lack of clear methodological standardization, which limits and biases our results regarding actual dietary similarity.

This is further evidenced by the lack of research in the states of Rondônia and Amapá. A preprint of a study conducted in Amapá is currently in circulation but was not included in this review, as it was not retrieved during the systematic database searches or cross-referencing, and because it has not yet been definitively approved through the peer-review process.

Regarding the types of traditional communities studied, there is still a long way to go in understanding their interactions, perceptions, and dietary biodiversity. The scarcity of data is evident, as to date, only 14 Indigenous ethnic groups have had their knowledge recorded, representing just 3.5% of the total population of such groups [47].

The scarcity of data is evident even among quilombola communities, the group with the highest volume of records, with only 14 studies identified. This deficiency becomes even more critical when observing groups such as Azorean-Brazilians, Geraizeiros, and Seringueiros, for which only a single published article was found, highlighting a gap of up to tenfold compared to quilombola records.

Furthermore, it is important to emphasize that the endemism factor must be considered regarding the similarity, availability, and variance of species within the tested community groups, particularly for those species obtained exclusively from the wild, even within their own domain, given their restricted geographical distribution to specific regions [48]. These species may significantly impact the interpretation of the results found in this review, especially since their influence was not directly measured.

Ultimately, this study was conducted using a database comprising a wide range of research that, for the most part, did not follow a standardized format for data collection, objectives, or analysis. The data presented here are valuable for understanding the distribution of food species diversity among different Brazilian communities and the preference for certain areas and community types in national research. This will aid in identifying gaps that need to be filled regarding the consumption and diversity patterns of food plants in Brazil, serving as a methodological foundation for ethnobotanical research in other countries or specific ethnic groups.

Conclusion

Brazil stands out for its high richness of food species, with a predominance of the Arecaceae, Fabaceae, and Myrtaceae families, distributed among 754 native and 325 exotic species. The analysis of ethnobotanical research reveals a heterogeneous distribution of research groups in the country, concentrated in specific regional hubs: the Northeast (Paraíba and Pernambuco), the Southeast (São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro, and Minas Gerais), and the North (Pará and Amazonas). This concentration is reflected in the majority focus on non-traditional rural communities, especially in the Northeast, and urban communities in the Southeast, while traditional communities have a more homogeneous research coverage across the country.

The predictors selected for this review did not demonstrate a consistent capacity to distinguish the analyzed groups based on their dietary diversity similarity. Although some detectable statistical differences exist, particularly concerning traditional community types and native food species, the variables of phytogeographical domain, community type, and traditional community were unable to delineate distinct classes according to the similarity of consumed food diversity.

The predictors chosen to determine the similarity of food species in the evaluated scientific articles, phytogeographic domain, community type (urbanity), and types of traditional communities, did not demonstrate a consistent capacity to distinguish the analyzed groups according to their dietary diversity similarity. Although there are some detectable statistical differences, especially when considering the type of traditional community and exotic food species, the groups could not delimit the different classes according to the similarity of the dietary diversity consumed.

This inability to delimit classes may be associated with biases in the selected articles and particularities not evaluated in the research, such as endemism, extinction, culture, and the low representativeness of specific social groups. These factors were not measured in this perspective, and their relevance must be considered when interpreting the results found in this review.

Although the last five decades of publications have significantly expanded the knowledge of dietary diversity in Brazil, there is still much to be explored. In this regard, it is crucial to highlight the gaps in study areas for future research, where the states of Amapá and Rondônia are priorities, as there are no published reports on the dietary diversity of their local populations. Furthermore, it is essential to diversify our sampling efforts to include traditional and urban communities, which remain underrepresented.

In this sense, we propose that the compiled data and mappings presented here serve as a foundation for future explorations of Brazil’s traditional food heritage.
