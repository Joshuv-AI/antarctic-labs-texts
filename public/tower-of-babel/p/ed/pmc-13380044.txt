Dietary regimens and cognitive function in multiple sclerosis: a systematic review
Authors: Hila Navadeh Shahla, Farid Jahanshahlou, Mehrdad Nourizadeh, Bita Ostad-Hassanzadeh, Shideh Roshani, Mahnaz Talebi, Sarvin Sanaie, Amirreza Naseri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCognitive impairment is a common and debilitating complication of multiple sclerosis (MS), but non-pharmacological strategies such as diet for its management have not yet been fully explored. The aim of this review is to synthesize the evidence on the relationship between diet and cognitive outcomes in people with MS. Clinical interventional and observational studies evaluating the association between the diet and cognitive outcomes in MS were considered. The diets most frequently evaluated were Mediterranean (n = 3), modified Paleolithic/Wahls (n = 3), Swank (n = 1), ketogenic (n = 2), and time-restricted eating (n = 1). Other included studies investigated the broader association between overall dietary quality, healthy-eating patterns, and cognitive outcomes in MS patients. Trials of the Mediterranean diet have generally shown no significant cognitive improvements, although a recent study on a low-calorie Mediterranean diet, as well as observational studies, has reported increased processing speed, manual dexterity, and fewer cognitive complaints. The Wahls/Paleolithic interventions showed improvements in processing speed and mental cognition that were comparable to the Swank diet. Limited evidence from small studies suggested potential benefits of ketogenic and time-restricted diets, while findings were inconsistent for general healthy or high-fiber diets. Dietary interventions may hold promise as adjunctive strategies for cognitive health in MS; however, inconsistencies in study designs, cognitive measures, and intervention duration, combined with limited high-quality evidence, undermine strong recommendations, which warrant further evaluations.Keywords: Multiple sclerosis, Cognition, Diet, Systematic review

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic, progressive immune-mediated disease of the central nervous system (CNS), characterized by demyelination, neuroinflammation, and axonal damage (Boutitah-Benyaich et al., 2025). Affecting over 2.8 million people around the world, MS often leads to a wide range of physical and cognitive dysfunction (Walton et al., 2020). Cognitive impairment is among the most challenging symptoms of MS, which probably affects approximately 40–70% of patients (Wu et al., 2025). It is characterized by a pattern of deficits affecting multiple cognitive domains. Information processing speed is considered the most common affected domain and is often regarded as a hallmark cognitive feature of the disease (Benedict et al., 2020). Impairments in attention, memory, learning, and executive functions are also frequently reported (Sahebari et al., 2024; Talebi et al., 2022). These deficits may occur even in the early stages of the disease and can progress independently of physical disability. While language functions seem to be preserved, some patients experience difficulties in visuospatial processing and social cognition, too (Portaccio and Amato, 2022). Cognitive impairment can affect employment status, social participation, treatment adherence, and overall quality of life, making it a major contributor to disease burden in people with MS (Macías Islas and Ciampi, 2019; Morrow et al., 2023). A 2023 Australian multidisciplinary review highlighted that cognitive impairment often remains under-recognized in clinical practice due to resource constraints (Lechner-Scott et al., 2023). While disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) have made substantial strides in reducing relapses and slowing disability, meaningful treatment options specifically targeting cognitive decline remain limited (Kandjani et al., 2023). A 2020 meta-analysis of pharmacological interventions found no significant overall benefit with acetylcholine esterase inhibitors, CNS stimulants, fampridine, and herbal remedies (Lechner-Scott et al., 2023; Motavalli et al., 2020). These issues highlight the need to investigate alternative, non-pharmacological interventions, such as dietary approaches, for the management of cognitive symptoms in MS, which have been proposed to improve cognitive function in people with MS (Zielińska and Michońska, 2023).

Dietary regimens encompass a range of structured nutritional approaches aimed at enhancing neurological health and managing disorders. Evidence indicates that dietary patterns can play a critical role in modulating disease and symptoms and progression in MS (Shakouri et al., 2026; Stoiloudis et al., 2022; Ware-Maloney et al., 2025). These regimens are informed by a growing understanding of the interplay between nutrition, cognitive function, and neurological health, emphasizing the critical role that diet plays across various neurological conditions (Badaeva et al., 2023). Diet plays a role in systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, gut microbiota composition, and metabolic health, all of which are involved in MS pathophysiology (Ahmadi et al., 2026; Alipour et al., 2026; Gill et al., 2022; Katz Sand, 2018; Stoiloudis et al., 2022). Different dietary interventions have been suggested to manage MS symptoms, including the Mediterranean diet (Abbasi et al., 2024), Modified Paleolithic Diet (Ramanathan et al., 2018), Wahls Diet and Swank Diet (Wahls et al., 2018, 2021), ketogenic diet (Lin et al., 2022), and time-restricted eating (Lin et al., 2023). Recommended diets for MS management often emphasize high intakes of vegetables, fruits, omega-3 fatty acids, whole grains, and other nutrient-rich foods, elements known for their anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective properties, suggesting that diets may be particularly beneficial in MS by modulating neuroinflammation and enhancing neurotrophic pathways (Naseri et al., 2022; Talebi et al., 2021).

The profound impact of diet on brain health and its potential to mitigate cognitive dysfunction in MS is an area of growing scientific interest (Stoiloudis et al., 2022). Understanding how specific dietary patterns influence the complex interactions between inflammation, neural pathways, and metabolic processes can pave the way for transformative approaches in managing MS-related cognitive challenges (Fitzgerald et al., 2018). The aim of this systematic review is to evaluate the association between diet and cognitive outcomes in MS, as well as assessing the effects of different dietary regimens on cognitive findings in this population.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 checklist (Supplementary Material 1) (Page et al., 2021). The present systematic review was registered in International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO registration number: CRD420251156238).

Eligibility criteria

Clinical investigations, including the studies with both observational or interventional designs, were eligible if they examined the relationship between dietary regimens and cognitive findings (both objective neuropsychological tests and/or validated self-report cognitive measures) in MS patients, regardless of disease phenotype or severity, age, sex, ethnicity. Studies were excluded if they were.

Studies including participants with comorbid conditions commonly associated with MS like depressive symptoms, were not excluded a priori. However, the presence of comorbid depression was variably assessed and reported across studies and represents a potential source of residual confounding.

Information sources and search strategy

PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and Web of Science databases were systematically searched without restrictions on language, publication year, or study design. Two independent researchers (S.S. and A.N.) conducted the search on April 9, 2023. Additionally, reference lists of the included studies were screened manually for potentially relevant articles. Also, a manual search of the databases was conducted on September 20, 2025, to cover newer articles. The search strategy combined both MeSH terms and free-text keywords to maximize inclusivity. The details are presented in Supplementary Material 2.

Selection process

All retrieved records were imported into EndNote, and duplicate entries were identified and removed prior to screening. Two reviewers (H.N. and F.J.) independently screened title/abstracts, and full texts using Rayyan web and mobile app (Ouzzani et al., 2016). Decisions were made blindly, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (A.N. or S.S.). Although no language restrictions were applied during the search, only English-language full texts were included in the final review.

Data collection process

A standardized data extraction table was developed to collect relevant study information (Table 1, Table 2). Data were extracted by one reviewer (H.N., B.O-H., or S.R.) and independently verified by another (A.N.) to ensure accuracy.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two researchers (F.J. and H.N.) using the JBI critical appraisal tools for both observational and interventional studies (Barker et al., 2023, 2024, 2025). Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with another author (S.S. or A.N.).

Synthesis methods

Meta-analysis was not conducted due to substantial clinical, methodological, and outcome heterogeneity across studies. Even within nominally similar dietary categories, intervention protocols and cognitive endpoints were not sufficiently comparable to support valid quantitative synthesis. Therefore, a meta-analysis, including subgroup or semi-quantitative pooling, was not methodologically appropriate.

Study characteristics and results of individual studies

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) formed the majority (Seven articles from five studies), and four non-randomized interventional studies as well as seven observational studies, comprised the rest included articles which offered insights into associations between diet and cognitive outcomes in MS. Sample sizes ranged from 12 participants in feasibility trials to 6989 in observational studies. Study durations varied from eight weeks to 12 months (except for studies with retrospective and cross-sectional designs). Female participants constituted the majority across studies. Structured dietary protocols included the Mediterranean diet (Felicetti et al., 2025; Katz Sand et al., 2019; Razeghi-Jahromi et al., 2020) (n = 3), Modified Paleolithic diet (Wahls™) (Lee et al., 2017, 2021; Saxby et al., 2024; Villa et al., 2022; Wahls et al., 2021) (n = 3, five reports), Swank diets (Saxby et al., 2024; Villa et al., 2022; Wahls et al., 2021) (n = 1, three reports, analyzed alongside the Wahls diet), ketogenic diet (Lee et al., 2021; Merlino et al., 2023) (n = 2, one of them was analyzed alongside the Wahls diet), time-restricted eating (Wingo et al., 2022) (TRE; n = 1), and an application-based diet program aiming to promote an intake of 800 g/d of fruit and vegetables (Silveira et al., 2025a) (n = 1). Observational studies (Amato et al., 2019a; Fitzgerald et al., 2018; Hartono et al., 2025; Katz Sand et al., 2023; Marck et al., 2021; Nag et al., 2021b) (n = 7) assessed dietary quality using adherence scales or dietary surveys such as the Dietary Habits Questionnaire (DHQ), Mediterranean Diet Adherence Screener (MEDAS), and Healthy Eating Index (HEI). The details of the dietary assessments and interventions are presented in Table 1, Table 2. Cognitive outcomes were assessed using objective neuropsychological tests including Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT) (assessing processing speed and attention) (Smith, 1973), Paced Auditory Serial Addition Test (PASAT) (assessing sustained attention, working memory, and information processing speed) (Gronwall, 1977), Delis-Kaplan Executive Function System (D–KEFS) (assessing verbal fluency) (Fine and Delis, 2011), the Second Edition of California verbal learning test (CVLT-II) (assessing verbal learning and memory) (Woods et al., 2006), brief visuospatial memory test-revised (BVMT-R) (assessing visuospatial learning and memory) (Benedict et al., 1996), Cognitive Stability Index (CSI)/Cognitive Screening Test (CST) (assessing attention, processing speed, motor speed, and memory) (Erlanger et al., 2002), Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS-III) (assessing intelligence) (Ryan and Lopez, 2001), and Minimal Assessment of Cognitive Function in MS (MACFIMS) battery (a comprehensive assessment of domains including processing speed, memory, executive function, visuospatial abilities, and language) (Benedict et al., 2002) and self-report measures such as Perceived Deficits Questionnaire (PDQ) (assessing subjective cognitive difficulties in attention, memory, and executive functioning) (Sullivan et al., 1990), Performance Scale (Schwartz et al., 1999), NeuroQol (Cella et al., 2012) and cognitive subscales of the MS Quality of Life Questionnaire (MSQoL-54) (evaluating the perceived impact of cognitive function on quality of life) (Vickrey et al., 1995). The details of the characteristics and results of individual studies are presented in Table 1, Table 2.

Risk of bias

The results of RoB assessments are presented in Table 3. In RCTs, due to the nature of the interventions, the researchers delivering the treatments were not blind. Also, there was concerns regarding the allocation concealment in three studies and open-label nature of these studies can potentially bias their outcome. Appropriate blinding of the outcome assessors was reported in two studies. In the quasi-experimental studies, there was no control groups and the comparisons were made with baseline condition which can bias their conclusions. Regarding the observational studies which were appraised using the cross-sectional analytical studies checklist (Barker et al., 2025), MS diagnosis based on the McDonald criteria (Montalban et al., 2025) was only reported in two studies. In addition, self-reported cognitive measurements were also another potential source of bias in the studies.

Mediterranean diet

The Mediterranean diet is defined by high consumption of vegetables, fruits, legumes, nuts, whole grains, and olive oil; moderate consumption of fish and poultry; and limited consumption of red/processed meat and sweets. In a retrospective cohort of 563 patients, Katz Sand et al. reported that higher Mediterranean diet adherence screening scores were correlated with better processing speed (based on SDMT) and fewer perceived deficits (based on PDQ) (Katz Sand et al., 2023). Three RCTs evaluated the cognitive effects of Mediterranean diet in MS (Felicetti et al., 2025; Katz Sand et al., 2019; Razeghi-Jahromi et al., 2020). Razeghi-Jahromi et al. assigned 80 patients with relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS) to a Mediterranean diet versus a standard diet for one year and assessed cognitive outcomes in 56 participants using the MACFIMS battery. No significant between-group differences were observed in processing speed, memory, visuospatial, or executive domains (Razeghi-Jahromi et al., 2020). In another randomized controlled trial, Katz Sand et al. assigned 36 patients to a United States Department of Agriculture (USDA)-based Mediterranean diet or a control group for 6 months. Similarly, no change was observed in the MACFIMS composite score (Katz Sand et al., 2019). In contrast, a recent RCT by Felicetti et al. evaluated three low-calorie Mediterranean diets, differing in macronutrient composition, among 53 overweight MS patients over six months. Significant improvements in processing speed and manual dexterity on the MS performance test were observed, with no differences between the diet types, suggesting that overall adherence to the Mediterranean diet and calorie restriction may support cognitive function in MS (Felicetti et al., 2025). In summary, while previous RCTs did not show significant cognitive benefits from Mediterranean diets in MS, a recent trial reported improvements in processing speed and manual dexterity with low-calorie Mediterranean diets. Observational data similarly suggest that greater adherence to a Mediterranean diet may be associated with better processing speed and fewer cognitive complaints.

Wahls/Modified Paleolithic Diet

The Wahls/modified Paleolithic diet eliminates grains, legumes, dairy, and processed foods and emphasizes vegetables, fruits, high-quality proteins, and omega-3-rich oils. Lee et al. conducted a 12-month open-label trial of a modified Paleolithic diet in progressive MS that examined within-group improvements in several cognitive tests (CSI (<55 years), CST (>55 years), D-KEFS, and WAIS-III). No significant changes in cognitive scores were observed in the first three months, however, by 12 months, significant improvements in mean scores were observed with the exception of CSI/CST speed (Lee et al., 2017). A subsequent 12-week RCT by Lee et al. tested a modified Paleolithic ketogenic diet based on medium-chain triglycerides in patients with RRMS, but found no significant changes in PASAT scores compared with controls (Lee et al., 2021). Wahls et al. randomized patients with RRMS to either the Wahls or Swank diets for 36 weeks and observed significant within-group improvements in SDMT and PDQ scores in both groups with no between-group differences (Wahls et al., 2021). Subsequent secondary analyses confirmed these findings. Villa et al. suggests improvements in processing speed and perceived deficits at weeks 24 and 36, while Saxby et al. reported clinically meaningful reductions in PDQ subdomains (Saxby et al., 2024; Villa et al., 2022). In summary, the Wahls diet interventions showed improvements in both objective processing speed and subjective cognition, although the benefits were comparable to those observed with the Swank diet.

Ketogenic diet

The ketogenic diet involves a very low-carbohydrate, high-fat, moderate-protein diet designed to induce nutritional ketosis. In a six-month exploratory study of 21 patients with RRMS, Merlino et al. reported improvements in the cognitive subscale of MSQoL after a ketogenic diet (Merlino et al., 2023). In contrast, Lee et al. tested a 12-week medium-chain triglyceride ketogenic protocol in four patients but found no change in PASAT scores compared with controls (Lee et al., 2021). Overall, preliminary data suggest that ketogenic diets may improve cognition in MS, but findings are inconsistent, and long-term efficacy and feasibility remain uncertain.

Swank Diet

The Swank diet is a low-fat diet with ≤20 g/day of saturated fat, increased intake of whole grains, fruits, and vegetables. The Swank diet was studied alongside the Wahls diet in the WAVES trial. Wahls et al. observed significant within-group improvements in the SDMT and PDQ after 36 weeks (Wahls et al., 2021). Villa et al. suggests improvements in processing speed and perceived deficits, and Saxby et al. (2024) described detailed improvements in several PDQ subdomains. No observational studies were found on the Swank or other low-fat diets (Saxby et al., 2024; Villa et al., 2022). Overall, evidence from a single RCT suggests that the Swank diet is associated with improvements in processing speed and cognitive function, with effects similar to those of the Wahls diet.

General healthy/fiber-rich diets

Healthy/high-fiber diets in general, are such as those with a balanced intake of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and adequate dietary fiber, without severe restrictions in macronutrients. In an eight-week single-arm trial of 16 patients, Silveira et al. found no changes in SDMT, CVLT-II, or BVMT-R after a high-fruit and vegetable intake intervention (Silveira et al., 2025). Jelinek et al. also reported that higher diet quality measured by the DHQ was associated with fewer cognitive complaints in 2464 participants, while Marck et al. found that moderate fiber intake was associated with fewer reported deficits (Jelinek et al., 2019; Marck et al., 2021). Fitzgerald et al., using recorded data from nearly 7000 participants, reported no association between diet quality and cognitive symptoms (Fitzgerald et al., 2018). In a long-term follow-up of the HOLISM cohort by Hartono et al., the association between diet and cognition was not significant after adjustment for confounders (Hartono et al., 2025). In summary, observational studies suggest a possible association between healthier dietary patterns and fewer cognitive complaints.

Other diets

Other dietary/specialized diets such as vegetarian, gluten-free, lactose-free, organic, low-sodium, low-sugar, or time-restricted dietary approaches. Wingo et al. (2022) conducted a small pilot study of time-restricted feeding (8-h eating window) in six patients and reported improvements in PASAT and MFIS cognitive subscale scores (Wingo et al., 2022). Amato et al., 2019a found no differences in cognitive status between patients following vegetarian, gluten-free, or lactose-free diets and controls (M. P. Amato et al., 2019a,b). Nag et al., 2021b, in a cross-sectional study of 1135 participants, reported that only the “other” diet category (including organic, low-sodium, and low-sugar) was associated with better self-reported cognition (N. Nag et al., 2021a,b). Overall, the evidence for specific diets such as vegetarian, gluten-free, or time-restricted diets is weak and inconsistent.

Discussion

Cognitive impairment is a common and debilitating feature of MS, and effective treatment strategies remain limited, making dietary approaches an attractive non-pharmacological option. While diet has long been studied for its potential role in modulating inflammation, oxidative stress, and neuroprotection, its impact on cognition in MS has received less attention. Our aim in this systematic review was to synthesize the available evidence on the relationship between diet and cognitive function in patients with MS. By systematically reviewing interventional and observational studies across a range of dietary patterns, this review sought to clarify the available evidence, identify consistent trends, and highlight knowledge gaps to guide future research. The limited available evidence suggests that both the Wahls and Swank diets are associated with improved cognition, suggesting that shared elements, such as anti-inflammatory properties, nutrient density, and adherence to structured eating patterns, may be more influential than the specific type of diet itself. While improvements in subjective cognition have been frequently reported, changes in objective neuropsychological measures have been less consistent, suggesting a potential gap between perceived and measurable cognitive benefits that has also been documented in previous research (Golabi et al., 2024; Thomas et al., 2023). Several dietary interventions were associated with improvements in self-reported cognition or quality-of-life cognitive subscales, while objective neuropsychological test performance showed more modest or inconsistent changes. Most intervention studies were small, several lacked blinding, and a substantial proportion of the evidence base consisted of observational designs. In addition, diversity in cognitive instruments, study designs, and intervention duration complicates comparisons and may lead to conflicting findings. This discrepancy highlights the need for larger and more robust interventional studies with cognition as the primary endpoint.

Diets rich in anti-inflammatory components, such as the Mediterranean diet, have shown the potential to improve cognitive function. This was evident in studies using instruments such as the SDMT and the MACFIMS cognitive battery. We observed that these benefits were associated with a reduction in systemic inflammation, a known factor in MS-related cognitive decline (Daneshvar et al., 2025; Katz Sand et al., 2023). Furthermore, the Wahls diet, which emphasizes nutrient-rich whole foods, showed promising effects in enhancing specific cognitive domains, including memory and processing speed. We attributed these improvements to increased intake of vitamins, antioxidants, and essential fatty acids that support neurological health (Lee et al., 2017). In contrast, while the MCT-based modified ketogenic diet has shown some metabolic benefits, its effects on cognitive function and fatigue have been less evident. Notably, TRE has shown promising improvements in cognitive function, specifically, increased SDMT scores (Wingo et al., 2022). Further research is needed to determine whether longer intervention periods or combination with other therapies might yield more consistent cognitive benefits.

In a study investigating the mechanisms of diets’ influence on brain health in neurodegenerative diseases, roles such as modulation of oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, neuroinflammation, and synaptic plasticity were highlighted (Silveira et al., 2025b). The reviewed studies also highlighted the potential role of dietary interventions in reducing inflammatory cytokines such as Interleukin (IL)-17 and improving lipid profiles, which found to be associated with modulation of systemic and neuroinflammation, and may underlie the observed cognitive improvements (Sanaie et al., 2024). It is also possible that specific dietary patterns and components like high fiber, omega-3, reduced saturated fat, and plant-based foods modulate neuroplasticity and neuroinflammation, strengthening the rationale for investigating diet-cognition links in MS (Di Majo et al., 2022).

Furthermore, the role of the gut-brain axis emerged as a key mechanism, with fiber-rich diets such as the Mediterranean diet shown to modulate the gut microbiome. This interaction reduces systemic inflammation and enhances neuronal health, offering a new avenue for addressing cognitive impairment in MS (Fitzgerald et al., 2018); However, evidence for microbiota-targeted supplementation is limited (Mosaddeghi-Heris et al., 2026). Despite the anti-inflammatory effects (Eghbal and Naseri, 2026), a recent randomized controlled trial did not demonstrate meaningful cognitive benefits of probiotics supplementation in relapsing–remitting MS (Naseri et al., 2025). Microbiota-targeted interventions represent a related and emerging research area that warrants further investigation using rigorous and standardized cognitive endpoints.

Depressive symptoms are prevalent among MS patients (Margoni et al., 2023). Considering the defined association between depression and cognitive outcomes (Glukhovsky et al., 2021), and possible effects of dietary approaches in improving depressive symptoms in MS (Jellinger, 2024; Staudacher et al., 2025), depression can also mediate these effects.

From another perspective, the interaction between dietary patterns and genetic background in MS is a new area of exploration. Patients with genetic alterations affecting lipid metabolism or inflammatory responses may benefit from customized Mediterranean or ketogenic approaches tailored to their unique biochemical needs (Marck et al., 2021). Personalized nutrition, guided by genetic profiling, could optimize dietary recommendations for specific patients (Aruoma et al., 2019). In addition, integrating dietary strategies with other therapeutic approaches, such as physical exercise and cognitive training, can enhance their effectiveness. Combining a Mediterranean diet with regular aerobic exercise may have synergistic benefits in reducing inflammation and enhancing cognitive function. These practical approaches can be implemented through multidisciplinary care teams to ensure comprehensive support for MS patients (Langeskov-Christensen et al., 2021; Lechner-Scott et al., 2023).

To effectively translate the findings of this study into clinical practice, healthcare providers should consider tailoring dietary recommendations to individual patients (Fiorella et al., 2022). Small, incremental changes, such as adding more vegetables to daily meals or replacing processed grains with whole grains, can significantly improve adherence. Additionally, providing education on the benefits of anti-inflammatory diets, along with support tools like meal-planning apps, may enhance patient engagement and long-term success (Salas-Groves et al., 2023). Silveira et al. showed that through an online intervention to increase fruit and vegetable consumption in people with MS, significant improvements were achieved in diet quality, vegetable and fruit consumption, and some metabolic markers (Silveira et al., 2025). Significant concern in this area was that cognitive symptoms would interfere with maintaining healthy habits. In an analysis of data from 671 people with MS over five years in the HOLISM cohort, cross-sectional associations showed that worse self-reported cognition was associated with lower dietary adherence. However, longitudinal cognitive symptoms did not significantly interfere with maintaining healthy habits, with the exception of mobility, which affects physical activity (Hartono et al., 2025). Several supplement-based strategies have also been explored for cognition in MS, including melatonin, caffeine, and vitamin D (Darwish et al., 2017; Naseri et al., 2023; Salekzamani et al., 2024). While these supplements show preliminary promise, the evidence is insufficient to recommend them as reliable interventions for cognitive impairment in MS.

Although this review highlights the potential of dietary interventions, it should be noted that heterogeneity in study designs, dietary protocols, and cognitive assessment tools complicates direct comparisons between the included studies. In addition, most studies were of short duration and small sample sizes, limiting the ability to assess long-term effects and generalizability. Progressive types of MS have been less studied, creating gaps in understanding the utility of these interventions for different patient populations. Future research should include standardized dietary protocols, larger, and more diverse groups to establish robust evidence for cognitive benefits. Mechanistic studies examining the gut-brain axis and inflammatory pathways in MS could provide deeper insight into how dietary interventions exert their effects. By addressing these gaps, more personalized and effective dietary strategies for MS management can be developed. Inclusion of only English-language papers was the limitation during the review process.

In summary, this systematic review suggests that dietary interventions may offer benefits for cognitive outcomes in MS, particularly in processing speed and self-perceived cognitive function. Among the dietary patterns studied, the Wahls, Swank, and Mediterranean diets appear to be the most promising, although findings from RCTs remain limited and inconsistent. However, the certainty of evidence is limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous interventions, lack of blinding, and reliance on observational designs. Well-powered, methodologically rigorous randomized controlled trials with standardized cognitive outcome measures are required before firm clinical recommendations can be made.
