From single cells to communities: Mathematical perspectives on bacterial quorum sensing
Authors: Sara Sadr, Bahram Zargar, Marc G. Aucoin, Brian Ingalls
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The complex social lives of microbes, defined by sophisticated communication networks and cooperative behaviors, play a pivotal role in their survival and adaptation. Central to these interactions is Quorum Sensing (QS), a mechanism widely used by bacterial species for population-scale communication and subsequent gene regulation. QS involves coordinated synthesis, release, and detection of signaling molecules known as autoinducers (AIs). By sensing the concentration of AI as a proxy for the local density of the bacterial population, QS orchestrates the modulation of target gene expression. The molecular foundations of QS have been revealed, but fundamental quantitative challenges remain in the pursuit of a complete understanding of QS dynamics. Mathematical modeling has become an essential tool for investigating QS dynamics at both single-cell and population levels. A variety of modeling approaches, including deterministic, stochastic, non-spatial, and spatial frameworks have been employed to explore the complexities of QS systems. We provide an overview of mathematical models developed to describe and analyze QS mechanisms and dynamics, highlighting their contributions and limitations. Looking ahead, QS modeling is poised to support synthetic biology, antimicrobial therapy, environmental management, and more, offering new strategies to manipulate bacterial behavior for improving biotechnological applications, combating infections, and optimizing industrial processes.

Introduction

Until the mid-20th century, bacteria were largely regarded as solitary entities functioning independently without significant communication [1]. This perception shifted with the foundational discovery of quorum sensing (QS) in the marine bacterium Aliivibrio fischeri (reclassified from Vibrio fischeri in 2007 [2]), which revealed that bacteria use chemical signals to coordinate collective behaviors, laying the foundation for understanding microbial social interactions [3]. QS involves the production and detection of small signaling molecules known as autoinducers (AIs), through which bacteria coordinate a wide range of behaviors across population scales. These signaling molecules are regulated by positive feedback loops (Fig. 1), leading to a switch-like response in gene expression based on local bacterial density [4], [5]. Such cell-cell communication facilitates both intra- and inter-species interactions, allowing bacteria to respond to environmental cues and adapt to diverse ecological niches [6], [7]. QS orchestrates complex behaviors, including the regulation of virulence factors, bioluminescence, biofilm formation, and sporulation [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]. Additionally, QS supports defensive strategies against toxins and fosters symbiotic relationships crucial for survival [13].Fig. 1Quorum sensing in bacterial populations. A. fischeri, a Gram-negative bioluminescent bacterium, lives freely in marine environments. It also forms a symbiotic relationship with the Hawaiian bobtail squid, Euprymna scolopes [14]. The first evidence of QS in bacteria was observed from investigations of this relationship [15]. Among the Gram-negative bacteria in which QS systems have been characterized, the primary QS circuit (Fig. 2A) often resembles that of A. fischeri, with notable exceptions, such as Vibrio harveyi, which employs a hybrid QS system, and Myxococcus xanthus, which uses a signaling system linked to multicellular development [3], [5]. Many Gram-negative bacteria use N-acyl homoserine lactones (AHL, classified as AI-1) as AIs [16], [17]. AHLs are small, hydrophobic molecules that can diffuse across bacterial cell membranes. After AHL ligands bind to their respective intracellular receptor proteins (which belong to the LuxR-like protein family, named after the A. fischeri receptor), receptor dimerization occurs. These dimers bind to the promoter regions of target genes, thereby regulating their expression (Fig. 2A).Fig. 2Quorum Sensing (QS) Mechanisms in Bacteria. The first QS system elucidated in Gram-positive bacteria was discovered in Staphylococcus aureus [18]. Gram-positive bacteria express small hydrophilic peptides as precursors to autoinducing peptides (AIPs) (Fig. 2B). Following post-translational modification and processing, these are converted to functional AIPs and exported by dedicated systems, such as adenosine triphosphate (ATP) binding cassette (ABC) transporters. Once exported, extracellular AIPs can bind to transmembrane receptor proteins, initiating a two-component signaling system. This involves autophosphorylation of the receptor kinase at a conserved histidine residue, followed by the transfer of a phosphoryl group to an aspartate residue of a corresponding response regulator. The phosphorylated response regulator binds to the promoter regions of target genes, activating or repressing gene expression [19], [20].

Bacteria exhibit a range of AI signaling molecules and QS circuit structures [21]. Some bacterial species use a distinct type of QS signaling molecule known as autoinducer-2 (AI-2). Unlike many QS signals that are species-specific, AI-2, which is synthesized via the LuxS pathway, is recognized as a universal signal involved in interspecies communication [22]. Another QS signaling molecule, autoinducer-3 (AI-3), has been identified in Escherichia coli as involved in interkingdom communication [23]. More details on signaling molecules are presented in [7], [24].

Quorum sensing (QS) networks exhibit diverse architectures across bacterial species (Fig. 3). For example, V. harveyi features a hybrid QS system that integrates two autoinducer molecules (Fig. 3A), each detected by a corresponding two-component sensor kinase protein, to regulate luminescence gene expression. Pseudomonas aeruginosa, a Gram-negative human pathogen, uses a hierarchically organized QS network composed of LuxR/LuxI-type regulatory pairs (Fig. 3B) to control its QS system, with these regulatory circuits working in tandem to modulate the expression of virulence factors [25]. Bacillus subtilis, a Gram-positive bacterium, employs an interconnected peptide-based QS system (Fig. 3C) to regulate processes such as sporulation and competence [5].Fig. 3Architectures of bacterial quorum sensing (QS) systems. Mathematical modeling provides valuable insights that complement empirical investigations of QS. Models have been used to unravel the molecular mechanisms of QS at the single-cell level, providing insight into how individual cells orchestrate population-wide behavior, and how QS systems operate in diverse contexts, particularly in spatially and ecologically complex environments. QS behavior is modulated by abiotic factors such as pH, nutrient availability, and temperature, as well as biotic factors including interspecies and intraspecies interactions [26], [27]. Moreover, mathematical tools have far-reaching implications for synthetic biology, guiding designs aimed at mitigating the harmful effects of QS, such as biofilm formation and virulence [28]. These models, while still evolving, lay a crucial theoretical foundation for strategies targeting QS-regulated behaviors, offering potential applications in combating bacterial infections and promoting beneficial microbial interactions [29].

Mathematical modeling of QS has undergone significant evolution, from simple descriptions to complex, multi-scale representations (Fig. 4). The first models (published in the early 2000s) focused on fundamental concepts such as feedback loops in single-cell systems, exemplified by the first QS model for A. fischeri [30] and the initial modeling efforts for Gram-positive QS systems [31]. Deterministic models further demonstrated QS bistability in structured environments [32]. Subsequent developments introduced stochastic and spatial dynamics to address environmental variability and noise [33], [34]. Multi-scale and environmental models then linked molecular mechanisms to population-level behaviors such as biofilm formation and virulence [35], [36], [37]. Hybrid models, along with models of multi-species and evolutionary QS dynamics have further advanced the understanding of microbial interactions within complex communities [38], [39], [40]. Recently, artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML) have been explored in QS modeling, with the potential to enhance predictive accuracy and inform future strategies for adaptive regulation of QS systems [41], [42]. Previous reviews have addressed the mathematical modeling of QS from various perspectives: the design principles and switch-like behaviors of QS gene networks under molecular noise [43]; the categorization of models based on QS-regulated process such as biofilm formation, virulence, and luminescence [44]; and the distinction between deterministic and stochastic modeling frameworks for exploring spatial dynamics, population behavior, and cost-benefit aspects [45]. In contrast, this review provides an integrated, multi-scale perspective that connects molecular-level mechanism to population and community-level QS dynamics. Beyond surveying classical modeling frameworks, we highlight emerging trends, such as the application of AI/ML in QS modeling technologies for real-time QS monitoring. By bridging foundational insights with current computational and biotechnological advancements, this review identifies current gaps and outlines future directions poised to shape the next generation of QS research.

Mathematical models

The mathematical modeling of quorum sensing (QS) systems has evolved over several decades, with foundational frameworks describing the dynamic of autoinducer synthesis, signal-receptor interactions, and feedback loops. The foundational differential equation formulations of QS models have been comprehensively reviewed [45]. These foundations provide a basis for extensions into stochastic, spatial, and multi-species dynamics. Here, we build upon these foundations by focusing on recent advances that expand the scope of QS modeling, including stochastic formulations, spatially structured systems, agent-based approaches, and evolutionary modeling to illustrate the range of mathematical frameworks used to reflect the complexity of bacterial signaling in real environments.

Single-cell models

Single-cell quorum sensing (QS) models provide insights into how individual bacteria sense and respond to changes in population density through autoinducer production and accumulation, receptor binding, and feedback regulation. Together, these models lay the foundation for understanding QS activation thresholds, bistability, and the impact of molecular noise on individual cells.

Deterministic (ordinary differential equation; ODE) models

As mentioned above, the first published mathematical model of QS, developed by James et al., described the biomolecular kinetics of luminescence in the lux regulatory system of A. fischeri [30]. This model ties the lux response to extracellular autoinducer (AI), but does not describe cellular population dynamics explicitly. Model analysis revealed that the system exhibits bistability. One stable state is luminescent, the other is non-luminescent; switching occurs at a threshold AI concentration. Goryachev et al. demonstrated that robustness of this switch-like behavior depends on multiple positive feedback loops and on transcription factor dimerization. Using a stochastic model, they showed that a minimal QS network without the LuxR-amplification loop fails to achieve robust switching under molecular noise [33].

Fagerlind et al. extended QS modeling to P. aeruginosa, which employs two interconnected acyl-homoserine lactone (AHL) circuits (Fig. 3B): the RhlI/RhlR and LasI/LasR systems, along with additional regulatory dynamics. Their ODE-based model demonstrated how these systems interact and highlighted the complexity of QS regulation in Gram-negative bacteria [46].

Gustafsson et al. presented the first mathematical model of QS in Gram-positive bacteria, describing the agr-QS system of S. aureus. Their ODE-based approach captured key molecular interactions, including those involving AgrC, AgrA, RNAIII and SarA, which regulate basal transcription of agr components. The model demonstrated bistability and hysteresis. Notably, they observed that increases in SarA concentration reduce the activation threshold, eventually eliminating hysteresis [31]. In later work, Müller et al. introduced spatial resolution into a modeling analysis of Pseudomonas putida. They combined ODEs for intercellular dynamics with partial differential equations (PDEs) to describe the diffusion of signaling molecules in the extracellular environment. This spatially structured model revealed that individual cells likely toggle between “on” and “off” QS states in response to local signal concentrations [47].

Liu et al. developed a computational model to explore how small regulatory RNAs (sRNAs) could enhance the precision and efficacy of QS responses in V. harveyi. Their work demonstrated how sRNAs could fine-tune the timing and sensitivity of gene activation, highlighting the importance of sRNA-mediated regulation [48]. In further analysis, they demonstrated how multiple feedback loops contribute to robust, switch-like regulation [49]. In complementary work, Hunter et al. modeled the dynamics of small RNA circuits and their interactions within the QS systems of V. harveyi and Vibrio cholerae, highlighting their homologous QS systems, the chemical similarity of their autoinducer molecules, and differences in their response to stimuli [50].

These deterministic models provide foundational insights into QS dynamics across bacterial species, capturing key dynamic features such as bistability, threshold responses, and signal integration. However, deterministic models have inherent limitations, particularly in representing low-density environments where random fluctuations in gene expression can significantly influence QS activation and fidelity [51]. To address these challenges, incorporating stochastic elements into QS models is essential.

Stochastic models

Stochastic models incorporate random fluctuations, providing a more realistic representation of QS dynamics. These models offer insights into system stability, activation thresholds, and adaptability in complex environments [52], [53], as exemplified by the robustness analysis of Goryachev et al. in the context of A. fischeri QS behavior [33].

Li et al. used a stochastic approach to model the LuxS/AI-2 QS system in E. coli K12, combining experimental data with a stochastic Petri net framework [54] to capture the probabilistic dynamics of AI-2 synthesis, uptake, and regulation. Their model revealed how random fluctuations influence AI-2 concentrations in extracellular environments. Notably, the study showed that the expression level of Pfs and LuxS—key enzymes in the AI-2 synthesis pathway—could not on their own explain the dramatic increase in AI-2 production in glucose-grown cells. Their findings, confirmed experimentally, suggested that glucose-dependent metabolic flux and alternative pathways contribute significantly to AI-2 synthesis [55].

Jangid & Chaudhury used a discrete-state stochastic framework to investigate how heterogeneity in QS cells influences gene activation. Their study focused on the transcriptional activator LuxR, which interacts with the autoinducer AHL to regulate QS-dependent gene expression. They revealed that variations in LuxR protein levels and AHL binding rates significantly contribute to intrinsic heterogeneity, even within genetically identical populations. This heterogeneity impacts the efficacy of LuxR-AHL complex regulation, as demonstrated through a theoretical framework and Monte Carlo simulations [56].

Population level models

Single-cell QS models address signaling and gene regulation, but their focus on average behaviors across populations limits their ability to capture emergent collective phenomena, such as biofilm formation, quorum-controlled bioluminescence, and virulence factor production [57]. In contrast, population-level models capture the interactions and environmental factors shaping coordinated actions across bacterial communities [58]. In this section, we survey deterministic and stochastic models, agent-based models (ABM), environmental and spatial models, and multi-species and evolutionary models, each focused on population-level dynamics.

Deterministic population models

Deterministic models, often based on ODEs and PDEs, provide a macroscopic view by averaging behavior across specific populations. Ward et al. developed a model to describe QS in A. fischeri populations. Their ODE model partitions cell populations into two classes: up-regulated and down-regulated, with the former producing autoinducer at a higher rate. Their results highlight the importance of rapid autoinducer accumulation in QS activation [59]. Dockery and Keener incorporated spatial dynamics into their QS model for Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Combining PDEs and ODEs, their model demonstrated how spatial heterogeneity and colony size could influence QS outcomes, revealing bistability and a hysteresis effect [32]. Subsequent studies modeled QS in P. aeruginosa in specific environmental contexts. For example, Koerber et al. demonstrated how signal accumulation drives bacterial invasion in a simulated wound environment [60]. Similarly, Viretta and Fussenegger developed a model to examine how P. aeruginosa populations coordinate virulence factor production [61].

Feedback mechanisms shape QS systems by modulating activation thresholds, stabilizing responses, and filtering noise. These mechanisms optimize bacterial behavior across diverse environmental contexts, ensuring efficient communication and adaptation within bacterial communities [62]. Haseltine et al. used an ODE model to examine how feedback mechanisms within the lux operon of A. fischeri shape QS behaviors. They demonstrated that single feedback loops typically produce threshold responses, while feedback on multiple regulatory elements or cooperatively bound regulators leads to bistability. By rewiring the Lux circuit, their experimental validation revealed graded, threshold, and bistable behaviors under steady-state conditions, with the native lux operon aligning most closely to a threshold response [63]. Similarly, Williams et al. demonstrated how dual-feedback structures, including LuxI-mediated autoinducer production and LuxR auto-activation, enhance QS sensitivity and robustness in A. fischeri [64].

Fekete et al. explored how molecular feedback mechanisms, particularly those involving enzymatic regulation, modulate signal concentration and reset QS systems, facilitating adaptive responses. Focusing on P. putida IsoF, a gram-negative bacterium with a LuxI/LuxR-like QS system, they developed a mathematical model to examine AHL production and degradation dynamics. Their model highlighted the role of lactonase, an enzyme that degrades autoinducer, in resetting signal levels for rapid adaptation to environmental changes [65]. Barbarossa et al. expanded Fekete’s model by introducing a time delay in lactonase activation. Their revised model highlighted how feedback timing enables bistable behavior, allowing bacterial populations to maintain QS activation despite fluctuating conditions [66]. Rai et al. developed a model for the Gram-negative LuxI/LuxR QS system to explore how feedback structures shape density-dependent responses. By treating the system as a simplified "black box," their study demonstrated how different feedback configurations, including positive feedback loops, yield monostable, bistable, and oscillatory responses under various biochemical conditions. This predictive framework highlights the influence of feedback topologies on QS dynamics [67]. Fan and Bressloff extended the investigation of feedback by examining how regulatory mechanisms modulate QS sensitivity. They proposed a “weight control” mechanism, where feedback loops selectively modulate pathway sensitivity to environmental signals, enabling bistability and adaptive switching between states. Their combined single-cell and population model underscores the role of feedback in enhancing QS sensitivity and adaptability [68]. Expanding on these insights, Fondi et al. investigated feedback mechanisms in Burkholderia, highlighting how additional regulatory modules could enhance QS optimization and adaptability. Their model suggests that strains with layered feedback structures exhibit faster QS responses and greater regulatory flexibility, providing evolutionary advantages [69].

Stochastic population models

Stochastic models explore how noise and signal diffusion can influence cell-to-cell communication. Zhou, Chen & Aihara developed a stochastic model to investigate the role of noise in QS. Using a synthetic gene network based on the lux operon of A. fischeri, their model assumes a homogenous environment where identical cells experience shared extracellular noise. They demonstrated that intracellular noise disrupts cooperative behaviors (consistent with the finding of Goryachev et al. [33]), while shared extracellular noise synchronizes cells through a stochastic synchronized oscillation, driving collective behavior [70]. Expanding this framework, Hong et al. modeled QS in heterogeneous environments with dissimilar cells and varying extracellular noise. Their findings underscore the critical role of extracellular noise in stabilizing synchronization across populations, highlighting its potential real-world relevance in clinical contexts and under environmental stresses like elevated temperature [71]. Weber and Buceta examined how stochastic fluctuations influence QS synchronization in A. fischeri. Using both deterministic and stochastic models, they demonstrated that variability in LuxR expression generates a bimodal distribution of QS activation, enabling asynchronous responses among cells. This response provides an adaptive advantage in fluctuating environments by balancing flexibility and robustness [72]. Tissera et al. further explored synchronization dynamics through a stochastic analytical model that links bacterial density to autoinducer production. By incorporating a logistic bacterial growth framework, they demonstrated how fluctuations in signal processing fine-tune population-wide QS responses, emphasizing the adaptive role of randomness in collective bacterial behavior [73].

Alfinito et al. investigated the cooperative roles of multiple autoinducers in V. harveyi using a stochastic random resistor network model grounded in classical percolation and conduction theory [74]. In this framework, QS interactions were simulated via a Coulomb-type field, representing long-range communication among bacteria mediated by ‘c-charges’—abstract variables encoding gene regulatory potential and AI-mediated signaling. This model treats bacterial communication as analogous to an electrostatic system, where each bacterium contributes to a field influencing neighboring cells, enabling the system to replicate bioluminescence behavior observed in AI-deficient mutants. Their findings, validated experimentally, demonstrated distinct roles for autoinducers, revealing a trade-off between bioluminescence and colony growth, where reduced luminescence in mutants correlated with faster growth. These findings provide new insights into how environmental conditions or genetic modifications influence bacterial communication and energy trade-offs, expanding the understanding of QS-regulated behaviors [75].

Agent based models

Agent-based models (ABM), also known as individual-based models, provide a powerful approach for studying QS by simulating individual bacterial cells as agents interacting with their spatial environment and with one another. This method offers valuable insights into how spatial organization shapes bacterial communication and QS regulation [76], [77]. Tang et al. developed an ABM to explore bacterial foraging behaviors and QS in spatially structured environments. Their model simulates bacterial cells equipped with receptors to assess environmental cues and make foraging decisions, with QS represented as density-dependent clusters that activate based on local density thresholds. A key feature of the model structure is incorporation of the metabolic cost of autoinducer production, highlighting how QS dynamics shift with population density. Notably, QS-enabled populations exhibit fewer cells but achieve greater total biomass than non-QS populations, while consuming fewer nutrients. This suggests an adaptive advantage of QS in resource-limited environments, optimizing growth and resource allocation in bacterial communities [78].

Following Tang et al.'s exploration of QS in resource optimization, Melke et al. investigated how spatial clustering and environmental confinement influence QS dynamics in A. fischeri. Using an ABM, they investigated how population-level behaviors emerge from molecular mechanisms, focusing on two positive feedback loops involving LuxI and LuxR production. The model confirmed that confined spaces, such as small cavities, can enhance QS activation by increasing local autoinducer concentrations, promoting synchronized population switching. Additionally, they simulated competition between autoinducer-producing cells and non-producing cheaters, showing that while cheaters initially gain an advantage, their proliferation eventually reduces autoinducer levels, causing producers to switch off. This dynamic prevents cheater cells from dominating, maintaining population stability and cooperation [79].

Müller and Uecker developed a model combining PDEs for signaling molecule diffusion and ODEs for intercellular dynamics, emphasizing how spatial configurations influence QS activation [35]. This work complements Tang et al.'s model, which explored QS-driven resource optimization in dynamic environments, and Melke et al.’s work, which highlighted the role of clustering in biofilm stability. Quan et al. offered a contrasting perspective by investigating the LuxS-regulated (Lsr) QS system, which exhibits bimodal activity within populations. Using deterministic and agent-based models, they demonstrated how negative feedback in the Lsr system generates subpopulations of activated and deactivated cells, fostering heterogeneity rather than synchronized activation [80]. Experiments revealed that the Las QS system in P. aeruginosa exhibits a graded population-level response to density, resultign from bimodal single-cell behavior [81]. This distinction underscores the importance of network topology, feedback structure, and environmental context in shaping QS responses, and broadens our understanding of how bacterial populations balance synchronization with diversity.

Environmental and spatial models

Incorporation of spatial structure into QS models helps resolve inaccuracies in descriptions of bacterial communication in heterogenous environments. Traditional models often assume well-mixed conditions, failing to capture the spatial variability present in natural settings. Spatially structured models address the distribution of bacterial cells in a given space, considering factors like cell density, proximity, and gradients of signaling molecules [82]. Darch et al. [83] provided experimental evidence for the importance of spatial organization in QS by using a cystic fibrosis lung infection model that mimics the natural distribution of P. aeruginosa aggregates. Their findings showed that QS activation depends on both aggregate size and spatial proximity, with signal activation occurring only when aggregates exceed a critical cell number threshold and are within a defined diffusion range. This study highlights the necessity of incorporating spatial constraints into QS modeling to better reflect natural environments. Moreno-Gámez et al. demonstrated how environmental factors, such as pH and antibiotic stress, influence QS by integrating cell density and environmental cues, highlighting the adaptive capacity of QS systems [84]. Similarly, Pérez et al. showed that water availability on leaf surfaces affects QS activation in Pseudomonas syringae, with smaller colonies activating more rapidly under dry conditions [85]. These experimentally validated findings underscore the importance of spatial features in accurate modeling of QS, offering insights into bacterial communication in complex environments.

Redfield introduced the concept of “diffusion sensing” (DS), focusing on how a single cell senses its environment through the diffusion patterns of signaling molecules, independent of population size. This concept emphasizes that QS behavior may reflect environmental constraints, such as molecule accumulation in confined spaces, rather than purely social interactions [86]. Subsequently, Hense et al. proposed “efficacy sensing” (ES), integrating aspects of QS and DS to describe how bacteria may optimize signaling by considering both population density and environmental diffusion factors [34]. Mathematical models have since advanced the understanding of QS, DS, and ES by simulating how spatial constraints, diffusion rates, and cell arrangement shape signaling dynamics.

Matur et al. explored the aforementioned dynamics by integrating reaction and diffusion aspects to their model, demonstrating how spatial heterogeneity and chemical gradients affect QS activation. Their work highlights how spatial factors, such as signal stability and environmental perturbations, influence bacterial communication, and provides computational tools for analyzing QS in complex environments [87]. Lang et al. explored how spatial dynamics and diffusion of signals enable synchronized bacterial behavior within extended colonies, modeling traveling wave solutions in the LuxR-LuxI QS system of A. fischeri. Their findings demonstrated how spatial heterogeneity, AHL diffusion, and decay rates influence QS activation and propagation [88]. Yusufaly et al. further investigated QS dynamics through a reaction-diffusion model, highlighting the transition from local quorum sensing, driven by dense localized populations, to global quorum sensing, where inter-colony interactions predominate. Their study revealed trade-offs between activation speed and spatial communication range, illustrating how spatial organization optimizes QS signaling in complex environments [89]. Marenda et al. expanded on these insights by showing that spatial configuration, particularly the height of agar tubes used in their experimental setup, impacts QS activation, often overweighting producer density. Their work highlighted species-specific biotic degradation of signaling molecules and the dominant role of spatial boundaries and gradients in shaping QS efficacy [90]. Uecke et al. examined how geometric confinement and flow conditions amplify signal accumulation, revealing the impact of confined environments, such as microfluidic devices and narrow tubes (at scales comparable to bacterial colonies) on QS dynamics [37]. Similarly, Jung and Meile explored the effects of advective transport on QS, confirming that higher flow rates dilute signaling molecules, requiring higher microbial densities or sensitivity for effective QS induction. These studies highlight the intricate balance between microbial signaling and environmental factors, providing a realistic depiction of QS in dynamic ecological systems [91].

The importance of spatial structure in QS is further highlighted in biofilm studies [92]. Gilbert et al. developed a computational model using a hybrid Petri net approach to explore how spatial factors influence QS in E. coli biofilm formation. By integrating gene transcription, signal transduction, and transport processes, they showed that bacterial colony density and spatial distribution significantly enhanced QS efficacy, particularly in denser regions. Their results emphasize the role of AI-2 diffusion in coordinating biofilm development, highlighting the importance of spatial organization in QS dynamics within structured microbial ecosystems [39]. Taghizadeh et al. developed a PDE model to simulate the growth and degradation of biofilms, incorporating QS mechanisms and environmental factors. Their model highlights bacterial cooperation as a QS-regulated response triggered when the local biofilm mass exceeds a quorum threshold, leading to increased antibiotic resistance [93]. Singh et al. developed a MATLAB-based GUI to simulate QS dynamics in biofilm formation, focusing on diffusion dynamics and biofilm thickness as spatial regulators of QS activation. Through ODE-based simulations, their study highlights the influence of diffusion coefficients, production rates, and cell densities on QS activation. Notably, it predicts the minimum biofilm thickness required for QS activation, demonstrating how spatial factors regulate bacterial coordination and communication in structured environments [94]. Expanding these spatial insights, Ben Ali et al. employed a P system-based framework, a computational algorithm inspired by biological cell structures, to simulate QS dynamics in A. fischeri. This innovative approach integrates a multi-compartmental stochastic Gillespie algorithm to model the parallelism of QS signaling across membranes. The framework emphasizes the importance of independent parallel evolution of individual bacteria, achieving scalability and mirroring real-world QS interactions within structured microbial environments [95].

Yan et al. developed a deterministic model, structured as a system of semi-linear reaction-diffusion equations, to capture the spatiotemporal dynamics of bacterial communication in P. putida. This approach incorporates variations in biomass density and signal molecule concentrations over time, revealing the spatial and temporal patterns that govern QS activation. Such models underscore how environmental heterogeneity and spatial constraints shape bacterial signaling, offering computational tools for exploring these dynamics in microbial ecology [96].

Multi-species and evolutionary models

QS extends beyond single-species dynamics, functioning within microbial ecosystems where interspecies interaction—cooperation, competition, and interference—shape community structure and function [97], [98]. Mathematical models can be used to unravel these complexities, exploring how species optimize signaling to adapt and thrive in shared environments through strategies such as signal interference, cross-talk, and competition, positioning QS as a key mediator of ecological balance [29], [99]. Mathematical models provide insights into the adaptive plasticity of QS systems under environmental pressures, such as antibiotic stress or resource scarcity, and how cooperative behaviors evolve despite the presence of cheaters: community members who benefit from public goods without contributing to their production [29], [100].

In multi-species environments, QS systems exhibit resilience and adaptability, maintaining stability despite signal interference and cross-talk. Silva et al. developed a reaction-diffusion model simulating QS dynamics in mixed microbial communities, demonstrating robustness even under overlapping signals. Their experimental validation confirmed the adaptive resilience of QS, maintaining stability in the presence of interspecies interactions [38]. Prajapat and Saini explored antagonistic interactions and intracellular regulation, highlighting how QS balances global and local mechanisms to ensure robustness and adaptability in diverse conditions [101].

QS systems evolve dynamically in response to environmental pressures such as resource scarcity, competition, and antibiotic stress. Mathematical models can provide insights into how QS balances cooperation and competition to optimize survival and resource use. Torres-Crena et al. modeled AI-2 dynamics in E. coli, examining intra-species competition between wild-type and LuxS knock-out strains, highlighting the competition for shared signaling molecules. In complementary work, van Gestel et al. used an ABM to explore the evolutionary significance of QS in B. subtilis, identifying key ecological conditions that drive QS evolution, such as variability in nutrient levels and colony-size-dependent resource consumption [40], [102]. Pai and You introduced a "sensing potential" metric, showing how QS-regulated behaviors, such as exoenzyme secretion, are fine-tuned to ecological contexts, emphasizing the evolutionary advantages of positive feedback in signaling [103]. Cornforth et al. demonstrated that dual-signal QS systems enable bacteria to outperform single-signal strategies by resolving ambiguities in density and mass transfer, enhancing adaptability in complex environments [104]. Studies by Rumbaugh et al. and Mellbye and Schuster revealed how QS systems counteract cheaters and optimize public goods production, illustrating the flexibility and resilience of QS systems in diverse microbial communities [105], [106].

QS systems mediate cooperation in bacterial communities by regulating public goods, such as extracellular enzymes, biofilm matrices, and siderophores. However, these shared resources generate an evolutionary tension between cooperative producers and exploitative non-producing cheaters. Moffett et al. demonstrated through a birth-death model that QS stabilizes cooperation when public good production is costly but can become detrimental at lower production costs [107]. Feng et al. emphasized the co-regulation of public goods and private goods in P. aeruginosa as a key strategy to constrain cheaters, ensuring QS balances individual and community benefits [108]. In multi-genotype systems, Aguilar et al. revealed that metabolic trade-offs and compatibility between producer and non-producers play an important role in stabilizing public good dynamics [109]. Czárán et al. further illustrated how cue-based quorum sensing—where the signal is an inseparable byproduct of the cooperative act itself, such as nisin production in lactic acid bacteria—can reduce the success of cheaters and promote stable cooperation in spatially structured and variable environments [110].

QS also provides an excellent framework for applying game theory to understand bacterial decision-making strategies over evolutionary timescales. Game-theoretic models explore how bacteria adapt cooperation, cheating, and interference to maximize fitness in resource-limited environments [111]. Noel et al. modeled noisy real-time QS signaling, showing how imperfect signal propagation influences resource-sharing behaviors and bacterial communication in fluctuating environments [112]. They demonstrated how local population uncertainty promotes bacterial cooperation by altering perceived payoffs, balancing cooperative and selfish behaviors to optimize individual and collective outcomes [113]. Game theoretic models also reveal how QS systems regulate the trade-off between signal production and metabolic efficiency to prevent overinvestment while maintaining cooperation. Hense et al. framed QS as a prisoner's dilemma, showing that different cost structures influence cooperation strategies, highlighting how QS optimizes signal production to maintain collective fitness while minimizing individual metabolic burdens [114]. Allen et al. demonstrated that bacteria adopt a "cooperate when surrounded by cooperators" strategy to reduce exploitation risks and ensure population stability [115]. Zhao et al. incorporated conditional defectors, who alternate between cooperation and defection based on resource availability, demonstrating how this flexibility prevents cheater dominance and stabilizes bacterial communities under diverse environmental pressure [116]. These studies underscore the utility of game theory in elucidating the evolutionary strategies that sustain QS-regulated cooperation and balance individual and collective benefits.

Comparison of modeling approaches

Mathematical models have been instrumental in unraveling the complex dynamics of QS, offering frameworks to explore bacterial communication across diverse ecological and evolutionary contexts. Deterministic ODE models are computationally efficient and well-suited for predicting population-level QS-regulated behaviors under well-mixed and controlled conditions, such as bioluminescence in A. fischeri or biofilm formation in P. aeruginosa [30], [32]. However, ODE-based models generally do not capture cell-to-cell variability, stochastic fluctuations, or spatial heterogeneity, which are key drivers of QS behavior in natural environment [34]. Stochastic models address these limitations by simulating noise and variability in gene expression and signaling molecule dynamics, providing insights into how QS systems function under fluctuating conditions, particularly in low-density or spatially structured populations [56], [117]. PDE-based models complement these approaches by capturing spatial gradients of signaling molecules and their diffusion across structured environments, enabling the study of local quorum effects and emergent spatial patterns [37], [89], [94]. Agent-based models (ABMs) simulate individual bacterial behaviors and interactions, providing insights into how spatial organization and local clustering influence QS-mediated cooperation and competition [78], [79]. Evolutionary and game-theoretic models expand the scope of QS research, revealing how bacteria balance cooperation, competition, and resource sharing under selective pressure. These models explore adaptive strategies that stabilize public good production and counteract cheater dynamics, often addressing how cross-talk and interference shape microbial community structure [109], [110], [115], [116]. Moreover, they abstract biological complexity by focusing on generalized traits and complement mechanistic approaches like ABM or stochastic frameworks, offering insights into spatial organization and ecological balances. Game-theoretic frameworks, for example, have shown how QS systems regulate metabolic trade-offs to maintain cooperation while minimizing individual costs, offering insights into resilience of microbial ecosystems under fluctuating conditions [115], [116]. Together, these approaches emphasize the importance of integrating deterministic, stochastic, and spatial frameworks to capture the multifaceted nature of QS dynamics. Hybrid models that combine complementary methodologies enable more comprehensive representation of the complexity of QS-regulated behaviors across diverse biological contexts.

Experimental validation of QS mathematical models

Mathematical models of QS have been supported by experimental studies that test and confirm their predictions across different scales. At the single cell level, bistability and hysteresis in the agr system of S.aureus were validated experimentally by comparing activation thresholds in wild-type, knockout, and overexpression strains, showing that cell-density dependence shifted in line with model prediction [31]; regulator dynamics were likewise confirmed in P. aeruginosa following model prediction [46]. Synthetic biology approaches further strengthened these links: the rewiring of the lux operon in A. fischeri experimentally demonstrated how feedback architecture shapes QS response, producing graded, threshold, or bistable behaviors in line with the model predictions [63], and the robustness of LuxI/LuxR regulation was shown to increase when dual-feedback loops were present [64]. Enzymatic regulation was also validated, as lactonase-mediated AHL degradation in P. putida was observed to match modeling predictions [65], with delayed feedback dynamics confirmed by experimental signal peaks [66].

Stochastic modeling predictions have similarly been validated. Cooperative switching stabilized by extracellular noise was demonstrated in synthetic lux networks [70], and persistence of synchronization under heterogeneous population conditions was demonstrated experimentally [71]. More recently, Alfinito et al. [75] validated their resistor network model of V. harveyi by reproducing experimental luminescence patterns of wild type and autoinducer-deficient mutants, supporting predictions about trade-offs between communication and growth.

At the population and community level, further validation has been provided. Moreno-Gámez et al. [84] validated their model of S. pneumoniae competence by experimentally demonstrating that competence initiation integrates cues from density, pH, and antibiotic stress in line with theoretical predictions. Similarly, Pérez-Velázquez et al. [85] validated their model of P. syringae by demonstrating that it reproduces biologically realistic responses to water availability on leaf surfaces, with drier conditions accelerating QS activation in small colonies, as predicted. A reaction-diffusion model of LuxI/LuxR system was experimentally parametrized with measured values, and it was shown that under realistic conditions a traveling wave of QS activation is plausible in spatially extended colonies [88]. Similarly, the predicted transition between local and global quorum sensing regimes was shown to be robust across biologically realistic conditions [89]. Spatial boundary effects were also validated experimentally in agar tube systems, where QS activation depended on colony extension and biotic degradation rather than density alone [90]. At the community level, Silva et al. [38] validated their reaction–diffusion model using mixed-species co-cultures, showing that QS signaling remains robust under interspecies crosstalk.

Together, these studies underscore the interplay between modeling and experimentation, highlighting how theoretical frameworks both guide and are refined by empirical validation across scales, from single molecules to complex microbial communities. At the same time, validation is limited by uncertain parameter measurement, the complexity of regulatory networks, and findings that often depend on specific experimental conditions.

Applications of QS mathematical models

Therapeutic applications

Mathematical models of QS can aid the development of therapeutic strategies to target QS-regulated processes like virulence and biofilm formation. For instance, Anguige et al. used mathematical models to explore how disruption of QS, e.g. by using AHL-degrading enzymes or anti-LasR drugs to interfere with LasR binding in P. aeruginosa, can attenuate virulence effectively [118]. Jiang et al. highlighted the potential of anti-QS agents, such as AHL lactonases, to enhance antibiotic sensitivity and reduce biofilm formation [119]. Such models can also expose host-pathogen dynamics. For example, Zhang identified therapeutic avenues for modulating immune responses through QS signal molecules [120]. Quorum quenching (QQ) is a key application of QS modeling, targeting bacterial communication pathways to mitigate biofilm-associated infections. Fozard et al. employed an ABM approach to study QQ strategies for disrupting QS in biofilm communities. Their 3D biofilm model highlighted the importance of spatial structure, revealing that quorum-sensing inhibitors (QSI) are most effective when applied before QS activation, enhancing treatment efficacy [36]. The reaction-diffusion model developed by Ghasemi et al. focuses on biochemical aspects of QQ, particularly the enzymatic degradation of signaling molecules to disrupt biofilm formation, highlighting the potential of quorum quenchers, such as signal-degrading enzymes, to enhance clinical interventions, such as antibiotic treatment [121].

Biofilm-associated infections are highly resistant to treatment, calling for targeted strategies guided by QS models. Wang et al. demonstrated that enzymatic treatments, such as glycoside hydrolases and deoxyribonucleases (DNases), enhance antibiotic penetration by disrupting biofilm structures [122]. Gulec and Eckford introduced a stochastic QS model that predicts critical thresholds for effective biofilm disruption using QS mimickers, validated through experiments with P. aeruginosa biofilms [123]. Additionally, Blouza and El Alaoui employed a reaction-diffusion QS model to evaluate the efficacy of QS inhibitors and antibiotics, revealing how QS dynamics shape biofilm resilience and treatment efficacy [124].

Industrial and environmental applications

Biofilms are essential for refining wastewater treatment and for a range of industrial bioprocessing applications. Baber et al. applied reaction-diffusion models to analyze biofilm models under the influence of QS dynamics, offering insights into the growth and cooperation of bacteria within biofilm in various industrial application such as wastewater treatment and antifouling technologies [125]. Similarly, Yang et al. investigated structured biofilm models, integrating multi-species interactions and transport resistances, advancing wastewater treatment system design. While not explicitly addressing QS, their study’s focus on biofilm structure and functionality indirectly informs QS-related dynamics in similar systems. [126]. These studies underscore the versatility of QS models in industrial and environmental systems, providing actionable strategies to enhance biofilm utility in wastewater treatment and bioprocessing while mitigating the risks in contamination-sensitive settings.

Synthetic biology applications

Quorum sensing models are being used to advance synthetic biology by enabling precise engineering of microbial systems for therapeutic, industrial, and environmental applications [127], [128], [129]. For instance, Nikolaev and Sontag developed a QS-regulated framework to stabilize synthetic gene circuits, offering innovations for precision applications, such as drug delivery [130]. QS models also optimize microbial consortia for efficient resource utilization and bioproduction, as demonstrated by Clavijo-Buriticá et al., who integrated dynamic flux balance analysis with QS networks for bioproduction processes [131], and Stephens & Bently, who used QS circuits for coordinating interspecies interactions in industrial applications [129]. Tuna et al. further advanced these efforts by introducing hybrid QS-machine learning systems for real-time gene expression control in precision medicine and biomanufacturing [42].

QS models facilitate advancements in synthetic circuit design. Barbarossa et al. modeled QS dynamics in P. putida using delay differential equations to analyze feedback loop stability, providing foundational insights for designing robust synthetic genetic circuits in microbial consortia [132]. Wu et al. explored QS-based genetic circuits, including toggle switches, and logic gates, to enable adaptive microbial responses and enhance bioprocessing efficiency [133].

Computational tools further extend QS applications. ABAQS (Agent-Based Architecture for Quorum Sensing Simulation) offers a modular platform for modeling bacterial populations, with applications in cancer research and environmental biotechnology [134]. Wu et al. further investigated QS crosstalk, revealing how interactions between QS systems facilitate diverse regulatory states and support precision biomanufacturing and therapeutic applications [135].

Challenges and future direction in QS mathematical modeling

Despite ongoing advances, the mathematical modeling of QS faces challenges that limit applicability and accuracy; these stem from computational constraints, experimental limitations, and the intrinsic complexity of QS systems. Addressing these barriers requires integrating innovative solutions and emerging technologies to advance QS modeling.

QS models often rely on static assumptions that treat factors such as nutrient availability, pH, and oxygen levels as constant, oversimplifying the complexity of real microbial ecosystems [136]. This limitation is particularly pronounced in spatial contexts, where nutrient gradients and localized QS activation are critical [82], [83]. The inherent stochasticity of QS systems, and the complexity of multi-species ecosystems shaped by microbial cooperation, competition, and community structure, pose significant challenges to developing models capable of effectively capturing microbial dynamics [97], [123], [137]. Data scarcity further complicates model parameterization and validation, as QS molecules are often present at trace levels (nM to pM), challenging detection and quantification with traditional methods like HPLC and qualitative reporter strains [29]. Advanced techniques such as UPLC-MS/MS and HPLC-TQ MS have been suggested to improve sensitivity and accuracy [138], [139].

Future research in QS modeling must address critical challenges by leveraging emerging technologies and interdisciplinary innovations. Hybrid modeling approaches, combining deterministic, stochastic, and spatial elements, are increasingly recognized for their potential to balance biological complexity with computational efficiency [140]. For instance, Kuttler et al. developed a hybrid stochastic fractional approach, integrating fractional PDEs with Monte Carlo simulations to model QS, providing realistic simulation of bacterial dynamics, while Gilbert et al. used colored hybrid Petri nets to model E.coli biofilm formation via Autoinducer-2 (AI-2), incorporating spatial distribution, validated through temporal logic properties [39], [141]. While these models successfully integrated stochasticity and continuous dynamics, challenges remain in refining these approaches to address real-world environmental complexities, multi-species interactions, and dynamic adaptations.

Artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML) can further enhance QS modeling, offering dynamic updates to simulations, improved predictive accuracy, and integration with microbial omics datasets [142], [143], [144]. For example, Wu et al. developed a deep neural network architecture to capture complex QS dynamics [41], and the review by Tuan et al. explores how advanced ML algorithms, such as reinforcement learning and deep learning, improve QS modeling by enabling real-time data processing, predictive modeling, and dynamic feedback control [42]. Leveraging ML algorithms to analyze large omics datasets has shown great promise in enhancing the estimation of model parameters. For instance, ML approaches have been used to estimate intercellular reaction kinetics and diffusion rates by integrating transcriptomics and metabolomics data into hybrid models, enhancing model accuracy and predictive power [145], [146]. Similarly, network-based approaches have utilized multi-omics datasets to refine parameters like molecular transport rates and regulatory dynamics in microbial systems [147], [148]. For example, Zhu et al. integrated stress-response multi-omics data with ML and network analysis to predict bacterial survival and adaptation under varying stress conditions. Can et al. employed probabilistic graphs to analyze regulatory interactions in microbial communities, uncovering dynamic responses across transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic layers [149], [150]. Future efforts should focus on standardizing omics datasets to enhance ML model reliability, integrating ML with frameworks like game theory to uncover novel insights into QS adaptation, and developing interpretable ML models to bridge computational and experimental biology for practical applications.

Emerging technologies like the Internet of Things (IoT)—a network of interconnected sensors and devices—are increasingly used for real-time biological monitoring. In the context of QS, IoT-linked biosensors can provide high-resolution, continuous data on microbial activity, gene expression, and biofilm formation, which is a key challenge in modeling QS systems. For example, localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) based biosensors offer real-time, label-free detection of biomolecular interactions and dynamic biological processes [151]; Wang et al. have demonstrated the utility of these sensors for analyzing dynamic changes in biofilm composition [152]. Integrating these biosensors with Internet of Things (IoT) technologies and cloud-based platforms offers adaptive monitoring capability. Somathinlaka et al. proposed a conceptual framework—an Internet of Biofilm Living Artificial Intelligence Devices—for responsive biofilm management, and Koca and Akan explored how bacterial communication mechanisms, such as QS, can be integrated within the broader Internet of Everything (IoE) framework to enhance adaptive monitoring and interconnected systems [144], [153]. Despite these advances, the application of LSPR and IoT technologies in the mathematical modeling of QS systems remains underexplored. Future efforts could focus on integrating LSPR-based monitoring with ML- driven QS models, enabling dynamic updates to model parameters based on real-time feedback. This could improve simulations of QS-regulated processes like biofilm growth and interspecies communication. Standardized protocols for integrating biosensor data with mechanistic models could enhance QS modeling’s ecological and industrial relevance.

Addressing computational constraints is equally crucial, as hybrid modeling frameworks demand substantial resources, limiting their scalability for large-scale, multi-species ecosystems. Advances in AI/ML, such as resource efficient AI models and interpretable frameworks, promise reduced computational costs while maintaining accuracy [154], [155]. Cloud-based platforms and modular system designs offer scalable solutions for simulating QS dynamics, but their integration with existing frameworks is still in its early stages [42]. Combining ML with cloud computing will streamline large-scale QS modeling, enabling dynamic, real-time simulations. These advancements will pave the way for scalable, accurate, and ecologically relevant applications in synthetic biology, industrial biotechnology, and microbial ecology.

Conclusion

The study of QS has been profoundly advanced by mathematical modeling, which has illuminated microbial communication and coordination at molecular and population levels. These models have unraveled key aspects of QS dynamics, such as autoinducer synthesis, feedback mechanisms, gene expression, and cross-species interactions, providing a theoretical framework that complements and extends experimental findings. However, significant challenges remain. Data scarcity, computational limitations, and the need for robust experimental validation highlight the importance of interdisciplinary approaches to refine QS models and expand their applicability. Integration of machine learning and high-throughput data hold great promise for improving model precision and predictive power. Furthermore, the development of hybrid and multi-scale models can bridge molecular and ecological scales, capturing the dynamic and stochastic nature of QS within complex microbial ecosystems. Looking ahead, QS modeling stands poised to advance fields such as synthetic biology, antimicrobial therapy, and environmental management. By enabling the manipulation of bacterial behavior, these models offer innovative strategies to combat infections, enhance biotechnological applications, and optimize industrial processes.
