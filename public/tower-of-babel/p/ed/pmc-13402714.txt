Coupling thin film nanofiltration membrane and photocatalyst for the removal of antidepressant drugs from aqueous solutions
Authors: Leila Roshanfekr Rad, Mansoor Anbia, Mohammad Irani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetal–organic frameworks (MOFs) have a high capability for the structure of membranes and photocatalysts to remove organic pollutants from water. In this study, a nanofiltration/photocatalysis coupled system was used to remove three antidepressant drugs—fluoxetine hydrochloride (FLX), sertraline hydrochloride (STL), and paroxetine hydrochloride (PRX)—from water. The thin film nanocomposite (TFN) nanofiltration membranes were loaded with varying amounts of NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) to filter the selected antidepressants under initial concentration of 10 mgL−1. The concentrated pharmaceuticals from the rejected stream of the nanofiltration system were then fed into the photocatalysis process. The NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibrous photocatalyst, in the presence of peroxymonosulfate (PMS) and solar light irradiation, was used to remove the antidepressants. After 48 h, the permeation fluxes and rejection percentages for FLX, PRX, and STL were 37.1 Lm−2.h−1 & 98.52%, 35.1 Lm−2.h−1 & 98.12%, and 37.8 Lm−2.h−1 & 98.74%, respectively. The final concentrations of FLX, PRX, and STL in the rejected stream after 48 h of membrane filtration experiment were 37.02, 35.06, and 37.80 mgL−1, respectively. The photocatalytic degradation efficiency of FLX, STL, and PRX using 0.5 gL−1 NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibrous photocatalyst, in the presence of 2 mM PMS and solar light irradiation, was 99.68%, 99.35%, and 99.21% during 30 min at a pH of 9. These results demonstrate that the coupling of membrane separation and photocatalysis processes, using MOFs-based TFN nanofiltration membranes/photocatalyst, is an effective strategy for treating pharmaceutical wastewater.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53612-2.Keywords: NH2-MIL-101 (Fe), Nanofiltration membrane, NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 photocatalyst, Membrane separation-photocatalysis, Coupling systemSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

In recent years, mental illness has been recognized as a major global health problem due to economic issues, unfavorable social conditions, and unhealthy lifestyles1. Depression and anxiety are two of the most prevalent mental disorders, affecting approximately 7–8% of the world’s population2. As a result, there has been an increase in the use of antidepressant drugs among individuals with mental health issues3. However, one of the main concerns with these drugs is their poor metabolism. For instance, after oral administration, less than 10% of fluoxetine is metabolized, leading to significant amounts of the drug entering wastewater treatment plants without being broken down4. The conventional wastewater treatment facilities are ineffective in the removal of antidepressants from water, where these drugs have been detected ranging from ngL−1–μgL−1 in urban wastewater treatment plants, surface water5. In another order of magnitude, pharmaceutical pollutants are present at concentrations to the order of mg L−1. Large volumes of highly concentrated recalcitrant antidepressants (ppm) being discharged from pharmaceutical effluents to the surface water drains and environment. Therefore, it is crucial to develop efficient methods for removing antidepressants from pharmaceutical effluent to reduce concern about possible risks to their discharge into the aquatic environment.

The use of nanomaterials in physicochemical wastewater treatment methods, such as adsorption6–8, ion exchange9, membrane filtration10–12, photocatalysis13–15, photofenton16, and various AOPs (advanced oxidation processes)17–19, has proven to be effective in removing antidepressant compounds from wastewater. Combining these methods has shown to significantly improve the removal efficiency of pharmaceuticals from water compared to using a single method20–23. Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), as active organic–inorganic hybrid nanomaterials, have demonstrated high capability in removing various contaminants from wastewater through adsorption24,25, photocatalysis26, and membrane filtration27,28. This is due to their uniform distribution of active sites, high surface-to-volume ratio, high porosity, and topological structure.

Membrane filtration is an excellent option for removing organic pollutants due to its eco-friendly nature, easy scale-up, facile maintenance, and high selectivity. Among the membrane processes, only reverse osmosis and nanofiltration are effective in removing pharmaceutical pollutants from wastewater. Ultrafiltration (UF) and microfiltration (MF) membranes are not suitable for treating pharmaceutical wastewater as they allow the pollutants to pass through their pores easily29. In recent years, researchers have focused on developing nanofiltration membranes over reverse osmosis membranes due to their lower energy consumption, higher energy efficiency, and higher permeation flux30. The most commonly used membranes for pharmaceutical wastewater treatment during the nanofiltration process are thin film composite membranes (TFCMs). Additionally, various nanomaterials such as zeolite31, graphene oxide32,33, carbon nanotubes34, covalent-organic frameworks35,36, and MOFs37–40 have been incorporated into the top layer of TFCMs to improve filtration performance. MOFs have shown excellent performance in the structure of thin film nanocomposite (TFN) membranes for removing organic pollutants from wastewater41. Loading MOFs in nanocomposite thin film membranes can improve the permeability, selectivity, and mechanical stability of nanofiltration membranes42. Recently, water-stable MOFs such as MIL, UiO, and ZIF have been loaded into the top layer of TFCMs to improve the wettability and hydrophilicity of the membrane’s surface37–42. Yu et al.40 reviewed the use of UiO-66-based TFNMs for efficiently separating heavy metals, dyes, and pharmaceutical wastewater. Tong et al.43 fabricated an ethylene vinyl alcohol/MIL-101(Fe) membrane for separating dyes and salts. Teng et al.44 examined the removal of tetracycline using a polyacrylonitrile/carbon-MIL-101(Fe) nanofibrous membrane. The hydrophilic MIL-101(Fe)-NH2, with negative surface charge, can improve the hydrophilicity, permeation flux, and selectivity of TFC membranes for separating positive molecules. The aim of this study was to eliminate fluoxetine hydrochloride (FLX), sertraline hydrochloride (STL), and paroxetine hydrochloride (PRX) from water using MIL-101(Fe)-NH2-based TFNMs.

The use of nanofiltration separation with MOF-based TFNMs has the potential to produce high-quality permeated water. However, it is important to note that this process must be coupled with further treatment in order to effectively treat the concentrated solution of retained pollutants in the secondary system45–49. One effective method for removing pharmaceutical contaminants from the concentrated solution is through the use of photocatalytic AOPs50. In a study by Martínez et al.50, the removal of six drugs was investigated using a coupled system of membrane separation (NF-90 nanofiltration membrane and BW-30 reverse osmosis membrane) and heterogeneous photocatalytic degradation (Fe2O3/SBA-15 photo-Fenton and TiO2 photocatalyst under UV irradiation). The focus of this study was on the performance of the nanofiltration/photocatalysis coupled system for removing antidepressants from water.

MOFs have been shown to be effective heterogeneous porous semiconductor photocatalysts for decomposing various organic compounds from water51,52. However, their lower charge separation efficiency compared to other heterogeneous photocatalysts limits their application in the photocatalytic process53. To address this issue, researchers have combined MOFs with metal oxides such as titanium dioxide and zinc oxide, which can provide optimal conditions for maximum oxidation/reduction of contaminants during photocatalysis. This is achieved through increased photogenerated charge transfer, easier electron–hole pair separation, and improved photodegradation efficiency54–60. For example, Hou et al.57 investigated the photocatalytic degradation of organic pollutants using a MOF-based C/TiO2/ZnO nanofiber. Similarly, Liu et al.60 utilized a MIL-101 (Fe)/B-TiO2 composite to degrade methylene blue from water under simulated sunlight irradiation and hydrogen peroxide. In this work, the performance of NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibers was investigated for the photodegradation of FLX (C17H19ClF3NO, Mw: 345.79 g/mol), STL (C17H18Cl3N, Mw: 342.688 g/mol), and PRX (C19H21ClFNO3, Mw: 365.829 g/mol) from concentrated solution in a nanofiltration membrane system. Additionally, the MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 NFs photocatalyst was used to activate peroxymonosulfate (PMS, HSO5-), resulting in the fabrication of sulfate radicals (SO4•−) to improve the degradation rate of antidepressants.

In previous studies, the MOF-based heterostructures have been applied as photocatalysis, and high-performance separation membrane for the removal of contaminants from water in the alone system. On the other hand, the high performance of the nanofiltration/photocatalysis coupled system using conventional photocatalysts/membranes has been proven. However, there is no study about the MOF-based photocatalysts/membranes in the binary system. Herein, a nanofiltration separation/heterogeneous photocatalytic degradation coupled system was used to eliminate antidepressant drugs from water. The performance of TFC and MOF-based TFN nanofiltration membranes was examined for separating the antidepressants. Additionally, the efficiency of NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 heterogeneous photocatalyst, in the presence of simulated sunlight and PMS, was investigated for the treating a concentrated solution of the retentate stream from the nanofiltration process.

Experimental

Materials

Materials utilized in this study are summarized in supplementary information.

Synthesis of NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibrous photocatalyst

NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) MOFs have been prepared via the solvothermal technique, as described previously61. To prepare the TiO2 nanofibers, 2 g of titanium (IV) isopropoxide and 0.6 g of PVP were mixed in an ethanol/acetic acid solvent (1:7 v:v) and agitated for 6 h. The blend was then refluxed for 12 h. The resulting sol solution was electrospun at a feeding rate of 0.5 mL/h, a potential of 20 kV, and a distance of 12 cm. The nanofibers were subsequently heated at 500 °C for 2 h62. To synthesize the NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) MOF/TiO2 nanofibrous photocatalyst, the synthesized TiO2 nanofibers were dispersed in solution A (NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) solution) and agitated for 4 h. Subsequently, the blend underwent sonication for a duration of 30 min, followed by a heating procedure at 110 °C over a 12-h period. The residue obtained after this step was then subjected to washing, and subsequently heated again at 60 °C for a supplementary 12 h.

Fabrication of NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) MOF-based nanofiltration membrane

The preparation of the PSf support layer employed the phase inversion technique. This process began by dissolving PSf particles in DMF to achieve an 18 wt.% concentration, followed by pouring this mixture onto a polyester non-woven base. Subsequently, this base layer was submerged in water maintained at 25 °C. The creation of the thin film composite membrane (TFCM) utilized interfacial polymerization. This process began by soaking the polysulfone (PSf) substrate in an aqueous blend featuring 1.0 wt.% piperazine (PIP), 3 wt.% citric acid (CSA), and 3.0 wt.% triethylamine (TEA) for a duration of 10 min. Following this, the surplus PIP solution was blotted away, and any residual liquid spheres on the substrate’s upper face were eliminated by passing a rubber roller across it until no further aqueous beads were visible. Subsequently, the support membrane surface was subjected to an n-hexane solution holding 0.06 wt.% trimesoyl chloride (TMC) monomer for 2 min. The resulting membranes were rinsed with hexane and then subjected to drying at 70 °C for 10 min. For the fabrication of nanofiltration membranes incorporating NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) MOF, differing quantities of NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) MOF were incorporated into the aqueous phase. Prior to their introduction into the solution, the MOF slurries underwent ultrasonication for 10 min, employing a Bandelin UW 3200 device from Germany.

Characterization tests and apparatus

All characterization tests of MOF-based TFN membranes and photocatalysts are listed in the supplementary information.

Nanofiltration/photocatalysis coupling system

The membrane separation and photocatalytic degradation coupling system has been utilized to effectively remove FLX, STL, and PRX antidepressants from aqueous solutions. Initially, the aqueous solutions containing 10 mgL−1 of each of the three antidepressants were introduced into the membrane filtration system. This system consists of two streams: the permeation flux (first stream) and the rejected flux, which contains high concentrations of FLX, STL, and PRX (second stream). The second stream was then directed to the photocatalysis unit, where it was treated using NH2-MIL-101(Fe) MOF/TiO2 nanofibrous photocatalyst under simulated sunlight irradiation.

Membrane experiments

The TFC membrane and MOF-based TFN membranes were utilized in a nanofiltration membrane system. The system operated with a cross-flow design and had a surface area of 19.6 cm2, with a pressure of 5 bar. The aim of this arrangement was to evaluate the efficacy of the TFC and TFN membranes regarding permeation flow (J) and removal percentage (R%) of antidepressants. The initial concentration of antidepressants in the 5 L water-based liquid was 10 mg L−1. Photocatalysis experiments

The retentate stream from the nanofiltration system was treated with a photocatalysis process using NH2-MIL-101(Fe) MOF/TiO2 nanofibrous photocatalyst under simulated sunlight irradiation. The experiment was conducted both with and without the addition of peroxymonosulfate (PMS) at a concentration of 2 mM. The photodegradation of FLX, STL, and PRX from the rejected solution of the nanofiltration system (1 L) was evaluated using a 300 W Xenon lamp with a λ ≥ 420 nm, placed 10 cm above the solution. Before the photocatalysis, the solutions were kept in a dark station for 20 min. The photocatalyst dosage was fixed at 0.5 gL−1. The schematic of photocatalysis process is illustrated in Scheme 1.

Characterization of photocatalysts

Figure 1b depicts the PVP/TiO2 nanofibers, noted for their consistent fibrous geometry, with diameters spanning from 150 to 250 nm. Following thermal treatment at 500 °C, the PVP/TiO2 nanofibers were changed to the thinner TiO2 nanofibers, possessing an average diameter of 135 nm (Fig. 1c). The NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) MOF /TiO2 nanofibrous composite (Fig. 1d) retains the structural appearance of the MOF particles largely unaltered, a result of their deposition upon the underlying TiO2 nanofibers.

The presence of these peaks in the XRD pattern of NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 indicates that both MOF and TiO2 nanofibers maintained their crystalline structure in the composite. Characterization of MOF-based TFN membranes

The surface morphologies of TFC and TFN membranes with varying concentrations of NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) are shown in Fig. 3. The pristine polyamide TFC membrane (Fig. 3a) had a rough surface with nodules. Embedding NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) during interfacial polymerization resulted in further cross-linkage of the membrane, connecting some of the nodules. With 0.002 wt.% MOF, the membrane surface had a “ridge and valley” structure lower than the pristine polyamide TFC membrane (Fig. 3b). As the MOF content increased to 0.004 wt.%, the ridges were partially connected and the valleys disappeared (Fig. 3c). At 0.006 wt.%, the membrane surface showed bumps in the chain structure (Fig. 3d)., and at 0.008 wt.%, the surface was smoother (Fig. 3e). This phenomenon stems from both a faster rate of crosslinking reactions and a smaller degree of surface unevenness, both resulting from the hydrogen-bonding interactions established between the amine functionalities present on NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) and the overlying polyamide stratum65,66. By observing the cross-sections of the TFC membranes featuring TFC and MOF loading, it was evident that the TFN membrane had a looser surface compared to the TFC membrane (Fig. 3a′–e′). The membrane thickness did not significantly change for 0, 0.002, and 0.004 wt.% MOF-loaded TFN membranes, but increased from 240 ± 30 nm for the TFC membrane to 1 μm ± 200 nm for 0.006 and 0.008 wt.% MOF-loaded TFN membranes. This is due to the reduction of crosslinking degree and the formation of a looser structure in the TFN membrane compared to the TFC membrane.

In the Fe 2p spectrum, the detected peaks at 711.6, and 724.6 eV assigned to Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2 demonstrated the successful grafting of the NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) to the TFN membrane.

Membrane experiments

The effect of NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) content on the permeation flux and removal efficiencies of FLX, STL, and PRX after 2 h is shown in Fig. 6. The pristine TFC membrane had a permeation flux of 19.9 L m−2 h−1 and removal percentages of 88.50%, 87.91%, and 88.21% for FLX, STL, and PRX, respectively. When 0.002%, 0.004%, 0.006%, and 0.008% NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) was loaded onto TFN membranes, the permeation flux increased to 43.3, 42.1, 43.8, and 51.3 L m−2 h−1 for FLX, STL, and PRX, respectively. This increase in permeability can be ascribed to the enhanced hydrophilicity of the NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) loaded-TFN membranes. The permeation flux was significantly increased when 0.006 and 0.008% NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) was loaded onto TFN membranes, due to the looser surface of the active layer, providing more pathways for water transport. There was no significant difference in permeation flux between FLX and STL, as they have similar molecular weights. However, the permeation flux of PRX was lower due to its higher molecular weight and steric hindrance effect, resulting in a denser fouling layer with higher hydraulic resistance. The removal percentages of FLX, STL, and PRX using 0.002, 0.004, 0.006, and 0.008% NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)-loaded TFN membranes were 92.32%, 93.12%, 92.85%, and 98.92%, 98.65%, 99.02%, and 67.25%, 65.21%, 65.89%, and 62.31%, 60.32%, 59.21%, respectively. These significant removal rates might be credited to the electrostatic interplay among the positive charges of the medicinal compounds and the negative charge of the surface of TFC and TFN membranes. Yet, at greater concentrations of MOF (0.006% and 0.008%), the more fragile active layer caused a substantial drop in removal efficiencies. The effect of the dually charged NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) and polyamide active layer on the removal efficiencies of FLX, STL, and PRX was higher when 0.002 and 0.004 wt.% were loaded, compared to the looser active layer of TFN membranes. Therefore, the 0.004% NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)-loaded TFN membrane was selected for further analysis of the retentate stream of the pharmaceutical compounds.

Permeation flux and removal efficiencies of (a) FLX, (b) PRX, and (c) STL using NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)-loaded TFN membranes.

The antifouling properties of prepared TFC and TFN membranes is illustrated in Fig. 6d. As shown, the decrease in the permeation flux is observed during BSA filtration for all membranes. For the bare TFC membrane, a high decrease in the permeation flux was achieved after 240 min (~ 35%). However, for the TFN membranes, the flux loss rate was lower than 15% after 240 min compared with the initial permeation flux, which demonstrated the high antifouling property of fabricated TFN membranes. The surface roughness of the TFN membrane containing 0.008% MOF was lower than that of other MOFs-loaded TFN membranes and TFC membrane, which resulted in the best antifouling property for this membrane (J/J0 ~ 95% after 240 min). The J/J0 for TFN membranes containing 0.002%, 0.004%, and 0.006% were 89.2%, 92.3%, and 93.7%, respectively. The lower surface roughness of MOF-based TFN membrane led to the lower accumulation of pollutants in the valleys” of the membrane surface and resulted in the better fouling resistance of MOFs-loaded TFN membranes compared to bare TFC membrane7.

Figure 7a shows the permeation fluxes of FLX, PRX, and STL solutions using a 0.004 NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)-loaded TFN membrane. As shown, the flux decline rates were 10.72%, 13.69%, and 8.51%, respectively, after 24 h compared to the initial permeation flux after 2 h. The higher molecular weight of PRX compared to FLX and STL, and their deposition on the TFN membrane surface, may have resulted in the production of a denser fouling layer and a greater reduction in the permeation flux of the PRX solution over time. Additionally, the blockage of pores by larger molecules may have increased the membrane resistance to water molecule transport, leading to a decrease in the TFN membrane’s performance over time. The gradual difference in FLX and STL permeation fluxes may be attributed to the greater attachment of FLX to the membrane surface or the formation of a cake layer, as well as the stronger electrostatic attraction between FLX molecules and the membrane surface compared to STL. This resulted in a greater drop in the flux of the FLX solution. After 48 h, the flux reduction rates for FLX, PRX, and STL solutions were 27.82%, 30.55%, and 27.03%, respectively. There was no notable variation in the permeation rates of FLX and STL solutions after 48 h. This suggests that the formation of an equal cake layer on the TFN membrane surface may have resulted in a similar decrease in flux for FLX and STL over time, as their molecular weights (FLX: 345.79 g/mol, STL: 342.688 g/mol) are almost the same. The concentrations of FLX, PRX, and STL solutions in the rejected flux of selected pharmaceutical compounds using a 0.004% NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)-loaded TFN membrane are illustrated in Fig. 7b. As expected, the concentrations of FLX, PRX, and STL solutions increased over time due to the decrease in water volume in the circulation system of the membrane filtration test. The increase in concentrations was equal up to 24 h, after which a gradual difference between the concentrations of FLX, PRX, and STL was observed. The formation of a denser fouling layer for the PRX solution decreased the amount of permeated water, resulting in a lower decrease in feed volume and the production of a lower concentrated solution of PRX compared to FLX and STL. The final concentrations of FLX, PRX, and STL in the rejected stream after 48 h of the membrane filtration experiment were 37.02 mgL−1, 35.06 mgL−1, and 37.80 mgL−1, respectively. The rejection percentages of FLX, PRX, and STL in the permeation system after 48 h were 98.52%, 98.12%, and 98.74%, respectively, indicating the higher performance of the fabricated 0.004% NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)-loaded TFN membrane.

Photocatalysis experiments

Figure 8a illustrates the impact of TiO2 nanofibers, NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) MOF, and NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 composite photocatalysts on the photodegradation of FLX, STL, and PRX. The initial concentrations of FLX, PRX, and STL were 37.02 mgL−1, 35.06 mgL−1, and 37.80 mgL−1, respectively, with a catalyst dosage of 0.5 gL−1, a solution pH of 5.5–6, a contact duration of 60 min, and simulated sunlight irradiation. The removal efficiency of FLX, STL, and PRX using TiO2 nanofibers, NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) MOF, and NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibers was 45.8%, 47.1%, and 44.3%, 55.6%, 58.9%, and 52.4%, and 86.4%, 87.9%, and 80.5%, respectively. It was observed that TiO2 nanofibers had lower activity under solar light irradiation compared to NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) MOF, which was confirmed by UV-DRS analysis. This can be attributed to the lower activity of TiO2 nanofibers in the visible region. The photoinduced electrons during the reduction of Fe3+/Fe2+ in NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) provided oxidative holes for the photodegradation of FLX, STL, and PRX from water. By blending NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) MOF and TiO2 nanofibers, the reaction between Ti4+ and Fe2+ produced Fe3+ and Ti3+. The reaction between Fe3+-O2 and Ti3+-hydroxyl ions generated superoxide and hydroxyl radicals for the photodegradation of FLX, STL, and PRX from water. Therefore, the photocatalytic activity of MOF was increased after loading TiO2 nanofibers into the MOF. This trend has also been reported by other researchers54,56,57.

(a) Effect of photocatalyst type, (b) NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) to TiO2 ratio in the NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 photocatalyst, (c) pH, and (d) reusability of NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 photocatalyst on the photodegradation of FLX, STL, and PRX from water.

The impact of the ratio of NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) and TiO2 (V/V) on the removal efficiency of FLX, STL, and PRX was also examined (Fig. 8b). The removal efficiency of FLX, STL, and PRX using NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibrous photocatalysts was in the order of NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibers (2:1 V/V) (93.1%, 93.9%, and 90.8%) > NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibers (1:1 V/V) (86.4%, 87.9%, and 80.5%) > NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibers (1:2 V/V) (80.2%, 81.4%, and 78.8%). Based on these results, NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 (2:1 V/V) nanofibrous photocatalyst was selected as the optimized photocatalyst for further experiments.

Figure 8c shows the impact of pH on the photodegradation of FLX, STL, and PRX using NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 photocatalyst. The removal efficiency of these drugs increased with increasing pH, reaching a maximum at pH 9. The highest removal efficiencies achieved were 96.2%, 97.0%, and 93.8% for FLX, STL, and PRX, respectively, using a 2:1 V/V ratio of NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibers at a pH of 9. The pKa values for FLX, STL, and PRX are 9.8, 9.77, and 9.56, respectively. The pHPZC of the NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibrous photocatalyst is approximately 5.8, resulting in a negative surface charge at pH levels above 5.8. However, the surfaces of FLX, STL, and PRX are positively charged at pH values lower than 9, allowing for rapid adsorption onto the photocatalyst surface through electrostatic interaction. This occurs within the pH range of 5.8 to 9.5. The increase in pH also leads to the generation of more hydroxyl radicals, which accelerates the photodegradation of FLX, STL, and PRX under solar light irradiation. Conversely, at acidic pH levels, the presence of protons in the solution and electrostatic repulsion between the drugs and photocatalyst decrease the production rate of hydroxyl radicals, resulting in slower degradation. At pH levels above 9, the formation of more hydroxyl ions may occupy the active sites of the photocatalyst, leading to a decrease in the degradation rate of FLX, STL, and PRX using NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibrous photocatalyst67. The pH of real wastewater samples is lower than 9, and this might require.

stabilization with a bufer solution.

The recyclability of the NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibrous photocatalyst was evaluated by regenerating it with ethanol for 10 runs (Fig. 8d). The results show that the removal efficiency of FLX, STL, and PRX after 10 runs was still higher than 95% of the initial removal efficiency, demonstrating the successful regeneration of the photocatalyst for practical applications.

The NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibrous photocatalyst was synthesized and used for peroxymonosulfate (PMS) activation under simulated sunlight irradiation as a visible light source. The generated ●SO4- and ●OH accelerated the degradation rates of FLX, STL, and PRX when using the synthesized photocatalyst. The impact of PMS concentration (ranging from 0 to 3 mM) on the removal efficiency of FLX, STL, and PRX was investigated, with a catalyst dosage of 0.5 gL−1 and a pH of 9 (Fig. 9a, b). The optimal PMS concentration for removing FLX, STL, and PRX was found to be 2 mM. Further addition of PMS decreased the degradation rate of contaminants due to scavenging of the produced ●SO4- and ●OH. The maximum removal efficiency of FLX, STL, and PRX was achieved at 99.68%, 99.35%, and 99.21%, respectively, using a photocatalyst dosage of 0.5 gL−1 and 2 mM PMS after only 30 min. The equilibrium time for removing 96.2% FLX, 97.0% STL, and 93.8% PRX was 60 min. The kinetic data of FLX, STL, and PRX photodegradation using synthesized photocatalyst in the presence of PMS and without PMS were successfully followed the pseudo-first order kinetic model. The reaction constant (kPSF) for the removal of FLX, STL, and PRX was estimated to be 3.35 × 10−3, 3.48 × 10−3, and 3.19 × 10−3 min−1, respectively. These values in the presence of 2 mM PMS were found to be 9.82 × 10−2, 9.12 × 10−2, and 1.08 × 10−3 min−1, respectively. The maximum k value reported by Zhou for the removal of ceftriaxone using CdSe QDs/MoS2 nanocomposite was 9.18 × 10−3 min−168.

(a) Effect of PMS concentration, (b) reaction time, and (c) photocatalyst dosage on the removal efficiency of FLX, STL, and PRX using the NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibrous photocatalyst in the presence of PMS.

The optimal catalyst dosage was found to be 0.5 gL−1 in the presence of 2 mM PMS (Fig. 9c). Higher photocatalyst dosages may result in decreased degradation efficiency due to the occupation of active sites by excess ●SO4- and ●OH radicals.

In the NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibrous photocatalyst/PMS/sunlight irradiation system, the simultaneous contribution of NH2-MIL-101 (Fe), TiO2 nanofibers, and PMS can produce more ●SO4- and ●OH radicals. The proposed oxidation mechanism for the degradation of FLX, STL, and PRX is presented in Fig. 10. The pharmaceutical contaminants and PMS are first adsorbed on the photocatalyst surface. When subjected to simulated sunlight, the energy delivered excites the photocatalyst material beyond its inherent band gap, thereby generating electron–hole pairs. These pairs react with O2/H2 and OH- to produce active species such as •OH, •O2−, and •HO2. Additionally, the adsorption of PMS on the photocatalyst surface and excitation of conduction band electrons can lead to the production of •SO4− species. These species can then oxidize OH− and H2O to active •OH radicals. Finally, the synthesized photocatalyst acts as a PMS activator to produce •OH, •O2−, and •SO4− species. The attack of these species on the selected pharmaceutical contaminants during the photocatalysis process results in chain-breaking reactions and oxidation to smaller organic molecules such as oxalic acid, carboxylic acid, formic acid, and inorganic ions such as F- and NO3-. These compounds are ultimately mineralized to water and carbon dioxide. Once the effluent has been first pre-concentrated via the TFN membrane, then, has been treated in the photocatalysis process. The mechanism of degradation of FLX/PRX/STL using MOF-based photocatalyst can be observed. When filtering, FLX, PRX, and STL pollutants can contaminate the membrane pores and its surface. On the other hand, the presence of MOF on the membrane’s surface can fully contact the pollutants and increased the removal efficiency on the basis of filtration process. Then, in the photocatalytic reaction, the presence of NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) MOF, promotes the electron transfer which enhancing the electron–hole separation ability of TiO2 nanofibrous photocatalyst. Based on the mentioned filtration and photocatalytic reaction mechanisms, NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) MOF have a high capability in better filtering and higher photocatalytic performance.

For the TFN membrane system-photocatalysis combined method, total operation costs include (I) electricity (energy consumed for membrane system), materials (chemical reagents for the preparation of TFN membranes), constant capital cost related to membrane system and (II) production cost of photocatalyst, energy consumption, labor and maintenance and the constant capital cost (pump, UV/LED lamp for the photocatalysis process. The optimization of the characteristics of TFN membrane and energy efficiency would show the ideal conditions to optimize the removal of pharmaceutical matter. However, the production cost of photocatalysis is a critical issue for the decrement of total cost of the effluent treatment using TFN membrane-photocatalysis process. The large-scale production of NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 photocatalyst could decrease the energy consumption and final cost of synthesized photocatalysts. However, it is critically important to predict the possible defects in MOF structures in large-scale production.

Conclusion

In this study, a TFN membrane and a heterogeneous photocatalyst using NH2-MIL-101 (Fe) MOF were successfully synthesized to remove FLX, STL, and PRX in a coupled nanofiltration/photocatalysis system. The average roughness, surface zeta potential, and contact angle of the optimized 0.004 wt.% MOFs-loaded TFN membrane were 6.96 nm, − 34.56 mV, and 60.8°, respectively. The permeation flux and removal percentages of FLX, STL, and PRX using the 0.004 wt.% NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)-loaded TFN membrane were 51.3 Lm−2 h−1 and 98.92%, 50.4 Lm−2 h−1 and 98.65%, and 51.8 Lm−2 h−1 and 99.02%, respectively. After 48 h of membrane filtration, the final concentrations of FLX, PRX, and STL in the rejected stream were 37.02, 35.06, and 37.80 mgL−1, respectively. The maximum photodegradation efficiencies of FLX, STL, and PRX with initial concentrations in the concentrated stream of the nanofiltration system were 96.2%, 97.0%, and 93.8%, respectively, using a 2:1 V/V ratio of NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibers at a pH of 9. When 2 mM PMS was added, the photocatalytic degradation efficiency of FLX, STL, and PRX reached 99.68%, 99.35%, and 99.21%, respectively, using 0.5 gL−1 of NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibrous photocatalyst after only 30 min. These results demonstrate the successful treatment of FLX, PRX, and STL in a coupled nanofiltration-photocatalysis system using NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)-loaded TFN membrane and NH2-MIL-101 (Fe)/TiO2 nanofibrous photocatalyst in the presence of PMS and solar light irradiation.
