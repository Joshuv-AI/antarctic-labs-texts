Congenital Pouch Colon in Males: Exploring the Risk of Epididymo-orchitis
Authors: Ashvin Damdoo, Mamta Sengar, Sauradeep Dey, Chhabi Ranu Gupta, Niyaz Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:Patients with anorectal malformations (ARMs) are known to have a higher association with epididymo-orchitis (EO). Congenital pouch colon (CPC), a rare subtype of ARM, presents with several predisposing factors that are presumed to be linked with EO. This study aims to investigate the occurrence of EO in this specific subgroup of ARM patients to enhance the understanding of its etiology.Materials and Methods:An ambispective observational study was conducted by analyzing medical records of male patients with CPC. Out of 28 patients managed primarily at our center, 25 fulfilled the inclusion criteria. The mean age at follow-up was 3.36 ± 1.33 years. Type IV CPC was the most common, seen in 12 (45.8%) patients. Hydroureteronephrosis was detected in 13 patients at initial presentation via abdominal ultrasonography, and 7 (28%) patients had a history of urinary tract infection (UTI). Cystoscopic evaluation revealed an ascended verumontanum into the bladder neck with the colovesical fistula near the verumontanum in nine patients. Only one patient, who had recurrent UTIs since infancy and voiding difficulties, experienced two episodes of EO. This patient also had multiple associated genitourinary and spinal anomalies. None of the remaining patients developed EO.Conclusion:Despite the anatomical abnormalities associated with CPC, only 1 out of the 25 patients developed EO. Early division of the pouch vesical fistula and minimal dissection around the prostatic urethra may significantly reduce the risk of EO in this population. However, further research involving larger patient cohorts is necessary to better understand the underlying mechanisms and validate these observations.

INTRODUCTION

Epididymo-orchitis (EO) is a known concern for patients with anorectal malformations (ARMs), especially in those with rectourethral fistulas. EO incidence among ARM patients has been reported to range between 1.2% and 6.1%.[1] This rate is approximately ten times higher than in the general pediatric population.[2] EO is the most common cause of acute scrotum in patients with ARM.[34] The recurrence of EO in ARM patients can lead to severe complications, including testicular infarction, testicular loss, and even necessitating vasectomy, as reported in various studies.[25]

The etiology behind the increased prevalence of EO in ARM patients appears to be multifactorial, with contributing factors including associated genitourinary anomalies, such as vasal anomalies and persistent mesonephric duct syndrome, as well as a predisposition to urinary tract infections (UTIs). Recurrent UTIs in ARM patients may result from contamination of the urinary tract with colonic contents, facilitated by fistulous connections that often accompany these malformations.[14] Additional findings by VanderBrink et al. suggest a high incidence of neurogenic bladder and renal injuries among ARM patients who develop EO, pointing toward a structural as well as functional predisposition in these patients to urinary tract complications.[6]

Congenital pouch colon (CPC) is a rare and distinctive form of ARM that is primarily observed in northern India. CPC is characterized by the pouch-like dilation of a shortened segment of the colon and is classified into four subtypes based on the length of the normal colon proximal to the pouch.[7] This condition has certain physical traits, such as the verumontanum often moving up into the bladder neck in male patients, as observed by Chadha and Khan.[8] Cysto-urethroscopic evaluations have revealed that, in these patients, the terminal colovesical fistula (CVF) frequently originates immediately proximal to the verumontanum.[8] These findings were similar to findings reported in few of the cases with recurrent EO in patients with ARM without pouch colon.[26]

Based on these observation, we expect that ARM with CPC, characterized by broader fistulas and with significantly higher incidence of ascending veru opening in close proximity to fistula, would exhibit a significantly greater predisposition for EO.

Although there is an increasing amount of research on EO in male ARM patients, information regarding the incidence and risk of EO in cases of CPC-associated ARM is still scarce. This study is to examine the correlation and prevalence of EO in male patients with CPC, hence enhancing the overall comprehension of the etiology of EO in individuals with ARM.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This ambispective observational study was conducted at a tertiary care centre from June 2023 to June 2024, following approval from the institutional ethics committee. All consenting boys with CPC who were primarily managed in our department between 2015 and 2023 were included in the study. The medical records of these patients were reviewed, documenting the type of CPC according to Rao’s classification, operative findings, including the site of the fistulous opening, and other surgical details. As per the departmental protocol, screening abdominal ultrasound was conducted at the initial presentation to check for any associated renal anomaly. Voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) was performed only in patients who had a history of urinary symptoms, UTI, or the presence of hydroureteronephrosis on ultrasonogram. Cystoscopy findings were noted for the patients wherever available.

After patients were enrolled, follow-up visits to the outpatient department included recording a detailed history of any cases of EO, findings from clinical exams, and results from lab and imaging tests in a prepared form. Any case of sudden pain or swelling in the scrotum, with or without fever or urinary issues, that improved with medical treatment was classified as EO. All patients underwent a scrotal ultrasound (My Lab ×75; Esaote) using a 3.5-MHz linear array transducer to check the size and texture of the testes. The testicular size was measured in all three dimensions, namely length (L), width (W), and height (H). Testicular volume was calculated using the formula for ellipsoid structure, L × W × H × 0.52.[9]

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis was done for the clinical features, and radiological features and results was presented as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and number (percentage) for categorical variables.

RESULTS

Case records of 28 patients with male ARMs with CPC could be retrieved. Of these, three patients could not be traced for follow-up, leaving 25 patients available for the study. Mean age of patients at follow up was 3.36 ± 1.33 years. Almost half (n = 12, 45.8%) of the patients presented with Type IV CPC, while two exhibited Type I CPC. Demographic, clinical, and radiological profile of these patients are summarized in Table 1. All but two patients underwent division of the associated terminal CVF during the primary surgical procedure. Hydroureteronephrosis was identified in 13 patients via abdominal ultrasonography at initial work up, with 12 cases being bilateral and one unilateral. Micturating cysto-urethrogram (MCU) was performed on seven patients experiencing UTI, revealing vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) in four patients; three bilateral, while one exhibited unilateral grade 5 VUR alongside a mid-penile congenital urethral fistula in a patient with sacral agenesis [Figure 1]. The patient with grade 5 unilateral VUR and sacral agenesis also displayed an hourglass-shaped bladder on MCU, likely due to communication with a large urachal cyst. MCU of rest of the three patients were unremarkable.

Among 7 patients with UTIs, 4 experienced repeated episodes throughout infancy. One of these patients experienced two episodes of epididymorchitis. This patient presented with a congenital urethral fistula, an hourglass-shaped bladder, sacral agenesis, and grade 5 VUR in the sole functioning kidney. His UTI and extravesical obstruction resolved following tube cystostomy. Another patient with recurring UTIs was diagnosed with a large vesical calculus during infancy; the patient’s condition improved following suprapubic cystolithotomy. UTIs in the other two patients resolved with chemoprophylaxis, leading to the cure during infancy. No incidences of EO were documented among these patients.

The physical examination of the scrotum revealed normal-sized testes in all subjects, and scrotal ultrasonography corroborated that testicular dimensions were adequate for each patient’s age. Ultrasound assessments revealed normal bladder morphology and total resolution of hydroureteronephrosis in 12 out of 13 patients beyond infancy.

DISCUSSION

EO occurs with markedly greater frequency in individuals with ARMs than in the general population. Published research estimates the incidence of rectourethral fistulas to range from 1.2% to 6.1%, however some studies indicate that up to 23% of individuals may be affected in the long term.[125] A significant number of affected patients endure recurring episodes of EO, frequently necessitating vasectomy to control the infections.[5] Identifying the causal causes is crucial for the implementation of appropriate preventive and remedial interventions, and this is the focus of publishing this study despite its negative findings in all but one patient. The sole patient with EO had numerous other variables predisposing to bouts of UTI and EO.

None of the published studies have identified a single etiological factor responsible for the increased incidence of EO in patients with ARM. Hypothetical links with UTI, VUR, lower urinary tract dysfunctions, congenital urethral anomalies, and spinal dysraphism have been explored.[1256] Vascular anomalies have also been considered as potential contributors.[126] Morandi et al. found the verumontanum ascended into the bladder neck in 2/29 patients with ARM.[2] VanderBrink et al. also reported a similar anomaly in one of their patients with recurrent EO. In their study, they found a statistically significant correlation between high ARMs (specifically recto-bladder neck fistula and recto-prostatic urethral fistula) and recurrent episodes of EO, with a P = 0.02.[6] These findings indicate that differences in the structure of the urinary tract might be important in causing problems linked to ARMs. Further investigation into these relationships could enhance our understanding of the underlying mechanisms and improve clinical management for affected patients.

The occurrence of EO reported before corrective surgery in different studies is between 17% and 34%, and the rectourethral fistula with fecal or mucus contamination of the urinary tract was thought to be the cause of this. Diverting stomas were therefore advocated by Pena et al. in all patients undergoing staged repair of ARM. Raveenthiran and Sam, in their study on EO in patients with ARM, concluded that corrective surgery and division of fistula cured EO only in 36% of individuals. They reported a statistically significant presence of additional risk factors like vasal anomalies and neurovesical dysfunction in patients who continued to have recurrent EO after corrective surgery.[1] Zaccara et al. found that nearly 1 in 4 cases with a rectourethral fistula developed EO during long-term follow-up, while none of the patients with bladder neck fistula or without urinary fistula developed EO during their study period.[10] The occurrence of late cases of EO in rectourethral fistula (after completion of all surgical stages) raises the probability of some form of injury to the ejaculatory ducts during urethral-rectal pouch separation.[410]

This study investigates the prevalence of EO in CPC, a subtype of ARM. This type of ARM features an expanded entero-urinary connection, typically positioned opposite the verumontanum, which is elevated into the bladder neck. The veru and the pouch vesical fistula are in close proximity to one another.[6] These individuals typically exhibit pronounced meconuria at presentation. In our cohort of patients, we validated the presence of meconuria, significant bladder contamination with meconium, and HUN in all cases. Cystoscopy verified the analogous observation of an elevated veru and fistula orifice situated directly above the bladder neck, in close proximity to the veru in 9 out of 13 individuals. Epididymorchitis was observed in a single patient who, in addition to type 4 CPC, presented with sacral agenesis, left renal agenesis, grade 5 unilateral VUR, atypical bladder anatomy, congenital urethral fistula, and recurrent UTIs.

Based on the review of literature a likely explanation for low incidence of epididymorchitis in this series could be that the fistulous communication (CVF) is usually divided during the primary surgery, which minimizes the ongoing fecal contamination of the urinary system. Additionally, the definitive surgery for patients with CPC typically involves a pull-through procedure, which reduces the risk of ejaculatory duct injuries compared to the posterior sagittal ano-rectoplasty procedure used for rectourethral communication. Published literature too reported EO prevalence less in patients with bladder neck fistula where the dissection during definitive surgery is usually away from the urethra and ejaculatory ducts.

The patients with ARMs are also known to have associated genito urinary anomalies and spinal anomalies which promote vasal reflux of urine either by anatomical structural alteration or by affecting function of lower urinary tract leading to raised urethral pressure and subsequent vasal reflux of urine.[5] In the series by VanderBrink,[6] ARM patients diagnosed with EO exhibited a high incidence of neurogenic bladder and renal injury. The incidence of spinal anomalies in patients with ARM (without CPC) is approximately 35%, lumbosacral vertebrae being the most commonly affected. The presence of VUR and neurovesical dysfunction, whether of neurogenic or nonneurogenic origin may play a contributory role in the development of EO in this patient population. Chadha and Khan, in their comprehensive review, noted lesser incidence of spinal anomalies in patients with CPC. Hydroureteronephrosis and VUR in these patients tend to improve with age.[8]

In our study, co-existing spinal dysraphism (sacral agenesis) was observed only in one patient. Vesico-ureteric reflux and hydronephrosis improved with age in all except in the case with sacral agenesis. Episodes of UTI too got resolved during infancy itself. No vasal reflux of contrast was observed in any of the patients who underwent a VCUG or magnetic resonance urography. On extensive search of the available English literature, we could not find any case of CPC presenting with EO. The only patient with EO in our series had unique associated predisposing factors.

We acknowledge that our research has limitations. The first is that it is a retrospective study, and the second is the short duration of follow-up. These limitations underscore the challenge of generalizing the results.

CONCLUSION

Despite the such a high association of genito urinary anomalies and anatomical abnormality of the verumontanum in patients with CPC, only one patient in our series had EO, possibly due to the immediate division of the fistulous communication during the primary surgery and potentially lesser risk of affecting ejaculatory ducts during definitive surgery. It is important for surgeons to be aware of the risk of EO in CPC patients where the fistula is not initially divided. Further research is necessary to determine the role of the unusual position of verumontanum and other factors in causing EO in male ARM patients with CPC.
