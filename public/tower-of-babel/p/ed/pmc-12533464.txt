Role of lipid droplets in pulmonary arterial hypertension: focusing on pulmonary artery smooth muscle cell proliferation
Authors: Baishi Huang, Yu Hao, Zhe Wen, Jie Dong, Shengwei Jin, Hui Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a devastating disease complicated by pathological features such as proliferation of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs), vasoconstriction, increased pulmonary artery pressure, and hypertrophic right heart failure. Among these features, excessive proliferation of PASMCs is the most significant pathological change in this disease. Lipid droplets (LDs) are ubiquitous cellular organelles that serve as energy storage sites. Previous studies have shown that large accumulations of intracellular LDs are observed in proliferating cells (such as stem cells and cancer cells) and that the modulation of LD accumulation can affect cell proliferation. These findings suggest that LDs play important roles in cell proliferation. This review aimed to investigate the role of LDs in PAH by focusing on the proliferation of PASMCs.Keywords: Lipid droplets, Pulmonary arterial hypertension, Cell proliferation, Hypoxia, Epithelial‒mesenchymal transition

Introduction

PAH is widely recognized as a vascular lesion caused by structural changes driven by excessive proliferative growth in endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells (SMCs) and inflammatory responses. According to incomplete statistics, up to 100 million people worldwide are strongly affected by various subtypes of this disease [1]. Excessive proliferation of PASMCs leads to neointimal hyperplasia, causing pulmonary artery occlusion, increased pulmonary circulatory resistance, and ultimately right heart hypertrophic failure, which is the most common and pivotal mechanism in the pathogenesis of PAH [2]. Hypoxia stimulates the proliferation of PASMCs by regulating the activity of hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) [3]. In addition, endothelial‒mesenchymal transition (EndMT) is a crucial source of vascular smooth muscle cells and may be a potential source of at least some PASMCs in thickened vessels [4–6]. Many experiments have shown that signalling molecules in the internal environment or secretions from other cells in the body, such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), 5-hydroxytryptamine 1B receptor and secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine (SPARC), promote PASMC proliferation through different mechanisms [7–9].

A new research topic in the past decade, LDs are complex organelles that reserve neutral lipids and regulate energy homeostasis in various types of cells [10]. Their structure is unique, with a single layer of phospholipids and proteins enveloping the central hydrophobic portion of lipids, and proteomic analyses have shown that proteins associated with LDs include ATGL, perilipin, Tip47, and ApoL6 [11, 12]. LDs play two major roles in cellular metabolism, namely, to store lipids, such as fatty acids, which are important for intracellular substances and energy storage and are mobilized for phospholipid membrane synthesis or energy production under conditions of nutrient overload. Once lipids become dysregulated during storage, they become toxic, which triggers cell death. In addition, LDs decrease the toxicity of lipids in cells, preventing lipotoxicity and playing important roles in preventing oxidative stress. LDs also contact the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), where their interactions are tightly coordinated [10]. Specifically, the ER protein seipin mediates the aggregation of triacylglycerol (TAG) at the site of LD biogenesis, catalysing the formation of neutral lipids within LDs and their budding from the ER membrane. Additionally, as an LD coat protein, perilipin regulates cellular lipid storage by mediating LD–organelle interactions via membrane contact sites [13, 14]. Another study revealed that in mouse PASMCs, Nogo-B, a regulator of ER structure, increases ER–mitochondrion distance, reduces phospholipid transfer and mitochondrial calcium, and drives PAH via PASMC proliferation [15]. Notably, several studies have shown that the number of LDs in cells in an abnormally proliferative state differs from that in normal cells. Taking this into account, it may be hypothesized that LDs play a previously undefined role in PAH-based pulmonary vascular remodelling.

The status of LDs in PASMCs and their changes during the development of PAH have not yet been thoroughly investigated. This review briefly summarizes the factors that influence the proliferation of PASMCs, introduces LDs and their potential mechanisms that mediate cell proliferation, and provides new perspectives on PASMC proliferation from the perspective of LDs.

Cell proliferation from a metabolic perspective

In mammalian cells, the uptake of nutrient molecules activates nutrient-sensing kinases that initiate signalling cascades to redirect the cellular state from catabolic to anabolic pathways and accumulate intracellular biomass to support cell proliferation [16].

Most abnormally proliferating cells primarily utilize glucose through the process of glycolysis [16, 17]. Cancer cells often rely on this metabolic mode, but this pathway is less efficient than ATP production by mitochondrial phosphorylation, which may be attributed to two explanations: First, proliferating cells have important metabolic requirements in addition to ATP, and second, in mammalian cells, nutrient resources are not scarce, and inefficient production of ATP may not constitute an obstacle to survival [17]. In addition to oncological diseases, this phenomenon occurs widely in noncancer diseases such as pulmonary fibrosis, atherosclerosis, cardiac hypertrophy and multiple sclerosis [18, 19]. Notably, one of the key pathogenic mechanisms of PAH is the induction of the Warburg mitochondrial metabolic state, in which aberrant bone morphogenetic protein signalling promotes vascular cell proliferation [20, 21]. JMJD1C, which belongs to the Jumonji C family of histone demethylases, enhances STAT3 signalling and stimulates the proliferation of SMCs through the induction of glycolysis in the context of PAH [22].

Hypoxia exerts a mediating influence on metabolism and cellular proliferation, with HIFs—comprising an O2-sensitive α-subunit (predominantly HIF-1α and HIF-2α) and a constitutively expressed β-subunit—playing important roles in this signalling pathway [23, 24]. For instance, the stimulation of mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) enhances glycolysis through a pathway involving dectin-1, Akt, and HIF-1α [25]. Furthermore, HIF activation is fundamental to lipid metabolism and anabolism and significantly affects hepatic and cardiac metabolic processes [23]. Elevated levels of HIF-1α and HIF-2α proteins have been reported across various cancer cell types, where they play critical biological roles in tumour proliferation and progression [26]. For example, HIF-1α promotes aerobic glycolysis by activating the expression levels of glycolysis-associated genes such as HK2, GLUT1 and LDHA, thus meeting the high metabolic demands of tumour cells [27, 28]. In addition to affecting glycolysis, HIF-1α accelerates the growth of Hepatocellular carcinoma cells by interacting with fatty acid-binding protein 5 (FABP5) to regulate lipid metabolism [29].

Molecular mechanisms contributing to PASMC proliferation in PAH

Continuous hypoxia significantly stimulates the proliferation of PASMCs. Reduced oxygen levels activate Rho kinase, increase vascular contraction, and upregulate HIF-1α expression, culminating in detrimental pulmonary vascular remodelling and the onset of PAH [3]. Furthermore, the AKT/FOXO3A/PCNA signalling pathway can be activated by the expression of the PARM1 protein induced by hypoxia or PDGF, thereby promoting PASMC proliferation [30]. As previously noted, the upregulation of HIF activity under hypoxic conditions is critically important for PAH progression. In patients with PAH, serum levels of selenium protein P (SeP) are elevated, contributing to oxidative stress and increased mitochondrial dysfunction, which promote the proliferation of PASMCs and apoptosis resistance; this phenomenon is associated with activated HIF-1α and disrupted glutathione metabolism [31](Fig. 1). TGF-β1 or HIF-2α signalling can induce the expression of SPARC in human PASMCs, and SPARC has been established as a factor in the development of pulmonary hypertension in humans and in mice induced by chronic hypoxia. In addition, as a paracrine factor secreted by pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells (PMVECs), SPARC initiates the proliferation of PASMCs [7]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) serve as crucial inducers of HIF-mediated cellular proliferation under low-oxygen conditions. Disruption of the mitochondrial-ROS-HIF-Kv pathway in PASMCs impaired their ability to effectively sense oxygen, resulting in diminished responses to hypoxic Pulmonary vasoconstriction and leading to polycythemia. Additionally, chromosomal abnormalities on chromosome 1 observed in fawn hooded rats—a strain predisposed to spontaneous PAH—can disrupt this mitochondrial-ROS-HIF-Kv pathway, contributing directly or indirectly to PAH pathogenesis; similar aberrations have also been documented in sporadic cases of human PAH [32]. Moreover, FUNDC1 serves as a hypoxic mitochondrial autophagy receptor that stabilizes HIF-1α during hypoxia by inhibiting ubiquitination processes. FUNDC1 enhances ROS production, which subsequently upregulates HIF-1α activity and leads to PASMC proliferation under anoxic conditions associated with PAH [33].

Growth factors are signalling molecules that promote cellular growth, proliferation, and differentiation [16]. Among the growth factors associated with PASMC proliferation in PAH are PDGF, endothelin-1 (ET-1), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), insulin-like growth factor (IGF), fibroblast growth factor (FGF), and epidermal growth factor (EGF) [9, 34, 35] (Fig. 1). ET-1 is acknowledged as the strongest vasoconstrictor in the human cardiovascular system [36]. PDGF is secreted by endothelial cells and platelets during vascular injury and inflammation [35]. A separate study demonstrated that the mRNA expression levels of PDGF-A, PDGFR-α, PDGF-B and PDGFR-β were elevated in small pulmonary arteries from idiopathic PAH patients compared with controls, confirming that PDGF promotes both the proliferation and migration of PASMCs [9]. IGF is released by the liver in response to stimulation by growth hormone [35]. Specifically, IGF1 (somatomedin C) and IGF2 (somatomedin A) have been identified as key mediators in this process. Upon binding to its receptor, insulin-like growth factor I receptor (IGF1R), IGF1 undergoes autophosphorylation, which activates intracellular signalling cascades, including the MAPK and AKT/mTOR pathways, thereby inducing cell growth and division [35]. Substantial evidence indicates that these vascular thickening-inducing growth factors also play a role in activating signalling pathways associated with EndMT [37].

EndMT refers to the shift of endothelial cells into mesenchymal-like cells, with significant involvement of TGFβ signalling pathways [37](Fig. 1). The glycosylation process involving tubulin-β2 and tubulin-β3 within microtubules can increase the responsiveness of human microvascular endothelial cells to TGF-β1 stimulation, potentially serving as a mechanism that inhibits endothelial transdifferentiation and identifying a potential therapeutic target for treating fibrotic diseases [38]. Multiple studies have confirmed that EndMT may contribute to endothelial dysfunction, facilitating the progression of PAH [4–6]. In vitro experiments have shown that mature bovine vascular endothelial cells can acquire SMC characteristics through a process dependent on TGF-β1 and cell–cell interactions. Importantly, transdifferentiation from endothelium to smooth muscle was noted in primary cultures derived from adult vasculature [5], indicating that this process could be a source of at least some PASMCs. Recent findings indicate that downregulating miR-204 in endothelial cells reduces EndMT by promoting autophagy, which may contribute to alleviating hypoxia-induced PAH to some extent [39]. Furthermore, both in vitro and in vivo experiments have demonstrated that the endogenous lipid mediator RvD1 inhibits the progression of EndMT in a hypoxia-induced pulmonary hypertension mouse model by downregulating Smad2/3 phosphorylation via FPR2 [40]. The macrophage-derived Lipid mediator Maresin 1 attenuates EndMT and inhibits PASMC proliferation by suppressing ERK, AKT, STAT and FoxO1 phosphorylation via LGR6 [41].

Increased intracellular calcium (Ca2+) signalling is linked to various abnormalities in PASMCs, such as abnormal proliferation, increased apoptosis, enhanced migration, and altered arterial contractility [42] (Fig. 1). The concentration of cytoplasmic calcium ions ([Ca2+]cyt) in PASMCs is regulated by ion channels located on the cell membrane [35]. For instance, TRPC6 functions as a receptor-operated Ca2+ (or cation) channel, and its upregulation and heightened activity are significant factors in the onset of hypertension and cardiac hypertrophy [43]. Additionally, research has indicated that increased expression of Piezo1 in proliferative PASMCs may be essential for providing the adequate amount of Ca2+ necessary for nuclear division and cell proliferation, thereby facilitating pulmonary vascular remodelling associated with PAH [44]. Furthermore, STIM2 acts as a Ca2+ sensor within the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) membrane; it is crucial for elevating resting [Ca2+]cyt levels in PASMCs from patients suffering from PAH, activating pathways that promote PASMC growth while inhibiting apoptosis [45]. In PASMCs, the activation of K channels expedites the decrease in apoptotic volume (AVD) and enhances apoptosis. Conversely, inhibiting these K channels decelerates AVD and diminishes apoptosis rates. Ultimately, blocking K channels increases cytosolic Ca2+ levels by promoting the opening of voltage-dependent Ca2+ channels through membrane depolarization, resulting in both the contraction and proliferation of PASMCs [46].

Studies have shown that upregulated proinflammatory cytokines can drive PASMC proliferation and vascular remodelling in PAH [47]. In particular, IL-6 induces the upregulation of VEGF, MAPK, and ERK expression. This, in turn, activates the proliferative c-Myc/Myc-associated protein X complex, increasing cell cycle gene expression and increasing PASMC proliferation [48]. Recent research has indicated that the overexpression of IL-6 receptors on SMCs can increase their proliferation, increasing overall vascular remodelling in PAH and positioning the classic IL-6 signalling pathway as a potential therapeutic target [49]. Research also suggests that reducing inflammatory cytokine levels might be beneficial for PAH treatment. In mice, IL-6 overexpression leads to changes in the pulmonary vasculature, whereas IL-6 knockout mice are more resistant to PAH [50]. An animal study has also demonstrated that treatment with IL-6 receptor antagonists can reverse PAH phenotypes [49].

Recent omics analyses in PAH research have revealed potential targets. For example, upregulation of ADGRG6/GPR126, an adhesion GPCR, may inhibit PAH PASMC proliferation [51]. Additionally, studies comparing differentially expressed genes across datasets have identified WDR43 and GNL2 as significantly upregulated hub genes in PAH, indicating their potential for further PAH research [52].

Through RNA sequencing and functional enrichment analysis, researchers have shown that the expression of AURKB is upregulated in PAH-associated PASMCs. Inhibiting AURKB can reduce the proliferation of PAH-PASMCs, induce cellular senescence, and ameliorate PAH symptoms in animal models. Thus, AURKB may represent a potential therapeutic target for PAH [53].

LD accumulation

LDs are cellular organelles that store surplus lipids within cells. The stored lipids consist of neutral forms, such as free fatty acids (FFAs) and cholesterol. These compounds are enzymatically transformed into TAG and cholesterol esters, respectively, prior to their incorporation into LDs [54]. Current LD detection techniques in cells involve Oil Red O staining for LD accumulation in tissues and Bodipy493/503 staining for monitoring LD dynamics in live cells [55, 56]. Quantitative lipidomic analysis can measure TAG levels in accumulated LDs [56].

When oxygen levels drop, there is an increase in the accumulation of LDs, which acts as a protective mechanism against toxicity caused by oxidative stress [54, 57]. The process leading to this accumulation under low-oxygen conditions involves several mechanisms. For instance, the absorption of both saturated and unsaturated fatty acids from external sources increases due to elevated levels of FABP3 and FABP7 when oxygen is scarce, resulting in more LD formation [58]. PLIN2 is a major surface protein of LDs, and its expression protects LDs from autophagy and LIPA-driven lipolysis in the liver and promotes LD fusion and accumulation [13, 59, 60]. In CTAD mutant mice, the protein levels of nuclear HIF-2α and its associated target PLIN2 are elevated, leading to increased TAG levels and LD accumulation in the livers of these mice and in primary hepatocytes extracted from them [61]. Moreover, under hypoxic conditions, PLIN-2 expression is tied to elevated fatty acid uptake and coordinated regulation of FABP3 and FABP7 [62]. Another study has revealed that hypoxia-induced lipid droplet-associated protein (HILPDA) inhibits adipose triglycerides lipase (ATGL). This inhibition of ATGL represents a key pathway through which HILPDA facilitates TAG buildup [63]. In colon and renal cancer cells, increased expression of HILPDA leads to greater TG-LD accumulation while decreasing reactive oxygen species production and fatty acid oxidation. Another mechanism is that HILPDA increases LD accumulation by stimulating triglyceride synthesis via DGAT1 [64, 65]. Additionally, carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (CPT1A) activity is suppressed by HIF-1α and HIF-2α, limiting the transport of fatty acids into mitochondria and instead directing them towards storage in LDs [57](Fig. 3). LD formation can occur regardless of the source of lipids but only takes place when CPT1A function is inhibited [57].

Recent research has connected LD accumulation with epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Acidosis promotes autocrine TSP1-dependent TGF-β2 activation to trigger signalling pathways that promote a shift towards a mesenchymal-like invasive phenotype while also facilitating both the uptake and storage of fatty acids in LDs. Fatty acid oxidation enhances Smad2 acetylation/activity and thereby promotes EMT [66]. Moreover, ZEB1—a critical transcription factor for EMT—is essential for LD formation. The generation of these droplets induced by TGF-β2 supports acidosis-driven EMT along with metastatic behaviour in cancer cells [66]. Furthermore, there is a positive feedback loop between ZEB2, a transcription factor involved in EMT, and ACSL4, an important enzyme in lipid metabolism. This leads to greater accumulation of LD and more vigorous fatty acid oxidation, both of which propel the metastasis of breast cancer. Mechanistically, ZEB2 binds directly to the promoter region of ACSL4 to activate its expression, whereas ACSL4 stabilizes and upregulates ZEB2 through various transcriptional and posttranscriptional methods [67].

Calcium signalling has been identified as a regulator for maintaining lipid homeostasis as well. Store-operated calcium entry (SOCE), governed by the stromal interaction molecules STIM1/2 and ORAI1 channels, serves as an essential pathway for calcium influx regulation. SOCE participates in the production of cAMP and the expression of neutral fat enzymes, as well as the transcriptional regulation of the lipid metabolism regulators PGC-1α and PPARα. Experimental findings indicate that mice lacking SOCE exhibit pathological accumulation of LDs across liver tissue as well as heart muscle fibres and skeletal muscles [68]. Lepr is expressed specifically within alveolar macrophages. Mechanistically, Lepr signalling sustains the activation of AMP-activated protein kinase in a manner contingent on calcium influx and rewires cellular metabolism, thereby forestalling excessive LD formation and metabolic stress in a lipid-rich alveolar environment [69].

Under inflammatory conditions, cells exhibit aberrant LD accumulation. The inflammatory cytokine IL-6 upregulates PLIN5 via the JAK/STAT3 pathway. As an LD-associated protein that inhibits lipolysis, elevated PLIN5 promotes lipid accumulation [70] (Fig. 3). In the central nervous system, aged microglia exhibit progressive hypertrophy, process retraction, and heightened secretion of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α). During immune responses, these cells become hyperactivated and dysfunctional, further promoting LD formation and accumulation [71, 72]. Accumulated LDs promote the release of inflammatory factors and exacerbate inflammatory responses by serving as a platform for the synthesis of inflammatory mediators and the activation of relevant signalling pathways. Eicosanoids, which are signalling lipids derived from arachidonic acid, are crucial in various physiological and pathological situations, including tissue balance, inflammatory responses, and cancer, with LDs serving as specialized sites for their biosynthesis [73]. Furthermore, experimental evidence has demonstrated that LPS promotes TAG accumulation in macrophages via induction of the expression of the LD-related protein HILPDA, reducing ATGL protein levels through enhanced proteasomal degradation. ATGL-mediated lipolysis is important for regulating the production of PGE2 and subsequent release of IL-6 [74]. Moreover, LD formation is a key feature of endothelial inflammation in human microvascular endothelial cells in response to LPS in vitro [75].

LDs and cell proliferation

Previous studies have shown a positive relationship between higher LD numbers and cell proliferation in certain states [76, 77]. An increase in LDs usually occurs when proliferation is triggered by cellular pathologies such as oxidative stress, hypoxia, inflammation, or cancer [78–80]. Among these conditions, cancer has been extensively studied as a context in which LD accumulation supports proliferation.

Tumour cells

Several studies have identified LDs as potential indicators of cancer [81]. On the one hand, LDs help alleviate ER toxicity, thereby facilitating cell growth. In clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) cells, the expression of PLIN2 driven by HIF-2α enhances lipid storage, cellular proliferation, and tumour viability in xenograft models. Mechanistically, the storage of lipids supports ER integrity, which is both functionally and physically linked to LDs. Specifically, lipid accumulation mediated by PLIN2 reduces the degree of cytotoxic stress on the ER and promotes ccRCC growth [82] (Fig. 2). APOL1 is found within intracellular LDs, where it promotes lipid accumulation and tumour advancement while accelerating the growth of ccRCC cells. Additionally, APOL1 interacts with the ER, and its absence triggers activation of the unfolded protein response, leading to apoptotic cell death [83] (Fig. 2). Furthermore, in a separate study, re-establishing carboxylesterase 1 (CES1) expression significantly reduced LD levels, resulting in increased apoptosis through heightened ER stress while also reducing de novo androgen production within tumours and hindering progression in castration-resistant prostate cancer [84].

Lipids stored in LDs may influence cell division and proliferation by modifying cellular metabolic pathways. In the premetastatic phase, lipid-rich lung-resident mesenchymal cells (MCs) transfer their lipids to tumour cells and NK cells through exosome-like vesicles [85]. The incorporation of these MC-derived lipids reprograms breast cancer cells, increasing their proliferation and resistance to chemotherapy, which enhances tumour cell survival while impairing NK cell function [85]. The presence of PLIN3 on the LD surface is associated with increased LD size and elevated triglyceride and cholesterol levels, which, in turn, can promote cellular changes such as enhanced migratory capacity [86]. Another study involving breast cancer cells demonstrated that reducing the expression of Elovl5—an ultralong-chain fatty acid protein—results in elevated DGAT expression [87]. This enzyme catalyses the conversion of diacylglycerol (DAG) with fatty acid-CoA into TAG, leading to increased TAG levels and subsequently increasing LD accumulation. This accumulation is associated with Smad2 acetylation-induced upregulation of TGF-β receptor expression, facilitating progression from G1 to S phase and initiating DNA replication along with cell division [87]. In glioblastoma cells, LDs promote growth and reproduction by stabilizing cholesterol levels while increasing cholesterol uptake without triggering feedback inhibition of sterol regulatory element binding protein-1, thus supporting tumour growth [88]. Liposomes within intestinal cancer cells are active organelles that play crucial roles in the synthesis of PGE2 and may be important in the development of colon adenocarcinoma [89]. Hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) are rich in LDs containing retinoids, which play crucial roles in regulating cell proliferation and differentiation. The depletion of retinoid in activated HSCs from chronically damaged livers may contribute to tumour suppression [90] (Fig. 2). In tumour-associated mouse dendritic cells (DCs), oxidatively truncated triacylglycerides accumulate within large LDs; these oxidatively truncated lipids possess reactive functional groups that can form covalent bonds with HSP70, a target for new anticancer therapies. This interaction subsequently influences the transport of peptide-loaded major histocompatibility complex class I (pMHC-I) molecules, which are essential for triggering the proliferation of cancer-killing T cells [91] (Fig. 2). Although fatty acids are vital energy sources, they can become toxic when their concentrations exceed normal physiological limits; this excess induces oxidative stress in mitochondria, leading to cell death [92]. To avoid such outcomes, breast cancer cells utilize DGAT 1 activity to channel fatty acids into LDs for storage [92] (Fig. 2). Similar mechanisms have been observed in intestinal cancer cells as well. Liposomes may serve as reservoirs for quickly absorbing free arachidonic acid (AA); however, this reactivity could pose risks for host cells since AA plays a significant role in inducing apoptosis among tumour cells [89].

Lipid droplet-derived fatty acid metabolites, such as polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), support tumour cell proliferation by promoting the production of lipid mediators [93]. A study conducted on MDA-MB-231 cancer cells revealed that DGAT1 facilitates the integration of PUFAs into LDs, followed by the release of PUFAs through ATGL. This process promotes the basal and hGX-sPLA2-primed production of COX- and LOX-derived lipid mediators. These mediators subsequently stimulate cancer cell division and tumour enlargement [94]. Additionally, ω−6 PUFAs, such as linoleic acid (LA), effectively activate mTORC1 signalling in triple-negative breast cancer cell lines, such as MDA-MB-436 and HCC1806, and significantly promote cell proliferation [95] (Fig. 3).

Nontumour cells

ROS promote tumour development, yet excessive accumulation of ROS can result in cytotoxic effects [96]. The formation of LDs helps reduce ROS levels. ATGL is a key lipolytic enzyme on the surface of LDs and is present in both adipose and nonadipose tissues. BETi induces the expression of ATGL and FoxO1 and reduces the expression of G0S2, leading to increased lipolysis and decreased LD content and bioavailability of neutral lipids [92]. Simultaneously, mitochondria become rich in fatty acids, which hinders cell growth, elevates the generation of ROS and intensifies mitochondrial stress. After ATGL expression is disrupted, the oxidative phenotype is partially restored to a glycolytic state, with neutral lipids being restored in LDs, thereby reducing mitochondrial oxidative stress [92]. During drosophila development, glial cell niches support neuroblast proliferation even under hypoxic and oxidative stress conditions. In these glial cells, LD formation during oxidative stress reduces ROS levels and prevents the oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids [97]. These droplets serve as a protective mechanism for glial cells and neuroblasts against peroxide chain reactions [97]. Additionally, the regulatory protein Lsd-2 on the surface of LDs inhibits TAG lipolysis while promoting neutral lipid storage during hypoxia; this synthesis and storage within LDs are essential for maintaining neuroblast division [97] (Fig. 2). The quantity of LDs varies among neural stem cells. However, those with a greater number proliferate significantly faster than those with fewer droplets do—likely because of enhanced metabolism in neural stem/progenitor cells and protection from intracellular lipid oxidation resulting from elevated ROS levels [98].

Lipids stored in LDs can be mobilized to contribute to the synthesis of membrane phospholipids and supply energy, providing material and energy conditions for cell proliferation. In hepatocytes during liver regeneration, lipids from these droplets are either transported to bile or utilized to generate new lipoproteins and bile acids, which aid in the formation of new membranes or provide the energy needed by residual hepatocytes for liver reconstruction [99]. In the context of allergen-induced tracheal inflammation, innate lymphocytes absorb external Lipids that are temporarily stored in LDs and converted into phospholipids to increase group 2 innate lymphoid cell (ILC2) proliferation [100]. IL-33 plays a supportive role in this process. Additionally, esterification and storage within LDs help prevent lipid toxicity resulting from excessive external lipid uptake or free fatty acid mobilization during lipolysis [100] (Fig. 2). The enzyme arylacetamide deacetylase was found to be overexpressed in human primary vascular SMCs. A notable reduction in the number of LDs was observed. Additional metabolomic analyses disclosed a significant decrease in stored lipids and a boost in membrane phospholipids, indicating alterations in the Kennedy pathway related to lipid bioassembly and a marked decrease in both cell migration and proliferation [101] (Fig. 2).

Lipid metabolism

Lipid metabolism in PAH

In PAH, there is a notable shift in energy metabolism, leading to increased reliance on glucose while fatty acid usage decreases [102]. These lipid metabolism-related changes are mediated by various regulatory mechanisms to induce PAH. In patients with PAH, the expression of lipid regulatory proteins is altered, with decreased expression of fatty acid transport protein CD36 and the low-density lipoprotein receptor in human lung tissue. Conversely, the levels of oxidized low-density lipoprotein are elevated in both lung tissue and plasma [103]. The increased uptake of fatty acids by the fatty acid transport protein CD36 leads to the accumulation of lipids within the cytoplasm, such as ceramides, diglycerides, and triglycerides, increasing lipotoxicity, which may be a characteristic of PAH-related right ventricular failure [102]. Moreover, a critical finding related to pathological hypertrophy and heart failure is the downregulation of PPARα, a vital transcription factor for lipid metabolism that is predominantly expressed in cardiac tissue. The activation of PPARα enhances the uptake and breakdown of fatty acids by increasing the expression of genes associated with fatty acid metabolism [104].

Lipid metabolism and LD

LDs typically function as centres for lipid metabolism [105]. Proteomic studies of LDs have identified numerous proteins associated with them, including enzymes that metabolize lipids [106]. In mammalian cells, acyl CoA plays a significant role in the final stage of triacylglycerol synthesis within these droplets [106]. PLIN1 serves as a common protein that forms the shell of LDs in mammalian adipocytes and plays a key role in regulating both lipid storage and breakdown. When PLIN1 is stimulated, phosphorylated PLIN1 facilitates the release of CGI-58, which then recruits the lipolytic enzyme ATGL [107]. Lipolytic dysfunction leads to the accumulation of triglycerides in the cytoplasm [108].

The role of LDs in other cardiovascular diseases

In PAH and other vascular diseases, LDs play a crucial role in various vascular cell types, influencing cell metabolism and function. In endothelial cells, LDs regulate cell function by modulating key signalling pathways [109]. For example, studies have shown that the accumulation of LDs is significantly related to endothelial dysfunction. Specifically, the absence of ATGL in endothelial cells leads to the accumulation of vascular neutral lipids, which impairs endothelium-dependent vascular tension and nitric oxide synthesis, thereby promoting endothelial dysfunction [110]. In other vascular cells, such as macrophages, LDs also play important roles. In atherosclerosis, the formation of foam cells is a hallmark of early lesions. Macrophages form foam cells by engulfing oxidized low-density lipoprotein through a process mediated by various scavenger receptors, such as CD36, SR-A1, and LOX-1 [111]. Several LD-related proteins, including HSPA5, UBE2G2, and AUP1, have been associated with the mechanism of lipid phagocytosis. Focusing on these proteins to specifically increase lipid phagocytosis and promote cholesterol removal in foam cells could be a new treatment approach for atherosclerosis [112].

LDs significantly impact cardiovascular disease progression, particularly in atherosclerosis. In arteries, macrophage LDs accumulate cholesteryl esters (CEs), driven by ACAT enzymes, advancing atherosclerosis [113]. A study in male mice revealed that LD accumulation in vascular endothelial cells induces cilia loss, promoting atherosclerosis. Mechanistically, triglyceride buildup in these cells alters cytosolic free fatty acid levels, stimulating LD formation and inhibiting protein S-palmitoylation. This reduces ciliary protein S-palmitoylation, causing cilia loss and accelerating atherosclerosis [114]. Another study revealed that mice with endothelial-specific ATGL deficiency exhibit increased endothelial LD formation and reduced endothelium-dependent vasodilation, thereby increasing the risk of arterial hypertension and atherosclerosis [115].

LD accumulation also contributes to arterial hypertension to some extent. Research has indicated that a high-fat diet in mice causes the buildup of LDs in endothelial cells. This inhibits endothelial nitric oxide synthase, leading to a reduction in NO production. As NO is a vasodilator, its reduction causes vasoconstriction and elevates blood pressure [115].

Hypothesis on the mechanism of lipid droplet-associated PASMC proliferation

First, the accumulation of oxidized lipids in the pulmonary cardiovascular system is considered a key factor in the development of PAH and serves as an indicator of lipid toxicity [103]. LDs can help dilute and buffer these harmful lipids. Similar protective mechanisms have been reported in the nervous system, with evidence suggesting a link between heightened sympathetic nervous system activity and PAH progression [116]. In periods of heightened ROS formation, fatty acids are transported from neurons to glial cells via fatty acid transport proteins and ApoE, where they are stored in LDs. These droplets are thought to protect neurons through the isolation of damaged peroxidized lipids [117, 118]. Glial cells also respond to neuronal activity by metabolizing stored fatty acids in LDs through mitochondrial β-oxidation and initiating detoxification gene expression programs. The levels of ROS are reduced, likely because of the activation of converging pathways that counteract the generation of ROS by increased β-oxidation and mitochondrial activity [119]. Moreover, downregulation of key enzymes that facilitate lipid breakdown, such as ATGL, can alleviate the effects of oxidative stress and prevent cell death by altering the mitochondrial metabolic state [92]. PLIN5 shows high expression levels in oxidative tissues and is the most extensively studied LD-associated protein in the context of oxidative stress [120]. PLIN5 expression is elevated by cellular oxidative stress through the JNK-p38-ATF signalling pathway. Elevated levels of PLIN5 expression promotes the formation of LDs and mitochondrial–LD contact and can also regulate the expression of mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase, thereby reducing the production of ROS [121] (Fig. 3). By mitigating oxidative stress and lipid toxicity, LDs play a role in promoting the survival and proliferation of PASMCs. Investigating how LD biological pathways influence PASMC responses to oxidative stress might lead to new therapeutic strategies for treating PAH characterized by excessive accumulation of oxidized lipids.

Second, the lipids contained within LDs serve as vital energy sources and substrates essential for cellular metabolism and biosynthesis processes. Mitochondria are closely associated with LDs, particularly in adipose tissue, and this relationship enhances pyruvate oxidation along with electron transport and ATP synthesis capabilities. The amplification of LDs is further supported by PLIN5-induced recruitment of mitochondria to these structures, which increases ATP-dependent triglyceride synthesis [122]. These findings indicate that LDs can indirectly influence ATP production through interactions with mitochondria. One study suggested that HSCs metabolize LDs via autophagy to generate the necessary cellular energy for catabolic pathways that activate cell functions [123]. Additionally, various types of lipids, such as phospholipids and triglycerides, found within LDs play crucial roles in facilitating cell division and proliferation processes [87]. From this analysis perspective, a potential strategy for controlling pathological PASMC proliferation arises: blocking pathways involved in lipid storage, for example, targeting TAG synthesis or storage by disrupting regulatory proteins such as Lsd-2 on LD surfaces under hypoxic conditions.

Moreover, hypoxia and calcium ion signalling imbalance affect the entire cellular microenvironment, such as via the alteration of cellular lipid metabolism or LD accumulation or mediation or inhibition of cell proliferation, and are potential targets for influencing PASMC proliferation. Hypoxia advances PASMC progression primarily through the upregulation of HIF activity [3]. HIF-1α regulates both expression levels related to protein components associated with LD formation and macrophage polarization dynamics [124]. Furthermore, the expression of the LD coat protein-encoding gene PLIN2, which is related to HIF-2α, is increased in tumours and inhibits cytotoxic endoplasmic reticulum stress responses while fostering ccRCC proliferation and metabolic synthetic activities [82]. Therefore, it is hypothesized that HIF could alter lipid metabolism represented by LDs to mediate the proliferation of PASMCs, and the specific mechanism may involve the ability of LDs to dilute lipid toxicity or alleviate cellular oxidative stress. The abnormal proliferation of PASMCs is partly due to enhanced Ca²⁺ signalling transduction. Calcium ions are stored mainly in the ER within cells [125], and the involvement of the ER in the biogenesis of LDs has been described above. Therefore, it is hypothesized that calcium ions interact in the ER and LDs. This hypothesis has been confirmed by previous studies in which sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase (SERCA) is reported to be an ER calcium pump. Suppressing dSERCA in drosophila fat cells causes lipodystrophy, abnormal LD formation, and inappropriate lipid buildup by affecting the intracellular calcium balance [126]. (Fig. 3). Additionally, LDs have the potential to buffer cytosolic Ca²⁺ and provide protection against cell death following ischaemia‒reperfusion injury [127]. Given the regulatory capacity of LDs for Ca²⁺, it may influence the proliferation of PASMCs by modulating intracellular calcium ion homeostasis, although further research is needed to confirm this.

Extensive research has shown the role of LDs in inflammation signalling in endothelial cells, macrophages, and vascular tissues stimulated by TNF-α [74, 128]. On the basis of the results of the above analysis, it is hypothesized that LDs may act as proinflammatory mediators in PAH, potentially indirectly promoting the proliferation of PASMCs through effects on ATGL and IL-6 signalling.

Conclusions

Although notable progress has been realized in elucidating the mechanisms that drive PASMC proliferation, critical gaps in our knowledge of LD characteristics and Lipid metabolism in these cells remain. Emerging evidence suggests a potential connection between LDs and cell proliferation. Sterol O-acyltransferase 1 (SOAT1), also known as acyl-CoA thioester transferase 1 (ACAT1), facilitates the transformation of cholesterol into cholesterol esters for storage within LDs. In cancer treatment, silencing genes or inhibiting SOAT1/ACAT1 activity with agents such as K604, ATR-101, or avasimibe has proven effective in disrupting the accumulation of LDs and curbing tumour growth in various cancer xenograft models [136]. The conventional treatment approaches for PAH mainly include therapies targeting the endothelin, nitric oxide or prostacyclin pathways [137]. However, lipid-targeted treatment represents a new research direction. Recent studies have shown that pioglitazone, an agonist of PPARγ, can completely reverse severe PAH in a SU5416/hypoxia (SuHx) rat model by targeting lipid metabolism and mitochondrial morphology/function disorders in cardiomyocytes [129] (Table 1). By integrating single-cell sequencing technology with a multiomics analysis framework, we can delve deeper into the regulatory networks governing PASMC lipid metabolism and pinpoint potential therapeutic targets. Focusing on critical points may help prevent excessive PASMC proliferation and reveal new approaches for treating PAH.
