mPower: a real data-based power analysis tool for microbiome study design
Authors: Lu Yang, Jun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPower analysis is a critical step in designing a microbiome study. Existing power calculation tools for microbiome studies mainly rely on parametric models of the sequencing counts, which underestimate the complexity of microbiome data and could produce overly optimistic power estimates. In this work, we present a new simulation-based power analysis tool, mPower, for microbiome study design. The tool uses a real data-based semi-parametric simulation framework to generate realistic microbiome data, upon which the power assessment is performed. Coupled with a select differential analysis tool, our power tool supports different study designs, including cross-sectional, case-control, and matched-pair studies, with or without confounders. It allows power analysis for both community-level and taxon-level testing. By using microbiome reference datasets from different environments, the users could perform power calculation based on the environment of interest. The mPower is primarily designed for 16S amplicon sequencing data, and it also incorporates a parametric simulation framework that enables power analysis for shotgun metagenomic data. We showcase the application of mPower with several real-world examples. The web interface of mPower is available at https://microbiomestat.shinyapps.io/mPower/. Background

When conducting an experimental or observational study, power analysis is an essential and critical step for ensuring both efficient resource use and study validity [1–3]. A well-powered study increases the likelihood of making true discoveries as well as improves the interpretation of negative findings. An underpowered study, on the other hand, may not be able to detect even commonly reported effects, making the negative results not very meaningful. There are mainly two settings requiring power analysis. In the first setting, the goal of power analysis is to determine the appropriate sample size needed to detect an intended effect size with a large probability. Ideally, the sample size should be equipped to detect a small yet meaningful effect size. However, due to resource constraint, investigators usually aim for an effect size that is realistic and has been frequently reported in relevant studies. In the second setting, the goal of power analysis is to calculate the detectable effect sizes at a given sample size. This “fixed sample size” setting can arise when the sample availability is very limited, for example, in the study of a rare disease, or when the resources can only afford a certain sample size. Such power analysis is especially important to make inferences about the non-detected effects if the study does not produce significant results.

Traditional power analysis concerns a single hypothesis test and depends on the study design and the statistical test used [1]. It involves four essential parameters: sample size, power, alpha and effect size. Power is defined as the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis when it is false while alpha, also called the type I error rate, is the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis when it is true. Effect size characterizes the degree of deviation of the alternative hypothesis from the null hypothesis. Depending on the specific setting, different effect sizes can be defined such as the Cohen’s d for Student’s t-test, the correlation coefficient ρ for Pearson’s correlation test, R2 for linear regression F-test. Given the values for three of the four parameters, the value of the fourth parameter can be calculated. Many software tools have been developed to facilitate power analysis such as G*Power [4], and pwr [3, 5].

When it comes to power analysis for microbiome studies, traditional power calculation tools cannot be directly applied due to the complexity of the microbiome data, specialized testing tools and unique signal structures [6, 7]. Firstly, microbiome data are high-dimensional. Microbiome-wide association analysis requires simultaneously testing a large number of microbial taxa at different taxonomical levels such as phylum, genus, and species. Thus, multiple testing procedures such as false discovery rate (FDR) control need to be used to control for false positives. In the presence of multiple testing, using the traditional alpha parameter, which concerns a single hypothesis testing, is no longer appropriate. When FDR control is used, the target FDR level is a more natural alpha parameter. As multiple microbial features can be associated with a variable of interest, a single effect size parameter is no longer sufficient. There should be multiple components of the effect size definition including the number of the microbial features associated (signal density) and the distribution of their individual effects (signal strength). Power analysis should consider plausible models of the signal structure. Secondly, microbiome data contains excessive zeros and most of the microbial taxa are only present in a small subset of samples. Due to the influence of numerous environmental and host factors, the human/environmental microbiome is highly variable, making simple parametric models such as Poisson model or even the negative binomial model inadequate to capture the intrinsic variability [8, 9]. Moreover, sequencing experiments usually measure the relative abundances of the detected taxa and the microbiome data are compositional in nature. Compositionality makes identifying differential taxa challenging as the change in the absolute abundance of one taxon can lead to the apparent change in the relative abundances of all other taxa. Due to the zero inflation, high variability and compositionality, specialized tools have been developed including MaAsLin2 [10], ANCOM-BC [11], LOCOM [12], LinDA [13] and ZicoSeq [6]. Power analysis thus should reflect the actual tool used in the analysis. Thirdly, the composition and diversity of the microbiome differ by sampling locations. For example, the gut or stool microbiome is highly diverse while the skin microbiome has very low diversity and the data contains far more zeros. It usually requires substantially more sample size to study the low-diversity microbiome. Thus, power analysis should consider the actual sampling site. Finally, microbiome association testing includes both community-level and taxon-level testing. The community-level test jointly tests all bacteria, focusing on detecting a global shift in the community structure and composition. As no multiple testing correction is needed, the community-level testing could be more powerful than taxon-level testing in establishing an overall association without identifying associated features. Thus, the community-level test is particularly useful for power analysis for pilot studies.

Several power analysis tools tailored for microbiome research have been developed [14–18]. They are either focused on community-level tests or taxon-level tests. The R package HMP [16] is the first community-level power analysis tool for microbiome data based on the generalized Wald test. Power is derived analytically assuming the microbiome compositional data has a Dirichlet-multinomial distribution. However, the Dirichlet-multinomial distribution has been shown to inadequately model the correlations among taxa and underestimate the variance [6, 18, 19]. The R package micropower [17] provides simulation-based community-level power analysis using PERMANOVA, a community-level test based on β-diversity distance matrices. It simulates artificial distance matrices that match the observed mean and variance of a real distance matrix through manually adjusting several parameters governing the subsampling process. The main challenges of the method are the computational efficiency, and the extent of manual interactions [20]. Evident [14], either as a standalone Python package or as a QIIME2 plugin, also provides community-level power analysis based on α- and β-diversity measures. One unique feature is that it provides realistic effect size estimates (Cohen’s d and F) for many metadata variables based on large-scale projects such as the American Gut Project [21], FINRISK [22], and TEDDY [23], followed by power analysis using simple t-test or ANOVA. As the pairwise distances have an inherent correlation structure, it is unclear if t-test and ANOVA, which assume the independence among observations, could provide accurate enough power approximation. Both micropower and Evident are currently restricted to handling categorical covariates. For taxon-level power analysis tools, existing tools predominantly employ parametric approaches, utilizing simulated microbiome data for power analysis. The web-based msWaldHMP [15] tool extends the capability of the HMP package and includes the option for taxon-level power analysis using the nonparametric Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney method. As the Dirichlet-multinomial model is used to simulate the compositional data, it shares the same disadvantage as its predecessor HMP. Conversely, powmic [18], another taxon-level power analysis tool based on the Gaussian Copula model, provides greater flexibility in choosing marginal distributions. It is equipped to handle a range of parametric models, including both multivariate and univariate models. Despite the sophistication of existing parametric models, they still fall short in capturing the complexity of the microbiome data [6]. The sample space generated by these models is constrained by a few parameters, which may be insufficient to encompass the data’s full complexity. The consequence on power assessment using an inappropriate parametric model is largely unknown. Finally, existing methods mostly handle categorical covariates and do not consider confounders. As confounders are prevalent for microbiome studies [24, 25], ignoring confounders can exaggerate the power estimates.

To address these issues, we present mPower, a tool designed to provide realistic power assessment for microbiome studies (Fig. 1). This tool incorporates a semi-parametric simulation framework [6, 7] grounded in real data, which is adaptable to various experimental designs. Additionally, it is enhanced by its compatibility with state-of-the-art differential abundance analysis method used in real data analysis. Our key contributions include the following: (1) We provide a user-friendly interface for power analysis tailored to microbiome study design, accessible even without coding expertise. (2) We curate a selection of large microbiome datasets, from typical human and environmental sources, for users to tailor their analysis. (3) We offer an integrated approach to power analysis that considers both community-level and taxon-level power. (4) We support diverse study designs, such as cross-sectional, case-control, and matched-pair studies, with or without confounders. (5) We offer flexible parameter setting to increase the capability of recapitulating the underlying biological/ecological models.

Methods

Simulation-based power assessment approach

mPower uses a simulation approach for power assessment. To generate realistic microbiome data, it employs the real data-based semi-parametric simulation framework [6, 7], which draws random samples from a large reference dataset (nonparametric part) and uses these reference samples as templates to generate new samples (parametric part). Specifically, for each drawn reference sample, underlying true composition is inferred based on a Bayesian model and covariate/confounder effects are then added to the composition vector parametrically, based on which a new sequencing sample is generated. Specifically, to infer the latent true microbial composition underlying each reference sample and to disentangle biological variation from sampling variability, we adopt an empirical Bayes framework. Specifically, for case-control and cross-sectional designs, we obtain posterior samples of the underlying composition based on an empirical Bayes model. Assume an informative Dirichlet prior for the underlying composition. Estimate the Dirichlet hyperparameters (γj) based on the observed counts (Cij,1≤i≤n,1≤j≤m) using the maximum likelihood estimation (R package “dirmult”). The posterior distribution of the underlying composition for sample i is then a Dirichlet distribution with parameter γij′=Cij+γj(1≤j≤m). Subsequently we obtain a posterior sample of the underlying composition for each reference sample (Pij,1≤i≤n,1≤j≤m) based on the posterior Dirichlet distribution. Next the absolute abundance (Cij′) was generated by multiplying a factor Si representing the microbial load at the ecological site, i.e., Cij′=PijSi, where log(Si)∼N(0,1) without loss of generality. To introduce confounding, we generate the confounder Zi∼N(0,1) and the covariate of interest Xi= R21-R2Zi+N0,1, where R is the desired correlation between the confounder and the covariate of interest. Binary Xi can be generated by dichotomizing Xi using some cutoff value to achieve the specified group sizes. Finally, we add covariate (Xi) and confounder (Zi) effect to the absolute abundance by Cij′′=Cij′expajXi+bjZi+ϵij, where aj,bj are coefficients controlling the effect size and ϵij∼N(0,σϵ2) is the random error. Non-differential taxa are simulated by setting the corresponding coefficients to 0. Therefore, our method circumvents the difficulty in modeling the intersubject variation of the microbiome composition. More details of the simulation steps for case-control and cross-sectional designs can be found in our previous publications [6], while extensions to longitudinal designs are presented in [7]. The realism and statistical validity of this semi-parametric framework have been established previously: simulated data recapitulate key features of real microbiome datasets [6, 7], and downstream analyses achieve appropriate false discovery rate (FDR) control under global null scenarios [6, 7], supporting its use for realistic data generation and reliable power estimation.

In mPower, we curated a database of both human samples (gut, skin, oral cavity, and vagina) and environmental samples (soil, lake water, wastewater, and the surface of plants) retrieved from the literature [21, 26–32]. Processed datasets were downloaded from the original publication-listed archive and were subject to quality control and prevalence-based and sequencing-depth-based filtering. Specifically, we kept the sequence units (ASV/OTU and genus-level) with prevalence in at least 5% of samples. For high-diversity communities such as the microbiomes of the human gut, soil, lake water, wastewater, and plant surfaces, only those samples with a sequencing depth of over 10,000 were included. For low-diversity microbial communities, such as the human skin and oral microbiomes, samples with a depth exceeding 1000 were retained. In the case of the vaginal microbiome, samples with a depth greater than 400 were preserved. The datasets available for user selection can be found in Table 1. These reference profiles are large enough to capture the main compositional variation in the population of interest.

Although we strive to curate a comprehensive database of reference datasets spanning diverse sampling locations, the database may not fully capture the characteristics of the data that an investigator intends to generate. For example, investigators may wish to study under-represented biological niches, non-human hosts, or generate data using emerging sequencing technologies. To improve the generalizability and flexibility of mPower, we introduce a parametric simulation framework that complements the original semi-parametric approach. This extension allows users to upload their own reference datasets, from which simulated samples are generated, ensuring that the simulated data more faithfully reflect the characteristics of the planned study. The parametric strategy is based on our recently developed Bimodal Dirichlet Distribution (BMDD) [33], which is specifically designed to model zero-inflated microbiome compositional data. BMDD captures key distributional features commonly observed in real microbiome studies, including sparsity, bimodality, and complex abundance patterns. By substantially expanding the parameter space relative to the standard Dirichlet distribution, BMDD flexibly accommodates both count-based and proportional microbiome data. Simulated samples are generated in a manner analogous to the semi-parametric approach, with the key difference occurring at the initial step: BMDD parameters (π and α) are first estimated from the user-uploaded dataset using the bmdd function in the R package MicrobiomeStat, and new microbiome compositions are then generated from the fitted model. The parametric mode of mPower is available at https://mpower-parametric.fly.dev.

Key components of power analysis by mPower

mPower delineates five components essential for comprehensive power analysis: statistical power, alpha level, effect size, differential setting, and statistical tests—each of which will be elaborated upon subsequently.

Statistical power: For community-level testing, where a single hypothesis test is conducted, power retains its traditional definition: the probability of correctly rejecting the null hypothesis when it is false. A study is generally considered well-powered if it has at least 80% power to detect an intended effect. For taxon-level testing, which involves multiple hypothesis testing, power differs from this traditional definition. In mPower, we employ two measures of power for taxon-level testing: the average true positive rate (aTPR) and the probability of making at least one correct rejection (pOCR). The aTPR represents the average proportion of truly differential taxa that are correctly identified as such across simulation runs. aTPR quantifies the average proportion of truly differential taxa with log2 fold changes exceeding a user-defined biologically meaningful threshold, providing insight into how comprehensively biological signals can be recovered. It can be interpreted as the average statistical power (traditional definition) across all differential taxa. As the effect sizes of the differential taxa could vary from very small to large, targeting 80% aTPR may be too demanding as those taxa with small effects are difficult to detect. Thus, we recommend aTPR to be at least 30–40% so that a significant portion of differential taxa can be identified. pOCR, which quantifies the probability of making any discovery, is more like the conventional power definition. It answers the question: will my study detect any truly differential taxa at all? A low pOCR implies a high risk of a completely negative study. However, it differs in that the specific taxa rejected in each simulated dataset need not be consistent. The target pOCR level is recommended to be set at a high threshold, such as 80% or 90%, to ensure a strong likelihood of obtaining true positive results.

Alpha level or type I error rate: For community-level testing, alpha has the traditional definition, which is defined as the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis when the null is true (i.e., a non-differential taxon is wrongly identified as differential). In this case, alpha level is typically set at 5%. For taxon-level testing, when multiple hypothesis testing is involved, we use the false discovery rate (FDR) as the alpha measure. FDR is the expected proportion of non-differential taxa in the identified taxa list. A typical FDR level can be 5% or 10%.

Effect size: In microbiome association studies, effect size is inherently heterogeneous: multiple taxa may be associated with a condition of interest, each with varying magnitudes of association. Moreover, both the number of associated taxa and the magnitude of their effects may differ substantially across diseases and clinical contexts. To capture this heterogeneity, mPower characterizes effect size using two complementary components. First, the percentage of differential taxa is an important effect size parameter, which can be estimated based on the association p-value distribution. We recommend estimating signal density (the proportion of differential taxa) using Storey’s tail method based on the distribution of p-values from a similar or related microbiome study. Moreover, instead of using a fixed effect size for all differential taxa, which is unrealistic, mPower allows the effect size (log2 fold change(LFC), between two groups, or in response to 1 S.D. change of a continuous variable) to come from a probabilistic distribution. In the current implementation, it assumes a uniform distribution on the interval [min LFC, max LFC]. Because the true distribution of effect sizes is generally unknown in practice, we assume a uniform distribution of true log fold changes across differential taxa, serving as a non-informative choice that avoids favoring either small or large effects a priori. The max LFC can be easily estimated from real data while min LFC is set to 0 by default. When min LFC = max LFC, a fixed LFC is set for all differential taxa.

Differential setting: In some studies, the differential taxa could be rare and less abundant (e.g., pathogens) while in others the differential taxa could be common and abundant (e.g., some commensals). Apparently, the abundance level of the taxa associated with a condition of interest affects the statistical power. mPower allows the differential taxa to be relatively abundant (top 50%), relatively rare (bottom 50%) or random (default); the user can define the abundance flexibly by their own data characteristics. The direction of change for the differential taxa is also an important parameter, with the same direction of change leading to stronger compositional effects and less statistical power. mPower allows the differential taxa to have either the same direction of change or random direction of change.

Statistical tests used: For community-level testing, mPower uses distance-based multivariate analysis of variance methods. When the sample size is small (n < 200), PERMANOVA [34] is used. For larger sample sizes (n > 200), DMANOVA [35], a faster version of PERMANOVA based on asymptotics, is used to expedite computation. Both Bray-Curtis distance (abundance-based, default) and Jaccard distance (presence/absence-based) can be selected. For taxon-level testing, the differential abundance analysis tool LinDA [13] is used as the default. LinDA is based on a log-linear model with compositional bias correction and is shown to be similar to ANCOM-BC [11] in power but is computationally more efficient.

Commonly used parametric simulation models prone to generate unrealistic high power

To illustrate the problems of power assessment using parametric simulation models, we carry out a comparative analysis of the power of popular parametric simulation models, including Dirichlet Multinomial (DM), Negative Binomial (NB), Zero-inflated Negative Binomial (ZINB), and Zero-inflated Poisson (ZIP), against our semi-parametric simulation approach [6]. The parameters of all these models are estimated using the maximum likelihood approach based on the same real datasets. The R packages dirmult, MASS, and pscl are used to fit the models.

To maintain a consistent comparison between parametric and semi-parametric methods, we establish the same simulation settings for all methods. A binary covariate is generated with the same effect size (LFC = 1), and simulations are executed over a range of sample sizes (20, 40, 60 per group). We simulate 200 taxa, with 10% differential taxa, and an average sequencing depth of 10,000. Taxa with less than 10% prevalence are then removed, and associations between the covariate and taxa abundance are assessed using LinDA under its default setting. Subsequently, we calculate the true positive rate as the power measurement on the datasets generated by different models. Results are averaged over 100 iterations for each setting.

Based on Fig. 2, we observe that different parametric models produce wildly different power estimates. For parametric models without zero inflation (DM and NB), they generally demonstrate greater power compared to the semi-parametric approach for both human and environmental samples. For zero-inflated models (ZIP and ZINB), their power could be greater or less than the semi-parametric approach depending on the specific sample type. Parametric models, especially those without zero inflation, operate under restrictive assumptions about the data distributions, often generating data with less variability and/or zero inflation, compared to real data [6, 7]. As a result, it is easier to declare statistical significance for those differential taxa, leading to a higher power estimate. However, real microbiome data are intrinsically complex and highly variable, and they may not be adequately fitted by parametric models with few parameters. In contrast, the semi-parametric model is more flexible and does not make parametric assumptions about the data distributions. Consequently, semi-parametric simulations, which make fewer assumptions, are expected to provide a more realistic estimation of power in the context of microbiome studies.

Effect sizes estimated from real microbiome data

A reasonable effect size estimate is critical for realistic power analysis. The effect sizes can be estimated based on pilot data or previously published research. mPower also provides the estimated effect sizes from real microbiome studies [47], helping users set appropriate parameter values (Table 2). Two effect size metrics are considered: percentage of differential taxa, and max log2 fold change. We estimate these two parameters by performing genus-level differential abundance analysis using LinDA (linda function in MicrobiomeStat R package [13]) on several real data sets, excluding taxa with a prevalence of less than 10% and samples with total reads less than 1000. Potential confounders (e.g., BMI, age, sex) were adjusted based on the metadata we retrieved. Max log2 fold change was estimated as the maximum observed log2 fold change among all taxa tested (although the estimate may be upwardly biased due to the winner’s curse, the magnitude should be close to the truth), while the percentage of differential taxa was estimated based on the p value distribution (pi0est function in qvalue R package [48]). We showcase the distribution of all taxa’s estimated log2 fold changes between the compared groups in Fig. 3a, and the distribution of centered log-ratio (clr)-transformed abundance and relative abundance of significantly different taxa in Fig. 3b and c. This will help users better understand the magnitude and variability of the effect sizes.

Discussion

A limitation of the proposed framework lies in its reliance on reference datasets. Reference-based power estimation is most accurate when the reference and target populations are well matched in terms of host characteristics (e.g., age, health status), environmental context (e.g., geography, diet), sequencing technology (e.g., sequencing regions, V1-V2 vs. V3-V4), and data processing pipelines. However, no single reference dataset can fully represent the diversity of microbiome structures across distinct niches, such as pediatric populations, non-human hosts, or site-specific communities (e.g., oral or skin microbiomes). Differences in age, geography, diet, health status and even technical factors (e.g., sequencing regions) can substantially alter microbial community composition and variability, potentially leading to over- or underestimation of statistical power when mismatched references are used. Thus, power estimates should always be interpreted in the context of the chosen reference dataset, and careful selection of references that closely resemble the intended study population is essential for valid inference. To directly improve generalizability, we have extended mPower by introducing a parametric simulation framework that complements the original semi-parametric approach. This extension allows users to upload their own reference datasets, from which simulated samples are generated. The parametric strategy is based on the Bimodal Dirichlet Distribution (BMDD) [33]. BMDD is specifically designed for zero-inflated microbiome compositional data and captures sparsity, bimodality, and complex abundance patterns. With nearly three times as many parameters as the Dirichlet distribution, it offers substantially enhanced modeling flexibility. While parametric simulation has inherent limitations, it provides a critical complementary option for under-studied niches, non-human hosts, and shotgun metagenomics studies. Users can supply pilot or closely related datasets to ensure that simulated data reflect the anticipated characteristics of their target study.

In microbiome studies, multiple microbial features are often associated with a variable of interest, giving rise to a distribution of effect sizes (e.g., log fold changes, LFCs) rather than a single common effect size, as implicitly assumed in traditional single-hypothesis testing frameworks. The assumption that all differential taxa share an identical effect size is generally unrealistic. Although summarizing effects by a median value may be intuitive, it is insufficient for power analysis, because statistical power depends not only on the central tendency of effect sizes but also on their dispersion. For example, we considered two scenarios in which effect sizes were drawn from Uniform(0, 2) and Uniform(1, 1), respectively. Although both distributions share the same median effect size of 1, they yield substantially different power (Fig. S2). This demonstrates that ignoring variability in effect sizes can lead to misleading power estimates. Accordingly, mPower models effect sizes as a distribution rather than a single value. By default, we assume a uniform distribution over a user-specified range [min LFC, max LFC], allowing both small and large effects to be represented. To simplify specification, the minimum LFC is set to zero by default, and users vary the maximum LFC. Together, these results highlight that realistic power analysis in microbiome studies requires modeling not only the typical magnitude of effects, but also their heterogeneity across taxa.

Effect sizes in microbiome studies are inherently disease- and context-dependent, as the magnitude and structure of microbiome differences between healthy individuals and patients can vary substantially across diseases and clinical settings. To obtain realistic effect size estimates for differential taxa, we recommend deriving these quantities from datasets collected under the same or closely related biological and experimental conditions as the planned study. Specifically, pilot data from the target population or publicly available datasets with similar disease status, body site, sequencing platform, and preprocessing pipelines can be used to estimate taxon-specific abundance shifts. These empirically derived effect sizes provide more accurate inputs for power analysis than generic assumptions. When suitable datasets are unavailable, mPower supports study planning by enabling power evaluation under realistic effect size ranges commonly observed in related diseases or comparable microbiome contexts, rather than assuming a universal effect size. This reference-driven strategy aligns with standard practices in power analysis and provides a practical framework for accommodating disease-specific heterogeneity while maintaining flexibility in study design.

Conclusions

In this study, we introduce mPower, a specialized tool designed to enhance the accuracy and flexibility of power analysis in microbiome research. By leveraging a semi-parametric simulation framework, mPower more realistically captures the complexities of microbiome data compared to traditional parametric approaches. It integrates LinDA for taxon-level analysis and DMANOVA/PERMANOVA for community-level analysis, ensuring robust and reliable differential abundance detection. As methods of differential abundance testing are evolving, mPower can be updated to incorporate the most state-of-the-art method.

Key features of mPower include a user-friendly web interface, support for diverse experimental designs (e.g., case-control, cross-sectional, and matched-pair studies), and the ability to perform comprehensive power calculations at both the community and taxon levels. Unlike conventional parametric models, which often oversimplify microbiome data and yield overly optimistic power estimates, mPower provides more realistic and data-driven power assessments by incorporating real microbiome datasets.

Overall, mPower addresses a critical gap in microbiome research, offering a practical, flexible, and statistically rigorous solution for power estimation. By accommodating varied study designs, complex microbial structures, and confounding factors, mPower serves as a useful tool for researchers aiming to design well-powered microbiome studies with greater confidence and precision.
