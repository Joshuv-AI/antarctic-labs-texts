A robust strategy for FE model updating of composite panels using a combined ANN-SMA surrogate-assisted optimization framework
Authors: Mohsen Kouhi, Alireza Mojtahedi, Mohammad Ali Lotfollahi-Yaghin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents a novel hybrid surrogate-assisted optimization framework for finite element (FE) model updating of composite panels. Accurately updated FE models offer a reliable basis for design and analysis of modern structural systems and are widely employed in various industrial and engineering applications. However, model updating of composite structures remains a significant challenge due to their inherent anisotropy and complex mechanical behavior, which often limit the effectiveness of conventional updating techniques. To address these limitations, a hybrid framework is introduced for the first time, combining surrogate modeling with the Slime Mould Algorithm (SMA), a recently developed metaheuristic optimizer. For the proposed model updating strategy, a baseline FE model was initially developed, and an objective function was defined to quantify discrepancies between numerical predictions and experimental measurements. The baseline model generated training data for surrogate models constructed using Response Surface Methodology (RSM), Kriging (KRG), and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN). Among these, the ANN-based surrogates achieved superior predictive accuracy and were selected for the optimization phase. Sensitivity analysis identified the most influential parameters for model updating. Optimization was then carried out to minimize the objective function using the SMA. The resulting ANN–SMA framework substantially reduced discrepancies between experimental and numerical modal responses, yielding a high-fidelity FE model. Comparative assessments against established metaheuristic algorithms, including Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) and Genetic Algorithm (GA), both integrated with ANN surrogates, revealed that the ANN-SMA framework exhibited enhanced convergence stability and computational efficiency, with reductions in runtime of approximately 7% and 30% relative to ANN-PSO and ANN-GA, respectively. These findings confirm that the proposed ANN–SMA hybrid framework provides a robust, efficient, and reliable tool for precise FE model updating of composite structures, with significant potential for broader applications in structural dynamics and structural health monitoring (SHM).

Introduction

Finite element (FE) modeling has become an essential tool in the design, analysis, and performance evaluation of modern engineering structures, enabling precise simulations of complex behaviors under various loading conditions1,2. Nevertheless, the reliability of FE predictions strongly depends on the accuracy of input parameters, including material properties, boundary conditions, and geometric characteristics, which are frequently influenced by inherent uncertainties3. In composite structures, these uncertainties are further compounded by their anisotropic nature, heterogeneous microstructure, and variabilities arising from manufacturing processes4,5. Even minor deviations in these factors can lead to substantial divergences between numerical simulations and experimental observations, especially in dynamic analysis. Moreover, the widespread use of composite panel-type components in many conventional and advanced engineering systems, and the need for precise evaluation of their structural behavior, further amplifies these challenges6,7. Consequently, these factors underscore the necessity of robust model updating procedures to ensure close alignment between FE predictions and actual structural performance.

FE model updating is an inverse engineering method that adjusts uncertain parameters to reduce differences between experimental and simulated responses8,9. The updated model can be employed to more accurately assess the structural behavior or to establish a robust damage detection strategy, which is a critical aspect in structural health monitoring (SHM)10,11. Successful FE model updating relies on a structured methodology that comprises three fundamental components. First, formulation of an objective function that quantitatively characterizes the discrepancy between model-generated and experimentally observed responses12. Second, strategic selection of updating parameters, ideally concentrating on those parameters that are most influential on the model’s dynamic or structural behavior, while simultaneously ensuring appropriate identifiability and mitigating potential ill-posedness within the optimization framework13. Finally, implementation of a robust and computationally efficient optimization algorithm to systematically explore the parameter space, minimizing the objective function, and ultimately achieving reliable correspondence between the FE model and the target experimental dataset14. Selection of an appropriate optimization strategy is of great importance, as it directly influences the convergence rate, computational cost, and the ability to avoid local minima, especially in complex, nonlinear, and multimodal search spaces15,16. In recent years, nature-inspired metaheuristic algorithms have gained significant adoption in FE model updating applications17. Among various metaheuristic algorithms, Genetic Algorithm (GA)18, Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)19, and Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO)20 are frequently employed in FE model updating. However, despite their effectiveness, these methods often involve high computational demands, as each iteration requires multiple FE model evaluations. This computational burden becomes particularly significant for models incorporating nonlinear material behavior, complex boundary conditions, or fine FE meshes, making the selection and tuning of the optimization algorithm a critical factor in FE model updating applications17.

A practical approach for mitigating these challenges is the use of surrogate or metamodeling techniques to approximate the FE response while requiring substantially fewer simulations21,22. These models provide an explicit representation of the input–output relationships, and when they are well-trained and integrated with a robust optimization algorithm, they can significantly reduce the computational cost associated with FE model updating while offering high accuracy. The integration of surrogate modeling with metaheuristic optimization algorithms has gained prominence in recent years as an effective strategy to reduce the computational cost in FE model updating, particularly for complex structures where direct evaluations are prohibitively expensive. Qin et al.23 employed a Kriging (KRG) surrogate model combined with PSO to update the dynamic model of bridge structures based on higher-order vibration modes. He et al.24 coupled surrogate models with metaheuristic optimization to update continuous bridge FE models, accurately detecting scour depths by minimizing modal discrepancies. Fatahi25 combined surrogate models with multi-objective GA for model updating of welded plates, achieving convergence faster while identifying dominant parameters through response surface approximations. Schnellenbach-Held et al.26 employed a hybrid approach combining kriging surrogate models and GA for model updating of prestressed concrete bridges, reducing frequency errors under complex loading via iterative surrogate refinement. Jiménez-Alonso et al.27 optimized Artificial Neural Network (ANN) models’ hyperparameters when they are used for the surrogate-assisted model updating of civil engineering structures using Bayesian optimization. Kamali et al.28 developed inverse surrogate models coupled with the Random Forest optimization algorithm for deterministic FE model updating. Yang et al.29 applied Kriging surrogate models with the efficient global optimization (EGO) algorithm for model updating. Zhou et al.30 integrated Response Surface Methodology (RSM) surrogate models with the Nutcracker Optimization Algorithm (NOA) for model updating of a Continuous Beam-Arch Composite Bridge. Wang et al.31 used a multi-quadratic radial basis function (RBF) for surrogate modeling and integrated it with GA for model updating of radio telescope antennas, achieving lower modal discrepancies. He et al.32 proposed a Wavelet Neural Network (WNN) surrogate trained via gradient descent, paired with the Wind-Driven Optimization (WDO) algorithm, for updating a continuous beam structure.

This study introduces a novel hybrid framework for vibration-based FE model updating of laminated composite panels, integrating ANN-based surrogate modeling with the Slime Mould Algorithm (SMA). Inspired by the adaptive foraging behavior of slime moulds, SMA employs a dynamic weighting strategy to balance exploration and exploitation, enabling faster and more reliable convergence compared with conventional metaheuristic algorithms such as PSO and GA. However, its application to FE model updating, particularly for composite structures, remains largely unexplored. Experimental modal analysis (EMA) was conducted to obtain reference natural frequencies and mode shapes of the composite panel. A detailed baseline FE model was developed in Abaqus, and a weighted objective function was established to systematically quantify the mismatch between simulated modal responses and experimental observations. The baseline model was then used to generate training data for the surrogate models, with Sobol sequence sampling ensuring a well-distributed representation of the input parameter space. Subsequently, a global sensitivity analysis based on Sobol indices was performed to identify the parameters with the greatest influence on the modal responses. These key parameters were then updated through the proposed ANN–SMA optimization by minimizing the defined objective function. The performance of the proposed ANN-SMA approach was compared with the well-established PSO and GA using the same ANN surrogate models to ensure a consistent and reliable comparison. Results demonstrated that SMA achieves faster convergence, higher prediction accuracy, and greater computational efficiency. Overall, this study shows that combining ANN-based surrogates with adaptive metaheuristic SMA provides a powerful and efficient tool for FE model updating. The developed framework lays a foundation for future applications in SHM, uncertainty analysis, and reliability-based design of advanced composite structures.

Composite structures present additional challenges due to their anisotropic and heterogeneous nature. Material and geometric properties exhibit considerable variability due to manufacturing variability, measurement noise, and modeling simplifications. These uncertainties can induce substantial shifts in modal responses because the dynamic behavior of laminated composites is highly sensitive to material and geometric properties38. Consequently, accurate model updating of composite structures requires careful selection of influential parameters and the use of efficient optimization techniques capable of navigating complex, nonlinear response surfaces.

Experimental program

The experimental program involved performing vibration tests and modal analysis on a fabricated composite panel to determine its dynamic characteristics. EMA was employed to extract the structure’s modal parameters, including natural frequencies and mode shapes, under controlled excitation and measurement conditions. In this study, the physical model of the composite panel was manufactured, and its experimentally measured modal parameters served as the benchmark for subsequent FE model updating.

Constituent materials and fabrication of the physical model

The physical model of the composite panel, investigated in this study, consisted of woven E-glass fibers embedded within an epoxy resin matrix. Fabrication was performed using ten plies of plain-weave E-glass fabric, each with a nominal thickness of 0.20 mm, serving as the reinforcement phase. The matrix phase was prepared by mixing epoxy resin and hardener at a weight ratio of 7:3. The laminate employed a symmetric stacking sequence of [0/90]10​, yielding a nominal overall thickness of 2 mm and ensuring a theoretically uniform cross-section across the entire panel surface. Fabrication was conducted in the Composite Laboratory of the University of Tabriz using a manual hand lay-up technique followed by vacuum bagging. The lay-up process was performed on a flat glass mold surface pretreated with a release agent to prevent adhesion. Each ply was carefully positioned and impregnated with resin to ensure full fiber wet-out and minimal entrapped air. The whole layup set was then covered with a perforated release film, followed by a breather fabric to facilitate uniform vacuum distribution and absorb excess resin. The assembly was sealed within a vacuum bag and subjected to a vacuum pressure for 36 h at ambient laboratory conditions.

The cured laminate was then removed from the mold and trimmed to the final dimensions of 800 × 750 mm using a precision waterjet cutting system, ensuring dimensional tolerances within ± 0.015 mm. Visual inspection was performed to ensure the absence of voids, delaminations, or surface defects before experimental testing.

EMA setup and instrumentation

EMA is a widely used technique for identifying the dynamic characteristics of a structure, including its natural frequencies, mode shapes, and damping ratios39. In EMA, controlled excitation is applied to a structure while its vibration response is measured using sensors like accelerometers or laser vibrometers. The measured input–output data are then processed to derive the structure’s modal parameters. These parameters are essential for validating and updating analytical models, diagnosing structural problems, and improving design performance40.

To determine the modal parameters of the composite panel, EMA was performed at the Vibration and Modal Analysis Laboratory of the University of Tabriz. The panel was tested under pinned boundary conditions. A roving hammer test setup was utilized, where excitation was applied with an instrumented impact hammer (Brüel & Kjær, Type 8202) at multiple points on the panel. The corresponding acceleration responses were measured at predetermined locations using contact accelerometers (Brüel & Kjær, Type 4500). The dynamic signals from the hammer and accelerometers were acquired using a two-channel data acquisition (DAQ) system (Brüel & Kjær, Type 3560 C). The acquired time-history data were processed in Pulse Labshop software to compute the frequency response functions (FRFs) for each excitation point. These FRFs were subsequently imported into ME’scopeVES software for post-processing and modal parameter estimation, which included the identification of natural frequencies and the extraction of corresponding mode shapes for the composite panel. A schematic of the EMA setup is presented in Fig. 1, with the instrumentation detailed in Fig. 2.

Response measurement

Prior to testing, the panel surface was meshed into a spatial measurement grid, as illustrated in Fig. 3, to define the excitation points and sensor locations with precision, and to achieve an adequate spatial resolution of global structural mode shapes. Two accelerometers were employed for vibration measurements. One accelerometer served as a fixed reference, while the second was roved among the predefined measurement points. In each measurement set, the reference accelerometer was also relocated to different positions to improve modal observability. This multi-reference strategy was adopted to enhance phase consistency, improve the identification of weakly excited modes, and increase the robustness of mode shape estimation.

FRFs were computed by transforming the measured time-domain excitation and response signals into the frequency domain using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). The FRFs were calculated as the ratio of the acceleration response spectrum to the corresponding hammer force spectrum, with multiple impact averages and Hanning windowing applied to minimize noise and spectral leakage. Overlaid FRFs from all measurement points are presented in Fig. 5, illustrating the dynamic response characteristics of the composite panel. Modal parameter estimation was then performed using a two-step procedure. First, the Complex Mode Indicator Function (CMIF) was applied to identify modal frequencies through singular value decomposition of the FRF matrix. Subsequently, the Global Polynomial Method (GPM) was employed for global curve fitting across the frequency band of interest, yielding accurate estimates of natural frequencies and mode shapes.

Baseline FE model development

FE model is a numerical representation of a physical structure used to simulate its mechanical behavior under various loading and boundary conditions. By dividing the structure into smaller, interconnected elements, the FE model allows complex geometry, material properties, and interactions to be accurately captured. This approach enables engineers to predict responses such as stress, deformation, and vibration with high precision41,42.

A baseline FE model of the composite panel was developed via Abaqus software to serve as the reference for subsequent model updating procedures. The panel geometry (800 × 750 × 2 mm), consistent with the fabricated physical model, was defined using eight-node layered shell elements (S8R) to capture the orthotropic behavior of the laminate. The plate theory employed was the first-order shear deformation theory (FSDT). FSDT accounts for transverse shear deformation and is widely used for laminated composite plates, where shear effects are non-negligible due to the moderate thickness and anisotropic stiffness of the laminate. This formulation provides an accurate representation of bending–shear coupling and is well-suited for vibration analysis of composite structures43. Modal parameter comparison and objective function formulation

To quantitatively assess the agreement between the numerical and experimental modal parameters, both frequency comparison and modal correlation analysis were performed. Mode shape correlation was evaluated using the Modal Assurance Criterion (MAC) defined as:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{MAC}}_{ij}=\frac{\mid\:{\phi\:}_{i}^{{\mathrm{exp}}^{T}}{\phi\:}_{j}^{\mathrm{FE}}{\mid\:}^{2}}{\left({\phi\:}_{i}^{{\mathrm{exp}}^{T}}{\phi\:}_{i}^{\mathrm{exp}}\right)\left({\phi\:}_{j}^{{\mathrm{FE}}^{T}}{\phi\:}_{j}^{\mathrm{FE}}\right)}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\phi\:}_{i}^{\mathrm{exp}}$$\end{document}and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\phi\:}_{j}^{\mathrm{FE}}$$\end{document} denote the experimental and numerical mode shape vectors, respectively. The experimental mode shapes were extracted in ME’scopeVES and defined at response degrees of freedom (DOFs) corresponding to the accelerometer measurement points on the composite panel (as shown in Fig. 3). Acceleration responses were measured in the out-of-plane translational direction (normal to the panel surface). Consequently, each experimental mode shape vector was defined by one translational DOF per measurement point, and its dimension equals the total number of measurement locations used in the modal identification process after assembling all roving-sensor data into a global mode shape. To ensure consistency in the MAC computation, for each experimental measurement point, the out-of-plane displacement component of the corresponding FE node was extracted, establishing a one-to-one correspondence with the experimental data. As eigenvectors derived from eigenvalue analysis are inherently arbitrary in scale and sign, both the experimental and numerical mode shape vectors were normalized before correlation. The MAC was subsequently computed to provide a quantitative measure of the degree of similarity between the corresponding mode shapes from experiment and simulation. Figure 8 presents the MAC values obtained from the comparison of experimental and numerical mode shapes. A MAC value approaching unity indicates a high degree of correlation between the two mode shapes, while lower values signify modal inconsistency or spatial mismatch.

To enhance the predictive accuracy of the FE model, the updating process was formulated to minimize the discrepancies between the experimental and numerical modal parameters. This requires defining an objective function that quantitatively characterizes these differences, as the choice of objective function plays a critical role in guiding the optimization toward physically meaningful and reliable model updates. Directly minimizing these kinds of objective functions with repeated FE analyses is computationally demanding. To mitigate this burden and enable efficient optimization, a surrogate modeling framework was introduced to approximate the FE responses and accelerate the model updating process.

Surrogate modeling and dataset generation

FE simulations of composite structures can be very computationally expensive, particularly when numerous model evaluations are required for optimization, sensitivity analysis, or model-updating processes45. To establish a computationally efficient predictive framework, surrogate modeling techniques were employed to accurately represent the complex, nonlinear functional relationships between input parameters and the system’s modal responses.

A surrogate model provides a black-box approximation of the primary simulation model’s response, expressed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:y\left(\mathbf{x}\right)$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{x}$$\end{document} represents the vector of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:m$$\end{document} design parameters, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{x}=[{x}_{1},\dots\:,{x}_{m}{]}^{T}$$\end{document}. The construction of such a model necessitates a well-defined training dataset, consisting of a design site matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{X}=[{\mathbf{x}}_{1};\dots\:{\hspace{0.17em}};{\mathbf{x}}_{N}]$$\end{document} of dimensions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N\times\:m$$\end{document} and the corresponding observation vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{Y}=[{y}_{1};\dots\:{\hspace{0.17em}};{y}_{N}{]}^{T}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{i}$$\end{document} denotes the system’s responses to the input vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{x}}_{i}$$\end{document}. In this study, the outputs include the natural frequencies and the MAC values for the first five vibration modes. Consequently, independent surrogate models were developed for each frequency and MAC value.

Three surrogate modeling approaches, based on RSM, KRG, and ANN, were rigorously developed to establish functional mapping between the input parameters and the target modal responses of the composite panel. The input parameters incorporated both geometric characteristics and material properties, as outlined in Table 3. The entire training and validation dataset, which defines the design site matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{X}$$\end{document} and the corresponding observation vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{Y}$$\end{document}, was systematically generated using a Design of Experiments (DoE) framework based on Sobol sequence sampling.

The quality of the surrogate models depends heavily on the quality of the design space sampling46. To ensure reliable surrogate model construction, Sobol sampling was employed to draw random samples uniformly from the design space. Sobol sequence sampling is a quasi-random, low-discrepancy method that efficiently fills a multi-dimensional space with minimal clustering or overlap between samples47. Unlike traditional sampling methods, which can result in uneven distributions with large gaps in the parameter domain, Sobol sampling ensures that each newly generated point enhances the overall uniformity of the sample set48. This characteristic makes Sobol sampling especially suitable for high-dimensional surrogate modeling, where accurate and efficient exploration of the input space is essential.

A total of 512 Sobol samples were generated to construct and validate the surrogate models. The adequacy of this sample size was verified through a convergence study, as later demonstrated in Fig. 9, which ensured that the surrogate predictions stabilized with respect to increasing training data. The upper and lower bounds for each parameter were defined as ± 15% of their corresponding nominal values. The resulting Sobol-based DoE dataset formed the foundation for training and evaluating the surrogate models. In the subsequent sections, the formulation, configuration, and implementation procedures for the three surrogate modeling techniques are presented in detail. Their comparative performance in predicting the modal characteristics of the composite panel is also assessed.

RSM

RSM is a statistical modeling approach that approximates the response of a system as a polynomial function of its input variables. It is particularly effective for problems with relatively smooth and continuous parameter-response relationships47. In this study, quadratic RSM models were developed for surrogate modeling. where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:y\left(\mathbf{x}\right)$$\end{document} is the predicted response, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{x}}_{i}$$\end{document} are the input parameters, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\gamma\:}_{i}$$\end{document} are regression coefficients estimated using least-squares fitting, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\epsilon\:$$\end{document} represents the residual error.

KRG

Kriging, also known as Gaussian Process Regression (GPR), is a stochastic interpolation method that models the response of a system as a realization of a Gaussian random process. Unlike polynomial-based models, Kriging captures both global trends and local deviations by defining a correlation structure among data points50. ANN

ANNs are powerful data-driven models inspired by the structure of the human brain. They are capable of approximating highly nonlinear mappings between inputs and outputs without requiring prior assumptions about the functional form of the relationship51,52.

In this study, a feedforward neural network was employed to model the relationship between the design parameters and the modal responses. Both input and output variables were normalized to a consistent numerical range to improve training stability and ensure all variables contributed equally to the learning process. The network architecture consisted of one hidden layer comprising 10 neurons, which was selected empirically through preliminary testing to achieve an appropriate balance between approximation capability and generalization performance. Hyperbolic tangent sigmoid (tansig) activation functions were used in the hidden layer, and the network parameters were optimized using a gradient-based algorithm. Network training was carried out using the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm, which provides rapid convergence for medium-sized problems. The training objective was defined as the minimization of the mean squared error (MSE) between the predicted and reference modal responses. To prevent overfitting, the dataset was randomly divided into training (70%), validation (15%), and testing (15%) subsets. The validation set was used to implement early stopping, whereby training was stopped if the validation error did not improve over a predefined number of iterations, ensuring that the network maintained generalization capability while avoiding overfitting.

Specifically, surrogate models were constructed using incrementally larger subsets of the dataset, ranging from 64 to 512 samples, and their predictive performance was assessed on a separate validation set. The normalized root mean squared error (NRMSE) was used as the quantitative metric to measure the deviation between the surrogate model predictions and the reference FE responses.

As shown in Fig. 9, the learning curves consistently demonstrated a steady decrease in NRMSE with increasing training sample size for all three surrogate modeling approaches. Notably, the rate of improvement in predictive accuracy diminished beyond 256 samples, with only marginal reductions in NRMSE observed when the dataset was expanded to 512 samples. This behavior indicates that the models had effectively captured the underlying input–output relationships, and additional samples provided limited incremental benefit.

Among the three approaches, the ANN-based surrogate models exhibited superior predictive capability, consistently achieving R² values above 0.97 for most modes in estimating both frequencies and MAC values. The KRG-based surrogate models yielded comparably accurate results, while the RSM-based surrogate models demonstrated relatively lower accuracy due to their limited ability to capture the complex nonlinear relationships inherent in the composite structure’s dynamic behavior. In terms of computational cost, the training times required to construct the RSM-, KRG-, and ANN-based surrogate models for estimating the natural frequencies across all modes were 1.48 s, 13.84 s, and 6.86 s, respectively. For the estimation of MAC values, the corresponding training times were 3.67 s, 36.11 s, and 10.83 s. These results indicate that the ANN-based surrogate models establish an effective balance between high predictive accuracy and moderate training time, making them well-suited for subsequent sensitivity analysis and model updating.

A comparison of the surrogate models in terms of computational efficiency further highlights their advantages over direct FE analysis. The average computational time required to perform a single modal analysis of the composite panel using the FE model was approximately 13 s. In contrast, the ANN-based surrogate models required approximately 0.3 s and 0.4 s to evaluate the natural frequencies and MAC values, respectively. This substantial reduction in computational time provides a highly efficient and cost-effective alternative to FE-based evaluations, particularly in scenarios involving a large number of function evaluations, such as sensitivity analyses and optimization procedures.

Global sensitivity analysis and parameter selection

Following the development of the surrogate models, a global sensitivity analysis was performed to identify the parameters that most significantly influence the modal responses of the composite panel. Sensitivity analysis constitutes a critical stage in FE model updating, as it enables the systematic exclusion of parameters with negligible effects on structural responses53. This reduction enhances computational efficiency and mitigates numerical ill-conditioning and parameter correlation, resulting in a more stable and physically interpretable updating process13.

In this study, a variance-based Sobol sensitivity analysis was employed to quantify the contribution of each input parameter to the variability of the modal responses. The analysis was implemented using the ANN-based surrogate models, which provide a smooth and computationally efficient approximation of the FE response surface. FE model updating using ANN-SMA

As demonstrated in the previous section, the MAC values were found to be relatively insensitive to the model parameters, indicating that discrepancies in natural frequencies primarily govern the updating process. Therefore, the MAC values were not directly included in the objective function but were instead used as weighting factors to prioritize modes with stronger experimental–numerical correlation. This weighting-based incorporation of MAC values has been employed in earlier model-updating studies44,55. The final form of the objective function was thus simplified to:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:J=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}{MAC}_{i}{\left(\frac{{f}_{i}^{Exp}-{f}_{i}^{Surr}}{{f}_{i}^{Exp}}\right)}^{2}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{i}^{Surr}$$\end{document} represents the frequencies calculated by the trained surrogate models. Based on the global sensitivity analysis results, four parameters were identified as most influential on the frequency response including, Thickness (t), Density (ρ), Transverse Elastic modulus (E₂), and Longitudinal Elastic modulus (E₁). These parameters were selected as the design variables for the model updating process.

Due to the nonlinear and multimodal nature of this optimization problem, a global metaheuristic optimization algorithm was employed. Metaheuristic algorithms are particularly well-suited for FE model updating problems, as they can effectively explore high-dimensional parameter spaces without requiring derivative information56. The optimization procedure iteratively updates the parameter vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{x}$$\end{document}, evaluates the FE model responses (or their surrogate model approximations), and minimizes the objective function until convergence. This yields an updated FE model that more accurately represents the structural dynamics of the composite panel. This study proposes a hybrid framework combining ANN-based surrogate modeling with SMA optimization for efficient and robust updating of the composite panel FE model.

Slime mould algorithm

The SMA is a nature-inspired metaheuristic optimization technique introduced by Li et al.57, drawing from the foraging behavior of slime moulds (Physarum polycephalum), a unicellular organism known for its remarkable ability to find the shortest path to food sources. By simulating the oscillatory propagation and adaptive network formation observed in slime mould, SMA efficiently balances exploration and exploitation in complex search spaces58. The algorithm models the dynamic expansion and contraction of the slime mould’s tubular network, which corresponds to the updating of candidate solutions toward optimal regions. In the following subsections, the key processes and mechanisms underlying the algorithm are described and mathematically modeled.

Tuning and selection of SMA control parameters

In metaheuristic algorithms, the selection of parameters is typically informed by existing literature and preliminary numerical experimentation. For SMA, appropriate tuning of the control parameters is essential, as these settings directly influence the balance between exploration and exploitation, the smoothness of convergence, and the overall computational efficiency of the surrogate-based FE model updating process. In this study, SMA parameters were chosen to achieve a compromise between convergence speed and numerical stability when optimizing the ANN-based surrogate model. The population size was set to 20, and the maximum number of iterations was fixed at 100, ensuring adequate search diversity while maintaining reasonable computational effort. The value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:z$$\end{document} was considered 0.03, adopted as recommended by Li et al.54, who reported it to yield consistently strong performance across benchmark optimization problems.

These improvements indicate that the ANN surrogate models successfully captured the nonlinear parameter–response relationships, while the SMA efficiently explored the multidimensional design space to identify globally optimal parameter sets.

When MAC was excluded from the objective function, the resulting updated frequencies remained comparable, demonstrating that frequency-based updating alone can recover much of the dynamic behavior of the structure. However, a consistent increase in frequency error was observed, especially in higher modes. This indicates that, although MAC exhibited low numerical sensitivity during optimization, its inclusion in the objective function provides an important regularizing effect by enforcing consistency in the spatial characteristics of the mode shapes. Consequently, MAC contributes to a more balanced updating process, improving the reliability of the solution without significantly altering convergence behavior. These findings confirm that MAC does not dominate the optimization process but instead acts as a complementary constraint that enhances modal fidelity. Therefore, its inclusion in the proposed ANN–SMA framework is justified, particularly for ensuring physically meaningful updates when multiple modes with close frequencies are involved.

Despite the overall improvement achieved through the updating process, the frequency error associated with the fifth vibration mode remains relatively higher than that of the lower modes. This behavior can be understood by considering the fundamental differences in how lower- and higher-order modes respond to structural properties. Lower-order vibration modes are predominantly governed by the global mass and stiffness characteristics of the structure and therefore exhibit high sensitivity to variations in global material and geometric parameters. In contrast, higher-order modes are increasingly influenced by localized stiffness variations, spatial gradients in material properties, and modeling idealizations, effects that are not fully captured when the updating process is restricted to a limited set of global parameters59,60.

In addition, experimental uncertainties in modal identification generally increase with frequency. Higher modes tend to exhibit lower signal-to-noise ratios, increased modal density, and greater susceptibility to measurement noise and sensor placement effects, all of which reduce the accuracy of experimentally identified frequencies and mode shapes61.

Importantly, the ANN–SMA framework consistently achieved superior accuracy for the dominant lower modes that govern the global dynamic response of the composite panel, while still providing a meaningful reduction in the fifth-mode error relative to the baseline model. This confirms that the proposed approach effectively captures lower-order modes, which are typically of greater practical relevance in structural dynamics and health monitoring applications62,63, while the remaining deviation in the highest mode reflects inherent modeling and experimental limitations rather than shortcomings of the optimization strategy.

While the baseline FE model captured the overall dynamic behavior of the composite panel, noticeable discrepancies in the predicted natural frequencies indicated the need for systematic parameter refinement. The updating process therefore aimed to identify a set of physically admissible parameters that minimizes these discrepancies while preserving the underlying structural representation of the composite panel. The resulting parameter changes reflect the sensitivity of the measured modal response to variations in stiffness, mass, and geometry, and provide insight into the effective properties governing the global dynamic behavior of the structure. These modifications collectively contributed to aligning the updated FE model more closely with the actual structural dynamics observed experimentally.

Among the updated parameters, the elastic moduli \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{1}$$\end{document}and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{2}$$\end{document}exhibit the most noticeable adjustments, with reductions on the order of approximately 9–10%. These changes suggest that the baseline stiffness assumptions were moderately overestimated with respect to the effective structural response identified from the experimental modal data. It is important to emphasize that the updated elastic constants represent equivalent or effective stiffness properties inferred through inverse vibration-based model updating, rather than direct measurements of constituent-level material properties. As such, they inherently account for the combined effects of material variability, laminate homogenization assumptions, experimental uncertainties, and modeling idealizations, including boundary condition approximations.

Similarly, the modest reduction in material density (approximately − 1.4%) and the slight increase in laminate thickness (approximately + 0.7%) can be interpreted as compensatory adjustments that improve the global mass–stiffness balance of the numerical model. Collectively, these parameter refinements led to a numerically stable and physically consistent updated FE model that reproduces the experimentally observed structural dynamics with substantially improved accuracy, while remaining within realistic bounds of expected variability.

While the updated elastic moduli provide valuable insight into the effective stiffness governing the global dynamic behavior of the laminate, it is emphasized that inverse modal identification alone cannot isolate the contribution of specific manufacturing-related mechanisms. Direct attribution of the observed stiffness reduction to particular microstructural features would require complementary experimental evidence, such as microscopic inspection, fiber volume fraction measurements, void content analysis, or detailed quality control data. Such characterization techniques can provide direct information on laminate architecture and manufacturing quality. Consequently, the updated elastic properties should be interpreted as equivalent parameters that best reproduce the measured modal response at the structural scale, rather than as indicators of specific material defects.

Overall, the results validate the ANN–SMA framework as an efficient and reliable approach for FE model updating of the composite panel. The updated model provides a refined and physically meaningful representation of the actual composite panel, ensuring enhanced reliability for subsequent structural analysis, optimization, and SHM applications.

The construction of ANN-based surrogate models required 6.86 s for natural frequency prediction and 10.83 s for MAC value prediction, as reported in Sect. 6.4. Using the trained ANN surrogates, the global sensitivity analyses were completed within approximately 1.0 s for frequency sensitivities and 1.2 s for MAC sensitivities. The model updating stage based on the proposed ANN-SMA framework needed an average of 24.3 s, as discussed previously. These results demonstrate that the proposed framework significantly reduces the online computational burden by replacing repeated FE evaluations with efficient ANN-based surrogate predictions, while retaining high accuracy in model updating.

Conclusion

This study developed and evaluated a vibration-based FE model updating framework for laminated composite panels by integrating surrogate modeling with a metaheuristic optimization strategy. Experimental modal data were combined with a baseline FE model to formulate a weighted objective function based on frequency and mode-shape discrepancies. To improve computational efficiency, several surrogate models were evaluated, and the most accurate model was subsequently employed for global sensitivity analysis and parameter updating using the SMA to achieve optimized agreement between experimental and numerical modal characteristics.

Among the evaluated surrogate models, ANN-based surrogates demonstrated the superior predictive accuracy for both natural frequencies and MAC values, achieving coefficients of determination exceeding 0.95 across all investigated vibration modes. Global sensitivity analysis identified panel thickness, density, and longitudinal and transverse elastic moduli as the dominant parameters governing the dynamic response, while MAC values exhibited low sensitivity to parameter variations, enabling effective dimensionality reduction prior to optimization.

The ANN–SMA framework reduced natural frequency prediction errors from 4.19 to 11.13% in the baseline FE model to 0.01–5.64% after updating, with errors below 1% achieved for the first four modes. Incorporating MAC values into the objective function did not significantly alter convergence behavior but consistently improved higher-mode accuracy and ensured spatial consistency of the updated mode shapes, leading to more physically meaningful solutions compared with frequency-only updating.

Comparative evaluation against ANN–PSO and ANN–GA demonstrated that the ANN–SMA framework achieved superior updating performance with high robustness and efficiency. Based on statistical analysis, ANN–SMA achieved the lowest mean objective function value and mean frequency error of 1.42% across the first five vibration modes with the smallest standard deviations. Moreover, ANN–SMA exhibited superior computational efficiency, requiring an average runtime of 24.3 s, which corresponds to approximately 7% and 30% reductions in computational cost relative to ANN–PSO and ANN–GA, respectively.

The present study was limited to flat laminated composite panels with simple boundary conditions and modal data obtained under controlled laboratory conditions. Environmental effects such as temperature and humidity were not considered, although these factors are known to influence matrix-dominated stiffness, mass density, and damping properties of composite laminates, potentially leading to shifts in natural frequencies and modal characteristics. Future work will extend the proposed framework to more complex geometries, realistic boundary conditions, and coupled environmental effects, while exploring advanced surrogate modeling strategies, including physics-informed neural networks, to further enhance predictive accuracy and computational efficiency. The extension of the framework toward real-time FE model updating for structural health monitoring applications also represents a promising research direction.
