Cost Effectiveness of Recombinant Zoster Vaccine and Live-Attenuated Vaccine Against Herpes Zoster for Adults Aged 50 and Over in China
Authors: Lulu Liu, Qilin Zhang, Yamin Shu, Lei Wang, Zhenqingyun Shuai, Mingming Chu, Zhe Zhang, Rong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTwo vaccines against herpes zoster (HZ) are currently authorized for use in China: the adjuvanted recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV) and live-attenuated Zoster Vaccine Live (ZVL). The significant disparities in prices and efficacy between the two vaccines necessitate an evaluation of their relative value in order to make an informed choice. This study aimed to evaluate the comparative cost effectiveness of RZV, ZVL, and no vaccination for older adults at different ages from the societal perspective.MethodsThe Markov decision model was parameterized using vaccine efficacy data, the latest incidence rates, costs, and health-related quality-of-life data. Participants consisted of a cohort comprising adults aged ≥ 50 years in China. The lifetime health impacts, cost, life-year (LY), quality-adjusted life-year (QALY) gained and the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), net monetary benefits (NMB), net health benefits (NHB), and expected value of perfect information (EVPI) were calculated. Sensitivity analyses, scenario analyses, and subgroup analyses were performed.ResultsCompared with no vaccination, RZV and ZVL could avoid 2,009,292 versus 510,192 HZ cases, and 334,637 versus 90,996 post-herpetic neuralgia (PHN) cases in the overall cohort, respectively. RZV and ZVL were more costly by US$1624 million versus US$896 million compared with no vaccination, with an additional 58,180 versus 23,390 QALYs. At threefold gross domestic product per capita, RZV had cost-effectiveness probabilities of 47.37% for ages 50–59 years, 83.24% for ages 60–69 years, 28.28% for ages 70–79 years, and 51.93% for ages ≥ 80 years, while ZVL had 7.07%, 12.79%, 69.55%, 2.82% probability, and no vaccination had 45.56%, 3.97%, 2.17%, 45.25% probability of being cost effective for the corresponding age cohorts.ConclusionsThe strategy utilizing RZV demonstrated superior efficacy in reducing the burden of HZ compared with ZVL. RZV was most cost effective in the 50–59, 60–69 and ≥ 80 years age groups, and ZVL was most cost effective in the 70–79 years group.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s41669-025-00604-z.

Key Points for Decision Makers

Our analysis suggests that recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV) is most cost effective in adults aged 50–59, 60–69 and ≥ 80 years; live-attenuated zoster vaccine (ZVL) is most cost effective in those aged 70–79 years. In the 70–79 years group, ZVL is most cost effective due to its strong protection against postherpetic neuralgia (PHN).Vaccine price, waning of efficacy, and adherence to the second RZV dose are key factors influencing cost effectiveness. Including herpes zoster vaccines in immunization programs or reimbursement and improving awareness in older adults can enhance public health benefits and optimize resource use.The expected value of perfect information (EVPI) is highest in the 50–59 years group, indicating high value in reducing uncertainty in this population.

Introduction

Herpes zoster (HZ) is caused by the reactivation of latent varicella-zoster virus (VZV), often occurring in people aged 50 years or older [1]. Post-herpetic neuralgia (PHN) is the most common complication of HZ occurring in 5–30% of the patients [2, 3]. PHN is characterized by a burning sensation in the skin and nerves that can persist for months to several years after the rash and blisters of shingles have subsided. The risk for HZ and PHN increases with age and immunosuppression, which have been shown to adversely affect patients’ health-related quality of life [4, 5].

Epidemiologic findings suggested that the incidence rate of HZ was estimated between 5.23 and 10.9 cases per 1000 person-years, rising at an annual rate of 5%, with a lifetime risk of approximately 30% [4, 6]. HZ is reported to be higher among the elderly and females globally [7]. A previous study found the annual incidence of HZ ranged from 2.9 to 5.8 per 1000 person-years in Chinese adults aged ≥ 50 years [8]. The burden of HZ on the health care system in China is substantial with at least 2.77 million new cases annually, resulting in 59.99 disability-adjusted life years per 100,000 people, with the highest burden among people aged ≥ 50 years, which represents 34.5% of the total population [9, 10]. With limited treatments available, vaccination is the most effective way to prevent HZ and its associated complications.

The live-attenuated Zoster Vaccine Live (ZVL, Zostavax) against HZ was approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2011 for adults aged 50 years and older. Clinical trial data among older adults aged ≥ 60 years showed that a single dose of ZVL reduced HZ cases by 51% and PHN cases by 67% [11]. Further age-stratified clinical findings revealed that the efficacy against HZ was limited to 38% among adults aged ≥ 70 years. Additionally, a long-term follow-up study indicated that the efficacy waned completely after approximately 10 years [12].

Adjuvanted recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV, Shingrix) was approved in China in June 2020 and was the only vaccine against HZ for adults aged ≥ 50 years at the time. Since January 2023, a live-attenuated vaccine produced in China has been marketed. RZV is administered as a two-dose schedule and demonstrated 97.2% and 91.3% efficacy against HZ in adults aged ≥ 50 and ≥ 70 years, respectively, in two large randomized clinical trials. Unlike ZVL, its efficacy against PHN among individuals who developed HZ was not considered because it was lower than its efficacy in preventing HZ, indicating no additional protection against PHN once HZ occurred [13, 14].

Notably, both HZ vaccines are currently classified as non-National Immunization Program (non-NIP) vaccines in China, meaning they are fully self-paid and not reimbursed. As a result, the vaccination uptake among adults aged ≥ 50 years remains extremely low, with a coverage rate of only 1.7% [15]. In contrast, coverage rates in Europe and the United States reached 5.2% and 26.8%, respectively, in 2021 [16]. Furthermore, China has been reported to have the lowest willingness to receive HZ vaccination globally [17], with two national surveys indicating that only 16.57% and 14.9% of older adults expressed willingness to be vaccinated [18, 19]. As China’s population ages and life expectancy increases, the absolute number of individuals at risk will continue to grow, amplifying the public health and economic burden of HZ and PHN. Although phase III studies of both ZVL and RZV have demonstrated substantial benefits, due to the late introduction of ZVL, their comparative cost effectiveness has yet to be explored in China, a country with a substantial elderly population. The availability of both ZVL and RZV necessitates healthcare professionals and patients to engage in a deliberative decision-making process. RZV appears to be more effective, particularly in older patients, and may be more expensive. Additionally, RZV requires two doses, which is a potential barrier to vaccination, affecting the cost and efficacy. Although a study has evaluated the cost effectiveness of RZV versus no vaccination in China [20], the economic comparisons between ZVL and no vaccination, as well as between RZV and ZVL, have not yet been assessed. To assist decision makers to choose the vaccine that offers the best value and improve the coverage of vaccines, this study aimed to evaluate the comparative cost effectiveness of RZV, ZVL, and no vaccination for older adults at different ages from the societal perspective.

Methods

Study Design

A model-based economic evaluation was performed on the cost effectiveness of the following three strategies: (1) vaccination with RZV, (2) vaccination with ZVL, and (3) no vaccination, from a societal perspective. The target population consisted of older adults aged ≥ 50 years in mainland China. The ZVL strategy included one dose, and the RZV strategy included two doses administered at months 0 and 2. Because the efficacy of both vaccines varied by vaccination age [21], we conducted analyses separately for four age cohorts, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, and ≥ 80 years. A lifetime horizon (model terminated after everyone died or reached age 120 years) was set, since the risk of developing HZ persists throughout one’s lifetime, and there is a lifelong risk of recurrence of HZ even after recovery.

Model, Inputs and Assumptions

A validated Markov model connected with decision tree [20] was developed to quantify the cost effectiveness of RZV, ZVL, and no vaccination (Fig. 1). The Markov model consisted of eight Markov states (health, HZ with PHN, HZ with auricular, HZ with ocular, HZ with neurological, HZ without complication, HZ resolved, and death) and three decision tree nodes (initial HZ, alive, and HZ recurrent). All simulated populations were entered into the model at the healthy state and transited to other states in a 1-year cycle. Healthy individuals could develop HZ with or without complications (PHN, ocular, auricular, or neurologic diseases) or die. Patients surviving HZ would transit to the state of resolved HZ, where they might relapse into recurrent HZ or die.Fig. 1Model structure

Demographics

The data regarding age- and sex- specific population size and all-cause mortality were obtained from the national population census in 2020 [23]. The target population (adults in China aged ≥ 50 years) comprised 486.58 million in total, including 222.57 million aged 50–59 years, 147.39 million aged 60–69 years, 80.83 million aged 70–79 years, and 35.80 million aged over 80 years [23].

Epidemiology

The age- and sex-specific HZ incidence was derived from a regional integrated health care database [24]. The incidence of recurrent HZ was obtained from the electronic healthcare record database of the regional community-based health information system in China [25]. The duration of HZ was assumed to be 30 days as reported in the Shingles Prevention Study (SPS) of the ZVL vaccine [11]. The age- and sex-specific proportions of PHN incidence among HZ cases were extracted from a health insurance register claims database in Taiwan, China [26]. The mean duration of PHN for HZ cases aged 50–69 years (8.3 months) and ≥ 70 years (10.9 months) was retrieved from the SPS [11]. The incidence of other complications was estimated from the health management system on epidemiologic features of HZ in China [27]. The mortality rate of HZ in Japan was applied in this study because of the absence of related data in China [28]. The mortality rate of recurrent HZ was assumed to be the same as that of initial HZ.

Vaccine

The vaccine coverage against HZ (1.70%) was estimated from a survey in nine cities across China [15], and we assumed that the coverage of RZV and ZVL was the same. The RZV required two doses, and a model-based study in China indicated that 80% of RZV-vaccinated individuals would receive the second dose [29].

The efficacy of RZV was obtained from the Zoster Efficacy Study in Adults 50 Years of Age or Older (ZOE-50) trial [13], the Zoster Efficacy Study in Adults 70 Years of Age or Older (ZOE-70) trial [14], and a long-term follow-up (LTFU) study [30]. The long-term efficacy of RZV against HZ incidence was fitted using four functions: linear, logarithmic, exponential, and 1-minus-exponential functions. All these functions could be monotone decreasing, consistent with the process of waning efficacy. The best fitted functions were selected according to visual assessment, goodness of fit (adjusted R2), and dermatologist opinion (eFigure 1 and eTable 2 in the ESM). For vaccination at age 50–69 years, the linear function was best fitted with R2 = 0.59. The efficacy of RZV against PHN in HZ cases was not considered in our study, because the efficacy against PHN was lower than the efficacy against HZ incidence [13, 14], meaning RZV had no additional efficacy against PHN incidence among HZ cases who were vaccinated. In clinical practice, it was possible that patients might receive one dose. Therefore, the efficacy of one-dose RZV was considered in our study. However, few participants received only one-dose RZV and the mean follow-up period was < 3 months in the ZOE studies [13, 14]. A post-hoc analysis [31] using these limited clinical data evaluated the initial efficacy of one-dose RZV, and the initial efficacy of one dose was 0.9077 in patients aged 60–69 years and 0.7063 in patients aged ≥ 70 years [31]. Due to lack of long-term efficacy data for one-dose RZV, we conservatively assumed that the efficacy waning rate (slope) for one dose was twice as much as for two doses, yielding efficacy functions for a single RZV dose.

The efficacy of ZVL was modeled from the SPS [11], the Short-Term Persistence Substudy (STPS) [32], and the Long-Term Persistence Substudy (LTPS) [12]. As trials showed ZVL had additional efficacy against PHN in HZ cases, this was considered in our study. The long-term efficacy of ZVL against HZ incidence and PHN incidence were fitted separately using the same four function mentioned above. Utility

The age- and sex-specific utility scores in the healthy state were obtained from the National Household Health Survey in older Chinese people [35]. The utility values of uncomplicated HZ, PHN, and ocular complications were extracted from studies on estimation of the health-related quality of life impacts of HZ and PHN [36–38]. The utility values of auricular complication were estimated from the average utility values for older patients with otitis media in Taiwan, China [39]. The utility values of neurologic complications were obtained from that of meningitis in a cost-effectiveness study in China [40]. It was assumed that utility values for ocular, auricular, neurologic complication states remained constant across age groups due to lack of age-specific evidence in established literature.

Moreover, a disutility due to grade 3 adverse events at injection site was included. In clinical trials of HZ vaccines, grade 3 adverse events were defined as events that prevented normal everyday activities. The risk difference of patients with grade 3 adverse events between the vaccine arm and placebo arm was 13.7% [13] for RZV and 1.4% [11] for ZVL. The disutility of a grade 3 adverse event was assumed at 0.193 ± 0.009 [41] (a shift from no problem with usual activities to severe problems with usual activities on the EQ-5D-3L instrument) and the median duration at 2 days [11].

Cost

The vaccine cost per dose of ZVL (US$227.48) and RZV (US$195.46) in our study was the average bid-winning price across China [42]. Vaccination service cost per dose (US$3.88) was the average cost in all provinces across China [42]. The age-specific hospitalization rates of HZ were estimated from the health information systems of medical institutions [43]. The average number of outpatient visits and outpatient and inpatient costs with uncomplicated HZ were derived from a study on the disease burden of HZ [25]. Direct and indirect costs of PHN were estimated from physician reviews and patient reports from eight hospitals in China [44]. Direct and indirect costs of ocular and auricular complications were estimated from the China Health Statistical Yearbook [45], Chinese economic evaluation of eye diseases [46], and a questionnaire on hearing loss [47]. Direct and indirect costs of neurologic complications were estimated using the economic burden of patients with meningitis [48] and ischemic stroke [49] in China. Direct nonmedical costs and indirect costs per HZ case were derived from a retrospective cohort study on the HZ economic burden [50]. The costs that did not correspond to 2023 prices were inflated to 2023 using the consumer price index. All cost inputs were reported as 2023 US dollars, with Chinese yuan converted to US dollars using the 2023 exchange rate: US$1 = ¥7.05 [51]. According to the Guidelines for Evaluation of Chinese Pharmacoeconomics, costs and effectiveness were discounted at an annual rate of 5% [52].

Statistical Analysis

Base-Case Analysis

The outcomes comprised (i) cost, (ii) life year (LY), (iii) quality-adjusted life year (QALY), (iv) the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), (v) net monetary benefits (NMB) and net health benefits (NHB), and (vi) health impacts on total cases of HZ, complications of HZ, and HZ-related death. An ICER was calculated as the incremental costs per additional QALY gained. NMB and NHB were obtained for each strategy by applying the following calculation: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{NMB }(\uplambda ) =\text{ QALY }\times\uplambda -\text{ Cost}$$\end{document}NMB(λ)=QALY×λ-Cost, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{NHB }(\uplambda ) =\text{ QALY }-\text{ Cost }/\uplambda$$\end{document}NHB(λ)=QALY-Cost/λ, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\uplambda$$\end{document}λ was the willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold [53].

The primary decision rule of cost effectiveness was based on ICER. When the ICER was lower than the prespecified WTP threshold, threefold gross domestic product (GDP) per capita per QALY (US$38,042.49/QALY), the strategy was considered cost effective [52]. Specifically, we first calculated the ICER for RZV and ZVL compared with no vaccination. Then, we calculated the ICER for RZV compared with ZVL.

Since ICER requires pairwise comparisons, this study involved three strategies, which need C(3,2) = 3 pairwise comparisons. In contrast, net benefit (NMB and NHB) only required a single comparison and is more straightforward for analysis, providing complementary insights. For each strategy, we calculated the net benefits and ranked them from the highest to the lowest. The strategy with the highest net benefit was considered to be the most cost-effective strategy.

Sensitivity Analyses

To assess the robustness of the base-case results, sensitivity analyses were performed. The sensitivity of the results to variable variation was defined as the impact of variable variation on cost-effectiveness outcomes (ICER).

One-way sensitivity analyses were performed for all model parameters. When the 95% confidence interval (CI) of a parameter was available, its lower and upper bounds were used as the range; if not available, the minimum and maximum values of parameters from different studies were used (Table 1). The results of one-way sensitivity were presented by tornado diagrams. We defined that the results were sensitive to a study variable variation when variable variation altered the cost-effective conclusions (shifting from cost effective to not cost effective, and vice versa) or made the greatest impact on the ICER.

In two-way sensitivity analysis, we examined the joint effect of price of RZV and ZVL, the adherence rate of the second dose of RZV and its price. The results of two-way sensitivity were presented by region graph, and each region was assigned to the most cost-effective strategy. The value of variables within their joint distributions that led to changes in the cost-effective conclusions would be reported.

In probabilistic sensitivity analyses (PSA), a Monte Carlo simulation with 10,000 iterations was generated by simultaneously sampling the key model parameters from the predefined distributions. The results from PSA were used to construct (i) a cost-effectiveness scatter plane, which showing the percentage of iterations that each strategy had an ICER not exceeding WTP; (ii) a cost-effectiveness acceptability curve, illustrating the probability of each strategy being cost effective over a range of WTP values; and (iii) the expected value of perfect information (EVPI) analyses, which quantified the value of acquiring perfect information about all aspects of the decision, that is, eliminating all uncertainty [54, 55].

Scenario and Subgroup Analyses

To investigate the uncertainty of economic outcomes caused by different scenarios, scenario analysis with respect to the following three conditions was conducted: (i) different adherence to second dose of RZV; (ii) different efficacy waning rate (slope) for one-dose RZV (equal to two-dose RZV, threefold to two-dose RZV); and (iii) different time horizons (10, 20, 30, and 40 years). Lastly, sex subgroup analyses were performed to investigate the uncertainty caused by the subpopulation.

Validation of the Model

Internal and external validity were performed to evaluate the accuracy of model simulations. In internal validity, the inputs, outputs, equations, and programming codes in the model were inspected, and two different types of software (Microsoft Excel 365 and TreeAge Pro 2022) were used to construct the model. In external validity, the simulated lifetime risk of HZ among people with no vaccination in our model was compared with results reported in published literature.

Results

Validation of the Model

In internal validity, the inputs, outputs, equations, and programming codes were consistent in Excel and TreeAge Pro. In external validity, the reported lifetime risk of a person having one occurrence of HZ was approximately 30% in a systematic review of 1242 studies from Asia Pacific countries [56] and lifetime HZ incidence was 28.38% in our model, within a 5% relative difference. The results of validation indicated high reliability of our model.

Scenario Analysis and Subgroup Analysis

Scenario analyses were applied to examine the impacts of different adherence to a second dose of RZV, efficacy waning rate (slope) for one dose of RZV, and time horizon of the model (eTable 4 in the ESM). The results of the scenario analyses were generally consistent with base-case analysis. Notably, if the efficacy waning rate (slope) for one-dose RZV was threefold to two-dose, the most cost-effective strategy would change from RZV to no vaccination in the 50–59 years age cohort. If the time horizon was < 10 years, the most cost-effective strategy would change from RZV to no vaccination in the 50–59, 60–69, and ≥ 80 years age cohorts. Significantly, no vaccination became cost effective for male individuals in the 50–59 years age cohort, and no vaccination seemed to achieve more cost effectiveness for female individuals in the ≥ 80 years age cohort.

Discussion

As a live attenuated vaccine, ZVL only requires a single injection and is relatively affordable. The limitations of this vaccine, however, encompass its relatively modest efficacy that wanes over time and its contraindication for usage in individuals with compromised immune function [12]. Although RZV demonstrates superior efficacy against HZ and provides longer-lasting protection, it is associated with a higher cost and necessitates administration of a second dose [42, 57]. For a developing country like China, particularly one with a substantial elderly population (approximately 500 million individuals aged ≥ 50 years) [23], it holds immense significance to ascertain the cost effectiveness of each vaccine. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first comprehensive evaluation and comparison of the health impacts and cost effectiveness associated with different vaccination regimens (RZV, ZVL, and no vaccination) among older adults across various age groups in China. Studies conducted in other countries have already evaluated the cost effectiveness of these two vaccines [21, 58–60], offering valuable insights into vaccination strategies. Previously, the economic and health impacts on Chinese adults aged ≥ 50 years were assessed solely through a two-arm comparison (RZV versus no vaccination) due to the delayed approval of ZVL in China until 2023 [20]. Our study distinguishes itself from the previous one in several key aspects [20]. Firstly, we utilized the most latest data sources, with model inputs based on data up to 2024, while the previous study used data up to 2022. Secondly, our study innovatively conducted an EVPI analysis, which quantified the value of eliminating all uncertainties in the decision-making process. The EVPI values were 144.67, 21.76, 30.06, and 15.01 million US dollars for the 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, and 80+ age cohorts, respectively, with the highest EVPI in the 50–59 years age cohort highlighting the need to prioritize addressing uncertainties in this age group. Thirdly, our study evaluated the model’s accuracy through both internal and external validity assessments, whereas the previous study only conducted external validity checks. Finally, the previous study found that RZV was most likely to be cost effective in people aged 60−69 years, but did not consider the option of ZVL.

Our model predicts that the cost effectiveness of RZV surpasses no vaccination in China for adults aged ≥ 50 years, and also providing superior health benefits compared with ZVL across all age groups. This can be attributed primarily to the enhanced vaccine efficacy and durability exhibited by RZV over ZVL. Our study also revealed that, with the exception of individuals aged 70–79 years, RZV exhibits superior cost effectiveness compared with ZVL, with a WTP threshold of US$38,042.49/QALY. This discrepancy primarily arises from the fact that within the age group of 70–79 years, ZVL demonstrates optimal and enduring protection against PHN, accompanied by a high incidence rate. The risk ratio of ZVL efficacy against PHN in the 70–79 years age group was 1.167, substantially higher than that in other age groups, making ZVL most cost effective in this population. Although individuals aged 80 years and above have a higher incidence of PHN among those who develop HZ, the vaccine’s efficacy in preventing PHN in this group was considerably lower (risk ratio = 0.602), resulting in poor cost effectiveness in the ≥ 80 years age group. Subsequent post-licensure studies have further substantiated the efficacy of ZVL in providing supplementary protection against PHN and other complications associated with HZ, while RZV does not demonstrate this effect [21, 61–64]. Furthermore, in individuals aged ≥ 70 years, RZV’s initial lower level of protection and rapid decay trend contribute to an accelerated decline in its protective efficacy. Although RZV is the most cost-effective option in the 50–59 years group, its stability is questionable as indicated by an ICER of US$36,687.03/QALY compared with no vaccination, which closely approaches the threshold value of US$38,042.49/QALY. Moreover, it exhibits a high susceptibility to reversal in one-way sensitivity analyses when compared with no vaccination. Although vaccination against HZ in the 50–59 years age group can effectively reduce the incidence of HZ and its related complications, leading to a decrease in overall mortality rate and HZ-related mortality rate, an extension of lifespan, and applicability to a larger proportion of the population, there are considerable economic benefits associated with vaccinating against HZ during advanced age, which is consistent with research findings from other countries [21, 65, 66].

The discount rate of cost and waning rate of two-dose RZV efficacy are identified as the primary determinants influencing the cost effectiveness of RZV in our study, aligning with previous research findings [20, 21]. In order to distinguish the impact of the discount rate of utility and discount rate of cost on ICER, we conducted separate one-way sensitivity analyses. The high cost of RZV could be mitigated by reducing the discount rate, while a decrease in the waning rate of two-dose RZV efficacy would lead to an increased gain in QALYs due to its long-term protective effects. However, the parameters that have the most significant impact on the cost effectiveness of ZVL are the risk ratio of HZ and intercept of ZVL for PHN. The efficacy of the ZVL vaccine against HZ and PHN can be assessed through the two parameters. Prevention of PHN is a unique property of ZVL that renders it the most cost effective option within the age group of 70–79 years. The results of the two-way sensitivity analysis indicated that ZVL did not confer any price advantage. To enhance cost effectiveness, in addition to price reduction, it may be worth considering ZVL with a booster, meaning an additional dose administered after the initial vaccination to restore or extend immunity when its protective effect diminishes completely within a span of 10 years [12, 67]. According to the scenario analysis, when the rate of efficacy waning (slope) for a single dose of RZV is threefold to that of a two-dose regimen, indicating a shorter duration of effectiveness with higher waning rates, the most cost-effective strategy would shift from RZV to no vaccination in the 50–59 years age group. However, if the model simulates < 30 years of time in the 50–59 years age group, the full realization of RZV’s effectiveness may be compromised, resulting in an underestimation of its impact and a decline in QALYs. Consequently, there is a possibility that no vaccination could replace RZV as a more cost-effective alternative. In contrast, the cost effectiveness of ZVL remains unaffected in the 70–79 years age group due to its treatment effects against HZ and PHN primarily manifesting within the initial 5-year period [21]. Compared with males, the incidence of HZ is lower in females aged ≥ 80 years. Additionally, health utility values for females aged ≥ 80 years are also comparatively lower. The fluctuating and decreasing reduction in utility values caused by HZ is observed within this population group. Consequently, not receiving vaccination has emerged as a cost-effective choice for females aged ≥ 80 years.

Currently, numerous Chinese enterprises are engaged in clinical research on RZV and ZVL, showcasing significant potential within the domestic market. According to the investigation, the vaccination coverage for HZ among individuals aged ≥ 50 years in Beijing is merely 1.7% [15], which significantly lags behind that of developed countries. For instance, in the United States, the immunization rate for herpes zoster among individuals aged 50–59 years stands at 5.7%, while it reaches approximately 35% among elderly individuals aged ≥ 60 years [68]. Our research findings hold significant implications for future HZ vaccination strategies, including the exploration of incorporating HZ vaccines into immunization plans or medical insurance coverage. Moreover, it is crucial to enhance awareness among the elderly population regarding the efficacy, cost effectiveness, safety, and contraindications of HZ vaccines across various age groups through targeted media campaigns. These efforts will contribute to an increased vaccination rate and alleviating associated public health burdens.

Our study is subject to several limitations. First, the model was conducted in a healthy, immunocompetent population. However, the effectiveness of RZV against HZ may be slightly lower in immunosuppressed individuals [66], and ZVL is not suitable for the population with compromised immune function, which could lead to an overestimation of the efficacy of vaccines. Moreover, the differences in baseline characteristics across trials may pose a challenge to direct comparability, potentially introducing bias into the interpretation of relative effectiveness or cost-effectiveness outcomes. Second, there is limited availability of data regarding compliance with the second dose of RZV, as well as the effectiveness and waning effects of a single dose of RZV. We have made assumptions similar to those found in previous literature [20, 21], and sensitivity analysis has shown that these assumptions do not greatly impact our results. Since there is a lack of long-term effectiveness data for ZVL produced in China, we have opted to utilize long-term efficacy data provided by Merck due to their similarity in terms of short-term efficacy. Third, although our model incorporates complications associated with HZ, the lack of sufficient data prevented us from including the potential impact of vaccination on HZ severity. However, there is evidence suggesting that vaccinated individuals may experience a shorter duration and less severe pain [69], which could potentially lead to an underestimation of the health benefits offered by the vaccines. Fourth, we did not account for the impact of childhood varicella vaccination on the incidence of HZ in adults. Previous research has indicated that exposure to varicella is believed to enhance immunity against zoster, and a decrease in varicella cases resulting from widespread vaccination could potentially lead to an increase in zoster occurrences [70]. However, recent studies have yielded inconclusive evidence regarding the potential association between varicella vaccination programs and an elevated incidence of herpes zoster [71]. Fifth, the limitation of epidemiological data sources (e.g., sources from regional Chinese cohorts and Japanese studies) could impact the representativeness of key parameters such as the proportion of PHN among HZ cases and HZ mortality rates. Finally, parts of utility values relied on European and the American population due to data gaps in China-specific estimates (e.g. uncomplicated HZ, PHN, and ocular complications). Future studies should validate these parameters using national data from China.

Conclusions

RZV demonstrated greater effectiveness in reducing the burden of HZ and its complications among immunocompetent older adults in China, compared with ZVL. RZV was most cost effective for those aged 50–59, 60–69, and ≥ 80 years, and ZVL was best for the 70–79 years age group, considering the US$38,042.49/QALY threshold. The findings are expected to provide valuable insights for policy makers in China and other countries contemplating the implementation of HZ vaccination.
