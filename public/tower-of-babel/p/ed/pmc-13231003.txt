A cloud-computing framework for downscaled global 300 m SIF retrieval from Sentinel-3 and TROPOSIF
Authors: Yuxin Zhang, Pablo Reyes-Muñoz, Jochem Verrelst
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSun-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (SIF) is a critical indicator of photosynthetic activity. Yet, existing satellite SIF products typically suffer from coarse spatial resolutions, generally coarser than 500 m, which limits their utility for fine-scale ecosystem studies. Here, we present a cloud-computing framework designed for the generation of a downscaled SIF product (S3-SIF743) derived from Sentinel-3 (S3) Ocean and Land Colour Instrument (OLCI), with a spatiotemporal resolution of 300 m and 4 days. Our approach uses the Google Earth Engine (GEE) cloud-computing platform to integrate SIF produced from TROPOspheric Monitoring Instrument measurements within the 743–758 nm retrieval (TROPOSIF743), S3 radiances, S3-based vegetation traits, latitude and longitude within a Random Forest (RF) regression framework. Model training over Europe achieved robust performance against TROPOSIF743 reference data (R2 = 0.767, RMSE = 0.137 mW m−2 sr−1nm−1), and the approach was subsequently extended globally. Validation against ground-based tower observations confirmed that S3-SIF743 effectively reproduces seasonal dynamics across diverse ecosystems. Comparisons against TROPOSIF743 demonstrated strong spatial consistency in temperate agricultural regions, with the highest R2 values in croplands and lower agreement in sparsely vegetated or persistently cloudy regions. Global mapping revealed coherent patterns of photosynthetic activity, with peak values in tropical rainforests and major agricultural zones. Importantly, S3-SIF743 reduces retrieval noise relative to TROPOSIF743 and provides unprecedented insights into sub-kilometer spatial heterogeneity. By combining S3’s rich spectral capabilities with GEE’s scalable computing environment, our approach bridges the gap between current coarse-resolution SIF products and ESA’s upcoming FLEX mission, offering a flexible and operational pathway for high-resolution monitoring of terrestrial photosynthesis.Keywords: Sun-induced fluorescence, Sentinel-3 OLCI, TROPOMI, Google Earth Engine, Machine learning

Introduction

Sun-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (SIF) is a critical indicator of photosynthetic activity, providing a direct link to the light reactions of photosynthesis in terrestrial ecosystems (Porcar-Castell et al., 2021, León-Tavares et al., 2024). Satellite-derived SIF observations at regional and global scales have facilitated research on vegetation phenology, ecosystem stress, and carbon cycle dynamics (Duveiller and Cescatti, 2016, Jonard et al., 2020, Zhang et al., 2023a, Meng et al., 2023). Various satellite missions, such as Orbiting Carbon Observatory-2 (OCO-2) (Sun et al., 2018), SCanning Imaging Absorption spectroMeter for Atmospheric CartograpHY (SCIAMACHY) (Joiner et al., 2016), Global Ozone Monitoring Experiment-2 (GOME-2) (Joiner et al., 2011, Joiner et al., 2013), the Chinese Carbon Dioxide Observation Satellite Mission (TanSat) (Du et al., 2018), Orbiting Carbon Observatory-3 (OCO-3) (Taylor et al., 2020, Doughty et al., 2022), and TROPOspheric Monitoring Instrument (TROPOMI) (Köhler et al., 2018, Köhler et al., 2020, Guanter et al., 2021), have provided foundational global SIF datasets. However, these products typically feature coarse spatial resolutions, limiting their utility for investigating fine-scale spatial heterogeneity in ecosystems (Wulder et al., 2004, Zhang et al., 2023b). The forthcoming European Space Agency (ESA) Fluorescence Explorer (FLEX) mission, planned for launch in 2026, promises to address this limitation by providing dedicated full-spectrum SIF measurements at 300 m resolution through its high-resolution imaging spectrometer FLORIS (Drusch et al., 2017). However, until FLEX becomes operational, sub-kilometer resolution SIF datasets are critically needed both to advance current ecosystem studies and precision vegetation monitoring (Tao et al., 2024), and to prepare the scientific community for integrating and exploiting FLEX’s unprecedented observational capabilities.

Spatiotemporal data fusion approaches have been widely adopted to downscale the spatial and temporal resolution of remote sensing products (Zhu et al., 2018). These methods integrate multi-source satellite observations with complementary spatial and temporal resolutions to generate continuous, high-spatial-resolution datasets (Benzenati et al., 2024, Guo et al., 2024, Meng et al., 2025). Classical spatiotemporal fusion techniques, such as Spatial and Temporal Adaptive Reflectance Fusion Model (STARFM) and its enhanced variants, have demonstrated their effectiveness in producing high-spatial-resolution images with frequent temporal coverage (Gao et al., 2006, Hilker et al., 2009, Zhu et al., 2010). More recently, machine learning regression algorithms further advanced this field by capturing complex nonlinear relationships between datasets, enabling the integration of moderate-resolution SIF retrievals with higher-resolution remote sensing data to improve spatial granularity and temporal consistency (Zhang et al., 2021, Qin et al., 2022).

Over the past decade, numerous machine-learning approaches have been developed to overcome the spatiotemporal limitations of native satellite SIF retrievals. Early products based on GOME-2 and SCIAMACHY include RSIF (500 m, bi-weekly) (Gentine and Alemohammad, 2018), SIF005 (0.05°, monthly) (Wen et al., 2020), downscaled-GOME2-SIF (0.05°, 8-day) (Duveiller et al., 2020), and DSIF (0.05°, 16-day) (Ma et al., 2022). OCO-2–based products such as CSIF (0.05°, 4-day) (Zhang et al., 2018), GOSIF (0.05°, 8-day) (Li and Xiao, 2019), and SIFoco2_005 (0.05°, 16-day) (Yu et al., 2019) further improved temporal continuity. TanSat SIF has also been reconstructed at 0.05° resolution (Ma et al., 2020). With higher native resolution, TROPOMI enabled products such as RTSIF (0.05°, 8-day) (Chen et al., 2022), SDSIF (0.05°, daily) (Hu et al., 2022), and SIFnet (0.005°, 16-day) (Gensheimer et al., 2022). Regional efforts such as CNSIF (500 m, monthly, China) demonstrate the value of medium-resolution sensors for landscape-scale reconstruction (Du et al., 2025). Despite these advances, three limitations remain. First, most products rely heavily on MODIS reflectance, limiting their ability to resolve fine-scale heterogeneity (Steele et al., 2017). High-resolution efforts such as SIFnet and CNSIF are constrained by temporal frequency and spatial coverage. Second, no global product yet approaches the 300 m resolution planned for ESA’s Fluorescence Explorer (FLEX), scheduled for launch in 2026, which will provide dedicated full-spectrum SIF measurements at 300 m via the FLORIS spectrometer (Drusch et al., 2017). Third, existing frameworks have not fully utilized sensors with inherently finer native resolution to enhance spatial detail in model inputs.

A key opportunity lies in exploiting data from the Sentinel-3 (S3) Ocean and Land Colour Instrument (OLCI), which provides a significantly finer spatial resolution (300 m) and rich spectral information across 21 bands. While the FLEX mission is specifically designed to measure SIF at a 300 m resolution and will operate in synergy with S3 (Drusch et al., 2017), its launch is planned for 2026 Therefore, the full potential of S3 for SIF retrieval remains largely underexplored. Realizing this potential, however, is often constrained by the challenges of generating and managing large-scale, improved-resolution datasets. The emergence of cloud-computing platforms such as Google Earth Engine (GEE) has mitigated these challenges, enabling the integration of multi-source, multi-scale global imagery and providing a collaborative environment for the application of scientific EO-based workflows. Within GEE, studies have successfully used pretrained GPR models for estimating vegetation traits (e.g., Estévez et al., 2022, Reyes-Muñoz et al., 2022, Kovács et al., 2023, De Clerck et al., 2024) and carbon fluxes (Reyes-Muñoz et al., 2024, Reyes-Muñoz et al., 2025). GEE’s built-in machine learning algorithms are widely employed for land use change detection, LAI/FPAR estimation (Sun et al., 2022), and biomass assessment (Shendryk, 2022). Among these, Random Forest (RF) is the most frequently reported method, primarily due to its robustness and lower susceptibility to training data quality (e.g., Breiman, 2001, Belgiu and Drăguţ, 2016, Pérez-Cutillas et al., 2023). These capabilities position GEE as a powerful and scalable platform for developing downscaled SIF products that integrate large data sources with machine learning algorithms.

Altogether, this study aims to develop a downscaling approach to estimate SIF by exploiting S3’s 300 m spatial resolution within the GEE platform. The objectives are to: (1) generate the first S3-based SIF product (S3-SIF743) at 300 m spatial and 4-day temporal resolution by integrating S3 observations with TROPOSIF743 through RF on GEE, (2) validate the product against ground-based and satellite observations across diverse ecosystems, and (3) demonstrate its potential to characterize spatial heterogeneity of photosynthesis at sub-kilometer scales. Ultimately, this work bridges the gap between existing coarse-resolution SIF products (S3-SIF743) and the upcoming FLEX mission. It introduces the first global 300 m SIF product derived from S3 and implemented within the GEE environment. The framework enables scalable and operational SIF mapping and is designed to be FLEX-like, with the same spatial resolution and complementary observation timing as FLEX.

Materials and methods

This study aimed to develop a downscaled-resolution SIF dataset (S3-SIF743) at 300 m spatial resolution and 4-day temporal resolution by establishing an RF model between S3 OLCI observations and satellite-derived TROPOSIF743. The 4-day interval was selected as an optimal trade-off between minimizing cloud contamination and maintaining sufficient temporal resolution to capture vegetation dynamics. The methodological framework consists of four main components (Fig. 1): (1) preparation of reference SIF targets from TROPOMI satellite measurements, including quality filtering, spatial resampling, and temporal aggregation; (2) extraction and preprocessing of S3 OLCI spectral radiances, derived spectral indices, and vegetation biophysical traits implemented on the GEE platform; (3) development of an RF regression model trained on European data to predict SIF from S3 features, also implemented within GEE; and (4) spatiotemporal validation of the resulting S3-SIF743 product against both ground-based tower observations and independent satellite retrievals across multiple biomes and time periods. A key advantage of our downscaling approach is that both model training and SIF prediction can be efficiently executed within the GEE cloud-computing environment, enabling large-scale processing and facilitating the generation of continuous S3-SIF743 products. The training period focused on April–November 2019 over Europe to ensure high-quality retrievals and broad bioclimatic representativeness, while the trained model can be subsequently applied to generate S3-SIF743 products spanning 2016–2025 globally. The data sources and processing steps are further outlined in the following sections.

Workflow for downscaled 300 m resolution S3-SIF743 generation integrating S3 OLCI and TROPOSIF743.

TROPOMI sun-induced chlorophyll fluorescence dataset

To train the S3-based SIF retrieval model, a reliable reference dataset is required. The TROPOSIF743 has been widely used in previous studies due to its robust retrieval algorithm and balance of spatial and temporal resolutions (e.g., Chen et al., 2022, Hu et al., 2022, Reyes-Muñoz et al., 2024, Reyes-Muñoz et al., 2025), making it an ideal reference for this work. The reference SIF data were collected from the TROPOSIF Level-2B (L2B) product (Guanter et al., 2021), retrieved from Sentinel-5P TROPOMI observations. This dataset provides ungridded, daily SIF retrievals based on two spectral fitting windows (743–758 and 735–758 nm), and was directly downloaded from http://ftp.sron.nl/open-access-data-2/TROPOMI/tropomi/sif/v2.1/l2b/. We selected the SIF product derived from the 743–758 nm window (i.e., TROPOSIF743) due to its optimal balance between minimizing cloud interference and ensuring retrieval precision, as opposed to the 735–758 nm window, which is more sensitive to atmospheric effects (Guanter et al., 2021).

To achieve high-quality training targets, we implemented a rigorous filtering scheme. Observations with cloud fraction (CF) greater than 0.1, solar zenith angle (SZA) above 70°, or viewing zenith angle (VZA) above 60° were excluded (Guanter et al., 2021). The filtered TROPOSIF743 values were then spatially resampled to a 0.05° grid using nearest-neighbor interpolation and aggregated into 4-day mean composites for the year 2019. These gridded composites were subsequently uploaded to GEE and used as the target variable for model training.

Input features from Sentinel-3 ocean and Land Colour Instrument

S3 OLCI offers a unique combination of 300 m spatial resolution, which is significantly finer than that of any existing satellite-based SIF products, frequent revisit capability (less than 2 days with dual satellites), and strategically designed spectral bands. With 21 spectral bands spanning 400–1020 nm and a spectral resolution of 2.5–40 nm, OLCI provides detailed information on vegetation optical properties and atmospheric absorption features. Moreover, the FLEX mission is designed to fly in tandem with S3, enabling coordinated acquisitions that combine OLCI’s reflectance and atmospheric observations with dedicated SIF measurements from FLEX. This synergy positions OLCI as a valuable data source for developing spatially improved SIF products and preparing for the operational use of FLEX-derived SIF data.

The S3 OLCI Level-1 top-of-atmosphere radiance data were acquired from the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem (Donlon et al., 2012). Rigorous quality control procedures were applied to ensure the reliability of the data. Pixels were retained if they met the following criteria: (1) classified as land (land flag = 1), (2) valid data (invalid flag = 0), (3) free from cloud contamination or high reflectance interference (bright flag = 0), (4) unaffected by sun glint (sunglint_risk flag = 0), (5) not impacted by stray light (straylight_risk flag = 0), and (6) free from saturation effects in all spectral bands (saturated@OaXX flags = 0).

The analysis used selected OLCI spectral bands and band combinations to enhance the sensitivity to SIF. The chosen bands included Oa12 (753.75 nm), Oa13 (761.25 nm), Oa17 (865 nm), and Oa18 (885 nm). Specifically, Oa12 falls within the TROPOSIF743 retrieval window, while Oa13 is located within the O2-A absorption region, providing critical information on fluorescence emission. The near-infrared bands Oa17 and Oa18 were incorporated to mitigate background and atmospheric effects.

S3-GPR essential vegetation traits

Essential vegetation traits, including leaf chlorophyll content (LCC) and fractional vegetation cover (FVC), are closely linked to photosynthetic efficiency and thus to SIF emissions (Porcar-Castell et al., 2014, Du et al., 2017, Yang et al., 2021). To incorporate structural and physiological information beyond the spectral signal, we employed the previously developed Sentinel-3 Gaussian Process Regression (S3-GPR) models (Reyes-Muñoz et al., 2022, Kovács et al., 2023). These consist of two independent hybrid models for LCC and FVC, each constructed from coupled canopy–atmosphere radiative transfer simulations using Soil Canopy Observation, Photochemistry and Energy fluxes (SCOPE) (van der Tol et al., 2009), the Second Simulation of a Satellite Signal in the Solar Spectrum–vector (6SV) code (Vermote et al., 1997), and Gaussian Process Regression (GPR). The S3-TOA-GPR-1.0 product was used to extract key vegetation traits for each 4-day period, including LCC and fractional vegetation cover (FVC). These variables were retrieved from S3 radiance data using the GEE platform and incorporated as input features in the RF model, as outlined next.

Random forest regression model for SIF downscaling

The RF algorithm is widely recognized for its robustness and interpretability, making it well-suited for integrating diverse spectral, biophysical, and geographic variables to predict SIF (Wen et al., 2020). Before model construction, we applied spatial smoothing to each image to suppress noise and improve spatial coherence. Specifically, all S3-based features were smoothed in GEE using a normalized 3 × 3 pixel kernel, where each pixel value was replaced by the mean of its neighboring pixels. This preprocessing step effectively reduced random noise while preserving broader spatial patterns essential for reliable model training.

An RF regression model was constructed in GEE to establish the predictive relationship between explanatory features and the reference TROPOSIF743 data. To secure both representativeness and robustness, we randomly sampled 6000 pixels from each image spanning the period from April to November. Data from January to March and December were excluded from model training due to their reduced reliability. During these months, TROPOSIF743 retrievals frequently contain negative values as a result of increased noise and lower solar illumination. Furthermore, valid retrievals are geographically biased toward lower latitudes, where solar zenith angle (SZA) and viewing zenith angle (VZA) constraints are less restrictive. Including these seasonally skewed observations would have introduced sampling bias and reduced model generalizability.

Before implementing the model on the GEE platform, we conducted local tuning using a representative subset of the data. By analyzing the loss function (out-of-bag error) across different forest sizes, we observed that model performance stabilized and achieved high robustness once the number of trees exceeded 50. Consequently, we set the number of decision trees to 50 to balance computational efficiency and accuracy. No evidence of overfitting was observed during the validation phase, therefore, the minimum leaf size was set to 1. The maximum tree depth was kept at its default value, as the RF implementation in GEE does not support depth adjustment.

The sampled dataset from Europe was randomly partitioned for training and evaluation, with 80% of the samples used for model training and the remaining 20% reserved for independent testing. Due to the computational constraints of the GEE platform, the final model testing was performed on a subset of 5000 points randomly selected from the independent test set. Model performance was assessed using the root mean squared error (RMSE) and the coefficient of determination (R2). The RF model was trained and tested exclusively over Europe because the continent encompasses a wide range of bioclimatic conditions, vegetation types, and phenological phases within a compact geographical domain, providing an efficient yet diverse training sample. The Europe-trained model was subsequently applied globally to generate the downscaled 300 m S3-SIF743 product at 4-day temporal resolution for the period 2016–2025. Its global generalization capability was further evaluated through comparisons against TROPOSIF743 and biome-wise seasonal analyses.

Spatiotemporal prediction and analysis

We employed the trained RF regression model in GEE to derive S3-SIF743 at a spatial resolution of 300 m and temporal intervals of 4 days. To assess the reliability and spatiotemporal consistency of this product relative to independent observations, we conducted a validation experiment using ground-based SIF measurements collected at four representative sites (Table 1). These sites were selected to capture diverse ecosystems, geographic settings, and temporal coverage, including: Majadas de Tiétar (39.9°N, 5.8°W) (Biriukova et al., 2021b, Biriukova et al., 2021a), a Mediterranean savanna in Spain (March 2017–October 2018), with data obtained from https://figshare.com/articles/dataset/13190858; XiaoTangshan (XTS; 40.2°N, 116.4°E), a winter wheat-maize rotation cropland, and HuaiLai (HL; 40.3°N, 115.8°E) (Du et al., 2019, L. Liu et al., 2022), a mixed grassland and crabapple orchard, both located in China (2018–2021) with datasets archived on https://zenodo.org/records/7244183; and US-Ne2 (41.2°N, 96.5°W) (Earth Science Data Systems, NASA, 2025), a corn-soybean rotation cropland in the United States (July 2017–October 2018), with observations provided via https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/catalog/ornl-cloud-crops-sif-vegindices-il-ne-2136-1. This site selection provides a diverse spatiotemporal representation across three continents, multiple vegetation functional types (savanna, agricultural systems with different management practices, and mixed vegetation), and a combined observation period spanning 2017–2021. The temporal sampling schemes differ among sites. Majadas de Tiétar provides near-continuous SIF observations, with measurements labeled by day/night acquisition codes. In this study, only daytime observations were used. The site includes measurements from over a tree and herbaceous stratum, we only used SIF data from the herbaceous stratum. US-Ne2 provides measurements between 07:00 and 16:30 local time. XiaoTangshan (XTS) and HuaiLai (HL) provide midday observations, typically between 12:00 and 14:00 local time. To ensure temporal consistency with S3-SIF743, all tower-based SIF measurements were aggregated into 4-day mean daytime values.

Due to scale mismatches between ground-based and satellite observations, SIF values derived from flux towers and from S3-SIF743 differ in magnitude. Therefore, the comparison focused on assessing their temporal consistency rather than absolute values (Zhang et al., 2023b). The S3-SIF743 data were predicted at 300 m spatial resolution and 4-day intervals, while the ground-based SIF measurements were aggregated into 4-day mean values to ensure temporal consistency. The Pearson correlation coefficient (R) and the associated probability value (P-value) were then calculated to assess the agreement of temporal variations between S3-SIF743 and ground-based SIF measurements.

To evaluate spatial consistency, we conducted a pixelwise comparison between S3-SIF743 and TROPOSIF743 for an independent period (January–November 2020) outside the training dataset. The 4-day S3-SIF743 data at a spatial resolution of 0.05° were generated using GEE. Considering the coarse native resolution of TROPOSIF743 (3.5 km by 5.6–7 km at nadir), adopting finer grids (e.g., 300 m) would introduce substantial uncertainty. To achieve a balance between spatial representativeness and reliability, TROPOSIF743 was aggregated to a 0.05° grid, with mean values computed over each 4-day interval to match the temporal resolution of the predicted S3-SIF743. We further derived annual averages of S3-SIF743 and TROPOSIF743 to examine their spatial distributions and calculated the coefficient of determination (R2) and root-mean-square error (RMSE) for each pixel across the study period. Particular emphasis was placed on Europe for detailed analysis.

We subsequently evaluated the temporal consistency between S3-SIF743 and TROPOSIF743 across major global land-cover types. Land-cover classification was obtained from the MODIS Collection 5 land cover product (MCD12Q1) (Friedl et al., 2010), which assigns each 0.05° pixel to a vegetation type (e.g., evergreen needleleaf forest, evergreen broadleaf forest, deciduous needleleaf forest, deciduous broadleaf forest, mixed forest, cropland, grassland, or shrubland). For each biome, we computed the mean SIF value across all valid pixels at each 4-day interval and evaluated the agreement between S3-SIF743 and TROPOSIF743 using R2 and RMSE. In addition, to quantitatively assess noise levels, we calculated the spatial standard deviation of SIF across pixels at each 4-day interval and then averaged these stepwise standard deviations (denoted as σ).

To further capture interannual variability in the downscaled S3-SIF743, we generated 300 m S3-SIF743 data at 8-day intervals in GEE for 2019. Monthly mean values for each pixel were then calculated to characterize the seasonal cycle over the Iberian Peninsula. These monthly composites were used to illustrate the spatial distribution and annual phenological evolution of the 300 m S3-SIF743 product.

For cross-product comparison, TROPOSIF743 data were interpolated to 1 km spatial resolution using cubic spline interpolation to ensure spatial consistency while preserving large-scale spatial structure. To enable climate-stratified analysis at fine spatial scale, we employed the 1 km global Köppen–Geiger climate classification dataset for the 1984–2013 period, developed by Cui et al. (2021). It was resampled to 300 m using nearest-neighbor interpolation, thereby aligning with the native resolution of TROPOSIF743 and S3-SIF743, respectively. The 300 m Copernicus Climate Change Service land cover product (Copernicus Climate Change Service, 2019), defined according to the United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization (UN FAO) Land Cover Classification System, was aggregated to 1 km resolution to respectively match the spatial scale of S3-SIF743 and TROPOSIF743 for irrigated-area comparisons. Irrigated or post-flooding croplands were identified based on the designated land-cover class and used as spatial masks in subsequent analyses.

Climate-type analysis was conducted using the 8-day S3-SIF743 and TROPOSIF743 datasets. Pixels were stratified according to the Köppen classification, and we focused on the two dominant and climatically contrasting regimes in the Iberian Peninsula: Csa (hot-summer Mediterranean climate) and Cfb (temperate oceanic climate). The 8-day temporal resolution allowed detailed comparison of phenological trajectories under contrasting hydrothermal regimes.

Results

Comparison of S3-SIF743 with TROPOSIF743

As an initial assessment of model performance, we evaluated the agreement between predicted S3-SIF743 and the reference TROPOSIF743 using the independent test dataset. The scatterplot between TROPOSIF743 and S3-SIF743 for the test data was shown in Fig. 2. A strong positive linear relationship is observed, as indicated by the high R2 and low RMSE, which are 0.767 and 0.137 mWm−2sr−1nm−1, respectively. When TROPOSIF743 is positive, S3-SIF743 predictions are generally distributed close to the 1:1-line, indicating good agreement over the dominant value range. However, for higher SIF values (>0.8mWm−2sr−1nm−1), the model shows a tendency toward systematic underestimation, while its predictive performance degrades for negative TROPOSIF743 values. The relatively small number of negative S3-SIF743 predictions suggests that negative TROPOSIF743 cases are not well reproduced. Together, the underestimation at higher values and the limited representation of negative values contribute to a fitted regression slope of less than unity (0.75).

Temporal validation against tower-based SIF observations

To assess the temporal reliability of the S3-SIF743 product, we validated it against continuous tower-based SIF measurements that capture realistic ecosystem dynamics across diverse biomes and climatic conditions. Fig. 3 presents the temporal evolution of S3-derived SIF (S3-SIF743, orange lines) against ground-based SIF observations (blue stars) across four representative sites: Majadas de Tiétar (a, 2017/03–2018/10), XiaoTangshan (b, 2018/03–2020/11), US-Ne2 (c, 2017/07–2018/10), and Huailai (d, 2018/05–2020/11). Across all sites, both datasets capture pronounced seasonal dynamics, with SIF values rising during the active growing season and declining in dormant periods, thereby confirming that S3-SIF743 effectively reproduces the phenological cycle observed in ground measurements. Nevertheless, systematic differences in magnitude are evident, with S3-SIF743 values being systematically lower than ground measurements. The correlation analysis indicates statistically significant relationships, with correlation coefficients of R = 0.51 at Majadas de Tiétar, R = 0.45 at XiaoTangshan, R = 0.81 at US-Ne2, and R = 0.36 at Huailai, and all P-values were below 0.001, demonstrating that S3-SIF743 provides a statistically significant representation of temporal SIF variability.

Spatial distribution and agreement with TROPOSIF743 globally

While temporal validation demonstrates the model’s capability to capture seasonal dynamics at the site level, spatial validation focuses on evaluating whether S3-SIF743 reproduces global SIF patterns consistent with reference satellite products across diverse biomes and environmental gradients, thereby confirming its generalization beyond the European continent. Fig. 4 presents the yearly-averaged global SIF distribution for 2020 from both S3-SIF743 and TROPOSIF743 datasets at 0.05° resolution, revealing broadly consistent spatial patterns between the two products. Both datasets exhibit the highest SIF values (> 0.6 mWm−2sr−1nm−1) in tropical regions, particularly the Amazon Basin, Central African forests, Southeast Asian rainforests, and the US Corn Belt, consistent with high photosynthetic activity in these ecosystems. Arid regions, high-latitude areas, and polar regions display the lowest values (< 0.2 mWm−2sr−1nm−1). Despite the overall spatial coherence, subtle differences are apparent between the datasets, with S3-SIF743 generally showing slightly higher values in Amazonian rainforests, and slightly lower values in Europe.

Spatial patterns and seasonal changes

Building upon the demonstrated temporal and spatial consistency of S3-SIF743, we further emphasize the advantages of the enhanced 300 m resolution by analyzing its spatial and seasonal dynamics across the Iberian Peninsula in 2019 (Fig. 7). The monthly averaged S3-SIF743 signal clearly captures the full annual phenological cycle. From January to March, SIF remains at near-dormant levels. A rapid green-up occurs in April and May as temperature rises and soil moisture conditions improve, culminating in a regional maximum around June. Beginning in July and intensifying in August, S3-SIF743 declines sharply across the central and southern interior regions, consistent with the onset of Mediterranean summer drought and indicating heat- and water-stress-induced down-regulation of photosynthesis. Importantly, within these generally water-limited central and southern areas, the high-resolution S3-SIF743 product clearly resolves persistently elevated SIF values over several irrigated agricultural zones (Wriedt et al., 2009, Zhu et al., 2025), distinguishing them from surrounding natural vegetation (Fig. A.1 in Appendix). This demonstrates the capability of the 300 m product to capture anthropogenic water management effects on productivity, signals that tend to be smoothed in coarser-resolution datasets (Fig. 8).

To further interpret hydrothermal controls, we focused on the two most representative Köppen climate types: Csa (hot-summer Mediterranean climate) and Cfb (temperate oceanic climate) (Fig. 8). These climates differ fundamentally in precipitation seasonality and thermal regimes, leading to contrasting phenological patterns. The Csa region exhibits a characteristic “spring peak–summer decline” pattern, with SIF reaching its maximum in April–May and decreasing markedly under summer drought conditions, indicating strong moisture limitation. In contrast, the Cfb region, characterized by more evenly distributed precipitation and moderate temperatures, shows higher peak values and a more sustained high-value plateau, reflecting an extended growing season under synergistic hydrothermal conditions.

At the climate-zone scale, S3-SIF743 offers a more distinct characterization of phenological contrasts between the two regimes. In Csa regions, it captures a sharper spring green-up and a more pronounced summer decline; in Cfb regions, it exhibits a higher peak magnitude followed by a more rapid senescence. In contrast, TROPOSIF743 displays dampened peak values and prolonged persistence in Cfb zones, while its summer decline in Csa regions is less defined, thereby masking the inherent climatic sensitivity.

At the irrigated-area scale, S3-SIF743 and TROPOSIF743 exhibit pronounced differences in their spatial structural characteristics (Table 2). Overall, S3-SIF743 consistently shows higher spatial variability (σ¯area) and edge gradient strength (G¯edge) throughout the year, indicating a stronger ability to capture spatial heterogeneity and sharper spatial transitions in photosynthetic activity. The contrast is particularly evident during the growing season (March–August). For example, in April, the σ¯area of S3-SIF743 reaches 0.0484, compared to only 0.0154 for TROPOSIF743; the corresponding G¯edge values are 0.3503 and 0.1300, respectively. Similar differences persist from May to July, during which S3-SIF743 maintains gradient magnitudes approximately two to four times higher than those of TROPOSIF743, suggesting enhanced sensitivity to field-scale variability under active crop growth and irrigation conditions. Notably, S3-SIF743 exhibits a pronounced seasonal variation, with both σ¯area and G¯edge tracking the vegetation phenology closely, characterized by a rapid increase during the spring green-up and a sharp peak in summer. In comparison, the seasonal fluctuations in TROPOSIF743 are considerably more muted. This disparity demonstrates that the 300 m S3-SIF743 not only captures more spatial detail at any given time but also provides a more dynamic and accurate representation of the temporal evolution of fragmented ecosystems.

Discussion

Performance and reliability of the 300 m S3-SIF743 product

Using a GEE–based RF to fuse S3 OLCI observations with TROPOSIF743 retrievals, this study delivers a downscaled global SIF product (S3-SIF743) at 300 m and 4-day temporal resolution. Despite the overall strong performance, several systematic biases warrant discussion. As described in Section 2.1, the use of gridded TROPOSIF743 as training targets inevitably introduces spatial representativeness uncertainty. While the 0.05° grid serves a intermediate training target because it approximates the native footprint size thereby minimizes artificial spatial artifacts, the exact spatial support of the original footprints cannot be fully retained (Parker et al., 1983). Despite this limitation, the high correlation indicates that the 0.05° grid preserves the dominant regional SIF patterns required for downscaling (Fig. 2). Future work could incorporate footprint-aware resampling or directly use native TROPOSIF743 geometries to further reduce representativeness errors.

The model shows a limited capacity to reproduce both negative values and very high SIF values in the TROPOSIF743 product (Fig. 2). This systematic discrepancy likely arises from both algorithmic and physical factors. On the algorithmic side, the RF models are known to exhibit regression-to-the-mean behavior, producing averaged predictions that bias outputs toward the central range when extreme SIF samples are relatively sparse (Wolfensberger et al., 2021, Zhang and Lu, 2012, Scornet et al., 2015). On the physical side, negative TROPOSIF743 values are largely associated with retrieval uncertainty and low signal-to-noise conditions rather than true physiological signals (Guanter et al., 2021), the model’s reluctance to replicate them may reflect its emphasis on physically plausible SIF patterns rather than propagating noise (Scornet et al., 2015).

As described in Section 2.4, a 3 × 3 spatial mean filter was applied to S3 OLCI imagery to suppress along-track stripe noise (Chen et al., 2017). The final S3-SIF743 product was generated from the original, unsmoothed data, preserving the 300 m spatial resolution (Fig. 7; Table 2). Although the filtering may reduce fine-scale variability in the training samples, it only affects parameter optimization and does not influence the spatial detail of the final maps, thereby improving model robustness without compromising resolution.

Another source of uncertainty relates to the sampling strategy employed during model training (Section 2.4). By excluding data from January through March and December due to reduced TROPOSIF743 reliability, the training dataset was optimized for April to November when SIF signals are strongest and most reliable (Guanter et al., 2021). While incorporating globally distributed sampling points might theoretically enhance representativeness, the inclusion of low-quality retrieval data from certain regions can negatively impact model training. In tropical regions, persistent cloud cover and atmospheric aerosols introduce significant noise into the SIF retrievals, while in arid or high-latitude zones, the signal often falls below the sensor’s reliable detection threshold (Hilker et al., 2012, Zou et al., 2026, Köhler et al., 2015). Integrating these low-fidelity samples into the training process can contaminate the model, leading to reduced predictive stability and the potential for the RF algorithm to overfit to noise rather than capturing genuine physiological signals. Therefore, we prioritized the European domain to ensure a high-quality training baseline, although this may result in specific biases when the model is extrapolated to biomes with distinct canopy structures or scattering properties. Future improvements are expected from ongoing advances in SIF retrievals, as well as from continued methodological developments across the research community. With continuous progress in retrieval algorithms and data-driven corrections, such as cloud-gap filling in humid regions and noise or soil adjustments in sparsely vegetated areas, future models will benefit from higher-quality, more representative global training samples. This can be further supported by more balanced spatial sampling and uncertainty-aware weighting to reduce biases introduced by strict filtering. In parallel, more flexible modeling strategies may enhance performance. Deep learning approaches that better exploit spatial and temporal context (LeCun et al., 2015, Reichstein et al., 2019), such as convolutional neural networks and LSTM-based architectures (Kattenborn et al., 2021, Lecun et al., 1998, Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997), together with hybrid frameworks that incorporate physical constraints from radiative transfer theory (Cortés-Andrés et al., 2022, Willard et al., 2020), could improve the ability to capture extreme conditions and generalize to data-sparse or previously unseen environments.

The comparison with ground-based observations indicates that S3-SIF743 captures the general seasonal patterns of SIF (Fig. 3), but it systematically underestimates the magnitude of sun-induced chlorophyll fluorescence relative to tower measurements. The weaker correlations at XiaoTangshan and Huailai indicate limited capacity to track inter-annual dynamics. These discrepancies can be attributed to multiple interacting factors. First, scale mismatches between satellite pixels (300 m) and tower footprints (meters) are inherent (Wu and Li, 2009, Loew et al., 2017, Du et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2023b, Zeng et al., 2024): satellite retrievals aggregate heterogeneous surface conditions including bare soil, shadows, and non-photosynthetic elements, whereas tower measurements represent point-scale canopy-level SIF under more controlled viewing geometries (Wu and Li, 2009, Du et al., 2023), Therefore, the primary value of ground-based validation lies in confirming temporal consistency rather than absolute magnitude agreement. Second, RF models inherently exhibit regression-to-the-mean behavior, which reduces sensitivity to extreme or anomalous conditions and may dampen interannual variability. Third, the training strategy, based primarily on European conditions, may limit the model’s ability to generalize to regions with different management practices, local environmental variability, including irrigation practices, mixed vegetation composition, and sub-pixel heterogeneity, can introduce signals not represented in the training data. Future work should consider more flexible modeling architectures, explicitly account for scale effects, and expand the geographic diversity of the training samples.

Spatial patterns and ecosystem-specific performance

Beyond overall validation statistics, spatial consistency provides a critical test of model generalization across biomes and environmental gradients (Ploton et al., 2020). Although the RF was trained solely on European data, the generated S3-SIF743 product performs well when applied globally, showing consistent spatiotemporal patterns relative to TROPOSIF743 and tower SIF observations, indicating acceptable generalization. The global spatial distribution of yearly-averaged S3-SIF743 in 2020 exhibited good spatial agreement with TROPOSIF743, with both products identifying tropical forests, the US Corn Belt, and Southeast Asian ecosystems as SIF hotspots, while arid and high-latitude regions displayed minimal SIF signals (Fig. 4).

The pixelwise R2 analysis, computed across the 4-day time steps throughout 2020, revealed that model performance varies systematically with ecosystem characteristics and environmental conditions (Fig. 5). High correlations in croplands in temperate regions can be attributed to the following factors: First, the pronounced seasonal dynamics in these ecosystems create distinct phenological stages that are well-represented in the training data, enabling the model to learn clear feature-SIF relationships across the full annual cycle. Second, the relatively simple canopy structure in these ecosystems facilitates the observations for satellite SIF (Zhao et al., 2025). Reduced performance in persistently cloudy tropical forests reflects a fundamental challenge for optical satellite data: the scarcity of cloud-free observations limits the availability of high-quality retrievals (Hilker et al., 2012, Langhorst et al., 2024). Nevertheless, S3-SIF743 may still offer certain advantages in heavily cloud-contaminated regions, as the model was trained on TROPOSIF743 data with high signal-to-noise ratios obtained under clear-sky conditions. This allows the model to infer surface–SIF relationships that can produce more stable estimates in areas where direct TROPOSIF743 are degraded by clouds (Zhang et al., 2018). In sparsely vegetated areas such as arid and semi-arid regions, weak vegetation signals approach the noise level of the sensor and become difficult to distinguish from retrieval uncertainties, while the high fraction of bare soil and non-photosynthetic surfaces further complicates vegetation detection, collectively leading to reduced predictive power (Huete, 1988, Shupe and Marsh, 2004, Sun et al., 2020).

Across Europe, spatial patterns of agreement reveal both the strengths and limitations of the S3-SIF743 product (Fig. 5). High R2 values in Central and Eastern Europe indicate strong model performance in agricultural and deciduous forest regions characterized by clear seasonal dynamics and uniform canopy structures. In contrast, lower performance in Northern Scandinavia and Alpine areas likely results from the compound effects of reduced observation quality at extreme latitudes (Guanter et al., 2021), complex structure of vegetation canopy (Mutanga et al., 2023) and topographic effects in mountainous terrain Ma et al. (2024). These findings highlight the need for training data that capture diverse environmental conditions and suggest that incorporating topographic features could further improve model robustness. Over the Iberian Peninsula, downscaled S3-SIF743 captured fine-scale seasonal patterns that may be related to water stress and irrigation (Wriedt et al., 2009, Zhu et al., 2025), distinguishing irrigated croplands from surrounding natural vegetation. Such spatially detailed mapping provides new opportunities for monitoring agricultural practices, drought impacts, and ecosystem functioning under climate variability.

Seasonal dynamics across vegetation types

We examined temporal correlations between S3-SIF743 and TROPOSIF743 across eight major biomes to understand how seasonal variability patterns (Fig. 6). The strongest agreement occurred in deciduous forests (R2 = 0.67–0.75) and croplands (R2 = 0.70), ecosystems characterized by pronounced seasonal cycles with distinct phenological transitions—spring green-up, summer peak productivity, and autumn senescence. These sharp temporal contrasts provide diverse and informative training data that can be effectively exploited by machine learning algorithms (Gong et al., 2019). Croplands benefit additionally from relatively homogeneous canopy structure, systematic management practices, and high S3-SIF743 amplitudes during peak growing season, all of which contribute to robust model predictions (Song et al., 2021). The moderate correlation in savannahs (R2 = 0.61) reflects the increased complexity of mixed vegetation-soil backgrounds and spatially variable vegetation cover, though the model still effectively captures the dominant seasonal pattern likely driven by water availability (Wriedt et al., 2009, Zhu et al., 2025). Conversely, evergreen forests posed greater challenges, particularly evergreen broadleaf forests (R2=0.18) and, to a lesser extent, evergreen needleleaf forests (R2=0.53). The relatively flat seasonal dynamics and smaller S3-SIF743 amplitude characteristic of these ecosystems make subtle variations harder to detect against retrieval noise. Evergreen broadleaf forests in tropical regions face the additional challenge of persistent cloud cover, which severely limits the availability of high-quality training data. The higher R2 for evergreen needleleaf forests compared to evergreen broadleaf forests may reflect their occurrence in temperate and boreal zones with more seasonal variation in photosynthetic activity and better observation conditions.

Fine-scale spatial heterogeneity

While global and biome-level analyses confirm the large-scale reliability of S3-SIF743, fine-scale evaluations are essential for revealing its added value in heterogeneous landscapes. The 300 m analysis over the Iberian Peninsula highlights the transformative potential of S3-SIF743 for resolving landscape-scale heterogeneity and capturing phenological dynamics at management-relevant spatial scales (Fig. 7). The product effectively reproduced the region’s complex seasonal behavior, depicting a synchronized spring green-up across the peninsula driven by rising temperature and photoperiod, followed by a pronounced mid-summer decline in S3-SIF743 over central and southern areas associated with water stress under Mediterranean drought conditions (Fig. 8). In contrast, irrigated agricultural zones maintained elevated S3-SIF743 values throughout the dry season, standing out as photosynthetically active patches within otherwise stressed landscapes—patterns that remain undetectable in coarser-resolution products (Table 2; Fig. A.1 in Appendix) (Wriedt et al., 2009, Zhu et al., 2025). Meanwhile, the Northern, Atlantic-influenced regions exhibited an extended growing season, where persistent moisture availability supported sustained photosynthetic activity well beyond the summer peak (Fig. 8).

Methodological advantages and future improvements

The demonstrated performance across temporal, spatial, and ecosystem scales highlights both the methodological strengths and the remaining challenges of the S3-SIF743 framework. As summarized in our introduction (see also Appendix Table A.1), the past decade has seen a proliferation of reconstructed SIF products. In contrast to many existing datasets, S3-SIF743 makes direct and systematic use of S3 observations and is generated within the GEE, enabling efficient processing of large volumes of data. Together with its spatially refined and temporally consistent global coverage, S3-SIF743 provides a practical and timely preparatory dataset for the forthcoming FLEX mission, which will fly in tandem with S3 and provide SIF with a resolution of 300 m. Unlike previous MODIS-based downscaling initiatives, our approach benefits from the finer spatial resolution and rich spectral information of S3 OLCI (Berger et al., 2012, Donlon et al., 2012). Our model achieves sub-kilometer SIF retrievals which are particularly valuable for capturing heterogeneity in agricultural landscapes and fragmented ecosystems. The lower temporal variability observed in the seasonal analysis across vegetation types further indicates that S3-SIF743 offers enhanced stability relative to TROPOSIF743. Beyond monitoring vegetation dynamics directly, the fine-resolution S3-SIF743 product offers significant potential for improving gross primary productivity (GPP) estimation (Reyes-Muñoz et al., 2024). Since SIF is mechanistically linked to photosynthetic electron transport and thus to actual carbon fixation (Porcar-Castell et al., 2014), S3-SIF743 can be integrated into process-based or data-driven models to improve both spatial detail and accuracy (Guanter et al., 2014, Bacour et al., 2019, Z. Liu et al., 2022, Knorr et al., 2025), given that traditional greenness-based indices (e.g., NDVI, EVI) often only indirectly and with a lagged effect relate to photosynthetic activity (Zhang et al., 2022, Yuan et al., 2024).

Implementing the RF framework on GEE offers significant computational advantages by enabling seamless cloud-based processing of petabyte-scale S3 data without requiring local data storage or high-performance computing resources (Gorelick et al., 2017). However, reliance on cloud infrastructure also entails certain limitations, including processing quotas and limited flexibility in algorithm optimization. The reduced quality of S3 data on GEE after 2021 constrained the temporal continuity of our workflow. Future deployments on other cloud-based platforms like openEO or the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem (CDSE), which offer comparable processing capabilities and direct access to Sentinel mission data while providing greater transparency and reproducibility in computational workflows (Schramm et al., 2019). While RF proved robust for this initial implementation, more advanced machine-learning algorithms, including ensemble and hybrid models that may further enhance retrieval accuracy and generalization. In addition, future implementations could incorporate uncertainty estimation frameworks, for instance, by quantile regression forests or Gaussian process regression (e.g., Koenker and Bassett, 1978, Verrelst et al., 2015, Vaysse and Lagacherie, 2017, De Clerck et al., 2024, García-Soria et al., 2024).

The current S3-SIF743 product is limited to the 743–758 nm range inherited from TROPOSIF743. This range is robust to noise and atmospheric effects (Guanter et al., 2021) but represents only a narrow portion of the full SIF spectrum. Full-spectrum observations spanning both red and far-red regions provide richer information on vegetation function and stress (Verrelst et al., 2016). Spectral features such as the red-to-far-red ratio and emission shape offer additional physiological insight (Porcar-Castell et al., 2021). The upcoming FLEX mission will deliver full-spectrum SIF and enable improved characterization of photosynthesis and stress responses (Cogliati et al., 2015). Full-spectrum shape metrics like the red-to-far-red ratio reveal chlorophyll reabsorption and physiological stress more effectively than narrowband data (Buschmann, 2007, Agati et al., 1996, Ač et al., 2015). Additional parameters including peak position and spectral width further enhance the detection of subtle environmental stressors and strengthen correlations with canopy net photosynthesis (Subhash and Mohanan, 1997, Van Wittenberghe et al., 2013, Verrelst et al., 2016). Given these advantages, future efforts should aim to extend S3-SIF743 toward full-spectrum reconstruction, making use of the 21 OLCI bands to infer broadband SIF via spectral unmixing or data-driven reconstruction methods (Zhao et al., 2018, Morata et al., 2025). Such approaches, together with synergistic integration of S3-SIF743 and FLEX data, will enable cross-validation, ensure long-term continuity at sub-kilometer scales, and facilitate the derivation of empirical or physically based conversion schemes that link narrowband and broadband SIF. Specifically, the 300 m spatial resolution of S3-SIF743 directly matches the footprint of FLEX, enabling the characterization of sub-kilometer vegetation heterogeneity and providing a decadal historical baseline (starting from 2016) to contextualize upcoming FLEX observations. The framework is readily adaptable to FLEX applications; by using tandem-flight S3 observations, it has the potential to enhance the effective temporal resolution of FLEX’s high-quality but less frequent acquisitions. Furthermore, the synergy between these two missions facilitates a robust mutual validation and fusion scheme: S3-SIF743 provides an independent reference for cross-validating FLEX’s far-red signals, while the full-spectrum SIF (680–780 nm) from FLEX can be used to calibrate S3-based reconstruction models. Ultimately, this synergy is expected to significantly improve our capacity to monitor photosynthetic dynamics and vegetation stress with unprecedented spatiotemporal and spectral precision (Reyes-Muñoz et al., 2025).

Conclusions

We developed a downscaled 300 m, 4-day resolution global S3-SIF743 product derived from S3 OLCI observations through a RF framework trained against TROPOSIF743. The results demonstrate that S3-SIF743 reliably captures the spatiotemporal variability of SIF across multiple ecosystems, with consistent spatiotemporal patterns with both satellite- and tower-based reference data. As opposed to existing coarse-resolution products, S3-SIF743 provides unprecedented detail for monitoring vegetation dynamics at 300 m scales, particularly in heterogeneous agricultural landscapes. The integration of S3’s 300 m spatial resolution with the scalable processing capacity of GEE establishes a robust and reproducible framework for downscaled global 300 m S3-SIF743 mapping. The GEE-based implementation not only reduces computational costs but also enhances accessibility for the scientific community, facilitating near-real-time applications in vegetation monitoring and carbon cycle research. The S3-SIF743 product provides an essential complement to existing coarse-scale SIF products and offers a timely precursor to the upcoming FLEX mission. Future efforts should focus on extending the temporal record, enhancing global applicability, and integrating hybrid modeling approaches, thereby strengthening the role of SIF as a key indicator of terrestrial ecosystem function.
