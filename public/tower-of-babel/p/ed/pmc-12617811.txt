Metabolomic insights into the dynamic changes of volatile and non-volatile compounds in Butyriboletus roseoflavus during vacuum drying
Authors: Ling Song, Shimei Yang, Xiangqian Lv, Ruiping Luo, Yurong Liu, Xing Zhou, Mingying Gui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The dynamic changes in flavor and quality of Butyriboletus roseoflavus during vacuum drying at 60 °C were systematically characterized using sensory evaluation, colorimetry, targeted free amino acid analysis, and untargeted metabolomics. Fresh mushrooms exhibited bright color with predominant mushroom and earthy aromas, while prolonged drying induced color darkening and shifted aroma toward fatty and roasted notes. Concurrently, drying significantly increased free amino acid levels, contributing to enhanced taste. Volatile analysis showed that these compounds were most abundant in fresh samples and decreased over the course of drying, with major aroma-active compounds such as 1-nonen-3-one, 2-methoxy-3,5-dimethylpyrazine, and 2-octenal declining as well. The 0–3 h and 6–9 h intervals were identified as critical periods showing the most pronounced changes in non-volatile metabolites. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified an interconnected network of metabolic pathways involved in quality formation during drying, including amino acid metabolism, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, one‑carbon metabolism, and secondary metabolism. This study provides the first comprehensive insight into the dynamics of flavor compounds in B. roseoflavus under vacuum drying, providing a theoretical framework for future research on its flavor chemistry and industrial processing.

Introduction

B. roseoflavus is a wild edible mushroom belonging to the phylum Basidiomycota, class Agaricomycetes, order Boletales, family Boletaceae, and genus Butyriboletus. It is favored by consumers for its distinctive aroma and flavor. This species predominantly inhabits coniferous forests dominated by Pinus yunnanensis and Pinus massoniana, exhibiting a typical “umbrella-shaped” morphology composed of a pileus and a stipe. The fruiting bodies, which can grow either upright or curved, are characterized by a pileus ranging from pink to rose-red and a lemon-yellow stipe. As a regionally characteristic edible mushroom, it is primarily found and consumed in Yunnan Province, with very few records documented beyond this region. Owing to its highly localized distribution and consumption, scientific research on B. roseoflavus remains limited, with most studies focusing on the isolation and identification of umami peptides (Yang, Che, Li, Zhang, & Hu, 2022), analysis of secondary metabolites (Su et al., 2020), rapid methods for determining total phenolic content (Chen, Liu, Li, & Wang, 2022), and the assessment of heavy metal and radionuclide risks (Falandysz, Zhang, & Saniewski, 2020). Nevertheless, systematic investigations into the volatile and non-volatile compounds of B. roseoflavus, as well as the impact of processing on its flavor quality, remain limited. Due to its high economic value and the current inability to achieve artificial cultivation, B. roseoflavus is entirely reliant on wild harvesting. However, its high moisture content and active metabolic activity make it highly perishable after post-harvesting. Drying, as a common post-harvest treatment, can effectively reduce product loss and extend shelf life (Kirkwood, Fisk, Xu, Reid, & Yang, 2025). Common drying methods include hot air drying, vacuum drying, and vacuum freeze-drying. Among these, hot air drying is the most energy-efficient but tends to cause significant degradation of bioactive components. In contrast, vacuum freeze-drying yields high-quality products, yet its high equipment and operational costs limit widespread use in conventional food processing (Li, Ren, et al., 2023). Vacuum drying is operated under reduced pressure, which effectively lowers the boiling point of moisture and enables it to evaporate at relatively low temperatures (Zhang et al., 2025). This method better preserves heat-sensitive compounds such as vitamins, pigments, and flavor substances (İsmail & Gökçe Kocabay, 2022). Compared with hot air drying, vacuum drying can retain more than twice the amount of free amino acids in mushrooms (Yin et al., 2022). As a result, vacuum drying has been widely applied in edible mushroom processing.

The unique geographic and climatic conditions of Yunnan Province, China, with a forest coverage rate of 59.3 %, support a rich diversity of wild edible fungi. Currently, 882 species of wild mushrooms have been identified in Yunnan, accounting for 91 % of all wild fungi reported in China (Wang et al., 2014). The genus Boletus is one of the most representative wild mushrooms in Yunnan, with its production accounting for over 90 % of the national Boletus yield. According to data from the China Edible Fungi Association (https://bigdata.cefa.org.cn/), the Boletus output in Yunnan reached 136,481.3 tons in 2023. In recent years, Boletus have garnered increasing consumer interest due to their nutritional, medicinal, and sensory attributes, which has driven the development of flavoromics studies in this group. For instance, Suillus punctipes is known for its characteristic mushroom, apricot, and citrus peel aroma, attributed to key volatiles such as 1-octen-3-one, 1-octen-3-ol, (2E)-oct-2-enal, linalool, δ-dodecalactone, as well as mixtures of octanal, nonanal, and decanal (Murray, Wickramasinghe, & Munafo, 2020). Boletus bainiugan develops nutty, fruity, and green notes after drying, mainly due to (2S,4R)-4-methyl-2-(2-methylprop-1-en-1-yl)tetrahydro-2H-pyran (Zheng, Li, Liu, & Wang, 2025). In Boletus edulis, C8 compounds, 3-methylbutanal, (E, E)-2,4-decadienal, and (E, E)-2,4-nonadienal contribute to its characteristic fresh aroma, whereas drying reduces its earthy and herbal notes. 3-(methylthio) propanal, 1-octen-3-one, and pyrazines have been identified as the predominant odor-active compounds in this species (Zhang et al., 2018). These findings suggest that identifying the key odorants in the Boletaceae species is essential for advancing the flavor chemistry of edible mushrooms.

In this study, B. roseoflavus samples subjected to different vacuum drying durations were analyzed using DNA sequence identification, sensory evaluation, colorimetric analysis, targeted determination of free amino acids, and untargeted metabolomic profiling of both volatile and non-volatile compounds. The study aimed to elucidate the dynamic changes in quality characteristics and flavor compounds of B. roseoflavus during processing. The findings provide scientific evidence for the influence of drying on flavor formation in B. roseoflavus and lay a theoretical foundation for its future processing and utilization.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Specimens of B. roseoflavus were collected from Yimen County, Yuxi City, Yunnan Province, China (24°67′N, 102°11′E) on July 2, 2024. To ensure consistency, all samples were harvested from a single site at an altitude of 1691.07 m, where the average annual temperature is 16.5 °C and the annual precipitation ranges from 800 to 900 mm. The maturity of B. roseoflavus was assessed based on its color, shape, and size. Samples of uniform maturity stage were harvested for subsequent analyses, with three biological replicates per treatment.

Molecular identification

Genomic DNA was extracted from B. roseoflavus fruiting bodies using a fungal DNA extraction kit (D2300, Solarbio Life Sciences, Beijing, China). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region was performed using universal primers ITS1 and ITS4 (White, Bruns, Lee, & Taylor, 1990). Each 50 μL PCR reaction contained 45 μL of T6 Super PCR Mix Ver.2 (1.1×), 2 μL of forward primer, 2 μL of reverse primer, and 1 μL of genomic DNA. The PCR conditions included initial denaturation at 94 °C for 4 min, followed by 25 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 39 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 20 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The amplified PCR products were visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis, purified, and subsequently sequenced using a 3730XL DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, USA). The assembled sequence files were analyzed by alignment against the NCBI nucleotide database using the NCBI BLAST tool.

Vacuum drying

Fresh B. roseoflavus samples (150 g each) were used for the vacuum drying experiments. The mushrooms were evenly placed in a vacuum drying oven (DZF6020, Yuhua Instrument Company, China). Based on preliminary trials and the methods reported by Yang et al. (2024) and Hou et al. (2022), the drying temperature was set at 60 °C, which effectively balanced the drying efficiency of Boletus and the preservation of heat-sensitive volatile compounds. Drying durations were set at 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 h. At each interval, three replicate samples of B. roseoflavus were collected, wrapped in aluminum foil, and stored at −80 °C in an ultra-low temperature freezer for subsequent analysis.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory analysis was conducted according to the method described by Yang, Chen, Fu, Liu, and Bi (2025). A trained panel consisting of eight members (four males and four females) from Yunnan Agricultural University, all of whom had at least one year of experience in sensory evaluation and participated voluntarily in the study. B. roseoflavus samples from different drying intervals were cut into uniform pieces and placed in covered black containers. Nine aroma attributes were evaluated: fresh mushroom, earthy, floral, fruity, sweet, roasted mushroom, nutty, meaty, and fatty notes. Aroma intensity was rated on a 0–5 scale, with 0 representing the lowest intensity and 5 the highest.

Color measurement

Color changes in B. roseoflavus under different drying durations were measured using a CM-5 spectrophotometer (Konica Minolta, Japan). Prior to measurement, the device was calibrated with standard white and black reference plates. Each sample was measured in triplicate, and the color coordinates L*, a*, and b* were recorded. The color difference (ΔE) and chroma (C) were calculated according to Eqs. (1), (2), respectively (Voss, 1992):(1)ΔE=L−L02+a−a02+b−b02

In Eq. (1), L, a, and b represent the measured color values of the samples at different drying times (0, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 h), while L₀, a₀, and b₀ represent the corresponding values of the fresh sample at 0 h.(2)C=a∗2+b∗2

Determination of free amino acids

Sixteen amino acid standards were purchased from TCI (Shanghai, China). Free amino acids in B. roseoflavus samples were analyzed using an S433D amino acid analyzer (Sykam GmbH, Germany) according to the GB 5009.124–2016 protocol. In brief, analyses were performed using a sodium salt buffer system with a chromatographic flow rate of 0.2 mL/min and an injection volume of 0.05 mL. The dilution factors were 1× for standards and blanks and 10× for samples. Detection was carried out at 440 nm and 570 nm, with proline monitored at 440 nm and all other amino acids were detected at 570 nm.

To assess the contribution of each free amino acid to the taste of B. roseoflavus, the taste activity value (TAV) was calculated using Eq. (3):(3)TAV=C/Twhere C is the absolute concentration of the free amino acid and T is its corresponding taste threshold.

Analysis of volatile compounds

Volatile compounds were analyzed using HS-SPME coupled with GC–MS/MS, employing an SPME Arrow device (CTC Analytics AG) and a GC–MS/MS system (8890-7000D, Agilent). Samples were ground in liquid nitrogen, and 500 mg was placed into a headspace vial with 20 μL of internal standard solution (3-hexanone-2,2,4,4-d4, 10 μg/mL). The SPME Arrow was equipped with a 120 μm DVB/CWR/PDMS fiber (Agilent, Austin, TX, USA). Extraction was performed after equilibration with agitation at 60 °C for 5 min, followed by adsorption for 15 min. The fiber was desorbed in the injector at 250 °C for 5 min. Separation was achieved on a DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). The injector temperature was set at 250 °C, with helium as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.2 mL/min. The oven program was as follows: 40 °C for 4 min; ramped to 100 °C at 10 °C/min and held for 1 min; ramped to 180 °C at 7 °C/min and held for 1.5 min; and finally ramped to 280 °C at 25 °C/min and held for 5 min. Mass spectrometry was conducted in electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV, with an ion source temperature of 230 °C.

Volatile compounds were identified by matching retention times, quantitative ions, and qualitative ions with established standards in our in-house database. A compound was considered identified only when its retention time was consistent with that of the corresponding standard and its characteristic ions were simultaneously detected in the deconvoluted mass spectra (Yuan et al., 2022). Quantification was performed by calculating peak intensities relative to the internal standard (Cheng et al., 2025).

To evaluate the contribution of each volatile compound to the overall aroma of B. roseoflavus, the relative odor activity value (rOAV) was calculated using Eq. (4):(4)rOAV=C/Twhere C is the relative concentration of the volatile compound and T is its odor threshold in water.

Analysis of non-volatile compounds

Non-volatile metabolites were analyzed using Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry (UPLC–MS/MS), following the method of Cheng et al. (2025) with minor modifications. The analysis was performed using a TripleTOF 6600+ mass spectrometer (SCIEX) coupled with a LC-30 A system (Shimadzu, Japan) and a UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, USA). Column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, the flow rate was set to 0.40 mL/min, and the injection volume was 4 μL. Mobile phase A consisted of ultrapure water with 0.1 % formic acid, and mobile phase B was acetonitrile with 0.1 % formic acid. Both positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes were applied, with an ion source temperature of 550 °C. Data acquisition was carried out over 10 min, with spray voltages of +3.5 kV and −3.5 kV, and collision energies of 10/30. Metabolite identification and quantification were performed following the method described by Huang et al. (2024).

Statistical analysis

The phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA 12 software. Sensory radar plots were generated using Origin 2022. Principal component analysis (PCA), orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), and heatmaps were performed using R (version 4.2.0). Venn diagrams, volcano plots, and enrichment map of differential metabolite pathways were generated via the Metware Cloud platform. Statistical significance analysis was conducted using SPSS 26. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (N = 3). Different letters within the same row indicate significant differences (p < 0.05).

Results and analysis

Molecular characterization of B. Roseoflavus

The collected specimens were identified based on morphological features and rDNA ITS sequencing. The obtained sequences were submitted to GenBank and assigned accession number PV294963. Comparative analysis with existing sequences in the GenBank database revealed a 99 % similarity with B. roseoflavus. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining method (Fig. S1), showing that the sample clustered closely with sequence KC184438.1 and shared a strong genetic relationship with B. roseoflavus, supported by a bootstrap value of 100. These results confirm that the wild mushroom collected was B. roseoflavus at the molecular level.

Sensory evaluation of B. Roseoflavus

The aroma profiles of B. roseoflavus at different drying durations are illustrated in the radar chart (Fig. S2). At 0 h, fresh samples displayed pronounced mushroom-like and earthy aromas, while the roasted note was the weakest. After 3 h, the mushroom and earthy notes slightly diminished, and meaty notes began to emerge. At 6 h, the initial aromas continued to weaken, accompanied by a progressive increase in nutty, roasted, and sweet attributes. By 9 h, the intensities of fatty, nutty, roasted, and sweet notes had further increased. At 12 h, a marked reduction in mushroom aroma was observed, while floral and fruity notes became more prominent. At 15 h, mushroom and earthy aromas were faint, whereas other sensory attributes showed strong intensities. These findings suggest that the aroma complexity of B. roseoflavus increases progressively with drying time.

Color analysis of B. Roseoflavus

The effects of vacuum drying duration on the color of B. roseoflavus are summarized in Table 1. The L* value, representing lightness, was initially the highest at 0 h and declined significantly with prolonged drying, reaching the lowest value at 15 h. Previous studies have indicated that a decline in L* reflects the degree of browning in dried samples (Sahoo, Titikshya, Aradwad, Kumar, & Naik, 2022), suggesting that extended drying times accelerate browning of the fruiting bodies. All samples exhibited positive a* values, indicating a red hue, which increased progressively with drying time. Similarly, the b* values, representing yellowness, remained positive and increased significantly over time. The color difference (ΔE) relative to the fresh sample increased with drying time, reflecting more substantial changes in surface appearance. Chroma values, which are positively correlated with color saturation and intensity (Maskan, 2001), also increased continuously during drying. Similar trends have been reported in dried shiitake mushrooms, yam, and kiwifruit, where both enzymatic browning (driven by cellular respiration) and non-enzymatic browning (e.g., Maillard reaction) contributed to color changes (Sissons, Davila, & Du, 2022). Determination of free amino acids

Amino acids are abundant in edible fungi, where they not only serve as the fundamental units of proteins but also contribute significantly to the unique flavor profile of mushrooms. In the present study, 16 free amino acids were identified and classified into four categories based on their taste characteristics: umami, sweet, bittersweet, and bitter (López-Martínez, Toldrá, & Mora, 2023). Their concentrations at different drying time points are presented in Table 2. At 0 h, each amino acids exhibited the lowest concentrations, whereas at 15 h, all 16 amino acids reached their peak levels. The total free amino acid content increased from 12.41 mg/g at 0 h to 65.18 mg/g at 15 h, a 5.25-fold enhancement, indicating that vacuum drying significantly promotes the accumulation of free amino acids in B. roseoflavus. This trend is consistent with previous findings in Lentinus edodes (Wen et al., 2022). The substantial increase may result from heat-induced protein breakdown, which releases free amino acids, along with the concentration of flavor-active components as moisture content declines (Wen et al., 2022). From the perspective of taste classification, bitter amino acids consistently exhibited the highest total concentration throughout the drying process (0–15 h), followed by sweet, umami, and bittersweet amino acids. Despite the predominance of bitter-tasting amino acids, the overall flavor perception of the dried samples is likely to remain favorable, as mushroom flavor arises from the complex interplay of various taste-active compounds (Manninen, Rotola-Pukkila, Aisala, Hopia, & Laaksonen, 2018). Amino acids with TAV >1 were defined as key taste-active components contributing significantly to flavor. The TAVs of B. roseoflavus samples at different drying times were calculated according to reported taste threshold values (Yin et al., 2022) (Table 3). Glutamic acid (Glu), alanine (Ala), and histidine (His) consistently exhibited TAV >1 across all drying stages and had the highest TAVs among the detected amino acids, suggesting their dominant roles in overall taste perception. Furthermore, the number of amino acids with TAV >1 increased progressively from 3 at 0 h to 15 at 15 h, indicating a substantial enhancement in overall flavor intensity during drying. Analysis of volatile compounds

A total of 446 volatile compounds were identified in B. roseoflavus samples and categorized into 15 chemical classes (Fig. 1A). Among them, ketones were the most abundant, accounting for 14.8 %, followed by esters (14.35 %). Alcohols and terpenes ranked third, each comprising 13.45 %, while heterocyclic compounds contributed 11.43 %. The remaining classes each accounted for less than 10 % of the total volatile profile. Principal component analysis (PCA) of the volatile compounds (Fig. 1B) revealed distinct differences in aroma composition among samples subjected to different drying durations. Samples dried for 3 h and 6 h clustered together, while those dried for 9 h, 12 h, and 15 h formed another group. In contrast, the fresh sample (0 h) appeared as a separate cluster, reflecting the strong influence of drying time on the aroma profile. According to Yang et al. (2024), the differences in volatile compounds between short-term and long-term drying stages can be attributed to the progression of Maillard reactions. During the early stages of drying, initial Maillard products such as Schiff bases and Amadori compounds are primarily formed, which contribute mild but distinctive flavor notes. At this point, the reaction between amino acids and reducing sugars is still limited due to the short exposure time, resulting in minimal nutritional degradation. As drying progresses, prolonged heating drives the Maillard reaction further, generating a variety of complex intermediates and advanced products—including reductones, melanoidins, pyridines, and benzopyridines. These heterocyclic compounds enrich the volatile profile and enhance the overall aroma complexity of the dried mushrooms. As shown in Fig. 1C, the total content of volatile compounds in B. roseoflavus was highest in the fresh sample (0 h), reaching 808.9 μg/g, and decreased steadily with extended drying, dropping to 176.8 μg/g at 15 h. A similar downward trend was observed across all individual chemical classes. However, the relative proportions of different compound classes exhibited distinct variation patterns during drying (Fig. 1D). The proportions of esters, heterocyclic compounds, hydrocarbons, terpenes, aldehydes, and others increased with drying time, whereas that of ketones showed a continuous decline. Previous studies by Li, Zhang, et al. (2023) demonstrated that drying leads to the evaporation and degradation of volatile compounds, as well as their conversion into new compounds via thermal reactions. These processes reduce the overall volatile content and may shift the composition by promoting the formation of new compounds—particularly heterocyclic compounds with roasted flavor characteristics. Therefore, precise control of drying duration and processing parameters is crucial to minimize the loss of desirable volatiles and preserve the characteristic aroma profile of the mushroom.Fig. 1Comprehensive analysis of volatile metabolites in Butyriboletus roseoflavus. Analysis of key volatile compounds

Although a wide variety of volatile compounds were detected in B. roseoflavus samples, most of them contribute only marginally to the overall aroma, acting primarily as modifiers rather than key aroma components. Only compounds with a relative odor activity value (rOAV) exceeding 1 are considered to significantly impact perceived flavor (Jiang et al., 2024). The 82 compounds with rOAV >1 were grouped using K-means clustering based on their normalized relative abundances, resulting in seven distinct clusters with similar variation patterns (Fig. 2). The 24 compounds in Cluster 1 exhibited a continuous decreasing trend, consistent with the overall reduction in total volatile content. These compounds were primarily associated with green, fresh, earthy, and raw mushroom-like aromas, such as 1-octen-3-ol, 1-octen-3-one, 2-octanone, (E)-2-octenal, and β-pinene. These trends were consistent with the results of sensory evaluation and in agreement with the finding reported by Hu et al. (2025). The decrease in these compounds may be attributed to chemical transformations during heat treatment, whereby volatile substances responsible for fresh and green aromas were converted into other compounds (Zeng, Watanabe, & Yang, 2019). Clusters 2 and 3 included 43 compounds that peaked at 3 h of drying, declined moderately at 6 h, and decreased sharply from 6 to 15 h. This pattern is likely driven by accelerated Maillard reactions, enhanced lipid oxidation, and the release of flavor precursors during the initial stages of drying, which promoted a rapid increase in alcohols, aldehydes, and heterocyclic compounds. As drying progressed, the decline in water activity inhibited water-dependent chemical reactions, leading to a gradual decrease in the levels of volatile compounds (Suharta, Hunaefi, & Wijaya, 2021). In addition, compounds in Clusters 5, 6, and 7 exhibited a significant increase in abundance starting at 9 h of drying. These compounds were predominantly ketones and aldehydes, such as undecenal, 2-undecanone, and 3-nonenone, and were associated with sweet, fatty, waxy, and roasted aroma characteristics.Fig. 2K-means clustering analysis of volatile metabolites.Fig. 2

Pairwise comparisons between samples dried for different durations were performed using OPLS-DA (Fig. S3). Additionally, 200-time permutation tests confirmed model robustness with Q2 values showing P <0.05 (Fig. S4), indicating that the models were both effective and stable. Variable importance in projection (VIP) scores was derived from the OPLS-DA models. Differential metabolites between paired drying time points were identified using a VIP score >1 combined with a fold change threshold of ≥2 or ≤0.5. The results are presented in Fig. S5. Among the differential metabolites, 35 volatile compounds with rOAV >1 were identified as key aroma-active substances in B. roseoflavus (Table S1). Of these, 1-nonen-3-one, known for its mushroom-like aroma, 2-methoxy-3,5-dimethylpyrazine, which imparts a bready note, and 2-octenal, which contributes fatty and green odors, were the top three compounds with the highest aroma contribution across all samples. These findings collectively demonstrate that vacuum drying substantially reshaped the characteristic aroma profile of B. roseoflavus.

Analysis of non-volatile compounds

A total of 3783 non-volatile metabolites were identified in B. roseoflavus and grouped into 14 chemical classes (Fig. 3A). Amino acids and their derivatives accounted for the largest proportion (42.12 %), followed by organic acids (13.46 %), benzene and substituted derivatives (8.86 %), and alkaloids (5.39 %), while other classes each contributed less than 5 %. Relative abundance, based on peak area, is shown in Fig. 3C. Notably, fresh B. roseoflavus samples exhibited lower levels of amino acids and their derivatives compared to dried samples. With increasing drying time, these compounds accumulated steadily, a trend consistent with the results of targeted free amino acid analysis. In contrast, the contents of glycerophospholipids, lipids, lignans, and coumarins gradually decreased throughout the drying process. Lipids are important nutritional components in Boletus species and are structurally diverse, often containing high levels of bioactive unsaturated fatty acids and phospholipids (Yang et al., 2021). Wang et al. proposed that the reduction in lipid and phospholipid content during drying is primarily due to enzymatic hydrolysis and lipid oxidation. Notably, these undesirable changes may be mitigated by optimizing drying parameters to minimize oxidation-induced quality loss (Wang, Chen, Zhao, Zhang, & Fang, 2022). In addition, we observed that the levels of alkaloids and phenolic acids increased with prolonged drying, while the contents of other compound classes remained relatively stable. Wild edible mushrooms are known to contain a wide variety of alkaloids—naturally occurring nitrogen-containing organic compounds. Some alkaloids exhibit desirable bioactivities and possess pharmacological effects in humans and animals, while others may contain toxic constituents that pose a risk of food poisoning if not properly processed. Alkaloids are generally heat-sensitive and prone to degradation at high temperatures. In this study, over 200 alkaloids were identified in B. roseoflavus, including muscarine, hypaconitine, and tryptamine. However, vacuum drying at 60 °C was insufficient to fully degrade the toxic alkaloids present in the samples. Therefore, high-temperature cooking is still required following drying to ensure the safety of B. roseoflavus for consumption.Fig. 3Comprehensive analysis of non-volatile metabolites in Butyriboletus roseoflavus. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on the non-volatile metabolites (Fig. 3B), revealing clear differences in compound profiles among samples subjected to different drying durations. The clustering pattern mirrored that of volatile compounds: the 0 h sample formed an independent cluster, the 3 h and 6 h samples clustered together, and the 9 h, 12 h, and 15 h samples formed a third group. Pairwise comparisons of samples using orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) further validated these differences ((Fig. S6). Additionally, permutation testing with 200 iterations confirmed model robustness, with all Q2 values showing P <0.05 ((Fig. S7). Based on the OPLS-DA models, variable importance in projection (VIP) scores were calculated, and differential metabolites were screened using the criteria of P <0.05, VIP >1, and fold change ≥2 or ≤0.5. The results (Fig. 3D) identified 19 shared differential metabolites detected across all comparisons. Notably, the highest number of unique differential metabolites was observed between 3 h and 0 h (451 compounds), followed by the 9 h versus 6 h comparison (324 compounds).

These results indicate that the periods of 0–3 h and 6–9 h represent the most significant phase of non-volatile compound transformation, while prolonged drying duration serves as a critical determinant for flavor development in B. roseoflavus. From a practical standpoint, identifying 0–3 h and 6–9 h as critical transformation stages provides valuable implications for process optimization. The 0–3 h stage corresponds to the early dehydration phase, during which rapid moisture loss and the initiation of Maillard reactions occur. Fine-tuning parameters such as vacuum degree and temperature during this stage could maintain a good microstructure, improve rehydration ratio, and reduce excessive volatile loss (Tian, Zhao, Huang, Zeng, & Zheng, 2016). In contrast, the 6–9 h stage represents a transition phase characterized by intensified amino acid accumulation and secondary metabolic activity. Implementing a segmented or stepwise drying strategy, such as program-controlled temperature drying, can promote the retention of key aroma-active compounds and enhance overall flavor preservation (Liu, Luo, Chitrakar, Liu, & Wang, 2025). Thus, recognizing these two critical time windows provides a practical basis for developing more precise, energy-efficient, and quality-oriented drying protocols for B. roseoflavus and related edible mushrooms.

The biosynthetic pathways involved the biosynthesis of amino acids, alkaloids, nucleotide sugars, polyketide sugar units, folate, secondary metabolites, and cofactors. We specifically focused on the amino acid metabolism and one‑carbon cycle network centered around the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle during the vacuum drying of B. roseoflavus (Fig. 5). Within the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, phenylalanine undergoes deamination catalyzed by phenylalanine ammonia-lyase to form cinnamic acid (Duan, 2025), which can be subsequently converted, either directly or indirectly, into caffeic acid. Certain fungi within the Basidiomycota and Ascomycota are capable of producing coumarins and their derivatives through secondary metabolism (Costa, Tavares, & Oliveira, 2016). In B. roseoflavus, caffeic acid is likely converted into coumarin via enzymatic hydroxylation and lactonization, which can further dimerize to form dicoumarol. Dicoumarol was identified as a differential metabolite in this study and is widely recognized for its anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and potent anticoagulant properties. It was first isolated from Melilotus officinalis (L.) Pall in 1940. The biosynthesis of amino acids is closely linked to the TCA cycle, which not only facilitates the production of several key differential metabolites but also provides essential intermediates that serve as precursors for amino acid synthesis. Specifically, α-ketoglutarate undergoes reductive amination catalyzed by glutamate dehydrogenase to form glutamate. Glutamate can then be amidated to generate glutamine, whose concentration peaked at 12 h of vacuum drying. Additionally, glutamate participates in a series of enzymatic reactions to form proline and ornithine. Ornithine, under the action of ornithine carbamoyltransferase, is carbamoylated to produce citrulline. Citrulline then undergoes two sequential enzymatic steps—argininosuccinate synthesis and cleavage—to ultimately yield arginine. During the drying process, the level of α-ketoglutarate gradually declined, likely due to its continuous involvement in metabolic reactions. The fluctuating levels of ornithine and citrulline suggest their dynamic participation in both synthesis and conversion processes. As end-products of the glutamate family amino acid biosynthetic pathway, the concentrations of arginine, proline, and glutamine continued to accumulate throughout the drying period. In the biosynthesis of aspartate family amino acids, oxaloacetate—a key intermediate of the TCA cycle—undergoes transamination with glutamate to produce aspartate. Aspartate serves as a precursor for the biosynthesis of lysine, homoserine, threonine, and isoleucine, as well as for folate metabolism and the metabolic pathways of cysteine and methionine. The synergistic relationship between folate and methionine metabolism plays a vital role in the nutritional value and flavor development of mushrooms. Studies have shown that humans lack the biochemical capacity to synthesize folate de novo. Instead, tetrahydrofolate (THF), the biologically active and absorbable form of folate, is essential for human growth and development. Folate deficiency can lead to severe health issues, including congenital heart defects in fetuses and megaloblastic anemia in adults (Wu & Tam, 2021). 5-Methyltetrahydrofolate (5-MTHF) participates in the remethylation of homocysteine to methionine. The primary function of the methionine cycle is to provide methyl donors in the form of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), which facilitates methyl group transfer and regeneration. This cycle is essential for the methylation of various secondary metabolites, such as flavonoids and terpenoids (Sakunphueak, Tansakul, Umehara, Noguchi, & Panichayupakaranant, 2013). Moreover, Strecker degradation of methionine serves as a major source of sulfur-containing volatile compounds (Ballance, 1961). Xi, Sun, Qian, Chen, and Zhao (2025) reported that the dynamics of sulfur compounds during the drying of Lentinus edodes are largely driven by cysteine and methionine metabolism. These sulfur-containing volatiles are key contributors to onion-like, meaty, and vegetable-like aroma notes in Boletus species (Yang et al., 2024).Fig. 5Amino acid metabolism and one‑carbon metabolic network based on the TCA cycle.Fig. 5

In summary, the metabolic pathway network represents a dynamic system of interconnected biochemical reactions within the organism, facilitating diverse biosynthetic processes through coordinated energy supply, precursor sharing, and enzymatic regulation. Moreover, it tightly links the formation of both volatile and non-volatile compounds. These findings provide valuable insights into the metabolic pathways underlying flavor compound formation in B. roseoflavus during vacuum drying.

Conclusion

In this study, vacuum drying had a significant impact on the flavor and quality attributes of B. roseoflavus. Sensory and color analyses revealed that fresh samples exhibited a more vivid and bright appearance, with characteristic mushroom-like and earthy aromas. As drying time increased, the samples gradually darkened, and their aroma profile shifted toward fatty and roasted notes. Absolute quantification of free amino acids revealed that fresh mushrooms contained low levels of free amino acids and a few taste-active compounds (TAV >1). However, prolonged drying promoted protein degradation, leading to a substantial increase in free amino acids and taste-active components, thereby enhancing overall taste intensity.

A total of 446 volatile compounds and 3783 non-volatile metabolites were identified using HS-SPME–GC–MS/MS and UPLC–MS/MS, respectively. Volatile content was highest in fresh samples and decreased with drying duration, mainly due to the progression of Maillard and lipid oxidation reactions. Among 82 key aroma-active compounds (rOAV >1), green and earthy volatiles such as 1-octen-3-ol and (E)-2-octenal declined over time, while sweet, fatty, and roasted volatiles including 2-undecanone and 3-nonenone became more prominent. Non-volatile metabolomic profiling and KEGG pathway enrichment revealed that amino acid metabolism, the TCA cycle, one‑carbon metabolism, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis collectively shaped the quality formation during drying.

Importantly, two critical transformation periods of 0–3 h and 6–9 h were identified as key windows showing the most pronounced metabolic changes. The early stage (0−3 h) corresponds to rapid moisture removal and the onset of Maillard reactions, whereas the later transition stage (6–9 h) is characterized by intensified amino acid accumulation and secondary metabolism. Targeted control of temperature and vacuum degree during these phases could effectively balance dehydration with chemical reactions, suppress excessive browning, and preserve desirable aroma-active compounds.

Overall, this study provides the first systematic insight into the dynamic flavor formation and quality evolution of B. roseoflavus under vacuum drying. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the underlying metabolic mechanisms but also offer practical guidance for the design of standardized, energy-efficient, and flavor-oriented drying protocols applicable to B. roseoflavus and other high-value wild mushrooms.
