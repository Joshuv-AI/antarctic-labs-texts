Research on drying kinetics, microstructure, and flavor changes of areca nut drying process based on water molecule migration
Authors: Jing Zhang, Xuejie Feng, Shenghua Xie, Yiwang Zhong, Yangyang Sun, Weizhong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study utilized LF-NMR, SEM, and GC × GC-TOFMS to analyze the distribution of water molecules, microstructure, and volatile compounds during drying process of areca nuts. A drying kinetic model was also established. The results showed that during drying, free water on the surface of the areca nut is expelled first, followed by the migration of moisture from the interior to the surface. In blanched areca nuts, a portion of free water is converted into bound water during 0–16 h drying stage. The fiber layers becoming more ordered, dense and compact as drying time increases. Blanching treatment and higher drying temperatures effectively reduce drying time and Ea. The Page model for drying kinetics demonstrated high fitting accuracy for the areca nut drying process with Pearson correlation coefficients and R2 values exceeding 0.99 and 0.98. Aldehydes were identified as the primary contributors to the characteristic “fresh aroma” of fresh areca nuts, while hydrocarbons were the main source of the “intense aroma” in dried blanched areca nuts. Drying significantly influenced the relative concentrations of aldehydes and hydrocarbons in areca nuts.

Introduction

Areca catechu L. is an evergreen palm tree cultivated in South Asia, Southeast Asia, and tropical and subtropical regions of southern China. In China, areca nuts are mainly cultivated in Hainan Province, with a current planting area of approximately 153,333 ha and an annual output exceeding 600,000 metric tons. In China, areca nuts are primarily consumed in two forms: fresh fruit and chewing blocks. While a small portion (approximately 5% of the total output) of fresh areca nuts is eaten directly, the majority (approximately 95%) are processed into chewable products for consumers. Fresh areca nuts have a high moisture content, making them highly perishable and difficult to store or transport over extended periods.

Thermal processing is a common food processing method aimed at sterilization, enzyme inactivation, and shelf-life extension (Masouleh, Nateghi, & Moslemi, 2022; Zhang, Zhu, Wu, & Liu, 2024). Drying is the most common method to address storage challenges and extend shelf life, serving as the first step in areca nut processing. By reducing moisture content, drying slows spoilage, prolongs preservation, and ultimately influences the quality of the final product (Sagar & Kumar, 2010; Zhang et al., 2017). Moisture in areca nuts exists primarily in two states based on binding characteristics: free water and bound water (Wang, Xu, Wei, & Zeng, 2018). The drying process involves dynamic changes in the distribution and binding states of water. Moreover, moisture migration during drying is closely linked to alterations in fibrous microstructure and the formation or loss of flavor compounds (Afolabi, 2014). Therefore, an in-depth study of water molecule migration patterns during areca nut drying and their effects on microstructure and flavor is crucial for optimizing drying techniques and enhancing product quality.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) proved to be a useful technique to observe the internal morphology and water dynamic processes in food processing. MRI is used to detect the movement of moisture during the drying process. In recent times, the applications of LF-NMR and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have significantly increased due to their sensitivity, fast analysis speed, and non-invasiveness. Existing studies have reported the use of LF-NMR and MRI to analyze the effects of drying processes on moisture distribution in tissues such as mushrooms, broccoli, carrots, and eggplants, as well as the dynamic moisture migration patterns during yellow peach processing and kiwifruit storage (Adiletta et al., 2014; Sun, Zhang, Mujumdar, & Yang, 2019; Taglienti, Massantini, Botondi, Mencarelli, & Valentini, 2009; Xu, Jin, Zhang, & Chen, 2017; Zhao et al., 2019; Zhou, Ye, Yu, Su, & Du, 2016). However, research on areca nut drying remains relatively limited. In particular, systematic investigations into the dynamic changes of water molecules during drying and their impacts on the microstructure and flavor compounds of areca nuts have yet to be thoroughly conducted. The combined analysis of LF-NMR, SEM, and GC × GC-TOFMS can help elucidate the correlation between water molecule migration and the microstructure of fibers as well as flavor compounds.

This study used LF-NMR, SEM, and GC × GC-TOFMS to investigate the dynamic migration and distribution of water molecules and the dynamic changes of flavor substances during the drying process of areca nut. A kinetic model of areca nut drying was established, and the correlation between water molecule distribution and areca nut microstructure and flavor substances changes was explored. The aim is to provide theoretical basis and technical support for the precise regulation of areca nut drying process, ultimately achieving the improvement of areca nut product quality and sustainable development of the industry.

Materials and methods

Materials

ANs were purchased from a farmer in Sanya, Hainan Province, China.

Methods

The drying process of areca nut

Uniformly sized fresh areca nuts were selected and blanched in boiling water for 30 min to obtain heat-treated areca nuts. Subsequently, both fresh areca nuts and blanched areca nuts were dried at 50 °C, 55 °C and 60 °C respectively. Samples were collected at drying intervals of 2 h, 5 h, 8 h, 12 h, 16 h, 20 h, 23 h, 26 h, 30 h, and 36 h for water status and distribution microstructure change analysis. At each time point, five areca nuts were sampled for analysis.

T2 relaxation spectra and magnetic resonance imaging of areca nut

The fresh and blanched areca nuts sampled at different drying intervals were analyzed using a low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) analyzer (MacroMR20-060H-I). The Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) sequence was employed to determine T2 relaxation values during the drying process, while the spin echo (SE) sequence was used to obtain proton density-weighted sagittal images of the samples. The testing parameters were configured as follows: Basic parameters: Resonance frequency = 21 MHz, Magnet temperature = 32.00 ± 0.01 °C, Probe coil diameter = 25 mm. CPMG sequence parameters: Spectral width (SW) = 200 KHz, Sampling frequency (SF) = 21 MHz, Offset frequency (O1) = 34,514 Hz, 90°pulse width (P1) = 6.2 μs, 180°pulse width (P2) = 11.6 μs, RF dead time (RFD) = 0.08 ms, Receiver gain (RG1) = 10.0 dB, Digital receiver gain (DRG1) = 3, Preamp gain (PRG) = 1, Echo time (TE) = 0.2 ms, Number of echoes (NECH) = 10,000, Repetition time (TW) = 4000 ms, Number of scans (NS) = 16. Inversion parameters: SRIT model, Smoothing factor = 0, Tmin = 0.01 ms, Tmax = 10,000 ms, Iterations = 10,000. SE sequence parameters: Field of view (FOV) = 80 × 40 mm, Repetition time (TR) = 600 ms, Echo time (TE) = 11.02 ms, Read size = 256, Phase size = 128, Slice count = 4, Slice thickness = 5 mm, Slice gap = 1 mm, Averages = 8, Echo position = 30%.

Establishment of drying kinetic models

Calculation of moisture content

Drying process: fresh areca nuts and blanched areca nuts were dried at 50 °C, 55 °C and 60 °C respectively, then collected at drying intervals of 2 h, 5 h, 8 h, 12 h, 16 h, 20 h, 23 h, 26 h, 30 h, 36 h 48 h, and 60 h. At each time point, five areca nuts were collected for analysis.

Fresh and blanched areca nuts were weighed at different drying intervals，denoted as wt, then dried to constant weight, denoted as w. The moisture content was calculated using the following formula:W%=wt−wwt∗100where wt represents the weight of areca nuts at drying time t, w denotes the dry weight of areca nuts.

Calculation of drying rate

The drying rate during areca nut processing was calculated according to the drying rate formula:DR=Xt+Δt−XtΔt

Where: DR represents drying rate g/(g·min), Δt denotes the time interval between two consecutive measurements (min), Xt indicates the dry-basis moisture content at drying time t (g/g), Xt+Δt represents the dry-basis moisture content at drying time t + Δt (g/g).

Calculation of moisture ratio

The moisture ratio was calculated using the following formula:MR=Xt−XeX0−Xe

Where MR represents Moisture ratio (dimensionless), Xt represents Dry-basis moisture content at time t (g/g), Xe represents equilibrium dry-basis moisture content (g/g), X0 represents initial dry-basis moisture content (g/g). Since Xe is significantly smaller than both X 0 and Xt, which can generally be neglected, the formula can be simplified to:MR=XtX0

Calculation of effective moisture diffusivity and activation energy

The effective moisture diffusivity (Deff) is a critical parameter for evaluating moisture migration in materials, reflecting dehydration characteristics under drying conditions. Deff was calculated using the following equation:lnMR=8π2−π2DeffL02tDeff=−L02kπ2

Where Deff represents moisture diffusivity (m2/h), L0 represents thickness of areca nut (m), t represents drying time (h), k represents slope of linear fitting between t and lnMR.

The relationship between Deff and drying temperature can be expressed by:Deff=D0exp−EaRT

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides of the above equation reveals a linear relationship between lnDeff and 1/T. The activation energy Ea can be calculated from the slope -Ea/R obtained through linear fitting.lnDeff=lnD0−EaRT

Drying model fitting

The drying curves of areca nut was fitted using five kinetic model equations, including Newton, Logarithmic, Page, Henderson and Pabis and Wang and Singh models. The mean square deviation (RMSE) and correlation coefficient (R2) were used as criteria to evaluate the goodness of fit of the mathematical models. A higher R2 (closer to 1) and a smaller RMSE indicate a better fit of the model to the actual process.

Model validation

The linear equation of the established model was used to calculate the MR of areca nuts at different drying times, recorded as the predicted values. Concurrently, areca nuts were removed at various drying time points after drying to determine their MR, recorded as the actual values. A t-test was conducted between the predicted and actual values to evaluate the model's accuracy based on the p-value. A P < 0.05 indicates a significant difference between the predicted and actual values, suggesting poor accuracy of the model. In contrast, P > 0.05 indicates no significant difference, demonstrating high accuracy of the established model.

Microstructural analysis

Areca nut at different drying stages were pre-frozen at −80 °C and subsequently freeze-dried using a vacuum freeze dryer. The central portion of the areca nut was selected and cut into 1 cm × 1 cm squares for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observation. The microstructure of the areca nut fibers was examined in cross-sectional and longitudinal views. Areca nut fiber blocks were mounted and sputter-coated with gold to enhance conductivity. Imaging was performed at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV.

Texture profile analysis

The textural properties of areca nuts at different drying stages were measured using a texture analyzer. After removal, the samples were equilibrated at room temperature for 1 h prior to testing. Testing Parameters: Mode: TPA, Probe: P36, Compression ratio: 20%, Pre-test speed: 1.0 mm/s, Test speed: 1.0 mm/s, Post-test speed: 1.0 mm/s, Target distance: 5 mm, Trigger force: 100 N, Interval between compressions: 3 s.

Analysis of volatile compounds

To investigate the impact of the drying process on the flavor profile of areca nuts, volatile compounds were analyzed at different drying stages. According to the areca nut drying curve and the results of water molecule migration, fresh areca nuts require more than 60 h of drying, while blanched areca nuts need 36 h to achieve a state where almost all free water is expelled. Therefore, the drying endpoints were set at 80 h and 36 h, respectively. Additionally, the moisture content of fresh areca nuts and blanched areca nuts was similar at 26 h and 5 h of drying, respectively. To better compare the changes in volatile substances during the drying process, these two time points were selected as intermediate drying stages. Drying process: fresh areca nuts were dried at 55 °C for 26 h and 80 h, blanched areca nuts were dried at 55 °C for 5 h and 36 h. The samples were divided into the following groups: fresh areca nuts (a), 26 h of drying (b),80 h of drying (c), blanched areca nuts (d), 5 h of drying (e), 36 h of drying (f).

Volatile compound extraction

Transfer 0.5 g of sample into a 20 mL headspace vial, then add 10 μL of internal standard solution to each samples, incubate the samples at 60 °C for 30 min. Put the SPME fiber to chamber at 270 °C for 10 min before extracted sample, then transfer the SPME to the incubator at 60 °C for 30 min, desorb the SPME fiber at 250 °C for 5 min in GC injector. Transfer 10 μL of the satursted alksnes into a 20 mL headspace vial, incubation extraction and injection.

GC × GC analysis

Analyses were carried out using a LECO Pegasus 4D instrument (LECO, St. Joseph, MI, USA) consisting of an Agilent 8890 A GC (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) system equipped with a split/splitless injector, and dual stage cryogenic modulator coupled with TOFMS detector. A Rxi-5Sil MS (30 m × 250 μm I.D., 0.25 μm) was used as first dimension column and Rxi-17Sil MS (2.0 m × 150 μm I.D., 0.15 μm) was used as a second-dimension column. The carrier gas was high purity helium (>99.999%) at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The temperature program of the oven was as followed: the oven temperature was held at 40 °C for 3 min at first, then raised to 250 °C at the rate of 5 °C/min and held for 5 min. The secondary oven temperature was operated at 5 °C higher than the first oven. The temperature of the modulator is always 15 °C higher than that of the second column. The modulator was operated with a 6 s modulation period. The GC injector temperature was 250 °C.

Mass spectrum conditions:Flavor substance was performed on LECO Pegasus BT 4D.The transfer line and TOF MS ion source temperature were set at 250 °C and 250 °Crespectively. The acquisition frequency was 200 spectra/s. The mass spectrometer was operated in the EI mode at 70 eV using a range of m/z 35–550 and the detector voltage of 2016 V.

Data analysis: The name of each compound, retention time, CAS number, database RI information, actual RI and its sample peak area and other information are obtained using ChromaTOF software. The final analysis result was obtained by integrating these interpretation information together. In order to enable data of different magnitudes to be compared, the internal standard normalization was performed on the raw data.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out in Excel, linear fitting, model establishment and graph plotting were carried out using Origin 2023 (Origin Lab, USA). One-way ANOVA and t-tests were perfprmed using SPSS 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA).

Results and discussion

Water status and distribution of areca nut during drying process

The proton density-weighted imaging effectively reflects the distribution and diffusion of water within the material. The signal intensity is directly proportional to the water content in the sample (Wang et al., 2018). In pseudo-color images, the color gradient from blue to red represents increasing proton density, where blue areas indicate fibrous tissue and red regions correspond to higher water content (Shi et al., 2018). During the drying process of both fresh and blanched areca nuts, the red-colored surface areas disappeared first, followed by a gradual inward progression (the disappearance of red areas occurred uniformly from the periphery toward the center). As drying time increased, the area of red regions progressively decreased (Fig. 1A, B). This phenomenon indicates a reduction in longer relaxation signals, corresponding to decreased free water content. On the other hand, it indicates the free water from surface layers initial deplete, followed by inward moisture migration as drying progressed. Similar observations have been reported during the drying of corn and shiitake mushrooms (An, Sun, Li, Wang, & Wang, 2024; Cheng, Li, Yang, Wang, & Tan, 2020). Notably, residual red areas persisted in fresh areca nuts even after 36 h of drying, whereas in blanched areca nuts these areas virtually disappeared by 16 h. This demonstrates that fresh areca nuts retained measurable moisture after 36 h of drying, while blanched areca nuts achieved significantly lower moisture content by 16 h, suggesting that the blanching pretreatment facilitates moisture removal. LF-NMR has been used to determine water distribution and mobility through the changes of transverse relaxation time (An et al., 2024). The relaxation time T2 is positively correlated with the mobility of water, the shorter the T2 signals, the weaker the mobility of water. The relaxation time in the range 0.1–10 ms represents tightly bound water (T21), 30–100 ms representing free water (T23) and the medium relaxation time corresponds to the weakly bound water (T22) (Cheng et al., 2020).

Fig. 1 shows the inversion spectrum of T2 relaxation times of areca nut during drying process. The relaxation time and peak area of different water fractions are shown in Table 2.

Free water and bound water are the main moisture forms in areca nuts, demonstrating significant changes during drying. The peak area of relaxation populations has been proved to be proportional to the amount of water (Jin et al., 2022). As drying time increases, the total peak areas of free water and bound water continuously decrease, indicating progressive moisture reduction. Both T21 and T23 values decline, showing that residual water mobility decreases with prolonged drying and water molecules form stronger bonds with macromolecules, which may relate to tissue shrinkage and formation of dense microstructure induced by drying (Lu, Guo, Li, & Ming, 2017; Zhao et al., 2019). Similar phenomena have been observed in corn and shrimp drying processes (An et al., 2024). For blanched areca nuts during early drying (0-16 h), total moisture content decreases with free water content (M23) reducing while bound water content (M21) increases, indicating initial expulsion of free water along with partial conversion of free water to bound water. Xia et al. (2025) observed similar behavior in shrimp drying, where moisture reduction promoted hydrogen bond formation between free water and proteins, leading to free-to-bound water transformation. At 16 h drying, M23 reaches 95.66 (99.54% reduction from initial 21,047.86) and S23 decreases from 94.18% to 3.86%, showing most free water has been expelled and confirming free water serves as the primary drying indicator, consistent with Kamal et al.'s observations in apple drying (Kamal, Song, Tan, Zhu, & Tan, 2019). During continued drying, bound water content (M21) begins to decrease with declining T21 value, indicating initiation of bound water expulsion, further reduction in water mobility, and decreasing freedom of remaining bound water molecules due to tight hydrogen bonds with macromolecules like cellulose that make removal difficult. At 36 h drying, M21 = 383.718 and M23 = 18.103 with S21 = 95.49% and S23 = 4.51%, demonstrating bound water becomes the dominant moisture form. Therefore, water distribution changes in blanched areca nuts mainly involve two stages: early drying where more mobile free water is first expelled while partially converting to bound water, and late drying where free water is almost completely removed and bound water begins to be expelled with increasing difficulty.

Interestingly, during the entire drying process of fresh areca nuts, both free water and bound water contents (M23 and M21) showed continuous reduction, indicating that bound water in fresh areca nuts was converted to free water and subsequently expelled along with the free water. As drying progressed, the decreasing T21 and T23 values demonstrated reduced mobility of all water forms and diminished freedom of the remaining water molecules. At the 36-h drying mark, the measured values were M21 = 509.26 and M23 = 107.854, with S21 = 82.52% and S23 = 17.47%, confirming the persistence of both water forms in the areca nuts. These findings reveal the moisture transformation mechanism in fresh areca nuts during drying: bound water with higher freedom first converts to free water and gets expelled, while bound water with lower freedom remains more resistant to removal.

For blanched areca nuts, free water was almost completely expelled by 16 h of drying (M23 = 95.666, S23 = 3.86%), whereas fresh areca nuts still retained free water even after 36 h (M23 = 107.854, S23 = 17.47%), demonstrating that the blanching treatment facilitates moisture removal during drying. Scanning electron microscopy observations of the fibrous structures revealed that the blanching treatment (30-min boiling) caused significant structural damage to the fibers, resulting in loosened fiber arrangements and broken fiber chains (the first line of Fig. 2). This structural disruption is presumed to enhance water molecule mobility during drying. Furthermore, the fracture of fiber chains exposes additional binding sites, and the damaged fiber structure forms stronger hydrogen bonds with water molecules. This explains the observed phenomenon of free water converting to bound water during the early drying stage of blanched areca nuts.Fig. 2The cross-sectional and longitudinal microstructure of areca nut during drying process (The left side shows fresh areca nut after different drying times, the right side shows blanched areca nut after different drying times).Fig. 2

Microstructure changes during areca nut drying process

The cross-sectional and longitudinal microstructural characteristics of areca nut fiber layers during the drying process were present in Fig. 2.

The images clearly show distinct fibrous structures, with both cross-sectional and longitudinal views of the areca nut fiber layers becoming progressively more ordered, dense and compact as drying time increases. This observation correlates with the thinning blue fiber layers visible in the previously mentioned proton density-weighted pseudocolor images. The microstructural transformation of the fiber layers likely results from water molecule expulsion causing cell wall dehydration shrinkage, which reduces the overall fiber layer volume, decreases inter-fiber spacing, increases contact area between adjacent fibers, and induces fiber rearrangement - collectively leading to denser, more compact fiber structures. Furthermore, the drying process enhances molecular interactions between lignin and cellulose while simultaneously solidifying gelatinous substances within the areca nut, ultimately increasing fiber layer hardness and producing an even more compact, tightly packed structure. These findings provide direct experimental validation of our earlier hypothesis regarding the relationship between decreased relaxation times, residual water mobility reduced water mobility and the formation of dense microstructures during drying.

Texture changes during areca nut drying process

Fig. 3 demonstrates the textural property changes of areca nuts during drying. With prolonged drying time, the hardness and chewiness of blanched areca nuts increased significantly while elasticity decreased markedly, indicating enhanced firmness of the fiber layers. Correlating with previous SEM results, this phenomenon arises because drying-induced water expulsion leads to cell wall dehydration shrinkage, strengthened lignin-cellulose interactions, and consequent densification of fiber structures. Similar texture hardening during shrimp drying has been attributed to muscle fiber contraction (Cheng et al., 2017). In contras, fresh areca nuts showed decreasing trends in hardness, chewiness and elasticity throughout drying, likely due to their higher initial moisture content and intact cellular structure. Drying curves and drying rate curves

The drying curves and drying rate curves of fresh areca nuts and blanched areca nuts at different temperature are shown in Fig. 4 (A, B, C, D).Fig. 4Drying curve (A, B), drying rate curve (C, D) of areca nut, relationship graphs of lnMR versus drying time t (E, F) and lnDeff versus 1/T (G, H). Drying curve (A, B), drying rate curve (C, D) of areca nut, relationship graphs of lnMR versus drying time t (E, F) and lnDeff versus 1/T (G, H). A, C, E, G represents fresh areca nut, B, D, F, H represents blanched areca nut.

The figures demonstrate that the dry-basis moisture content of areca nuts progressively decreases with extended drying time, while the rate of decrease gradually slows. Drying curves (Fig. 4 A, B) under different temperatures exhibit similar exponential decay trends, representing a typical drying behavior consistent with various materials during dehydration processes (An et al., 2022). The drying rate curve of fresh areca nut drying at 50 °C and 55 °C (Fig. 4 C) reveals two distinct phases-acceleration and deceleration, while the drying rate curve of fresh areca nut drying at 60 °C and blanched areca nut exhibit decay trends, indicating non-uniform hot-air drying characteristics of areca nuts (Shi et al., 2018). As shown in the figure, when fresh areca nuts were dried at 50 °C, 55 °C, and 60 °C, their drying rates reached maximum values of 0.15 g/g·min, 0.19 g/g·min, and 0.27 g/g·min at 5 h, 5 h, and 2 h of drying, respectively. Subsequently, the drying rates began to decline. For blanched areca nuts, the maximum drying rates occurred at the 2 h, with values of 0.31 g/g·min (50 °C), 0.56 g/g·min (55 °C), and 0.76 g/g·min (60 °C), respectively. During initial drying, the drying rate reaches its maximum and moisture significant reduced, suggest efficient water removal. According to the proton density-weighted imaging and water migration results (Fig. 1 A, B), this because the moisture on the surface of areca nut is first expelled during the initial drying stage. Abundant free water in cellular structures initially escapes through loose channels, driving the drying rate to peak values with substantial moisture loss. Subsequent drying shows progressively declining rates and slower moisture reduction, reflecting increasing difficulty in water removal, this phenomenon consistent with cereal and seed drying observations. Combined SEM and moisture migration results, we attribute this phenomenon to the progressive compaction of areca nut fiber structures during prolonged drying, coupled with a shift in moisture diffusion from surface to interior regions. The water removal mechanism transitions from initial free water expulsion to subsequent bound water release, where the strong molecular interactions between bound water and macromolecules (e.g., proteins, cellulose) significantly impede dehydration (Jiang, Jin, Xu, & Chen, 2019). As drying continues, the drying rate approaches zero and the dry-basis moisture content stabilizes, indicating the areca nuts have reached equilibrium weight. Comparative analysis reveals that blanched areca nuts achieve moisture stability at 20 h (drying rate ≈0 g/g·min), whereas fresh samples still exhibit declining moisture content and drying rate at 36 h (Fig. 4 C, D), which consistent with the water migration patterns described earlier. Furthermore, elevated drying temperatures produce steeper drying curves, faster drying rates, and more rapid moisture reduction, consequently shortening the time required to reach equilibrium. Specifically, blanched areca nuts attained equilibrium in 48 h at 50 °C, 30 h at 55 °C, and 26 h at 60 °C, demonstrating the significant impact of temperature on drying kinetics. This temperature dependence likely results from enhanced thermal gradients between the sample and environment at higher temperatures, which promote water molecule diffusion and accelerate moisture evaporation (Cheng al., 2020; Sampaio et al., 2017; Clemente, Sanjuán, Cárcel, & Mulet, 2013; Wan Nadhari, Hashim, Danish, Sulaiman, & Hiziroglu, 2014).

Comparative analysis of drying curves and drying rate curves between fresh and blanched (30-min boiling) areca nuts reveals that the blanching treatment significantly increases the drying rate and enhances the reduction of dry-basis moisture content, shortening the time required to reach equilibrium weight from 60 h to 30 h. This demonstrates that blanching facilitates water molecule migration. Combined with SEM results, we believe that this is due to the fact that blanching process disrupts the arecal nut's fibrous structure, resulting in looser fiber layers that promote easier water molecule expulsion. In addition, blanching process breaking the chemical bonds between bound water and macromolecular substances, thereby facilitating the release of bound water.

Effective moisture diffusivity and activation energy

Regression analysis was performed on lnMR versus drying time t under hot-air drying conditions for areca nuts, where the drying time t was selected up to the inflection point (i.e., the time when the moisture ratio no longer changed), and the effective moisture diffusivity was calculated according to the formula.

As shown in Table 3, the linear equations of lnMR versus drying time t exhibited R2 values greater than 0.96, indicating excellent linear correlations. The Deff values for both fresh and blanched areca nuts ranged from 2.6005 × 10−6–4.9860 × 10−6 m2/h, with higher drying temperatures resulting in greater Deff values. This demonstrates that elevated temperatures accelerate moisture migration in areca nuts, a phenomenon also observed during corn drying processes (Kumar, Sagar, & Seth, 2022; da Silva Timm et al., 2023). Furthermore, blanching treatment significantly increased Deff, indicating enhanced moisture diffusion rates and faster water molecule removal. These findings consistent with the drying curve and drying rate curve results. This improvement can be attributed to the structural modification of fibers induced by the blanching process.

The activation energy (Ea) is an energy barrier that must be overcome for water diffusion (Perea-Flores et al., 2012). Linear fitting was performed for lnDeff versus 1/T, results are shown in Fig. 4 (G, H). Both fresh and blanched areca nuts exhibited excellent linear correlations during hot-air drying, yielding the following equations: y = −6932.26× + 8.1318 (R2 = 0.9318) for fresh areca nuts and y = −4603.87× + 1.6368 (R2 = 0.9573) for blanched areca nuts. The corresponding slopes were − 6932.26 and − 4603.87, respectively. Based on these slope values, the activation energies for hot-air drying were calculated to be 57.63 kJ/mol for fresh areca nuts and 38.27 kJ/mol for blanched areca nuts. This indicates that the minimum energy required to remove 1 mol of water was 57.63 kJ and 38.27 kJ for fresh and blanched areca nuts, respectively. The activation energy of materials is known to depend on sample characteristics including size, type, structure, maturity, and processing conditions (Perea-Flores et al., 2012). Notably, the blanching treatment significantly reduced energy consumption during drying, demonstrating its potential for energy conservation in industrial drying processes.

Flavor changes during the drying process of areca nut

Sensory analysis

The sensory flavor profiles of areca nuts were characterized using eight distinctive aroma attributes: sweet, fruity, green, waxy, fatty, woody, floral, citrus, and herbal.

Radar chart (Fig. 6 A, B) analysis revealed that sweet, fruity, and green aromas consistently received the highest sensory scores across fresh areca nuts, blanched areca nuts, and throughout the drying process, establishing them as the dominant flavor characteristics. During drying of fresh areca nuts, significant increases were observed in the sensory scores of sweet, fruity, and floral aromas, while other attributes maintained relatively stable scores. In contrast, blanched areca nuts exhibited minimal score variations across all aroma attributes during drying, with only slight elevations in fatty, woody, herbal, and sweet characteristics. Comparative analysis demonstrated that blanching treatment enhanced sweet and fruity aroma scores while reducing green, waxy, fatty, herbal, and citrus notes relative to fresh samples. Collectively, both drying and blanching treatments preferentially intensified sweet, fruity, and floral aromas while causing relatively minor modifications to other flavor components. These olfactory modifications correlate with the decomposition and interactions of terpenoids, aldehydes, alcohols, and esters induced by thermal processing. Given that aroma profiles originate from volatile compounds, the observed changes signify substantial alterations in both the composition and concentration of volatile organic compounds during processing. Composition and dynamic changes of volatile compounds in areca nuts during drying

The flavor profile of areca nuts primarily consists of hydrocarbons, aldehydes, esters, acids, ketones, alcohols, ethers, phenols, and heterocyclic compounds, generated through complex biochemical reactions of flavor precursors during processing. Volatile compounds were annotated using the PubChem database and ClassyFire software, with analysis of their categorical distribution and relative abundance. A total of 2147 volatile compounds were identified, including 129 alcohols, 74 aldehydes, 204 benzenoids, 41 carboxylic acids, 168 esters, 53 ethers, 51 heterocyclic compounds, 381 hydrocarbons, 166 ketones, 168 lipids and lipid-like molecules, 20 organic acids and derivatives, 54 organic oxygen compounds, 263 organoheterocyclic compounds and 375 other (Fig. 6 C). For fresh areca nuts, 837 volatile compounds were detected at 0 h (252 unique), 823 at 26 h (217 unique), and 961 at 80 h (198 unique). For blanched areca nuts, 835 volatile compounds were identified at 0 h (148 unique), 818 at 5 h (129 unique), and 909 at 36 h (126 unique) (Fig. 6 D).

PCA analysis was conducted on areca nuts with different drying times using identified volatile substances as indicators. The results showed that samples from different drying periods were distributed in different regions of the PCA (Fig. 6 E), demonstrating that drying significantly alters the volatile profile of areca nuts. According to PCA loading plot (Fig. 6 F), pentadecanoic acid ethyl esters, ethyl 9-hexadecenoate, tetradecanoic acid ethyl ester, decanoic acid ethyl este, heptanoic acid ethyl ester, pentadecanal, phenylethyl Alcohol, 1,3-Octadiene, benzyl alcohol are the main characteristic volatile compounds of fresh areca nuts, indicating esters are identified as the main characteristic volatiles during the drying process of fresh areca nuts. While 1-Decene, p-Cymene, cyclopentane pentyl, 1-Pentanol, beta-Myrcene, gamma-Terpinene, cyclohexane propyl are the main characteristic volatile compounds of blanched areca nuts, indicating hydrocarbons are identified as the main characteristic volatiles during the drying process of blanched areca nuts. Besides, in the PCA score plot, blanched samples and those dried for 6 h occupied proximate positions, while both groups showed clear separation from samples dried for 36 h. This spatial distribution indicates volatile composition between freshly blanched and short-term (6 h) dried samples are similar. The changes in volatile substances are closely related to drying time, and the longer the drying time, the more significant the changes.

The relative concentrations of eight volatile compound categories exhibited distinct changes during drying (Fig. 6 G). Analytical results revealed that aldehydes (13%–37%), alcohols (5%–13%), esters (6%–13%), and hydrocarbons (6%–11%) constituted the predominant volatile components in fresh areca nuts, while hydrocarbons (15%–38%), aldehydes (14%–21%), and alcohols (5%–8%) dominated in blanched samples. During fresh areca nut drying, carboxylic acids, esters, heterocyclic compounds, hydrocarbons, and ketones showed increasing relative concentrations, whereas alcohols and aldehydes decreased progressively, with aldehydes declining markedly from 37% to 13%, suggesting their primary contribution to the “fresh aroma” profile. Under absolute dry conditions, fresh samples retained aldehydes (13%), esters (13%), and hydrocarbons (11%) as major volatile components. Conversely, blanched areca nuts demonstrated decreasing trends for alcohols, aldehydes, carboxylic acids, heterocyclic compounds, and ketones during drying, while hydrocarbons increased significantly from 15% to 38%, indicating their key role in generating the “intense aroma” of dried blanched areca nuts. The final dried blanched products contained hydrocarbons (38%) and aldehydes (14%) as principal volatile constituents, this is similar to the PCA results.

Notably, although alcohols represented major volatile components in both fresh and blanched areca nuts during early drying stages, their concentrations decreased substantially upon complete drying, demonstrating thermal sensitivity and susceptibility to decomposition during processing. This findings consistent with previous research by Guan et al. (2024). Furthermore, the blanching process caused a reduction in the relative concentrations of alcohols, aldehydes, and esters, while increasing the proportions of carboxylic acids, heterocyclic compounds, hydrocarbons, and ketones. Combined with the observed increase in hydrocarbon content during drying, these findings demonstrate that both blanching and drying processes promote the accumulation and transformation of hydrocarbons.

The changes in volatile compounds may be attributed to multiple chemical pathways during drying. Alcohols primarily form through reduction of corresponding aldehydes and oxidative decomposition of lipids (Zhang et al., 2019), while esters typically derive from esterification reactions between alcohols and acids (Yun et al., 2021), both are thermally labile compounds that readily decompose under high temperatures, explaining the decreased relative concentrations of alcohols and esters during drying and blanching processes observed in this study. Aldehyde variations correlate with lipid oxidative degradation and Maillard reactions (Ni et al., 2023), whereas ketones generally associate with lipid oxidation, hydrocarbon degradation, and Maillard pathways. Hydrocarbons act as mediators among various volatile components (Guan et al., 2024). The dynamic transformations of these compound classes collectively indicate the occurrence of Maillard reactions, lipid oxidative degradation, and hydrocarbon decomposition during drying, though the specific synthetic and degradative pathways require further investigation.

In summary, both the blanching treatment and drying process induced significant alterations in the relative composition of volatile metabolites in areca nuts, with aldehydes and hydrocarbons emerging as the most substantially modified compound classes.

These findings align with earlier results showing increased relative concentrations of ketones after blanching (Section 2.5.2). During mid-drying, the dominant aromatics (ROAV>20) included vanillin (100), 2-nonenal (65.20), 1-octen-3-one (63.77), 2-octenal (30.48), 2-pentylfuran (27.38), and heptanal (24.62), contributing vanilla, caramel, sweet/fatty, cucumber/mushroom/nutty, green, fatty/mung bean, vegetal/citrus, fatty, and lime characteristics. By late drying, the prominent compounds were vanillin (100), 2-pentylfuran (67.01), 2-nonenal (60.09), 2-octenal (24.22), heptanal (21.70), and 1-octen-3-one (20.28), imparting vanilla, caramel, sweet/mung bean, vegetal/fatty, cucumber/nutty, green, fatty/citrus, fatty, and lime/mushroom notes. Notably, while ketones showed decreasing flavor contributions during drying of blanched areca nuts and furans increased, contrasting somewhat with the observed declining trends of ketones and heterocyclic compounds and the ultimate predominance of hydrocarbons and aldehydes in fully dried samples, this discrepancy demonstrates that relative concentration doesn't necessarily correlate with flavor contribution. As reported in reference (Li et al., 2024), a compound's impact on overall flavor depends not just on concentration but also on its odor threshold.

Collectively, aldehydes emerge as the primary contributors to areca nuts' characteristic flavor profile. Numerous studies have documented aldehydes' potent aroma-enhancing properties and their significant influence on olfactory perception (Ni et al., 2023).

Correlation analysis

Correlation analysis of Deff, Ea, eight volatile compound categories, and eight characteristic aromas revealed significant relationships.

As shown in Fig. 8, Deff and Ea showed a strong negative correlation, indicating higher moisture diffusivity reduces energy consumption during drying. Among aromas, sweet, fruity and floral exhibited significant positive correlations with each other but negative (non-significant) correlation with woody, while woody and herbal were significantly positively correlated. Green aroma showed positive correlations with all other nine aromas, most significantly with citrus. Whereas fresh and waxy were significantly positively correlated but fresh and woody trended negatively (non-significant). For volatile compounds, alcohols and aldehydes displayed positive (non-significant) correlation but negative correlations with the other six compound classes. In volatile-aroma relationships, esters significantly positively correlated with fresh, floral and waxy, and hydrocarbons showed significant positive correlation with herbal. Whereas ketones, carboxylic acids and heterocyclics primarily exhibited negative (non-significant) correlations with most aromas. Notably, Deff significantly positively correlated with hydrocarbons but negatively (non-significant) with alcohols, aldehydes, esters, waxy and fresh, while showing positive (non-significant) trends with herbal and woody. These results suggest higher moisture diffusivity promotes hydrocarbon accumulation while reducing relative contents of alcohols, aldehydes and esters during moisture dissipation. The comprehensive correlation analysis demonstrates complex interdependencies between drying kinetics, volatile compound profiles and aroma characteristics in areca nuts.

Conclusion

This study investigated the moisture distribution, microstructural changes, and flavor compound dynamics during the drying of fresh and blanched (30-min boiling) areca nuts using low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS), while establishing drying kinetic models. The results revealed that free water and bound water constituted the primary moisture forms, with surface free water being preferentially expelled during early drying before moisture migration progressed inward, accompanied by reduced T2 relaxation times and decreased water mobility. Blanched samples exhibited partial conversion of free water to bound water during initial drying, achieving near-complete free water removal by 16 h, whereas fresh samples showed bound-to-free water transformation, retaining detectable free water even after 36 h drying. SEM imaging demonstrated progressive densification, compactness, and structural reorganization of fiber layers cross-sectionally and longitudinally, correlating with increased hardness, chewiness, and reduced elasticity. Elevated drying temperatures and blanching pretreatment significantly shortened drying duration, with the Page model demonstrating high predictive accuracy (Pearson correlation and R2 > 0.98) for moisture content dynamics. GC–MS analysis identified 2147 volatile compounds spanning alcohols, aldehydes, carboxylic acids, esters, heterocyclics, hydrocarbons, and ketones, with aldehydes and hydrocarbons exhibiting the most significant blanching- and drying-induced variations. Differential volatile analysis showed organoheterocyclic compounds and benzenoids were the main differential volatile compounds of drying progression. Aldehydes emerged as dominant aroma contributors, where 2-nonenal, 2-pentylfuran, 2-octenal, and heptanal characterized fresh areca nut flavor, while 1-octen-3-one, 2-nonenal, 2-octenal, vanillin, heptanal, and 2-pentylfuran defined blanched sample aromas.
