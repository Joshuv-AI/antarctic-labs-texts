The Interplay of Salivary Leptin, Taste Perception, and Dental Caries in Adolescent Girls: A Cross-Sectional Analysis Across Body Mass Index Categories
Authors: Basma A Abdullah, Dalia K Abbas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives:The biological mechanisms linking obesity to dental caries (DC) remain multifaceted. Salivary leptin, an adipokine known to modulate sweet taste sensitivity, may play a pivotal role in dietary choices and subsequent caries development. The aim was to evaluate the association between salivary leptin levels, taste perception thresholds, and DC severity among adolescent girls across different body mass index (BMI) categories.Methods:This cross-sectional study included adolescent girls in Baghdad, Iraq. Participants were categorized by BMI (normal, overweight, obese) using the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention growth references. DC was assessed via the decayed, missing, and filled surfaces (DMFS) index (World Health Organization criteria). Unstimulated whole saliva was analyzed for leptin using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Taste perception was evaluated for four basic qualities. Statistical analysis included analysis of variance for continuous variables, Kruskal–Wallis for ordinal taste scores, and multivariate linear regression.Results:Statistically significant differences in salivary leptin were observed across BMI groups (P < 0.05), with the highest levels in the obese group. Interestingly, the overweight group exhibited a lower mean DMFS (6.28) compared to the normal weight group (7.87), supporting the hypothesis of varying dietary patterns. Multivariate regression identified salivary leptin and total taste scores as significant independent predictors of DMFS severity (P < 0.05). High leptin levels correlated with elevated sweet taste thresholds, potentially driving sugar-seeking behavior.Conclusion:Salivary leptin acts as a critical link between metabolic status and oral health by modulating taste perception. These findings suggest that leptin-mediated taste desensitization is a significant biological risk factor for caries, independent of BMI alone.

INTRODUCTION

Dental caries (DC) and obesity are common chronic conditions with multiple contributing factors and are associated with adverse health consequences and significant healthcare expenses.[1] The correlation between DC and obesity can be attributable to a high-sugar diet, infrequent utilization of health services, and inadequate health literacy, as these common risk factors increase the prevalence of both conditions.[2,3]

Adolescents who are overweight might have diets that are comparatively higher in fat and protein but not necessarily higher in sugar, which are the leading causes of DC. The World Health Organization states that the main dietary factor influencing DC is free sugar intake. This idea leads to the hypothesis that differences in caries experience across body mass index (BMI) groups might be explained by changes in food composition rather than total calorie intake.[4,5]

Taste perception is one factor that determines an individual’s choice of certain foods, which in turn affects oral health and BMI.[6] Taste perception is essential, as it transmits information about food and influences dietary intake and food choice,[7] and taste sensitivity differs between individuals with and without obesity.[8] Thus, investigating the relationship between diet quality and taste perception, including overall taste scores, is crucial.[9] Most previous studies investigating connections between taste perception and DC have focused on sweet and bitter flavors, while little is known about how children with varying caries statuses perceive sour or salty flavors.[10]

Leptin, a type of adipocytokine, is an important modulator of food intake and basal metabolism.[5] Leptin has varied effects on different tissues and organs, connects metabolism and the immune system, and is crucial in obesity. Leptin receptors are found in several sense-related organs, including the mouth. It has been proposed that leptin plays a role in the reaction to sweet tastes. Salivary leptin might affect dental bacterial colonization, leading to differences in dental cavity prevalence between individuals with overweight or obesity and those of normal body weight.[11]

To our knowledge, few studies have examined the relationships among DC, taste perception, and salivary leptin in adolescent girls with varying BMIs. Accordingly, this study aims to fill this gap.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

SETTING AND STUDY DESIGN

This cross-sectional comparative study was conducted from January to the end of May 2025 and included 90 adolescent girls aged 16–17 years, selected from six government schools in Baghdad, Iraq. A random sampling method was used to select participants.

ETHICAL APPROVAL AND INFORMED CONSENT

This study was approved by the Scientific Committee and conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki’s ethical guidelines for research involving human participants. Permission was obtained from the District Educational Officer for government schools. The study protocol was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06949917) for transparency and to meet ethical approval requirements, despite the fact that the study was observational and non-interventional. Informed consent was obtained from participants and their parents before the study. In addition, participants were provided with a clear, age‑appropriate explanation of the aims and procedures of the study, ensuring respect for and compliance with ethical considerations for studies involving minors.

SAMPLING AND GROUPING

A computer-generated randomization process in Microsoft Excel was used to select schools at random from a comprehensive list.

Sample size was estimated using G*Power software version 3.1.9.7 (developed by Franz Faul, University of Kiel, Germany).[12] The calculation was based on a study power of 85%, a two-sided alpha level of 0.05, and a large effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.40). Given the inclusion of three study groups, the minimum required sample size was estimated at 72 participants. To account for a potential dropout or non-response rate of 20%, the sample size was increased to 86 participants. Therefore, the inclusion of 90 participants was considered sufficient and exceeded the minimum sample size required according to the G*Power calculation.[13]

Adolescent girls with systemic diseases affecting salivary secretion, undergoing therapy or fluoride treatment, under orthodontic treatment, with cognitive impairments, or who did not provide consent were excluded.[14] Participants’ heights and weights were measured, and the individuals were categorized using the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) BMI-for-age growth charts. The selection of the CDC criteria was strategically made to ensure methodological alignment with recent regional and local literature. Although World Health Organization (WHO) growth standards are recognized as the global standard, we used a more practical age-specific BMI based on CDC criteria. This selection was driven by its demonstrated applicability in contemporary regional literature, including a recent Iraqi study and the most recent data published in 2026[15,16] and regional data from Jordan,[17] which showed no appreciable clinical discrepancies between CDC and WHO guidelines for the adolescent population. To achieve accurate charting on the growth charts, age was recorded strictly in completed years. The participants were divided into three groups according to CDC BMI percentile criteria for boys and girls aged 2–20 years: normal weight (5th–85th percentile), overweight (>85th–95th percentile), and obese (>95th percentile).[18]

SALIVA COLLECTION, TASTE PERCEPTION ASSESSMENT, AND DENTAL CARIES EXAMINATION

An unstimulated whole saliva sample was collected in the morning (between 8:00 and 11:00). Each participant fasted for 1 h before the sample was taken and rinsed their mouth with distilled water to remove any excess food. After a few minutes of tilting the head forward, approximately 2 mL of saliva was collected from the front of the mouth and immediately placed in a sterile tube on ice. The saliva samples were then centrifuged for 15 min at 4°C at 3000 rpm. The supernatant was collected and stored at −20°C. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Saliva-Human ELISA Kit, Shanghai YL Biotech Co., Shanghai, China) were then used to measure salivary hormone levels.[7,19]

Taste perception was assessed by a single researcher using a reliable and reproducible filter paper disc. Five concentrations of the four flavors were used: sucrose (sweet), sodium chloride (salty), tartaric acid (sour), and quinine hydrochloride (bitter). The following concentrations of each taste were ranked from 1 (least concentrated) to 5 (most concentrated): sour, 0.02%, 0.2%, 2%, 4%, and 8%; bitter, 0.001%, 0.02%, 0.1%, 0.5%, and 4%; sweet, 0.3%, 2.5%, 10%, 20%, and 80%; and salty, 0.3%, 1.25%, 5%, 10%, and 20%.

A score of 6 was assigned to indicate no perception within the tested concentration range if the participant was unable to identify even the highest concentration. This score was used solely as a coding value and did not correspond to an additional concentration. The circular disc was soaked in the solutions sequentially, starting with the lowest concentration, and then placed on the tongue’s chorda tympani nerve, 3 cm from the center of the tongue. The disc was placed on both sides of the participant’s tongue. If the taste was not identified, the next concentration was applied. A mouth rinse was performed between each flavor application to prevent flavor mixing.[20] The decayed, missing, and filled surfaces (DMFS) index was used to record DC severity.[14]

DATA ANALYSIS

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS version 22, Chicago, Illinois, USA) was used to analyze the data using Pearson correlation, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and the Games–Howell post hoc test. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess whether continuous variables were normally distributed, which the results confirmed. The ratings for taste perception were treated as ordinal quantitative variables. Spearman’s rank correlation was deemed more appropriate than Pearson’s correlation and non-parametric statistical tests, and the Kruskal–Wallis test and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test were used for these ordinal variables because these ratings were derived from ranked concentrations. A P value of <0.05 was considered to indicate significance.

RESULTS

A box-and-whisker plot was used to visually demonstrate the distribution and variability of DC severity scores among the study groups. The median DMFS value was highest in the obese group [Figure 1]. The mean DMFS values in the normal weight, overweight, and obese groups were 7.87 ± 0.90, 6.28 ± 0.57, and 10.74 ± 1.21, respectively (one-way ANOVA, F [2,87] = 4.777, P = 0.010). The Games–Howell post hoc analysis revealed significant differences in the DMFS values between the overweight and obese groups (mean difference = −4.467, P = 0.005).

The distribution of salivary leptin levels was further illustrated using a box-and-whisker plot. The median salivary leptin level was highest in the obese group [Figure 2]. The mean ± SD salivary leptin levels for the normal weight, overweight, and obese groups were 629.7 ± 44.5, 693.1 ± 59.7, and 834.5 ± 63.6, respectively (one-way ANOVA, F [2,87] = 3.432, P = 0.037). The Games–Howell post hoc analysis revealed a significant difference in salivary leptin levels between the obese and normal weight groups (mean difference = 204.814, P = 0.032). Significant positive correlations (Spearman’s rho) were found between the total taste perception score and DMFS in the normal weight (rs = 0.617, P < 0.001) and overweight groups (rs = 0.436, P = 0.016). No significant correlation was found between leptin levels and caries severity [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

DC and obesity in adolescents represent two of the most common chronic conditions worldwide and are becoming significant global public health issues.[21,22] The relationship between DC and obesity remains unclear and is likely complex.[4] This study revealed a correlation between DC severity and BMI, with significantly greater DC severity in the obese group than in the overweight group. These results are consistent with earlier research showing that adolescents with higher BMIs have a considerably higher risk of developing DC.[23] Although some studies have demonstrated that adolescents with obesity experience worse DC,[2] others have found no associations between obesity measures and caries.[24] This disparity might result from the fact that adolescents who are overweight or obese have a lower stimulated salivary flow rate.[25] Obesity is significantly correlated with xerostomia, compromised salivary gland function, and other oral diseases.

Body weight alone is not the only factor influencing the complex, non-linear association between BMI and DC; other factors include diet quality, salivary composition, hormonal factors, and taste perception. Diets that include more high-fat, high-energy meals might increase body weight but have less impact on DC in overweight people, while frequent consumption of free sugars plays a major role in the development of DC. Research demonstrates that different eating patterns, in which sugar intake is not proportionately higher despite increased overall caloric consumption, might explain reduced caries experience in high-BMI groups. Despite the lack of statistical significance, these observations might explain why DC severity was less common in the overweight population than in the normal weight group.[4,5,6,8,26]

The correlation between decay levels and obesity is more evident in older children and adolescents because both obesity and DC share common, cumulative dietary risk factors.[27] Obesity is associated with alterations in taste sensitivity accompanied by changes in food intake.[8] Taste cells express many hormones and their corresponding receptors, which mediate taste perception. Taste perception is a dynamic process at the taste bud level, influenced by both dietary and metabolic factors.[8,28] This study revealed significant differences in sensitivity to sweet and sour flavors and in total taste perception scores, largely aligning with recent research.[29] Obesity is associated with several metabolic diseases, including high blood sugar levels that lead to insulin resistance and alterations in adipokine and cytokine levels that cause low-grade inflammation, and these changes have been found to reduce the number of taste buds.[28]

Given that salivary leptin measurements are noninvasive and straightforward, they are an acceptable alternative to plasma sampling.[30] This study investigated the salivary levels of the proinflammatory leptin, also known as the “satiety hormone,” one of the main hormones controlling food intake. In this study, the obese group’s mean leptin level was significantly higher than that of the normal weight group. While these findings align with those reported in many previous studies,[31] one study conducted among healthy adult males demonstrated conflicting results.[32] The difference could be due to BMI’s effect on salivary flow rate, as adolescents who are overweight or obese have lower stimulated salivary flow rates. The quantity of leptin secreted per unit of time (pg/min) is positively correlated with BMI.[33] Although adipose mass significantly influences leptin concentrations, additional factors are also pertinent, including transient variations in caloric consumption, sex, adipose tissue-specific characteristics such as adipocyte dimensions and the distribution of visceral vs. subcutaneous fat, as well as other hormones (e.g., insulin and glucocorticoids) and cytokines.[34] Although leptin did not significantly correlate with the total taste perception score, a negative correlation between leptin and taste perception was observed, possibly because leptin dampens sweet taste. Leptin was found to have no effect on salty, sour, bitter, or umami taste, consistent with previous research demonstrating that leptin reduces sweet-stimulus-evoked adenosine triphosphate release from type II TBC and increases 5-HT release from type III TBC, which might not directly involve leptin binding.[8]

A significant positive correlation was observed between the DMFS index and the total taste perception score in the normal weight and overweight groups, but not in the obese group.[35] The weak or complex association between taste perception and caries severity in obesity might be attributable to the fact that individuals with obesity generally exhibit a uniformly blunted sense of taste, and differences in caries severity might depend on factors other than taste perception. These findings are largely consistent with those reported in other studies.[36]

The findings imply that BMI is not directly associated, whereas biological and behavioral factors are both independently related to the experiencing of DC. BMI is not an independent predictor of DMFS, unlike leptin and total taste score, suggesting that biological and behavioral factors are more important determinants of the outcome than anthropometric measures.

Although this study presents important insights into the relationships among taste perception, salivary hormones, and DC, it is necessary to consider its methodological limitations. Some participants were reluctant to have their weight measured, even when privacy was ensured. The absence of radiographic examination might have led to the underestimation of early or proximal caries lesions. Some participants were also hesitant to provide saliva samples, and the taste perception test, which required approximately 15 min per participant, was time-consuming and inconvenient due to participants’ school schedules.

CONCLUSION

DC severity was highest in the obese group, although BMI did not have a direct independent influence. The total taste perception score, sweet taste score, sour taste score, and leptin level increased with increasing BMI. This finding may explain the positive correlation between caries severity and obesity. Salivary leptin may serve as a potential biomarker of obesity. The relationship between taste perception and DC severity was more pronounced in the normal weight and overweight groups and less pronounced in the obese group. However, the direct role of obesity in caries development and taste sensitivity remains unclear. More intensive research on oral health and taste perception in adolescents is needed, especially with the increasing prevalence of obesity in this population.
