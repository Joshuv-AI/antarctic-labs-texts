A biomimetic cryoprotectant preserves extracellular vesicles bioactivity for intravenous administration and potentiates wound healing
Authors: Pingao Zhang, Xinkai Nie, Hongwei Mao, Youyou Zhang, Jing Wang, Lufeng Wang, Rui Qin, Xiaoli Yang, Wanying Huang, Xiaoling Gao, Chenghuan Song, Yongfang Zhang, Hao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as promising vehicles for drug delivery and disease therapy owing to their intrinsic biocompatibility and capacity to transfer functional biomolecules. However, limitations in current storage technologies, including structural degradation and bioactivity loss during long-term freezing, and the incompatibility of conventional cryoprotectants such as DMSO and glycerol with intravenous administration, pose major barriers to clinical translation. Herein, we developed DDAS, a biomimetic cryoprotectant formulation, rationally designed through generative artificial intelligence (GAI)-assisted prioritization of physiologically relevant candidates from the Human Metabolome Database. DDAS comprises dextran, albumin, and sialic acid, which were selected for their synergistic stabilization and inherent biocompatibility. Compared with standard preservation media, DDAS markedly improved EVs stability during prolonged cryopreservation and repeated freeze-thaw cycles, effectively preserving vesicle morphology, particle concentration, and cargo protein functionality while reducing aggregation and membrane damage. Notably, DDAS exhibited negligible cytotoxicity and excellent biocompatibility, enabling direct intravenous administration without re-isolation or washing. Importantly, stem cell-derived EVs preserved in DDAS fully retained their therapeutic activity in vivo, significantly attenuating inflammation and accelerating wound repair upon systemic administration. Together, this study develops and validates DDAS, a practical, clinically feasible biomimetic cryoprotectant assisted by GAI. Introduction

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as essential mediators of intercellular communication and hold significant promise as therapeutic agents and drug delivery vehicles. Their lipid bilayer structure enables the encapsulation and protection of proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids, while their biocompatibility and ability to traverse biological barriers render them well-suited for clinical applications in tissue regeneration, disease treatment, and biomarker detection [1]. These properties also contribute to their clinical utility in drug delivery, regenerative medicine, and immune modulation [2]. Consequently, EV-based therapies are advancing rapidly toward clinical translation, with demonstrated efficacy in wound healing and age-related degenerative diseases [3, 4].

Despite these advances, the clinical uses of EVs are hampered by the lack of simple and reliable methods for long-term preservation. EVs are inherently unstable and easily undergo aggregation, fragmentation, and loss of bioactivity during storage, as consistently observed during our previous studies [5–7] and acknowledged by the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) [8]. Conventional storage in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at -80 ℃ fails to preserve vesicle integrity and function beyond several weeks [8], whereas alternative approaches such as lyophilization yield poor recovery and irreversible membrane damage [9, 10]. Hydrogels and colloidal carriers can provide partial stabilization but are not amendable to intravenous administration due to their high viscosity and complex handling processes. Therefore, a key unmet need is the development of an intravenously compatible, low-toxicity formulation that ensures long-term EVs stability while preserving their structural and functional integrity.

However, traditional cryoprotectants such as DMSO and glycerol exhibit cytotoxic and are thus unsuitable for systemic use [2, 11]. Even relatively benign polyols such as trehalose or mannitol offer only transient stabilization and cannot prevent vesicle aggregation upon thawing [12, 13]. Moreover, current approaches largely fail to recapitulate the biochemical environment that naturally stabilizes EVs in vivo, such as plasma, saliva, and cerebrospinal fluid. These physiological fluids contain balanced osmolytes, proteins, and ions that could maintain vesicle integrity and prevent cargo degradation [9, 14]. Therefore, a biomimetic cryoprotectant design that replicates such natural protection mechanism offers a rational route toward clinically safe and scalable preservation systems.

Generative artificial intelligence (GAI) models (e.g., ChatGPT, DeepSeek, and KIMI) have recently shown notable success in biomedical research, leveraging large-scale data integration and reasoning capabilities to assist in drug discovery and clinical screening [15]. Here, we advanced the method of Yan, et al. [15] by employing GAIs to assist in ranking candidates from the Human Metabolome Database, with the aim of deriving a biomimetic formulation for EVs preservation under moderate freezing conditions. This led to the development of DDAS, a composite cryoprotectant comprising dextran, albumin, and sialic acid. We verified that this biomimetic preservation system could maintain vesicle morphology, cargo stability, and bioactivity during long-term storage at -20 ℃, thereby allowing for intravenous injection without post-thaw processing. Through integrated multi-omics and functional validation, we demonstrated that stem cell EVs stored in DDAS retain not only their nanoscale structure and cargo composition, but also their therapeutic efficacy in promoting wound repair and modulating inflammation. Collectively, this study introduces DDAS, a biosafe, scalable, and practical EVs cryoprotectant formulation designed with GAI assistance. DDAS overcomes the critical bottlenecks of EVs long-term stability and clinical translation, enabling a seamless transition from stabilized storage to ready-to-use therapeutic administration.

Results

GAI-driven screening of biomimetic EVs cryoprotectants

EVs are naturally stabilized in body fluids (e.g., blood, saliva, and semen) [16, 17], suggesting endogenous components facilitate their preservation. As native body fluids are clinically impractical for EVs storage, we designed a biomimetic screening strategy using The Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) to select ubiquitous, physiologically relevant metabolites in body fluids (including urine, saliva, blood, cerebrospinal fluid, feces, and breast milk for broad applicability) (Fig. 1A). This strategy yielded 39 components, including amino acids, carbohydrates, organic acids, and their derivatives after Venn analysis (Fig. 1B, C, Supplementary Table 1). All these components exhibit high biosafety and have been used as stabilizers in pharmaceuticals (e.g., D-glucose, glutamine), suggesting their potential as EVs protectants.

We next employed GAI to prioritize lead candidates. Building upon the framework of Yan, et al. [15], ChatGPT (GPT-4o), DeepSeek, and KIMI were prompted to: (1) Select 15 top candidates from the 39 metabolites; (2) Refine selections via self-reflection and assign predicted efficacy scores (Fig. 1D; Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 2). To account for probabilistic variability, this process was repeated independently 10 times per model with at least 10 times self-refutations, yielding 15 unique candidates (Fig. 1D, E; Supplementary Fig. 2A, B). Compounds appearing in ≥ 7 iterations (frequency threshold) and ranking in the top five by average predicted score were advanced. Each candidate appeared in at least one AI-generated list for its potential application as a protectant. To assess consistency across models, we evaluated multiple GAI versions including the online version of DeepSeek-R1, the local deployment version of DeepSeek-R1 and DeepSeek-V3 (Fig. 1E). These experimental results demonstrated that the prediction results of these GAI versions or models were highly consistent and exhibited a significant positive correlation (Supplementary Fig. 2C-E). Through the ranking process, N-acetylneuraminic acid (sialic acid) was prioritized across all models, showing high selection frequency and score correlation by Fisher’s exact test and ternary plot analysis (Fig. 1F, Supplementary Fig. 2F).

Sialic acid is a nine-carbon monosaccharide abundant in mammalian glycoconjugates (Fig. 1G), which could be present on cell surfaces to create a negatively charged, acidic microenvironment to functionally inhibit EVs aggregation [18]. Sialic acid sialidase and sialic acid synthetase are widely distributed in body tissues (Fig. 1H; Supplementary Fig. 3). Sialic acid has been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as a safe additive for food, pharmaceutical excipient, and cosmetic ingredient [19]. These attributes position it as a core component for the development of EVs cryoprotectant.

Constructing a biomimetic protective EVs barrier named DDAS

Effective cryoprotectants typically contain moderate concentrations of protein (e.g., albumin) and high concentrations of carbohydrate (e.g., glucose, trehalose) [10, 20]. To develop a humoral-mimetic formulation, we analyzed existing cryoprotectants (Supplementary Table 3) and confirmed that elevated carbohydrate levels, optimal albumin concentrations, and mildly acidic conditions maximize EVs stability, aligning with recent systematic analyses [21]. Guided by this framework and GAI virtual screening, carbohydrates and sialic acid emerged as lead candidates.

To formulate a carbohydrate-based cryoprotective agent, dextran, a clinically used plasma volume expander with well-established biocompatibility, was investigated. Dextran is a polysaccharide formed by the dehydration condensation of glucose (Fig. 1I), with the functions of maintaining plasma colloid osmotic pressure, reducing blood viscosity, and preventing thrombosis [22]. Given the common clinical use of 6% dextran 70 (70,000 Mw) and dextran 40 (40,000 Mw), we evaluated the properties and biological effects of these dextrans at or near this concentration. The pH values of dextran 70 and dextran 40 solutions were comparable at low concentrations (0.5-6%), while dextran 40 showed higher acidity at high concentration (8%) (Fig. 2A). Neither dextran exhibited cytotoxic effects, while both enhanced the proliferation of human cerebral microvascular endothelial cells (HCMEC/D3) at 2% (Fig. 2B). Dextran 70 exhibited a more pronounced proliferative effect than dextran 40. Subsequently, the stability of EVs in dextran solutions was assessed. At a concentration of 2%, dextran 70 significantly reduced the zeta potential of stored EVs compared to dextran 40 (Fig. 2C), suggesting enhanced EVs stability.

Sialic acid was included to establish an optimal acidic pH of approximately 6 (Fig. 2D). The concentrations below 0.25% w/v ensured safety (Fig. 2E) along with greater stability in zeta potential (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, to mimic the protein component of blood, albumin was incorporated. Albumin is a readily available, safe, and stable alternative that can form stable complexes with dextran via Maillard reactions [23, 24]. In addition, albumin has been reported to form a complex with sialic acid, thereby providing a structural basis for the stability of the protective agent [25]. The albumin solution exhibited slightly acidic and enhanced cell proliferation in a concentration-dependent manner within the range of 0.5-2% (Fig. 2G, H). Albumin serves as a common nanoparticle carrier and had minimal effect on the zeta potential of EVs, except at a higher concentration (4%). Therefore, a concentration below 4% is established as optimal for cryoprotective formulation (Fig. 2I).

Based on the efficacy, safety, and stability data of each component, a cryoprotective formulation was developed and designated DDAS from the first letter of its components in a proportional order: Dulbecco’s PBS (D-PBS), dextran 70, albumin, and sialic acid (Fig. 2J). DDAS formulations were prepared with different concentrations of each component (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Both medium- and high-concentration DDAS formulations significantly reduced zeta potential of EVs (Fig. 2K, L). Considering the comparable stabilization performance of medium- and high-concentration formulations, the medium-concentration formulation (D-PBS, dextran 2% w/v, albumin 2% w/v, and sialic acid 0.25% w/v) was chosen for subsequent analyses due to its favorable cost and safety profiles.

DDAS maintains the stability of EVs during long-term storage

To evaluate the cryoprotective efficacy of DDAS on EVs, HCMEC/D3-derived EVs were extracted by ultracentrifugation (Supplementary Fig. 4B) and confirmed their purity by the absence of histone 3 contamination (Supplementary Fig. 5A). EVs were stored in DDAS or PBS at -20 °C for one year (Fig. 3A) and the results showed that the EVs stored in PBS were instability during low-temperature storage, as evidenced by aggregation and swelling (Supplementary Fig. 5B-E). In contrast, DDAS-preserved EVs exhibited superior stability, as shown by less size change and particle loss (Fig. 3B), and enhanced cell proliferation activity (Fig. 3C). The effect of DDAS on RNA extraction and recovery was validated using standard substances with known nucleic acid concentrations, and paired analysis showed no significant difference in detected RNA concentration relative to the PBS control (Fig. 3D). The results also demonstrated that DDAS effectively preserved the RNA content of EVs samples during one-year storage at -20 ℃ (Fig. 3E). At the 12th month, DDAS achieved a nucleic acid recovery rate of nearly 50%, significantly higher than that of other formulations (Fig. 3F). Analysis revealed that omitting any single DDAS component impaired long-term nucleic acid and vesicle preservation, indicating that each component is essential for maintaining EVs stability (Fig. 3B-E). Specifically, the absence of dextran accelerated EVs particle expansion (Fig. 3B), whereas removal of sialic acid disrupted vesicle size homeostasis and significantly reduced nucleic acid recovery (Fig. 3E).

Electron microscopy confirmed that EVs preserved in DDAS retained their characteristic cup-shaped morphology and intact membrane bilayers after one year of storage and the EVs stored in PBS exhibited aggregation (Fig. 3G). DDAS formed a discontinuous colloidal protective layer around the EVs, reducing vesicle-vesicle collision and extrusion (Fig. 3G, H). This protective mechanism likely involves the introduction of sialic acid-mediated negative surface charges [26, 27] and the establishment of dextran-dependent physical barriers on EVs. The formation of this barrier also relies on an albumin-sialic acid complex (Supplementary Fig. 5F), which further enhances the barrier’s stability and has been used in nanomaterial preparation [24, 25, 28]. The formation of albumin-sialic acid complex was identified in the excitation spectrum, which was not affected by the presence of dextran (Supplementary Fig. 5G). In addition, this combination was further enhanced by the use of phosphate buffer, facilitating the formation of a more compact and stable albumin adsorption film around EVs [29]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the efficacy of DDAS in preserving EVs integrity and bioactivity during prolonged cryogenic storage, supporting its potential as a cryoprotectant.

DDAS maintains the stability of EVs during freeze-thaw cycles

EVs fragmentation and loss of bioactivity during freeze-thaw cycles are the main challenges restricting their clinical application [8]. To evaluate the cryoprotective performance of DDAS, characterized EVs were subjected to cyclic freeze-thaw experiments in the presence of DDAS (Fig. 4A). DDAS effectively prevented particle loss and RNA degradation of the EVs from HCMEC/D3 and Human umbilical mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) during repeated cycles (Fig. 4B, C). The identification of hMSCs and hMSC-EVs can be seen in Supplementary Fig. 6A-D. Introduction of protective agents did not affect the detection of EVs proteins after storage (Supplementary Fig. 6E). Notably, DDAS outperformed the commercially available cryoprotectant (CN111226902A, CNA) in preserving both EVs particle numbers and RNA content (Fig. 4B, C), meanwhile CNA did not affect nucleic acid recovery (Supplementary Fig. 6F). Scanning electron microscopy revealed that EVs stored in PBS underwent significant rupture and aggregation after five freeze-thaw cycles, whereas those preserved in DDAS maintained structural integrity (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, EVs in DDAS exhibited more regular morphology and uniform distribution with minimal aggregation, compared with those in CNA (Fig. 4D).

While sharing some components with CNA, DDAS is free of cytotoxic agents (e.g., DMSO and glycerol) and features a mildly acidic pH (Fig. 4E) and higher solution clarity (Fig. 4F), suggesting lower cytotoxicity and enhanced biocompatibility for cellular uptake [30]. This aligns with our observations of significantly increased uptake of DDAS-preserved hMSC-EVs (DDAS-hMSC-EVs) by human epidermal keratinocytes (HaCaT) compared to those in CNA and PBS, as quantified by PKH26 fluorescence intensity (Supplementary Fig. 6G-I). Moreover, DDAS-hMSC-EVs increased HaCaT cell proliferation activity by 61%, whereas hMSC-EVs stored in CNA showed no significant proliferative effect (Supplementary Fig. 6I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that DDAS confers superior protection against freeze-thaw damage compared with both standard buffers and a commercial alternative. DDAS’s favorable biocompatibility and efficacy in preserving EVs functions support its potential as a valuable protectant for intravenous delivery of hMSC-EVs.

DDAS protects EVs proteins associated with wound healing and immune-inflammation

To further elucidate the impact of DDAS on EVs protein during freeze-thaw cycles, ultra-deep proteomic profiling was performed on hMSC-EVs after five freeze-thaw cycles (Fig. 5A). Proteomic analysis revealed that DDAS significantly modulated the protein profile of EVs subjected to freeze-thaw stress (Fig. 5B). A total of 932 proteins were identified, with 243 proteins detected in ≥ 3 samples per group (Fig. 5C; Supplementary Table 4). Comparative analysis identified 49 proteins significantly upregulated and 30 proteins significantly downregulated in DDAS-preserved EVs versus PBS-preserved EVs (|log2FC| ≥ 2, P < 0.05) (Fig. 5D, E).

Cell Component (CC) analysis of differentially expressed proteins demonstrated that DDAS effectively preserved proteins associated with vesicular structures (vesicle: GO:0031982, extracellular vesicle: GO:1903561, extracellular exosome: GO:0070062), while reducing those linked to external encapsulating structures (GO:0030312) and the extracellular matrix (GO:0031012) (Fig. 5F). Subcellular location analysis using Uniprot (https://www.uniprot.org/) further indicated that DDAS strongly stabilized intracellular proteins (cytoplasmic, nuclear, organellar) and certain membrane proteins, but exhibited weaker stabilization of extracellular or secreted proteins (Fig. 5G; Supplementary Table 5). Collectively, these findings indicate that DDAS maintains exosomal protein stability and mitigates degradation during freeze-thaw cycles, which is critical for preserving EVs bioactivity.

Biological Process (BP) analysis of the modulated proteome revealed functional enrichment patterns: upregulated proteins in DDAS-preserved EVs were significantly associated with wound healing processes, while downregulated proteins were enriched in immune-inflammatory pathways (Fig. 5H). The key pro-regenerative proteins (e.g., ECM1, FGFR1, HGF, IGF1, S100A4) were significantly elevated (Supplementary Fig. 7A; Supplementary Table 6), whereas immunogenic/inflammatory mediators (e.g., C3, IGHG1, RBP4, ORM2, SERPINA3) were significantly reduced (Supplementary Fig. 7B; Supplementary Table 6). This shift is functionally relevant, as supplementation with HGF, IGF1, and S100A4 [31–33] or inhibition of C3 [34] and SERPINA3 [35] are established mechanisms to promote wound healing and suppress immune inflammation. ELISA assays confirmed increased HGF and decreased C3 protein levels in DDAS-preserved hMSC-EVs, contrasting with poor protein maintenance in PBS (Supplementary Fig. 7C). These results demonstrate that DDAS not only preserves the protein stability of hMSC-EVs through multiple freeze-thaw cycles, but also sustains their wound-healing bioactivity and reduces immunogenicity, highlighting its significant therapeutic potential for hMSC-EVs-mediated wound healing.

DDAS enhances hMSC-EVs-mediated wound healing

Previous studies have demonstrated the efficacy of hMSC-EVs in accelerating re-epithelialization in wound healing models by intravenous injection [36, 37]. To investigate the potential synergistic effect of DDAS on hMSC-EVs in burn wound healing, a deep second-degree burn model was established in mice according to Yan, et al. [38]. Experimental groups received intravenous injections of PBS, DDAS alone, PBS-preserved hMSC-EVs (PBS-hMSC-EVs), or DDAS-preserved hMSC-EVs (DDAS-hMSC-EVs) (Fig. 6A). In vivo imaging confirmed significant enrichment of intravenously administered hMSC-EVs at the wound site (Fig. 6B). Macroscopic and histological assessment (H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining) demonstrated significantly accelerated wound closure in both PBS-hMSC-EVs and DDAS-hMSC-EVs groups compared to PBS and DDAS controls from days 6 to 12 post-injury (Fig. 6C, D). Notably, the DDAS-hMSC-EVs group showed superior wound healing versus the PBS-hMSC-EVs group on days 6 and 9, achieving near-complete closure (~ 90%) by day 12 (Fig. 6D). Masson’s trichrome staining revealed increased myofibroblast density and collagen deposition in PBS-hMSC-EVs-treated wounds compared with PBS controls. More importantly, compared to the PBS-hMSC-EVs group, wounds treated with DDAS-hMSC-EVs exhibited better tissue organization, characterized by increased epithelial thickness, more orderly arranged collagen fibers, and reduced overall collagen deposition (Fig. 6E, F). These findings demonstrate that DDAS, functioning as a protective adjuvant for EVs, enhances the therapeutic efficacy of hMSC-EVs in burn wound healing.

DDAS promotes wound healing through anti-inflammatory pathways

As our cellular studies demonstrated, the mildly acidic environment of DDAS promotes EVs uptake (Supplementary Fig. 6H, I). To assess DDAS’s impact on hMSC-EVs trafficking in vivo, PKH26-labeled EVs were administered into the wound model mice. Compared to PBS-preserved EVs, DDAS-hMSC-EVs showed faster accumulation at the wound site (Fig. 7A, B). However, this enhanced kinetics did not significantly increase total EVs retention at the wound by 48 h (Fig. 7B), suggesting that DDAS primarily facilitates EVs accumulation rather than long-term deposition. Twenty-four hours after final administration (day 12), the DDAS-hMSC-EVs group showed 1.6-fold higher EV-specific fluorescence than the PBS-hMSC-EVs group (Fig. 7C, D), along with a 49% reduction in total CD11b+ signal (Fig. 7D), indicating both elevated EVs content and attenuated inflammatory cell infiltration at the wound site. Furthermore, RNA sequencing revealed significant enrichment of pathways related to immune-inflammatory responses and skin development (Supplementary Fig. 8A, B), implying a cooperative role for DDAS and EVs to regulate wound healing. Collectively, these data suggest that DDAS-preserved EVs promote wound healing through immune cell-mediated inflammatory pathways.

To further elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying DDAS-hMSC-EVs enhanced wound healing, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed on wound tissue samples from the mice treated with DDAS-hMSC-EVs or PBS-hMSC-EVs (Fig. 7E). GSEA revealed significant upregulation of proteasome-mediated protein folding, sorting, and degradation pathways and downregulation of inflammatory mediator regulation of TRP channels in the DDAS-hMSC-EVs group relative to controls (Fig. 7E). Given that hMSC-EVs carry TRP-regulating proteins (e.g., ECM1, HGF, IGF1, and S100A4; Supplementary Fig. 8C) [39, 40], which suppress NF-κB-mediated immunomodulation via TRP pathways (Supplementary Fig. 8D) [41], we further examined NF-κB activation. Western blotting analysis confirmed that DDAS-hMSC-EVs significantly inhibited TRPV-dependent NF-κB phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig. 8E, F), subsequently reducing the expression of IL-6, IFN-γ, and VEGF (Supplementary Fig. 8G). Complementing these findings, in vitro assays demonstrated that DDAS-hMSC-EVs significantly enhanced the pro-migratory effects of hMSC-EVs on HaCaT cells (Fig. 7F, G). These studies suggest that DDAS can enhance the anti-inflammatory and proliferative effects of hMSC-EVs, thereby collectively accelerate wound healing.

DDAS has high biocompatibility

The biological safety assessment revealed that DDAS working solutions at various concentrations were non-hemolytic (Supplementary Fig. 9A, B), a property that retained even after a two-fold dilution with distilled water (Supplementary Fig. 9C, D). In hemagglutination assays, DDAS exhibited no significant coagulant activity, supporting its biocompatibility and safety for direct intravenous administration (Supplementary Fig. 9E). In hemagglutination inhibition assays, influenza virus hemagglutinin mediates hemagglutination through sialic acid receptors on host cells [42]. The addition of DDAS markedly reduced hemagglutinin-induced coagulation (Supplementary Fig. 9F), consistent with a sialic acid-dependent interference mechanism. The coagulation safety of DDAS in human blood has also been confirmed, supporting its safety for in vivo use (Supplementary Fig. 9G).

Besides, mice received intravenous injections of DDAS-hMSC-EVs every three days for 12 days, and serum biochemistry was assessed at the endpoint. No clinically relevant abnormalities were observed in key parameters of liver function (ALT, AST), kidney function (Urea, Crea), or heart function (CK, LDH) compared with vehicle controls (Supplementary Fig. 9H), indicating favorable systemic tolerance under the repeated-dose regimen. ALT and AST showed a downward trend after DDAS-hMSC-EVs treatment, suggesting a potential hepatoprotective effect. In vivo long-term safety assessment of DDAS following intravenous administration in mice revealed no significant alterations in body weight (Supplementary Fig. 10A, B), spleen weight (Supplementary Fig. 10C), and histopathological staining of main organs (brain, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney; Supplementary Fig. 10D, E), indicating an absence of severe immune-inflammatory pathology or systemic toxicity. Collectively, these findings establish DDAS as a biocompatible, well-tolerated, and effective EVs preservation that maintains vesicle integrity.

Discussion

Current EVs cryopreservation strategies are substantially limited by the challenges of achieving long-term stability alongside compatibility with clinical administration routes such as intravenous injection [8]. The existing approaches often focus on drug delivery or stability enhancement, neglecting synergistic integrations. To overcome these challenges, we screened the body fluid component database based on the observation that physiological fluids naturally stabilize EVs, and developed DDAS, an innovative biomimetic cryoprotectant comprising dextran, albumin, and sialic acid. DDAS is characterized by a high carbohydrate proportion, appropriate protein content, and acidic environment, and aligns with fundamental cryoprotectant principles, thereby enhancing EVs stability for therapeutic applications. Unlike conventional agents such as DMSO and glycerol that exhibit cytotoxicity and preclude direct intravenous administration, DDAS leverages physiologically compatible components to preserve EVs integrity and bioactivity. Critically, DDAS enables direct intravenous delivery without post-thaw processing, which is a significant advantage for translational EVs therapies.

Recent benchmark studies have demonstrated robust performance of GAI platforms in biomedical applications [43, 44]. Our approach integrated a body-fluid-inspired design with GAI-assisted prioritization, therefore enhancing both the efficiency and rationality of biomimetic cryoprotectant screening. To counter GAI limitations (e.g., database lag and false positives), we narrowed the search space and restricted retrieval to uploaded datasets to circumvent database lag, and cross-validated hits in several models and implemented virtual scoring and ranking to minimize false positives. Finally, we rigorously validated the lead candidates in both in vivo and in vitro experiments. Beyond simple ranking, this prioritization strategy of human-GAI collaboration provides three unique advantages over conventional methods: (1) unbiased screening of 39 database-derived metabolites without bias toward known cryoprotectants; (2) multi-model cross-validation with statistical confidence, reducing subjective bias; (3) usage of GAI as a decision support system that expands search space, uncovers synergistic components, and prioritizes clinically translatable candidates from the outset. Moreover, this method shows exceptional clarity and transparency throughout the candidate screening process, displays the thinking process and code, and ensures research efficiency, without relying on specialized hardware and complex algorithms, offering an advantage over deep learning approaches.

The cryoprotective efficacy of DDAS arises from synergistic interactions among its components, as evidenced by the finding that omitting any single component compromised its protective efficacy (Fig. 3B-F). Mechanistically, dextran forms a viscoelastic network that mechanically immobilizes vesicles during liquid storage and freeze-drying [45]. Similarly, albumin inhibits non-specific adsorption through the interaction with various protein surfaces, serving as an ideal protein stabilizer widely employed in medicine and drug delivery [23]. Importantly, albumin forms strong couplings with dextran [23, 46] and sialic acid [24, 25, 28], creating a flexible proteinaceous capsule that blocks EVs surface adsorption and fusion. Phosphate buffer tightens this film by promoting albumin conformational order [29]. Simultaneously, sialic acid confers a further defense layer by increasing negative surface charge, which suppresses EVs aggregation, provides an acidic environment, and enhances cell endocytosis [30, 47].

Leveraging above mechanism, we applied DDAS-hMSC-EVs formulation in a burn wound model, and the results confirmed that DDAS could enhance the anti-inflammatory and pro-proliferation activities of hMSC-EVs. In this study, -20 ℃ was adopted as the primary storage temperature for EVs because it better maintains EVs stability than 4 ℃ and is more feasible for practical applications [48, 49]. The concentrations of each DDAS component were selected based on licensed intravenous formulations to ensure biosafety. Medium concentrations exhibiting optimal efficacy were chosen due to their greater suitability in cost-sensitive, large-scale applications, and toxicity control relative to higher concentrations. Our cryoprotectant formulation is introduced after EVs isolation and purification, rather than during cell culture. This design allows DDAS to act during storage and freeze-thaw cycles, without affecting EVs biogenesis or cargo loading. Another practical advantage is that EVs preserved in DDAS can be administered intravenously without a post-thaw re-isolation or washing step.

Both local and intravenous administration play significant roles in wound healing. Compared with local delivery, intravenous injection may offer advantages in anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and skin regenerative effects, possibly because local administration is limited by EVs loss at the wound site and by repeated manipulation of the injured tissues [36, 50]. In addition, intravenous delivery has also been widely used in other EV-based applications such as drug delivery, genetic engineering, and the treatment of degenerative diseases, which may further broaden the application scenarios of DDAS. Therefore, for the similar considerations, hMSC- and HCMEC/D3-derived EVs were prioritized for their relevance to central nervous system diseases via brain-blood barrier targeting and drug delivery [51]. While the dosing regimen and administration interval employed here were informed by previous preclinical and early clinical studies [52–55], they should not be directly interpreted as the final clinical regimen and should be optimized in future translational studies.

Male mouse models are commonly used in wound healing research [56], however, sex-related differences in immune responses may exist, and this should be considered in future mechanism studies. Moreover, we acknowledge that different vesicle sources and isolation methods may influence EVs susceptibility to the external environment. A systematic comparison of multiple cell sources and purification methods is currently underway. Likewise, although GAI significantly narrows the search space, database lag and residual false-positive risks necessitate orthogonal experimental validation. The iterative refinement process will be continued with expanded metabolomic libraries and retrained machine-learning models. The formulation still needs necessary exploration and adjustment in actual large-scale production and application. Overall, this study is still at the preclinical stage. In the future, it is still necessary to evaluate long-term stability, batch-to-batch consistency, and the therapeutic window in large animal models and under GMP-compliant conditions before any definitive clinical translation can be claimed.

Conclusion

In summary, this study develops DDAS, a GAI-designed, biomimetic cryoprotectant that addresses the critical bottlenecks in EVs preservation and clinical uses. Its multi‑component formula confers synergistic cryoprotection, maintaining EVs integrity and bioactivity through freeze‑thaw cycles and enabling direct intravenous administration. In a murine burn wound model, DDAS‑preserved EVs potently accelerated healing, with comprehensive biosafety validation. Methodologically, we establish a GAI-assisted rational design platform, shifting from empirical screening to knowledge-driven formulation. DDAS thus shows promise as a clinically translatable, ready-to-use platform for advanced EV-based therapies.

Materials and methods

Interactions with GAI

In this study, we attempted to analyze and screen reliable EVs protectants with the assistance of GAI. We improved the method of Yan, et al. [15] by interacting with ChatGPT (GPT-4o), KIMI, and DeepSeek (DeepSeek-R1 and DeepSeek-V3) to generate promising EVs cryoprotectants. Interaction with ChatGPT and KIMI began in March 2024, and with DeepSeek in March 2025, at which time the models accessed to information current until October 2023 for ChatGPT, November 2023 for KIMI, and July 2024 for DeepSeek. Ten independent queries were performed, ensuring no query served as the context for another. Each query consisted of two prompts. The first prompt described the instructions for protectant candidates, whereas the second prompted GAI to self-correct its output from the previous prompt.

Prompt 1: Please provide a list of the 15 most promising ingredients for a protective or stabilizing agent for exosomes or extracellular vesicles from these compounds in the file provided to you, and indicate their potential efficacy. Please rank them in descending order of potential effectiveness and use the JSON format to include the “Drug” and “Potential efficacy” keys. Refute yourself at least 10 times.

Prompt 1: Please provide a list of the 15 most promising ingredients for a protective or stabilizing agent for exosomes or extracellular vesicles from these compounds in the file provided to you, and indicate their potential efficacy. Please rank them in descending order of potential effectiveness and use the JSON format to include the “Drug” and “Potential efficacy” keys. Refute yourself at least 10 times.

Prompt 2: Please check if the generated list meets the requirement: (1) exclude the compounds that have been designed as exosome or extracellular vesicles protectants, (2) 15 distinct drugs from source file, (3) in JSON format, (4) generate a hypothesis score (1 to 100) based on potential validity and rank it in descending order. If not, please regenerate the list that meets the requirement.

Prompt 2: Please check if the generated list meets the requirement: (1) exclude the compounds that have been designed as exosome or extracellular vesicles protectants, (2) 15 distinct drugs from source file, (3) in JSON format, (4) generate a hypothesis score (1 to 100) based on potential validity and rank it in descending order. If not, please regenerate the list that meets the requirement.

In the inquiry, we deliberately emphasized retrieving the original list to limit the return of the original candidates in the final list of each query. We prompted GAIs to provide a theoretical rationale for compound prioritization, which was conducive for candidate evaluation (Supplementary Fig. 1A). We asked GAIs to conduct in-depth thinking and self-refute at least ten times to eliminate false positive results. We also specified a standardized output format for returned compounds to streamline subsequent processing (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Furthermore, we required GAIs to perform predictive scoring and ranking based on the potential efficacy of the drugs to facilitate subsequent statistics (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Representative ChatGPT’s responses are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Mice and treatments

C57BL/6J mice were purchased from the Cavens Biogle Model Animal Research Co. Ltd (Suzhou, Jiangsu, China). Mice were housed 4–5 mice per cage at a constant temperature (24 ± 2 ℃) with ad libitum access to food and water on a 12 h light/dark cycle. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (JUMC2025-324-A).

Cell culture transfection and reagents

HCMEC/D3 cell line was purchased from Cell Resource Center, Shanghai Institute of Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences. HaCaT and bEnd.3 cell lines were purchased from FuHeng Biology (Shanghai, China). All cell lines had corresponding STR identification and were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. HCMEC/D3 (#GNHu68), bEnd.3 (#FH0356), and HaCaT (#FH0186) cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Cells were digested with 0.25% EDTA Trypsin (Gibco, USA) for 2 min, centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 5 min, and then seeded in the plates. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. The list of all reagents and consumables can be found in Supplementary Table 7.

Preparation of DDAS cryoprotectant

In brief, a sterile solution of Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (D-PBS, Ca2+ and Mg2+ free; Gibco, USA) was prepared by autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 min. Following cooling to approximately 50 °C, dextran (McdChemExpress, USA) was added to a final concentration of 2% (w/v) and dissolved completely by vortex mixing. Subsequently, precisely weighed N-acetylneuraminic acid (sialic acid; Aladdin, China) and albumin (Beyotime, China) were added to achieve final concentration of 0.25% (w/v) each. The resulting solution, designated DDAS, was sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 μm filter (Supplementary Fig. 4A) and was employed directly for EVs resuspension or dilution. EVs stored in DDAS were amenable to nucleic acid extraction, BCA protein quantification, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), and electron microscopy following appropriate dilution with sterile D-PBS.

EVs isolation and purification

EVs were extracted from HCMEM/D3 cells across five separate cell batches. HCMEM/D3 cells were cultured in medium for three passages and then seeded in T75 flasks. Cells were maintained until 80% confluence, then replaced the medium with serum-free medium and cultured for 24 h. EVs were enriched from the collected supernatants, and then isolated by ultracentrifugation according to well-established methods [57, 58]. In brief, a successive high-speed centrifugation at speeds of 300 g, 3,000 g, 10,000 g and 110,000 g was adopted to isolate EVs and eliminate the large dead cells and cell debris at 4℃ for 10 to 90 min. Subsequently, the pellet was washed in 25 mL PBS, and recentrifuged at 110,000 g, 4 ℃ for 90 min in a Beckman SW32Ti rotor with 0.22 μm pore to precipitate the EVs. Histone 3 (Abcam, #ab176842, USA, 1:1000) was used as reference for contaminant. The final pellet was purified EVs and was resuspended in DDAS or PBS and stored at -20 ℃. Human umbilical mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs (hMSC-EVs) were purchased from Macklin (#E939302, China). Fresh EVs samples were verified by western blotting of protein markers Alix (Abcam, #ab275377, USA, 1:1000), HSP70 (Abcam, #ab2787, USA, 1:1000), TSG101 (Abcam, #ab125011, USA, 1:1000), and CD63 (Abcam, #ab134045, USA, 1:1000). Then, EVs morphology was evaluated using transmission electron microscopy (FEI G2 Spirit, Tecnai) and nanoparticle tracking analysis.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis(NTA)

According Dikpati A, et al. [59], the concentration and particle size of purified EVs were measured by Nanoparticle tracking microscope ZetaView® (PMX120, Germany), equipped with a sample chamber and a 520 nm laser. The samples were injected into the chamber using 1 mL injection syringes. All measurements were performed at room temperature. Nanosphere® size standards were diluted 250,000 times and the EVs samples were diluted 1,000 to 10,000 times in purified water to achieve optimal detection concentration (10⁷-10⁹ particles/mL). All acquisition parameters, including sensitivity (80), shutter (100), and camera level (1–11) were kept constant across all samples. For each sample, at least three 30-second videos were recorded per position in light scatter mode. To minimize potential matrix effects arising from differences in viscosity and refractive index between buffers, residual reagents were removed through a purification column, and all samples were diluted equally prior to analysis. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using ZetaView software (version 8.05.16 SP7). Measurements were conducted on EVs preparations obtained from 5 independent isolations.

Transmission electron microscopy

The EVs samples, retrieved from a -20 ℃ freezer, were initially placed in an ice box to thaw and then briefly centrifuged. Fifteen microliters of the EVs samples were carefully pipetted onto a copper grid and allowed to stand for 1 min, ensuring that the grid was not damaged during handling. A filter paper was then used to remove excess liquid from the grid, followed by the application of 15 µL of 2% uranyl acetate stain at room temperature for 1 min. After staining, the excess stain was also removed with filter paper, and the stained samples were baked under a lamp for 10 min before being observed by transmission electron microscope (Tecnai, G2 spititi FEI, USA).

Protein extraction, quantification, and immunoblotting

EVs samples were lysed and homogenized in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) before the vesicle repackaging. The protein concentration of the sample was determined according to the protocol of BCA protein assay kit (Thermo, USA). Immunoblotting was carried out according to previous procedures (Zhang, et al. [6]). The antibodies used for immunoblotting in this study were shown as follows: P-p65 NF-κB (phospho S536) (Abcam, #ab76302, USA, 1:1000), p65 NF-κB (Abcam, #ab16502, USA, 1:1000), α-Tubulin (Abcam, #ab52866, USA, 1:1000), and GAPDH (Abcam, #ab181602, USA, 1:1000).

RNA isolation, reverse transcription, and RT-qPCR

The total RNA was extracted from EVs using a SteadyPure universal nucleic acid extraction kit (Accurate Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Changsha, China). Solvents such as DDAS or CNA do not affect the extraction of RNA by the kit, and the residual reagents were removed through a purification column. The final obtained nucleic acid samples were resuspended in 40 µL of sterile and enzyme-free DEPC water. With reference to established methods, nucleic acid concentration was measured using an ultramicrospectrophotometer (Thermo, NANODrop 2000 C, USA) [60].

EVs labeling and tracing

EVs were labeled by PKH26 Red Fluorescent Cell Linker Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, #PKH26GL, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions [7]. In brief, EVs diluted in PBS were mixed with PKH26 dye (1 µL PKH26 in 200 µL diluent C) for 5 min, added 400 µL stop buffer for 1 min, and then ultracentrifugated at 110,000 g for 90 min to remove unbound dye. Then the pellet was resuspended in PBS or DDAS. The labeled EVs were intravenously injected into the mice with deep second-degree burns, and the distribution of EVs was observed 30 min and 24 h after administration. Subsequently, the mice were anesthetized and transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS to eliminate remaining blood-derived EVs in tissues. Slices of wound tissue were excised and traced the red fluorescence signals of EVs under confocal microscopy (ECLIPSE Ti2, Nikon, Japan).

In vivo real-time imaging and biodistribution of EVs

The biodistribution of EVs was investigated in C57BL/6 mice using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS Spectrum, PerkinElmer). The excitation wavelength was 551 nm and the emission wavelength was 567 nm with PKH26 as the fluorescent probe. PBS was injected at the same volume (200 µL) as a blank and unlabeled PKH26 dye solution was injected as a control.

CCK-8 assay

Cells were plated at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well on 96-well plates in 100 µL culture medium. At the indicated time, CCK-8 (10 µL, Beyotime, China) was added to each well and incubated for an additional 24 h. The OD was measured with a Multiscan spectrum (Thermo, VARIOSKAN FLASH, USA) at the wave length of 450 nm.

Scratch wound healing assay

Cells were cultured in 6-well plates overnight. Linear scratch wounds were created by 200 µL sterile pipette tip when each well was filled with cells. The drifting cells were washed and removed with PBS. After cells were replenished with fresh medium, the wound healing status was observed and photographed 24 h post-wounding. The wound-healing rate was quantitatively evaluated using the Image J software.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence was performed as our previous procedures [6]. Briefly, mouse wound samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and dehydrated in 20% and 30% sucrose in PBS and then microtome-cut into 20 μm thick sections on a cryostat. Cells were washed with PBS three times for 5 min each time and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. The sections and cells were blocked with blocking buffer for immunol staining (Beyotime, China) at room temperature for 2 h, and then incubated with the primary antibodies as follows: rabbit anti-mouse CD11b (Abcam, #ab184308, USA, 1:200). Next, appropriate secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488, Abcam) were used followed by incubation with DAPI (Beyotime, China). Finally, the fluorescence signal was observed and recorded under confocal microscopy (Nikon, ECLIPSE Ti2, Japan).

ELISA

Mouse tissues about 5 mg were lysed and homogenized in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors (APExBIO, #K1007, USA). ELISA assay was carried out according to the instructions and the plates were read at 450 nm on a Synergy MX Plate Reader (BioTek, USA). The ELISA kits used in this study were shown as follows: IL-6 ELISA kit (MultiSciences, #70-EK206/3, China), VEGF ELISA kit (MultiSciences, #70-EK283/2, China), and TNF-α ELISA kit (BOSTER, #FEK0527, China).

Steady-state fluorescence spectroscopy

Excitation spectra of both albumin and albumin-sialic acid complexes were acquired using a Spark multimode microplate reader (TECAN, Switzerland), scanning from 250 to 500 nm with a 5 nm slit width for both excitation and emission. The maximum emission wavelengths of albumin and albumin-sialic acid complexes occurred at 350 nm [25]. The concentration of albumin (300 µM) and sialic acid (3842 µM) for the binding assay are their concentrations in the DDAS working solution.

Measurement of hemolytic activity

Fresh rabbit red blood cells were extracted using erythrocyte sedimentation solution (DAKEWE, #7131011, China) and prepared into a 4% cell suspension. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with serial dilutions of DDAS at a ratio of 1:1 at 37℃ for 1 h, with Triton X-100 and PBS as the positive and negative controls, respectively. After centrifugation (1,500 rpm, 5 min), the absorbance of supernatants was measured at 540 nm. The percentage hemolysis was calculated relative to the positive control (defined as 100% hemolysis).

Hemagglutination and hemagglutination inhibition

Fresh 4% rabbit red blood cells were incubated with serial concentrations of DDAS (1:1) at 37℃ for 1 h in blood coagulation plate. Hemagglutinin (Aladdin, #I118968, China) and PBS served as the positive and negative controls, respectively. In the hemagglutination inhibition assay, red blood cells were treated with serial dilutions of DDAS and 2 hemagglutination units of hemagglutinin. In the assay, red blood cells in negative wells sedimented and formed red pellet, whereas the positive wells displayed an opaque appearance with no sedimentation [61].

Deep second-degree burns in mice and treatments

Due to the stable physiological cycle and disease phenotype, male mice are often prioritized in wound healing models to avoid estrogen-related effects [56]. Healthy male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks) were used to generate deep second-degree burns as previously described [38]. Specifically, the burn depth was standardized by using a cylindrical plastic tube with a fixed inner diameter (1 cm) that was placed vertically onto the shaved abdominal skin during water pouring. The tube was pre-marked to ensure consistent vertical placement, and the boiling water was poured to a fixed height (2 cm) and maintained for exact 15 s, thereby controlling the three key parameters that influence burn severity: contact area, exposure time, and water column height [38].

According to the protocols of Chen, et al. [52] and Chuang, et al. [53], each mouse received intravenous injection of 200 µL PBS, PBS-hMSC-EVs, DDAS or DDAS-hMSC-EVs immediately after burn and once every three days for 12 days, and the final concentration of EVs in the preparation was 1.8 µg/µL. Investigators were blinded to the group allocation. After 3, 6, 9 and 12 days, the healing rate for each group was recorded by taking photos with a Leica camera and analyzed by ImageJ software.

RNA sequencing

High-throughput RNA sequencing analysis was conducted using the double-ended sequencing mode of illumina Hiseq sequencing platform by the Xurangene Company (Shanghai, China). Following normalization, log2 transformation, and probe annotation, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on the criteria of |log2 fold change|> 1 and adjusted P value < 0.05. Hub DEGs were selected for further investigation.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 and all data were presented as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). Two-sided unpaired Student’s t-test was used for difference between two independent samples and one-way ANOVA followed by post-hoc Tukey test was performed for multiple comparisons. The comparisons among groups were analyzed by two-way ANOVA with post-hoc Tukey multiple comparison tests. The statistical analyses used for the different experiments were described in the respective figure legends. Difference was considered statistically significant when P < 0.05 and the P-value was reported on the graph. Sample sizes were determined based on common practices in the field and insights gained from our previous work.
