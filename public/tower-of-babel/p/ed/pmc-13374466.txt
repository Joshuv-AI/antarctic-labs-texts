Effects of China’s National Volume-Based Procurement policy on utilisation and affordability of statins in children: an interrupted time series analysis
Authors: Yongyi Wu, Wei Bai, Fanyu Liu, Xiaohan Fan, Siyu Liu, Lili Liu, Bin Jiang, Zhao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundStatins are effective in lowering lipids and reducing cardiovascular risk. In recent decades, rising paediatric lipid levels, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), have led to growing demand for lipid-lowering drugs. However, access remains limited in LMICs due to financial constraints. To address this, China launched its National Volume-Based Procurement (NVBP) policy in 2019, significantly improving the price and affordability of statins. This study evaluates the policy’s impact on statin use and expenditures in children.MethodsThe study used interrupted time series regression to analyse all levels of children’s hospitals across the country’s monthly procurement records of statins, covering the period from January 2019 to December 2020. It investigated the fluctuations in monthly drug procurement quantity (measured in defined daily doses) and expenditures (affordability) at both the national level and across different tiers of child medical facilities.ResultsFollowing the implementation of the NVBP policy, the utilisation and affordability of statins across all children’s hospitals nationwide exhibited a positive upward trend. After the NVBP policy, the affordability of atorvastatin in children’s hospitals nationwide improved by at least 90.54% and the affordability of rosuvastatin in children’s hospitals nationwide increased by no less than 89.85%. Both before and after the NVBP policy, the majority of utilisation and expenditure in statin was contributed by atorvastatin and rosuvastatin. After the NVBP policy, the utilisation of atorvastatin and rosuvastatin bid-winning generic has increased significantly, making bid-winning generic change from the variety occupying the least market to the variety occupying the most market in children’s hospitals.ConclusionThe implementation of China’s NVBP policy has resulted in a marked increase in the dosage of atorvastatin and rosuvastatin, while effectively reducing cost expenditure for children. This achievement has been realised without compromising the quality of healthcare services. China’s NVBP policy provides a reference and path for the medical reform of all countries in the world especially LMICs.Keywords: Drug Utilization, China, Child, Hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA Reductase Inhibitors, Health policy

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading non-communicable chronic disease worldwide, with dyslipidaemia recognised as a major risk factor. In recent years, the rising prevalence of childhood obesity and unhealthy lifestyles has contributed to an increasing burden of dyslipidaemia among children and adolescents. Epidemiological data indicate that approximately 20% of US adolescents aged 12–19 years have dyslipidaemia1; in China, the prevalence ranges from 19.0% to 28.5%, with urban areas showing higher rates than rural regions.2,5 Similar trends have been observed in India, where prevalence estimates range from 19% to 23%,6 underscoring the growing need for intervention in paediatric populations.

Since the 1980s, China has experienced a marked increase in dyslipidaemia among children and adolescents due to rapid socio-economic development, dietary transitions, reduced physical activity and other lifestyle changes.7 8 Atherosclerotic changes can begin in childhood and are strongly associated with elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels,9 10 suggesting that early intervention may be critical in preventing CVD later in life. Consequently, early screening and timely intervention, particularly among high-risk groups such as those with familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH), are of great importance. Substantial clinical evidence supports the safety and efficacy of early statin therapy in these populations. Statins can significantly reduce LDL-C levels, slow the progression of atherosclerotic plaques and ultimately decrease the risk of cardiovascular events in adulthood, while also improving population health and reducing long-term healthcare costs.11 12

Despite robust evidence supporting statin therapy for high-risk children, especially those with FH,13 14 its use remains suboptimal, particularly in low-income countries and primary healthcare settings. This may be due to low screening rates (with FH diagnosis rates below 1%),15,18 limited availability of paediatric formulations, high out-of-pocket costs and persistent clinician scepticism regarding long-term safety, even though numerous studies have confirmed that statins do not adversely impact paediatric growth or development.19,24 Furthermore, paediatric patients frequently face significant disparities in access to essential medicines compared with adults. They are often regarded as therapeutic orphans who must rely on the off-label use of adult formulations due to a severe lack of age-appropriate dosage forms and underrepresentation in clinical trials.25 26 In the context of lipid-lowering treatments, these barriers are compounded by the specificities of paediatric care: unlike adult statin therapy, which heavily emphasises secondary prevention, treating high-risk paediatric cohorts necessitates decades-long, continuous adherence for primary prevention. This lifelong requirement profoundly magnifies the impact of drug affordability.

Cost is a major barrier to paediatric statin access. The Prospective Urban Rural Epidemiology (PURE) study found that statin use among adults was only 3.3% in low-income countries, compared with 66.5% in high-income countries,27 reflecting the strong impact of drug affordability on access. This issue may be even more pronounced in children. compared with high-income countries, statin prices are higher and insurance coverage is lower in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs),27,29 posing further barriers to access. These limitations hinder the WHO’s goal of achieving 50% statin treatment coverage among eligible individuals by 2025. In China, the long-term use of statins by paediatric patients imposes a substantial financial burden on both the national health insurance system and individual households, necessitating the availability of affordable alternatives.

Driven by an unsustainable growth in national healthcare spending, where pharmaceutical outlays climbed rapidly over the past decade to well above the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) average,30 China initiated systemic pricing reforms. In 2018, pharmaceutical expenditures in China accounted for 33% of total healthcare spending, well above the OECD average of 17%. In China, total health expenditure rose from ¥6584.14 billion in 2019 to ¥7230.64 billion in 2020, representing a compound annual growth rate of 8.94%. Meanwhile, China’s pharmaceutical outlays climbed from US$103 billion in 2014 to US$163 billion in 2023. In order to improve access to medicines, a number of countries have pursued different strategies to reduce the price of medicines, and the effects of these policies have varied from region to region. High-income countries have an advantage in innovative drug development and investment in research and development, while LMICs focus more on access to essential medicines.31 In response to rising drug costs and mounting pressure on the health insurance fund,32 China launched the National Volume-Based Procurement (NVBP) policy in 2018, initially piloted in 11 ‘4+7’ cities and expanded nationwide in 2019.33 The NVBP leverages pooled procurement across regions, links volume with pricing and requires generic drugs to undergo the generic consistency evaluation (GCE), ensuring both cost savings and drug quality. The policy has promoted the substitution of originator drugs with high-quality domestic generics, resulting in an average price reduction of 25–96% for selected drugs,34 thereby significantly reducing patients’ financial burden.

However, a critical policy blind spot remains. Macrolevel drug pricing policies like the NVBP are predominantly designed around the high-volume chronic disease burdens of the adult population.35 Paediatric medications, characterised by smaller clinical demand, complex dosing requirements and lower market incentives for manufacturers to pursue GCE, are frequently under-represented in these frameworks. Consequently, it remains largely unknown whether the drastic price reductions and volume shifts observed in adult statin procurement successfully spill over to improve affordability and access for vulnerable paediatric patients.

Although the NVBP policy has demonstrated remarkable success in improving the affordability and utilisation of medications for chronic diseases in adults, limited evidence exists regarding its impact on paediatric medications, particularly statins. This represents a critical policy blind spot. Large-scale procurement policies like the NVBP are traditionally driven by high-volume adult chronic diseases, often under-representing paediatric needs and failing to account for the specificities of paediatric clinical practice. Consequently, it remains largely unknown whether the drastic price reductions and volume shifts observed in adult statin procurement successfully ‘spill over’ to improve affordability and access for paediatric patients. To fill this gap, the present study employs an interrupted time series (ITS) design using national procurement and utilisation data to evaluate the effect of NVBP on statin use and affordability in children. It focuses on differences between domestic generics and originator drugs, as well as between NVBP-winning and non-winning products. The findings aim to inform paediatric pharmaceutical policy and provide empirical insights for the global advancement of centralised drug procurement.

Materials and methods

Data source

This study used data from the Centralised Drug Procurement fast acquisition subsystem of the National Healthcare Security Administration. It covers the monthly procurement records from 2018 to 2020, recording drugs purchasing information including all relevant data of the whole Chinese sample before and after the implementation of the NVBP policy. In the database, each purchase order record included region names (provinces and cities), drug basic information (generic name, dosage form, specification, pharmaceutical manufacturer, originator or generic drug, whether passed GCE or not), purchasing information (drug price, purchase date, unit, volume and expenditures) and medical institution information (location, level, nature and the number of beds). In China, children’s hospitals are specialised medical institutions primarily dedicated to the diagnosis and treatment of paediatric diseases. The vast majority of their patients are under 18 years of age (or under 14 years in some regional variations). Given the institutional specialisation of these facilities, the inclusion of adult patients is negligible and highly unlikely to affect the results. Therefore, this study restricted the sample exclusively to children’s hospitals to accurately represent the paediatric population.

In addition, Fujian Province and Hebei Province that have previously been followed up, so the implementation time is not unified with other provinces and there are differences in the bid-winning drugs. Shaanxi Province, Liaoning Province, Sichuan Province and Guangdong Province failed to upload valid data, and part of the uploaded data in Jiangsu Province is missing. Therefore, the seven provinces above are not included in the scope of this study. The study covered the period from January 2019 to December 2020. A general database containing 20 provinces, more than 80 000 medical institutions and over 15 million purchase order records was established. Importantly, the 20 included provinces span the eastern, central and western regions of China, covering areas with diverse levels of economic development and healthcare resource distribution. Although some provinces were excluded due to data limitations, the retained sample captures a substantial proportion of the national population and healthcare utilisation, thereby maintaining robust national representativeness for paediatric patients.

We included drugs listed in the procurement catalogue and their clinically interchangeable alternatives, as defined by the National Healthcare Security Administration. The final sample included 39 tertiary, 8 secondary and 20 primary or lower-level hospitals. Furthermore, due to the nationwide implementation of the NVBP policy and the specific clinical indications of statins, the entire statin market was subject to direct or indirect policy spillover effects. Consequently, constructing a strictly unaffected external control drug (a non-NVBP statin) within the same therapeutic class was not methodologically feasible. Additionally, we lacked access to data on medications other than statins. To address this, our study employed internal comparative analyses (eg, bid-winning vs non-winning products and originator vs generic drugs) to partially account for secular trends and isolate the policy effects.

Ethical approval was not required for this study because the analysis was based exclusively on aggregated institutional procurement data obtained from the National Healthcare Security Administration database. The dataset did not contain any individual-level patient information, personal identifiers or clinical records. Therefore, informed consent was not required.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Outcome

Two outcome variables were measured in this study: expenditures and daily drug costs. The methodology of this study is the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system and defined daily dose (DDD) recommended by WHO.

DDD is the most commonly used measurement in drug utilisation research. Combined with drug consumption, therapeutic indicators such as DDDs can be calculated, so that the indexes of pharmacoeconomics can describe the use of drugs more accurately.

The DDDs were calculated by dividing the total number of milligrams consumed in a given time period by the DDD. DDDs can reflect the medication dynamics and structure in different years. The larger, the higher frequency of use and the greater clinical tendency of the drug.

The quantification of affordability is based on WHO/Health Action International standard survey method, which is to calculate the total cost of a drug for treatment of a disease at a standard dose within a course of treatment is equivalent to the number of days of minimum salary for non-skilled personnel in government departments.36 To accurately assess this, minimum wage data were obtained from official provincial government announcements and national statistical reports for the corresponding years. Where available, province-specific minimum wages were applied to match the regional procurement data; otherwise, the closest available regional values were used. All wage data were standardised to daily income levels to ensure uniform comparability across different regions and time periods.

If affordability is less than 1 day’s wage, we think it is affordable. The lower the affordability value, the smaller the financial burden on the family due to taking drugs.

Statistical analysis

First, ITS analysis was adopted to describe 24 months’ change of DDDs and expenditures of policy-related statins under the expansion of NVBP policy.37 ITS is a quasi-experimental design. Through the segmented regression analysis of the index result values of at least 12 observation points before and after the implementation of the intervention, it describes the level change and trend change brought by the policy implementation on the index value respectively, so as to distinguish whether the impact is instantaneous or continuous. At the same time, it presents the impact of the index value clearly and intuitively, so as to judge the impact brought by the policy implementation.

Although the utilisation of the originator decreased, it remained the most expensive product due to its high unit price. While the bid-winning generics dominated in volume, their expenditure was slightly higher than that of other generics and non-equivalent drugs but remained substantially lower than that of the originator.

Discussion

This study aims to evaluate the impact of China’s NVBP policy on the use and expenditure of statins among children. With economic development and lifestyle changes, the prevalence of dyslipidaemia among Chinese children and adolescents has significantly increased.7 Atherosclerotic CVD (ASCVD) remains one of the leading causes of mortality,38 39 creating a growing demand for lipid-lowering therapy. However, the market has long been dominated by costly originator statins, with limited use of generics, leading to substantial economic burdens. Historically, macrolevel procurement policies have predominantly targeted adult chronic diseases, leaving the paediatric population as a persistent policy blind spot. This study provides urgently needed empirical evidence on whether massive price reduction mechanisms can successfully cascade into paediatric clinical practice, thereby addressing a critical gap in global pharmaceutical policy literature.

The study found that following the implementation of the NVBP policy, the average unit prices of atorvastatin and rosuvastatin decreased by 90.71% and 92.14%, respectively, with a concurrent increase in their affordability. The average affordability of atorvastatin improved from 0.242 to 0.014, and rosuvastatin from 0.247 to 0.019, making them the most cost-effective statins. Across provinces, their affordability increased by at least 90.54% and 89.85%, respectively. Prior to the policy, simvastatin had the best affordability due to full insurance coverage. Postpolicy, atorvastatin and rosuvastatin became more affordable than simvastatin. Pitavastatin, initially the least affordable, improved due to price reductions, suggesting a spillover pricing effect of the policy. Over the past decades, the prevalence of dyslipidaemia among children in China has steadily increased,40 while rates of lipid-lowering treatment and target attainment have remained low. For paediatric patients, particularly those with FH who require decades-long, continuous statin adherence for primary prevention, these exponential improvements in affordability are highly clinically significant but also crucial for long-term public health outcomes.41 By reducing drug prices and leveraging health-insurance incentives, the NVBP policy has not only expanded coverage of selected statins but also prompted non-selected manufacturers to cut prices, thereby significantly improving access and easing financial burdens for patients nationwide and promoting equity in health outcomes.

ITS analysis showed significant immediate level increases in the use of atorvastatin (620.6 DDDs) and rosuvastatin (493.9 DDDs) in the month of policy implementation (p<0.01), while expenditures dropped by ¥14 580 and ¥4,690, respectively (p<0.05), demonstrating the ‘volume-for-price’ effect. However, while subsequent long-term trends were generally not significant, this does not mean that the NVBP policy was only a short-term effect. The observed sharp changes in price and usage reflect the rapid market adjustment in the short term, but the lack of significant long-term trends more likely reflects the complexity of market adjustments and the inertia in medication usage patterns. This phenomenon may be related to the gradual establishment of new long-term behaviour patterns within the healthcare system as it adapts to the new policy.

We believe that the NVBP policy, through structural reforms such as linking procurement volumes to hospital performance evaluations and physician key performance indicators (KPIs), has indeed achieved a rapid reset of the market baseline, promoting significant changes in the short term.42 Furthermore, despite the lack of significant long-term trends, this does not mean that the policy has no sustained effects. While the short-term shock triggered dramatic changes in prices and usage, this rapid market reset may bring about new prescribing behaviours in hospitals and physicians,43 44 and as the healthcare system becomes more closely integrated with the insurance system, it is likely to lead to longer-term changes.

Therefore, the NVBP policy has not only improved the accessibility of drugs through immediate price changes but may also influence the allocation of resources and drug usage patterns in the healthcare system over time by gradually driving behavioural changes within healthcare institutions.33 This phenomenon sharply contrasts with the gradual, market-driven generic substitution observed in Western countries (eg, reference pricing systems in Europe).45 46 We attribute this instantaneous reset to China’s command-and-control policy design, in which strict administrative mandates, such as linking procurement volumes to hospital performance evaluations and physician KPIs, force an immediate structural shift in prescribing behaviour.

According to the 2023 China Guidelines for Lipid Management, moderate-intensity statins are first-line therapy for children.11 Atorvastatin and rosuvastatin are widely used due to their long-acting profiles and favourable safety.47 48 Despite low volumes, their high prices contributed disproportionately to expenditures. compared with high-income countries, statin use in China has been costlier while ASCVD treatment is relatively cheap, making prepolicy cost-effectiveness low.13 38 49 The significant price cuts under NVBP have enhanced economic efficiency and reduced household burdens, improving access and equity.

The bid-winning generics refer to those that won the twice daily in the NVBP process; all these drugs have passed the GCE, ensuring their therapeutic equivalence and bioequivalence to the originators.50,52ITS analysis showed a spike in the use of these generics postpolicy, surpassing originators, which remained second in market share. This reflects institutional enforcement and intelligent monitoring driving generic substitution. Hospitals promoted policy adherence through procurement performance targets and IT systems.53 54 The result was a sharp increase in generic use and expenditure, alongside a decrease in originator drug sales and overall spending, highlighting the volume-price linkage. Previous studies have reported similar results.3455,57 In paediatric pharmacotherapy, clinicians and caregivers historically exhibit a profound scepticism toward generic drugs, often strongly preferring originators due to heightened concerns regarding long-term safety, growth impact and efficacy.58 59 The widespread substitution observed in our study underscores the critical role of the GCE mechanism. By guaranteeing quality and equivalence, the NVBP policy effectively overcame historical biases, standardising the quality of paediatric medications and ensuring stable long-term supply chains. Meanwhile, some institutions retained originator stocks to ensure continuity amid short-term supply fluctuations, reflecting a pragmatic clinical compromise to accommodate patient heterogeneity.

The study also identified spillover effects from the policy on certain non-winning statins, such as pravastatin and lovastatin. Lovastatin experienced a brief surge in both usage and expenditure in the month of policy implementation, followed by a rapid return to baseline levels, consistent with market adjustments during the transitional period. Before the policy, the use and expenditure of lovastatin remained relatively stable; the short-term spike may reflect institutional or patient responses to potential supply uncertainties. As the first statin introduced to the market and a relatively affordable option, some institutions or patients may have stockpiled the drug in anticipation of disruptions. Additionally, prescribing inertia and variability in policy enforcement may have contributed to the transient fluctuation. In contrast, pravastatin showed a steady prepolicy trend, but experienced a marked increase in usage and expenditure after implementation. Rather than simply shifting demand from one drug to another due to restrictions, we argue this reflects a rational clinical reallocation. Due to its unique hydrophilic properties, favourable safety profile, minimal central nervous system side effects and limited hepatic and renal impact, pravastatin is highly suitable for children with mild hyperlipidaemia or comorbidities.60 61 As the NVBP policy reshaped the lipid-lowering ecosystem, heightened physician awareness and overall improved affordability likely unlocked the suppressed clinical demand for pravastatin. These spillover effects may result from downstream price reductions and improved overall affordability, thereby expanding its clinical use and dynamically altering the entire therapeutic class landscape.

Strengths of this study include the use of nationwide paediatric hospital procurement data, providing a comprehensive view of policy impacts compared with pilot cities. The inclusion of multiple statins, analysed through pharmacological profiles and expert opinions, enables nuanced assessment across products, offering valuable insights for LMICs. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, due to the nationwide implementation of the NVBP policy and the broad therapeutic spillover effects across the entire statin class, identifying a strictly unaffected statin to serve as an external control group was unfeasible. We lacked access to data on medications other than statins. To mitigate secular trend bias, our study used internal quasi-control comparisons (eg, bid-winning vs non-winning, generic vs originator). Second, due to the de-identification constraints of the procurement data, we were unable to conduct a detailed heterogeneity analysis to explore how the policy’s impact varies across different regional economic levels, hospital tiers or specific health insurance schemes. Third, relying on institutional procurement data limits our ability to capture individual patient-level out-of-pocket costs or exact medication adherence. Furthermore, defining utilisation via DDDs, a metric standardised for adults, represents an inherent pharmaco-epidemiological limitation when applied to paediatric populations, potentially overestimating or underestimating true clinical consumption.

Future research should bridge these gaps by transitioning from procurement data to patient-level electronic health records and real-world claims data. This would allow for longitudinal assessments of actual paediatric clinical outcomes, biochemical target attainment (eg, LDL-C reduction) and long-term safety profiles postpolicy. On a practical level, while the NVBP successfully addressed the pricing barrier, the critical lack of age-appropriate paediatric formulations (eg, oral suspensions or microtablets) persists. Future policy iterations and centralised procurement frameworks must incorporate specialised incentives to stimulate the research and development and targeted procurement of child-friendly formulations, moving beyond the current paradigm of merely using adult dosages in paediatric care.

Conclusions

By analysing the impact of China’s NVBP policy on statin use and expenditure in children’s hospitals, we found that the implementation of the policy significantly increased the use of atorvastatin and rosuvastatin. This improvement enhanced both the accessibility and affordability of statins for children, optimised drug utilisation patterns and reduced overall healthcare spending. These findings underscore the importance and necessity of implementing the NVBP policy and offer scalable, evidence-based strategies for national paediatric drug procurement. The results hold substantial value for public health advancement and policy dissemination.
