Integrated taxonomy and genomic analysis of Marssonina zanthoxyli, the causal agent of prickly ash leaf defoliation
Authors: Jiahui Jiao, Le Zhao, Siyu Zhong, Juanjuan Xue, Guanghui Tang, Peiqin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundZanthoxylum bungeanum is an economically and ecologically significant species, but its production and quality are increasingly threatened by widespread leaf defoliation disease in China. The absence of molecular identification and genomic characterization of the causal pathogen has hindered both fundamental research on host–pathogen interactions and the development of effective control measures.ResultsIn this study, the fungal isolate PL-2024a was isolated from defoliated Z. bungeanum and established as the causal agent via Koch's postulates. Through an integrated morphological analysis that included histopathology (optical, scanning, and transmission electron microscopy) and in vitro culture characteristics, coupled with phylogenetic analyses, we provided the first molecular confirmation and robust characterization of the pathogen as Marssonina zanthoxyli, a species previously described solely by oversimplified morphological characteristics. Subsequent whole-genome sequencing, de novo assembly, and comprehensive annotation of M. zanthoxyli yielded a high-quality genome assembly spanning 80.49 Mb, comprising 19 contigs and encoding 8,494 putative protein-coding genes. Pathogenicity-related functional repertoire analysis identified 354 CAZymes, 100 effector proteins, 70 CYP450s, and 159 enzymes involved in secondary metabolic pathways. Comparative genomic analyses elucidated the phylogenomic evolutionary relationships, estimated divergence times, and characterized gene family expansion/contraction events within M. zanthoxyli. Molecular dating revealed that M. zanthoxyli diverged from its closest relatives approximately 18.5 MYA. Additionally, 221 lineage-specific genes were identified in M. zanthoxyli, with the most enriched functional domains including DNA/RNA polymerases and reverse transcriptases associated with long terminal repeats.ConclusionThis study identified and genomically characterized M. zanthoxyli, clarifying its taxonomic status and revealing pathogenicity’s genetic basis. These findings lay a crucial foundation for understanding Z. bungeanum-M. zanthoxyli interactions and developing targeted leaf defoliation management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12954-x.

Introduction

Zanthoxylum bungeanum (Prickly ash) is a globally valuable economic tree species with both culinary and medicinal uses [1]. Currently, it is extensively available in China, Korea, Japan, India, and other Asian nations [2, 3]. As economically important tree species of Asian agriculture, it is renowned for its unique pungent flavor and therapeutic properties. However, since the 1990 s, a devastating leaf defoliation disease has emerged as a major threat to Z. bungeanum cultivation. The disease is characterized by large amount of black extremely small lesions on leaf surfaces, followed by extensive defoliation, which can drastically reduce photosynthesis, severely stunt plant growth and lead to significant reductions in both fruit yield and quality [4]. Initial investigations attributed the disease to a putative novel Marssonina species and tentatively identified as Marssonina zanthoxyli based on morphological characteristics, particularly conidial morphology and acervuli structure [5]. For decades, and despite its considerable economic importance, this pathogen has suffered from a longstanding lack of standardized Koch's postulates validation, photographic records of key morphological traits (e.g., spore and colony morphology), and conclusive molecular or genomic data to clarify its taxonomic position. In addition, subsequent literature inconsistently referred to the pathogen as M. zanthoxyli [5, 6] or M. zanthoxyla [7, 8], reflecting linguistic ambiguities in Latinization of its host plant Z. bungeanum. Such uncertainties hinder the development of species-specific disease management strategies, as the prevention and management of those emerging diseases require precise taxonomic anchoring [9]. This is particularly important for tree diseases where large contiguous forest ecosystems can be threatened by invasive and emerging pathogens, resulting in ecosystem-wide irreversible damage when prevention and management fail [10].

Species recognition criteria have historically been based on morphology, culture characteristics and host affiliation [11]. However, it is not always easy to identify plant pathogenic fungi to the species level through this method, as they often lack discriminatory morphological characters or cultivable life stages [9]. With the development of molecular technology, DNA sequences and molecular data have become the gold standard and key resource for fungal identification. These data are highly objective because they represent intrinsic characteristics of an organism, impervious to its life stage, morphological plasticity, environmental or cultural conditions, and are highly reproducible [9, 12]. However, simple DNA detection method has limitations when applied to taxa that diverged recently since they often share highly homologous or even identical sequences in these conserved gene regions [10]. Even with multi-gene phylogenetic analyses, reliable differentiation of morphologically similar species remains problematic [13]. Fortunately, the emergence and rapid development of whole-genome sequencing technology provide an unprecedented resolution in discriminating highly related genetic backgrounds [13, 14], which has been widely applied to various animals [15], plants [16], fungi [17], and their closely related species. Whereas, such critical evidence remains conspicuously absent from M. zanthoxyli. Previous studies on this pathogen relied solely on superficial morphological descriptions without photographic documentation [5, 7], casting doubt on the accuracy of its taxonomic assignment and leaving fundamental questions unanswered: Is M. zanthoxyli a legitimate species or a morphological variant of known Marssonina taxa? Do its pathogenicity mechanisms differ from related pathogens infecting rosaceous hosts like apple and pear? Such ambiguities have dire practical consequences. Farmers rely on generic fungicides with diminishing efficacy, while breeders lack molecular markers to develop resistant cultivars. Moreover, global phytosanitary regulations require pathogen-specific diagnostics to prevent cross-border spread—a need unmet by current morphology-based identification. Therefore, bridging these gaps by integrating genomic tools with classical phytopathological methods is critical to resolving the taxonomic ambiguity of M. zanthoxyli and enabling sustainable disease management.

The genus Marssonina (Dermateaceae, Ascomycota) comprises a group of phylogenetically complex plant pathogens responsible for leaf spot and blight diseases across economically vital crops, including rose (Marssonina rosae) [18], poplar (M. brunnea) [19] and apple (M. coronaria) [20], whose taxonomic identities have been extensively characterized through pathogenicity assays [21], multilocus phylogeny [22], and comparative genomics [23]. In stark contrast, M. zanthoxyli, the causal agent of prickly ash leaf defoliation disease, remains poorly understood, with its classification relying solely on outdated morphological descriptions. Meanwhile, public databases (NCBI, UNITE) reveal a striking void: No internal transcribed spacer region (ITS) or genomic sequences are linked to this species, while its presumed congeners possess extensive molecular records. Such disparity underscores a critical knowledge gap, which severely hinders the development of targeted control strategies against the disease. What's more, the mechanisms driving its pathogenicity remain entirely unexplored. Comparative genomic studies of other Marssonina pathogens have identified conserved pathogenicity arsenals, including carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) for cell wall degradation, secondary metabolites for the cells and tissues of host plants damages [24, 25] and effector proteins suppressing host immunity [26–28]. In addition, the interaction mechanism between Marssonina pathogens and their host plants has also been explored [29, 30]. At present, modern biological research, including pathogen studies, heavily relies on public genome databases. These repositories provide essential genomic and functional data, such as gene annotations, protein sequences, and metabolic networks, enabling efficient experimental design and analysis across diverse organisms [31]. In essence, a genomic data can facilitate extensive exploration of various plant pathogenic fungi and open up new horizons in our understanding of fungal pathogenicity.

In this study, we isolated and systematically identified the causal pathogen of prickly ash leaf defoliation disease through integrated histopathological, ultrastructural, and phylogenetic analyses. To further resolve its taxonomic position and decipher its pathogenicity arsenal, we generated a high-quality genome assembly, enabling comprehensive annotation of pathogenicity-related genes, including CAZymes, effectors, and enzymes involved in secondary metabolite biosynthesis. Furthermore, comparative genomic analyses between the isolate and 28 reference species representing Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, and Oomycota phyla elucidated its evolutionary relationships, divergence times, and gene expansion and contraction events, while lineage-specific genes of were functionally annotated to uncover potential host adaptation mechanisms. Our work not only establishes a foundational genomic resource for the pathogen of prickly ash leaf defoliation disease but also provides critical insights into its pathogenic evolution, facilitating future disease management strategies.

Materials and methods

Field observation, collection and identification

Typical infected leaves of Z. bungeanum in the field were observed, and photographs were taken at the experimental demonstration station of Forestry, Northwest A&F University, Yangling, Shaanxi 712,100 (34°18′25.780"N, 107°58′33.582"E). Leaves from infected Z. bungeanum were collected and brought into the laboratory for observation of plant tissue section and fungal isolation.

Pathological tissue from Z. bungeanum leaves was sliced into thin filaments using a sharp blade, with ricepaper pith as the mounting medium. After rinsing in water, sterile water was applied as a floatation agent to prepare temporary slides. Fungal fruiting bodies and spore morphology were directly examined under a microscope and photographed for documentation.

Scanning electron microscope observation

Leaves of Z. bungeanum at different infection stages were collected for scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis to examine pathogen morphology, hyphae, and conidia development. Two millimeter (2 mm) long leaf segments were excised with a sterile scalpel, fixed in 4% glutaraldehyde overnight, and rinsed three times with 0.1 M PBS (5–10 min each). The samples were then dehydrated using a series of ethanol (10%, 30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, 15 min per step), and then rinsed with 100% ethanol two times for 20 min. Followed dealcoholization was performed in 25%, 50% and 75% tert-butanol concentration gradient solution for 15 min each, pure tert-butanol for 30 min. After which the samples were dried in a critical point drier, sputter coated with gold, and examined under a scanning electron microscope (S-3400N) at accelerating voltages of 5 to 15 kV.

Transmission electron microscope observation

Transmission electron microscope (TEM) was performed to examine pathogen morphology and host tissue alterations at different infection stages. The diseased leaf samples were cut into 1 × 5 mm pieces by a blade, fixed with 4% glutaraldehyde (4 °C, overnight). After washing with PBS (0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer, pH = 6.8) four times for 10 min each time, post-fixation was performed with 0.1 mol/L osmium tetroxide at 4 °C for 2 h and briefly washed with PBS at room temperature five times. These leaf tissues were dehydrated through an alcohol series soaking in 30, 50, 70, 80, and 90% alcohol (each for 15 min), and then soaked with 100% ethanol for 30 min, 100% acetone two times for 30 min. After that, they were polymerized with acetone:resin (3:1) for 1.5 h, acetone:resin (1:1) for 3 h, acetone:resin (1:3) overnight, pure resin for 24 h, and transferred to the embedding frame for fixation at 30 °C for 48 h and 60 °C for 48 h. Next, the fruiting bodies of the pathogen were located by semi-thin section technique and staining. Finally, they were sliced into slices using ultra-thin microtome, stained with lead citrate for 15 min, rinsed three times with ddH2O, and dried naturally before being examined.

Fungal isolation and pathogenicity test

The infected leaves of Z. bungeanum were rinsed with sterile distilled water. Diseased leaf tissue fragments (3–5 mm2) were surface-sterilized with 75% ethanol (30 s) and 0.1% HgCl₂ (1–3 min), then rinsed three times with sterile water. The sterilized tissues were subsequently plated on potato dextrose agar (PDA) (potato 200 g, glucose 20 g, and agar 20 g per liter of water)、V8 Juice Agar (V8) (V8 Juice 200 mL, CaCO3 3 g, and agar 20 g per liter of water) and Carrot dextrose agar (CDA) (carrot 200 g, glucose 20 g, and agar 20 g per liter of water), then incubated at 25 °C in darkness. Hyphal tips were transferred to fresh media using sterile toothpicks. Pure cultures obtained after 2–3 subcultures were preserved in 25% glycerol at −80 °C.

Conidia were harvested from hyphal cultures of isolates by adding ~ 5 mL of sterile water to the plate and dislodging hyphae with sterile gauze to prepare a conidial suspension. The suspension was adjusted to 105 conidia/mL and brush-inoculated onto healthy Z. bungeanum seedlings, with sterile water as control (three biological replicates). Inoculated plants were kept at high humidity in darkness (3 days), then moved to a 12-h light/dark cycle for symptom development. Plants were watered as needed to maintain consistent moisture. Koch's postulates were fulfilled by reisolating and confirming the pathogen identity through morphological/molecular analysis.

Morphological identification and molecular identification

The isolate PL-2024a, which reproduced leaf defoliation symptoms consistent with field observations on Z. bungeanum, has been deposited in the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC) under accession CCTCC AF 2026001, and was subsequently selected for detailed morphological characterization. The cryopreserved isolate (−80 °C, 25% glycerol) was revived on V8 medium (25 °C, 14 days), then subcultured. A total of ten replicate cultures were established, starting from an inoculum plug of 5 mm in diameter. Colony morphology (including diameter, texture, and pigmentation) was recorded weekly over a 3-month period. The radial growth rate (mm/day) was calculated using the formula: (final colony radius – 2.5 mm)/time interval (days). Microscopic features containing hyphae and reproductive structures were examined using light microscopy. In addition, a temporary microslide was prepared by picking conidial masses from a 4-week-old culture. Under a light microscope, 100 bicellular conidia were randomly selected and measured precisely for their conidial length (L), width (W), and distance from the base (the smaller end of the bicellular conidia) to the septum (LS). The septation position was calculated as a percentage based on the formula: Septation position (%) = (LS/L) × 100. The length and width of the unicellular conidia were measured using the same method.

Genomic DNA was extracted from one-month-old V8-grown PL-2024a mycelium using a modified CTAB method [32]. The universal primer pair ITS1/ITS4, HSP-F/HSP-R and RPB2-F/RPB2-R synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) was respectively used to amplify the internal transcribed spacer (ITS), heat shock 70 kDa protein (HSP70) and DNA-directed RNA polymerase II (RPB2) for molecular identification (Table S1). Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) reaction was performed in a 25 μL volume containing 1 μL of each primer, 9.5 μL of double distilled water (ddH2O), 12.5 μL of PCR mixture (PrimeSTAR Max DNA Polymerase, Takara), and 1 μL of DNA template under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min; 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The amplified products were sequenced by Sangon Biotech, and the resulting sequences were submitted to GenBank. According to the obtained sequence results, a BLAST analysis was performed in NCBI, and Phylogenetic analysis was performed using MEGA11 (Neighbor-Joining method, 1,000 bootstraps).

Genome sequencing and assembly

The genome of isolate PL-2024a was sequenced using Single Molecule Real-Time (SMRT) technology [33] by the Beijing Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Genomic DNA of PL-2024a was extracted from pure mycelial cultures of isolate PL-2024a using the sodium dodecyl sulfate method [34]. The quality and integrity of the DNA were verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and quantified using a Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

To initially assess the genomic characteristics and confirm sample suitability for PacBio HiFi sequencing, a pilot survey was conducted using Illumina short-read technology. The genomic DNA was fragmented, end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated with Illumina adapters to construct a paired-end library (insert size ~ 350 bp). After PCR enrichment and quality control, the library was sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq PE150 platform. The resulting raw reads were filtered to remove adapter sequences, reads with excessive ambiguous bases (N), and low-quality bases, yielding high-quality clean data. K-mer analysis was performed on the clean data to estimate genome characteristics based on K-mer frequency distribution. Next, de novo genome assembly was performed using SOAP denovo v2.04, SPAdes v4.2.0 [35], and Abyss v2.1.5 [36], followed by integration with CISA and gap-filling with GapClose v1.12 [37]. The assembly was filtered to remove sequences shorter than 500 bp and evaluated for key assembly metrics, including total length, the number of contigs/scaffolds, N50, N90, maximum and minimum lengths. Furthermore, k-mer and GC-depth analyses were conducted by mapping reads back to the assembly.

Subsequently, PacBio HiFi sequencing was performed. Qualified DNA was sheared using a Covaris g-TUBE, and a SMRTbell library with a target insert size of 20 kb was constructed. Sequencing was conducted on a PacBio Sequel II platform. The raw sequencing data were processed to generate subreads by removing adapter sequences. High-fidelity (HiFi) reads were then derived from these subreads using the official CCS software (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/ccs) with stringent parameters (min-passes ≥ 3, min-rq ≥ 0.99), ensuring that all final reads possessed a quality score (Q-score) above 20. This high-quality HiFi read dataset constituted the exclusive input for the subsequent de novo genome assembly. De novo genome assembly was performed using Hifiasm v0.19.9 [38] with all PacBio HiFi reads as input.

Genome component prediction and gene annotation

Whole-genome coding gene prediction for isolate PL-2024a was performed with GeneWise 2.4.1 using the homologous protein sequence of Diplocarpon coronariae NL1 (GCA_002204255.1), Drepanopeziza brunnea MB_m1 (GCF_000298775.1) and Diplocarpon rosae NL1 (GCA_011750715.2) as the reference sequence. The dispersed repeats sequences (DRs) were predicted using the Repeat Masker v4.1.5 (https://www.repeatmasker.org/). The tandem repeats sequence (TRs) were predicted by the Tandem repeats finder v4.07b [39]. Transfer RNAs (tRNAs) were identified with tRNAscan-SE v1.3.1 [40], and ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) were predicted using rRNAmmer v1.2 [41]. For the prediction of small RNAs (sRNAs), small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), and microRNAs (miRNAs), sequences were first aligned against the Rfam database, and candidates were subsequently confirmed using the cmsearch program v1.1rc4 with default parameters. The circular genome map was generated using TBtools V2.056 [42].

Functional annotation of predicted proteins was performed as follows. First, protein sequences were aligned against the integrated NCBI non-redundant (NR) protein database using DIAMOND v2.1.6 with an E-value threshold of ≤ 1e-5. For each query, the top-scoring hit (subject to ≥ 40% identity and ≥ 40% coverage) was retained. Subsequently, functional terms were extracted from the database entries associated with these best hits: Gene Ontology (GO) terms were obtained directly from the corresponding NR entries, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Orthology (KO) identifiers were assigned by mapping the best-hit protein accessions to the KEGG database. Additional annotations were derived similarly from alignments to the Swiss-Prot, KOG, Pfam, and TCDB databases. Carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) were annotated using the dbCAN online server [43]. The predicted protein sequences were submitted to the server, and annotation was performed using HMMER (E-value ≤ 1e-15, coverage ≥ 0.35), DIAMOND (E-value ≤ 1e-102) and and CGCFinder (distance ≤ 2, signature genes = CAZyme + TC). Only CAZyme families supported by at least two of the prediction tools were retained. The method used for the prediction of the PL-2024a secretome and effectors was modified from previous studies [44, 45]. The secretory proteins were predicted using the online tools of SignalP v6.0 [46], WoLF PSORT [47], TargetP v2.0 [48], Deep TMHMM v1.0 [49] and big-PI Predictor [50]. Finally, based on the above results, EffectorP v3.0 [42], a predictive tool for effectors of plant pathogenic fungi and oomycetes, was used to predict candidate effector protein of PL-2024a. Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) genes were identified by searching the predicted protein sequences against the specialized CYP450 database using BLASTP. Biosynthetic gene clusters for secondary metabolites were predicted and characterized using antiSMASH v2.0.2 [51]. The secondary metabolites genomic distribution was visualized using Chiplot.

Genome sequencing quality assessment

Assembly quality was comprehensively evaluated through multiple approaches. First, the taxonomic origin and purity of the assembly were assessed by blasting 10-kb genomic fragments against the NCBI NT database. Second, data-to-assembly concordance was quantified by independently aligning the Illumina reads (using BWA-MEM v0.7.17 [52]) and the HiFi reads (using Minimap2 v2.24 [53]) back to the PL-2024a PacBio HiFi-based genome assembly to calculate mapping rates and coverage depth for each data type. Third, consensus accuracy was evaluated with Merqury v1.3 [54] by independently comparing the k-mer spectra of each original dataset to the assembly, deriving separate consensus quality values (QV). Fourth, assembly completeness was assessed using BUSCO v5.7.1 [55] and Compleasm v0.2.6 [56] against the helotiales_odb10 lineage dataset. Assembly accuracy and heterozygosity were estimated by calling SNPs and InDels from the aligned Illumina reads using the GATK v4.4.0.0 best practices pipeline and calculating homozygous and heterozygous variant rates. Finally, the genome circle was generated using Circos v0.69.6 (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/) to present the overall assessment results.

Comparative genomics analysis

Genome sequences of 29 phytopathogenic species, including isolate PL-2024a, representing eight phyla across Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, and Oomycota with three distinct nutritional modes (Table S2), were retrieved from NCBI. Orthologous gene clusters were identified using Orthofinder v2.5.4 [57], followed by alignment of single-copy orthologs with MAFFT v7.0 [58] and conservation filtering using Gblocks v0.91b [59]. A phylogenomic tree was constructed using RAxML v1.2.0 [60] based on maximum likelihood inference to reveal the evolutionary relationships of PL-2024a. Molecular clock models were applied to further estimate the time of divergence in the evolutionary history of PL-2024a. The MCMCTree program in the PAML package was used to incorporate multiple calibration points and account for uncertainties in divergence time estimation. Correction points were obtained from the TimeTree website (http://www.timetree.org/). Additionally, gene family expansion and contraction events were also analyzed using CAFE v2.1 [61].

Genomic synteny analysis was performed using TBtools v2.056 [42] to better investigate the genetic organization and evolutionary divergence of PL-2024a relative to six other Marssonina species (including their teleomorphs) — Diplocarpon coronariae (GCA_037039515.1 and GCA_964058965.1), Diplocarpon rosae (GCA_032760415.1), D. mali (GCA_024741835.1) and Drepanopeziza brunnea (GCF_022702325.1 and GCF_000298775.1). The conserved domains of expanded, contracted and unique genes in PL-2024a were identified using InterProScan [62] integrating multiple predictors (Pfam, SMART, Gene3D, SUPERFAMILY and NCBI-CDD). The domain architecture was subsequently visualized using TBtools v2.056 [42]. The conserved motifs were identified using the MEME program v5.5.2 accessed online at the MEME Suite website (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme). The analysis was configured to identify up to 10 motifs with a width range of 6 to 100 amino acids. All other parameters were kept at their default settings.

Results

Field symptoms of prickly ash leaf defoliation disease

Through the investigations of the field hazards of prickly ash leaf defoliation disease, it was observed that the disease primarily affects the leaves of Z. bungeanum, with minimal damage to the petiole, shoots or tender tips (Fig. 1, Figure S1). Compared to healthy trees with vigorous growth and intact foliage (Fig. 1A, Figure S1A), infected trees exhibited weakened growth, with leaves turning yellow, and shedding in large quantities (Figure S1B). In the early stages of the disease, yellowish patches appear on the leaf surfaces (Fig. 1B). As the disease progresses, circular dark brown lesions (0.5–1.0 mm diameter) developed on both the upper and lower surfaces of the leaves, often concentrated in clusters (Fig. 1C, Figure S1C). In the later stages of the disease, distinct rash-like protrusions appeared on the leaf surfaces. Under high humidity conditions, these protrusions (i.e., the acervulus of the pathogen) often produce white needle-shaped conidial mass (Fig. 1D, E). Analysis of histological section characteristics and microscopic features of the pathogen causing prickly ash leaf defoliation

To characterize the pathogen responsible for prickly ash leaf defoliation, symptomatic leaf tissues were sectioned and examined under the microscope. The observations showed that the pathogen can form typical acervulus (Fig. 2A), which can rupture the leaf epidermis and produce abundant conidia (Fig. 2B, C). The observed conidia were bicellular, asymmetrical in size, and either straight or slightly curved, with each cell containing two distinct oil globules. These morphological characteristics align with those described by Cao et al. (1994) [5] for a pathogen causing prickly ash leaf defoliation in Shaanxi and Gansu provinces. Besides, we also observed numerous small, oval unicellular spores (Fig. 2B, C), morphologically consistent with the spermatia of Marssonina zanthoxyla, as reported by Lu & Li (1995) [7] for the pathogen causing prickly ash leaf defoliation in Shandong province. The typical structures and infection characteristics of the pathogen on infected leaf tissues at different disease stages were further observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). It was found that the pathogen's conidia can invade through the leaf stomata. Germ tubes emerging from bicellular and unicellular conidia were clearly observed penetrating stomatal openings (Fig. 2D-F). Numerous acervulus of varying sizes developed on both the adaxial and abaxial surfaces of the infected leaves. In the later stages, the acervulus rupture the leaf epidermis and split open, releasing abundant bicellular conidia that aggregated into prominent conidial masses (Fig. 2G). Additionally, during the advanced stages of infection, a large number of small unicellular conidia will be produced in the acervulus (Fig. 2H).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of Z. bungeanum leaf tissues revealed striking ultrastructural differences between healthy and diseased specimens. TEM observations revealed the presence of pathogen acervulus in the leaf tissues infected with prickly ash leaf defoliation disease (Fig. 2I), within which a large number of bicellular and unicellular conidia were formed (Fig. 2J,K). In healthy Z. bungeanum leaves, the mesophyll cells exhibited intact structures and clearly visible organelles (Fig. 2L). Mitochondria, exhibiting an ellipsoidal shape, were distributed in the interstices between chloroplasts. The chloroplasts displayed characteristic spindle-shaped morphology, tightly appressed against the cell walls with well-preserved double membranes and distinct grana thylakoid stacks. Within the chloroplast stroma, several elongated-oval starch grains were clearly visible. In stark contrast, infected tissues exhibited severe cellular damage with complete degradation of intracellular organelles (Fig. 2M). Based on our morphological observations, we hypothesize that the pathogen invades the leaf tissues and secretes chemical substances that disrupt the structural integrity of the mesophyll cells.

Isolation and pathogenicity verification of pathogen of prickly ash leaf defoliation disease

Combined SEM and TEM analyses comprehensively characterize the morphological features and infection process of the pathogen affecting Z. bungeanum leaves. Through tissue isolation, we obtained isolate PL-2024a (Fig. 3A), which was confirmed as the causal agent of the defoliation disease. On artificial media, the isolate initially formed white colonies with dense mycelial cords and exhibited slow radial growth. Microscopic examination revealed that the conidia identical in morphology to those observed in infected leaf tissues through histological sections, SEM, and TEM analyses (Fig. 3B). Pathogenicity of isolate PL-2024a was confirmed through fulfillment of Koch's postulates. Artificial inoculation of Z. bungeanum seedlings with PL-2024a consistently reproduced field-observed symptoms, including characteristic leaf defoliation and production of grayish-white conidial masses (Fig. 3C-E). Disease progression followed a distinct temporal pattern: initial pinhead-sized dark brown lesions appeared at 5 days post-inoculation (dpi) (Figure S2A), followed by lesion expansion and increased symptom severity by 12 dpi (Figure S2B). By 20 dpi, plants exhibited extensive defoliation accompanied by the appearance of conidial masses (Fig. 3D, E). The fallen leaves in 12 dpi (Figure S2C) were subjected to moist chamber incubation for 3 days, grayish-white secretions, identified as conidial masses of the pathogen, were observed on the lesions (Figure S2D). After 7 days of incubation, the conidial masses became larger and more distinct (Figure S2E). Successful re-isolation of PL-2024a from infected tissues, exhibiting identical morphological characteristics to the original isolate, provided conclusive evidence that this isolate is the causal agent of prickly ash leaf defoliation disease.

Morphological and molecular identification of PL-2024a

The growth characteristics and morphological features of isolate PL-2024a were systematically documented during cultivation on V8 medium (Fig. 4, Figure S3). The colony demonstrated an exceptionally slow growth rate of 0.27 ± 0.0042 mm/day, reaching a diameter of only 46.6 ± 0.65 mm after 77 days of cultivation (Figure S3A-K), with subsequent growth plateauing (Figure S3L-S). During the early growth phase (1–42 days), colonies appeared white with prolific rhizomorph formation across the surface (Fig. 4A). Low-temperature cultivation induced distinct colony differentiation, featuring dark brown margins and off-white centers (Fig. 4B). During the mid‑to‑late stages of cultivation, abundant milky‑white conidial masses formed on mature colonies (Fig. 4C). Macroscopically, milky-white, spherical protrusions were observed on the rhizomorphs (Fig. 4D). Microscopical examination confirmed that these protrusions correspond to acervulus developed from the rhizomorphs. The rhizomorph itself is a rope-like structure formed by intertwined hyphae (Fig. 4E), and acervulus can arise directly from it (Fig. 4F). Within these acervulus (Fig. 4G), large numbers of both bicellular and unicellular conidia were produced (Fig. 4H). The bicellular conidia were hyaline, straight to slightly curved, obovoid, with a single septum forming two unequal cells, and ranged from 21.1–26.3 × 5.3–6.3 μm in size. The septum is located at 29.17%–36.36% of the conidium length from the base. Unicellular conidia were markedly smaller than bicellular conidia (5.2–6.3 × 2.1–3.2 μm), exhibiting elongated-oval shapes. Phylogenetic analyses of isolate PL-2024a was performed using three genomic markers: ITS, RPB2, and HSP. Successful PCR amplification generated products of 574 bp (ITS), 1,433 bp (RPB2), and 1,885 bp (HSP), which were subsequently sequenced for phylogenetic analysis. Due to significant discrepancies in species matches for ITS, RPB2, and HSP in NCBI (Table S3), phylogenetic trees were constructed separately for each gene (Fig. 4I-K). The ITS sequence of PL-2024a showed only 95.10% similarity with the closest Marssonina species in NCBI (below the typical 97% species delineation threshold) [63] and formed a distinct branch in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 4I). However, the RPB2 and HSP sequences exhibited higher similarity with Drepanopeziza brunnea (97.02% and 97.85%, respectively), while displaying no significant matches with known Marssonina or its teleomorph Diplocarpon or Drepanopeziza species (Fig. 4J, K). Instead, they showed weak concordance (70–90% identity) with phylogenetically distant genera such as Fusarium and Aspergillus, likely attributable to the current lack of reference sequences for Marssonina and its teleomorphs in public databases. Collectively, these molecular findings underscore the taxonomic novelty of PL-2024a.

The PHI database [66], a key resource for studying pathogen-host interactions and the molecular mechanisms underlying plant diseases, was used to identify coding genes in PL-2024a that are associated with pathogenicity and effector functions. Secondary metabolism plays a crucial role in the pathogenicity of plant pathogen fungi by producing mycotoxins to kill plant cells. The biosynthesis of mycotoxins is believed to be regulated by various genes, including those encoding CYP450, PKS, NRPS, and more. In the case of PL-2024a, a total of 70 genes were annotated as CYP450 (Table S12) and categorized into nine classes, with the most annotations were "E-class P450, group I" (Fig. 6D). These findings highlight the importance of studying the functions of genes associated with the secondary metabolism of PL-2024a to further elucidate the biosynthesis mechanisms of mycotoxins.

Discussion

The leaf defoliation disease of prickly ash was first reported in Shaanxi, China in 1990. Subsequent taxonomic studies by Cao et al. (1994) [5] and Lu & Li (1995) [7] independently described the pathogen of the disease as a new Marssonina species only based on conidial morphology, naming it M. zanthoxyli and M. zanthoxyla respectively (a nomenclature discrepancy likely arising from different Latinizations of the host genus). Although subsequent studies characterized various biological traits (including the optimal growth temperature, pH, colony growth, and nutrient utilization for spore germination), there is still a lack of photographic documentation of key morphological features (conidia and colony morphology) and molecular evidence [6, 8]. With advancements in plant pathogen identification systems and molecular technologies, relying solely on morphological characteristics has become insufficient for accurate taxonomic classification of the prickly ash leaf spot pathogen [67]. In this study, we employed a polyphasic approach, including Koch's postulates (Fig. 5, Figure S2), morphological examination (Fig. 4A-H), phylogenetic analysis (ITS、HSP and RPB2) (Fig. 4I-K), and comparative genomics (Fig. 7A), to supplement and deepen the research on Marssonina zanthoxyli and clarify its phylogenetic position. The unique morphological features and molecular phylogenetic analysis offer compelling support for the recognition of M. zanthoxyli as a new species. In contrast to closely related species like Drepanopeziza brunnea (15.0–21.4 × 3.8–7 μm) [68, 69], Diplocarpon rosae (19.28–21.68 × 5.36–6.38 μm) [70], and Diplocarpon coronariae (12–20 × 4–6 μm) [71, 72], M. zanthoxyli PL-2024a exhibits significantly larger bicellular conidia, measuring 21.1–26.3 × 5.3–6.3 μm. This measurement is well within the broader range (13.8–26.8 × 5.0–10.0 μm) of M. zanthoxyla reported by Lu et al. [7]. This phenotypic divergence aligns with established fungal taxonomic criteria [73], where conidial dimensions are key diagnostic traits for Marssonina species delineation. Molecular analyses of multiple loci (ITS, RPB2, and HSP) revealed that the ITS sequence shares only 95.10% similarity with the closest Marssonina species in NCBI (below the typical 97% species demarcation threshold) [63]. While RPB2 and HSP sequences showed marginally higher similarity (97.02% and 97.85%, respectively) to Drepanopeziza brunnea, none of these loci matched other Marssonina or Diplocarpon species. Instead, they displayed weak alignment (70–90% identity) with phylogenetically distant genera (Fusarium, Aspergillus), likely due to the scarcity of reference sequences for Marssonina and its teleomorph Diplocarpon in public databases. In addition, RPB2 and HSP are typically highly conserved fungal genes which may exhibit unusually slow evolutionary rates within Marssonina [74], leading to artifactual similarity with distantly related taxa. Critically, whole-genome phylogenetic analysis resolved M. zanthoxyli as a distinct lineage separate from all reference Marssonina species (Fig. 7A). While M. zanthoxyli showed a close phylogenetic relationship with D. brunnea, M. zanthoxyli diverged approximately 18.5 MYA, which is about 17.9 MYA earlier than the divergence of this species. The unique morphological traits, genomic distinctiveness and the host specificity (Z. bungeanum) all supported the formation of new ecological species.

Genome sequencing and annotation have enabled the identification and characterization of pathogenesis-related genes and gene families in both hosts and pathogens. These genomic insights can provide useful information about genes that may be important for plant infection and colonization and will form a solid foundation for systems biology studies of plant pathogenic fungi [75, 76]. To date, the genomes of majority of Marssonina teleomorphs have been sequenced and analyzed, such as Drepanopeziza brunnea (GCA_000298775.1) [77], Diplocarpon rosae (GCA_002317995.1) [78], Diplocarpon coronaria (GCA_002204255.1) [79] and Diplocarpon mali (GCA_024741835.1). These important genomic data have provide crucial resources for the identification of virulence factors of Marssonina pathogen and the study of their interaction mechanisms with host plants [79, 80]. To reduce the research gap with other Marssonina species, we sequenced, assembled, and annotated the genome of M. zanthoxyli PL-2024a (GCA_046580505.1), resulting in a relatively high-quality genome with a size of 80.49 Mb containing 19 contigs with a contig N50 value of 4.49 Mb (Table S2). The genome assembly quality of Marssonina zanthoxyli (contig N50 = 4.49 Mb, BUSCO completeness = 97.47%) represents a significant improvement over existing Marssonina genomes (e.g., D. brunnea, D. rosae, D. coronariae and D. mali), which often suffer from fragmentation (contig N50 typically < 4.2 Mb) (Table S20). Notably, M. zanthoxyli exhibits a substantially larger genome size (80.49 Mb) compared to other Marssonina species (~ 30–60 Mb) in NCBI database (Table S20). This expansion may be driven by the proliferation of long terminal repeat retrotransposons (LTR-RTs), which are known genomic elements that facilitate genome expansion in fungi and plant [81, 82]. Comparative genomic analysis revealed a substantial enrichment of long terminal repeat retrotransposon (LTR-RT)-related sequences in the unique and expanded gene repertoire of M. zanthoxyli (Table S21). Further genome-wide domain prediction revealed that M. zanthoxyli contains 171 LTR-RTs spanning 268 kb, accounting for 0.33% of the total genome length. This proportion is significantly higher than that observed in related species: D. brunnea (0.0%, 0.02%), D. rosae (0.004%), D. coronariae (0.008%, 0.1%), and D. mali (0.0%) (Table S21). This finding aligns with established mechanisms of transposable element-driven genome expansion observed across fungal taxa. Such as the variation in genome size between Ustilago hordei isolates can largely be attributed to intraspecific differences in LTR-RT proliferation and/or retention [81]. The exceptional LTR-RT accumulation in M. zanthoxyli may represent an evolutionary strategy to shape the genome structure and function or generate genetic diversity through induce gene translocation, chromosome rearrangement, and regulate gene expression [83, 84]. Notably, Z. bungeanum represents the sole known host plant for M. zanthoxyli. The enrichment of LTR-RT-related sequences in M. zanthoxyli-specific genes suggests these mobile genetic elements may have contributed to host-specific adaptation to Zanthoxylum plants.

To successfully colonize host plants while evading or suppressing their defense systems, phytopathogens have evolved diverse virulence factors that facilitate host penetration, intracellular multiplication and systemic spread, and eventual exit the host for a new infection cycle [85]. Sequenced genomes of plant pathogens provide blueprints from which potential virulence factors and genes associated with different pathogenic strategies can be identified and predicted [86]. Based on the acquisition of the genome of M. zanthoxyli, its potential pathogenic factors, including CAZymes, effectors and secondary metabolite synthases were preliminary predicted using bioinformatics technology. In our study, we predicted 354 CAZymes coding genes in the genome of M. zanthoxyli, a hemibiotrophic pathogen (Fig. 6A, Table S12), which was dramatically larger than those in bitrophic phytopathogenic fungi, such as Ustilago maydis [87], Melampsora larici [88], Blumeria graminis [89]. This finding is consistent with the previous viewpoint that hemibiotrophic phytopathogenic fungi have more CAZymes than bitrophic pathogens [90, 91]. A total of 100 effectors were obtained in M. zanthoxyli (Fig. 6B, Table S13). Currently, effector protein identification has been performed in several Marssonina species, including M. rosae [24] and M. brunnea [26, 27]. Notably, research on Marssonina rosae has advanced to elucidate the mechanisms by which effector proteins mediate pathogen-host [28]. Fungal secondary metabolites are classified into four major chemical groups: polyketides, terpenoids, shikimate-derived compounds, and non-ribosomal peptides [92]. Their biosynthesis is typically governed by multi-gene systems, including CYP450, PKS, NRPS, and terpene synthase genes, etc. In M. zanthoxyli, we identified 159 secondary metabolism-related genes (Fig. 6E), with T1PKS and terpene synthases being the most abundant. Intriguingly, Z. bungeanum leaves are rich in defensive metabolites like xanthoxylin and limonene [93, 94], suggesting that the prevalence of T1PKS and terpene-related genes in M. zanthoxyli may reflect evolutionary adaptation to its host’s chemical defenses, though functional validation is needed to confirm this hypothesis. Notably, comparative genomic analysis with 28 other phytopathogenic species identified an expansion of CAZymes (Glycosyl Transferases) genes in the PL-2024a genome (Figure S7, Table S16). This gene duplication and amplification may reflects the hemibiotrophic lifestyle of PL-2024a. During the initial biotrophic phase, hemibiotrophic fungi suppress host defenses and maintain cell viability, while the subsequent necrotrophic phase requires efficient cell wall degradation for nutrient acquisition [95, 96]. The amplification of CAZyme genes may therefore represent an adaptive strategy to balance these dual-phase requirements, particularly for breaching the cell wall of its specific host Z. bungeanum during the transition to necrotrophy. Interestingly, the expansion of retrotransposon-related genes suggests a highly dynamic genomic architecture. The transposable element activity may drive genomic rearrangements and generate genetic variability, potentially accelerating the evolution of pathogenicity-related genes in response to host resistance [97, 98]. This co-occurrence of retrotransposon expansion with genes involved in pathogenicity, including retropepsin [99], amino acid permease [100], trehalose synthase [101] and retropepsin [99], raises the possibility that transposon-mediated events have contributed to the neofunctionalization or copy number variation of these virulence factors. Collectively, all the expanded genes likely contributes synergistically to the pathogen's virulence and adaptive success.

Advances in sequencing technology have led to the identification of a growing number of taxon-specific 'orphan' genes. While the origins of these genes remain elusive, evidence suggests their involvement in lineage-specific adaptive mechanisms [102–104]. These orphan genes are hypothesized to regulate fundamental biological processes such as development, reproduction, and survival, while enhancing genetic diversity and evolutionary fitness through niche-specific adaptation [105]. In this study, we conducted a comparative genomics analysis of seven Marssonina species, resulting in the identification of 68 gene families encompassing a total of 221 genes, that were found to be unique to M. zanthoxyli (Fig. 7C). Of these, 118 specific genes, accounting for 53.39% of all the unique genes, were anticipated to harbor conserved domains (Fig. 8B, Table S18). Among them, conserved domain DNA/RNA polymerases, Retrovirus zinc finger-like, and RT-LTR were the most frequently observed. A striking observation is the prevalence of retrotransposon-related domains within both expanded and unique gene sets, reinforcing the notion that transposable element activity is a major driver of genomic divergence in this lineage [106, 107]. This dynamism has facilitated the evolution of species-specific traits, including those involved in host specialization and the hemibiotrophic lifestyle. Among the unique genes, several encode proteins with domains directly implicated in host–pathogen interactions. The presence of lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases [108], glycosyl hydrolases [109, 110], and cellulose binding domains [111] suggests a refined capacity to degrade the plant cell wall, which is particularly important during the necrotrophic phase of hemibiotrophic infection. MFS transport proteins [112–115] and Cytochrome P450 [116–118] genes are often co-opted in pathogen genomes to detoxify plant secondary metabolites and efflux antifungal compounds. Given that Z. bungeanum is rich in xanthoxylin and limonene [93, 94], these unique CYP450 and MFS genes could be critical for overcoming host chemical defenses during both biotrophic and necrotrophic phases. Although the function of PKc [119], P-loop NTPase [120] in pathogenic fungi remains largely unknown, they have been shown to play important roles in fungal development and plant infection in many plant pathogen. Existing studies have reported that genes containing the fungal_TF_MHR domain are related to the fungicide resistance of pathogenic bacteria. The genes containing fungal_TF_MHR domain have been reported to be related to the fungicide resistance of Magnaporthe oryzae [121]. Therefore, further investigation of the pathogenic functions and mechanisms of these specific genes may provide insights into the co-evolutionary dynamics between M. zanthoxyli and Z. bungeanum.

Conclusions

This study established isolate PL-2024a as the fungal pathogen responsible for prickly ash leaf defoliation by fulfilling Koch’s postulates. Furthermore, morphological differences and comprehensive phylogenomic analysis identified isolate PL-2024a as M. zanthoxyli, likely representing a unique Marssonina lineage with an estimated divergence time of 18.5 million years from its closest known relatives. The high-quality genome assembly and comparative genomic analysis of PL-2024a revealed a specialized pathogenicity arsenal, including CAZymes, effectors, and secondary metabolite synthases. These genomic features collectively illuminate M. zanthoxyli's adaptive evolution to its Zanthoxylum host and provided the molecular data for developing diagnostic markers and intervention strategies. Our findings not only clarified the classification status of M. zanthoxyli but also provide a critical foundation for understanding host–pathogen interactions and advancing sustainable management of this economically significant disease.
