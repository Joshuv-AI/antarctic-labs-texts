Gene expression profile, and role of baicalein in the inhibition of thyroid cancer
Authors: Nan Wu, Qian Zhang, Muhammad Naeem, Ren Jing, Yuanbin Luo, Yang Wu, Shijian Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnaplastic thyroid cancer (ATC) is the highly undifferentiated form of thyroid cancer, and its incidence is still high all around the globe. The current treatment for ATC includes surgery, chemotherapy, and radioactive iodine therapy. These treatment options are not reliable due to high cost, drug resistance, and toxicity issues. Baicalein (BA) is a natural flavonoid isolated from Scutellaria baicalensis that exhibits several pharmacological activities, including anticancer, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antitumor. The role of BA in elucidating the mechanism of ATC, emphasizing the CLU-mediated mitophagy, remains unclear. This study was designed to investigate the impact of BA on gene expression and cellular pathways in CAL62 ATC cells by CLU-mediated mitophagy.MethodsDifferential gene expression and pathway enrichment were assessed using RNA sequencing and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). Mitochondrial fluorescence and mitophagy markers (PINK1, PRKN, ATG5, MFN1) were evaluated by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and fluorescence microscopy. CLU expression and its correlation with cancer progression were analyzed using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data.ResultsBA treatment altered gene expression and pathway activity, impacting processes including cell proliferation, mitophagy, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition. It reduced the mitochondrial fluorescence intensity and mitophagy marker levels, consistent with an inhibition of mitophagy. BA also modulated thyroid cancer markers, indicating the induced dedifferentiation. TCGA analysis confirmed the CLU upregulation in thyroid cancer, linking it to disease progression and survival. BA inhibits ATC cell growth, an effect associated with the alteration of CLU-linked mitophagy and key signaling pathways.ConclusionsThese findings highlight that CLU as a potential therapeutic target and suggested BA as a promising strategy for thyroid cancer intervention.

Introduction

Thyroid cancer (TC) is a malignant tumor of the endocrine system. It is characterized by metastasis to the neck lymph nodes and by invasion of various organs, such as the bones, liver, and lungs. It has shown a consistent rise in mortality and incidence worldwide (1,2). Advances in diagnostic imaging and screening have enabled earlier detection, yet, the disease still presents with diverse clinical outcomes depending on histological subtype. Among TCs, anaplastic thyroid cancer (ATC) is the most aggressive and fatal type, characterized by profound dedifferentiation, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, rapid proliferation, and marked drug resistance to available treatments (3). Prognosis for ATC remains extremely poor, with a median overall survival of 5-6 months, and progression-free survival is about 3-16 months.

Current treatment options for TC are limited to surgery, targeted therapies, and immunotherapy. These treatment options are not reliable due to some limitations, such as chemoradiotherapy can cause significant toxicity issues, targeted therapies frequently face resistance, and immunotherapy has shown limited efficacy. These challenges underscore the urgent need for new therapeutic approaches and a better understanding of ATC pathogenesis (4,5).

Baicalein (BA), a natural, bioactive flavonoid derived from the traditional Chinese herb Scutellaria baicalensis, demonstrates diverse pharmacological activities, including antitumor, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects (6,7). Previous literature has shown that BA inhibits cell proliferation, induces apoptosis, and suppresses metastatic potential by modulating key signaling pathways in TC research. Its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects further support its role in reducing tumor progression. These findings suggested that BA could be used as a promising candidate for complementary therapeutics in TC management (8,9).

BA has been reported to exert anticancer effects in thyroid malignancies by regulating cell growth, apoptosis, and metastatic behavior. The role of clusterin (CLU) has been linked to cellular differentiation and tumor progression in ATC. BA also downregulates CLU expression in ATC CAL62 cells, influencing multiple signaling pathways and potentially affecting ATC cell dedifferentiation (10). As a molecular chaperone, CLU promotes the class III phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) activity near the damaged mitochondria, with disruption of mitochondrial autophagic flux leading to reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated apoptosis, in which ROS induced programmed cell death. CLU also mediates the mitochondrial biogenesis through the PPARGC1A/PGC1α pathway, maintaining the mitochondrial homeostasis (11-13). However, the role of CLU in ATC dedifferentiation remains unexplored.

In this study, the impact of BA on gene expression and cellular pathways in CAL62 ATC cells, with emphasis on CLU-mediated mitophagy and differentiation, was investigated. Differential gene expression and pathway enrichment were assessed through RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). Mitochondrial fluorescence and mitophagy markers (PINK1, PRKN, ATG5, MFN1) were evaluated by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and fluorescence microscopy. We also explored the role of BA in modulating the CLU/PPARGC1A/PGC1α pathway. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-aw-2437/rc).

Methods

Materials and reagents

BA (purity: 98.94%, HY-N0196) was procured from MedChemExpress Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, 11995065) and fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10099141) were obtained from Gibco (Grand Island, NY, USA) and Invitrogen (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA), respectively. Enhanced Cell Counting Kit-8 reagent (CCK-8, C0042) and Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) apoptosis detection kits (C1062L) were supplied by Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), along with Golgi-Tracker Red (C1043) and Mito-Tracker Green (C1048). The Human CLU ELISA Kit was purchased from Sangon Biotech (D711022-0048; Shanghai, China). Abcam (Cambridge, UK) provided the anti-CLU antibody (ab79280) and Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) secondary antibody (ab150079). The anti-GAPDH antibody (5174S) was acquired from Cell Signaling Technology, Ltd. (Beverly, USA). For qPCR, the PrimeScriptTM RT reagent Kit with genomic DNA (gDNA) Eraser (Perfect Real Time) (RR047A) and TB Green® Premix Ex TaqTM (Tli RNaseH Plus) (RR420A) were purchased from Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Purification of CLU

CLU was purified from fresh, normal human plasma using a previously established protocol (14). Plasma was precipitated with 12–23% polyethylene glycol and overnight at 4 ℃. The resulting precipitate was dissolved, and further purification was performed through DEAE-Sepharose and heparin-Sepharose column chromatography (GE, Piscataway, USA). CLU-positive fractions were subjected to affinity chromatography using a monoclonal anti-CLU antibody (1G8) column. The 1G8 antibody was developed using the human full-length recombinant CLU (rCLU) expressed in Escherichia coli and covalently bound to the cyanogen bromide-activated Sepharose 4B (Sigma-Aldrich, New York, USA). Eluted proteins were dialyzed against phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stored at −80 ℃ until use. Endotoxin levels in the purified CLU were confirmed by the limulus amoebocyte lysate kinetic turbidimetric assay (Endosafe, Charles River Laboratory, Korea).

Cell culture and treatment

Human ATC cell lines CAL62 were obtained from Zhong Qiaoxinzhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 0.1% FBS and 1% streptomycin/penicillin (B21210; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA) and incubated at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2. To investigate changes in gene expression and transcriptomic profiles, CAL62 cells were treated with BA at concentrations of 0, 50, 100, and 150 µM for 24 h. The cells were divided into four groups: control (Ctrl), BA (100 µM), and a combination of BA (100 µM) with rCLU protein (rCLU, 1 µg/mL). Ultrastructural and morphological changes in cultured ATC cells were observed using the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

Cell viability assay

CAL62 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1×105 cells per well and incubated for 24 h at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2. Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA).

Wound-healing assay

CAL62 cells were plated into the six-well plates and cultured to 90% confluence. A vertical scratch was created at the center of each well using a 1.0 mL pipette tip. Cells were treated with BA (100 µM) and a combination of BA (100 µM) and rCLU (1 µg/mL), and incubated for a further period. Cell migration was assessed by monitoring the scratch closure under a microscope at 24 and 48 h. The wound closure rate was calculated as the percentage reduction in scratch width compared to the initial width.

Apoptosis analysis

To assess the apoptosis induced by BA or rCLU, an Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide (PI) apoptosis detection kit was used. Following treatment, CAL62 cells were resuspended in 100 µL of 1× Annexin V binding buffer, treated with 2.5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 2.5 µL PI solution, and incubated on ice in the dark for 20 min. After adding 400 µL of the 1× Annexin V binding buffer, samples were analyzed by flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy for apoptosis.

Mitochondria and mitophagy staining

Mitochondrial and mitophagy staining were performed to visualize mitochondrial structures in CAL62 cells. These cells were incubated at 37 ℃ for 30 min in the medium containing Mito-Tracker, followed by Hoechst 33342 staining for 5 min. Images were captured with multiplex confocal microscopy (LSM980, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from tumor tissues and CAL62 cells using TrizolTM reagent (Invitrogen, #15596026), followed by complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis with the PrimeScriptTM RT Kit (with gDNA Eraser) and subsequent qRT-PCR analysis using TB Green® Premix Ex TaqTM II on a qTOWER384G system (Analytik Jena AG, Thuringia, Germany). The list of primers and their particular sequences is shown in Table 1. Reactions were performed in triplicate, and relative gene expression was normalized to GAPDH using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Immunofluorescent staining

CAL62 cells were washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. After fixation, the cells were washed again with PBS and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min. Blocking was performed with 5 % bovine serum albumin for 30 min, followed by an overnight incubation at 4 ℃ with anti-PLAU antibody in a humidified chamber. Cells were then washed three times with PBS and incubated with Alexa Fluor® 594-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) F(ab')2 fragment as a secondary antibody for 60 min at 4 ℃. Imaging was conducted using the multiplex confocal microscopy (LSM980, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

ELISA quantified protein levels according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cell culture supernatants were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min to remove debris. Diluted samples (100 µL) or standards were added to 96-well plates pre-coated with capture antibodies and incubated at 37 ℃ for 2 h. After PBS-T washes, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated detection antibodies (100 µL) were added to the mixture, followed by a 1-hour incubation. 3,3',5,5'-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate (100 µL) was incubated in the dark for 15 min to develop color, then stopped with sulfuric acid. Absorbance was recorded at 450 nm, protein concentrations were determined from a standard curve, and each sample was tested in triplicate.

RNA-seq library preparation and Illumina sequencing

RNA integrity was assessed with the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Total RNA served as the input material for the RNA library preparation. Briefly, mRNA was isolated using poly-T oligo-conjugated magnetic beads and reverse-transcribed into first-strand cDNA using random hexamer primers and M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase (RNase H−). The second-strand cDNA synthesis followed, using the DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. After adenylation of the 3' ends, adaptors with hairpin loop structures were ligated to the cDNA for hybridization. To achieve the desired fragment size of 370–420 bp, library fragments were purified using the AMPure XP system. PCR was performed with Phusion High-Fidelity DNA polymerase, universal PCR primers, and Index (X) primers. The final PCR products were purified using the AMPure XP system, and library quality was verified on the Bioanalyzer 2100. The mRNA sequencing (mRNA-seq) data quality was assessed through FastQC.

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from RNA-seq data

Index-coded samples were clustered on the cBot Cluster Generation System using the TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After cluster generation and gene abundance analysis, libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform to obtain 150 bp paired-end reads. Differential expression analysis was performed to identify DEGs across pairwise comparisons, with gene expression quantified based on fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). DEGs in KTC-1 cells were selected using a threshold of |log2 fold change (FC)| ≥2.0 and P<0.01. The top five upregulated and down-regulated DEGs in each comparison were ranked using the RobustRankAggreg package in R software. These DEGs were used to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network using the STRING database (version 10.5). Significant hub DEGs within the network were identified using Cytoscape’s cytoHubba plugin, applying maximal clique centrality (MCC) and eleven other algorithms. The RobustRankAggreg core algorithm was further applied to rank hub DEGs, with a frequency ≥12 and a score <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Bioinformatics analysis of mRNA-seq data

Enrichment analyses of hub DEGs were conducted using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways via the DAVID database (version 6.8). The expression profiles and survival relevance of hub DEGs were examined through the GEPIA, UALCAN, and Human Protein Atlas (HPA) databases. Additionally, the relationship between hub DEGs and immune function, as well as immune cell infiltration levels, was analyzed using the Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER) database.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed and processed using GraphPad Prism 9 (San Diego, CA, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For multiple groups, one-way ANOVAs were conducted, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. When comparing numerous groups across various time points, two-way ANOVAs with Bonferroni correction was used. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

BA-induced differential gene expression and pathway enrichment analysis in CAL62 cells

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which BA inhibits ATC cells, CAL62 cells were treated with varying BA concentrations (0, 50, 100, 150 µM). The substantial numbers of DEGs genes were identified in each pairwise comparison, highlighting the top 5 upregulated, and the top 5 down-regulated genes (Figure 1A). Subsequently, a PPI network was constructed for these top 10 DEGs (Figure 1B), and the Robust Rank Aggreg package was used to generate the ranked gene list from these DEGs (Figure 1C). GO analysis revealed the significant enrichment in genes related to vascular endothelial growth factor production, and regulation of cell proliferation. Additionally, these genes were enriched in multiple KEGG pathways, including the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and the amino acid biosynthesis (Figure 1D).

Pathway enrichment and gene set analysis of BA-induced responses in CAL62 cells

GSEA and pathway enrichment revealed the multiple pathways associated with BA-induced inhibition in CAL62 cells. Compared with the control group, BA50 treatment could enhance apical junction, interferon-α, and interferon-γ responses (Figure 2A), while BA100 treatment promoted the interferon-α response and downregulated hypoxia and TNF-α/NF-κB signaling (Figure 2B). BA150 further strengthened the interferon-α response, the apical junction, and the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (Figure 2C). Additionally, BA100 increased the apical surface and spermatogenesis and unfolded the protein response (Figure 2D), while BA150, relative to BA50, inhibited hypoxia, peroxisome, and ultraviolet responses (Figure 2E). BA150 facilitated the unfolded protein response but downregulated the apical surface and K-RAS signaling (Figure 2F). In comparisons between control and BA-treated cells, DEGs were enriched in pathways related to collagen-containing extracellular matrix and extracellular matrix organization. DEGs between BA50 and BA100, as well as BA50 and BA150, were associated with response to extracellular stimuli, while DEGs between BA100 and BA150 were enriched in pathways related to hormone metabolic process regulation (Figure S1A-S1F).

Expression patterns and prognostic implications of key DEGs in TC from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) analysis

Notably, CLU is significantly upregulated in TC and positively correlates with both tumor stage and overall survival (Figure 3A). The TCGA database analysis revealed that IL-6 is downregulated in TC tissues compared with normal tissues (Figure 3B). Conversely, PLAU is an upregulated DEG in TC relative to the normal tissues and associated with the tumor stage (Figure 3C). However, IL-6 and PLAU shows no association with overall survival. CBS, HIST2H2BE, PSAT1, and TNFRSF9 expression levels show no statistically significant difference between TC and normal tissues and are similarly unlinked to tumor stage. These findings suggested that CLU may serve as a key upregulated DEG involved in tumor progression, and survival outcomes in TC patients.

BA inhibits CLU-mediated mitophagy in CAL62 cells

Mitochondrial fluorescence intensity was decreased in the BA + CLU group compared to the control and BA groups (Figure 6A). Additionally, BA-treated cells exhibited significantly reduced mRNA expression of mitophagy markers, including PINK1, PRKN, ATG5, and MFN1, compared to the control and BA + CLU groups. In contrast, CAL62 cells treated with both BA and CLU showed more active mitophagy, with higher mRNA levels of PINK1, PRKN, ATG5, and MFN1 (Figure 6B). These findings indicated that BA could suppress CAL62 cells by inhibiting CLU-mediated mitophagy.

Discussion

BA is natural flavonoid used in treating the ATC due to its ability to inhibit the cancer cell proliferation, induce apoptosis, and reduce metastasis. It targets key processes such as cell cycle regulation, apoptosis pathways, and inhibition of matrix metalloproteinases (14). BA exerts anti-inflammatory effects by modulating the NF-κB pathway and offers antioxidant protection, reducing oxidative stress in cancer cells. Additionally, it can regulate signaling pathways like PI3K/Akt and MAPK, which are critical in cancer cell survival and migration. Some studies suggest that BA may also enhance the effectiveness of chemotherapy, offering a potential adjunctive treatment for ATC (15,16). This study primarily evaluated the antitumor effects of BA on targeting ATC cells, especially CAL62 cells, by modulating key molecular pathways. Notably, BA can lead to substantial changes in gene expression associated with cellular proliferation, mitophagy, and the epithelial-mesenchymal transition, indicating its capacity to alter cellular processes integral to TC progression (17).

The study’s significant observations revealed a reduction in mitochondrial fluorescence intensity. The downregulation of mitophagy markers, such as PINK1, PRKN, ATG5, and MFN1, suggested that BA could effectively inhibit CLU-mediated mitophagy, a crucial defensive mechanism that cancer cells exploit to preserve mitochondrial function and evade apoptosis (18). Interestingly, BA is a potential GLP-1R small-molecule agonist that could enhance mitophagy to maintain mitochondrial quality control. Moreover, BA induced autophagy specifically targeting mitochondria, promoting the translocation of Parkin and TBK1 to these organelles and facilitating mitophagy by phosphorylating TBK1 at Ser172 and ubiquitin at Ser65 (19). This disruption might increase TC cells’ susceptibility to metabolic stress, ultimately leading to heightened cell death and a potential reduction in tumor viability.

Furthermore, BA could induce cellular dedifferentiation, characterized by increased expression of TC markers and decreased expression of poorly differentiated markers. This phenotypic shift may lead to a less aggressive cancer type with reduced metastatic potential, thus improving responsiveness to conventional therapies (20). However, the implications of dedifferentiation on the tumor microenvironment and its long-term effects warrant further investigation (21,22).

The analysis of TCGA data indicated a significant upregulation of CLU in TC, which correlates with tumor progression and patient survival, thereby identifying CLU as a viable target for therapeutic intervention (23,24). CLU typically functions as a stress chaperone rather than a direct transcriptional regulator. CLU is known to activate the class III phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PtdIns3K) activity around damaged mitochondria; inhibition of mitophagic flux results in accumulation of the excessive mitophagosomes, thereby triggering the ROS-dependent apoptosis, as observed during cisplatin treatment in oral cancer cells. In our study, BA effectively inhibited CLU-associated pathways, offering an innovative strategy to impede tumour growth by disrupting essential processes, such as mitophagy and cellular proliferation.

Moreover, these findings open avenues for BA’s integration with existing therapeutic strategies, such as combining with metabolic inhibitors, to potentially amplify their efficacy (25). By also influencing epithelial-mesenchymal transition, BA may help hinder metastasis, thereby addressing a major challenge in TC treatment (26,27). Despite these promising insights, the in vitro nature of this study necessitates further validation through in vivo models to establish the clinical relevance of BA. Intricate interactions within an organism may influence BA’s therapeutic efficacy and safety, emphasizing the need for animal studies and clinical trials. Future studies are needed to conducted for evaluation in different aspects of transcription and molecular events affected the different cancers.

Conclusions

In this study, the impact of BA on CAL62 ATC cells, emphasizing CLU-mediated mitophagy and differentiation, was investigated. Differential gene expression and pathway enrichment were assessed through RNA-seq and GSEA. CLU co-treatment reversed the BA-induced upregulation of dedifferentiation genes. Moreover, BA reduced the mitochondrial fluorescence intensity, and mitophagy marker levels indicated the suppression of CLU-mediated mitophagy. TCGA analysis revealed the CLU upregulation in association with cancer progression and survival. These findings suggested that CLU could be a valuable therapeutic target in TC, with BA offering a promising treatment strategy in clinical therapeutics.
