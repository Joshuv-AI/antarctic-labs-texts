SPMs exert anti-inflammatory and pro-resolving effects through positive allosteric modulation of the prostaglandin EP4 receptor
Authors: Mohamad Wessam Alnouri, Kenneth Anthony Roquid, Rémy Bonnavion, Haaglim Cho, Jan Heering, Jeonghyeon Kwon, Yannick Jäger, ShengPeng Wang, Stefan Günther, Nina Wettschureck, Gerd Geisslinger, Robert Gurke, Christa E. Müller, Ewgenij Proschak, Stefan Offermanns
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceSpecialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs) are poly-unsaturated fatty acid derivatives, which have anti-inflammatory and pro-resolving properties. However, their mechanism of action is still unclear. Here, we report that major synthetic SPMs function as positive allosteric modulators (PAM) of prostaglandin E2 receptor subtype 4 (EP4). Interestingly, this modulation of EP4 in macrophages not only enhances coupling of EP4 to the Gs signaling pathway but additionally endows EP4 with the new ability to couple to Gi-proteins, thereby enhancing the phagocytic activity of macrophages. Our findings identify a central mechanism of action for SPMs and may enable the development of synthetic PAMs of the EP4 receptor as a therapeutic strategy for inflammatory diseases.

The inflammatory response is a complex process, which protects against pathogens and promotes reparative and regenerative processes (1, 2). It involves various cell types and multiple chemical mediators. In particular, neutrophils and macrophages play central coordinating roles, and a variety of lipid and peptide mediators help to orchestrate the inflammatory response (3). Inflammatory processes can also be damaging, necessitating self-limiting and resolving mechanisms. Resolution of inflammation is due to reduced activity of pro-inflammatory cells and mediators, but has also been shown to involve activation of anti-inflammatory and pro-resolving mechanisms, which help to terminate acute inflammation and to initiate tissue repair and regeneration (4–6). Evidence has been provided that specialized pro-resolving lipid mediators (SPMs) can terminate inflammation by exerting anti-inflammatory and pro-resolving activities (7–9). SPMs are a diverse group of lipids formed by the cellular metabolism of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs). Prominent among them are D-series resolvins, protectins, and maresins, which derive from docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), and E-series resolvins, which are metabolites of eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) (10). It is, however, unclear whether SPMs have physiological relevance as they are often undetectable or only found at extremely low levels in biological fluids (11–15). Nonetheless, multiple studies show that DHA-derived protectins, maresins, and D-series resolvins have anti-inflammatory effects in vitro and in vivo, which suggests that their mechanism of action at least represents a pharmacological target in various disease areas (16–19).

Especially D-series resolvins have been proposed to induce their effects through activation of G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) (20, 21), but the identity of these receptors has remained controversial (11). N-formyl peptide receptor 2 (FPR2/ALX), GPR32, and GPR18 have been described as receptors for different D-series resolvins (22–27). However, independent studies failed to confirm this (28–31). Thus, how SPMs exert their effects remains unclear.

Results

D-series Resolvins and Protectins Activate EP4.

In a screen for lipid mediators acting on human umbilical venous endothelial cells (HUVECs), we found that resolvin D5 (RvD5) induced a dose-dependent increase in the intracellular cAMP concentration (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B). Similar to isoproterenol, which increased cAMP levels by activating β-adrenergic receptors, the effect of RvD5 was strongly reduced after siRNA-mediated knock-down of Gαs (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C), which indicated the involvement of a GPCR. However, GPR32, which has been suggested to function as a GPCR for RvD5 (24, 25), was not expressed by HUVECs (SI Appendix, Table S1).

To identify a potential Gs-coupled receptor responding to RvD5, we screened the PRESTO-Tango GPCR library consisting of 314 GPCRs (32) for RvD5-induced increases in the intracellular cAMP concentration and β-arrestin recruitment (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A and Fig. 1A). As shown in Fig. 1B, only the PGE2 receptor EP4 responded in both assay modes. Besides RvD5, also resolvin D4 (RvD4) and resolvin D1 (RvD1) were able to activate human EP4, whereas resolvin D3 (RvD3) and resolvin D2 (RvD2) had only a weak or no effect, respectively (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). The efficacy of the effects of RvD5, RvD4, and RvD1 was indistinguishable from that of PGE2, but PGE2 was much more potent (Fig. 1C). The effect of D-series resolvins was specific for EP4 as neither human nor mouse EP1, EP2, and EP3 did respond to these resolvins (Fig. 1 D–F and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–E).

Also, the protectins protectin D1 (PD1) and protectin DX (PDX) induced increases in cAMP in cells expressing human and mouse EP4 with an efficacy similar to PGE2 and D-series resolvins (Fig. 1 G and H and SI Appendix, Figs. S1 D and S2 F–H), and a potency which was lower than the potency of RvD5, but comparable to that of other active D-series resolvins (Fig. 1 G and H). In contrast, the E-series resolvins resolvin E1 (RvE1) and resolvin E2 (RvE2), the maresins maresin R1 (MaR1) and maresin R2 (MaR2) as well as lipoxin A4 (LXA4) had no effects on human EP4 with regard to the induction of cAMP accumulation (Fig. 1 G and H and SI Appendix, Figs. S1D and S2 G and H). Thus, D-series resolvins and protectins activate EP4 with relatively low potency.

D-Series Resolvins, Protectins, and Maresins Affect Binding of PGE2 to EP4.

To further characterize the interaction of D-series resolvins and protectins with EP4, we performed [3H]PGE2 competition binding assays on EP4 expressed by HEK-293 cells. Nonlabeled PGE2 displaced the radioligand with a Ki value in the lower nanomolar range as shown before (33, 34). RvD5 almost completely displaced PGE2 at a concentration of 10 µM, and the Ki value was calculated at 1.3 µM (Fig. 2 A and B). RvD4 and RvD1 as well as the protectins PD1 and PDX were also able to displace [3H]PGE2 from the EP4 receptor with Ki values in the range of 6.6 to 130 µM (Fig. 2 B–F), whereas RvD2 and the maresins MaR1 and MaR2 were inactive (Fig. 2 G–I). This suggests that RvD1, RvD4, RvD5, and the protectins, at relatively high micromolar concentrations, exert an orthosteric agonism at EP4.

During the radioligand displacement studies, we noticed that RvD1, RvD4, RvD5, and protectins at nanomolar concentrations displayed a small effect on [3H]PGE2 binding, resulting in a biphasic displacement curve (Fig. 2 A and C–F). RvD1 slightly increased binding whereas RvD4, RvD5, and protectins decreased binding. Also, RvD2 and the maresins MaR1 and MaR2, which did not directly compete with PGE2, increased or decreased, respectively, [3H]PGE2 binding at nanomolar concentrations (Fig. 2 G–I). This may indicate an additional allosteric effect which appears to occur with much higher potency. When fitting the binding data by applying the allosteric modulation equation based on the ternary complex model (35), we found Kb values and α-factors for the tested SPMs of 6 to 548 nM and 0.76 to 1.17, respectively (Fig. 2J).

Positive Allosteric Modulation of EP4 by D-Series Resolvins, Protectins, and Maresins.

To further analyze potential allosteric effects of D-series resolvins and protectins at low concentrations, which are not able to induce orthosteric effects, we tested the effect of nanomolar concentrations of D-series resolvins, protectins, and maresins on PGE2-induced β-arrestin recruitment and increases in cAMP concentration in cells expressing EP4. None of the tested SPMs had an effect on PGE2-induced β-arrestin recruitment (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–L). However, nanomolar concentrations of RvD1, RvD2, RvD4, RvD5, the protectins PD1 and PDX as well as of the maresins MaR1 and MaR2 increased the efficacy of PGE2 to stimulate cAMP formation but had no effect on the potency of PGE2 (Fig. 3 A–D and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 M–R). RvD3 and the E-series resolvins RvE1 and RvE2 as well as LXA4 had no effect (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–D), and the phenomenon was not seen when other EP receptors such as EP1, EP2, and EP3 were analyzed (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 E–G). Similar effects were observed when the murine EP4 receptor was tested (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 H–J).

To further validate this effect, we analyzed the effects of SPMs on PGE2-induced GTPγS binding (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A) in membranes of HEK-293 cells expressing the human EP4 receptor. PGE2 had no effect on GTPγS binding in membranes from nontransfected HEK-293 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B) but induced a dose-dependent increase in GTPγS binding in plasma membranes prepared from EP4-expressing cells (Fig. 3E). PGE2-induced GTPγS binding was strongly increased by D-series resolvins, protectins, and maresins at nanomolar concentrations, which by themselves were without effect (Fig. 3 E–G and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 C–F). When membranes from pertussis toxin-treated cells were analyzed, in which Gi-type G-proteins are ADP-ribosylated and are unable to be activated by the receptor, PGE2-induced GTPγS binding in the absence and presence of D-series resolvins, protectins, and maresins was strongly reduced (Fig. 3 E–G and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 C–F). This indicated that the EP4 receptor overexpressed in HEK-293 cells does not only couple to Gs, leading to increases in cAMP, but that PGE2 also induced an EP4-mediated Gi activation, although the net effect of PGE2 is an increase in cAMP formation. This EP4-mediated activation of Gi is also potentiated by D-series resolvins, protectins, and maresins. To analyze the coupling of the overexpressed EP4 receptor to Gi in a more direct manner, we employed the NanoBiT-Gi protein dissociation assay (SI Appendix, Fig. S5G), which allows to determine receptor-mediated activation of Gi (36). As shown in Fig. 3 H–J and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 H–L, D-series resolvins including RvD1, RvD2, RvD4, and RvD5 as well as protectins and maresins increased PGE2-induced EP4-mediated activation of Gi at nanomolar concentrations, an effect which was sensitive to pertussis toxin (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 M and N). We therefore conclude that D-series resolvins, protectins, and maresins at nanomolar concentrations allosterically modulate the EP4 receptor to promote PGE2-induced activation of Gs as well as of Gi-type G-proteins, which results in an increased PGE2-induced cAMP formation.

Since the effect of SPMs on EP4 was not observed with any other EP receptor, we performed computational binding site analysis using the Cryo-EM structure of the EP4 receptor coupled to the Gs complex and PGE2 (37) and superimposed the structures of EP2 (38) and EP3 (39) to identify potential allosteric binding sites specific for EP4. A large cavity next to the interface between the EP4 receptor and the Gs complex was considered as the most promising, because it contained a deeply buried positively charged arginine residue, R117, which could interact with the carboxylate group of the SPMs (Fig. 3K). Furthermore, this cavity has a sufficient amount of hydrophobic contacts, which are required for the accommodation of the lipophilic residue of SPMs (40). Consistent with this, we found that the R117A and R117Q mutants of human EP4 lost the ability to be allosterically modulated by SPMs (Fig. 3L and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A–E). In addition, the R117Q mutant lost the modulation of PGE2 binding by SPMs, whereas binding of PGE2 to this mutant was indistinguishable from its binding to the wild-type (Fig. 3M and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 F and G). Then, we selected residues H258 and E262 of EP2 and W273 and R350 of EP3, which would sterically clash with the proposed allosteric binding site in EP4 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A–D) and replaced the corresponding amino acids in EP4 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A–D) with these residues. The A264W, A266E, M270H, and L332R mutants of EP4 still responded to PGE2 but were not modulated by SPMs any more (Fig. 3 N and O and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 E–J). Binding of PGE2 to the M270H and L332R mutants was unaffected (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 K and L), but modulation of PGE2 binding by SPMs was lost (Fig. 3 P and Q and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 M and N).

GPR32, GPR18, FPR2, GPR37, and LGR6 are not Activated by SPMs.

Since previous reports had indicated that particular D-series resolvins activate the GPCRs GPR32, GPR18, and FPR2 (22, 24–26, 41), whereas GPR37 and LGR6 had been proposed to be activated by PD1 and MaR1, respectively (42, 43), we tested these receptors. The synthetic GPR32 agonist NCGC00135472 (44) had no effect in nontransfected HEK-293 cells (Fig. 4 A and B) but concentration-dependently led to an increase in [Ca2+]i and to β-arrestin recruitment in cells expressing GPR32 (Fig. 4 C and D). However, none of the D-series resolvins reported to activate GPR32, namely RvD1, RvD3, and RvD5 had an effect (Fig. 4 C and D), although tandem mass spectrometry showed that the concentrations of these D-series resolvins in the experimental buffer at the end of the experiment were close to the theoretical value (Fig. 4E). Similarly, the synthetic FPR2/ALX and GPR18 agonists MMK1 (45) and PSB-KD477 (30) dose-dependently induced β-arrestin recruitment in HEK-293 cells expressing the respective receptors (Fig. 4 F and G), but not in nontransfected cells (Fig. 4 H and I). However, neither RvD1 nor RvD2 had an effect (Fig. 4 F and G). Likewise, MMK1 induced an increase in intracellular free calcium concentration in HEK-293 cells expressing FPR2, whereas RvD1 and RvD5 had no effect (Fig. 4 J and K). We were also not able to reproduce the reported agonistic activity of the related SPMs PD1 and MaR1 at GPR37 and LGR6, respectively, (Fig. 4 L–O).

Biased Positive Allosteric Modulation of Macrophage EP4 by D-series Resolvins, Protectins, and Maresins.

We then tested whether the EP4 receptor also mediated effects of DHA-derived SPMs in murine bone marrow–derived macrophages, which express EP4 endogenously (46, 47) and which are targets of anti-inflammatory and pro-resolving effects of SPMs including D-series resolvins, protectins, and maresins (5, 48, 49). When D-series resolvins, protectins, and maresins were given at concentrations of 30 nM, at which they alone were without effect (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A), they increased the PGE2-induced elevation of cAMP levels in macrophages (Fig. 5 A–C and SI Appendix, Fig. S8 B–D). Since macrophages express EP2 and EP4, which both couple to Gs to mediate increases in cAMP concentration (46, 47), we tested whether the modulating effects of SPMs depended on EP4. We therefore crossed mice carrying a floxed allele of Ptger4, the gene encoding EP4, (50) with the Csf1r-Cre mouse line, which preferentially recombines in monocytes and macrophages (51) to generate Csf1r-Cre;Ptger4flox/flox mice (herein referred to as Mϕ-Ptger4-KO). None of the SPMs shown to increase PGE2 effects on cAMP levels in macrophages from wild-type mice showed an effect in EP4-deficient macrophages derived from Mϕ-Ptger4-KO animals (Fig. 5 D–F and SI Appendix, Fig. S8 E–G). PGE2 was still able to induce increases in [cAMP]i in macrophages from Mϕ-Ptger4-KO mice by acting through EP2 receptors as indicated by the sensitivity of this effect to the EP2 receptor antagonist PF04418948 (SI Appendix, Fig. S8H).

To analyze the effect of D-series resolvins, protectins, and maresins on PGE2-induced activation of Gi via EP4, we determined PGE2-induced GTPγS binding in plasma membranes of macrophages. As seen before in EP4 expressing HEK-293 cells, D-series resolvins, protectins, and maresins potentiated PGE2-induced GTPγS binding in membranes of control macrophages (Fig. 5G and SI Appendix, Fig. S8I). This effect was not seen in membranes from pertussis toxin-treated macrophages (Fig. 5H and SI Appendix, Fig. S8J) and from macrophages lacking the EP4 receptor (Mϕ-Ptger4-KO) (Fig. 5I and SI Appendix, Fig. S8K). In membranes of macrophages derived from Csf1r-Cre;Gnasflox/flox mice, which lack Gαs in macrophages (Mϕ-Gnas-KO), PGE2 had no effect on GTPγS binding (Fig. 5J and SI Appendix, Fig. S8L) indicating that in macrophages, the PGE2-activated EP4 and EP2 receptors are exclusively coupled to Gs. In the absence of Gαs, D-series resolvins, protectins, and maresins still increased GTPγS binding when given together with PGE2 but had no effect on GTPγS binding when given alone (Fig. 5J and SI Appendix, Fig. S8L). However, this effect of D-series resolvins, protectins, and maresins was abrogated in membranes from Gαs-deficient macrophages pretreated with pertussis toxin (Fig. 5K and SI Appendix, Fig. S8M). Thus, SPMs are able to positively modulate the EP4 receptor in macrophages at nanomolar concentrations resulting in increased PGE2-induced Gs-mediated cAMP formation. In addition, SPMs allow the PGE2-activated EP4 receptor to couple to Gi, indicating that they act as biased positive allosteric modulators (Fig. 5L).

SPMs Modulate PGE2-Induced EP4 Activation to Increase Its Anti-inflammatory Activity and to Induce Pro-phagocytotic Activity.

We then tested whether SPMs modulated PGE2-induced, EP4-mediated regulation of inflammatory signaling and phagocytotic activity in macrophages. It has been described that activation of macrophages by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) leads to formation and release of TNFα as well as of PGE2 and that PGE2 through activation of its receptor reduces the effect of LPS on TNFα formation (52–55) (Fig. 6A). Consistent with this, we found that LPS induced the formation of PGE2 and that the LPS-induced TNFα formation was increased after loss of the EP4 receptor (Fig. 6 B and C). D-series resolvins, protectins, and maresins in turn reduced LPS-induced TNFα formation (Fig. 6D and SI Appendix, Fig. S9A), an effect not seen anymore in macrophages derived from Mϕ-Ptger4-KO mice (Fig. 6E and SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). The effect of D-series resolvins was also gone after treatment of macrophages with indomethacin, which blocks LPS-induced PGE2 formation (Fig. 6F). Exogenous addition of PGE2 further reduced LPS-induced TNFα formation (Fig. 6G), and D-series resolvins further increased the effect of PGE2 (Fig. 6G). The effects of PGE2 as well as of D-series resolvins were not seen in macrophages from Mϕ-Ptger4-KO mice (Fig. 6H). These data indicate that DHA-derived SPMs promote EP4-mediated anti-inflammatory effects of PGE2.

We also analyzed the effect of D-series resolvins, protectins, and maresins on the phagocytotic activity of macrophages, which is normally inhibited by PGE2 (56, 57). Consistent with this, PGE2 inhibited phagocytosis of control macrophages (Fig. 6I), an effect mediated by EP4 and Gs, since it could not be seen in macrophages lacking EP4 or Gαs (Fig. 6 J and K). Interestingly, when RvD5, PD1, or MaR1, which alone had no effect on the phagocytotic activity of macrophages (Fig. 6I and SI Appendix, Fig. S9 C and G), were given together with PGE2, PGE2 lost its inhibiting effect (Fig. 6I and SI Appendix, Fig. S9 C and G). This effect of SPMs depended on Gi-type G-proteins, as it was gone after pretreatment of cells with pertussis toxin (Fig. 6L and SI Appendix, Fig. S9 D and H). In macrophages from Mϕ-Gnas-KO mice, RvD5, PD1, and MaR1 stimulated phagocytosis when given together with PGE2 (Fig. 6K and SI Appendix, Fig. S9 E and I). Again, this stimulatory effect in Gαs-deficient macrophages was lost after pertussis toxin treatment (Fig. 6M and SI Appendix, Fig. S9 F and J). This is consistent with the known pro-phagocytotic role of Gi-mediated signaling in macrophages (58–61). The ability of SPMs to induce pro-phagocytotic effects in the presence of PGE2 via EP4 and Gi-type G-proteins involved activation of phosphoinositide-3-kinase (PI3-kinase), as it was blocked by the PI3-kinase inhibitor LY294002 (Fig. 6N). Consistent with this, RvD5 when given together with PGE2 stimulated formation of phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-trisphosphate (PIP3) by PI3-kinase whereas PGE2 alone rather inhibited PI3-kinase (Fig. 6O). Thus, D-series resolvins, protectins, and maresins abrogate the anti-phagocytotic effect of PGE2 mediated by the Gs-coupled EP4 receptor by inducing the coupling of EP4 to Gi-type G-proteins, which results in the activation of PI3-kinase (Fig. 6P).

We finally tested whether the EP4-dependent effect of SPMs on the regulation of inflammation can also be seen under in vivo conditions. In the model of zymosan-induced peritonitis, we found that treatment of mice with 10 µg/kg body weight RvD5 resulted in nanomolar plasma concentration (SI Appendix, Table S2) and led to a reduced accumulation of peritoneal neutrophils and macrophages (Fig. 7 A and B), but this effect was not seen in mice lacking the EP4 receptor in myeloid cells (Fig. 7 A and B). Similarly, when we induced sepsis in mice by i.p. injection of Escherichia coli, we found that treatment of animals with RvD5 improved disease severity in control mice, whereas this effect was not seen in myeloid cell-specific EP4-deficient animals (Fig. 7C).

Discussion

In this study, we show that the DHA-derived SPMs, namely D-series resolvins, protectins, and maresins, exert their anti-inflammatory and pro-resolving effects through allosteric modulation of the EP4 receptor. In various in vitro and in vivo models, we show that the effects of these SPMs are not seen anymore in the absence of the EP4 receptor. This positive allosteric effect involved an increase in the efficacy of the orthosteric ligand PGE2, which via EP4 induces activation of Gs and, subsequently, of adenylyl cyclase, the major downstream signaling pathway activated by EP4 (62). The SPMs had no effect on the potency of PGE2 to induce Gs activation and formation of cAMP. This indicates that they primarily modulate the interaction of EP4 with G-proteins but not the affinity of EP4 for PGE2. This is also reflected by the relatively small positive or negative effects of SPMs at nanomolar concentrations on the binding of PGE2 to EP4. Differential effects of allosteric modulators on orthosteric ligand affinity versus signaling efficacy have been shown in various other cases (63–65). Based on computational modeling and mutagenesis studies, we propose an allosteric SPM binding site near the G-protein interface, which is comparable to allosteric sites occupied by other intracellular allosteric agonists or PAMs previously described for parathyroid hormone 1 receptor (66), GLP-1 receptor (67), or the β2 adrenergic receptor (68) (SI Appendix, Fig. S10).

It had been shown in the past that in cells overexpressing EP4, PGE2 can also induce activation of Gi-type G-proteins (69), albeit with lower efficacy and potency than Gs activation (70, 71). This is consistent with our observation showing that EP4 overexpressed in HEK-293 cells couples to Gs and Gi and that DHA-derived SPMs increased the efficacy of PGE2 to induce Gs as well as Gi activation in HEK-293 cells expressing EP4. In contrast to the overexpressed EP4 receptor, we found that the native EP4 receptor expressed in murine macrophages, a primary target for the pro-resolving effects of SPMs, only couples to Gs but not to Gi when activated by PGE2, as had been reported before (72). Interestingly, in macrophages, all tested SPMs induced coupling of EP4 to Gi in the presence of PGE2. Thus, DHA-derived SPMs are biased positive allosteric modulators of the EP4 receptor, which bind at nanomolar concentrations to the allosteric site and enhance coupling of EP4 to the Gs signaling pathway but not to β-arrestin signaling. In addition, they endow the PGE2-activated endogenous EP4 receptor with the new ability to couple to Gi, thereby converting the native Gs-coupled EP4 receptor into a receptor, which activates both Gs- and Gi-type G-proteins (Fig. 5L). While a functional bias toward downstream signaling pathways is often seen when studying orthosteric ligands, there are only a few examples of synthetic allosteric ligands, which bias the signaling of the receptors in response to its orthosteric ligand either between G-protein and β-arrestin or between G-protein subtypes (73–75). The biased allosteric regulation of EP4 by SPMs is a rare example of an endogenous biased allosteric modulation, which leads to coupling of the GPCR to an additional G-protein subtype. This underscores the concept that GPCRs are allosteric microprocessors, which can be modulated in multiple ways to induce a large diversity of functional effects (75–77).

The EP4 receptor is known to mediate anti-inflammatory and pro-resolving effects (78–82). However, increasing phagocytotic activity of myeloid cells, a hallmark mechanism of the resolution of inflammation by SPMs (83), is rather inhibited by PGE2 acting through the EP4 receptor, an effect mediated by Gs activation and subsequent formation of cAMP (56, 57, 84). Consistent with the known stimulatory regulation of phagocytosis via Gi (58–61) and PI3-kinase (85–87), we found that SPMs were not only able to bias signaling of EP4 in response to PGE2 toward Gi but to also lead to stimulation of phagocytotic activity in response to PGE2 in a PI3-kinase-dependent manner. Thus, DHA-derived SPMs convert the native EP4 receptor on macrophages from a solely Gs-coupled receptor to a receptor, which activates both Gs- and Gi-mediated signaling pathways in response to PGE2. This enhances classic Gs/cAMP-mediated anti-inflammatory signaling of PGE2-activated EP4 and in addition endows EP4 with the additional ability to couple to Gi-mediated signaling and to exert pro-phagocytotic activity (Fig. 6P).

In particular for D-resolvins, several receptors have previously been proposed, including FPR2 for RvD1 (22), GPR18 for RvD2 (26), and GPR32 for RvD1, RvD3, and RvD5 (22, 24, 25, 41). However, there is an existing controversy regarding the role of these receptors in mediating resolvin effects (11), and various reports have not been able to support that D-resolvins act on FPR2 (31), GPR18 (30), or GPR32 (28, 29). Similarly, we were also not able to see any agonistic activity of D-series resolvins on any of the 3 receptors, although unrelated control agonists were able to specifically activate the receptors, and we could not confirm agonism of PD1 on GPR37 (43) and of maresin 1 on LGR6 (42).

There has also been quite some controversy regarding the physiological role of SPMs. When the formation of SPMs by various human leukocyte populations is determined in vitro, the dihydroxylated RvD5, protectins, and maresins are detectable at very low levels, whereas the trihydroxylated D-series resolvins RvD1, RvD2, and RvD3 are often undetectable or only found at very low levels (88–93). In addition, an increasing number of studies show that SPMs are undetectable or only found at extremely low levels in biological fluids (11–15, 94–96). This has led to concerns that SPMs do not reach concentrations in vivo that are consistent with a physiological or pathophysiological function. Exogenously given, SPMs have, however, multiple effects (5, 7, 16, 19), which indicates that they can have pharmacological relevance. However, the development of drugs based on SPM-mediated resolution of inflammation has been hampered because it has been unclear how SPMs exert their effects. Allosteric modulation of GPCRs has only recently been recognized as a common way of physiological and pharmacological GPCR regulation, and the spectrum of allosteric regulatory mechanisms and their pharmacological potential are probably not yet fully explored (75–77). The biased positive modulation of EP4 function by SPMs is another example of a complex allosteric GPCR modulation. Mimicking this form of EP4 receptor modulation by synthetic ligands may be an approach to mitigate inflammation.

Materials and Methods

We used HUVEC, HEK293T, and HTLA cell lines and murine primary macrophages. Intracellular levels of cAMP were determined either by HTRF assay (Cisbio, 62AM4PEC) or GloSensorTM assay (Promega, E2301). The β-arrestin and NanoBit assays have been described previously (32, 36). For the calcium aequorin assay, we followed the same protocol as previously described (97). PGE2 and GTPγS binding were determined using [3H]PGE2 or [35S]GTPγS, respectively. For the TNF-α, PIP3, and phagocytosis assays, we used commercially available assays. The levels of RvD1, RvD2, and RvD5 were determined with liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis. All mice used in the in vivo experiments were backcrossed onto a C57BL/6 N background, and experiments were performed with littermates as controls. A brief materials and methods summary follows with detailed descriptions of assay conditions, specific reagents, instrumentation, and data analysis procedures has been included in supplement SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.
