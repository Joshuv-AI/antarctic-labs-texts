DELETION INVOLVING EXON 18 OF RPGRIP1 IS a MAJOR CAUSE OF ACHROMATOPSIA
Authors: Taiga Inooka, Kei Mizobuchi, Takaaki Hayashi, Akiko Suga, Kazushige Tsunoda, Kazuki Kuniyoshi, Hiroyuki Kondo, Junya Ota, Taro Kominami, Kazutoshi Yoshitake, Naoko Minematsu, Takeshi Iwata, Mineo Kondo, Koji M. Nishiguchi, Shinji Ueno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.Variants of homozygous deletion involving exon 18 of RPGRIP1 are unique hotspots highly prevalent among Japanese patients with achromatopsia, exhibiting clinical findings consistent with those in patients with achromatopsia from other causative genes.

Achromatopsia (ACHM) is a rare congenital condition characterized by the impairment of visual function mediated by cone photoreceptors, despite the almost normal functioning of rod photoreceptors, typically resulting in reduced visual acuity, photophobia, color blindness, and nystagmus.1 Variants in six genes (CNGA3, CNGB3, GNAT2, PDE6C, PDE6H, and ATF6) are linked with this inherited autosomal-recessive manner inherited disease.1–3 In a previous study, we reported that genetic variants causing ACHM in Japan exhibit differences compared with those identified in Western countries; notably, our research conducted in Japan4 indicated a contrasting pattern of genetic variant prevalence compared with Western populations,5,6 with PDE6C variants being more common and CNGB3 variants being less common. Furthermore, the proportion of genetically proven cases in whole-exome sequencing (WES) in our previous study of ACHM in Japanese patients was only 55%, suggesting the possible role of other major variants in the alleles of each gene or other genes in a manner that cannot be identified using ordinary WES.4

Recently, we identified a homozygous deletion involving exon 18 of RPGRIP1 (NC_000014.9: g.21326547_21327885del, NM_020366.4: c.2710+374_2895+78del; RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL) using whole-genome sequencing (WGS) in a Japanese inherited retinal disease (IRD) cohort7; however, we have not yet examined the prevalence of these variants or phenotype of these patients in detail. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the prevalence of Japanese patients with ACHM associated with variants of RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL, which were not detected using WES, and to confirm that these phenotypes were consistent with ACHM.

Materials and Methods

Participants and Clinical Examination

We recruited cases from five institutions with ophthalmologists specializing in IRD throughout Japan and enrolled patients diagnosed with ACHM at each institution, as previously reported.4 The study was conducted in adherence to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki developed by the World Medical Association and approved by the Ethical Review Committee of each institution. All included patients provided informed consent, and all patient data were anonymized before analysis.

Details of clinical examination, including inclusion and exclusion criteria, were previously reported.4 In brief, the medical records of patients with ACHM diagnosed by specialists in IRDs (T.H., K.T., K.M., K.K., H.K., and S.U.) based on electroretinogram (ERG) results (a representative case of patient with variants of RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL is shown in Supplemental Digital Content 1, Figure , http://links.lww.com/IAE/C808) and clinical symptoms (poor visual acuity from childhood, color vision abnormality, and nystagmus)8 were retrospectively reviewed. Age at diagnosis was confirmed based on the information provided in the medical records. Myopia was defined as having a spherical equivalent refractive error (SER = spherical refractive error + 1/2 cylinder) of −0.50 D or lower, hyperopia was defined as having an SER of +0.50 D or higher, and emmetropia was defined as an SER greater than −0.50 D but less than +0.50 D.

Fundus appearance was graded as within normal level, retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) change (abnormality without apparent atrophy), or retina/RPE atrophy (well-demarcated atrophic lesions with choroidal transmission). The fundus autofluorescence (FAF) patterns were classified as within normal level, reduced autofluorescence (AF) with subtle hyper-AF, foveal/parafoveal hyperautofluorescent, or a central hypoautofluorescent area surrounded by a hyperautofluorescent ring. Representative cases are shown in Supplemental Digital Content 2, Figure, http://links.lww.com/IAE/C808.

All patients underwent spectral domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) at their respective institutions. Foveal structures observed in the SD-OCT images were categorized based on the criteria established by Sundaram et al9,10: Grade 1, a continuous ellipsoid zone (EZ) band at the fovea; Grade 2, a focal interruption of the EZ; Grade 3, absence of the EZ at the fovea; Grade 4, a discrete hyporeflective zone within the outer retina; and Grade 5, outer-retinal atrophy extending to or including RPE loss. Representative cases are also shown in Supplemental Digital Content 2, Figure, http://links.lww.com/IAE/C808. Central retinal thickness (CRT) was automatically measured using an equipped analyzer in cases imaged with Cirrus HD-OCT (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA) or RS-3000 advance (Nidek, Gamagori, Japan), and manually in cases imaged with SPECTRALIS OCT (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). In manual measurements, CRT was measured by setting the display ratio to 1:1 µm on the vertical line scan passing through the center of the foveal pit. Measurements were taken at the fovea from the border of the inner limiting membrane and the outer of the retinal pigment epithelium. For cases in which a scan through the center of the foveal pit was not performed because of nystagmus, CRT was defined as the smallest distance between the border of the inner limiting membrane and the outer of the retinal pigment epithelium.

Genetic Analyses

Peripheral venous blood leukocytes were used to extract DNA and genetic diagnosis was performed as previously described.11 Variants in the following causative genes were identified using WES: CNGA3, CNGB3, GNAT2, and PDE6C. For cases with no variants in the 281 causative genes reported in RetNet, the genome of patients Nagoya0051, Nagoya1051, Nagoya58, JU0147, JU0308, JU0960, KA-143, KA-199, KA-472, and Kinki 399 to 480 was sequenced using the same pipeline as part of the Japan Leading Project for Rare Disease WGS. Details of genome sequencing, mapping and variant calls, annotation, and detection of structural variants were previously reported.7 For patients Nagoya54, Nagoya66, Nagoya320, Nagoya0330, Nagoya1330, Nagoya791, and JU0161, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed to confirm the presence of the RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL variant. A pair of primers was designed to screen for the RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL variant; forward: 5′-GAG​CCC​GAG​TGC​CTT​TAC​TG-3′; reverse: 5′-CCA​GCT​TCA​ATG​GGA​ACC​TC-3′.12 The presence of RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL variant was confirmed by the differences in the size of the PCR product amplified by Prime Star HS DNA polymerase (TAKARA, Japan) using electrophoresis (1.5% agarose gel). A detailed schematic explanation to confirm RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL was provided in our previous report7 and representative PCR band patterns are shown in Supplemental Digital Content 3, Figure, http://links.lww.com/IAE/C808.

Statistical Analyses

Finger counting is assumed to be 1.85 logarithm of the minimal angle of resolution (logMAR), as previously detailed.13 For patients with multiple follow-up visits, generalized linear models were applied to the longitudinal data. Baseline values were used as an offset, whereas follow-up duration or final visit values served as independent variables and the right or left eye was treated as a random effect. Baseline comparisons of SER and best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and the longitudinal BCVA analyses were also performed using eye-level linear mixed-effects models. Categorical variables were compared using the Fisher exact test and nonparametric continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis or Steel–Dwass test. P-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using statsmodels version 0.12.2 based on Python version 3.6.7 (https://www.python.org/downloads/release/python-367/).

Results

Baseline Demographics

We enrolled 52 patients from 47 families, including five sibling pairs. Baseline demographic characteristics were as previously reported4 (see Supplemental Digital Content 1, Table, http://links.lww.com/IAE/C808). In brief, the mean BCVA at the first visit was approximately 20/160 (mean ± SD [SD], 0.92 ± 0.36 logMAR; range, 0.0 to 1.85) for the right eye and approximately 20/200 (0.95 ± 0.33 logMAR; range, 0.046–1.85) for the left eye.

Genotype

Flow diagram for identification of genes is shown in Figure 1 and the distribution of genes with disease-causing variants in patients with ACHM is shown in Figure 2. As in our previous report,4 WES was performed for all 52 patients from 47 families. Causative homozygous or compound heterozygous variants for ACHM were identified in 28 patients from 26 families (55.3% of 47 families; 95% CI 41.2–68.6) and suspected causative heterozygous variants were identified in three patients from two families (4.3% of 47 families; 95% CI 1.2–14.2) using WES.4 We could not detect plausible genetic variants in 21 patients from 19 families (40.4% of 47 families; 95% CI 27.6–54.7) using WES. WGS was conducted for 10 of these patients from nine families. In seven patients from six families, variants of RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL were identified using WGS: patients Nagoya0051, Nagoya1051, Nagoya58, JU0960, KA-199, and KA-472 were homozygous for RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL and KA-143 was compound heterozygous for RPGRIP1 (variants of RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL and c.3339 + 3361_3748 + 362dup in RPGRIP1). Variants of CNGA3 were also identified in two patients from one family using WGS; KA-177 and KA-178 were compound heterozygous (variants of c.1126G > A and c.864_865insN[356] in CNGA3). Details of the genomic notations and genotypes were provided in previous reports.4,7 No variants were identified using WGS for three patients from three families (JU0147, JU0308, and Kinki 399–480). For nine patients from eight families, for whom no causative variants were identified using WES and WGS was not performed, genomic PCR was performed to confirm the existence of RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL variants. Homozygous variants of RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL were identified in four patients from four families (Nagoya54, Nagoya66, JU1014, and JU1191) and heterozygous variant of RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL was identified in one patient (Nagoya791) using PCR. For two patients from two families (Kinki 100–1247 and R66), neither WGS nor PCR was performed because the blood samples were insufficient for testing.

In summary, variants were observed in four causative genes for ACHM: PDE6C (15 patients from 13 families, 27.7% of 47 families), CNGA3 (12 patients from 11 families, 23.4%), CNGB3 (two patients from two families, 4.3%), and GNAT2 (two patients from two families, 4.3%). Furthermore, the RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL variant was identified in 12 patients from 11 families (23.4%) (Fig. 1). Overall, genetic etiologies were identified in 43 patients from 39 families (83.0% of 47 families), whereas nine patients from eight families remained unresolved (17.0%) despite additional WGS and/or PCR.

Genotype and Phenotype

To identify correlations between genotypes and phenotypes, clinical symptom differences were analyzed for each causative gene group. Table 1 summarizes the main clinical symptoms associated with the causative genes. Statistical analyses were conducted on three groups with adequate patient numbers: CNGA3, PDE6C, and RPGRIP1. No significant differences were observed among the groups for age at diagnosis, SER, BCVA, fundus appearance, OCT grade, FAF pattern, or refractive error classification (myopic/emmetropic/hyperopic). In brief, the proportion of patients showing fundus appearance of the retina or RPE atrophy was similar across genotypes—CNGA3 (3/12, 25.0%), PDE6C (4/15, 26.7%), and RPGRIP1 (3/12, 25.0%)—with no significant between-group difference (P = 0.36). The FAF patterns were heterogeneous: normal FAF was relatively more frequent in RPGRIP1 (5/12, 41.7%) and less frequent in PDE6C (1/15, 6.7%), whereas a central hypoautofluorescent area surrounded by a hyperautofluorescent ring was observed more often in CNGA3 (3/10, 30.0%, two nongradable) and PDE6C (6/15, 40.0%) and less often in RPGRIP1 (2/12, 16.7%), with no significant between-group difference (P = 0.28). The OCT grades, although not significantly different overall (P = 0.07), showed a nonsignificant trend with RPGRIP1, most often Grade 2 (focal EZ interruption; 8/12, 66.7%), whereas PDE6C included relatively more advanced grades (e.g., Grade 5 with outer retinal atrophy with RPE loss; 4/15, 26.7%).

Longitudinal Findings of Best-Corrected Visual Acuity

For 45 patients who visited more than once, the mean follow-up period ± SD was 12.0 ± 7.6 years, ranging from 1 to 37 years. During this time, no significant change in BCVA was observed (P = 0.15). The mean annual change in BCVA between the first and final visits was approximately −0.45 Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) letters/year in the right eye and approximately −0.05 ETDRS letters/year in the left eye (mean ± SD, 0.009 ± 0.045 and 0.001 ± 0.032 logMAR/year, respectively), and no statistically significant differences were observed among the groups in the change in BCVA (P = 0.15). Characteristics of Patients with ACHM Harboring RPGRIP1 Variants

Table 2 summarizes the clinical findings for 12 patients with RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL. Notably, Nagoya0051 had particularly good visual acuity, which could be due to residual cone cells in the central fovea, as reported previously.14 We obtained clinical data for five patients who were followed up for more than 10 years (range: 12–13 years). No statistically significant change in BCVA was noted over the follow-up period (P = 0.068); the mean Snellen acuity at the first visit was approximately 20/125 (mean ± SD, 0.80 ± 0.32 logMAR) in the right eye and approximately 20/125 (0.79 ± 0.30 logMAR) in the left eye, and at the final visit was approximately 20/160 (0.92 ± 0.41 logMAR) in the right eye and approximately 20/200 (1.00 ± 0.43 logMAR) in the left eye. In the OCT imaging, no change in the EZ grading was observed for any of these patients over time. Moreover, no statistically significant change in CRT was noted over the follow-up period, with a mean ± SD at the first visit of 145.6 ± 29.6 µm for the right eye and 143.2 ± 33.2 µm for the left eye and of 135.4 ± 43.7 µm for the right eye and 138.6 ± 49.7 µm for the left eye (P = 0.317) at the final visit.

Discussion

We previously reported RPGRIP1, especially the variants of RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL, as a causative gene for ACHM.7 In this study, we expanded the ACHM cohort and confirmed the RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL variant using WGS or PCR in 12 patients from 11 families who were not genetically resolved using conventional WES analyses. This is the first cohort study to focus on the prevalence and longitudinal clinical course of patients with ACHM harboring variants of RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL. Our results show a high prevalence (approximately 23%) of RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL variants in Japanese patients with ACHM, indicating that RPGRIP1 is one of the three major causative genes besides PDE6C and CNGA3.

The clinical presentation of patients with this variant had typical phenotypes of ACHM, including nystagmus, photophobia, and color blindness. Considering the lack of statistically significant differences in phenotypes, including visual acuity and OCT grade, in baseline comparisons with patients having other ACHM-associated variants (PDE6C and CNGA3), we believe that RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL is a major etiological cause of ACHM in Japan.

Previous reports have shown that the causative variant is detected using WES in at least 70% of patients with clinically diagnosed ACHM in European countries including the United Kingdom,1 Germany,5 and the Netherlands.1,5,15 On the contrary, in our previous study on clinically diagnosed ACHM cases in Japan, only 55% of the cases could be genetically proven using WES.4 With the addition of ACHM cases associated with RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL variants in this study, the proportion of genetically proven cases increased to 83.0%. One of the reasons for the high prevalence of RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL variants in Japanese patients with ACHM is the relatively high variant frequency of RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL in the Japanese population (MAF = 0.000058 in 60 KJPN; https://jmorp.megabank.tohoku.ac.jp/genome-variations/sr-snvindel/070a2b569b99f9bab; accessed on January 12, 2025). This variant has not been reported in Europe or other foreign countries (in gnomAD; https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/variant/14–21326547-T-TAGGTGCAGTGGCTCACGCCTGTAATCCCAGCACTTTGGGAGGCCATGGCAGGCGAA?dataset=gnomad_r3; accessed on January 12, 2025), and it is possible that the RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL variant is a unique ACHM hotspot in Japanese patients. It would be interesting to determine whether this variant is found in other East Asian countries, such as China16 or Korea,17 which have similar racial backgrounds.

RPGRIP1 codes for a coiled-coil protein crucial for anchoring RPGR to the photoreceptor primary cilia.18 The initial identification of a disease-causing variant in RPGRIP1 was in patients with Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA),19 characterized by severe and early visual loss, an apparently normal or salt-and-pepper pigmented fundus, and nondetectable scotopic and photopic ERGs. Nevertheless, RPGRIP1 variants have also been identified in IRD cases that were clinically characterized as cone-rod dystrophy (CORD)20,21 or pigmented paravenous chorioretinal atrophy.22,23 We found three reports of RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL in our search,12,24,25 and all cases involved Japanese patients. Among these reports, only two cases of siblings diagnosed with LCA were homozygous for RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL.12 These siblings exhibited a flattened 30 Hz flicker ERG; however, unlike typical LCA cases, the waveform for the scotopic flash ERG was notable. Based on the molecular genetic data, the two siblings were diagnosed with LCA. However, their cases did not display the typical LCA symptoms, such as the oculo-digital sign, vessel attenuation, retinal pigment epithelium mottling from the mid-retina to the periphery, or an undetectable ERG. We believe their cases are phenotypically more consistent with ACHM. Patients with compound heterozygous for RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL also showed a variety of clinical phenotypes. Two cases with compound heterozygous for RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL and c.1467+1G>T22 or g.21276147_21280265del (ex1-del)23 were reported to have undetectable photopic ERG and subnormal scotopic ERG. In addition, one case showed normal fundus,22 whereas no description of the fundus was provided for the other.23 These findings indicate that these patients had clinical conditions similar to those of ACHM. By contrast, the other patients harboring compound heterozygous RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL showed clinical findings of LCA or early onset retinitis pigmentosa.25 The clinical findings of the compound heterozygous variant of RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL would depend on other variants in RPGRIP1. KA-143 (compound heterozygous for RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL and c.3339 + 3361_3748 + 362dup) and Nagoya791 (heterozygous RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL, as identified through genomic PCR) exhibited a typical ACHM phenotype with comparatively preserved retinal structure, indicating the involvement of RPGRIP1 alterations that are difficult to detect with ordinal WES. In Nagoya791, this pattern further suggested the presence of a second RPGRIP1 variant, such as an excessively large deletions or insertions, or intronic variant, which escaped detection by WES. Further molecular studies are also required to determine why RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL causes complete cone dysfunction.

In this study, patients with RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL exhibited an ACHM phenotype, with a variety of grade structures on OCT, ranging from disruptive EZ to outer retinal atrophy and retinal pigment epithelium loss. Change of visual acuity was consistent and differences compared with patients with CNGA3 or PDE6C were not detect. Among cases with OCT follow-up, the overall grade generally remained unchanged; however, in a phenotype of patients with RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL, we suspected slight progression of EZ disruption. These features may represent similarity of the phenotypes of patients with RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL and those with PDE6C, which can be accompanied by progression, as previously reported,2,4 and is also known as one of the causative genes in CORD.

This study had certain limitations. First, this was a retrospective study and may not have considered patients who dropped out of hospital visits because of stability of visual function. Second, this was a multicenter study and the conditions under which the clinical examinations were performed were not identical. Third, although visual function, including visual acuity and visual field, is important, sufficient visual field data could not be collected. Furthermore, this was a follow-up cohort study over a period of approximately 10 years, and the patients were difficult to distinguish clinically from those with CORD; therefore, further long-term changes remain unknown. In addition, because we did not evaluate rod function over time, it will be necessary to evaluate whether rod functions are normal over time using ERG or a new-generation adaptive optics scanning laser ophthalmoscope.26,27

In conclusion, variants in RPGRIP1-ex18-DEL are unique hotspots with a high prevalence (approximately 23%) among Japanese patients with ACHM. Moreover, the clinical findings for these patients are consistent with ACHM associated with other causative genes.
