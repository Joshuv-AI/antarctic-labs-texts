A Framework of Institutional Obligations for Pragmatic Clinical Trials
Authors: Stephanie R Morain, Matthew W Semler, Jonathan D Casey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPragmatic clinical trials (PCTs) embed research into clinical care. This deliberate research-care integration means that healthcare institutions are key partners for PCTs. Prior literature has identified several ethical challenges in PCTs for which institutional involvement is a necessary condition for their resolution. However, no comprehensive account exists to describe the moral obligations of healthcare institutions for PCTs. To that end, we propose a framework of ten obligations for institutions when leading or participating in PCTs. These obligations span the lifecycle of research, beginning with decisions about whether to implement a given study within a particular institution through to decisions about (de)implementation following study completion. For each obligation, we describe both the relevant moral considerations and the corresponding practical implications. While we offer several practical suggestions that can inform current research activities, future work will be needed to further specify these obligations to provide more granular guidance for decision-making.Keywords: Research ethics, pragmatic clinical trials, comparative effectiveness research

INTRODUCTION

Pragmatic clinical trials (PCTs) offer the potential to improve the efficiency and relevance of evidence generation. Unlike explanatory trials, which traditionally involve highly restrictive eligibility criteria and use separate data and personnel systems than those used for clinical care, PCTs aim to represent “real-world” patients and settings and are conducted using clinical data and clinical personnel (Table 1). The deliberate embedding of research into ongoing clinical care characteristic of PCTs means that healthcare institutions, such as hospitals, clinics, and other healthcare settings, are more fully partners in embedded research than they are in explanatory trials (Morain and Largent 2023). While this partnership with healthcare systems is important for ensuring the successful conduct of PCTs (Weinfurt et al. 2017), it also creates moral obligations. Institutions act as gatekeepers for PCTs, shaping investigators' access to critical resources needed to conduct research—including patient-subjects and clinicians (whom themselves may be research subjects) (Whicher et al. 2015). This granting of access brings with it corresponding duties, for example, like ensuring appropriate data protections when permitting the use of clinical data for research activities. Consequently, institutions are important parties when assessing what we owe to each other in the conduct of embedded research, and for ensuring that those obligations are fulfilled.

While prior literature has identified several ethical challenges in PCTs for which institutional involvement is a necessary condition for their ethical resolution (Morain, Bollinger, et al. 2022; Morain, Mathews, Geller, et al. 2021; Morain, Bollinger, et al. 2024), no comprehensive account has been provided of the moral obligations of healthcare institutions for PCTs, and how they should inform the conduct of these trials. To that end, we propose a framework of ten obligations for healthcare institutions when leading or participating in PCTs (Table 2). These obligations are intended to supplement rather than replace general ethical requirements for clinical research, including that it have social value, be conducted in a scientifically valid way, and demonstrate respect for potential and enrolled subjects, among other requirements (Emanuel et al. 2000). In presenting these obligations, our focus is on PCTs conducted within hospitals and integrated delivery systems. However, the analysis may also inform decision-making in other settings, including community health centers, nursing homes, and extended care facilities. The framework is intended to support a range of stakeholders, including clinical and operational leaders, investigators, and institutional review boards.

Before describing these obligations, four preliminaries are in order. First, healthcare institutions have an obligation to provide the best possible care to their patients. Delivering the best possible care requires high-quality evidence. However, patients, clinicians, health system leaders, and other stakeholders often lack high-quality evidence to inform which treatments produce the best outcomes for patients—even for common conditions and high-stakes decisions. Pragmatic clinical trials (PCTs) are an important means of generating high-quality evidence to inform treatment decisions. Therefore, PCTs are, all things equal, a morally good activity in which healthcare institutions should participate. Second, the obligations set forth in this framework are not held solely by institutions, but instead generally will be held jointly with other stakeholders, including investigators. Third, we recognize that our account may presuppose resources that may not currently exist in many healthcare institutions. Historically, systems of research and clinical care have functioned in different silos from one another, even within the same institutions, with different staff, different financial incentives, and different systems of oversight. Therefore, as research and care are deliberately integrated, institutions may need to develop new systems or processes to support fulfillment of these obligations. Fourth, there is not a clear dichotomy between explanatory and pragmatic trials, and a given trial may be more pragmatic in some dimensions while less pragmatic in others (Loudon et al. 2015). Correspondingly, the strength and scope of these obligations may vary based upon specific features of a given PCT.

THE FRAMEWORK OF INSTITUTIONAL OBLIGATIONS FOR PCTS

The following framework presents the obligations of healthcare systems when participating in or leading PCTs. These obligations are intended as moral guidelines for institutional organization and conduct, rather than as absolute rules without exception. They are presented to correspond (roughly) with the lifecycle of a research project, from decision-making about whether and how to implement a given study within a particular institution, to obligations related to post-study completion. For each obligation, we describe both the relevant moral considerations that support the obligation, and the corresponding practical implications. Future work will be needed to further specify these obligations to provide more granular guidance for decision-making.

ASSESS AND PRIORITIZE PCTS IN COMPARISON TO OTHER INSTITUTIONAL PRIORITIES

Ethical clinical research requires the responsible use of scarce resources. Studies that enroll patients but fail to complete enrollment expose individuals to unjustified risks and burdens. Because PCTs are embedded within routine clinical care, prioritization decisions about whether, how, and when to initiate a given PCT must be evaluated within a broader set of institutional goals and activities than those that would generally be required for explanatory research (Morain, Semler, et al. 2025). A characteristic feature of PCTs is that they often rely on clinical personnel and infrastructure rather than separate research systems. Consequently, they often present additional burdens on clinical and operational systems, particularly information technology (IT) systems (Haff and Choudhry 2018; Platt et al. 2021; Richesson et al. 2021). Relatedly, their embedded nature requires a high degree of collaboration with—and buy-in from—clinical and operational partners (Johnson et al. 2014; Larson et al. 2016; Weinfurt et al. 2017; Tuzzio et al. 2019). In some cases, participating in a PCT may require an institution to forgo or delay quality improvement activities or practice changes that could disrupt the ability to measure an intervention's effect, such as when a cluster-randomized trial of strategies to reduce healthcare-acquired infections requires participating sites to refrain from implementing new quality improvement initiatives that would change current bathing practices (Huang et al. 2019)—a requirement that goes well beyond the range of impacts on usual care generally seen within explanatory trials. Further, a key motivation for PCTs is that embedding research into real-world systems may accelerate the adoption of evidence from PCTs into clinical practice (Palazzo et al. 2022). Realizing this goal, however, often requires intensive engagement with operational leadership. Yet traditional approaches to institutional research oversight, including IRBs, scientific review committees, and required sign-off by departmental leadership, typically assess studies on a case-by-case basis, and often without consideration of the broader set of institution-level resources required for the conduct of many PCTs, or the downstream implications for de-implementing or sustaining tested interventions following study completion.

The embedded nature and purposes of PCTs therefore requires institution-level consideration of a given PCT at two stages: (1) the impact of the research on ongoing clinical and operational activities, and (2) whether and how the research outcomes should be incorporated into future clinical and operational systems.

Elsewhere, we have proposed that institutions assess the appropriateness of a given PCT through three lines of inquiry (Morain, Semler, et al. 2025). First, should the study be conducted? What is the clinical or operational importance of the targeted condition, and is the research question framed to address an issue of operational or clinical uncertainty? Second, can this institution do the study? Can the study be integrated into existing workflows, and at reasonable resource allocation? Third, is the institution prepared to act on the results? Is there willingness to sustain, implement, or de-implement tested interventions, consistent with study results?

Admittedly, issues of prioritization cannot be solved solely at the level of institutions, and must ultimately involve coordination of other actors, including research sponsors and payers. Nevertheless, institutions can and should prioritize their resources. Further, this prioritization process should be transparent to investigators, funders, and other stakeholders—including the criteria by which studies will be evaluated for prioritization. Formal and transparent prioritization processes will be most appropriate for research-intensive institutions that conduct multiple, large-scale PCTs, and may not be necessary for smaller institutions that engage in PCTs only intermittently.

FACILITATE AND SUPPORT THE IDENTIFICATION & ENGAGEMENT OF RELEVANT RESEARCH PARTNERS

Engagement in research refers to the “meaningful involvement of patients, caregivers, clinicians, and other health care stakeholders throughout the research process—from topic selection through design and conduct of research to dissemination of results.” (Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute 2024) Some influential ethics bodies (including the World Medical Association) have argued that engagement is a fundamental ethical principle for all research with human participants (World Medical Association 2024). Our emphasis here is on the distinctive considerations for institutions and PCTs related to this engagement.

Stakeholder engagement in PCTs (like in research involving human participants more generally) is supported by (at least) two types of rationales: one practical and one normative. From a practical perspective, stakeholder engagement in research can minimize potential barriers to study completion, improve the relevance of research, and accelerate its adoption into practice (Forsythe et al. 2016). (Note the latter two outcomes—improving relevance and accelerating adoption—are themselves central motivations for PCTs (Concannon et al. 2012)). Stakeholder engagement might therefore advance social value—a foundational ethical requirement of clinical research (Emanuel et al. 2000). From a normative perspective, engaging patients and other relevant stakeholders in research design and execution can demonstrate respect and promote accountability and transparency. Engagement arguably has particular importance for PCTs involving waivers or alterations of consent, serving to help protect and promote the welfare interests of those enrolled, and offering insight into how the proposed intervention(s) could affect participants' rights and well-being (Morain et al. 2022; Largent et al. 2025).

Prior literature has explored how engagement can respect patients enrolled in research (Domecq et al. 2014; Morain et al. 2022). However, the design of many PCTs means that respect-based justifications may also be owed to clinicians, who are themselves research subjects in many PCTs (and, in some cases, may not have been given an opportunity to make a voluntary decision about participation). Additional justifications for engaging clinicians in PCTs include minimizing burdens to clinical and operational activities, as clinicians have distinct knowledge about how a research activity may influence their ability to fulfill their professional obligations. Furthermore, engaging clinicians about a PCT and its rationale promotes transparency, which can build trust between clinicians and the study team (Rose and Morley 2023).

Existing literature on stakeholder engagement in research, including for PCTs, generally presents investigators as having the primary—if not sole—responsibility for engaging stakeholders in research (Concannon et al. 2014). However, the embedded nature of research activities in PCTs means that appropriate engagement will often require institutional coordination for both the identification of and access to relevant stakeholder groups. Institutional coordination may also improve the efficiency and quality of engagement, as many investigators lack the training and experience to effectively engage diverse stakeholders. In response, several institutions have established community engagement studios, in which investigators can work with dedicated teams of faculty and staff with experience in patient and community engagement to recruit stakeholders and facilitate engagement (Joosten et al. 2015;Coulter et al. 2019). This centralized approach avoids the need for investigators to create de novo processes “on the fly” for each project while supporting the development of best practices for effective engagement (Joosten et al. 2018).

ENSURE TREATMENTS UNDER EVALUATION ARE CONSISTENT WITH USUAL CARE PRACTICES WITHIN THE INSTITUTION OR OTHERWISE REFLECT HIGH-QUALITY CLINICAL PRACTICE

Selection of appropriate comparator arms has long been a source of controversy for trials for involving one or more usual care interventions (Silverman and Miller 2004;Horn et al. 2018). Decisions about comparator arms implicate a trial's risk-benefit ratio, its scientific validity, and its social value. Critics have raised concern that inappropriate characterization of a trial arm as “usual care” in comparative effectiveness studies can put trial subjects at increased risk and undermine safety monitoring (Applefeld et al. 2020). These critiques raise particular concern in the context of embedded PCTs using broad inclusion criteria for which participation by the healthcare institution results in changes to the treatment that many or even most patients with the condition under study would otherwise receive if the institution were not participating in the trial.

The broader debate about comparator arm selection is outside the scope of this article. For our purposes, we will assume that if a randomized trial is comparing two treatments, for which there is no scientifically validated reason to believe that either treatment arm is superior to the other, nor that another treatment is superior to those being compared within the study, the trial may be ethically appropriate to conduct. However, it does not necessarily follow that this trial is ethically appropriate to conduct at any given site. Consider, for example, a trial comparing two surgical procedures. Even if both are commonly used at the national level, if one approach is not routinely used within an institution, randomization might introduce risk resulting from the surgeons' relative lack of skill in the given approach. Any resulting differences in outcomes between study arms might reflect this skill differential, rather than true inferiority—introducing inappropriate risk and undermining scientific validity. Additionally, there might be something distinct about the patient mix at a given site that may influence the appropriateness of treatment selection. To take another example, a PCT examining whether a single, prophylactic dose of ceftriaxone delivered at the time of intubation can prevent ventilator-associated pneumonia and improve outcomes for patients presenting with brain injury after trauma or cardiac arrest. While participation in this PCT may be well-suited to hospitals at which rates of antimicrobial resistance to ceftriaxone are low, participation may not be appropriate at hospitals serving populations for which rates of antimicrobial resistance to ceftriaxone are high (Dahyot-Fizelier et al. 2024).

Unfortunately, no broadly accepted guidance exists for how institutions should assess the local appropriateness of participating in a trial comparing existing therapies. Developing this guidance would be a welcome contribution. In the interest of supporting this evaluation, we propose that institutions assess the appropriateness of local participation via two lines of inquiry: one related to clinical training and institutional capacity, and the second related to ensuring appropriateness for a given patient population.

With respect to the first line of inquiry, institutions should assess whether clinicians have (or could easily acquire) the appropriate training to deliver the interventions under study, and, relatedly, whether the appropriate infrastructure exists to support the intervention, both during the trial and for future sustainment, should the intervention prove superior to the comparator(s). Such assessments are critical both for minimizing undue risk and for promoting scientific validity. Simple descriptive statistics of current clinical care practices within the institution can inform this assessment, such as the overall number of times an intervention is performed within the institution each year as part of clinical care. However, the absence of an intervention within clinical practice at a given institution does not necessarily preclude its acceptability for use within a PCT. For example, some interventions—such as bathing wipes to prevent healthcare-acquired infections—may require little specialized training and involve low absolute risk and thus present little concern regarding their safe and effective use within the context of a PCT, regardless of clinicians' prior experience with their use.

With respect to ensuring appropriateness of interventions for the local patient population, engagement with diverse stakeholders may support identification of any relevant characteristics that may influence the clinical or cultural appropriateness of a given intervention for a site's particular population. Here, the literature on local context considerations for single IRB review may be instructive (Morain, Singleton, et al. 2024). Surveys to assess clinician buy-in to randomization may also be valuable, surfacing not only potential concerns about appropriateness for the local patient population, but also providing information about likely adherence to randomization—which, if too low, may undermine the social value of the study (and, in turn, undermine its ethical justification).

PROMOTE FAIR DISTRIBUTION OF THE BENEFITS AND BURDENS OF EMBEDDED RESEARCH

Clinical research is a system of social cooperation in which investigators, sponsors, participants, and others work together to produce socially valuable knowledge. Consequently, this system should be governed by principles of justice or fairness to determine how the benefits and burdens of that cooperative activity should be distributed (MacKay and Saylor 2020).

At least two structural challenges frustrate the ability of individual institutions to promote fair distribution in the benefits and burdens of PCTs. First, only a minority of healthcare institutions currently conduct PCTs. Consequently, those institutions that are not engaged in PCTs act as “free riders,” accruing much of the benefits without assuming any of the associated burdens. Second, healthcare systems often reflect broader patterns of societal injustice that influence access to healthcare resources (Propes et al. 2025). Therefore, PCTs that recruit from routine care settings may mirror these background patterns of injustice, for example by recruiting non-representative cohorts due to longstanding patterns of racial, ethnic, and income-based disparities in access to healthcare at a societal level.

Addressing these challenges will require policy changes far upstream of the research infrastructure. Nevertheless, healthcare institutions can—and should—take steps to avoid exacerbating patterns of injustice, both in the context of individual trials, and when assessing broader research portfolios within their respective institutions. At the level of individual trials, healthcare institutions can assess whether the setting and eligibility criteria of the PCT are likely to produce a trial population that represents the diversity of patients cared for within the system (including, where appropriate, how selection of participating sites within the system may support, rather than impede, that diversity). While the question of whether the overall trial population reflects the broader population for whom the trial results are meant to be informative is also important, such questions of trial generalizability are likely better addressed by investigators and funders, rather than participating healthcare institutions. At the level of broader research portfolios, healthcare institutions can assess the extent to which PCTs and related research activities underway within the health system collectively distribute the benefits and burdens of research, with particular attention to their impact on historically marginalized populations and those who have been traditionally underrepresented in research.

COLLABORATE IN THE IDENTIFICATION OF AND PLANNING FOR POTENTIAL COLLATERAL FINDINGS AND RELATED SIGNALS OF POTENTIAL RISK

PCTs may generate collateral findings (CFs), understood as information that emerges during a PCT that is unrelated to the primary research question, yet may nevertheless have implications for the patients, clinicians, or healthcare systems from whom or within which research data were collected (Morain et al. 2020). For example, a PCT examining the effect of adding prevalence data to spine imaging reports on downstream spine-related intervention and opioid use resulted in the study coordinating center identifying several thousand individuals for whom imaging findings indicated possible osteoporosis but for whom there was no documentation in the EHR either that the diagnosis had been communicated to the patient nor that osteoporosis treatment had been initiated (Morain, Mathews, Geller, et al. 2021).

Recent literature has highlighted a range of challenges related to the ethical management of CFs (Bollinger et al. 2020; Morain, Mathews, Geller, et al. 2021; Morain, Mathews, Weinfurt, et al. 2021). A through-line of that literature is the central role for institutions in managing CFs (Morain, Mathews, Geller, et al. 2021). Institutions are key stakeholders at every stage of CF management, including evaluating CFs to assess whether they are indeed “true” findings (rather than the result of incomplete or fragmented data), deciding whether a given finding be disclosed to patients and/or clinicians, and, if a decision is made to disclose, designing and implementing processes for disclosure and subsequent clinical follow-up, as appropriate. Importantly, as the spine-imaging example suggests, CFs, unlike incidental findings discovered in explanatory trials, may have implications for the well-being of patients beyond those enrolled in the PCT—implications that, in some cases, may indicate the presence of broader care gaps that may require remediation by the healthcare institution (Morain and Largent 2023).

The specific process for addressing a given CF—and the extent of institution's obligation related to its ethical management—will vary based upon a range of contextual features, including the clinical relevance of the CF, the timeliness of identification and implications for clinical management, the uniqueness of the knowledge (whether the information is uniquely known to the study team or readily accessible by others, such as the treating clinician), the system-level impact of disclosure (including the opportunity costs for both clinical care and future research), and whether prospective consent for the PCT was obtained (Morain, Mathews, Geller, et al. 2021). Institutions—in collaboration with investigators—should prospectively consider the potential for CFs within a given PCT and develop processes for managing any CFs that may arise.

MINIMIZE IMPOSITION OF ADDITIONAL BURDENS ON PATIENTS, CLINICIANS, AND BROADER HEALTH SYSTEM OPERATIONS

As PCTs aim to inform real-world care, they are generally designed to be less intrusive or disruptive than explanatory trials. This intent notwithstanding, PCTs can still “interrupt the flow of clinical practice.” (Greene 2023) Concern about such disruptions are particularly salient against the contemporary backdrop of workforce attrition, widespread burnout, and financial strain facing many health systems following the COVID-19 pandemic (Melnick and Maerki 2023). Consequently, prior scholarship has called upon investigators to be “conscientious” when designing PCTs to minimize disruptions to clinical and operational activities (Greene 2023). Among the various strategies offered in the literature for minimizing burdens on clinical and operational activities include scheduling patient-facing study activities to avoid overlap with busy clinical times (Larson et al. 2016), identifying data that can be collected without burdening anyone on the care team (Greene 2023), and being parsimonious with the collection of any additional data (Greene 2023).

Designing PCTs to minimize their impact on the work of patient care is straightforwardly respectful of patients. It also demonstrates respect for clinicians, whose primary role is providing clinical care (Greene 2023). Additional support is provided by the principle of nonmaleficence, given the potential impact of clinician burnout and job dissatisfaction on patient mortality (Aiken et al. 2002). Ideally, designing PCTs to minimize burden on clinicians and operational systems can also promote clinician well-being, as clinicians participating in PCTs report deriving satisfaction from their contributions, and from developing the knowledge that can improve care quality and patient outcomes (Leatherman et al. 2025; Casey et al. 2023).

Prior scholarship has argued that the onus of minimizing burdens should fall to PCT investigators, particularly when PCTs are supported by public funds (Greene 2023). Yet institutions also have obligations to minimize burdens, deriving from their gatekeeping role. As discussed previously, PCTs require cooperation with institutional leadership (and other entities, such as IRBs), who facilitate access to a range of resources necessary for the successful conduct of a given PCT, including access to patients and clinicians (and their data) (Whicher et al. 2015). The design and scale of many PCTs also means that they can impact a far greater proportion of patients (and/or clinicians) than many explanatory trials—and, correspondingly, may present greater burdens for affected stakeholders. In some cases, such as for cluster-randomized PCTs, gatekeepers may also provide consent for randomization to a study intervention (in lieu of individual informed consent) (Gallo et al. 2012). This granting of access by gatekeepers comes with corresponding obligations, including the responsibility to protect the interests of patients, clinicians, and others affected by the intervention.

ENSURE APPROPRIATE DATA PROTECTIONS FOR PATIENT AND EMPLOYEE DATA

It is generally uncontroversial in research ethics that data collection procedures be adequately justified, and that the risks of data collection be minimized consistent with sound research design and reasonable in relation to the potential knowledge to be gained. Ensuring appropriate data confidentiality is supported by the principles of non-maleficence and respect for persons, as inappropriate disclosures of health information can both cause harm and undermine autonomy interests. Data breaches can also have widespread and adverse effects on institutions, undermining trust and institutional reputation, and bring the threat of punitive legal and administrative actions (and corresponding financial harms) (Whicher et al. 2015).

Many of the mechanisms to ensure data confidentiality in PCTs resemble those within health research more generally, such as collecting and sharing the minimum amount of data necessary for the research in question, using secure systems to control access to identifiable health information, and establishing systems of accountability for data breaches and other harms (Morain et al. 2022). However, at least three features of PCTs present distinctive considerations for data protections (Morain, Bollinger, et al. 2022). First, the use of clinical or operational data for research purposes may enhance the risk for violations of data privacy, due to the greater potential for data to inadvertently contain identifiable information, the increased logistical challenges for ensuring data deidentification, and the enhanced potential of reidentification through data linkage (Morain, Bollinger, et al. 2024). Second, the use of extant data often requires permission from institutional actors, which presents corresponding obligations to ensure adequate protections (especially, but not exclusively, when specific consent for the use of data for research purposes was not obtained from individuals). Third, PCTs often involve the collection of data about employees, such as clinicians or other staff—populations that are often not considered in standard research ethics guidance and associated practices. These three features of PCTs each signal to the critical role of healthcare institutions in ensuring appropriate data protections, given the role of these institutions in facilitating access to patient and/or employee data.

The specific means by which institutions ensure data protections will vary by both the nature of the trial and available institutional resources. Some have advocated for the use of data enclaves, which enable the sharing of information derived from the data, without sharing the actual data themselves (Platt and Lieu 2018). Alternatively, institutions can establish review processes to ensure appropriate deidentification before sharing. Regardless of the approach, institutions must ensure appropriate protections for secondary use of extant health data, including compliance with any applicable federal regulations related to the use of protected health information.

PROMOTE APPROPRIATE TRANSPARENCY ABOUT PCTS UNDERWAY WITHIN THE INSTITUTION

Transparency about research activities and the reasons underlying them demonstrates respect for individuals, recognizing them as persons who should be offered genuine explanations about practices that affect them, rather than merely as means to an end (Morain et al. 2022). For many PCTs, this transparency can be fulfilled through standard informed consent practices (and thus there may be little additional role for institutions to fulfill this obligation, beyond what is normally required for explanatory trials). However, a substantial (and potentially increasing) number of PCTs are conducted under a waiver of consent (Lin et al. 2021). Consequently, alternative mechanisms—beyond consent processes—may be needed to promote appropriate transparency.

Institutions have a key role in decision-making about transparency for PCTs for at least three reasons, one normative and two practical. The first is that, for many PCTs, the institution acts as a gatekeeper, permitting access to its patients (and/or clinicians) for the purposes of research. This gatekeeping duty is elevated for PCTs conducted using a waiver of consent, in which individuals themselves lack the opportunity to subsequently affirm their agreement to participate in research. Transparency about such decisions can promote trustworthiness and help assure patients and other stakeholders that the institution is acting appropriately.

Second, institutions control many of the mechanisms used to notify patients of their participation in a specific study (e.g., posting study-specific flyers, providing handouts in clinics or waiting rooms) or to provide more general disclosure about PCTs within the institution (such as including a statement within a consent-to-treat form about an institution's commitment to deliberate integration of research into ongoing clinical care as part of a commitment to continuous improvement). Third, and relatedly, transparency-promoting mechanisms often require involvement of and coordination with clinical and operational staff beyond members of the research team, such as by ensuring environmental services or janitorial staff do not remove posted notifications in clinic areas or working with IT leaders to disseminate messages through patient portals (Morain et al. 2026).

Various strategies have been advanced for promoting transparency in PCTs. For example, in the TiME trial, which compared different lengths of dialysis, the study team provided patients with information sheets describing the trial and its rationale, the study arm to which their clinic had been randomized, and instructions how to request their data be withdrawn, should they prefer their data not be included in the study (O'Rourke et al. 2025). Additional mechanisms for promoting transparency include notices in patient newsletters, messages in patient portals, displays on video monitors throughout clinics, or posting on institutional websites (Kass and Faden 2018; Morain et al. 2022).

Note, however, that this obligation to promote transparency does not necessarily require study-specific notification of every PCT underway within the institution. While transparency is ethically valuable, this value may be outweighed by other considerations, such as scientific validity (such as for an educational intervention for which notification might alter behavior, even of those within the control arm) (Melnick and Maerki 2023). Nevertheless, institutions have an obligation to—in collaboration with investigators and other stakeholders—proactively assess whether (and if so, how and when) to share information about research activities to those enrolled, and to others whose interests may be affected by the trial's activities.

DISSEMINATE RESEARCH FINDINGS WITHIN THE INSTITUTION

Disseminating research results is a fundamental obligation of ethical research, as research can only have social value if society gains knowledge from sharing its results (Emanuel et al. 2000). Correspondingly, international ethics guidance emphasizes that research results must be made publicly available so as to maximize the benefits from research, reduce risks to future research subjects, inform decision-making about clinical care, improve resource allocation for both future care and research, and promote societal trust in research (World Medical Association 2024;Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences 2016). Arguments are also increasingly being made for the importance of sharing aggregate research results or lay-friendly summaries of overall study findings with research participants (MacDuffie et al. 2025). Sharing aggregate results is supported by the ethical principles of respect and reciprocity, recognizing the integral role of research participants in the development of new knowledge, and demonstrating appreciation for the service those participants provide to advance research (Morain, Brickler, et al. 2025).

As applied to PCTs, these arguments suggest the importance of disseminating research findings to two different (but potentially overlapping) groups: those positioned to use those results to inform future decision-making within the participating healthcare institutions, and to the patient-subjects who contributed to the generation of those findings.

Dissemination to Support Future Decision-Making

A key justification for PCTs is that they can generate results that are “immediately applicable to clinical practice.” (Kalkman et al. 2017) However, this social value cannot be realized if the results are not disseminated to those who are positioned to act on them. Consequently, there is a shared obligation of investigators and institutional leadership to ensure that the findings of PCTs are disseminated to those positioned to make decisions based on those results, both within and beyond the institution. In many cases, this will require sharing in different modes than scholarly publications, such as through grand rounds or other clinician-facing educational strategies, such as institutional newsletters.

Dissemination of Aggregate Results to Patient-Subjects

Disseminating lay-friendly summaries of research results to those enrolled can be an important means to demonstrate respect (MacDuffie et al. 2025; Morain, Brickler, et al. 2025). However, this dissemination is not without tradeoffs, and requires a range of decisions, including whether, what, and how to share results. Institutions must be key partners in this decision-making, as they often control many of the mechanisms by which to disseminate results.

Traditionally, dissemination about research results has been presented as an obligation held primarily by investigators and sponsors. However, such dissemination in PCTs often requires institutional resources or permissions. For example, when considering dissemination to patients, institutions control access to patient portals and patient-facing newsletters and may require sign-off for the posting of fliers or handouts in clinic spaces. (Similar considerations face dissemination approaches targeted at clinicians or operational systems.)

At least two additional features of PCTs create distinct considerations for institutions related to sharing aggregate results with patient-subjects. First, decisions about sharing aggregate results may present tradeoffs between privacy and transparency, such as for trials conducted under waivers of informed consent and for which the only purpose for which protected health information (PHI) would be shared would be for the purpose of future sharing of aggregate research results (Morain, Brickler, et al. 2025). In such cases, institutions must decide whether the value of sharing aggregate results outweighs the disvalue of the potential threats to privacy and confidentiality related to the additional collection and sharing of health information. Second, clinicians may face inquiries from patients related to the disclosure of research results, including the implications of those results for individual patient health. Institutions choosing to share aggregate results should plan accordingly for these patient inquiries. In some cases, it may be appropriate to develop, in coordination with the research team, patient-facing materials to communicate the purposes of such research and its implications for patients (Morain, Brickler, et al. 2025).

IMPLEMENT, SUSTAIN, EXPAND, OR De-IMPLEMENT THE EVALUATED INTERVENTIONS BASED ON THE FINDINGS OF THE PCT

One of the key rationales for PCTs is their promise to generate real-world social value, including by facilitating uptake of results by relevant decision-makers (Kalkman et al. 2017). This value cannot be realized, however, if the results of PCTs are not subsequently used to inform future decision-making. Consequently, there should be a presumptive default that study results from PCTs (whether positive or null) be incorporated into future care delivery processes (Morain, O'Rourke, et al. 2024). This is consistent with a general, prima facie obligation to act on evidence, consistent with clinician (and administrative leader) obligations to provide optimal care to each patient (Faden et al. 2013). It is also supported by the social compact undergirding societal investment in biomedical research, as societies invest in this research with the expectation that insights generated from those investments will be translated into improved health for current and future populations (Kass and Faden 2018). An additional reason to incorporate the results of PCTs into future care delivery processes is grounded in the principle of reciprocity. In explanatory research, reciprocity is often described as an obligation owed by investigators and research sponsors: as individuals enrolled into trials assist investigators in generating valuable data (and, in some cases, assume additional risks or burdens in the interest of that end), investigators (and sponsors), in turn, should help to ensure that those who contributed benefit from what is learned through research. These arguments could provide special weight for incorporating newly generated evidence from PCTs into ongoing practice within participating institutions. However, the magnitude of this obligation, and the group(s) charged with its fulfillment, may vary from the explanatory trial context based upon a range of features, including the degree of burden or risk associated with trial participation, and the group(s) that hold the decisional authority to implement changes based upon trial results.

Existing ethics literature on post-trial obligations has generally emphasized the role of investigators, sponsors, and, in some cases, host country governments. However, at least two features of PCTs suggest that healthcare institutions also share responsibility for post-trial decisions—including decisions to incorporate the results of PCTs into ongoing clinical and operational processes. First, as noted previously, healthcare institutions serve as gatekeepers for PCTs, permitting access to patients, clinicians, and their data to facilitate knowledge development—in some cases without the explicit permission or even knowledge of those enrolled. This access creates corollary obligations, including to respect and recognize the contributions of those enrolled. One means to demonstrate this respect is to ensure that future care processes incorporate the knowledge gained to improve care. Second, healthcare institutions often influence—if not directly control—many of the resources necessary for (de)implementing or sustaining tested interventions. Institutional leaders therefore play a critical role in shaping whether and how PCTs will, in fact, generate direct social value.

Recent scholarship has identified several factors that influence post-trial sustainment and (de)implementation of interventions in PCTs, including the resource intensiveness of tested interventions and alignment with policy incentives (Green et al. 2024). A key lesson from this scholarship is the importance of engaging with relevant decision-makers—including institutional leadership—before beginning a PCT. As part of this engagement, institutions should consider whether the health system would change practice based upon trial results—and, importantly, what metrics would be relevant for that assessment. To make these assessments, institutions may need to implement more robust processes for evaluation of and institutional sign-off on research proposals than existing models, which often rely solely on department chairs or divisional leaders, and instead proactively engage operational leadership when deciding about whether to participate in a PCT (Morain, O'Rourke, et al. 2024). The likelihood of post-trial sustainment is not itself determinative of the appropriateness of a site's participation, but it is nevertheless an important criterion for institutional consideration.

CONCLUSION

PCTs offer the promise to improve the relevance, generalizability, and efficiency of evidence generation, yet the deliberate embedding of research into clinical care also presents distinct ethical considerations. Prior ethical analysis of PCTs has identified that the institutions where PCTs take place have a central role in ensuring the ethical conduct of these trials. To support this role, we have proposed a framework of ten obligations for healthcare institutions when leading or participating in a PCT. Although this framework builds upon prior scholarship pertaining to the role of institutions in PCTs, it nevertheless is the first to propose a comprehensive set of institutional obligations for PCTs. We therefore anticipate and welcome suggestions and commentary for how to further advance both our collective understandings of institutional obligations, and strategies for their practical realization.
