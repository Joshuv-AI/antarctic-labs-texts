Neighborhood social environment and physical activity among persons living with dementia
Authors: Eun Young Choi, Susanna M Mage, Jennifer A Ailshire, Joseph E Gaugler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesPhysical activity (PA) helps delay the progression of cognitive and functional decline among persons living with dementia. Neighborhood social environments are well-known to support or hinder PA among older adults, but their associations with PA among persons living with dementia remain poorly understood. This study examines how neighborhood characteristics (affluence, deprivation, safety, friends, relatives, cohesion, and disorder) relate to PA among U.S. community-dwelling persons living with dementia.Research Design and MethodsWe used 2006–2018 Health and Retirement Study data merged with census-tract data from the Contextual Data Resource. The analytic sample included 2,031 respondents classified as having dementia. PA was measured as a summary score reflecting activity frequency and intensity (light, moderate, and vigorous), along with binary indicators for being “regularly active” (≥1×/week) at each intensity. We used negative binomial regression to model total PA and logistic regression for intensity-specific activity.ResultsNeighborhood friendships were associated with higher overall PA score (B = 0.28; 95% CI = 0.16–0.41) and regular engagement across intensity levels. Social cohesion was also linked to increased total activity (B = 0.16; 95% CI = 0.03–0.29) and light PA (OR = 1.31; 95% CI = 1.01–1.71). Affluent neighborhoods supported moderate PA (OR = 1.39; 95% CI = 1.08–1.77), while having nearby relatives was associated with lower odds of vigorous activity (OR = 0.61; 95% CI = 0.42–0.88). No significant associations were observed for neighborhood deprivation, disorder, or safety.Discussion and ImplicationsOur findings provide empirical evidence for the importance of neighborhood social resources as facilitators of sustained PA among persons living with dementia and highlight neighborhoods as key components of the continuum of dementia care.Keywords: Environmental health factors, Dementia friendly communities, Activity engagement, Social-ecological model

Physical activity (PA) is critical for health and well-being among persons living with dementia. Evidence shows that PA improves mobility and physical functioning (Pitkälä et al., 2013), supports independence in activities of daily living (Blankevoort et al., 2010), and reduces fall risk among persons living with dementia (Cezar et al., 2021). In addition, PA is associated with better mental health, including reductions in depression, apathy, and psychological distress, as well as improvements in mood and quality of life (Kaur et al., 2024). A growing number of studies suggest that sustained engagement in PA may help slow dementia progression, reporting associations with improved executive function and language ability among patients with Alzheimer’s disease (Holthoff et al., 2015) and reduced brain atrophy in its early stages (Burns et al., 2008). Furthermore, several studies indicate that PA can help reduce dementia symptoms (Law et al., 2020). Yet, despite strong evidence of its benefits, most community-dwelling persons living with dementia do not engage in sufficient levels of PA and remain far less active than their cognitively healthy peers (Hartman et al., 2018).

Prior research indicates that while persons living with dementia often describe PA as a source of enjoyment and positive experiences across the stages of dementia, their ability to remain active is constrained by personal barriers, including memory problems, confusion, and progressive gait and balance impairments (Telenius et al., 2022). A dementia-specific adaptation of the social-ecological model highlights the importance of not only intrapersonal factors but also neighborhood contexts in shaping PA (van Alphen et al., 2016). In particular, the neighborhood social environment, which refers to the broader social characteristics of a local area that shape interpersonal interactions, norms, and collective resources, has been proposed as a key domain influencing activity levels among persons living with dementia (Feenstra et al., 2023; van Alphen et al., 2016). From a social health perspective, neighborhoods are the most immediate and influential setting for supporting social connectedness, autonomy, and participation in daily life following a dementia diagnosis (Kane & Cook, 2013). Ward et al. (2018) emphasize that persons living with dementia draw upon personal capabilities and localized social resources within their neighborhoods to sustain participation and autonomy, thereby helping to compensate for cognitive and functional limitations. A recent GPS-tracking study of persons living with dementia found that while they are less likely to go outside, when they do, they tend to stay closer to home, covering shorter distances and spending more time within their neighborhoods (Puthusseryppady et al., 2022), highlighting the centrality of the neighborhood environment for supporting outdoor activity and mobility.

Several qualitative studies have identified various features of neighborhood social environments that may facilitate PA for persons living with dementia. Social connections within neighborhoods are consistently reported as strong facilitators of PA engagement by providing companionship, helping to manage risks, and reinforcing a sense of belonging. A study of 67 persons living with dementia living in England and Sweden found that neighborhood connections enabled continued outdoor engagement, indirectly promoting PA through regular movement and participation in local life (Clark et al., 2020). Findings from a study with 22 persons living with dementia living across the United Kingdom also emphasized the value of informal encounters with neighbors and friends during outdoor activities (Duggan et al., 2008).

Family members living nearby or providing care play an important role in facilitating neighborhood engagement. In a study of 19 persons living with dementia living in Northern England, access to social support within community networks was directly linked to walking behavior; participants who had family to accompany them were more likely to go outdoors (Wright, 2018). However, family caregiver involvement may also have unintended consequences, such as acting as gatekeepers who at times restrict activity out of safety concerns or the additional burden of supervision. For example, Malthouse and Fox (2014) found that caregivers sometimes limited opportunities for independent outings among persons living with dementia due to concerns about falls or getting lost.

In addition to individual relationships, neighborhood social cohesion has been identified as an important contextual factor promoting PA among persons living with dementia. Defined by trust, reciprocity, and mutual support, social cohesion facilitates emotional security and sustained community engagement (Ward et al., 2018; Wright, 2018). For example, persons living with dementia have described how recognition and familiarity within the neighborhood, even via interactions with “familiar strangers” such as shopkeepers and bus drivers, contributed to a sense of being watched over and supported, which reinforced confidence to remain active outdoors (Birt et al., 2023; Clark et al., 2020). While fewer studies have explicitly examined the role of neighborhood socioeconomic conditions, some evidence suggests that affluence may support PA by increasing access to walkable and appealing public spaces. In a Canadian study of 26 persons living with dementia, indoor walking environments such as malls and senior centers, often more prevalent in affluent areas, can offer climate-controlled alternatives to help persons living with dementia maintain PA during inclement weather (Chaudhury et al., 2025).

While neighborhood environments can facilitate PA among persons living with dementia, they can also present significant barriers. Safety concerns are consistently cited. Persons living with dementia noted traffic and other neighborhood hazards, such as overgrown bushes and sidewalk blockages, as barriers that limited their daily walks (Chaudhury et al., 2025; Ward et al., 2018). Beyond safety, a Canadian study of 26 individuals with early-to-moderate stage dementia found that, based on spatial analyses, physical disorder, including poorly maintained communal areas, was associated with reduced outdoor walking distance (Nouri & Chaudhury, 2025). Evidence regarding the role of neighborhood-level socioeconomic deprivation is limited. In a study of 87 older adults with cognitive impairment in North East England, neighborhood deprivation was not significantly associated with step count, possibly because individuals in disadvantaged areas were already more likely to spend time indoors regardless of external conditions (McArdle et al., 2023).

Although existing studies highlight the importance of neighborhood-level social factors in shaping PA among persons living with dementia, the current evidence base remains limited. Most studies are small, qualitative, and conducted outside the United States, limiting their generalizability to other contexts and populations. Although a robust body of literature exists demonstrating that neighborhood social conditions play a significant role in supporting PA among older adults more broadly (Peters et al., 2020), it remains unclear whether these associations extend to persons living with dementia.

To address this gap, the present study examines how multiple aspects of the neighborhood social environment, specifically area affluence, deprivation, safety, friends, relatives, social cohesion, and disorder, are associated with PA levels among persons living with dementia living in U.S. communities. We use data from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), a U.S. nationally representative sample, which includes thousands of persons living with dementia, linked to contextual neighborhood data. Furthermore, in addition to examining total overall PA levels, we also assess whether these associations vary by activity intensity (light, moderate, or vigorous). Prior research suggests that different activity intensities may have distinct health implications, with moderate-intensity programs offering particularly strong cognitive and functional benefits for persons living with dementia (Nuzum et al., 2020). Disaggregating by intensity, therefore, allows us to better identify the types of activity most likely to be influenced by neighborhood social features. Accordingly, we propose two research questions: (a) Which aspects of the neighborhood social environment are associated with higher levels of PA following dementia onset; (b) Do these associations vary by the intensity of PA?

Method

Data and sample

Data were drawn from the HRS, a nationally representative longitudinal study of U.S. adults older than 50 years, conducted biennially since 1992. This study used data from the 2006–2018 waves, which align with the availability of consistent PA measures and neighborhood data. Neighborhood socioeconomic status was derived from tract-level 5-year estimates from the U.S. Census Bureau’s American Community Survey (ACS) and linked to HRS respondents through the HRS Contextual Data Resource (Ailshire et al., 2025). Respondents’ addresses were geocoded in each interview year and linked at the census-tract level. We matched each HRS wave to the corresponding ACS period based on interview year, following a biennial sequence (e.g., ACS 2005–2009 for HRS 2006, ACS 2006–2010 for 2008, through ACS 2016–2020 for 2018) to measure neighborhood affluence and deprivation. Data on other aspects of the neighborhood social environment were captured from HRS. Perceived neighborhood safety was obtained from the 2006–2018 core interviews. Neighborhood cohesion was assessed through the Psychosocial and Lifestyle Questionnaire (SAQ) administered in alternating waves since 2006, with half the sample receiving the SAQ in each wave. Information on the presence of friends and relatives in the neighborhood was collected through the core interview from 2006 to 2012 and the SAQ from 2014 to 2018. Measures of neighborhood disorder were derived from the 2006–2018 Interviewer Observation files. In sensitivity analyses, we used 2004 HRS data to capture prior PA levels for the 2006 sample. When respondents were unable to complete the interview, proxies were asked to report on variables including PA and neighborhood measures (i.e., safety, friends, relatives, and cohesion) when available.

Our analysis focused on the first wave in which respondents were classified as having dementia. We began with 30,669 community dwelling individuals who completed at least one core interview with cognitive data between 2006 and 2018. Because clinical examinations of dementia are impractical in large-scale population studies, dementia status in the HRS is determined through the validated Langa–Weir classification (Langa et al., 2023), which applies empirically derived cut-points to scores from the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS) for self-respondents, and uses a composite measure for proxy respondents that incorporates proxy-rated memory, interviewer assessments of cognitive impairment, and proxy-reported limitations in instrumental activities of daily living. The algorithm has demonstrated good predictive validity against consensus dementia diagnosis made by expert panels based on neuropsychiatric and clinical assessments (Crimmins et al., 2011). To further reduce false positives and account for possible status reversals, we adopted a “persistent dementia” definition (Zissimopoulos et al., 2018). Respondents were classified as having persistent dementia if they met dementia criteria in one wave and showed continued impairment (i.e., dementia or cognitive impairment without dementia [CIND]) in the subsequent wave. For respondents who met dementia criteria but died before a follow-up wave, we assumed they had dementia prior to death.

We identified 3,897 respondents who met the criteria for persistent dementia between 2006 and 2018. To focus on incident cases, we excluded 1,461 individuals who were classified as having dementia prior to the baseline of 2006 and 207 individuals whose first dementia classification occurred in their initial year of cognitive assessment, as their timing of dementia onset could not be determined. We further excluded 43 individuals without valid census tract IDs needed to link contextual data, 122 who had moved to a new area in the past year, and 33 with missing key variables or covariates. The final analytic sample included 2,031 respondents. Analyses were conducted separately for each neighborhood variable to maximize available data, resulting in slight variations across models: 2,008 for affluence and deprivation, 2,009 for perceived safety, 2,019 for neighborhood friends, 2,018 for relatives, 1,533 for perceived cohesion, and 1,716 for observed disorder. All 2,031 respondents had valid data for at least one neighborhood variable. Supplementary Figure 1 presents the detailed sample selection process.

Measures

Physical activity

Physical activity was assessed based on self- or proxy- reported frequency of engagement in light, moderate, and vigorous activities. Respondents (or their proxies) were asked how often they engaged in mildly energetic activities (e.g., vacuuming, laundry, home repairs), moderately energetic activities (e.g., walking at a moderate pace, gardening, dancing), and vigorous activities (e.g., running, swimming, aerobics). Response options ranged from 0 = never to 4 = every day. To construct a composite PA score indicating total activity, we followed Cheng and Nicklett (2022), who applied average MET (Metabolic Equivalent of Task) values from the Compendium of Physical Activities to HRS intensity categories: 1 MET for light, 2.5 MET for moderate, and 4.25 MET for vigorous activity. Reported frequency was multiplied by the corresponding intensity weight and summed. The final PA score ranged from 0 to 31, with higher scores indicating greater total volume and intensity of PA. We also constructed binary indicators for each PA type. Respondents were classified as “regularly active” if they reported engaging in the activity at least once per week versus less frequently.

Neighborhood socioeconomic affluence and deprivation

Neighborhood socioeconomic conditions were measured using census tract-level indicators for all U.S. tracts from each ACS year. We conducted factor analyses, which supported a two-factor solution: affluence with the highest loadings for college education and professional employment and deprivation with the highest loadings for poverty indicators and home ownership (negatively). We generated continuous factor-based scales by combining the standardized values of all input variables based on their contribution to each factor, with higher values indicating greater affluence or deprivation. After merging factor scores into our sample, we created binary indicators for high affluence and high deprivation based on the top tertile of each score distribution. Although the correlation between these two factors was high (r = −0.62), as expected, it was not perfectly inverse, supporting our decision to examine them separately. Complete details of the factor analyses are provided in Supplementary Text 1.

Neighborhood safety

Neighborhood safety was assessed via self- or proxy-report using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “bad” to “excellent” in response to the question, “How would you rate the safety of the neighborhood you live in?” We created a binary variable to reflect high safety of neighborhood: bad/fair/good vs very good/excellent (57%). An alternative cutoff (bad/fair vs good/very good/excellent) was considered but produced a highly skewed distribution (85% in the high-safety group) and poor covariate balance in the inverse probability of treatment weighting procedure (described in the analysis section), with standardized mean differences exceeding 0.1 postweighting for gender. Therefore, the final dichotomization (bad/fair/good vs very good/excellent) was selected for analysis. Nonetheless, sensitivity analyses using the alternative specification yielded consistent results.

Neighborhood friends

Presence of neighborhood friends was assessed using self- or proxy-reports. Respondents (or proxies) were asked whether they had any good friends living in their neighborhood. Binary indicators were created for each: no or yes.

Neighborhood relatives

Presence of family in the neighborhood was assessed using self- or proxy-reports. Respondents (or proxies) were asked whether they had any relatives (besides co-residing family members) living in their neighborhood. Binary indicators were created for each no and yes.

Neighborhood social cohesion

Neighborhood social cohesion was assessed through self- or proxy-reported perceptions of trust and solidarity toward one’s neighborhood and neighbors. Respondents (or proxies) rated their agreement with four statements about their local area, defined as anywhere within a 20-min walk from home: “I really feel part of this area,” “Most people in this area can be trusted,” “Most people in this area are friendly,” and “If you were in trouble, there are lots of people in this area who would help you” on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). A mean score was calculated across the four items, with higher scores indicating greater perceived neighborhood social cohesion. Respondents missing two or more items were coded as “missing.” The scale demonstrated high internal consistency in our sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.84). We also created a binary indicator to identify respondents in the highest tertile of cohesion (high vs low/moderate cohesion).

Neighborhood disorder

Neighborhood disorder, defined as the presence of visible signs of physical or social disorder in the area surrounding the respondent’s home, was assessed using interviewer observations. They reported whether each of the following conditions was visible from the respondent’s housing unit: vandalism, boarded-up houses, abandoned cars, abandoned or demolished houses, trash or litter in the street, trash or litter around buildings, and run-down or poorly maintained yards or communal areas. A total disorder score (range: 0–7) was calculated by summing the number of observed conditions. Because the disorder score distribution was highly skewed, with ∼80% of respondents scoring 0, we created a binary variable indicating the presence of any observed disorder versus none. This approach is consistent with prior research demonstrating that even a single sign of neighborhood disorder can meaningfully impact a residents’ perceptions of safety (Keizer et al., 2008).

Adjacent-wave replacement

Because several neighborhood measures (i.e., friends, relatives, social cohesion, and disorder) were asked of only one-half of the HRS sample in alternating waves, data on these measures were not available for all respondents at the wave in which they were first classified as having dementia. Beginning in 2006, half of respondents were randomly assigned to enhanced face-to-face interviews, which included an SAQ and interviewer observations capturing neighborhood measures, while the other half received telephone interviews that did not. Therefore, we used adjacent-wave replacement, substituting values from up to three prior waves or, when necessary, the next wave. This approach minimized potential bias due to survey design limitations while preserving analytic power. Replaced values were highly similar to observed values, and full details on replacement frequencies, descriptive comparisons, and exclusions are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Confounders

We adjusted for key demographic and socioeconomic characteristics measured at baseline that may confound the association between the neighborhood social environment and PA levels. These included age, sex, race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and non-Hispanic other), education (high school or above vs less than high school), labor force status (currently working full- or part-time, unemployed, or partly retired vs retired, disabled, or not in the labor force), and the net value of household nonhousing wealth (i.e., total nonhousing assets minus debts), transformed using the inverse hyperbolic sine function to reduce skewness.

Analysis strategy

We first examined distribution of PA score, which was highly right-skewed with a large proportion of zero values (Supplementary Figure 2). Given evidence of overdispersion, we used negative binomial regression models. In all models, the dispersion parameter (α) was significantly greater than 0. For binary outcomes (regular light, moderate, and vigorous activity), we fitted logistic regression models. We used single-level regression models given the minimal tract-level clustering (N = 1,456; mean = 1.4 respondents per tract; 77% of tracts had one respondent). Nevertheless, to reduce the risk of underestimating variability in the single-level models, we adjusted standard errors for within-tract correlation.

To reduce potential confounding from nonrandom selection into neighborhoods, we applied inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). IPTW reweights the sample so that the distribution of covariates is comparable between individuals exposed versus unexposed to a given neighborhood condition. Propensity scores were estimated using generalized boosted models (GBM) via the twang R package (Ridgeway et al., 2024), which employs iterative tree-based methods to flexibly capture nonlinearities and interactions among covariates. GBMs used up to 5,000 trees with a shrinkage parameter of 0.01, interaction depth of 2, and targeted the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT). Separate propensity score models were constructed for each binary neighborhood exposure variable. All models adjusted for baseline confounders, as previously described. The IPTW procedure achieved good model performance, with convergence and balance diagnostics meeting conventional standards (Supplementary Figure 3; Supplementary Table 2). Final weights were constructed by multiplying the IPTWs by the respondent-level HRS survey weights. The HRS survey weights correct for complex survey design and ensure that results are representative of the U.S. older adult population. We employed doubly robust estimation, combining IPTW with covariate adjustment. This approach offers additional protection against model misspecification (Funk et al., 2011). Estimated coefficients represent the ATT, capturing the effects of neighborhood exposures on PA among those exposed.

We fit three models. Model 1 (main model) adjusted for all aforementioned confounders. To assess the robustness, we estimated two expanded models. Model 2a additionally adjusted for predementia PA to account for baseline differences in activity levels that could confound associations with neighborhood exposures. Predementia PA was defined as PA measured at the wave immediately preceding the wave in which an individual was first classified as having dementia. For example, for participants classified as having dementia in 2014, PA measures from the 2012 wave were used. Predementia PA was assessed using the same instruments and operational definitions as the main PA outcomes, as described in the Measures section. We used the prior total PA score in models predicting total PA and preonset binary indicators of light, moderate, vigorous activity in models predicting corresponding outcomes. A total of 126 individuals had missing data for these predementia PA variables. Model 2b added covariates to address potential bias from survey design and health-related limitations. We included an indicator for proxy respondent status, given that 23% of individuals with cognitive impairment had proxy interviews and that SAQ data were no longer collected from proxies beginning in 2016. We also adjusted for mobility limitation (difficulty walking across a room) and for death before the next wave, as both could influence PA participation and reporting. All analyses were conducted using Stata version 18.0. Figure 1 presents a diagram of the hypothesized relationships among study variables.

Results

Sample characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the study sample (N = 2,031), incorporating survey weights. The mean total PA score was 6.59 (SD = 7.22) on a scale from 0 to 31. By intensity, 51% reported engaging in light PA at least weekly, 42% in moderate PA at least once per week, and only 15% in vigorous PA on a weekly basis. In terms of neighborhood social environment, mean census tract affluence was −0.27, with 35.1% of respondents residing in highly affluent tracts. Conversely, mean tract deprivation was 0.16, with 28.6% residing in highly deprived tracts. Perceived neighborhood safety was relatively high (57.1%). Social relationships within the neighborhood varied: 61.8% reported having good friend(s) living nearby, while only 30.5% had relative(s) residing in their neighborhood. The perceived social cohesion scale had a mean of 5.29, with 32.6% of respondents classified as living in highly cohesive neighborhoods. One-fifth of participants (19.6%) lived in areas with observed signs of neighborhood disorder.

Neighborhood social environment and PA among persons living with dementia

We fitted negative binomial regression models predicting total PA scores and logistic regression models to estimate the odds of engaging in PA intensities (light, moderate, vigorous) based on neighborhood exposures. Table 2 presents the results of these models, adjusted using IPTW. In Model 1, having friends in the neighborhood (B = 0.28; 95% CI = 0.16–0.41, p < .001) and perceiving high neighborhood cohesion (B = 0.16; 95% CI = 0.03–0.29, p < .05) were significantly associated with higher total PA scores. These associations remained after adjusting for predementia PA levels (Model 2a) and after further adjusting for proxy interview status, mobility limitations, and death within the next year (Model 2b). Similar findings emerged for models predicting light PA. Neighborhood friends (OR = 1.69; 95% CI = 1.34–2.13, p < .001) and high perceived neighborhood cohesion (OR = 1.31; 95% CI = 1.01–1.71, p < .05) were significantly associated with greater odds of regular engagement in light activity. After adjusting for predementia light activity or additional indicators (Models 2a and 2b), the presence of neighborhood friends remained a robust predictor. At the same time, perceived cohesion became marginally significant but continued in a positive direction. For moderate activity, residents in highly affluent tracts (OR = 1.39; 95% CI = 1.08–1.77, p < .01) and those having neighborhood friends (OR = 1.39; 95% CI = 1.11–1.75, p < .01) were more likely to engage in moderate activity in Model 1. However, after adjusting for prior moderate activity (Model 2a) and additional indicators (Model 2b), only high tract affluence consistently remained significant, while the effect of neighborhood friends attenuated. For vigorous PA, having friends in the neighborhood was positively associated (OR = 1.70; 95% CI = 1.24–2.32, p < .001), whereas neighborhood relatives were negatively associated (OR = 0.61; 95% CI = 0.42–0.88, p < .01) in Model 1. Both associations persisted in Models 2a and 2b, indicating robust findings across sensitivity analyses.

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted sensitivity tests to evaluate the robustness of our main findings. To address potential bias introduced by adjacent-wave replacement, we included an indicator for replaced neighborhood social capital, cohesion, and disorder measures; results were consistent with the primary analyses (Supplementary Table 3). We also tested alternative thresholds for classifying PA frequency to assess sensitivity to different cutoffs (Supplementary Table 4). For light activity, cohesion remained significant using a less strict threshold (“any” vs “never”) but weakened to marginal significance under a stricter threshold (“more than once per week”). For moderate activity, neighborhood friends remained consistently significant under the less strict threshold, while affluence became marginally significant; under the stricter threshold, both neighborhood friends and social cohesion were significant only in Model 1. Findings for vigorous activity remained robust across thresholds. Overall, these sensitivity analyses support the robustness of our primary results.

Discussion

This study explored how aspects of the neighborhood social environment shape PA among persons living with dementia in the United States. Findings indicate that social characteristics of the neighborhood environment can act as barriers or facilitators to PA. Specifically, we found that having nearby friends, higher neighborhood social cohesion, and more area affluence were associated with greater PA, while the presence of family in the neighborhood was associated with reduced PA. Deprivation, disorder, and safety were not significantly related to PA among persons living with dementia.

Persons living with dementia were more likely to report PA engagement when they had friends in the neighborhood. These relationships were consistently associated with all intensities of activity (light, moderate, vigorous), as well as the summary level of PA. These findings are consistent with literature on the role of neighborhood friends as facilitators of PA for persons living with dementia (Nouri & Chaudhury, 2025; Odzakovic et al., 2021), and also align with broader literature on older adults, where friend support has been shown to enhance PA, particularly leisure and recreational participation (Orsega-Smith et al., 2007).

While neighborhood friendships were positively associated with PA, proximity to relatives was only associated with reduced vigorous PA. Though family support has been identified as influential in supporting PA among persons living with dementia, studies often do not distinguish between family living nearby and co-residing family (Clark et al., 2020; Ward et al., 2018). Research on co-residing family indicates that they play a dual role, acting both as barriers (Hancox et al., 2019) and facilitators (McCurry et al., 2010). Similar to our findings, research on older adults shows how family living nearby can be unrelated (Loprinzi & Joyner, 2016) or negatively associated (Larsen et al., 2014; Loprinzi & Joyner, 2016; Orsega-Smith et al., 2007) with PA. These contrasting patterns reflect differences in the roles that friends and family can play in daily life and reinforce the idea that kin support may substitute for, rather than encourage, PA. Family members may provide instrumental assistance (e.g., transportation, grocery shopping) that lessens the need for the persons living with dementia to engage in activity, or may prioritize shared sedentary or caregiving-related activities over exercise, reducing opportunities for PA. Friends, on the other hand, are more likely to be peers with shared interests and serve as direct companions in activity, thereby encouraging participation.

In addition to social support from nearby friends, broader neighborhood relationships that foster trust, mutual support, and a sense of belonging are especially important for PA among persons living with dementia. We found that those who perceived higher levels of neighborhood social cohesion were more likely to engage in PA, particularly light and moderate activities. Everyday interactions with neighbors, local merchants, and service providers may create informal opportunities for PA and support continued engagement. These findings are consistent with qualitative work among persons living with dementia living in more socially cohesive neighborhoods (Clark et al., 2020; Duggan et al., 2008; Odzakovic et al., 2021; Qin et al., 2021; Ward et al., 2018; Wright, 2018). Evidence from broader older adult populations echoes this pattern; neighborhood cohesion is associated with a higher likelihood of meeting PA guidelines and overall engagement (Quinn et al., 2019). Beyond its direct association with PA, social cohesion may also buffer or attenuate the influence of adverse neighborhood conditions (Qin et al., 2021), further strengthening its importance.

Lastly, we found that neighborhood affluence was positively associated with moderate PA, suggesting that local socioeconomic resources may enable persons living with dementia to participate in more structured or sustained activities, such as walking in green spaces or indoor facilities, which are more commonly available in affluent neighborhoods (Chaudhury et al., 2025). Consistent with this interpretation, findings among broader older adult populations have shown that more advantaged social environments with greater access to recreational resources and amenities have been linked to higher levels of PA (Hamer et al., 2014; Tucker-Seeley et al., 2009).

We did not find associations between PA and deprivation, disorder, and safety among our sample of persons living with dementia. These findings are consistent with prior work that found no association between these factors and daily step count among individuals with cognitive impairment (McArdle et al., 2023). Although physical disorder has been associated with reduced PA among older adults by limiting opportunities for activity outside the home (Qin et al., 2021), we did not find this relationship in our sample. The lack of significance in our models suggests that, while important for older adults more broadly, such barriers may be less influential for persons living with dementia and may not directly impact their activity patterns.

Much of the literature on persons living with dementia and PA engagement has examined safety concerns related to traffic and sidewalk conditions rather than crime, identifying them as important factors supporting outdoor PA (Chaudhury et al., 2025; Nouri & Chaudhury, 2025; Ward et al., 2018). When safety is compromised, persons living with dementia may be at greater risk of remaining indoors, due not only to their own concerns but also to those of companions who might otherwise accompany them (Hancox et al., 2019), thereby limiting opportunities for PA. Evidence on the impact of safety and disorder among older adults, although mixed, supports our interpretation. While some studies suggest that these factors constrain activity (Mahmood et al., 2012; Mendes de Leon et al., 2009), others report no significant associations (Cheng & Nicklett, 2022; Kim et al., 2023). Since PA among persons living with dementia is often dependent on the involvement of others (Puthusseryppady et al., 2022), perceptions of companions may be more influential than those of persons living with dementia themselves, which could help explain why we did not observe significant associations in our study.

However, interpretations that barriers may be less important for persons living with dementia than supportive neighborhood features are complicated by the fact that persons living with dementia are generally less likely to engage in or sustain PA compared to older adults (Chaudhury et al., 2021), with many studies reporting high rates of nonadherence or attrition. For instance, only 31% of persons living with dementia in a structured intervention achieved recommended activity frequency (Lowery et al., 2014), and greater cognitive decline has been linked to higher dropout rates (McCurry et al., 2010). This suggests that selection bias may influence reported effects, as individuals who remain in PA interventions may represent more active or higher-functioning subgroups of persons living with dementia. By leveraging a large, nationally representative data set, our study addresses this limitation by capturing activity patterns across a broader spectrum of persons living with dementia, including those less likely to be retained in intervention-based research. Whereas prior research has largely relied on non-U.S., small-scale, localized, or qualitative samples, our study offers broader generalizability to the U.S. population of persons living with dementia. Leveraging the size and diversity of the HRS allowed us to assess multiple neighborhood dimensions and capture activity patterns across a broad range of the population of persons living with dementia.

Limitations

This study has limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to determine the directionality of the observed associations, whether neighborhood social environments influence PA, or whether more active individuals are more likely to perceive their neighborhood environments as more supportive and/or to reside in environments that facilitate activity following dementia onset. While we attempted to mitigate selection bias into neighborhoods by applying IPTW, the possibility of residual confounding due to unmeasured factors cannot be entirely ruled out. Second, several neighborhood exposures were captured through subjective perceptions, which may not fully correspond to objective neighborhood conditions. Moreover, neighborhood disorder was assessed through interviewer observations of the area surrounding the respondent’s home, which may not fully capture conditions across the broader neighborhood environment or reflect respondents’ own perceptions. Even after adjusting for demographic and socioeconomic factors and controlling for bias from neighborhood selection, unmeasured factors (e.g., health status, caregiving arrangements, built environment features) may still have influenced the results. While the HRS provides a nationally representative sample, the lived experience of “neighborhood” is not uniform across the United States. Accordingly, our findings are intended to generalize to population-level associations among community-dwelling U.S. older adults, rather than to imply equivalence in neighborhood meaning or experience across all social and geographic contexts. Residential segregation by race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status creates fundamentally distinct environmental contexts that robust statistical adjustments, such as IPTW, may not fully capture. Consequently, population-level average effects may obscure critical within-group variation. For example, subjective reports of neighborhood safety may lack measurement equivalence across racial groups due to divergent historical contexts of policing and structural disinvestment. Similarly, although our supplemental analyses (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6) did not reveal significant gender differences or effect modification, gendered neighborhood experiences likely persist in ways not captured by quantitative measures. Identical safety ratings, for instance, may mask gendered behavioral adaptations, such as women maintaining security through restricted mobility, or utilizing more emotionally intensive local social networks than men. Future research employing within-group and qualitative approaches is necessary to elucidate how these nuanced environmental navigations influence PA among persons living with dementia.

Implications

Neighborhoods represent both a risk and resource context that must be seen as part of the continuum of dementia care. As prior work has shown, when the activity spaces of persons living with dementia shrink, often due to anxiety or disorientation in unfamiliar environments, nearby places and incidental community interactions become increasingly central to sustaining autonomy, personhood, and social inclusion (Gan et al., 2022). Our study demonstrates that neighborhood-level social factors are critical for supporting PA, underscoring the importance of conceptualizing neighborhoods as integral sites of care for persons living with dementia. By recognizing these spaces as central sites of care, policy and practice can move beyond clinical settings to create supportive environments that enable PA for persons living with dementia. Given the well-established role of PA as a nonpharmacological intervention to support physical function, cognition, and quality of life among this population, identifying neighborhood factors that consistently foster or hinder engagement is essential. Our study offers valuable insight into the barriers and facilitators of PA, revealing the relevance of the neighborhood in shaping activity engagement for persons living with dementia.

First, friendships are a critical driver of PA across activity intensities. Interventions that intentionally foster peer networks, such as walking clubs (Malthouse & Fox, 2014) and peer-support initiatives (Odzakovic et al., 2021), may therefore be especially effective in sustaining engagement and well-being for persons living with dementia. Building on evidence from the older adult literature (Phillips & Flesner, 2013), group-based activities may offer persons living with dementia greater benefits than individualized programs, providing additional opportunities for companionship, motivation, and shared enjoyment. Social cohesion plays an equally important role, as it reflects trust and mutual support within neighborhoods, creating environments where persons living with dementia feel safe, recognized, and motivated to remain active. Welcoming and familiar community spaces provide opportunities for companionship that extend beyond physical assistance, supporting a sense of belonging and psychological security (Nouri & Chaudhury, 2025). This is particularly critical for persons living with dementia, a population at heightened risk of social isolation (Puthusseryppady et al., 2022). Dementia-friendly community initiatives illustrate how strengthening cohesion through dementia awareness campaigns, community events, recreational gatherings, and low-impact exercise can both promote PA and rebuild neighborhood ties (Chaudhury et al., 2025). Embedding these activities in trusted, culturally familiar, and accessible spaces, supported by advisory groups, volunteers, and local authorities, further enhances inclusion and sustained activity engagement (Hadley et al., 2024). Additionally, macrolevel neighborhood conditions and resources shape opportunities for PA. Although our study found null associations for deprivation, disorder, or safety, we believe that this exemplifies how supportive social neighborhood environments can mitigate such risks. At the same time, area affluence was associated with moderate PA, which has particularly strong benefits for persons living with dementia (Nuzum et al., 2020), showing how socioeconomic resources and amenities can enable more structured or sustained forms of activity. These findings underscore the importance of designing equitable, resource-rich environments that reduce barriers and expand access to PA for persons living with dementia.
