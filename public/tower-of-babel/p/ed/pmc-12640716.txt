Phage nuclease-mediated defense activation of the bacterial Retron-Eco7 toxin–antitoxin system
Authors: Zhikang Dai, Chang Liu, Yanjing Wang, Xueting Chen, Xiaofang Fu, Kaiyue Yang, Rui Zhu, Xianyue Jia, Yanke Chen, Pan Tao, Zeyuan Guan, Tingting Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRetrons are bacterial antiphage defense systems comprising a reverse transcriptase (RT), a non-coding RNA (ncRNA), and cognate effector proteins. The RT synthesizes multicopy single-stranded DNA (msDNA) from the ncRNA template to detect phage invasion. This study focuses on Retron-Eco7, which integrates retron-based sensing with the effector module of Septu—a characterized antiphage system in which the PtuAB complex mediates nuclease-dependent defense. However, the activation mechanism of this hybrid system remains unclear. Here, we determined cryo-electron microscopy structures of the RT–msDNA–PtuAB quaternary complex and the PtuAB binary complex in Retron-Eco7. Structural analyses reveal that the DNA stem-loop of msDNA extensively interacts with PtuA subunits via electrostatic interactions. We establish Retron-Eco7 as a novel toxin–antitoxin system, in which RT–msDNA acts as the antitoxin, directly binding and neutralizing the PtuAB toxin. Furthermore, we identified a phage-encoded flap endonuclease as a trigger for Retron-Eco7 activation, which cleaves msDNA to release the PtuAB toxin. Our findings demonstrate the diversity in bacterial retron defense systems and uncover a novel activation mechanism of the Septu-derived retron toxin–antitoxin system.

Introduction

Retrons are bacterial genetic elements encoding a reverse transcriptase (RT) and a structured non-coding RNA (ncRNA) [1, 2]. Retron RT initiates DNA synthesis on the ncRNA template flanked by inverted repeats (IRa1 and IRa2), using a branched guanosine residue’s 2′-OH group within the msr region as the primer. This forms a unique 2′,5′-phosphodiester bond covalently bridging the msrRNA to the nascent msdDNA strand [3–10]. During RT elongation along the msd template, host RNase H degrades the RNA component, inducing structural termination [8, 11]. The product is mature multicopy single-stranded DNA (msDNA), a msrRNA–msdDNA hybrid molecule featuring the 2′,5′-phosphodiester linkage [3, 10, 12]. In some retrons, msdDNA is liberated from msrRNA through cleavage by exonuclease VII composed of XseA and XseB subunits (Fig. 1A) [13–15]. Given the widespread distribution of retrons across diverse bacterial genomes, a general naming convention designates retrons using the genus initial, the first two letters of the species, and a sequential numeral indicating their discovery order within that species (e.g. Retron-Eco1 was the first retron identified in Escherichia coli) [16–18]. Although retrons were discovered in the 1980s and have been characterized biochemically since then, their physiological role remained enigmatic until 2020, when multiple retrons were shown to reside within bacterial defense islands, and, together with various accessory effector proteins, to confer resistance against diverse phages [19–21].

Recent studies have begun to illuminate the immune mechanisms underlying different retron systems. For instance, Retron-Eco6 (also known as Ec48) surveils the integrity of the host RecBCD complex, and, upon the inhibition of RecBCD by phage proteins (e.g. λ Gam or T7 gp5.9), triggers a membrane-disrupting effector to kill the cell and halt phage propagation [20]. Retron-Sen2 and Eco9 form tripartite, DNA-regulated toxin–antitoxin systems in which phage-encoded factors directly degrade or methylate msDNA to trigger toxin activation [22]. Retron-Eco1 assembles into supramolecular filaments comprising RT, msDNA, and its effector protein. Upon modification of the msDNA stem-loop by the phage-encoded DNA methyltransferase Dcm, these Eco1 filaments become activated and deplete host NAD⁺ to block phage infection [23–25]. A systematic screen further revealed that many retrons are triggered by phage-encoded DNA-binding proteins, such as single-stranded DNA-binding protein (SSB) and A1, suggesting that the retron-generated msDNA likely serves as a sensor of phage invasion (Supplementary Fig. S1A) [26]. Together, these findings establish that retron defense systems universally consist of two modules: an RT–msDNA sensor module and an effector module with diverse enzymatic activity, which may either be a standalone protein (or proteins) or an RT-fused domain [20, 27, 28]. However, the remarkable diversity of retron systems means that the precise mechanisms by which different systems detect and respond to phage invasion remain to be fully elucidated.

Retron-Eco4 and -Eco7 (also known as Ec83 and Ec78, respectively) encode, alongside their RT and msDNA, two accessory effector proteins: PtuA, an ATPase-domain-containing protein, and PtuB, an HNH endonuclease (Fig. 1A) [16, 20]. This mirrors the Septu antiphage system comprised of PtuA and PtuB [29]. Accordingly, PtuA-PtuB-associated retrons, including Retron-Eco4 and -Eco7, are also defined as type Ⅱ Septu system, representing ∼7.6% of known Septu systems (Supplementary Fig. S1B) [30]. Phylogenetic analysis further revealed substantial sequence variability among the RT, PtuA, and PtuB components derived from Retron-Eco4 and -Eco7 (Supplementary Fig. S1C–E). The PtuA and PtuB in the E. coli Septu system (EcSeptu) assemble into a 6:2 PtuA–PtuB complex that cleaves DNA upon activation [31]. Recent cryo-EM structural analysis of the Retron-Eco4 complex revealed that its activation involves disassembly of the complex, leading to the release of PtuAB, which cleaves single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) to execute antiphage defense [32]. The PtuAB complex associated with Retron-Eco7 has been shown to degrade bacterial tRNATyr, inducing abortive infection (Abi) and thereby defending against phage invasion [33].

Here, we dissect the antiphage defense mechanisms of Retron-Eco7 through structural and functional approaches. We determined the cryo-EM structures of the RT–msDNA–PtuAB quaternary complex in two forms (Form Ⅰ at a 2.5-Å resolution; Form Ⅱ at a 2.6-Å resolution) and the PtuAB binary complex at 3.3 Å resolution. These findings reveal that RT–msDNA directly interacts with PtuAB to constrain its toxicity. Through screening for phage mutants that can evade Retron-Eco7–mediated defense, we further identified three distinct phage triggers that activate Retron-Eco7 via cell toxicity assays. Chief among them is a T5-derived flap endonuclease (FEN), which cleaves the msDNA within the Retron-Eco7 quaternary complex. Our findings establish a unified retron-based toxin–antitoxin paradigm in which phage nuclease directly disrupts the RT–msDNA antitoxin complex, thereby unleashing a potent antiviral response.

Materials and methods

Plasmids and cloning

The Retron-Eco7 system was cloned from the isolated E. coli strain 42–1 [34], including its native promoter. The full fragment was inserted into pACYC184 via Gibson assembly to assay the antiphage ability of this defense system. For the Retron-Eco7 expression and purification, the coding sequence was polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-amplified and inserted into pET21b to generate a C-terminal 8× His-tagged fusion construct. Genes encoding phage proteins were PCR-amplified from phage genomic DNA and cloned into the arabinose-inducible pBAD vector for controlled heterologous expression. Site-directed mutations were introduced using overlapping PCR. All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). All constructed plasmids were successfully verified by sequencing before transformation.

Protein expression and purification

The recombinant plasmid pET21b-Retron-Eco7 was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) for protein expression. A single transformant colony was inoculated into 10 ml LB medium (supplemented with 100 µg ml−1 ampicillin) and grown overnight. The overnight culture was transferred into 1 l LB-ampicillin medium and incubated at 37°C with shaking at 210 rpm until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 1.0. The temperature was then lowered to 16°C. The protein expression was induced with 0.2 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactoside.

After 16 h of induction, cells were harvested by centrifugation. The pellet was resuspended in lysis buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM Phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF)) and lysed using a French press at 4°C. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation (14 000 × g, 1 h, 4°C). The supernatant was loaded onto a Ni2+-NTA resin column (Qiagen) pre-equilibrated with lysis buffer. The column was washed with wash buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 15 mM imidazole). The protein was eluted in Ni2+-NTA elution buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 250 mM imidazole). Subsequently, the Ni2+-NTA eluate was diluted and further purified by anion-exchange chromatography on a SOURCE™Q 10/100 column (GE Healthcare), pre-equilibrated with Buffer A (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 2 mM Dithiothreitol (DTT)), and eluted using a linear gradient of 25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 2 mM DTT, and 1 M NaCl. Target-containing fractions were pooled and concentrated, followed by SEC on a Superose™ 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare) in SEC buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM DTT). Elution profiles were monitored by UV absorbance at 260 and 280 nm. All samples were analyzed using SDS–PAGE and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data collection

A 3.5-µl sample at a concentration of 0.5 mg/ml was dropped onto glow-discharged holey carbon grids (Quantifoil Cu R1.2/1.3, 300 mesh), blotted for 3.5 s in 100% humidity at 8°C, and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using an FEI Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The prepared cryo-EM grids were stored in liquid nitrogen. Subsequently, the cryo-EM grids were screened using a 300 kV Titan Krios electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a Gatan K3 Summit detector positioned to post a GIF Quantum energy filter. Images of the complex were captured in the counting mode at a magnification of 105 000× with a pixel size of 0.824 Å. Each micrograph stack, which contains 40 frames, was exposed for 2.13 s with a total electron dose of 50 e−/Å2. Summit direct electron detector with a slit width of 15 eV on the energy filter was used with a preset defocus range from −1.2 to −1.8 µm. Micrographs were collected at a 30° specimen tilt. EPU v2.9 software was used for fully automated data collection.

Cryo-EM data processing

For the Retron-Eco7 complex, the data processing workflow is summarized in Supplementary Fig. S3. Initially, 7685 movies were collected and processed. Following motion correction and CTF estimation in cryoSPARC [35], 7439 micrographs were selected for subsequent processing. Using Auto-pick combined with Topaz train and Topaz extract [36], a total of 8 559 053 particles were selected for 2D and 3D classification. From these, 1 000 248 high-quality particles were chosen for further processing. During 3D classification, preferred-orientation issues were observed in the cryo-EM density maps. To address this orientation bias, an additional 3142 movies were collected with the specimen stage tilted at a 30° [37]. After processing, 319 416 particles were selected and combined with the previous 1 000 248 particles, yielding a total of 1 319 664 particles that were re-extracted in 320-pixel boxes for further 3D classification. Following 3D classification into 10 classes, four high-quality classes were selected for 3D refinement. This process ultimately yielded: a cryo-EM density map of the Retron-Eco7 complex (Form Ⅰ) at an overall resolution of 2.5 Å from 413 082 particles; a cryo-EM density map of the Retron-Eco7 complex (Form Ⅱ) at an overall resolution of 2.6 Å from 291 981 particles; and a cryo-EM density map of the RT–msDNA–PtuAB complex at an overall resolution of 2.6 Å from 175 722 particles; and a cryo-EM density map of the RT–msDNA–PtuA complex at an overall resolution of 2.6 Å from 189 612 particles.

For the PtuAB complex, the data processing workflow is summarized in Supplementary Fig. S9. Briefly, 1732 movies were collected with the specimen stage tilted at 30°, from which 1693 micrographs were selected. A total of 1 599 317 particles were picked and extracted for subsequent processing. After three rounds of 3D classification, 165 087 particles were selected for 3D refinement, yielding a cryo-EM density map of the PtuAB complex at an overall resolution of 3.3 Å. Resolution assessment for all cryo-EM density maps was based on gold-standard Fourier shell correlation [38]. The assessment of local resolution was conducted using cryoSPARC.

Model building

Atomic models of RT, PtuA, and PtuB proteins were predicted using AlphaFold3 [39] and fitted into the cryo-EM density map of the Retron-Eco7 complex (Form Ⅰ) using ChimeraX [40]. Multiple rounds of manual adjustment and optimization were performed in Coot [41], and atomic coordinates for the nucleic acids (msdDNA and msdRNA) were built de novo, resulting in the refined structure of the Retron-Eco7 complex (Form Ⅰ). Subsequently, the atomic coordinates of the Retron-Eco7 complex (Form Ⅱ) were built using a similar approach, with the msdDNA and msdRNA atomic coordinates extracted from the Form Ⅰ structure and docked into the Form Ⅱ cryo-EM density map to obtain the complete structure of the Retron-Eco7 complex (Form Ⅱ). Upon analysis, the RT–msDNA–PtuAB complex and RT–msDNA–PtuA complex cryo-EM density maps exhibited severe overfitting due to orientation bias; therefore, model building was not performed for these two density maps. The structure of the PtuAB complex was built using a similar approach. Structure refinement was carried out using real-space refinement with secondary structure and geometry restraints in PHENIX [42]. Model validation included MolProbity score analysis [43], Ramachandran statistics, and EMRinger assessment [44]. Structural figures were prepared using ChimeraX v1.2.5 and PyMOL v2.5.1.

Phylogenetic analysis

The RT, PtuA, and PtuB sequences of Septu and Retron-Eco7 systems were downloaded from the RefSeq database of DefenseFinder (https://defensefinder.mdmlab.fr/wiki/refseq) [45], using CD-HIT to deduce the redundant sequences based on 80% identity/coverage [46]. Finally, the homologous proteins were left for the construction of the phylogenetic tree. The multiple-sequence alignment was conducted using MAFFT version 7.310 with an automatic model [47], followed by automated alignment trimming using trimAl version 1.4 [48]. Subsequently, a phylogenetic tree based on the trimmed sequence alignment was constructed using IQ-TREE version 2.0.3 with parameters -m MFP (ModelFind plus) -bb 1000 (1000 iterations of the ultrafast bootstrap) [49]. The tree was visualized using iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/) [50].

Phage plaque assays

Retron-Eco7 antiphage activity was assessed by plaque assay. E. coli MG1655 strains expressing either empty vector, wild-type Retron-Eco7, or the corresponding mutants were cultured overnight in chloramphenicol (34 μg ml−1) MMB medium (LB medium supplemented with 5 mM MgCl₂, 0.1 mM MnCl₂) at 37°C with shaking at 210 rpm. Bacterial lawns were prepared by mixing 500 μl culture (OD ∼0.5) with 10 ml molten top agar (MMB + 0.45% agar + 34 μg ml−1 chloramphenicol) overlaid on MMB supplemented with 1.5% agar plates. Phage suspensions were serially 10-fold diluted in SM buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 8 mM MgSO4), with 2 μl spots applied per dilution. After 8 h of incubation at 37°C, the plaques were imaged and analyzed. The phages used in this study were obtained from the HZAU Phage Library (Huazhong Agricultural University, Wuhan, China). The genomic sequencing data have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive [51] in National Genomics Data Center [52], China National Center for Bioinformation/Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (GSA: CRA028549) that are publicly accessible at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa.

Isolation of mutant phages evading defense

Mutant phages that evade Retron-Eco7 defense were isolated through iterative plaque assays [26]. Bacterial lawns were prepared by mixing 500 μl Retron-Eco7-expressing cultures with 10 ml molten top agar (MMB + 0.5% agar + 34 μg ml−1 chloramphenicol), overlaid on bottom agar. Aliquots (2 μl) of 10-fold serially diluted phage suspensions were spotted onto lawns. Individual plaques from high-titer infections were picked into 500 μl SM buffer, incubated for 1 h at room temperature with vortex mixing every 10 min, and serially re-diluted. Plaque formed by high-titer phages was picked to repeat this cycle until the plaque phenotype on the plate containing the Retron-Eco7 system was consistent with that of the control group.

Single plaques underwent three additional purification rounds. Well-isolated plaques were excised into 50 μl SM buffer, incubated for 30 min at room temperature with vortex mixing every 10 min, and centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 2 min. For phage propagation, eluate (50 μl) was mixed with 200 μl E. coli MG1655 (OD600 ≈ 0.3) to achieve a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1, adsorbed statically at 37°C for 15 min, mixed with top agar, and plated. Following 8 h of incubation at 37°C for plaque development, 5 ml of SM buffer was carefully applied to cover the agar surface. Plates were maintained at 4°C overnight to allow passive phage diffusion into the buffer solution. The eluate was subsequently collected and sterile-filtered through 0.22 μm pore-size membrane filters.

Genome sequencing and analysis of phage mutants

Phage genomic DNA was extracted using the GreenRoot Biotech λ Phage Genome Extraction Kit with the following optimized protocol: 1 ml of phage lysate was treated with 1 μl RNase A (10 mg ml−1) and 1 μl DNase I (1 U μl−1), vortexed at maximum speed for 15 s, and incubated at 37°C for 30 min to degrade free nucleic acids. Then, 400 μl of phage precipitation solution was added, vortexed until fully dissolved, and incubated at 4°C overnight. Samples were centrifuged at 12 000 × g at 4°C for 20 min, followed by two washes with 200 μl SM buffer. Pellets were resuspended in 8 μl Phage Lysis Buffer, incubated at 68°C for 15 min, and then mixed with 200 μl Protein Precipitation Reagent. After centrifugation (12 000 × g, room temperature, 5 min), supernatants were combined with 600 μl pre-chilled 100% ethanol and incubated at −20°C for 1 h.

Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing (2 × 150 bp PE) was performed by Annoroad Gene Technology (Beijing) and Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai). BWA and SAMtools software were used with default parameters to map the mutant sequencing data to the wild-type genome, generating .bam and .bai files [53, 54]. Integrative Genomics Viewer software was then used to visually compare the sequences of the phage mutant and wild type, identifying mutation sites in the mutant phages [55]. The raw sequencing data of phage mutants have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive [51] in National Genomics Data Center [52], China National Center for Bioinformation/Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (GSA: CRA027650, CRA028453) that are publicly accessible at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa.

Toxicity assays

Mutant genes from escape phages were cloned into the pBAD expression vector under control of an arabinose-inducible promoter, with corresponding wild-type genes cloned in parallel as controls. These expression plasmids were co-transformed with either the Retron-Eco7 system or an empty vector control into E. coli MG1655. For the spot toxicity assay, single transformant colonies were inoculated into LB medium supplemented with 100 μg ml−1 ampicillin and 34 μg ml−1 chloramphenicol and grown at 37°C (210 rpm). When bacterial cultures reached an OD600 of ∼0.5, serial 10-fold dilutions were prepared in LB medium. Aliquots (2 μl) of each dilution were spotted onto LB-agar plates containing antibiotics and 0.2% (w/v) arabinose. Plates were incubated overnight at 37°C for 8 h, and then the plates were imaged.

For the growth curve analysis, overnight cultures were diluted 1:100 in fresh LB medium with antibiotics and grown at 37°C (210 rpm) until the bacterial cultures reached an OD600 of ∼0.3. Aliquots of 180 μl were transferred to a 96-well plate and supplemented with 20 μl of either arabinose solution with a 0.2% (w/v) final concentration or glucose solution with a 1% (w/v) final concentration, respectively. The plate was immediately placed in an automated system (FLUOstar Omega) maintained at 37°C (210 rpm) with continuous shaking. Post-induction OD600 measurements were recorded every 6 min.

msDNA isolation and detection

Co-transferring Retron-Eco7 (ncRNA-RT-PtuA-PtuBH57A) constructed on pACYC plasmid and pBAD plasmids (containing wild-type FEN, mutant FEN, or empty vector) into E. coli MG1655 strain to detect whether msDNA was cleaved by FEN. One percent of the overnight bacterial liquid was inoculated in the 15 ml LB supplemented with 100 μg ml−1 ampicillin and 34 μg ml−1 chloramphenicol, and incubated for 3 h at 37°C with shaking at 210 rpm. When bacteria reached an OD600 of 0.6, cultures were induced by the addition of 0.2% arabinose and incubated for a further 2 h. After this, the DNA was extracted from the above sample using the TIANprep Mini Plasmid Kit (TIANGEN; DP103). The purified nucleic acids containing msDNA were resolved by denaturing urea polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Urea-PAGE, 15%) with 1× TBE buffer (180 V, 1 h), and the results were visualized using the Luna Ⅱ gel imaging system after staining with GelRed.

In vitro characterization of msDNA cleaved by FEN using purified 0.1 μM Eco7 tetrameric complex protein as nucleic acid substrate. The Eco7 complex was incubated with 0.1 μM FEN protein and its mutants in 10 μl reaction buffer (25 mM, pH 9.0 Tris–HCl, 50 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 2 mM DTT) at 37°C for 30 min and terminated by adding 5 μl loading (90% formamide, 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 20 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, pH 8.0, 0.05% bromophenol blue, and 0.05% xylene cyanol). DNase I was used as a positive control to cleave the Retron-Eco7 complex using 1 mg ml−1. The reaction was subsequently analyzed using denaturing 15% Urea-PAGE electrophoresis with 1× TBE buffer (180 V, 1 h), and the results were visualized using the Luna II gel imaging system after staining with GelRed.

Fluorescence microscopy

Overnight cultures of E. coli MG1655 expressing Retron-Eco7 or mutant constructs were diluted 1:100 in fresh medium and grown at 37°C to an OD600 of 0.3–0.4. Cultures (1 ml) were infected with phage at MOI = 5. Aliquots were collected at 0, 10, and 30 mpi, pelleted by centrifugation (3300 × g, 30 s, 25°C), and resuspended in 60 μl phosphate-buffered saline. Samples were co-stained with 57 μM FM4-64 (membrane dye; Thermo Fisher) and 20 μM DAPI (4',6-Diamidino-2-Phenylindole; DNA stain; Sigma). One microliter aliquot of stained cell suspension was examined using Leica TCS STED super-resolution microscope (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Germany) equipped with HC PL APO CS2 100×/1.40 oil objective (numerical aperture = 1.40). Emission was detected with a PMT detector. For each field of view, z-stacks consisting of 3–4 optical sections were acquired to reconstruct three-dimensional cellular architecture. Image acquisition was controlled by LAS X imaging software with LIGHTNING real-time super-resolution processing enabled for enhanced resolution. For each condition, at least three fields from each of two independent experiments were manually scored (n = 240–524 cells for each condition). Raw image data were analyzed using ImageJ and GraphPad Prism. Statistical significance was determined by repeated-measures one-way analysis of variance followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test.

Results

Antiphage activity and assembly of Retron-Eco7

Retron-Eco7 from E. coli 42–1 comprises a retron RT, a cognate 144-nt ncRNA containing msr and msd regions (while Retron-Eco4 carries a 167-nt ncRNA), and two accessory effector proteins, PtuA and PtuB (Fig. 1A) [34]. To evaluate the antiphage activity of Retron-Eco7, the E. coli MG1655 expressing this system was challenged with a diverse panel of phages. As a result, Retron-Eco7 conferred robust resistance specifically against phages belonging to the Tequintavirus genus (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S2A). Point mutations on the catalytic residues of the PtuA ATPase domain (D395A) and the PtuB HNH nuclease domain (H57A) completely abolished antiphage activity, indicating these effector proteins are essential (Fig. 1C). Attempts to disrupt the catalytic residues of RT or the critical branched guanosine in the ncRNA repeatedly yielded constructs harboring unintended mutations in PtuA (Supplementary Fig. S2B and C). This observation is consistent with an independent study [56].

To investigate the molecular architecture of ncRNA, RT, and effector proteins in Retron-Eco7, we co-expressed these components in E. coli BL21 and purified the Retron-Eco7 complex. SEC revealed the formation of a stable complex containing RT, PtuA, and PtuB (Fig. 1D and E). Urea-PAGE analysis confirmed the presence of msDNA within this complex. DNase Ⅰ and RNase A treatments of the purified complex confirmed the presence of both DNA and RNA, indicating that these nucleic acids (msdDNA and msrRNA) associate noncovalently within the assembly (Fig. 1E and F).

Structure of the Retron-Eco7 defense complex

To further elucidate the structural basis underlying Retron-Eco7-mediated phage defense, we performed cryo-EM analysis of the purified complex and solved two conformational states at resolutions of 2.5 Å (Form Ⅰ) and 2.6 Å (Form Ⅱ), respectively (Supplementary Fig. S3 and Supplementary Table S1). Both maps contain one RT, one msDNA, four PtuA subunits, and one PtuB protein (1:1:4:1 stoichiometry), spanning ∼146 Å × 115 Å × 70 Å (Fig. 2A and B). The principal difference lies in the orientation of PtuB relative to the PtuA assembly (Fig. 2A and B; Supplementary Fig. S4A); we thus focused on the higher-resolution form Ⅰ for detailed structural investigation.

RT, PtuA, PtuB, and msDNA were respectively shown in colorful cartoons. (C) A schematic, drawn according to the cryo-EM structure, shows the assembly of the Retron-Eco7 complex. (D) Superposition of the PtuAB in the Retron-Eco7 quaternary complex with that from EcSeptu (PDB code: 8EE7, RMSD of 3.898 over 536 Cα atoms).

The overall architecture of Retron-Eco7 adopts a butterfly-shaped fold comprising two asymmetric wings: one wing is a PtuA-PtuB heterotrimer (2:1 stoichiometry), in which the C-terminal triple-helix domain (CTD, residues 480–551) of the PtuA dimers scaffolds PtuB binding; the other wing consists of RT component and a PtuA dimer; here, the CTD of PtuA and its associated PtuB are invisible, likely due to flexibility (Fig. 2A–C). This observation suggests that PtuB binding may stabilize the CTD of PtuA, analogous to findings in the reported EcSeptu system. The DNA stem loop (DSL) region of msDNA threads through two PtuA dimers, bridging the RT-containing moiety and the PtuA-PtuB effector module (Fig. 2A–C). Structural superposition of Retron-Eco7 PtuAB with its EcSeptu counterpart revealed conservation (RMSD of 3.90 Å over 536 Cα atoms) in the PtuAB complex, suggesting evolutionary homology between PtuABs in Retron-Eco7 and Septu antiphage systems (Fig. 2D). Additionally, we performed a structural comparison between the Retron-Eco7 and Retron-Eco4 systems. We found that the RT-msDNA in Retron-Eco4 stably binds two PtuAB complexes. In contrast, our Retron-Eco7 structure reveals that only a single PtuB protomer contacts the PtuAC and PtuAD subunits, with the C-terminal segments of PtuAA and PtuAB remaining disordered and unmodeled. Comparative analysis revealed structural conservation in the RT (RMSD of 2.11 Å over 256 Cα atoms) but a shifted relative orientation of the msDNA. Furthermore, within the PtuAB complexes, PtuA shows little conformational change, while PtuB undergoes a significant shift (RMSD of 3.71 Å over 376 Cα atoms). Notably, the PtuA subunits in Retron-Eco4 contain two distinctive inserted motifs not observed in Retron-Eco7: an N-terminal triple α-helical bundle and an α-helical hairpin motif located within the ATPase domain (Supplementary Fig. S4B–D).

During image processing, we also observed particles in which RT–msDNA recruits only one side of the fully assembled complex, revealing the critical role of RT–msDNA complex in Retron-Eco7 assembly (Supplementary Fig. S4E and F). Based on these structural observations, we propose a putative assembly process for Retron-Eco7: RT reverse-transcribes and loads msDNA, forming an RT-msDNA complex, which interacts with two pre-formed PtuA dimers. One PtuA dimer then recruits PtuB, ultimately culminating in the full Retron-Eco7 complex.

The architecture of RT–msDNA in Retron-Eco7 complex

The RT in Retron-Eco7 (RT-Eco7) adopts a canonical right-handed-like fold, containing 13 α-helices and 6 β-strands with conserved fingers, palm, and thumb domains (Fig. 3A and B). DALI-based structural alignment demonstrates significant homology to diverse prokaryotic RT families, including retrons, diversity-generating retroelements (DGRs), defense-associated RTs, group Ⅱ introns, CRISPR-associated RTs (CART), and abortive infection (Abi) systems (Supplementary Fig. S5A) [57–65]. Structural alignment indicates that CART and Abi-P2 RT, which exhibit divergent topologies due to thumb domain deletions and Abi-P2 featuring a helical domain replacement (Supplementary Fig. S5B–I). RT-Eco7 exhibits the highest structural similarity to DGRs among non-retron RTs (Supplementary Fig. S5D). Some RTs fused with other domains, such as group Ⅱ introns, have a DNA endonuclease domain (Supplementary Fig. S5F).

In the quaternary complex, both msdDNA and msrRNA wrap around the positively charged surface of RT (Fig. 3C). The msdDNA structure comprises two single-stranded regions (ssDa, ssDb), a DSL, and the DNA component of a DNA–RNA duplex. Conversely, msrRNA contains inverted repeats (IRa1/IRa2), a branched guanosine (rG9), two single-stranded regions (ssRa, ssRb), an RNA stem-loop (RSL), and the RNA component of the duplex (Fig. 3D and E). Notably, the msDNA of Retron-Eco7 has been reported to be cleaved between the fourth and fifth deoxyribonucleotides at the 5′-end of msdDNA, separating the ssDNA segment from RNA. Urea-PAGE analysis has shown the noncovalent nucleic acid associations in the complex (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, our structural data confirmed this cleavage: the 5′-TTGA-3′ terminus and 2′,5′-phosphodiester linkage at rG9 are absent, while the msrRNA 5′-terminal region (including IRs, branched rG9, and ssRa) remains unresolved due to conformational flexibility (Fig. 3E).

The DNA–RNA duplex within the RT catalytic core comprises base-paired regions of msdDNA and msrRNA (Fig. 3F). The highly conserved catalytic core YADD motif (residues 185–188), located at the loop between β4 and β5, anchors the 3′-terminus of the msdDNA (Fig. 3B and F). Two conserved motifs, NAxxH (residues 82–86) at α2 helix and VTG (residues 236–238) at the loop between β6 and α10, flank the YADD active site and likely stabilize it to promote catalysis (Fig. 3B and F). rU33, the next nucleotide to be reverse transcribed, flips out and forms hydrogen bond interactions with Y32 located at the β1 of RT, stabilizing the conformation (Fig. 3G). Structural alignment of RT–msDNA complexes in Retron-Eco7 and Eco1 reveals highly conserved architectures (RMSD of 3.27 Å over 248 Cα atoms) (Supplementary Fig. S6A). Although RT-Eco1 exhibits N-terminal α-helix-mediated dimerization through its α1 helix, this feature is absent in RT-Eco7 (Supplementary Fig. S6B). The 3′-terminal msrRNA regions in Retron-Eco7 align well with these regions in Retron-Eco1, whereas the orientation of DSL regions is quite different (Supplementary Fig. S6C–E). Notably, the conserved msrRNA–RT binding interface where RSL tightly associates with the thumb domain, confirming RSL’s essential role in RT recognition. (Supplementary Fig. S6F–H).

RT–msDNA antitoxin binds and neutralizes PtuAB toxin

Within the Retron-Eco7 complex, the interactions between RT–msDNA and PtuABs are mainly mediated by msDNA binding to PtuA subunits. The DSL region of msdDNA binds two PtuA dimers through electrostatic interactions with their positively charged surfaces (Fig. 4A). Specifically, an interaction network was identified between PtuAC residues (R126, R163, R171, S174, K177, and R180) and base-paired nucleotides dA6-dA11 and dT55-dA60. Additionally, PtuAC R238 forms a hydrogen bond with dC17. PtuAA, PtuAB, and PtuAD also exhibit interactions with msDNA, although their contact patterns differ significantly. Compared to PtuAB, the msdDNA terminus is more oriented toward PtuAD, forming broader interactions (Fig. 4B–E). Mutations in the PtuA (R163A/R171A/R180A and S174A/K177A/R180A) completely abolished the antiphage activity of Retron-Eco7, whereas the R221A/R222A/R238A mutation had no significant effect on the phage resistance (Fig. 4F). These findings indicate that the assembly of the Retron-Eco7 complex primarily depends on the interactions between msdDNA and PtuAA, PtuAC, or PtuAD units, and this assembly is essential for the antiphage function of Retron-Eco7.

This structural architecture demonstrates that the RT–msDNA complex recruits the Septu effector module, where the PtuAB complex exhibits nuclease activity to execute antiphage functions in a standalone Septu system [31, 32]. Analogous to Retron-Sen2 [22], we hypothesize that in Retron-Eco7, the RT–msDNA acts as an antitoxin by suppressing PtuAB’s nuclease activity through the formation of an autoinhibited complex (Fig. 4G). To directly assess PtuAB’s toxic effect, we expressed the complex in E. coli cells and monitored bacterial growth. Our results demonstrated that the PtuAB complex significantly inhibits colony formation, while the Retron-Eco7 complex, PtuA alone, or PtuB alone does not. Growth inhibition was abolished by active-site mutations in PtuA or PtuB, suggesting that PtuA and PtuB function cooperatively (Fig. 4H). Collectively, our findings establish Retron-Eco7 as a toxin–antitoxin (TA) system, with the RT–msDNA functioning as the antitoxin that neutralizes PtuAB’s toxicity (Fig. 4G).

The interface between RT and PtuA also contributes to the complex formation. Specifically, the PtuAA–RT interface is mainly mediated by (PtuA residue-RT residue) K3-D270/E272, Y5-K36, E19-T39, and R172-E98 interactions. The interactions between the PtuAA and msrRNA are mediated by K8-rU30 and R67-rA28 (Supplementary Fig. S7A and B). We performed alanine substitutions of these interface residues and evaluated the antiphage activity of these Retron-Eco7 mutants. The PtuA K3A/Y5A/E19A mutant exhibited slightly reduced antiphage activity. No significant impact was observed for the PtuA mutation (R172A) and RT mutations (T39A, E98A, and D270A/E272A) (Supplementary Fig. S7C). These results suggest that the association of RT and PtuA is not required for the defense.

In the Retron-Eco7 complex structure, three ATP molecules were observed in three PtuA molecules: two at the PtuAA–PtuAB dimer interface, and one at the PtuAC–PtuAD dimer interface (Supplementary Fig. S8A–C). All three ATP molecules interact with conserved N135 residues (Supplementary Fig. S8D–F). Notably, the PtuAC–PtuAD dimer binds PtuB at its C-terminal domain, while the PtuAA–PtuAB dimer lacks PtuB association. Structural superposition revealed PtuAC shifts outward relative to PtuAA when aligning PtuAB and PtuAD, suggesting PtuB binding correlates with ATP-bound states (Supplementary Fig. S8G–I).

Structure and function of PtuAB effector in Retron-Eco7

To understand the molecular mechanism by which the PtuAB effector functions in antiphage defense, we performed cryo-EM analysis of the PtuAB complex. Co-expression of wild-type PtuA and PtuB is unfeasible due to cellular toxicity (Fig. 4H). Instead, we successfully isolated the PtuAB complex during ion-exchange chromatography purification of the reconstituted Retron-Eco7 complex. SEC analysis confirmed a stable PtuAB binary complex formation (Supplementary Fig. S9A and B). Consequently, we determined the cryo-EM structure of PtuAB complex (2:1 stoichiometry) at a resolution of 3.3 Å (Fig. 5A and B; Supplementary Fig. S9C–E and Supplementary Table S1). The structure reveals that the CTD regions of the PtuA dimer scaffold a single PtuB molecule, consistent with the feature observed in one side of the RT–msDNA–PtuAB complex. Superposition of the structure of the PtuAB complex with that of the RT–msDNA–PtuAB complex exhibits a high similarity in the PtuAB region (RMSD of 1.408 over 368 Cα atoms), suggesting dissociation of the full Retron-Eco7 complex (Fig. 5C). The PtuA–PtuB interface is primarily stabilized by an extensive network of hydrogen bonds and salt bridges (Fig. 5D). Specifically, the hydrogen bonds are formed by the side chains of PtuAC (residue S530) and the backbone chains of PtuB (residue A165), as well as between the side chains of PtuAD (residues E471) and the side chains of PtuB (residues R160, E178, E182). Additionally, salt bridges are formed between the side chains of PtuAC (residues K526, R533, R545) and the side chains of PtuB (residues E179, D191, E192, E200), and between the side chains of PtuAD (residues E471, K540, R545) and the side chains of PtuB (residues R160, E178, E182) (Fig. 5E and F). We generated alanine substitution mutants to investigate the functional significance of the above-mentioned residues in Retron-Eco7-mediated antiphage activity. The PtuA mutant (K538A/K540A/R545A) and PtuB mutants (R160A, E178A/E179A/E182A, D191A/E192A/E200A) completely abolished the phage defense ability of Retron-Eco7, highlighting the essential role of PtuA–PtuB interactions in antiphage defense (Fig. 5G).

We then employed the in vivo fluorescence microscopy assay to characterize the function of Retron-Eco7 during phage infection. In uninfected cells, staining with FM4-64 (membrane) and DAPI (DNA) revealed normal cellular morphology in both E. coli MG1655 expressing wild-type or mutant Retron-Eco7. However, upon phage infection, bacterial cells expressing Retron-Eco7 underwent progressive DNA loss until most cells became “phantom cells,” which were devoid of visible DNA by 30 min post-infection (mpi). In contrast, cells expressing mutant Retron-Eco7 systems maintained their DNA integrity (Fig. 5H). Microscopy data were quantified by measuring the cross-sectional areas of DAPI-stained DNA under each experimental condition. Bacterial cells expressing Retron-Eco7 showed a median DNA cross-sectional area of <0.15 μm² at 30 mpi. Statistical analysis revealed a significant reduction compared to cells harboring inactive mutant systems (Fig. 5I). Given the known Abi mechanism of Retron-Eco7, which halts phage propagation by inducing premature cell death, we thus speculate that phage invasion may trigger Retron-Eco7 activation for indiscriminate genomic DNA degradation, inducing Abi for antiphage defense.

Phage-encoded nuclease cleaves msDNA to activate Retron-Eco7

To further decipher the activation mechanism of Retron-Eco7, we isolated phage mutants capable of evading defense and identified the phage triggers that activate Retron-Eco7 (Fig. 6A). T5-like HZA24 and HZA33, Vequintavirinae HZA56, and Dhillonvirus phage98, were selected for the mutant screen. Whole-genome sequencing pinpointed distinct candidate triggers: HZA24 and HZA33 (T5-like phages) both carried mutations in a flap endonuclease (hereafter named this trigger as “FEN” for clarity) [66–68], while HZA56 and phage98 each possessed mutations in unique genes encoding proteins of unknown function (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. S10A–C). Expression of each wild-type phage trigger—but not its escape variant— in E. coli MG1655 cells elicited pronounced growth arrest, confirming their role in activating Retron-Eco7 defense (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S10D–F). Given that FEN exhibits the 5′-exonucleolytic and structure-specific endonucleolytic hydrolysis of DNA-branched nucleic acid molecules. Among defense-inactivating FEN mutations (T152I, K83N, F32S, D130E, R75L), residues K83 and D130 have been demonstrated to be crucial for the nuclease activity (Fig. 6B and C) [68, 69]. We hypothesized that FEN activates Retron-Eco7 by cleaving msDNA within the retron complex. Indeed, expressing wild-type FEN in E. coli MG1655 containing Retron-Eco7 eliminated the msdDNA in vivo, whereas expression of catalytically inactive FEN mutants failed to do so (Fig. 6D). Moreover, purified wild-type FEN rapidly degraded msdDNA within the RT–msDNA–PtuAB complex, whereas mutant FEN (K83N, D130E, F32S, T152I) proteins exhibited greatly reduced cleavage in vitro (Fig. 6E). Taken together, we identified multiple phage-encoded proteins that trigger the activation of Retron-Eco7. Among these, FEN from T5-like phages directly disrupts the msDNA component of the retron complex to initiate defense (Fig. 6F).

Discussion

In the perpetual arms race between bacteria and bacteriophages, bacteria have evolved sophisticated defense mechanisms [18, 70–72]. Our work characterized Retron-Eco7 as a chimeric defense system that functionally integrates retron-based sensing with Septu effector machinery into a toxin–antitoxin (TA) module. Cryo-EM and functional analyses demonstrated that the RT–msDNA complex functions as an antitoxin to inhibit the toxicity of the PtuAB effector. Phage infection triggers defense activation via diverse viral proteins, including a T5-encoded FEN that specifically cleaves msDNA to disrupt antitoxin sequestration. This liberation of PtuAB nuclease activity induces abortive infection, ultimately blocking phage propagation through population-level protection. (Figure 6F).

TA systems constitute a major class of antiphage defense mechanisms, primarily operating through Abi to sacrifice infected cells for population protection [73–78]. Antitoxin constitutively neutralizes its cognate toxin, deploying diverse activation strategies when phages attack [78]. For example, DarTG, CapRel, PARIS, and CmdTAC employ protein antitoxins that directly inhibit toxins, where DarTG activation liberates DarT to ADP-ribosylate phage ssDNA yet its triggering mechanism remains unclear [79, 80]; CapRel undergoes autoinhibition relief via binding of phage-encoded major capsid protein or Gp54 to its C-terminal sensor domain, enabling 3′-end pyrophosphorylation of transfer RNA (tRNA) that blocks translation [81, 82]; PARIS utilizes AriA antitoxin sequestration of AriB toxin until phage Ocr protein binding induces ATP hydrolysis-dependent conformational changes for toxin release [83, 84]; and CmdTAC responds to T4 capsid protein (Gp23) binding its chaperone CmdC, triggering ClpP-mediated degradation of antitoxin CmdA and liberation of CmdT toxin, which ADP-ribosylates adenine at GA dinucleotides in single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) to arrest messenger RNA translation [85]. Alternatively, ToxIN represents a type Ⅲ TA system, which employs toxI-encoded antitoxic RNA to suppress ToxN ribonuclease [86]. The Retron-Sen2 system demonstrates the conserved tripartite architecture of bacterial retrons wherein an RT–msDNA antitoxin complex represses the RcaT toxin [22]. Phage invasion activates this system through either Dam methyltransferase-mediated msDNA methylation or RecE exonuclease-driven msDNA degradation, disrupting the antitoxin complex to activate RcaT toxin [22]. Our work establishes that Retron-Eco7 utilizes a similar defense paradigm, with its RT–msDNA complex maintaining constitutive inhibition of the effector nuclease until phage-induced msDNA cleavage triggers toxin activation. These findings suggest that retrons as precisely regulated TA defense modules with conserved activation mechanisms (Fig. 4G and H).

Retron-Eco7 represents an evolutionarily converged immune strategy, unifying retron-based sensing and Septu effector functions within a single TA cassette. It provides a paradigm for modular recombination of sensor-effector domains in Abi systems, enabling bacteria to rapidly restore defensive efficacy against phage escape mechanisms targeting specific defense components [28, 87]. Reported ATP binding inhibits the nuclease activity of the PtuAB complex across Septu systems, suggesting RT–msDNA recruitment evolved as a dual-inhibition safeguard preventing cytotoxic autoactivation [31]. Functionally, PtuAB nuclease mediates tRNATyr depletion in Retron-Eco7, cleaves ssDNA in Retron-Eco4, and nicks plasmid in standalone Septu systems, exhibiting context-dependent substrate specificity [31–33]. This functional divergence may be attributed to evolutionary variations among PtuAB homologs. Despite extensive efforts, we were unable to reconstitute PtuAB’s nuclease activity in vitro. However, in vivo fluorescence microscopy analysis reveals that phage infection triggers genomic DNA degradation in host cells containing Retron-Eco7, leading to Abi for defense (Fig. 5H and I). Further biochemical analyses are needed to elucidate the precise mechanism of PtuAB-mediated toxicity in Retron-Eco7 system.

Recently, Wang et al. revealed that Retron-Eco4 employs an “arrest-and-release” activation mechanism analogous to Retron-Eco7, but its phage-sensing trigger remains unknown [32]. Through phage defense escape screening, we identified T5 FEN as a Retron-Eco7 trigger, demonstrating that FEN-mediated cleavage of msDNA releases the toxin activity for defense (Fig. 6). Furthermore, we discovered two additional phage-encoded proteins capable of activating the Retron-Eco7 (Supplementary Fig. S10). This multi-trigger activation strategy was observed in a few defense systems, which may enhance their capacity for broad-spectrum phage resistance [82, 88]. Increasing evidence highlights the essential role of RT and its cognate ncRNA in antiphage defense [89, 90]. In retron defense systems, the msDNA synthesized by RT using the ncRNA template typically serves as a molecular sensor to trigger defense activation [28]. Recent studies reveal diverse phage-sensing mechanisms wherein phage-encoded proteins disrupt msDNA, including nuclease-mediated enzymatic cleavage executed by RecE and T5 FEN, alongside Dam and Dcm methyltransferase-catalyzed site-specific methylation, while SSB functions via direct binding [20, 22, 26]. Taken together, these findings reinforce the critical role of msDNA as a central regulatory hub for activating retron-mediated immunity.

Notably, in the Retron-Eco7 quaternary complex, the PtuAC–PtuAD dimer recruits PtuB, while the PtuAA–PtuAB dimer lacks the binding of PtuB. This observed loss may be attributed to the flexibility of the PtuA CTD region. In contrast, the Retron-Eco4 quaternary complex exhibits an assembly containing two complete PtuAB effector modules with no loss of PtuB. The possible reason for such difference might be that ATP molecules in PtuA dimers may affect its binding to PtuB (Supplementary Fig. S8G–I). However, the binding pocket of PtuA dimers in the Retron-Eco4 complex is occupied by ADP rather than ATP [32]. Several recent studies have reported that ATP containing PtuA dimers facilitate asymmetric PtuB binding, whereas ADP containing PtuA dimers enable binding of two PtuB subunits [56, 91, 92]. Based on these findings, we speculated that ADP occupancy in the nucleotide-binding sites of PtuA might improve structural stability of the complex.

Beyond their role in bacterial immunity, retrons represent powerful tools for precision genome engineering due to their unique ability to produce multiple ssDNA via reverse transcription [93–96]. Our structural elucidation of the Retron-Eco7 complex provides a framework for rational engineering of retron-based editing systems.
