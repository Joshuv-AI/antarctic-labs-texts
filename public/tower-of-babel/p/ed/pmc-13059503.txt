Successful prehospital resuscitation of a young female patient with myasthenia gravis experiencing cardiopulmonary arrest due to complete airway obstruction: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Ziliang Ruan, Tao Wang, Wen Luo, Yubo Xia, Xiaohan Zhou, Yuwei Tian, Kaiyun Li, Yuan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients with myasthenia gravis are at high risk of fatal airway complications due to impaired swallowing mechanics. We present a prehospital resuscitation case involving complete airway obstruction leading to cardiopulmonary arrest in a young patient with myasthenia gravis.Case presentationA 21-year-old Han Chinese female patient with a reported history of myasthenia gravis developed sudden respiratory arrest during oral intake. The diagnosis of myasthenia gravis was initially provided by on-scene witnesses (classmates), and later confirmed by family who reported her medication regimen. Prehospital interventions—including modified Heimlich maneuvers, rapid endotracheal intubation, and advanced cardiac life support—achieved return of spontaneous circulation within 10 minutes. Persistent hypoxia and hemodynamic instability were managed during transport. The patient was transferred to the hospital intensive care unit but subsequently died due to the consequences of prolonged cerebral hypoxia.DiscussionMyasthenia-gravis-associated bulbar weakness predisposes patients to catastrophic aspiration events. This case highlights three critical prehospital strategies: (1) modified supine-position Heimlich maneuvers for intubated arrest patients, (2) time-critical airway clearance using direct laryngoscopy, and (3) multidisciplinary coordination between telemedicine dispatchers and field responders.ConclusionOur findings highlights the unique challenges of managing airway obstruction in patients with myasthenia gravis and proposes an integrated prehospital strategy for airway management. This case underscores that successful prehospital return of spontaneous circulation is possible in patients with myasthenia gravis with acute airway obstruction; however, prevention remains paramount due to the risk of fatal outcomes. It emphasizes the need for integrated prehospital strategies, patient education on aspiration risk, and protocolized airway algorithms for neuromuscular disorders.Keywords: Myasthenia gravis, Complete foreign body airway obstruction, Cardiopulmonary arrest, Heimlich maneuver, Prehospital emergency care

Introduction

Myasthenia gravis (MG), an immune-mediated disorder impairing neuromuscular transmission, affects 150–250 per million globally [1, 2]. Recent epidemiological data indicate rising incidence rates worldwide (4–10 per million annually), with China reporting 0.68 cases per 100,000 population [3, 4].

Despite advancements in treatment, MG remains a chronic condition requiring long-term management, with a heightened focus on patient education and preventive care [5]. Patients with MG are prone to aspiration during eating or drinking due to weakness in the pharyngeal and respiratory muscles, which can rapidly progress to complete airway obstruction and cardiopulmonary arrest [6, 7]. Studies indicate that acute respiratory failure during myasthenic crisis carries 15–45% mortality without immediate intervention [7].

Case presentation

A 21-year-old Han Chinese female university student collapsed while eating. Witnesses reported abrupt coughing followed by cyanosis and loss of consciousness. Bystanders initiated back blows without relief and emergency medical services (EMS) were alerted, and telephone-assisted cardiopulmonary resuscitation (T-CPR) was initiated. The on-scene witnesses reported the patient had a history of myasthenia gravis (MG). After hospital transfer, communication with the family confirmed she was on a daily regimen of pyridostigmine and low-dose prednisone. More specific details regarding her MG subtype, disease duration, and antibody status were not available to our prehospital team.

Upon arrival, the patient exhibited fixed dilated pupils, absent carotid pulse, and no spontaneous breathing. The prehospital diagnosis included: (1) cardiopulmonary arrest; (2) complete airway obstruction; and (3) underlying MG.

Post-ROSC management and outcome

Sustained hypotension (Mean arterial pressure 50–60 mmHg) managed with crystalloid bolus. Transport metrics: SpO2 stabilized above 92% (FiO21.0), sinus tachycardia (120–150 beats per minute) (as shown in Fig. 2). The patient was transferred to the receiving hospital for advanced care. As the prehospital care team, our detailed knowledge of the in-hospital course is limited. We were informed that the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit but unfortunately succumbed due to the consequences of prolonged cerebral hypoxia following the arrest. Discussion

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is characterized by skeletal muscle fatigue and weakness, with clinical manifestations varying significantly depending on the affected muscle groups [8, 9]. When the masticatory and pharyngeal muscles are involved, patients may experience chewing weakness, dysphagia, and slurred speech [10]. During eating, impaired swallowing function in patients with MG increases the risk of aspiration [11]; in such cases, weakened respiratory muscles further compromise the ability to generate effective cough forces, exacerbating the obstruction and accelerating clinical deterioration [12]. Respiratory muscle involvement, a hallmark of myasthenic crisis, is particularly fatal, which can rapidly escalate to complete airway obstruction and cardiopulmonary arrest—a life-threatening emergency with high mortality rate if untreated [13, 14].

Complete airway obstruction demands immediate intervention due to its rapid progression and catastrophic consequences [15, 16]. For patients with MG, even transient hypoxia from airway occlusion can trigger severe cardiac dysrhythmias or asystole [17, 18]. In this case, the primary event was an acute, complete airway obstruction from a food bolus, directly leading to cardiopulmonary arrest. This must be distinguished from a primary myasthenic crisis characterized by progressive respiratory muscle failure, although both represent life-threatening emergencies in patients with MG.

The Heimlich maneuver, first described by Henry Heimlich in 1974 [19], is critical for dislodging airway obstructions. In this case, the prehospital emergency medical team attempted to employ the modified Heimlich maneuver (supine abdominal thrusts) as an initial measure to relieve obstruction during CPR, subsequently performing tracheal intubation to secure airway patency. This strategy aligns with recent guidelines emphasizing rapid airway control in neuromuscular disorders [20, 21]. Notably, traditional upright Heimlich maneuvers may be less feasible in unconscious or arrested patients, underscoring the need for protocol adaptations in prehospital settings [22]. We acknowledge that abdominal thrusts carry a risk of visceral injury and their use in cardiac arrest must be carefully considered. In this instance, definitive relief of the obstruction was achieved by direct removal of the foreign material under laryngoscopic visualization prior to intubation.

In this case, the modified technique (supine abdominal thrusts) proved effective. Combined with early endotracheal intubation and high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation, this strategy underscores the importance of personalized airway management for patients with myasthenia gravis. It further demonstrates that successful prehospital emergency care hinges upon rapid assessment, teamwork, strict adherence to protocols, and dynamic, flexible adjustment of airway management strategies [23].

Conclusion

This report details the first prehospital resuscitation of a Han Chinese female patient with MG with complete airway obstruction. While prehospital ROSC was successfully achieved, the patient’s ultimate in-hospital death highlights the severe consequences of prolonged hypoxia and the critical importance of prevention in this vulnerable population. It highlights the need for dynamic swallowing evaluations [27], patient education, and standardized prehospital protocols. Current MG management guidelines focus predominantly on pharmacological and inpatient care [5]. However, our findings underscore the need for dynamic swallowing evaluations, patient and caregiver education on aspiration prevention, and the development of standardized prehospital protocols for airway emergencies in patients with neuromuscular disorders. Proactive measures, including texture-modified diets and mealtime supervision, could mitigate aspiration risks in high-risk populations [28].

However, the limitations of this study include its single-center design and lack of long-term follow-up data, which may affect generalizability. Future research should explore standardized prehospital processes for neuromuscular emergencies and evaluate their impact on survival rates across diverse healthcare settings.
