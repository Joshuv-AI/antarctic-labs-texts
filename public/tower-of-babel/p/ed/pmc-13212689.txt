Intelligent irrigation management system for arid and semi-arid regions under climate change
Authors: Somayeh Emami, Hossein Dehghanisanij, Hojjat Emami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In arid and semi-arid regions, optimal irrigation management requires a detailed and integrated understanding of the interactions among climate, soil, and crop physiology on a daily time scale. In this study, an intelligent system for determining irrigation timing and depth was developed by integrating actual and predicted climate data with a physical water–soil–plant model, and its efficiency was evaluated for three crops, wheat, tomato, and apple, under water-limited conditions in the Miandoab Plain (Lake Urmia Basin). The system accurately estimated the water requirements of crops by simulating soil moisture at different layers, calculating crop evapotranspiration (ETc), and dynamically updating soil evaporation coefficient (Ke) and crop transpiration coefficient (Kcb) based on leaf area index (LAI) and phenological stages. The results showed that the temporal patterns of temperature and ETc changes were fully consistent with the semi-arid climate, and an increase in temperature during the middle of the growing season led to a significant rise in ETc. The highest ETc values were observed in the apple orchard (up to approximately 29 mm/day), while tomato and wheat exhibited peak-decline and flowering-to-grain-filling patterns, respectively. The results showed that the intelligent system provided estimates comparable to those of the Penman–Monteith method, with no significant difference (p > 0.05). Analysis of Kcb and Ke coefficients indicated that, as leaf area expanded, the contribution of soil surface evaporation decreased, and crop transpiration became the dominant component of water consumption. Field validation demonstrated that the intelligent system predicted water needs more accurately than conventional methods, resulting in up to 41% reduction in water use, up to 12.6% increase in yield, and up to 87% improvement in water productivity, depending on the crop and irrigation system. Correlation analysis confirmed the strong relationship between temperature, LAI, and ETc, demonstrating the system’s capability to represent climatic and physiological factors simultaneously. Overall, the study shows that intelligent systems based on physical–physiological models can significantly enhance the accuracy of water demand forecasting, improve productivity, and promote sustainable irrigation management at both farm and basin scales, serving as a generalizable tool for climate-change-adapted agriculture in arid and semi-arid regions.

Introduction

Agriculture in arid and semi-arid regions has always faced serious challenges due to water scarcity, climate fluctuations, and declining soil quality. In recent decades, these challenges have intensified, especially under the influence of climate change, rapid population growth, and excessive exploitation of natural resources, leading to decreased agricultural productivity, reduced product quality, and increased vulnerability of farmers’ livelihoods in these regions. Considering that more than 70–80% of renewable water resources in arid and semi-arid countries are used in the agricultural sector1, optimizing and scientifically managing the irrigation process is considered one of the most effective solutions to address the water crisis and improve resource productivity. In recent years, climate change has caused higher temperatures, altered precipitation patterns, and reduced surface and groundwater resources2. The decline in groundwater levels, increased soil salinity, and decreased land productivity are direct consequences of these conditions, significantly impacting crop yields and agricultural sustainability, particularly in the Lake Urmia Basin3,4. Under such circumstances, achieving efficient and intelligent irrigation systems is not only a technological necessity but also an economic and environmental imperative. To cope with water resource constraints, researchers worldwide have developed innovative irrigation methods, including regulated deficit irrigation (RDI) and managed deficit irrigation (DI) techniques, which aim to conserve water and increase water productivity (WP)2. Although these methods can reduce water consumption in certain situations, many depend on historical data and lack the flexibility to respond to short-term climate changes5. Furthermore, focusing solely on WP sometimes reduces farmers’ net income; therefore, irrigation must be managed in a way that enhances both water productivity and economic profitability6,7. At the same time, the use of intelligent systems and physical water-soil-plant models has been recognized as an effective tool for optimizing water consumption. Models such as SWAP and HYDRUS simulate water movement in soil and salinity distribution, and by integrating environmental data, including soil moisture, temperature, solar radiation, wind speed, and rainfall forecasts, they can calculate the actual crop water requirement under field conditions and optimize irrigation timing and depth8–12.

Studies that combine weather forecast data with numerical simulation models have shown that this approach can reduce water use and increase farmers’ net income13–16. However, most of these studies are limited to humid climates or simplified relationships between water and crop yield, and have rarely been validated under real field conditions in arid and semi-arid regions17–19. Emami et al.20 predicted soil moisture and crop parameters with high accuracy using data mining and machine learning algorithms. Similarly, Dehghanisanij et al.21 developed a web-based decision-support system for irrigation management in the southern Urmia Lake Basin, showing significant reductions in water consumption and increased crop productivity. However, many existing systems lack integration between predicted climate data and the physical water-soil-plant balance model, and they are not operational at the field scale under real climate conditions. Given these limitations, it is necessary to develop an intelligent, web-based system that combines live environmental data and weather forecasting with a physical water-soil-plant model and decision-support optimization algorithms. Such a system can dynamically calculate crop water requirements, optimize irrigation timing and depth, and prevent water stress and soil salinity. The web-based design, with cloud processing and accessibility via smartphones, allows widespread use and operationalization even in underserved areas22,23.

Despite recent advances in intelligent irrigation systems, most existing systems use historical climate data or simple empirical relationships, and integration of climate forecast data with physical soil–water–plant models at the field scale has rarely been done. Additionally, the simultaneous effects of water, salinity, and temperature stresses in a coupled manner have been less studied under real field conditions. Therefore, the main objectives of this research are: (1) development of an intelligent irrigation system by integrating real and predicted climate data with a physical–physiological model, (2) dynamic updating of plant coefficients and simulation of multiple stresses, and (3) validation of the system in real field conditions for three crops: wheat, tomato, and apple. Considering the literature review and the limitations of existing systems, this research presents several innovations and scientific contributions that distinguish it from previous studies. Unlike many previous systems, the proposed system can dynamically update soil evaporation coefficient (Ke) and crop transpiration coefficient (Kcb) on a daily basis, based on simulated leaf area index (LAI) and phenological stages, while simultaneously modeling water, salinity, and temperature stresses and their cumulative impact on actual evapotranspiration, crop growth, and final yield. Field validation at the farm and orchard scale, under real water deficit conditions in the semi-arid climate of the Lake Urmia basin and Alborz province, has narrowed the gap between theoretical models and practical application, demonstrating that this system can provide accurate and dynamic irrigation decisions under real field conditions. By simulating soil moisture at different depths, actual crop evapotranspiration, and physiological changes throughout the growing season, the system estimates crop water requirements more accurately than conventional methods and models.

In addition to optimizing water consumption, the system aims to improve irrigation efficiency and minimize the number and volume of irrigation cycles, thereby preventing water stress, over-irrigation, and salinity accumulation in the root zone. Furthermore, by considering economic and social indicators, such as reducing irrigation costs and improving production sustainability, this system can serve as a scientific and practical tool to enhance farm management and increase farmers’ income. The implementation of this system in sensitive and water-scarce areas demonstrates its high potential for application in arid and semi-arid regions. This approach can be considered a generalizable model for similar regions, both nationally and internationally, and represents an effective step toward reducing water scarcity, improving water productivity, maintaining soil ecosystem sustainability, and enhancing national food security.

Materials and methods

Study area

To develop and evaluate an intelligent system for determining irrigation timing and depth based on the water-soil-plant model, the Miandoab Plain in the south of the Lake Urmia Basin was selected as the study area. This area was chosen due to its semi-arid climate, strong dependence on surface and groundwater resources, significant contribution to the water balance of Lake Urmia, and availability of accurate climatic and hydrological data. These characteristics make the Miandoab Plain a suitable case for analyzing and managing irrigation optimally under real field conditions and water resource constraints (Fig. 1). The Miandoab Plain is located in West Azerbaijan Province, between latitudes 36°50′ and 37°15′ North and longitudes 45°50′ and 46°15′ East. The average elevation of the region is approximately 1,290 m above sea level, and its topography is mostly flat. According to the Amberge classification, the climate is cold and semi-arid. The average annual precipitation is about 284 mm, calculated based on 20 years of climate data (2000–2019) from the Miandoab synoptic station24. These conditions have resulted in a negative water balance and high reliance on artificial irrigation. The main sources of water for the plain include the two major rivers, Zarrineh river and Simineh river, which together provide more than half of the water entering Lake Urmia. The plain’s irrigation system is primarily fed by the Shahid Kazemi-Bukan Dam, which releases approximately 300 million cubic meters of water annually for agricultural lands. However, in recent years, extensive withdrawals from deep wells, particularly during periods of water scarcity, have caused declines in groundwater levels and exacerbated soil salinity. Estimates indicate that annual groundwater withdrawal in the region exceeds 240 million cubic meters, constituting about 20% of the total groundwater extraction in the Lake Urmia Basin25.Fig. 1Geographical location of the Miandoab Plain.

Geographical location of the Miandoab Plain.

Regarding land use, a large portion of the plain, approximately 90,000 hectares, is devoted to irrigated agriculture, with dominant crops including wheat, sugar beet, barley, alfalfa, and tomatoes. Despite relatively adequate irrigation infrastructure, current irrigation efficiency is estimated at less than 40%, resulting in significant water waste and reduced productivity.

Due to the extensive surface irrigation network, the high dependence of the agricultural sector on groundwater resources, and challenges such as declining groundwater levels and increasing soil salinity, the Miandoab Plain is recognized as one of the critical areas for water resource management in the Lake Urmia Basin. Therefore, evaluating the performance of the intelligent irrigation management system under these conditions is of particular scientific and practical importance and can play an effective role in improving water productivity and reducing pressure on the region’s water resources.

Selected sites

This research was conducted in the Miandoab region using three selected farms as project implementation sites. The selected farms were chosen to represent the common agricultural conditions of the region in terms of climate, water resources, soil characteristics, and cropping patterns. Three monitored farms (one farm for each crop) and three conventionally managed farms were selected. In each farm, three replicate plots of 0.5 ha were randomly selected and monitored to account for spatial variability. Field experiments were conducted for three crops, wheat, tomato, and apple, with different water requirements, considering differences in phenological stages and the effect of soil salinity on plant response to water during the 2024–2025 cropping season. Wheat was monitored from November 2024 to June 2025, tomato from May to September 2025, and the apple orchard from April to October 2025. Climatic data were obtained from the Miandoab synoptic station using WMO-standard equipment, including temperature, humidity, wind speed, solar radiation, and precipitation. Initial soil salinity (ECe) was measured at a depth of 0–90 cm and ranged between 2.1 and 6.5 dS/m. In the monitored farms, required data, including soil moisture, water flow rate, water consumption, and crop yield, were measured and recorded with high accuracy to enable detailed analysis of the system’s performance. Soil moisture was measured using the gravimetric method, considering at least three sampling points at the beginning, middle, and end of each field to account for spatial variations. Water consumption was recorded using appropriate measuring instruments, depending on the type of irrigation system employed. In non-monitored plots, farm management and irrigation operations were controlled by the farmers, and only the irrigation schedule recommended by the system was provided. In these farms, the final decision on irrigation timing and quantity was made by the farmers themselves. This approach allows for comparison between monitored farms and conventionally managed farms, clearly demonstrating the role of the system in determining irrigation depth based on weather forecasting and improving water productivity in optimizing irrigation management.

Model description

The intelligent irrigation depth and timing determination system used in this research was developed by integrating climate, soil, crop, and irrigation system data to improve the accuracy of irrigation management and increase water productivity. The main innovation of this system compared to similar systems is its ability to dynamically simulate soil salinity in the root zone and explicitly consider crop phenological stages in irrigation decision-making. In this system, the crop’s water requirement and optimal irrigation depth are adjusted according to growth stages, including establishment, vegetative growth, flowering, crop formation, and ripening, while also accounting for salt accumulation and crop sensitivity to salinity at each stage. This approach increases the accuracy of irrigation recommendations, reduces water and salinity stress, and improves crop performance under real field conditions, particularly in areas with limited water resources and variable irrigation water quality.

System framework

The intelligent irrigation depth and timing management system is designed as a decision-support tool based on an integrated and modular framework that establishes dynamic relationships among climate, soil, crops, irrigation systems, and financial considerations. The main goal of this framework is to accurately predict crop water needs, simulate soil-water balance, analyze crop stresses, optimize cropping patterns, manage water reservoirs, and estimate the farmer’s final profitability. By relying on actual and predicted climate data and using physical models and mathematical relationships, the system updates irrigation recommendations daily and provides outputs including irrigation timing, optimal water depth, root zone moisture status, and stress warnings. The system comprises four main modules: climate, crop, soil, and irrigation decision support. Communication and data exchange among these modules are dynamically configured, and the computational core is implemented in Python, allowing development of new models such as medium-term climate analysis or economic simulations.

Input details

The intelligent irrigation system receives a set of climatic, soil, crop, irrigation system, and water access conditions to simulate water exchange between the crop, soil, and atmosphere. Based on the outputs of evapotranspiration and soil-water balance models, it provides an optimal irrigation plan for the farm’s cropping pattern. Required inputs include online meteorological data, soil and crop characteristics, water source status and operating constraints, characteristics of the irrigation system, and farm water storage information (Table 2). The combination of these inputs enables the system to manage and optimize irrigation with high precision, consistency, and efficiency.

Soil water balance simulation

The soil water balance model in the system is developed in a layered manner and dynamically simulates moisture, salinity, and water flow in each soil layer. This model is based on physical soil parameters, including texture, field capacity, wilting point, bulk density, and porosity. Field capacity and wilting point values are extracted from the soil moisture characteristic curve and are also referenced in the report file. This curve provides the basis for all calculations of crop-available water and stress ranges. Given that many soils are saline; the system first corrects the wilting point based on salinity. Crop stresses

In this system, crop stresses are calculated on a daily basis and include water, temperature, and salinity stresses. These stresses directly influence crop growth, evapotranspiration, and final crop yield, and are incorporated into the model through stress coefficients (Ks) ranging from 1 (no stress) to 0 (full stress). Water stress is calculated based on the concept of relative stress (Srel), which represents the soil moisture status between upper and lower depletion thresholds. In the apple orchard managed under basin irrigation, the reduction in gross irrigation depth was relatively small (2.8%); however, crop yield increased by 12.6% and water productivity by 15.9%. As a result, the system was required to supply additional water to ensure adequate irrigation at the end of the orchard, limiting the potential reduction in water use and associated gains in productivity. In wheat fields irrigated using a drip system, gross irrigation depth decreased by 25%, while crop yield and water productivity increased by 6.5% and 35%, respectively. These results indicate a moderate effectiveness of the intelligent system in improving water productivity and crop performance. Climate change and evapotranspiration dynamics

Analysis of the climatic data indicated that daily air temperature during the growing period of the three crops, tomato, wheat, and apple orchard, followed a clear and interpretable temporal pattern (Fig. 6). At the beginning of the growing season in mid-May, temperatures remained within a moderate range of 20–22 °C. These conditions were well suited for early tomato establishment, the initiation of vegetative growth in wheat, and the resumption of physiological activity in apple trees. As the season progressed and both solar radiation and day length increased, air temperature gradually rose, reaching approximately 25–30 °C during the mid-growth period. This temperature range, characteristic of semi-arid climates, increased the available energy for evaporation and transpiration, resulting in higher actual crop evapotranspiration and greater daily water requirements. This thermal trend and its direct influence on water demand are consistent with global standards, including FAO-5627, as well as with findings from international studies conducted in arid and semi-arid regions. In particular, temperatures within the range of 25–30 °C are widely recognized as a major driver of increased ETc in both field crops and horticultural systems.

As the growing season progressed toward its end and solar radiation declined, air temperature decreased by approximately 10–15 °C. This reduction in temperature was accompanied by decreased crop physiological activity, a decline in leaf area index (LAI), and a natural reduction in evapotranspiration. Berry and Björkman38 similarly reported that lower temperatures toward the end of the growing season reduce transpiration rates and crop water requirements, which is fully consistent with the findings of the present study. Overall, the temperature pattern observed closely reflects the typical climatic behavior of semi-arid regions worldwide. Studies conducted in regions with comparable climates, such as Spain, Turkey, and the United States, have shown that increasing temperatures during the peak of the growing season significantly raise crop water requirements, whereas declining temperatures toward the end of the season lead to reduced water consumption.

For tomato, temperatures in the range of 27–30 °C resulted in increased evapotranspiration rates and water demand, in agreement with the findings of Xue et al.39, reported higher tomato water consumption at temperatures approaching 30 °C. In wheat, since temperatures during the growing period did not exceed the crop’s heat stress threshold (approximately 32 °C), no heat stress symptoms were observed and growth conditions remained favorable. This observation is consistent with the results reported by Hatfield and Dold40. In the apple orchard, temperatures between 24 and 30 °C provided suitable conditions for vegetative growth and leaf area development, fully aligning with the findings of Dragoni et al.41. Although no precipitation occurred during the growing period, analysis of this variable remains essential from a climatic and hydrological perspective. Even in the absence of rainfall, precipitation plays a critical role in the calculation of effective rainfall, net irrigation requirements, and drought assessment. Internationally recognized water management models and standards, including FAO-56 and AquaCrop, identify temperature and precipitation as essential inputs for water demand estimation and irrigation planning. Therefore, evaluating these parameters, even under zero-precipitation conditions, is scientifically necessary and fully consistent with international modeling standards.

Seasonal dynamics of crop evapotranspiration

Actual evapotranspiration changes in the three crops, apple, tomato, and wheat, during the growing season showed a distinct temporal pattern that was directly influenced by temperature, leaf area index (LAI), phenological progress, and soil moisture conditions (Fig. 6a to c). Data extracted from the intelligent irrigation depth determination system indicated that ETc was low at the beginning of the growing season for all three crops and gradually increased with leaf area expansion and rising temperatures. In apple trees, ETc ranged from 3.8 to 5 mm/day at the beginning of the growing season.

With increasing temperatures to 25–30 °C and intense vegetative growth during the mid-season stage, ETc increased to 6–10 mm/day. Maximum ETc was observed in the mid- to late growing season, coinciding with the period of highest physiological activity of the trees, reaching values between 21 and 29 mm/day. This increase reflects the highest water requirement during periods of peak transpiration and maximum leaf area. Toward the end of the growing season, ETc decreased to approximately 7–11 mm/day as temperatures declined, leaf area was reduced, and the crop entered the late-season phase. This trend is fully consistent with the findings of Dragoni et al.41 reported that in semi-arid regions, apple ETc peaks during maximum physiological activity and declines as growth slows. In tomato, a similar pattern was observed, but with lower ETc values. At the beginning of the season, ETc ranged from 0.7 to 3 mm/day and increased to 5–9 mm/day with leaf development and increasing radiation intensity. Maximum ETc values of 10–12 mm/day were recorded during the reproductive stage, coinciding with fruit ripening. Toward the end of the season, ETc declined to 0.5–3 mm/day due to decreasing temperature and leaf area loss. This increase–decrease pattern is consistent with the findings of Xue et al.39, reported that tomato water consumption peaks during combined vegetative and reproductive growth and is highly sensitive to temperatures between 25 and 30 °C.

In wheat, ETc was very low during the early growth stages (before tillering), ranging from 0.5 to 2.5 mm/day. As the crop entered the tillering and heading stages, ETc increased and reached 4–6 mm/day at flowering. Maximum ETc values, ranging from 5 to 7 mm/day, were recorded during the grain-filling stage, when crop metabolic activity was highest. This trend is fully consistent with Hatfield and Dold40, who reported that wheat ETc is minimal in early growth stages and peaks during flowering and grain filling. Overall, the ETc values observed for the three crops in this study are in full agreement with internationally accepted findings. Global studies conducted in Spain, Turkey, and the United States have shown that ETc increases with rising temperature, increasing LAI, and advancing growth stages, and decreases toward the end of the growing season, a pattern that was clearly observed in the present study. The ETc range for apple (approximately 5–29 mm/day) is consistent with reports by Dragoni et al.41. For tomato, the observed range of 3–12 mm/day agrees with findings reported by Xue et al.39. These consistencies confirm that the system outputs are scientifically reliable and aligned with the international body of knowledge.

Inter-crop comparison and irrigation implications

Comparison among crops showed that apple exhibited higher ETc throughout the growing season than tomato and wheat. This difference is attributed to the woody nature of apple trees, their larger leaf area, longer growing period, and continuous deep root activity. Tomato showed moderate ETc values with a distinct peak during fruiting, while wheat exhibited the lowest ETc, consistent with its agronomic characteristics and shorter growth cycle. Accordingly, irrigation recommendations should focus on the mid- to late-season stages for apple, the mid-season stage for tomato, and the flowering to grain-filling stages for wheat. Comparison with international studies further demonstrated that the temporal patterns, magnitude, and temperature response of ETc in this study are fully consistent with FAO-56, AquaCrop, and studies by Hatfield and Dold40 and Xue et al.39, confirming the efficiency and scientific accuracy of the proposed system. The intelligent irrigation system developed in this research has several key advantages over commonly used tools such as CROPWAT, IrriTool, NetBeat, and AquaCrop. Unlike most conventional systems that primarily provide ETc estimates, this system simultaneously calculates ETc, RAW, TAW, irrigation depth and duration, and soil moisture balance. The system utilizes real field soil data, including sand, silt, and clay percentages, drainage coefficients, water-holding capacity, and effective root depth, as well as detailed irrigation system parameters such as lateral and drip spacing, flow rate, and installation depth. These features enhance the practicality and operational applicability of the outputs. In addition, Kcb and Ke coefficients are dynamically updated based on actual crop growth stages, and the system is capable of simulating future climate scenarios. Together, these characteristics ensure high accuracy, adaptability, and reliability in irrigation recommendations and intelligent water resource management.

The observed ETc trends in the three crops, apple, tomato, and wheat, are in complete agreement with global standard patterns. The gradual increase in ETc from the beginning of the season to the mid-season peak and its decrease at the end of the season have been confirmed in authoritative studies, including FAO-56, AquaCrop41, SWAP42, and HYDRUS. In apple, ETc ranged from 3.8 to 5 mm at the beginning of the season, increased to about 6–10 mm in mid-season, and reached a maximum of 21–29 mm/day. These results are consistent with the findings of Dragoni et al.40, reported that in semi-arid regions, apple ETc can exceed 20 mm at the peak of the growing season. This agreement confirms the validity of the modeling system and the accuracy of the data used. In tomato, ETc ranged from 0.7 to 3 mm at the beginning of the season and increased to 10–12 mm during the reproductive period, consistent with Xue et al.39, Kroes et al.43, and Kodešová et al.44. The pattern observed in this study, an increase in ETc within this temperature range and a decrease at the end of the season, aligns with global calculations.

In wheat, ETc increased from very low values in the early stages to peak values at flowering and grain filling, consistent with Hatfield and Dold40 and USDA reports for winter wheat. Maximum ETc for wheat in semi-arid regions is typically 4–6 mm/day, which matches the values recorded in this study. In terms of agreement with global models, the ETc range for all crops is fully consistent with outputs from FAO-56, AquaCrop, and SWAP. In wheat, the water requirement pattern reflected the three classical growth phases. In the early stages, when LAI was small, ETc values were very low. Due to the use of advanced physical-physiological models, dynamic and multivariate modeling capabilities, and daily updating of climatic and crop parameters, the intelligent system was able to determine crop water needs with high accuracy, preventing water stress, excessive irrigation, and water waste. Furthermore, the recorded correlations between temperature, LAI, and ETc are fully consistent with international studies, demonstrating that the system is valid not only at the theoretical and modeling level but also at the practical level, accurately representing crop behavior in the semi-arid climate of Iran. These findings indicate that using such a system in irrigation management can simultaneously increase water productivity, prevent physiological stresses, and reduce unnecessary irrigation costs at the farm scale.

The strong positive correlation observed between temperature and ETc in this study is fully consistent with the reports of FAO-56, Vries and Van Wij43, Hatfield and Dold40. In tomato, the increase in ETc at temperatures of 27–30 °C aligns with the findings of Xue et al.39. Conclusion

The results of this study, beyond providing a intelligent irrigation system, indicate the development of a new approach to agricultural water management, an approach that goes beyond simply calculating water needs and achieves an understanding of the dynamics of the water-soil-plant system as a coherent whole. Analysis of the outputs showed that by combining physical models and crop physiological relationships in a data-driven structure, it is possible to simulate the real behavior of soil and crops in response to climate change with high accuracy. This achievement demonstrates that the future of irrigation management in arid and semi-arid regions depends on systems capable of learning, adapting, and making dynamic decisions based on environmental feedback. From a scientific perspective, this research represents a step toward integrating two historical approaches to water management: a physical approach based on balance and a biological approach based on crop response. The developed system successfully links these two dimensions in a correlated model, so that its output is not solely based on theoretical equations but also reflects the actual behavior of crops in the field. This feature clearly distinguishes traditional modeling from intelligent decision-support systems and opens a new path for future research in simulating agricultural ecosystem dynamics. From a practical perspective, the results demonstrate that deploying such systems can redefine the concept of water productivity in agriculture. Instead of merely increasing irrigation efficiency, the focus shifts to the ecological efficiency of water use, that is, maintaining yield while preserving soil stability and preventing biological stresses, as the primary criterion. This paradigm shifts from volumetric to functional water management is one of the most significant theoretical contributions of this research. Moreover, the implementation of the system in real-world settings, such as the Miandoab Plain, showed that integrating climatic, soil, and physiological data in a dynamic model can provide an effective tool for intelligent water policymaking and climate-adapted agriculture. The system offers decision-making capabilities not only at the farm level but also at the watershed level and can be used to design cropping patterns, schedule irrigation, and optimally allocate water resources. Ultimately, this research emphasizes that the future of agricultural water management in drylands should focus on increasing the intelligence of consumption systems rather than solely on increasing supply. Combining physical models with machine learning, remote sensing, and economic analysis could transform the next generation of irrigation systems into tools that ensure not only water efficiency but also ecological sustainability and food security. Although the system was validated for three representative crops, its performance should be investigated for other crops with different root architectures and phenological patterns. The system was also tested under typical climatic conditions; therefore, its performance under extreme conditions such as heat waves, prolonged drought, or heavy rainfall should be evaluated in future studies. Future developments will include the integration of remote sensing data and machine learning algorithms to enhance spatial scalability and real-time decision-making.
