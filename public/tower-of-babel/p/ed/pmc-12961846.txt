Clinicopathologic, molecular and tumor immune microenvironment features of mismatch repair-deficient glioblastomas in Lynch syndrome: a multicenter study of 29 cases with therapeutic implications
Authors: Zhi-Gang Yao, Xue-Long Li, Wen-Juan Wen, Xiao-Yan Lin, Gui-Hui Zhang, Yi-Ping Sun, Yi-Cong Nie, Zhi-Ming Zheng, Yu-Qiao Xu, Mu Yang, Tai-Fei Yu, Yin Dong, Yang-Hao Hou, Lin-Lin Dang, Shu-Guang Chu, Jia-Mei Li, Ji-Wei Ma, Zhou Wang, Fang Hua, Xing-Fu Wang, Dai-Zhong Wang, Wan-Ming Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glioblastoma (GBM) is a relatively rare manifestation of Lynch syndrome (LS), and its defining characteristics are not yet fully defined. This study employs an integrated analysis of the clinicopathological, molecular, and tumor immune microenvironment features of LS-GBMs to elucidate its distinct biology and inform diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. We collected GBM samples from 29 LS patients across multiple medical centers. Germline MMR gene testing confirmed the following mutations among the 29 LS-GBM cases: MSH2 (16 cases, 55.2%), MLH1 (6 cases, 20.7%), MSH6 (4 cases, 13.8%), and PMS2 (1 case, 3.4%). Two cases (6.9%) exhibited no detectable pathogenic germline variants. Patients presented at a mean age of 45.7 years (range 10–69), significantly younger than those with IDH-mutant astrocytoma (WHO grade 4) (P < 0.05) or conventional IDH-wildtype GBMs (IDH-wt cGBMs; P < 0.001). Notably, IDH-wt LS-GBMs demonstrated superior overall survival compared to IDH-wt cGBMs (P < 0.05). Histopathologically, 96.6% (28/29) of cases displayed multinucleated giant cells, with 89.7% (26/29) exhibiting a wreath-like nuclear pattern. Additionally, 58.6% (17/28) demonstrated areas with oligodendroglioma-like characteristics. Molecular profiling revealed high-frequency mutations in TP53 (82.8%, 24/29) and SETD2 (53.6%, 15/28), suggesting concomitant dysregulation of cell cycle control and chromatin remodeling pathways. Furthermore, frequent pathogenic mutations were observed in NF1 (64.3%, 18/28), along with activating mutations in PDGFRA (39.3%, 11/28) and EGFR (32.1%, 9/29), suggesting tumor proliferation and invasion driven by receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) signaling, such as the Ras/MAPK pathway. Moreover, the MMR-deficient state results in a high tumor mutational burden (100%, 16/16, ≥ 10 muts/Mb) and an inflamed tumor microenvironment with abundant CD8+ T-cell and CD163+ macrophages infiltration. Our findings establish LS-GBMs as a distinct molecular subtype of GBM, driven by convergent defects in DNA repair, cell cycle regulation, and RTK signaling, and highlighted by an immunogenic microenvironment. An integrated diagnostic approach is crucial for its identification, and tailored therapeutic strategies, including immune checkpoint inhibitors and targeted agents, should be explored.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40478-026-02246-6.

Introduction

Lynch syndrome (LS), also known as hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC), is an autosomal dominant hereditary cancer predisposition syndrome caused by germline mutations in one of the DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes, including MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, or the EPCAM gene [1]. These mutations impair MMR function, resulting in mismatch repair deficiency (MMRD), which hinders the correction of replication errors. This defect leads to the accumulation of somatic mutations and ultimately drives tumorigenesis. Moreover, the presence of MMRD is closely associated with high microsatellite instability (MSI-H) and a high tumor mutational burden (TMB-H), both of which are considered potential biomarkers for immune checkpoint therapy [2, 3].

Individuals with LS have a significantly increased lifetime risk of developing a range of malignancies, including colorectal cancer, endometrial cancer, urinary tract cancer, gastric cancer, and skin cancer [4]. LS patients exhibit a risk approximately six times higher than that of the general population for developing primary brain gliomas [5, 6]. Recent studies report that the incidence of LS-associated gliomas ranges from 0.41 to 1.4% [7, 8], with glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) being the most common [7–9]. Despite its relative rarity, the potential association between LS and GBM raises important questions regarding tumorigenesis in the central nervous system (CNS) under conditions of germline variants of MMR genes. Existing research has primarily focused on the genetic and clinical characteristics of LS-associated colorectal cancer and endometrial cancer, leaving a significant gap in our understanding of the pathological and molecular events underlying LS-associated GBMs (LS-GBMs). Furthermore, although the 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of CNS Tumors references MMRD associated GBM in the context of giant cell glioblastomas, it does not specifically address LS-associated CNS tumors in the section on hereditary tumor predisposition syndrome. Accordingly, the present study aims to investigate the clinicopathological, molecular, and tumor immune microenvironment characteristics of LS-associated GBM by analyzing a multi-center cohort of LS-GBM cases. This study seeks to identify the unique characteristics of these tumors and provide insights into their underlying molecular mechanisms. We believe that a deeper understanding of LS-associated GBM will not only enhance our knowledge of this rare malignancy but also inform clinical decision-making and the development of targeted therapeutic strategies for affected patients.

Methods

Patients and samples

Twenty-nine samples were collected from the pathology departments of eight general hospitals across four provinces in China. Pathological diagnosis was established based on the diagnostic criteria for astrocytoma, IDH-mutant (IDH-mut), WHO grade 4 and glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype (IDH-wt) as outlined in the 5th edition of the WHO Classification of Central Nervous System Tumors, integrating findings from imaging, histopathology, and molecular alterations. The diagnosis of Lynch Syndrome (LS) in all patients was established by clinical criteria and/or the presence of germline MMR gene variants. Clinical characteristics together with treatment strategies were collected from electronic medical records in hospitals. Fifty-three IDH-mut astrocytomas (WHO grade 4) and ninety-three IDH-wt conventional GBMs (cGBMs) were used as controls to investigate the clinical characteristics of LS-GBMs.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was conducted on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections on the Ventana BenchMark ULTRA platform (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) to evaluate the expression of various markers. The sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in a microwave oven with citrate buffer (pH 6.0). The slides were then blocked with 3% H2O2 and 5% goat serum at room temperature. The sections were incubated with the primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1. After washing, the sections were incubated with appropriate biotinylated secondary antibodies, followed by incubation with avidin–biotin-peroxidase complex (ABC kit, ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China) and visualized using diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen. Counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin, and the slides were dehydrated and mounted. Positive and negative controls were included in each staining run to ensure specificity and accuracy of the staining. The stained sections were analyzed under a light microscope.

Complete absence of nuclear staining in neoplastic cells was interpreted as deficient expression of MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2, while intact nuclear staining in adjacent non-neoplastic vascular endothelial cells, or lymphocytes served as internal positive controls. CD8+ T cells and CD163+ macrophages were quantified using ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/index.html). The ten randomly chosen high-power fields (400×) were analyzed, depending on CD8+ T-cell/ CD163+ macrophages density (number/mm2). The tumor proportion score (TPS) was defined as the percentage of tumor cells (TCs) with PD-L1 membrane staining (partial or complete) relative to the total number of TCs, excluding interstitial, necrotic, normal, or non-neoplastic cells. The combined positive score (CPS) was calculated as the number of PD-L1 positive cells (TCs, lymphocytes, and macrophages) divided by the total number of TCs, multiplied by 100.

Genomic DNA isolation and targeted NGS

Targeted NGS was performed at Di’an Diagnostics (Hangzhou, China) using an Illumina Hiseq4000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Genomic DNA was extracted from FFPE tissue samples using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration of the extracted nucleic acid was accurately quantified with a Qubit 3.0/4.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA), and its integrity was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). A sufficient amount of DNA was sheared into 150–200 bp fragments using a Covaris LE220 focused ultrasonicator (Covaris, USA) for DNA library preparation. The fragmented DNA was then end-repaired, and adapters were ligated. Enrichment of the adapter-ligated fragments was performed using PCR, generating a library compatible with sequencing on an Illumina platform (Illumina, USA). The library underwent hybridization capture using specially designed probes. Streptavidin magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were used to capture the probe-bound library fragments, while unbound fragments were washed away. The captured fragments were then enriched through PCR amplification and prepared for sequencing on an Illumina system (Illumina, USA). Sequence reads were mapped to the reference human genome build GRCh37 using the Burrows-Wheeler aligner (BWA) software. Microsatellite stability analysis was performed by MSIsensor2 as part of the sequencing assay. Glioblastomas were deemed positive for microsatellite instability when ≥ 40% of the 105 microsatellites assessed by the 550 NGS Panel were unstable. Somatic tumor mutation burden (TMB) was determined by calculating the number of somatic mutations in the coding regions of genes in the 550 NGS Panel, counting both single nucleotide variants and short indels, divided by the total coding footprint of the assay (1.5 Mb). TMB-H was defined as those tumors with TMB values of ≥ 10 somatic mutations/megabase (muts/Mb) [10].

O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase methylation analysis

Five hundred nanograms of extracted DNA were treated with sodium bisulfite using the MGMT plus Kit (Gene Tech, Shanghai, China) to convert unmethylated cytosines to uracils. The bisulfite-treated DNA was then amplified using methylation-specific primers, and the PCR products were sequenced. The average methylation of 10 CpG sites was calculated using the PyroMark Q96 ID system (Qiagen, USA), with a 5% cut-off to distinguish methylated from unmethylated samples.

TERT promoter mutation analysis

The TERT promoter was amplified from extracted DNA using the primers 5′-GTCCTGCCCCTTCACCTT-3′ and 5′-CAGCGCTGCCTGAAACTC-3′. The PCR products were purified and sequenced by GATC Biotech (Cologne, Germany), and the chromatograms were manually interpreted using 4Peaks Software (version 1.7.1, Mekentosj).

Fluorescence in situ hybridization analysis

EGFR and PDGFRA amplification, as well as 1p/19q and CDKN2A deletions, were identified using fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH). The FISH assay was performed with locus-specific probes for EGFR (7p12), 1p36, 19q13, PDGFRA (4q12), and CDKN2A (9p21), along with corresponding reference centromeric probes, following the manufacturer's recommendations (Agilent, Beijing, China).

Chromosomal copy number analysis

As previous reports [11], tumor genomic DNA was extracted from FFPE samples using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quality/quantity was verified by Qubit assay (Thermo Fisher). Copy number analysis was performed using the OncoScan CNV Plus Reagent Kit (Thermo Fisher) with 80 ng DNA input per sample. Arrays were processed on the GeneChip Scanner 3000 7G system, with data acquisition via Affymetrix Command Console (v4.0). CEL files were converted to OSCHP format using OncoScan Console (v1.1.034), followed by CNV/LOH analysis and tumor purity estimation in Nexus Express for OncoScan 3 with TuScan algorithm.

Statistical analysis

Differences in age, CD8+ T-cell density, and CD163+ macrophage density between LS-GBMs and cGBMs were analyzed using Student’s t-test. The progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) of the 26 IDH‑wt LS‑GBM patients were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. PFS was defined as the interval from first surgery to documented disease progression, and OS as the interval from first surgery to death from any cause or last follow-up. Survival curves were compared with the log‑rank test, and multiple comparisons were adjusted by the Bonferroni correction. Statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27 (IBM Corp., NY, USA). Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism, version 9.1.2 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA) or OriginPro 2024 (OriginLab, MA, USA). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Germline MMR gene variants and LS diagnosis

Genetic testing identified a total of 27 germline heterozygous mutations in mismatch repair (MMR) genes among 29 patients. As shown in Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 2, MSH2 germline variants were the most prevalent (16 cases, 55.2%), followed by MLH1 (6 cases, 20.7%), MSH6 (4 cases, 13.8%), and PMS2 (1 case, 3.4%). Among the MSH2 germline mutations, 15 of 16 cases were classified as (likely) pathogenic mutations, whereas only one case (#28) harbored a missense variant of uncertain significance (VUS). These included four frameshift mutations, five nonsense mutations, and three splicing mutations. Three patients (#1, #2, and #3), who belonged to the same family, carried a deletion in exon 7 of the MSH2 gene, leading to the production of a nonfunctional truncating protein. Additionally, both #11 and #12 harbored an identical frameshift mutation in exon 15 (p.E878Afs3). Among the six MLH1 germline mutations, there were three nonsense mutations and three missense mutations. Except for one VUS (#13), the remaining five mutations were classified as (likely) pathogenic. All four MSH6 germline mutations were pathogenic, consisting of two identical p.F1088Sfs2 frameshift mutations and two nonsense mutations. The single PMS2 germline mutation identified was a pathogenic truncating nonsense mutation. The amino acid alteration profile caused by germline gene mutations is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Moreover, two patients (#15 and #22) (6.9%) with GBM exhibited loss of MSH2 and MSH6 expression as detected by IHC staining; however, no detectable germline mutations were identified. Notably, both patients had personal histories of colorectal cancer and endometrial cancer, respectively, along with family histories of LS-associated cancers (Supplementary Table 2). Based on the Amsterdam Criteria II and the Revised Bethesda Guidelines, they were clinically diagnosed with LS. Clinical characteristics of LS-GBMs

The 29 cases with LS-GBM (16 females, 13 males) had a mean age at diagnosis of 45.7 years (range 10–69 years). This was significantly younger than cases with IDH-mut astrocytomas (WHO grade 4) (53 cases; mean age 52.7 years, P < 0.05) and IDH-wt cGBMs (93 cases; mean age 57.3 years, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1b), both lacking somatic and germline MMR gene variants. Eighteen cases (62.1%) presented with symptoms of increased intracranial pressure, including headache and dizziness in 12 cases (41.4%) and nausea and vomiting in 7 cases (24.1%). Sensory or motor dysfunction was observed in 15 cases (51.7%). Other clinical manifestations included speech disorders (4 cases, 13.8%), cognitive impairment (3 cases, 10.3%), blurred vision (2 cases, 6.9%), and epilepsy (1 cases, 3.4%) (Fig. 1c). One patient (#14) was asymptomatic, with the tumor incidentally discovered during a CT scan for head trauma evaluation.

Imaging characteristics of LS-GBMs

The preoperative MRI characteristics of the 29 LS-GBMs showed no significant differences from those of IDH-wt cGBMs lacking somatic and germline MMRD. All tumors were located supratentorially, with 17 involving the left cerebral hemisphere, 14 the right hemisphere, and 3 the corpus callosum. The frontal lobe was the most commonly affected region (19 cases), followed by the temporal lobe (12 cases), parietal lobe (4 cases), and occipital lobe (3 cases) (Fig. 1d). Tumor involvement was observed in the basal ganglia and hippocampus in 3 cases each, and 9 cases (32.1%) had tumors affecting two or more brain regions. All tumors exhibited variable sizes, with an average maximal diameter of 5.0 cm (range 1.7–8.7 cm). Patient #3 and #15 presented with two distinct intracranial tumors.

On MRI, the lesions demonstrated low signal intensity on T1-weighted imaging (WI) (Fig. 2a) and high signal intensity on T2-WI (Fig. 2b, c). Peritumoral edema was observed, often accompanied by ventricular compression and narrowing. Post-contrast sequences revealed rim-like or heterogeneous enhancement (Fig. 2d). Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) showed hyperintensity (Fig. 2e), with corresponding reduced apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) revealed a significantly elevated choline (Cho) peak and reduced N-acetylaspartate (NAA) peak, resulting in an increased Cho/NAA ratio in both enhancing and non-enhancing regions (Fig. 2f), consistent with the imaging diagnosis of high-grade gliomas. Histopathologic characteristics of LS-GBMs

Histopathologically, tumors showed brisk mitotic activity (29 cases, 100%), microvascular proliferation (24 cases, 82.8%), and pseudopalisading necrosis (20 cases, 69.0%). Intraoperative smears revealed abundant multinucleated giant cells (Fig. 3a), which were also prominently observed in paraffin-embedded sections, either focally or diffusely, in 28 cases (96.6%). Notably, these multinucleated giant cells exhibited giant or bizarre nuclei (16 cases, 55.2%, Fig. 3b) and a wreath-like (resembling a beaded bracelet) nuclear arrangement pattern (26 cases, 89.7%, Fig. 3c-i). The average density of these giant cells was 205.3/mm2 (range 60–550/mm2), which was significantly higher than that in IDH-wt cGBMs with TP53 mutation (59.6/mm2, range 0–250/mm2; n = 23, P < 0.001). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining revealed diffuse or focal expression of GFAP and Olig2 in all tumors. Three tumors (6.9%, #10, #13, and #19) expressed IDH1 R132H, and nuclear p53 was diffusely expressed in 23 cases (79.3%, Fig. 3j). ATRX loss of expression was observed in six tumors (20.7%). The MIB-1 proliferation index averaged 41.8% (Supplementary Table 3). All tumors were negative for H3K27M, EMA, CD34, BRAF V600E, and H3.3G34R.

MMR IHC staining revealed concurrent loss of MSH2 and MSH6 protein expression in 17 tumors (58.6%, Fig. 3k, l), including two (#15 and #22) without detectable germline mutations. Six tumors (20.7%) showed simultaneous loss of MLH1 and PMS2 expression, with tumor #7 also exhibiting MSH6 loss. Five cases (17.2%) showed isolated loss of MSH6, while one case (3.4%, #9) displayed isolated loss of PMS2. Sanger sequencing identified TERT promoter mutations (TERTp-mut) in tumors #15 (C250T) and #22 (C349T), occurring in 6.9% (2/29) of cases. Pyrosequencing showed high MGMT promoter methylation (MGMTp-me) in six cases (28.6%, 6/21). FISH analysis revealed EGFR amplification in seven tumors (24.1%, 7/29, Fig. 4i), PDGFRA amplification in three tumors (10.7%, 3/28), while no cases demonstrated 1p/19q codeletion.

In summary, by integrating histopathology, immunohistochemistry (Supplementary Table 3), and essential molecular alterations, three tumors in this cohort (#10, #13, and #19) that expressed IDH1 R132H were diagnosed as IDH-mut astrocytoma, grade 4. The remaining 26 cases were diagnosed as LS-associated IDH‑wt GBM. Moreover, despite the imaging finding of two separate intracranial tumors, Patient #3 and #15 were diagnosed with multifocal GBM based on concordant histopathological, immunohistochemical (p53, ATRX, MMR), and molecular (IDH, TERT, TP53, TERT/MGMT promoter methylation) profiles.

Genomic landscape of LS-GBMs

The somatic genes mutational landscape was displayed in Fig. 5. The available molecular data revealed that the top 3 most frequently (likely) pathogenic mutated genes were TP53 (82.8%, 24/29), SETD2 (67.9%, 19/28), and NF1 (64.3%, 18/28) (Supplementary Fig. 2). A total of 28 TP53 variants were identified, including 23 (82.1%) (likely) pathogenic variants, predominantly clustered in exon 8 (7 variants in 14 cases), exon 5 (9 variants in 10 cases), and exon 7 (4 variants in 6 cases). Notably, 14 out of 28 cases (50%) exhibited ≥ 2 variant sites, with case #11 harboring five (likely) pathogenic mutations. Among the 28 mutation sites, mutations at the Arg (R) site were observed in 13 cases. Hotspot mutations included exon 8 c.817C > T, p.R273C (6 cases), c.818G > A, p.R273H (5 cases), exon 5 c.524G > A, p.R175H (4 cases), and exon 7 c.743G > A, p.R248Q (2 cases) and c.742C > T, p.R248W (2 cases) (Supplementary Fig. 2a). A total of 23 SETD2 gene variants were identified, 16 of which were classified as (likely) pathogenic. Mutations in exon 3 were observed in 11 cases, with hotspot mutations detected in three cases at c.843dup, p.E282Rfs*9 (Supplementary Fig. 2b). For NF1, 42 variants were identified, including 34 (likely) pathogenic variants. Fourteen out of 28 cases (50%) exhibited ≥ 2 variant sites, with case #21 harboring five (likely) pathogenic mutations (Supplementary Fig. 2c). The genomic landscape of LS-GBMs

In addition to MMR germline mutations, multiple cases in our cohort had exclusively somatic inactivation of the MMR gene (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Other frequently observed (likely) pathogenic mutations with potential oncogenic significance were recurrently identified in genes associated with DNA damage response and chromatin modulation, such as BRCA2 (17.9%, 5/28), RAD50 (17.9%, 5/28), KMT2 family (25%, 7/28), and ARID1 (25%, 7/28) and (likely) pathogenic mutations of POLE were detected in 10.7% (3/28) (Supplementary Table 3), while no (likely) pathogenic mutations were found in POLD1, BRAF and H3K27M. For mutations in genes related to growth factor signaling pathways, PDGFRA single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) associated with functional activation were detected in 11 cases (39.3%), in addition to gene amplification events observed in cases #5, #22, and #29. Four cases (#3, #4, #25, and #29) carried the hotspot exon 5, c.704G > A (p.C235Y) mutation, while three cases (#17, #26, and #27) exhibited the exon 6 c.863A > G (p.Y288C) mutation, which were all predicted to be deleterious by MutationTaster2021 (https://www.mutationtaster.org/). Similarly, EGFR variants resulting in functional activation were found in nine (31.0%) cases, including seven cases of EGFR amplification and two cases of gene missense mutation. Furthermore, activating missense mutations in PIK3CA were detected in 28.6% (8/28) of cases, while PTEN inactivation was found in 21.4% of cases (6/28).

NGS was also performed to evaluate microsatellite instability (MSI) and tumor mutational burden (TMB) in 16 of 29 cases (55.2%, Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 3). The results revealed that MSI-high (MSI-H) status was observed in 43.8% (7/16) of the samples. For TMB, all 16 (100%, 16/16) cases demonstrated values ≥ 10 muts/Mb, with an average of 32.9 muts/Mb. Among these, 12 cases (75%, 12/16) exhibited moderate TMB levels (10–49 muts/Mb), while 4 cases (25%, 4/16) displayed high TMB levels (50–89 muts/Mb).

Chromosome copy number variations analysis

Chromosome copy number variation (CNV) analysis revealed multiple genomic imbalances, including both gains and deletions in 13 cases (Supplementary Table 4). CNV gains were most frequently observed at 9q in 5 cases (38.5%) and at 4p in 4 cases (30.8%). In contrast, CNV deletions were identified at 13q in 6 cases (46.2%), as well as at 9p and 14q, each occurring in 4 cases (30.8%). The concurrent + 7/− 10 were found in #4 (+ 7p/− 10) and #15 (+ 7q/− 10q).

Tumor immune microenvironment analysis

Morphologically, abundant CD8+ T cells were distributed in patches or clusters within the tumor cell nests of LS-GBMs (Fig. 6a), whereas such infiltration was rare in IDH-wt cGBMs (Fig. 6b). Treatment and survival analysis

Among the 29 cases with LS-GBM, 24 cases underwent standard maximal safe resection, while 5 cases received subtotal resection to preserve neurological function and minimize surgical complications. Postoperatively, all cases received combined chemoradiotherapy (Stupp's regimen), except for Patient #4 and #15, who were unable to tolerate chemoradiotherapy due to poor postoperative physical condition. Both patients experienced disease progression and died within 4 months after surgery.

Discussion

In this study, we performed an integrated analysis of the clinicopathological characteristics, molecular landscape, and tumor immune microenvironment of Lynch syndrome-associated primary glioblastoma (LS-GBM), providing insights for diagnosis and treatment. Primary brain tumors occur in approximately 14% of LS families, with GBM as the most frequent subtype (56%) [9]. In our cohort, MSH2 germline mutations predominated (59.3%), followed by MLH1 (22.2%) and MSH6 (3.7%), consistent with prior data indicating an increased GBM risk in MSH2-mutated LS families [9]. LS-GBMs patients presented at a significantly younger age compared to those with IDH-wildtype (IDH-wt) conventional GBMs (cGBMs), aligning with previous reports [7]. LS-GBMs demonstrated no significant differences from cGBMs in either clinical presentation or radiological features. Histologically, however, as previously reported [7, 12, 13], these tumors exhibited prominent multinucleated giant cells. More specifically, our analysis reveals that a wreath-like nuclear pattern within these giant cells, together with occasional oligodendroglioma-like areas, is a frequent and characteristic finding, which provides powerful morphological evidence for underlying MMRD. In addition, MMR immunohistochemistry was confirmed as a rapid and reliable screening tool, demonstrating high sensitivity (100%) and specificity (96%) [14].

At the molecular level, most LS-GBMs were IDH-wildtype and notably lacked the TERT promoter mutations and the + 7/− 10 signature, distinguishing them from IDH-wt cGBMs. Consistent with prior findings [7, 15, 16], we found TP53 was the most frequently altered gene. The loss of p53 function impairs cell cycle control and apoptosis, permitting unchecked proliferation, accumulation of DNA damage, and contributing to the development of multinucleated cells in GBMs [17, 18]. SETD2 mutations were specific to high-grade gliomas (HGGs) affecting 15% of pediatric HGGs and 8% of adult HGGs and predominantly observed in tumors located in the cerebral hemispheres [19]. It encodes a histone methyltransferase responsible for catalyzing trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 36 (H3K36me3). This epigenetic mark recruits hMutSα (via MSH6) to chromatin to initiate DNA mismatch repair before replication [20]. Furthermore, SETD2 mutation impairs α-tubulin trimethylation, causing genomic instability by disrupting spindle formation and cell division [21]. We therefore posit that the convergence of MMRD, TP53 loss, and SETD2 inactivation creates a synergistic effect, leading to profoundly impaired DNA repair, extensive mutation accumulation, and severe genomic instability (Fig. 10).Fig. 10Schematic overview of potential pathogenic mechanisms and therapeutic targets of glioblastoma in Lynch syndrome

In this study, a notable finding in our cohort was the nearly universal administration of TMZ. The PFS in LS-GBM patients was not significantly shortened compared to other subgroups, and the OS was significantly longer than in IDH-wt cGBMs. This clinical outcome suggests that TMZ may retain therapeutic efficacy in primary MMR-deficient setting. However, it has been reported that down-regulation of DNA MMR protein is one of the mechanisms leading to TMZ resistance in glioma, as it impairs the recognition and repair of TMZ-induced O6-methylguanine adducts [22]. These studies, however, have primarily focused on and demonstrated acquired MMRD and subsequent TMZ resistance in GBM following TMZ treatment [23–25]. Observations of TMZ resistance in an MMRD context have largely been limited to MMR-knockout glioma cell lines (reviewd by Lee) [26] and mouse xenograft models [27]. Clinical evidence specifically addressing the efficacy of TMZ in patients with primary MMRD GBM remains scarce. An earlier clinical analysis even suggests that MMRD may not be the primary driver of TMZ resistance in adult glioma [28]. The discrepancy between the real-world survival data and the preclinical model of resistance highlights a critical knowledge gap. It underscores the need for future, prospectively designed multicenter studies with larger cohorts to definitively evaluate the activity of TMZ in primary MMRD GBMs and to elucidate the underlying molecular determinants of treatment response.

We also identified functional activation of PDGFRA and EGFR in LS-GBMs, indicating that tumor proliferation and invasion are driven by receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) signaling, particularly through the Ras/MAPK pathway (Fig. 10). In addition, we observed a high frequency of NF1 inactivating mutations, which contributes to sustained Ras/MAPK pathway activation by disrupting its negative regulatory control. In a recent GBM classification based on MAPK pathway activation, over 25% of GBM were grouped into the G3/NF1 category, with LS-GBMs falling within the G3/MMR molecular subgroup, which may respond to targeted therapies such as RAF or MEK inhibitors [29, 30]. Moreover, a subset of LS-GBMs in our study exhibited PTEN inactivation and PIK3CA activation, suggesting aberrant signaling through the PI3K/AKT pathway, which may further drive gliomagenesis [29, 30]. Reflecting these findings, Patient #3 and #12 in our cohort received adjuvant RTK inhibitors (Anlotinib and Erlotinib, respectively) targeting EGFR, FGFR, and PDGFR, with analysis of RTK gene variants revealing that many patients experience survival benefits from specific RTK inhibitors [31–33].

The potential impact of the immune microenvironment was explored in two recurrent GBM patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Although used as second-line therapy, both achieved sustained tumor control and long-term survival, suggesting that multiple immunological factors may contribute to clinical outcomes. Patient #3, despite being microsatellite stable (MSS) and having no PD-L1 expression (CPS 0), achieved a remarkable response to pembrolizumab. This response may be attributed to the combination of a high tumor mutational burden (TMB) of 52.25 muts/Mb and a pre-existing, robust T-cell infiltrate (CD8+ density 147/mm2). This observation aligns with pan-cancer data suggesting that a T-cell-inflamed tumor microenvironment, coupled with a high neoantigen load, can drive responses to PD-1 blockade independent of PD-L1 status [34, 35]. In contrast, Patient #12, with high PD-L1 expression (CPS 40) and CD8+ density (626/mm2), initially responded to toripalimab but later progressed, illustrating the challenge of adaptive immune resistance. The subsequent response to the PD-1/CTLA-4 dual-specific antibody cadonilimab suggests that targeting complementary immune pathways may re-invigorate anti-tumor immunity after initial immune checkpoint blockade failure [36]. However, it should be noted that immunotherapy is not part of the current standard of care for GBM. Consequently, only two patients in our cohort received immune checkpoint blockade. Additionally, while MSI, TMB, and PD-L1 data were collected for the LS-GBM group, these biomarkers were not assessed in the IDH-wt cGBM control group, limiting meaningful comparative analysis across the entire cohort. Future studies should expand the cohort size and explore the relationship between these biomarkers and treatment response.

In conclusion, our comprehensive clinical, histopathological, molecular, and tumor microenvironment analysis provides substantial evidence that LS-GBMs constitute a distinct subtype of glioblastoma. These tumors are characterized by a prior history of cancer, located supratentorially, presence of multinucleated cells with a wreath-like pattern, MMRD immunohistochemistry, frequently inactivating mutations in TP53, SETD2, and NF1, activating mutations in PDGFRA and EGFR, co-occurring high TMB, an abundant CD8+ T-cell infiltrate. Individual germline genetic testing and family counseling offer life-saving surveillance for variant carriers. Further studies will focus on the dynamics of the immune microenvironment and the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade therapies. Therefore, an integrated clinical, histopathological, molecular, and microenvironmental data is essential for accurate diagnosis and treatment strategy selection for these tumors.
