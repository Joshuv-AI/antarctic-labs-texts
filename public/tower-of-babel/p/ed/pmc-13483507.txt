Associated factors of undernutrition among women of reproductive age living with HIV in Mahama Refugee Camp, Rwanda
Authors: Uchechukwu Kevin Nwanna, Rebecca Suubi, Owoisinke Victor Archibong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLow dietary diversity in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) usually results from households basing their diets on a small number of food groups beyond their staple foods. Numerous studies have demonstrated that among people in both developed and developing nations, dietary diversity is a reliable indicator of dietary quality. Humanitarian crises over the past few decades have altered the patterns of food consumption in refugee camps, where the prevalence of food insecurity has significantly grown, resulting in undernutrition and chronic diseases. Therefore, our study determined the factors associated with undernutrition among women of reproductive age living with HIV in Mahama refugee camp, Rwanda.MethodsUsing a cross-sectional design, our study examined the association between undernutrition (outcome) and household diversity scores, access to kitchen gardens, and chronic illness. The study was conducted at the Mahama refugee camp, which was home to 58,248 people. Yemane’s algorithm was used to recruit a sample size of 170 women of reproductive age living with HIV for this study, while utilizing a random sampling technique. Anthropometric measurements (BMI and MUAC) of the respondents were examined; SPSS version 25 was used for data analysis.ResultsAccording to this study, the majority of women in the refugee camp ate grains (98%) and pulses (86%). The majority had a 68% medium diversity score. Additionally, access to kitchen garden (AOR = 0.391, p = 0.014, 95% CI = 0.185–0.825), dietary scores of highest (AOR = 0.039, p = 0.001, 95% CI = 0.033–0.475) and medium (AOR = 0.125, p = 0.002, 95% CI = 0.006–0.242) were found to be significantly associated with lower odds of undernourishment in the camp. Though not statistically significant, having any other chronic illness was associated with a higher risk of undernourishment (AOR = 2.359, p = 0.102, 95% CI = 0.843–6.602).ConclusionsGrains, legumes, fruits, seeds, nuts, and vegetables made up the majority of the food consumed by the 170 sampled women refugees. The risk of undernutrition was also significantly reduced in women who had access to kitchen gardens and showed high and medium dietary scores; a tendency was also observed for the risk of undernutrition to increase in respondents with extra chronic conditions. To enhance dietary scores and promote fair access to the kitchen garden, comprehensive healthcare interventions should be implemented in the camp.Keywords: Dietary practices, Household Diversity Score, Kitchen gardens, Undernutrition, Reproductive-aged women, HIV

Introduction

HIV induces immune dysfunction, leading to opportunistic infections (OIs) that can reduce appetite, increase energy expenditure, affect nutrient absorption, and alter the body’s ability to metabolize and excrete nutrients, resulting in nutritional deficiencies and poor overall nutritional status [1]. Weight loss, fever, diarrhea, and cough are prominent symptoms of the phases of AIDS that make the immune system susceptible to opportunistic infections [2]. However, poor nutrition and nutritional deficiencies can subsequently expedite the progression of women living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA) to lose their immunity and advance to the AIDS stage more quickly and heighten the risk of developing opportunistic infections [3–6]. Thus, undernutrition results from immunological dysfunction brought on by HIV/AIDS and dietary practices that weaken the immune system [7].

Depression, decreased appetite, and several frequent opportunistic infections like oral thrush are known to contribute to undernutrition in PLWHA [8]. Undernutrition is regarded as one of the most important, yet untreated, health issues in the world [9]. Underweight, wasting, stunting, and vitamin and mineral deficits are all included. According to the data, women in developing nations who have a body mass index (BMI) of less than 18.5 kg/m2 are more likely to get sick and have a steadily rising death rate [10]. Goal 3 of the United Nations’ 2015 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) aims to enhance health for people of all ages [11]. Targets 3.3 and 3.4, which aim to stop the HIV/AIDS epidemic and lower premature mortality from non-communicable diseases, including undernutrition, are part of this goal [12]. Nonetheless, these illnesses remain serious global public health concerns. An estimated 38 million people worldwide were anticipated to be living with HIV/AIDS in 2019, and 67% of those who were aware of their status were receiving antiretroviral therapy [13].

Due to conflicts and climate-related shocks, including droughts, floods, and storms, internally displaced people (IDPs) and refugees face health, nutrition, and food insecurity issues while staying at camp sites. An unprecedented 100 million people are currently experiencing forced displacement, according to the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) [14]. Women and children are especially at risk among the displaced persons. In the larger delivery of health services, their unique nutritional and food security needs are frequently disregarded. Refugees often differ in complete government support, rates of disease, food insecurity, and malnutrition differ between refugee camps [15]. This population is occasionally overlooked by humanitarian actors, and their well-being is further affected by the complex interactions of factors such as age, gender, socioeconomic status, access to health and nutrition services, and specific dietary requirements [15].

Research shows that 2.6 million Africans were internally displaced in 2016, and another 5.4 million were officially recognized as refugees [16, 17]. Political violence, banditry, terrorism, natural disasters like floods or droughts, and compounding factors like the growing famine and drought emergency have all been identified as causes of displacement in Africa [17–19]. There is a significant lack of compiled data on the health, nutrition, and food security outcomes of displaced women and children in nations like Somalia, Uganda, and Rwanda, where the number of displaced people is among the highest in the world [20]. Existing vulnerabilities are made worse by displacement, and meeting the needs of women and children in these situations is still very difficult. Reproductive-aged women and children in refugee camps scattered across sub-Saharan Africa suffer from undernutrition and food insecurity due to a variety of factors, including insufficient nutritional intake, age and gender demographics, healthcare access, caregiver health, family poverty, prenatal care, and the frequency of childhood diseases [21]. These conditions frequently result in symptoms including stunting, wasting, and underweight [15, 20, 21].

The World Food Program (WFP) and the United Nations High Commissioner of Refugees (UNHCR) provide inadequate food assistance, but refugees in Rwanda, like those in other camps around the world, live in challenging conditions that have a detrimental impact on their nutritional status due to limited access to a variety of foods and a lack of space to grow crops for harvest [22]. Stunting was 23.8% in 2013 and 28.8% in 2017 among children and pregnant/lactating women, while 9.8% of women had anaemia in 2017 despite regular cash-for-food assistance and nutrition awareness campaigns, according to assessments conducted in Kigeme refugee camp in 2013 and 2017 [23, 24]. Therefore, the objective of this research was to identify the factors determining undernutrition among women of reproductive age living with HIV in Mahama refugee camp, Rwanda.

Methodology

Research design

This study utilized a cross-sectional methodology to identify the determinants of dietary patterns among undernourished women of reproductive age living with HIV in Mahama refugee camp.

Study setting and population

The study site was the Mahama refugee camp, located in the Kirehe District of Rwanda’s Eastern Province and established in 2015. On 160 hectares of land, the camp houses refugees [25]. There are currently 58,248 refugees living there. Our study focused on women who were of reproductive age (15–49 years) and living with HIV.

Inclusion criteria

All women in the Mahama refugee camp who were of reproductive age and living with HIV during the study period.

Exclusion criteria

Women who were seriously ill, incapable of communicating, or incapacitated were not included in this study.

Sample size

This study was conducted in the Mahama I and Mahama II medicalized health centres, which are a part of the Mahama refugee camp. According to the health facilities’ registry books and data, 294 registered childbearing women received nutrition services in 2023. All registered women of reproductive age (living with HIV) who ate fortified blended meals were eligible to participate in the research. The entire sample size was determined to be 170 women of reproductive age living with HIV using Yamane’s (1967) [26] technique for a specific, confined population.

Sampling strategies and procedures

Simple random sampling was employed to ensure that each eligible participant had an equal probability of selection. From a sampling frame of 294 women living with HIV as evidenced in the clinic registry, 170 participants were selected. To maintain fairness and transparency, participants anonymously drew folded papers from a container containing 170 marked “Yes” and 124 marked “No.” Those who selected “Yes” were invited to participate in the study, while those who selected “No” were not included. The process was conducted privately to maintain discretion and minimize potential discomfort. Participation was entirely voluntary, and declining or not being selected had no impact on access to services or support. The study protocol, including the sampling procedure, received ethical approval from the relevant ethics review committee. This approach was used to maintain randomness while avoiding researcher-driven selection that could introduce bias.

Data collection methods and analysis

Respondents were asked if they had any chronic disease apart from HIV, and questions on dietary practices included the availability of kitchen gardens and food groups. These food groups (grains, white roots, tubers and plantains, nuts and seeds, milk and milk products, meat, poultry and fish, eggs, dark green leafy vegetables, vitamin A-rich fruits and vegetables, other vegetables and fruits), determined the Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS) [27]. In addition, women of reproductive age were asked if they had consumed any of these food groups the week before the data collection commenced.

Thereafter, every food group that was ingested among the women of reproductive age was given a score of “1,” but any food group that was not consumed was given a score of “0.” The overall HDDS was calculated by adding the results for each food group. Low dietary diversity was defined as having an HDDS of three or less (≤ 3), medium dietary diversity as having an HDDS of four or five (4 and 5), and high dietary diversity as having an HDDS of six or above (≥ 6) [28].

Women living with HIV were measured and documented anthropometrically using the MUAC for pregnant women and those still exclusively breastfeeding; whereas BMI measurement was used for non-pregnant and lactating women older than six months. Individuals were classified according to the WHO reference standard using BMI and MUAC cut-offs; those with a BMI of less than 18.5 kg/m2 and a MUAC of less than 23 cm were deemed undernourished.

Descriptive statistics, such as percentages and frequencies, were then used to analyze these data. Our study’s primary finding was the women's dietary diversity score. IBM SPSS version 25 was used to conduct the binary regression analysis, which identified the factors predicting undernutrition among the respondents. Variables were deemed significant if their significance level was less than or equal to 5%.

Furthermore, potential covariates were selected based on prior literature on determinants of undernutrition among people living with HIV, as well as variables that showed associations in the bivariate analysis. These included socio-demographic characteristics (age, religion, marital status, education level, occupation, household number, and monthly income), health-related factors (presence of chronic illness), and dietary-related factors (dietary diversity score and access to kitchen gardens). These variables were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model to control for potential confounding.

Results

Descriptive analysis

Most of the women reported not having any other chronic disease (62.4%), and the majority of them had a kitchen garden (61.8%). Respondents were asked to state their consumption of the ten different food groups in the past week; majority of the women of reproductive age stated that they had consumed grains, white roots, tubers and plantains (97.6%), 85.9% consumed pulses, 85.3% ate fruit, and 26.5% ate vitamin A-rich fruits. Discussion

Food rations and dietary diversity scores

Our findings revealed that most women of reproductive age in our sample consumed grains (98%), pulses (86%), fruits (85%), seeds and nuts (74%), and dark green leafy vegetables and other vegetables (71%) and (52%), respectively. In addition, majority of women of reproductive age in Mahama refugee camp fell into a medium household dietary diversity score. Eating a variety of food types improves immune system performance, energy balance, and dietary adequacy (protein, iron, and vitamin A). Our analysis further revealed that women who had medium and highest dietary diversity scores showed significantly lower odds towards undernutrition (AOR, P-value – 0.125, 0.002; 0.039, 0.001). These findings suggest that attaining a high level of dietary diversity provides significantly lower odds against undernutrition. Furthermore, our findings contrast with data from Saharawi refugees living in Algeria [29], which revealed that most foods consumed were staple foods, flesh foods, and dairy products, and that the majority had a low dietary score. In addition, studies in low- and middle-income countries, such as Cambodia (23%) [30], Tanzania (69%) [31], Bangladesh (26%) [32] and Nepal (26%) [33], all reported poor dietary diversity and low household dietary diversity scores. These data differences relative to our study can be attributed to the humanitarian assistance received in camp [34], such as regular food distribution programs, nutritional supplementation initiatives, and support aimed at improving food security among refugee households. In addition, the protracted nature of the camp environment may facilitate the development of coping strategies such as small-scale food production and livelihood activities. However, previous research suggests that humanitarian assistance and food support programs are not uniformly distributed across refugee settings in sub-Saharan Africa, which may contribute to variations in dietary diversity and nutritional outcomes across camps [35, 36].

Kitchen garden

Respondents who had access to kitchen gardens from our study showed lower rates of undernutrition compared to those who did not. Specifically, access to a kitchen garden showed significantly lower odds of undernutrition (AOR = 0.391). Having direct access to veggies that are high in micronutrients enhances the quality of one’s diet, increases the frequency of meals, and lessens the need for restricted food rations. Access to kitchen gardens may prevent undernutrition, according to our findings, which were statistically significant at the p < 0.05 level (0.014). These findings align with studies from women refugees in Palestine (p < 0.01) [37], Syria [38, 39], Lebanon [40], and Canada [41], which showed a significant decline in undernutrition among women when access to a kitchen garden is made possible. These similarities in data suggest that family food production through access to kitchen gardens has a significant protective effect on undernutrition.

Chronic disease

HIV-related wasting is made worse by illnesses like TB, which raise the catabolic state, decrease appetite, and compete for nutrients. Women in the camp who stated they had another chronic disease (apart from HIV), such as TB and Hypertension, had more than twice the odds (AOR = 2.359) of being undernourished as compared to those without TB and Hypertension. Despite these findings not being statistically significant (p = 0.102), they indicate a trend towards higher odds of undernutrition and suggest that chronic illness may increase the risk of undernutrition among the respondents. Our findings align with studies from Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh [42], Western Sahara refugees [43], Lebanon [44], and low-income countries (Somalia, Syria, Cambodia, Bhutan, Palestine, Sudan) [45], which showed significant associations/risks between chronic diseases (obesity, hypertension, type-2 diabetes) with undernutrition among refugees. These findings underscore the urgent need for targeted interventions to reduce the burden of chronic disease and improve holistic healthcare accessibility in refugee camps.

Conclusion

We discovered that most of the refugees consumed grains, pulses, fruits, seeds, nuts, and vegetables. Additionally, access to kitchen gardens showed a significant decrease in the risk of undernutrition; a majority of women had medium to high dietary diversity scores. There was a tendency for the respondents’ risk of undernutrition to rise when they had additional chronic illnesses. Therefore, to lessen the burden of undernutrition in refugee camps among women of reproductive age living with HIV, interventions that offer holistic care are crucial for raising women of reproductive age’s household dietary diversity scores and ensuring that everyone has equitable access to kitchen gardens.

Limitations of the study

Despite the strengths of this study, these stood as a limitation. Although the study employed a statistically adequate random sample of 170 participants, which was adequate to address the study objectives, including a larger proportion of the eligible population (n = 294), it could have increased variability in responses and further strengthened the findings, particularly given the vulnerability of the study population.

Additionally, while the study’s findings suggest a potential protective role of household food production, these results should be interpreted within the context of the structural limitations present in refugee camps. Future research is needed to explore context-appropriate and sustainable approaches to improving dietary diversity and food security among refugee populations, including strategies that complement existing food assistance programs.

The use of a 7-day recall for dietary diversity may introduce recall bias compared to the standard 24-hour recall. Future studies in this population should consider the MDD-W 24-hour protocol.

Additionally, due to the study design employed, the results cannot establish a causal relationship with the study’s outcome variable.
