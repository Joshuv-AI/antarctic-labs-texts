T2-FLAIR mismatch sign in nonenhancing astrocytoma, IDH-mutant: impact of tumor area on diagnostic sensitivity
Authors: Iori Ozono, Fumiyuki Yamasaki, Shumpei Onishi, Ushio Yonezawa, Akira Taguchi, Yusuke Takeishi, Novita Ikbar Khairunnisa, Vishwa Jeet Amatya, Yukio Takeshima, Nobutaka Horie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe T2-fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2-FLAIR) mismatch sign is a highly specific imaging biomarker of astrocytoma, IDH-mutant; however, its clinical utility is limited by its low sensitivity. This study aimed to investigate factors contributing to false-negative findings, focusing on the impact of tumor area and quantitative thresholds used for diagnosis.MethodsThis retrospective, single-institution study included 38 patients with pathologically confirmed, nonenhancing astrocytoma. The T2-FLAIR mismatch sign was evaluated using two quantitative area cutoffs (> 50% and > 33%). Associations with CNS WHO grade, CDKN2A homozygous deletion (HD) status (assessed by MTAP surrogate), and tumor area were analyzed. Subgroup analysis was performed for 33 patients excluding cases with CDKN2A HD.ResultsThe positivity rate of the mismatch sign in nonenhancing astrocytoma increased from 55.3% at the > 50% cutoff to 73.7% at the > 33% cutoff. In the overall cohort, tumor area was significantly smaller in the mismatch-negative group compared to the mismatch-positive group; however, this difference was significant only at the > 33% threshold (p = 0.0432, Mann–Whitney U test). The group difference in tumor area became more significant at the > 33% threshold after excluding CDKN2A HD cases (p = 0.0078, Mann–Whitney U test). We observed mismatch-negative nonenhancing astrocytomas without CDKN2A HD became mismatch-positive during the tumor growth.ConclusionAbsence of the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign is an ambiguous finding representing two distinct entities: biologically aggressive tumors with CDKN2A HD or early-stage, small astrocytoma. A negative mismatch sign should not exclude astrocytoma, IDH-mutant from the differential diagnosis—particularly in small nonenhancing glioma cases.Keywords: Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, T2-FLAIR mismatch sign, Nonenhancing glioma

Introduction

Diagnosis of central nervous system (CNS) tumors has been fundamentally reshaped by the 5th Edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification, which integrates molecular diagnostics with traditional histology [1]. This approach has led to a more precise and biologically relevant subclassification of gliomas, establishing “astrocytoma, IDH-mutant” as a distinct entity. Reliance on postsurgical molecular data for diagnosis underscores the critical need for reliable, noninvasive biomarkers that can preoperatively predict tumor subtypes. Such biomarkers are crucial for guiding surgical strategy, predicting prognosis, and planning subsequent therapies.

In this context, radiological features correlating with molecular tumor subtypes have become an area of intense research. Among such markers, the T2-fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2-FLAIR) mismatch sign has emerged as a highly specific imaging biomarker for astrocytoma [2]. This sign is characterized by a hyperintense signal on T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) with near-complete signal suppression on FLAIR imaging, often with a hyperintense peripheral rim. The high specificity of this sign—over 95% for distinguishing astrocytoma from other diffuse lower grade gliomas [3, 4]—has established its value in clinical practice. Although the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign is occasionally observed in other entities such as dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumors (DNET) [5] and diffuse midline gliomas [6], its diagnostic utility for astrocytoma remains robust.

Despite this excellent specificity, the clinical utility of the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign is significantly hampered by its low sensitivity, with reported rates ranging from 30% to 60% [2–4]. Because a substantial proportion of astrocytomas do not exhibit this mismatch sign, this may lead to misinterpretation and diagnostic uncertainty. Several factors have been proposed to explain the limited sensitivity of the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign, including progression to a higher CNS WHO grade [7] and the presence of a CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion (HD) [8, 9], both of which are associated with lower prevalence of this sign. Interobserver variability in qualitative assessment and dependence on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) acquisition parameters have also been suggested as potential confounders [10, 11]. Recent studies have extended the evaluation of the T2-FLAIR mismatch phenomenon beyond qualitative assessment. Cho et al. demonstrated that larger tumor volumes are associated with greater quantitatively measured mismatch extent using T2-FLAIR subtraction maps and whole-tumor segmentation [12]. In addition, longitudinal analyses have reported redevelopment and evolution of the mismatch sign during tumor progression [7]. However, it remains unclear how tumor size at initial presentation affects visually interpreted, reader-estimated mismatch thresholds used in routine clinical practice, particularly as a contributor to false-negative mismatch findings.

Accordingly, we focused on tumor area at initial presentation as a potential contributor to false-negative mismatch classification in nonenhancing astrocytoma, IDH-mutant. We hypothesized that, in smaller or earlier-stage tumors, the underlying biological processes responsible for the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign may not be fully developed or that imaging artifacts, such as partial volume effects of surrounding normal brain tissue, may obscure the sign’s appearance. To assess this hypothesis, this study quantitatively investigated the relationship between T2-FLAIR mismatch sign positivity and key clinical-pathological factors, focusing specifically on the impact of tumor area.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective study identified patients treated at our institution between October 2009 and November 2025 who met the following inclusion criteria: (1) pathologically confirmed diagnosis of astrocytoma, IDH-mutant; (2) availability of preoperative MRI, including T2WI, FLAIR, and pre- and postcontrast T1-weighted imaging (T1WI); and (3) tumors without obvious gadolinium contrast enhancement on postcontrast T1WI. A total of 38 cases met these eligibility criteria. Clinical data, including age at diagnosis, gender, pathological data, CNS WHO grade, T2-FLAIR mismatch sign (> 50% or > 33%), and tumor area, were obtained from medical records. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hiroshima University (approval number: E2022-0038). The requirement for informed patient consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of this study.

Histopathological diagnosis and molecular signature analysis

Surgically resected tumor specimens were fixed in 10% phosphate-buffered formalin and subsequently embedded in paraffin blocks. Standard histological diagnosis was confirmed by staining representative slides with hematoxylin-eosin reagent. Immunohistochemical staining for IDH1-R132H and α-thalassemia X-linked intellectual disability (ATRX) was performed using an automated immunostainer (BenchMark GX; Ventana). The 1p/19q codeletions were detected by fluorescence in situ hybridization analysis of paraffin-embedded tumor tissue samples [13]. S-methyl-50-thioadenosine phosphorylase (MTAP) was assessed by immunostaining, which is reported to be an excellent surrogate marker of CDKN2A HD [14, 15]. All tumors were diagnosed according to the 5th Edition of the WHO Classification of Tumors of the CNS guidelines [1].

MR acquisition and evaluation

All MR scans were acquired using 3.0 T scanners (Ingenia CX 3.0 T, Philips Healthcare, Best, Netherlands; or Signa Excite HD 3.0 T, GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, WI, USA). The preoperative imaging protocol included T1WI, T2WI, FLAIR, and postcontrast T1WI imaging. MRI examinations were performed using standard clinical protocols at our institution, consistent with previously published studies [16].

Tumor area (mm2) was measured on the axial FLAIR image demonstrating the largest cross-sectional tumor extent. The maximum diameter and its perpendicular diameter were recorded on this slice, and the tumor area was calculated as the product of these two measurements. Axial FLAIR images were obtained with a slice thickness of 5 mm, consistent with standard clinical imaging protocols at our institution. Multiple imaging datasets were available for three patients due to course observation after tumor detection. In these cases, we calculated the tumor area at initial diagnosis and immediately before surgical intervention. The presence or absence of the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign was determined using “initial” imaging dataset. Radiological evaluation was independently performed by two board-certified neurosurgeons (I.O., 10 years of experience; S.O., 12 years of experience) who were blinded to pathological data and each other’s assessments. The T2-FLAIR mismatch sign was designated as per its original definition [2]: the presence of a complete/near-complete hyperintense signal on T2WI and a relatively hypointense signal on FLAIR, except a hyperintense peripheral rim. Following qualitative identification of the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign, the proportion of the lesion demonstrating FLAIR suppression was estimated based on structured visual assessment of the entire lesion across axial images rather than a single slice. In line with established methods, the extent of mismatch was stratified and tumors were classified as positive using two separate thresholds, namely involvement of > 50% of the total lesion and a more inclusive threshold of > 33% [17]. These thresholds represent reader-estimated proportions rather than segmentation-based quantitative measurements.

Interrater agreement for the presence of the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign at both thresholds was calculated using Cohen’s kappa statistic (κ values were interpreted as follows: 0–0.40, poor; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, good; and 0.81–1.00, excellent). In cases of discordant initial assessments, the two reviewers discussed their findings to reach a consensus. If a consensus could not be reached, a senior neurosurgeon (F.Y., 32 years of experience) served as an adjudicator to make the final determination.

Statistical analysis

Patients were divided into two groups based on the presence or absence of the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign. Characteristics and clinical information were compared between these two groups using Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables, such as tumor area, were compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP® Pro version 18 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Results with a p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical and radiological characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the clinicopathological characteristics of the 38 patients included in the final analysis. Regarding the primary imaging finding, the positivity rate of the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign was dependent on the applied quantitative threshold: When the > 50% cutoff was used, 21 of 38 patients (55.3%) were classified as mismatch-positive. When the more inclusive > 33% cutoff was used, the number of positive cases increased to 28 of 38 patients (73.7%). Inter-reviewer agreement for these mismatch signs was excellent (κ = 0.839 and 1.000, respectively).

Subgroup analysis excluding cases with CDKN2A HD

As CDKN2A HD is a powerful molecular determinant of the absence of the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign [8], we performed a subgroup analysis of the 33 patients without CDKN2A HD to specifically isolate the influence of tumor area on the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign. Using the cutoff of > 50% in this CDKN2A-intact cohort, there were no significant associations between the mismatch sign and any factors, including CNS WHO grade and tumor area (Table 2). However, when the > 33% cutoff was used, tumor area emerged as the sole factor significantly associated with the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign. Mismatch-negative tumors were significantly smaller than mismatch-positive tumors (median area: 250.7 mm² vs. 1842.9 mm², p = 0.0078, Fig. 1B). No other factors, including CNS WHO grade, were associated with the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign in this subgroup.

Illustrative cases: emergence of the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign with tumor growth

In addition to the cross-sectional analysis based on preoperative imaging at the time of surgery, we present three representative cases with available earlier imaging to illustrate the temporal evolution of the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign (Fig. 2). In these patients, the tumors were initially mismatch-negative but became mismatch-positive at the time of surgical intervention. The statistical analyses were performed using the preoperative images included in the cohort; earlier images are shown solely to demonstrate longitudinal evolution. These cases of pathologically confirmed, CNS WHO grade 2, astrocytoma, IDH-mutant were initially T2-FLAIR mismatch-negative at diagnosis, but became mismatch-positive at follow-up.

Illustrative cases: Emergence of the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign with tumor growth. Case 1: A 34-year-old female patient underwent MRI due to headache. Axial T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) (A) and FLAIR (B) images revealed a small, 12.5 × 8.2 mm lesion in the right frontal lobe that was hyperintense on both sequences, consistent with T2-FLAIR mismatch-negative status. The lesion increased in size, and follow-up MRI at 3 years and 1 month indicated a tumor area of 31.0 × 15.5 mm (C–E). The postcontrast T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) (C) confirms continued absence of enhancement. The lesion now showed marked signal suppression in its central portion on the FLAIR image (E), consistent with conversion to a mismatch-positive status (mismatch area > 50%). Case 2: A 35-year-old male patient underwent MRI due to epilepsy. Axial T2WI (F) and FLAIR (G) images revealed a 24.4 × 18.4 mm lesion in the left frontal lobe that was hyperintense on both sequences and classified as T2-FLAIR mismatch-negative. After a follow-up period of 6 years and 9 months without chemotherapy and radiotherapy, MRI revealed significant growth (H–J) with the tumor area increasing to 59.1 × 49.2 mm. The lesion developed a typical T2-FLAIR mismatch sign with a hypointense core and a distinct hyperintense peripheral rim on the FLAIR image (J). The mismatch area was > 50%. The postcontrast T1WI (H) showed no evidence of gadolinium enhancement. Case 3: A 68-year-old male patient was diagnosed with a right frontal lobe lesion after a medical checkup. Initial T2WI (K) and FLAIR (L) images at diagnosis revealed a 22.5 × 14.5 mm, nonenhancing lesion in the right frontal lobe, classified as T2-FLAIR mismatch-negative. He was misdiagnosed as having asymptomatic infarction. MRI at 9 years and 6 months revealed significant growth (M–O), with the lesion now measuring 47.4 × 32.0 mm. The core of the tumor was clearly hypointense on the FLAIR image (O), indicating conversion to a mismatch-positive status with a mismatch area of > 50%. The lesion remained nonenhancing (M)

Discussion

This study investigated factors influencing the variable presentation of the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign, which is strongly associated with astrocytoma, IDH-mutant. While previous studies have established this sign’s high specificity for differentiating these tumors from other glioma subtypes [2–4], its limited sensitivity remains a significant clinical challenge. The principal contribution of this study is its demonstration that a negative T2-FLAIR mismatch sign is not a singular phenomenon but a radiological finding driven by two distinct and identifiable pathways: (1) biological progression, characterized by a higher CNS WHO grade and/or the development of CDKN2A HD; and (2) physical or developmental limitations inherent to small tumor volume in lower grade astrocytoma.

Biologically, the histopathological hallmarks underlying the mismatch sign, low cellularity, and prominent microcystic changes leading to high free water content [2, 16] may not be fully established at early stages of tumorigenesis. This hypothesis is supported by our three illustrative cases, which revealed clear conversion from T2-FLAIR mismatch-negative to mismatch-positive findings with tumor growth. Our observations are consistent with those of prior studies, suggesting that mismatch conspicuity can vary with tumor size and evolution. Cho et al. revealed an association between tumor volume and quantitatively measured mismatch extent using segmentation-based subtraction methods [12]. In addition, van Garderen et al. reported longitudinal evolution of the mismatch phenomenon during tumor progression and repeat resections in astrocytomas [7]. In contrast to these quantitative or longitudinal cohort analyses, our study specifically addresses the practical scenario of routine clinical interpretation by evaluating reader-estimated mismatch thresholds and a simple bidimensional size surrogate. When tumor area is modest, it may be difficult to capture heterogeneity inside the tumor on images. This could be physically explained by the partial volume effect, where the signal within a single imaging voxel is averaged when it contains a mixture of tumor and adjacent normal brain parenchyma [18]. For small lesions, such voxel averaging may dilute the tumor’s intrinsic signal, thereby obscuring the complete FLAIR suppression required to identify the mismatch sign. The practical significance of this effect in neuro-oncology imaging is well-recognized. For instance, automated tumor segmentation studies, such as that by Mureddu et al. [19], intentionally apply erosion techniques to mitigate this issue by removing boundary voxels, which are the most affected by partial volume averaging. This common practice underscores the tangible impact of the partial volume effect at lesion boundaries and provides strong support for our hypothesis that this mechanism is a primary contributor to false-negative T2-FLAIR mismatch findings in small astrocytoma.

Our findings also underscore the importance of the threshold used to define the mismatch sign. In the present study, mismatch proportions were determined by structured radiologist-estimated visual assessment rather than segmentation-based volumetric quantification. The stringent cutoff of > 50% failed to detect a significant association with tumor area even within the CDKN2A-intact subgroup, whereas the more relaxed > 33% threshold was sufficient to reveal this relationship. These observations should be interpreted in the context of prior literature. Cho et al. reported an association between tumor volume and quantitatively measured mismatch extent using segmentation-based subtraction methods [12], while Lasocki et al. stratified visually assessed mismatch into > 50%, 25–50%, and < 25% categories, and demonstrated preserved specificity at a ≥ 25% threshold [17]. In contrast, our study specifically examined the interaction of visually estimated thresholds with tumor size within routine clinical interpretation. Together, these findings indicate that in smaller or developing tumors, T2-FLAIR mismatch may initially appear in a limited or patchy distribution rather than throughout the entire lesion. Accordingly, more stringent visual thresholds may reduce sensitivity in small lesions.

We also noted a case presentation by Jen et al. [20] in which the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign was absent in small lesions but became positive after tumor growth. Our three illustrative cases are consistent with their findings. Moreover, our data confirmed that the tumor area of astrocytoma without CDKN2A HD is an independent factor statistically associated with the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign. Larger studies are necessary to confirm our findings regarding the cutoff value of < 50%.

This study is subject to several limitations. Firstly, its retrospective design and the relatively small cohort from a single institution may limit the generalizability of the findings. Secondly, our study was based on two-dimensional area measurements rather than segmentation-based volumetric analysis. Although volumetric methods may provide a more precise geometric quantification of tumor burden and mismatch extent, we intentionally adopted a simple bidimensional measurement approach to reflect routine clinical neuroradiological practice. In daily settings, perpendicular diameter measurements remain widely used because they are rapid, reproducible, and do not require dedicated segmentation software or additional post-processing. Our aim was to evaluate whether tumor size, estimated through a pragmatic and broadly applicable method, influences the sensitivity of the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign. Future studies incorporating volumetric segmentation may further refine these findings and help determine whether three-dimensional quantification provides incremental diagnostic value. Thirdly, we did not perform direct molecular analysis of CDKN2A/B HD. Instead, we used MTAP immunohistochemistry as a surrogate for CDKN2A HD, which is a well-validated method. Lastly, correlations with advanced imaging metrics, such as perfusion MRI or apparent diffusion coefficient values, could provide further insights into the underlying pathophysiology. Despite these limitations, the findings of this study can be used to support differential diagnosis of astrocytoma and IDH-mutant tumors.

Conclusions

The absence of the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign should be interpreted with caution when diagnosing gliomas. The findings of this study reveal that the absence of this sign can represent a biologically aggressive tumor with CDKN2A HD or, equally important, early-stage, small astrocytoma. Particularly in small, nonenhancing gliomas, a negative mismatch sign does not exclude the diagnosis of astrocytoma or IDH-mutant tumors. Such cases warrant close radiological follow-up to monitor tumor growth and potential emergence of this key imaging biomarker.
