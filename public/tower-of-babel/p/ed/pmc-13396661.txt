Automated image analysis for intracellular ice detection in monolayers by freeze-substitution
Authors: Ming Han Yu, Leah A. Marquez-Curtis, Janet A. W. Elliott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A major marker of cryoinjury is intracellular ice formation (IIF), and its prevention is a key design goal of a successful cryopreservation protocol. Visualization and automated image analysis of IIF will be helpful in gathering data for understanding the mechanisms of the freezing process. There is a need for high-quality frozen-cell images and a tool to quantify IIF for investigating the effects of various cryopreservation protocol parameters. Here, we report the development of a freeze-substitution-based method for light microscopy imaging of cryopreserved endothelial cell monolayers and an automated ice detection algorithm for high-throughput analysis of visible intracellular ice crystals in the nuclei of cells within the captured images. Our method detected ice crystals ≥1.10 ± 0.28 μm in the frozen-cell images and yielded comparable accuracy in ice detection to that achieved by manual counting. This study describes a tool for the identification and quantification of IIF visible under light microscopy.

Introduction

Cryopreservation is the only method for long-term storage and stable transport of cells and tissues while maintaining their viability and functions1–3. It plays an integral part in the handling of biological materials, from transplant therapies, translational research, and tissue engineering4–6, to genomic banking of plants and animals7,8. Cryopreservation enables on-demand accessibility to biologics for modeling and drug testing6,9, donor cells and tissues for transplant10–12, and pharmaceuticals and vaccines13. Cryopreservation has been identified as a global research priority1,14, whose advancements can translate to significant improvements in medicine and engineering. Not surprisingly, it has been attracting extraordinary interest as highlighted on covers of Science15 and Nature Biotechnology16, and in papers in Nature Methods17, Nature Medicine18, and Nature Communications19–23, among others.

The extreme stresses experienced by cells during cryopreservation can cause significant damage, compromising the cells’ post-thaw survival and function1,24,25. One of the predominant concerns is intracellular ice formation during the cooling process. As cells are cooled to low storage temperatures, intracellular water may freeze and grow as pure ice crystals, disrupting cell organelles and concentrating solutes3,25–27. For cells in suspension, formation of large intracellular ice during cooling is generally accepted to be fatal25,27–30. Large intracellular ice crystals greater than 3 µm, visible under standard optical microscopy such as light or fluorescence, usually result in cell death, while those less than 0.5 µm do not show immediate injury31,32. Cryopreservation protocols are developed with avoiding intracellular ice as a top priority. To this end, various chemicals called cryoprotectants or cryoprotective agents (CPAs) are continuously sought to manipulate the water thermodynamics and prevent ice formation30,33,34. Different cooling methods are also explored to control ice formation: either by safely dehydrating the cells in the presence of extracellular ice using an optimized slow-cooling rate determined through membrane permeability parameters1,3,27,29; or by forcing both intracellular and extracellular water to rapidly solidify into a glass-like state through vitrification, thereby evading ice crystal formation altogether1,32,35–37. For slow-cooling cryopreservation, intracellular ice formation during cooling is of primary concern, whereas in vitrification, ice recrystallization during warming dominates27,38. While the cryopreservation of cells in suspension is well established, the cryopreservation of multi-dimensional structures is much more challenging. Additional complexities in the freezing of cell monolayers compared with the freezing of cells in suspension include increased intracellular ice incidence and possible non-lethal intracellular ice formation39–41, suggesting that tissue-specific features such as cell−cell junctions and cell−surface adhesion41–45 can play a significant role in the cryobiological response of tissues.

Despite the importance of intracellular ice formation in cryopreservation, there exist very limited low-temperature imaging methods for visualizing and quantifying intracellular ice in tissue constructs during a cryopreservation protocol. Cryomicroscopy, coupled with high-speed video capture, is often used to image intracellular ice in monolayers during cooling46–48. However, this technique is limited to high sub-zero temperatures compared to the −196 °C liquid nitrogen storage temperature. In addition, the time and operational requirements of cryomicroscopy limit the number of samples that can be processed, resulting in most studies only examining a few protocol parameters39,46,49, which are not representative of a complete cryopreservation process. Freeze-substitution is another common low-temperature imaging technique used to visualize cell ultrastructure. It involves the gradual replacement of solid water with liquid organic solvent and cell fixative at around −80 °C, where ice recrystallization is deterred. This allows the cell materials to be fixed and the water from within the sample to be dissolved in the solvent, thus preserving the cell structures when warmed up to room temperature47,50–54, making it ideal for visualizing cells post-plunge into liquid nitrogen. Freeze-substitution has been extensively used with electron and light microscopy for cryo-imaging with no significant visual artifacts50,53–55. However, few studies have explored freeze-substitution for visualizing intracellular ice in cryopreservation applications, mostly focused on imaging visible intracellular ice in single cells56,57 or extracellular ice in large tissue sections58,59. The simplicity and established methodology of freeze-substitution make it an adaptable procedure, providing an opportunity to harness its potential for intracellular ice visualization of simple tissues undergoing the process of cryopreservation, and for high-throughput imaging of cryopreserved samples by light microscopy. The standard light microscope has a minimum resolvable distance of around 0.25 µm, allowing for the capture of visible ice crystals with a size greater than this limit.

Alongside these visualization methods, quantifying the number of cells with visible intracellular ice as seen under optical microscopy is typically done through manual counting by eye46,47,59, which is time-consuming, limited to low sample numbers, and prone to human bias. Other detection and quantification methods, such as X-ray diffraction32,60, tomography32, calorimetry61, and Raman spectroscopy31,37 have been used in cryopreservation studies of single cells, with the advantage of detecting tiny, non-optically visible ice crystals. However, these methods require specialized equipment; can detect and quantify ice, but require additional imaging methods to directly visualize ice; and are restricted to either measuring a single cell at a time or limited to small sample sizes due to operational complexity. Therefore, they are not appropriate for distinguishing intracellular ice in multicell constructs with closely packed cells and in large numbers of samples. Specific patterns47,48 and changes in image pixel intensities when ice forms within cells62 have been reported, but image processing techniques have not been explored due to complexities in image quality, such as inconsistencies in contrast and deformation of cell geometry. This is especially true for monolayers undergoing a complete cryopreservation protocol, because the closely packed cells and CPA molecules create staining artifacts that can further complicate analysis of the captured images.

To address these challenges, we developed a freeze-substitution-based intracellular ice visualization method by light microscopy and an associated automated ice detection algorithm to quantify the number of nuclei containing visible intracellular ice within the captured light microscopy images for cells in monolayers at liquid nitrogen storage temperature. We achieved rapid detection of visible intracellular ice in a large quantity of cryopreserved samples, producing comparable accuracy to manually counting by eye. The low-resource requirements and adaptability of our ice visualization and ice detection methods support their use in intracellular ice formation studies to assess different cell types, tissue constructs, and cryopreservation protocols, and in predicting cryopreservation outcomes.

Results

Description of the examined conditions

We developed our freeze-substitution-based ice visualization method and automated ice detection algorithm using four combinations of two monolayer cell types cultured on two adhesion surfaces. For each combination, we optimized our methods with five conditions, of which three have known visible intracellular ice crystal patterns in the cell nuclei (known conditions), and two have unknown but theoretically expected intermediate visible intracellular ice crystal patterns in the cell nuclei (unknown conditions), for a total of 20 conditions (Supplementary Table 1). The two monolayer cell types were human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and porcine corneal endothelial cells (PCECs). These two cell types had been successfully cryopreserved as monolayers using our published slow-cooling cryopreservation protocol63, and the optimal cryopreservation condition that produced the highest post-thaw viability was used as a known reference to evaluate whether modifications to the cryopreservation protocol had impacted the cryopreservation outcomes. The two adhesion surfaces, the materials on which the monolayers were cultured, were glass and Rinzl plastic. Glass is the typical surface used for imaging monolayer cultures. Rinzl is a transparent material with a coefficient of thermal expansion matched to that of ice and was shown to improve monolayer post-thaw survival in our previous studies64–67. It is hypothesized that mismatches in thermal expansion could introduce stresses that impact ice formation68.

The three known conditions were: (1) positive control (PC), with monolayers imaged directly from culture and not treated with any CPA nor cooled, representing no visible intracellular ice formation; (2) vitrified monolayers following our published protocol69, representing no visible intracellular ice formation and also demonstrating that recrystallization at −80 °C is slow enough to not form visible ice crystals before fixation, thus serving as a known condition for validating our freeze-substitution procedures by comparing with the PC; and (3) negative control (NC), with monolayers not treated with CPA but plunged into liquid nitrogen from −10 °C, representing complete intracellular ice formation. The two unknown conditions, chosen with representative CPA and cooling parameters identified in our previous slow-cooling cryopreservation studies64,65, were: (1) dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) −10 °C, with monolayers treated with 5% DMSO and slow-cooled to −10 °C before plunging into liquid nitrogen; and (2) hydroxyethyl starch (HES) −40 °C, with monolayers treated with 6% HES and slow-cooled to −40 °C before plunging into liquid nitrogen. We expect unpredictable intermediate visible intracellular ice crystal patterns in these two unknown conditions because they represent competing mechanisms. As a permeating CPA, DMSO reduces ice formation33,70, but rapid cooling from −10 °C with insufficient dehydration promotes intracellular ice formation1–3,24. As a non-permeating CPA, HES pre-emptively removes intracellular water but has a lesser effect on intracellular ice inhibition than DMSO33,71, but plunging from −40 °C allows sufficient slow cooling with reduced intracellular ice formation as determined in our previous studies64,65, where further cooling would not improve post-thaw viability. Each of the 20 conditions was repeated for three biological replicates (n = 3) using different batches of each cell type.

Combining freeze-substitution with cryopreservation

While transferring the samples from −196 °C liquid nitrogen storage temperature to freeze-substitution temperature at −80 °C can result in ice recrystallization, −80 °C is a sufficiently low temperature that secondary ice formation or ice crystal growth is slow enough to not be visually impactful before the dehydration of solid water from the sample and the subsequent fixation of the cell structures50,52–54,72. Disruptions to the cell morphology caused by the formation of visible intracellular ice during the cryopreservation process are thereby captured before significant visible changes can occur to the ice crystal morphology during substitution, and these patterns are further preserved for visualization once the monolayers are warmed back up to room temperature. Since freeze-substitution needs to be performed while the cells are frozen, it can naturally follow the plunge step of a cryopreservation protocol, effectively creating a “snapshot” of the samples when they are stored in liquid nitrogen. We utilized a freeze-substitution solution composed of the organic solvent ethanol for water replacement and precipitation fixation48,50,51, the cell fixative formaldehyde for additional protein cross-linking fixation48,50, and acetic acid to speed up the reaction48.

We adapted our published slow-cooling monolayer cryopreservation protocol63–67 and vitrification protocol69 alongside the freeze-substitution process to create the basic framework for our intracellular ice visualization method. The slow-cooling protocol involves a 1 °C/min cooling of confluent monolayers to sub-zero temperatures followed by a rapid plunge into liquid nitrogen. The vitrification protocol involves treatment of monolayers with special CPA cocktails (3 M ethylene glycol followed by 4 M ethylene glycol + 4 M DMSO) and a rapid plunge into liquid nitrogen from high sub-zero temperatures. A workflow of the ice visualization method is shown in Fig. 1a, with two key modifications to the published slow-cooling and vitrification protocols. First, we removed all supernatants prior to cooling such that the monolayers had minimal liquid surrounding them. We found that the solvent–water replacement process could not occur efficiently when there was excess water surrounding the samples (Supplementary Fig. 1b and c). Second, when required, we nucleated ice by shaving ice crystal “seeds” onto the samples rather than through mechanical conduction, i.e., touching the sample holders with liquid-nitrogen-cooled metal forceps. The seeding method prevents deadly spontaneous ice nucleation73 and reduces excess thermal shock to the monolayers. We examined the three known conditions to optimize the freeze-substitution process. The substitution solution was added to the samples while in liquid nitrogen to prevent ambient warming. The substitution solution freezes at the −196 °C liquid nitrogen temperature and melts at the −80 °C replacement temperature. Vitrified sample images did not show cell structure damage caused by the freezing of the substitution solution when compared to unfrozen PC images (Supplementary Fig. 2a). This is expected because, unlike water, organic solvents do not expand when frozen. We found storage at −80 °C for three days with substitution solution replacement after one day to provide the most consistent capture of intracellular ice in the NC conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2b and c).

Sample visualization with light microscopy

Hema 3TM staining is a simple and cost-effective technique that provides a robust visualization of the cell nuclei and cytoplasm by light microscopy74. SYTO 13 fluorescent staining was tested and proved untenable (Supplementary Fig. 3). The Hema 3TM staining procedure was adapted from a published protocol74 with minor modifications. We added an extra wash step using the Hema 3TM fixative solution for all HUVEC conditions and the PCEC HES −40 °C conditions to remove artifacts, including those caused by the large HES molecules. This extra wash step was not done for PCEC conditions without HES because it caused inconsistent stain intensity (Supplementary Fig. 4a and b). We captured nine images per sample at 1000× magnification to maximize the clarity of intracellular features (Supplementary Fig. 4c). All images from the 20 conditions across three replicates were used to develop and optimize the ice detection algorithm. The algorithm overview is outlined in Fig. 1b and discussed in more detail in the latter sections.

Figure 2b shows representative stained images of the five conditions for PCEC monolayers on Rinzl. The PC and vitrified conditions displayed no visible intracellular ice crystal patterns in the nuclei, and the NC condition displayed clearly defined visible ice crystal patterns in the nuclei, as expected. The vitrified conditions showed sporadic large “fractures” across the monolayer sample, which was expected due to the extreme thermal gradients and the brittleness of the vitrified “glassy” state35. The DMSO −10 °C condition displayed nuclei containing no visible ice patterns, as well as a mixture of nuclei containing less defined visible small ice crystal patterns, including “hole”-like, “branch”-like, or “grain”-like patterns (Supplementary Fig. 5a–c). The HES −40 °C condition displayed sporadic nuclei containing distinct visible large singular ice crystal patterns. In order to provide a simple categorization of the observed visible intracellular ice patterns for ease of quantification, we define nuclei displaying “no ice” crystal patterns as those containing an even dark pink stain intensity; “small ice” crystal patterns as those containing a large number of darker edges sectioning lightly stained regions; and “big ice” crystal patterns as those containing a singular darker edge encircling a large area of lightly stained region.

Nuclei segmentation with Cellpose3

We focused on the nuclei for visible intracellular ice detection because the nuclei clearly displayed the ice crystal patterns. As shown in Fig. 2a, NC image, while ice crystal patterns can be identified by eye in the cytoplasm, these patterns can be easily confused with random staining noise from the cytoplasmic contents, such as those seen in the PC image, making them difficult for an automated algorithm to discriminate. Conversely, the nucleus consistently displayed more uniform staining, making the distinction between cells with or without visible ice patterns more obvious. In addition, the large quantity and close proximity of cells in a confluent monolayer mean that we cannot easily identify which cell’s cytoplasm displayed visible ice crystals. Other ice visualization studies in cell monolayers and tissue slices have also focused on the nucleus as the primary site of ice detection43,48,75. An overall manual examination of all images obtained in this study showed no conditions that exhibited obvious visible ice crystal patterns in the cytoplasm, but not in the nucleus. Therefore, we chose to detect visible ice crystals within the nucleus as a proxy for intracellular ice in the cell monolayer.

The Cellpose3 AI-guided generalist cell and nuclei segmentation program76,77 was used for identifying the nuclei in the captured images. We used the publicly available noisy image set from the Cellpose website as the training dataset for the Cellpose3 program. We utilized the base nuclei segmentation function with parameters determined from manually applying the program to selected PC and NC images. The output nuclei regions of interest (ROI) were further processed to remove any mis-captures by the Cellpose3 program. All ROIs with area smaller than the typical nuclei were deemed as false captures, due to staining or imaging artifacts, and removed (Fig. 2d). ROIs that had extended contacting borders were merged, because Cellpose3 tended to mis-identify single nuclei containing two to three large ice crystals as two to three closely adjacent nuclei (Fig. 2e). As shown in Fig. 2c, the Cellpose3 program was able to identify the cell nuclei in all 20 conditions with high accuracy post-processing, including in the complicated, noisy conditions with large amounts of ice edges.

Canny edge detection and Ring algorithms

Conditions that exhibited the formation of visible small ice crystals displayed a high number of dark-stained patterns within the nuclei due to the ice crystals displacing cell materials, making edge detection a good basis for identifying visible intracellular ice. The Canny edge detection (CED) operator is a widely applied edge detection technique with robust output78. We adapted the publicly available CED plugin for ImageJ as the basis for our CED algorithm. As shown in Fig. 3a–h, grayscale images were first blurred using a Gaussian blur function to remove as many small, low-contrast edges from staining artifacts as possible, while preserving the larger and higher contrast ice-displaced edges. Then, the CED plugin with optimized parameters was applied, producing a binary image with a black pixel background and white pixel edges. The nuclei ROIs were then overlaid on top, and the percentage of white pixels within each ROI was calculated. Because the formation of intracellular ice results in a high number of edge patterns, nuclei without ice will contain a low percentage of white pixels from the stained DNA/RNA materials and artifacts (Fig. 3a–d), while nuclei exhibiting ice will contain a high percentage of white pixels (Fig. 3e–h). The percentage of white pixels within the ROI that corresponded to the nuclei containing “small ice” was determined using the difference-tuning method described below. To estimate the minimal detectable size of small visible ice crystals by this process, we randomly selected five NC sample images with both clearly observable “small ice” patterns in the non-processed images and clearly defined white-pixel edges outlining those corresponding ice patterns in the CED algorithm-processed images. For each of the five images, we measured the diameter of the five smallest defined white-pixel ice pattern outlines in the CED processed images for a total of 25 measured “small ice” crystal sizes, averaged to be 1.10 ± 0.27 µm.Fig. 3Application of the Canny edge detection algorithm for “small ice” detection.a–d The Canny edge detection (CED) algorithm applied to positive control conditions with no visible ice crystals in the nuclei. While visible small ice crystals can be identified by the high number of edge patterns, nuclei exhibiting singular visible large ice crystals cannot be detected this way. These large ice crystals completely displaced all cell materials to the nuclear border, creating a singular dark-stained (low pixel intensity) ring-shaped edge with a uniformly light-stained (high pixel intensity) center. The Ring algorithm was developed to take advantage of this specific pattern to detect visible large ice crystals by thresholding each nucleus to retain only the low intensity pixels, then detecting these pixels specifically in the nuclear border. The threshold value was chosen such that all pixels with intensity below the threshold were set to black, while all pixels with intensity above the threshold were set to white. As shown in Fig. 4, we determined a specific threshold value for each nuclear ROI by first computing two intermediate thresholds based on the values from the ROI’s pixel intensity histogram, which measured the number of pixels (y-axis) with each intensity value (x-axis), ordered from left to right, i.e., the lowest (dark) to the highest (light) intensity value. The two intermediate threshold values were: the intensity range threshold, calculated by first identifying the lowest and highest pixel intensities within the ROI and then selecting a specific value within this range (for example 40% of the way from lowest to highest); and the pixel count threshold, the intensity value for which a specific percentage of the total number of pixels in the ROI possessed lower intensities (for example 30%). The minimum of the two intermediate thresholds was selected as the final threshold for that ROI, corresponding to the lower intensity value on the histogram. After thresholding, a ring-shaped ROI that only enveloped the nuclear border was created based on the original ROI and applied to the binary image. The percentage of black pixels within this ring-shaped ROI was measured, where a high percentage indicates that the nucleus contains the specific large ice crystal pattern. In nuclei with no ice patterns, the histograms displayed a singular, central peak because of the similar pixel intensities from the uniformly stained cell material, with right or left skew depending on the overall stain strength. The pixels measured by this peak must not be set to black because they are not associated with ice patterns. Therefore, the final threshold must be set to the left of this peak to set the majority of the nucleus as white, leaving any black pixels concentrated in the nucleus’s center where the ring-shaped ROI cannot capture (Fig. 4a and b). For light-stained nuclei with no ice patterns, the range threshold is lower and sets the majority of the right-skewed peak as white (Fig. 4a). For dark-stained nuclei with no ice patterns, the count threshold is lower and sets the majority of the left-skewed peak as white (Fig. 4b). Using the other threshold in each case only partially sets the central peak, leading to large areas of black pixels that bleed into the ring-shaped ROI and cause false detections; in nuclei with small ice crystals and many edge patterns, the histograms displayed no distinct peaks. Since the edges in the center of the nucleus were generally more heavily stained than those at the border, selecting the lower of the two intermediate thresholds sets the majority of the lighter edges in the border as white and therefore not captured by the ring-shaped ROI (Fig. 4c); in nuclei with a singular large ice crystal, the histogram displayed at least two distinct peaks: the rightmost peak for the light-stained center and the leftmost peak for the dark-stained border. Both intermediate thresholds were computed to be between the two peaks; the lower of the two would set the majority of the border pixels as black for the ring-shaped ROI to capture (Fig. 4d). We automatically processed all the data on labeled nucleus ROIs outputted by the algorithms. Nuclei that were not labeled by either algorithm were classified as “no ice”. Nuclei that were labeled by only the CED algorithm or by both algorithms were classified as “small ice”, since the Ring algorithm may over-detect nuclei containing visible small ice crystal patterns, depending on the image’s quality or stain intensity. Nuclei that were only labeled by the Ring algorithm were classified as “big ice”. For each condition and each replicate, nine images were captured per sample, and the number of nuclei displaying “no ice”, “small ice”, or “big ice” was summed across the nine images. Percentages were calculated out of the total number of nuclei, then averaged over the three replicates.

Difference-tuning method

We developed the difference-tuning method to quickly optimize the algorithms’ parameters by comparing them to manually counting the images by eye. One replicate from each of all 20 conditions was chosen as the training case, and a non-blinded eye count was determined by manually labeling each nucleus as containing “no ice”, “small ice”, or “big ice”. The eye count’s percentage results were subtracted from the algorithms’ results, producing a difference value that quantified how closely the algorithm detected ice compared to the human eye. Each of the algorithms’ parameters was optimized through iterations while keeping the other parameters constant, aiming to reduce this difference value as much as possible, with 10% set as the maximum acceptable difference. Figure 5a shows the tuning method applied for the CED algorithm on the application of the Gaussian blur, which suggests that the Gaussian blur was highly necessary for accurate ice edge detection in the PC conditions. Figure 5b shows the tuning method applied for the Ring algorithm on the threshold choices, which suggested that selecting the minimum from the two thresholds performed better than either threshold by itself (higher weight placed on optimizing the known ice formation conditions: PC, vitrified, and NC). Additional examples of the tuning method applied for testing various parameters on all conditions are shown in Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7. The final parameters for the CED algorithm were: CED plugin with kernel radius 1, low threshold 1, and high threshold 2; ROI containing >4% white pixels labeled with “small ice” for HUVEC monolayers and >5% for PCEC monolayers. The final parameters for the Ring algorithm were: intensity range threshold set at 40% between the ROI’s minimum and maximum intensities; pixel count threshold set at 30% of total pixels in the ROI; ring-shaped ROI containing >15% black pixels labeled with “big ice” for HUVEC monolayers and >20% for PCEC monolayers. Figure 6 shows that the PC and vitrified conditions both exhibited close to 100% of nuclei containing no visible ice crystals, as expected. Both the algorithm and the blinded eye counts showed small standard deviations, indicating consistency in detecting no ice formation. The NC conditions exhibited close to 100% of nuclei containing visible small ice crystals, as expected. The standard deviations were small, indicating the algorithm’s consistency in detecting small visible ice crystal formation in the cell nuclei. For the HUVEC monolayer DMSO −10 °C conditions, both the algorithm and the blinded eye counts showed similar results, detecting a moderate number of nuclei containing visible small ice crystals (Fig. 6a and b). Close examination of these images revealed unpredictable intracellular ice formation, where some replicates exhibited complete visible ice formation while others did not, resulting in high standard deviations. This observation was not surprising for the DMSO −10 °C conditions, likely due to the competing actions of DMSO protecting against intracellular ice formation, while the insufficient dehydration from rapid plunge at −10 °C promotes ice formation. Due to the stochastic nature of ice formation, conditions eliciting some ice formation are expected to have high variability. We note that the PCEC monolayer DMSO −10 °C conditions had a higher number of nuclei with small ice crystals as compared with the HUVEC monolayer at the same conditions, indicating some cell-type-specific freezing response likely due to membrane-permeability differences79. The comparable standard deviations and individual replicate results between our algorithm and the blinded eye counts indicated good consistency in detecting variable visible small ice crystal patterns in the nuclei. For the HES −40 °C conditions, the algorithm showed similar results to the blinded eye counts, detecting a high percentage of nuclei containing no visible ice crystals, and any ice that formed was large ice crystals. This is especially evident in the HUVEC monolayer on glass HES −40 °C condition, which had a high percentage of nuclei containing visible large ice crystals in two replicates and a low percentage in one replicate, resulting in high standard deviation. Overall, our algorithm accurately captured this difference between replicates and produced similar standard deviations to the blinded eye counts, indicating consistency in detecting visible large ice crystals.

Discussion

We developed a freeze-substitution-based light microscopy method that allowed direct visualization of intracellular ice in the nuclei of cells in monolayers after they were subjected to a complete slow-cooling protocol. The associated automated ice detection algorithm that we developed showed high consistency in the identification of visible intracellular ice with crystal size greater than 1 µm in the nuclei of cells in the captured light microscopy images, producing comparable results to the ice detection standard of manually counting by eye for both the known conditions with predictable ice formation patterns and for the unknown conditions with unpredictable but distinctly observed ice formation patterns. Combining our ice visualization and ice detection methods provides a significant advantage in the identification of visible intracellular ice features over the traditional manual ice detection method. The freeze-substitution process is easily adaptable to various cryopreservation protocols, allowing the evaluation of a large number of samples for various cell types and tissue constructs. The ice detection algorithm automates the evaluation process, enabling accurate quantification of visible intracellular ice in a large number of images. Our human counters reported spending ~2 complete workdays each to manually count the 540 images across the 20 conditions and three replicates. If scaled up to include more conditions, the time and labor requirements are immense. On the other hand, the ice detection algorithm processed all images within 2 h, much more efficiently than manual detection. Considering the estimated 3 workdays required to cryopreserve, stain, and image the 540 samples, analyzing the images with the manual counting method would take 5 total workdays, while analyzing with the algorithm would take slightly more than 3 workdays, a 40% improvement in total processing time. This automated process also eliminates possible human bias in the identification of visible intracellular ice in the cell nuclei for more reproducible ice detection results, as well as prevents the considerable physical and mental fatigue associated with manual counting of large sample sizes. The basic edge detection operations and image processing principles utilized in designing our algorithm were selected for being readily available for use with the open-source ImageJ software. Exploration of other image processing techniques, such as curvelet transforms80, and implementing them for use with the ImageJ framework, may be a direction for future research to build on our algorithm.

One limitation of our method is the removal of staining artifacts. These artifacts manifest as small gradients in the staining intensity, creating localized regions of excessive dark or light staining. Notably, these artifacts result in false edges that can interfere with both the nuclei segmentation and the edge detection, increasing the data’s standard deviation and potentially obscuring actual visible ice patterns in the nucleus, such as the very small “grain”-like ice patterns. Possible sources for these staining artifacts include the stain quality itself or physical debris. To mitigate these artifacts, we chose the shelf-stable and ready-to-use Hema 3TM staining kit because it reduces possible discrepancies from stain preparation74. We also applied a substitution solution replacement during sample fixing and multiple wash steps before staining, particularly for conditions containing HES, to remove as much debris as possible. Despite this, we still observed sporadic artifacts in random samples. Therefore, during image capture, areas with significant artifacts were avoided, and samples that randomly exhibited poor overall staining quality were rejected and repeated. During image pre-processing, camera noise removal with a median filter and a Gaussian blur was applied to reduce these small intensity gradients while preserving as many large intensity gradients from actual ice crystals as possible. The staining quality may also be linked to cell type. In the PC untreated samples, the PCEC monolayers exhibited a greater number of artifacts from small, low-contrast edges compared to the HUVEC monolayers, despite following the same staining process. After image processing, some HUVEC monolayers on Rinzl samples still exhibited small staining artifacts, resulting in statistically significant differences from the blinded counting results. This cell-type discrepancy in staining was also observed in the image-specific differences as seen in Supplementary Fig. 8, with HUVEC monolayers showing greater average differences with higher standard deviation between the algorithm and the blinded manual count. The exact reason for this cell-type discrepancy is unclear. Future application of our method to other cell types may need small cell-type-specific adjustments to the ice detection algorithm’s thresholds for optimal results. Future work on further optimizing artifact removal may focus on exploring different staining methods to produce a more uniformly colored image or applying more complex denoising algorithms, such as dynamic or adaptive filters81,82, during image pre-processing.

Another consideration with our freeze-substitution method is the influence of the cryoprotectant on the freeze-substitution process. The presence of large molecules such as HES may act as a physical barrier that impedes the penetration of the organic solvent into the samples for water replacement. However, most previous freeze-substitution studies for cell ultrastructure imaging have not identified cryoprotectants as a significant factor in the inhibition of the substitution process, only highlighting their capability in modifying cell structures53,54,83 which is not a concern in our application. To mitigate this possible issue, we designed our process following traditional freeze-substitution design guidelines50–54 to ensure confidence in its success. This included steps such as: the removal of the CPA solution before addition of the freeze-substitution solution to lower the amount of interfering chemicals; utilizing pure ethanol and acetic acid reagents in the substitution solution to achieve the highest rate of water replacement; replacement of the substitution solution to further remove leftover CPA solutions; and processing for three days which is within the range of the traditionally established duration for freeze-substitution of difficult CPA-treated samples. However, the possibility of the CPA interference on the freeze-substitution process should be kept in mind when applying our methods to different cryopreservation protocols. Single-image level examination of outlier cases where the algorithm disagreed with the manual eye count revealed two main sources of discrepancies. First is the inconsistency in fully detecting the “grain”-like visible small ice crystal patterns in images with overall low illumination levels. These “grain”-like ice patterns are the smallest observable ice patterns that we have found in our sample images, making them fundamentally more difficult to identify compared to other larger patterns due to limits in resolution. As seen in Supplementary Fig. 9a, with low overall illumination in the image, the contrast within the image also decreases84, resulting in the already small “grain”-like ice crystal edges being barely detectable by eye and not detectable by the algorithm. In images with brighter illumination, as seen in Supplementary Fig. 9b, the “grain”-like ice crystals were more readily detected by the algorithm. To minimize the discrepancies due to image illumination in the future, a reference background image with the optimal illumination level should be determined and utilized to dynamically adjust each image before capture to ensure consistent brightness across sample and staining variations. Post-processing low-illumination image enhancement methods may also be explored for this purpose84. Second, the formation of partial or non-uniform large visible ice crystals results in over-detection of “small ice” in the current algorithm’s definition. As seen in Supplementary Fig. 9c, when large singular visible ice crystals form but only partially fill a cell nucleus, or when they still leave some non-uniform darkly stained regions within the nucleus, it results in more edges within the nuclear regions of interest that can be detected by the CED algorithm, in addition to being detected by the Ring algorithm. This double detection is currently defined as “small ice” to prevent over-detection of large ice patterns in the NC conditions with small visible ice crystal patterns and heavily stained nuclei boundaries, but it has led to these partial and non-uniform detection discrepancies observed in a few other images. To address this, additional processing of cell nuclei labeled with both visible small and large ice crystals can be implemented in future versions of the algorithm, such as establishing stricter threshold levels on the doubly detected nuclei to better optimize the separation of “small” and “large” ice patterns.

Our current methods are optimized to detect and quantify visible intracellular ice of crystal size >1 µm in the nuclei of HUVEC and PCEC monolayers stained and imaged by brightfield microscopy. Possible directions for future improvements of our methods include validating and adapting for additional cell and tissue types, adapting for different imaging modalities, and potentially incorporating machine-learning methods in the adaptation steps. For other researchers to expand the current intracellular ice visualization and quantification methods for other cell types in a monolayer, we have detailed in the Methods section optimization steps that can be followed for freeze-substitution, staining, and determining algorithm parameters with the difference-tuning process. For larger and more complex three-dimensional tissue structures, potential challenges exist, such as requiring longer freeze-substitution time with increased sample size and the need for precise cryo-sectioning and embedding in order to procure thinly sliced samples for imaging50,51. The possibility of utilizing machine learning or deep learning networks for ice detection is an interesting future direction, with a possibility of improvements in efficiency in ice pattern recognition, artifact removal, and universal applicability to different cell and image types. However, considerable investment in resources is required to establish a sufficiently large image data bank and for training the model85–87, making it very difficult to apply currently.

Our ice visualization and ice detection methods provide a quantifiable and time-efficient way to capture, through light microscopy, the visible intracellular ice formation in the cell nuclei of monolayer samples undergoing a complete cryopreservation protocol. This is achieved by utilizing readily available reagents and equipment for easy adaptation and allowing high-throughput screening of protocol parameters such as various CPA compositions and cooling rates. The rapid collection of large sets of data on intracellular ice formation in the cell nuclei, visible under light microscopy, in conjunction with other analyses, such as cell survival and functionality assays, can be used in predicting cryopreservation outcomes or in thermodynamic modeling to evaluate cryobiological responses. Thus, although this study focused on the detection of intracellular ice in frozen-cell images, the freeze-substitution method for visualizing ice under light microscopy and the associated algorithm for quantifying ice can enable future investigations on the mechanisms of lethal cryoinjury due to intracellular ice formation. We also quantified the accuracy and showcased the difference-tuning method for refining the parameters of the ice detection algorithm, which can be used to optimize the algorithm for other cell types or tissue constructs. Our method presents a novel tool that will enable a better understanding of the nature of intracellular ice formation in tissue constructs during slow-cooling cryopreservation and can be used to efficiently design optimal cryopreservation protocols for these constructs.

Methods

Preparation and characterization of the vitrification solutions

Two vitrification solutions were prepared in the corresponding cell medium and stored at 4 °C until needed. Vitrification Solution 1 (VS1) was prepared from 3 M ethylene glycol (107-21-1, Fisher Scientific, Fair Lawn, NJ, USA) in cell medium, and Vitrification Solution 2 (VS2) was prepared from 4 M ethylene glycol and 4 M dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (ACS reagent, ≥99.9%, 472301, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in cell medium. These solutions were adapted from a previous study on cartilage vitrification cryopreservation69.

We measured the glass transition temperature, Tg, of VS2 using a polymer differential scanning calorimeter (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland), and the temperature trace output was recorded with the STARe Thermal Analysis Software (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). Briefly, the VS2 samples were rapidly cooled to −150 °C and then heated to 0 °C at three different heating rates of 5, 10, and 20 °C/min. The inflection point on the temperature trace was selected to represent Tg at a given heating rate, and the final Tg(0) at a heating rate of 0 °C/min was extrapolated from the three Tg measurements at heating rates of 5, 10, and 20 °C/min to eliminate thermal lag88. A total of three VS2 samples were analyzed (n = 3), and the final Tg(0) result was calculated as the mean ± standard deviation of the three independent experiments to be −121.87 ± 8.44 °C.

Preparation and description of cryoprotectant, fixative, and stain solutions

Cryoprotective agent (CPA) solutions were prepared in the corresponding cell medium. The DMSO-only solution comprised of 5% wt/wt DMSO (67-68-5, Fisher Scientific). The HES-only solution comprised of 6% wt/wt hydroxyethyl starch (HES) (from a 20% pentastarch solution, PST002, Preservation Solutions Inc., Elkhorn, WI, USA). These CPA solutions were adapted from previous studies on slow-cooling cryopreservation of HUVEC and PCEC monolayers63,65. They were stored at 4 °C and used within 14 days.

The freeze-substitution stock solution was prepared from 85% vol/vol ethanol (100% anhydrous ethyl alcohol, P016EAAN, Commercial Alcohols Inc., Brayton, ON, CA), 10% vol/vol formaldehyde (36.5–38% in H2O, F8775, Sigma Aldrich), and 5% vol/vol glacial acetic acid (ACS reagent ≥ 99.7%, Sigma Aldrich). The stock solution was stored at room temperature and pre-cooled in a −80 °C freezer the day before the experiment. The freeze-substitution solution was adapted from a previous study on freeze-substitution of liver tissues59. Anhydrous ethanol was used as the organic solvent because the presence of water in the fixative solution can impact the rate of water replacement51,53. Formaldehyde is a cell fixative, and acetic acid increases the rate of replacement and fixation.

The Hema 3TM staining kit (Hema 3TM Stat Pack, 112-911, Fisher Healthcare, Pittsburgh, PA, USA) is composed of three different solutions, namely a methanol (MeOH)-based fixative solution, a water-based acid red staining solution 1, and a water-based methylene blue staining solution 2. The solutions were stored at room temperature until use.

Culture of cell monolayers

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (C2519A, Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) were purchased as cryopreserved, pooled primary cells in suspension. They were received in dry ice and stored in liquid nitrogen until needed89. Once thawed, HUVECs were cultured in T-75 tissue culture flasks with 15 mL of endothelial growth medium (EGM-2, CC-3162, Lonza) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 following previous methods63,65. Medium was changed on the following day post-thaw to remove residual CPAs, after which medium was changed every other day. At 80% confluency, the HUVECs were harvested using trypsinization following the supplier’s instructions89 and sub-cultured at passage 3 on Rinzl plastic or glass for the freeze-substitution experiments.

Porcine corneal endothelial cells (PCECs) were isolated and pooled from fresh pig eyes following our previous method90. They were cultured, cryopreserved, and stored in liquid nitrogen as passage 2 cells in suspension until needed for the experiments. Once thawed, PCECs were cultured in T-75 tissue culture flasks in 15 mL of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM high glucose, D5671, Millipore-Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% v/v fetal bovine serum (FBS, 12483-020, GIBCO, Life Technologies, Burlington, ON, Canada), 4 mM L-glutamine (25030-081, GIBCO), and 1% v/v antibiotic–antimycotic (15240-062, GIBCO, Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY, USA), at 37 °C and 5% CO2, as described previously63. The medium was changed the following day, then every other day. Upon reaching 80% confluence, the PCECs were detached and harvested using TrypLE Express (12605010, GIBCO, Canada)90,91 and sub-cultured as passage 3 cells on Rinzl plastic or glass for the freeze-substitution experiments.

The remaining passage 3 HUVECs and PCECs were sub-cultured following the described culture process for use in other replicates of the experiments at passage 4. Only passage 3 and passage 4 of HUVECs and PCECs were used for the freeze-substitution experiments. Confluency and morphology of the cell monolayers were assessed using a phase-contrast light microscope (Leitz, Labovert, Los Angeles, CA, USA) at 100× magnification. The use of primary porcine cells had been exempted from review by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Alberta because the eyes were obtained from pigs slaughtered for meat consumption, and no animal was killed solely for the purpose of this study.

Culture of monolayers on Rinzl plastic and glass coverslips

The Rinzl plastic (clear vinyl plastic, 18 × 18 mm, 72261-19, Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA) was cut into 8 × 8 mm square coverslips. The glass coverslip (89015-725, VWR, Darmstadt, Germany) was a 12-mm diameter round borosilicate glass. Coverslips were prepared for cell adhesion following previous methods63,65. Briefly, coverslips were first sterilized by immersing them in 70% isopropanol for at least 30 min, then washed with sterile Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (1× DPBS, 10010023, GIBCO, Canada) for at least 15 min. The sterilized coverslips were transferred into a 24-well plate and coated with a dilute fibronectin solution (from bovine plasma, F1141, Millipore Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) at a concentration of 18 µg/mL for at least 30 min. Cells were then seeded on the coated coverslips at a density of 5000 cells/cm2 and incubated in cell culture conditions at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Medium was replaced every other day, and the monolayers were visually assessed daily for morphology and confluency using phase-contrast light microscopy.

PCEC growth kinetics on Rinzl plastic and glass

Previous cell cultures of PCECs on Rinzl plastic and glass coverslips suggest a difference in growth rate depending on the adhesion surface. Thus, a growth kinetics experiment was conducted to measure the population doubling level and determine the optimal culture time for PCECs grown on Rinzl plastic or glass coverslips to reach visual confluence. Fluorescence imaging and membrane integrity assessment were used to measure the number of live and adherent cells on the coverslips across 10 days, adapted from a published protocol65. Briefly, 11 samples of PCECs were seeded on either Rinzl plastic or glass coverslips as outlined above, for a total of 22 samples. Day 0 samples were evaluated four hours after the initial seeding, allowing the cells time to attach. One sample from each surface was assessed on each of the following days up until day 10. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 10a and b, PCEC monolayers cultured on both Rinzl plastic and glass exhibited a short lag phase after initial seeding, as shown by the minimal increase in cell concentration between days 0 and 1. Modified vitrification and slow-cooling processes

Monolayers that have reached visual confluence were used for the experiments. HUVEC monolayers grown on Rinzl plastic and glass, and PCEC monolayers grown on Rinzl plastic coverslips, reached visual confluence after 4 days of culture. PCEC monolayers grown on glass coverslips reached visual confluence after 7 days of culture. Monolayer samples were transferred into glass vials (60965D-1, Kimble Chase, Rochester, NY, USA) for the freeze-substitution-adapted cooling processes. Samples for the positive control (PC) condition were kept in the well plate at cell culture conditions until needed for staining and imaging.

For the vitrified conditions, samples were cooled following a vitrification process modified from a previous study for cartilage69. Briefly, monolayer samples were first incubated in 200 µL of VS1 for 20 min in an ice bath at 0 °C; then, the VS1 was discarded, and the samples were incubated in 200 µL of VS2 for 15 minutes in a stirred methanol bath at −5 °C. The VS2 solution was then discarded, such that there was barely any liquid remaining within the vial; then the samples were plunged directly into liquid nitrogen and stored there for at least 1 h.

For the negative control (NC), DMSO −10 °C, and HES −40 °C conditions, samples were cooled following a slow-cooling process modified from previous studies44,50. Briefly, samples were incubated at 0 °C for 15 min in 200 µL of the corresponding cell medium for the NC conditions, or the 5% DMSO or 6% HES CPA solutions for the DMSO −10 °C and HES −40 °C conditions, respectively. The supernatant was then discarded, such that there was barely any liquid remaining in the vial. The samples were then moved into a stirring methanol bath set at −5 °C to equilibrate for 2 min. Ice nucleation was done for the DMSO −10 °C and HES −40 °C conditions by “seeding” small ice crystals in the sample vials to serve as ice nuclei through shaving from frozen condensations. Ice was not nucleated in the NC conditions. After ice nucleation, the samples were held at −5 °C for 3 min to release the latent heat of fusion, then cooled at −1 °C per minute. At −10 °C, the NC and DMSO −10 °C conditions were quickly removed and plunged directly into liquid nitrogen. We did not plunge the NC conditions at 0 °C because the resulting fixed ice crystals were too small to be viewed consistently at that plunge temperature, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1a. At −40 °C, the samples for the HES −40 °C conditions were removed and plunged directly into liquid nitrogen. The samples were stored in liquid nitrogen for at least 1 h. A total of three replicates (n = 3) with different cell batches were conducted for each condition across the four combinations of PCEC or HUVEC monolayers on Rinzl plastic or glass.

Optimization of the volume of solution covering monolayers

The presence of excess water covering the monolayer in the vial can severely impact the substitution process during freeze-substitution because the organic solvent would need to first replace all the water within the solution before it can penetrate and reach the water within the cells50. Therefore, the previous vitrification and slow-cooling protocols, where the samples were immersed in 200 μL of CPA solution during cooling, needed to be modified. The NC condition with known complete ice formation was used to optimize the remaining volume within the vial, which was needed for sufficient fixation of the ice-disrupted cell material, such that it can be easily observed using light microscopy. The percentage of images with visible ice patterns was determined quickly by eye using a 0−3 labeling method. Each image was assigned a number between 0 and 3, with 0 corresponding to all cells containing no visible ice patterns, 1 for a small number of cells containing visible ice patterns, 2 for a medium number of cells containing visible ice patterns, and 3 for a large number of cells containing visible ice patterns. These numerical labels were then averaged across all images and divided by 3 to calculate a proxy percentage value quantifying the amount of intracellular ice in each image. Remaining solution volumes of 0, 100, and 200 µL were tested with 4 days of substitution, and additional trials with 200 µL of remaining solution volume were tested with 5 and 7 days of substitution to see if more time would allow the solvent to replace the higher water volume. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1b, 200 µL of the remaining solution volume within the vials heavily interfered with the substitution process, resulting in a low amount of visible ice patterns regardless of the duration for which the samples were substituted. With 100 µL of remaining solution volume, images displaying visible ice patterns were produced but were less consistent when compared to 0 µL of remaining solution volume, which produced around 80% of images with obvious visible ice patterns (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Therefore, an additional step was added to the previous vitrification and slow-cooling protocols, i.e., to discard all solutions from the vial prior to cooling and ice nucleation.

Optimization of ice nucleation

Post-thaw membrane integrity of monolayers cryopreserved following the optimal procedure, as determined in a previous study64,65 (with known high post-thaw viability), but with no solution in the vial, was assessed to determine if the remaining solution volume had an impact on monolayer survival. It was discovered that nucleating ice by the mechanical conduction method in the original protocol, through touching the external walls of the vial with liquid nitrogen-cooled metal forceps, was not appropriate when there was no solution in the vial, because it caused significant membrane damage. Various ice nucleation methods were evaluated for an alternative that would work in the absence of solution in the vial and cause no impact to post-thaw viability. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1d, the conditions evaluated were: the positive control (PC) with no cooling treatment, representing the high viability control; ice nucleation by liquid nitrogen-cooled forceps with 0, 100, or 200 µL of remaining solution, where the 200 µL remaining solution condition is the previous method in the original protocol; ice nucleation by small and large ice crystal seeding with 0 µL of remaining solution, where ice crystal condensations were shaved off and dropped into the vials; no ice nucleation with 0 µL of remaining solution; and ice nucleation by −20 °C cooled forceps with 0 µL of remaining solution. The ice-seeding method with 0 µL of remaining solution yielded comparable post-thaw membrane integrity to the positive control, as well as comparable membrane integrity to the original protocol’s mechanical conduction method with 200 µL of remaining solution, no matter the size of the ice crystals dropped into the vial, whereas the other methods all yielded lower viability. These results also indicated that having no solution within the vial did not impact the membrane integrity of the monolayer post-thaw. Therefore, the ice-seeding nucleation method was chosen for the modified cryopreservation protocol.

Freeze-substitution process

While the samples were stored in liquid nitrogen, the pre-cooled freeze-substitution stock solution was removed from the −80 °C freezer and transferred into a dry ice bath to keep it at a constant temperature of −80 °C. One mL of the substitution solution was added to each sample vial while the vials were still in liquid nitrogen. Once the samples were transferred to the −80 °C freezer and stored there overnight, the frozen substitution solution would melt, and the water replacement and cell fixation process would begin. The stock solution was kept in the −80 °C freezer. After one day of substitution, the samples were moved into a dry ice bath, and the substitution solution in the vials was discarded and replaced with one mL of fresh substitution solution. After solution replacement, the samples were returned to the −80 °C freezer and stored for an additional two days, for a total of three days of substitution. The samples were then warmed up to room temperature for at least one hour before they were stained for imaging.

Optimization of fixation time

One major element of the freeze-substitution process was the duration of the substitution period at −80 °C. At low temperatures, the water–solvent replacement process is relatively slow, ranging from 12 h to more than 1 week, depending on the type and thickness of the sample50,52,93. To determine the optimal time of substitution, the NC conditions with known complete intracellular ice formation, for PCEC monolayers grown on Rinzl plastic, were fixed for 1−7 days with substitution solution replacement after the first day. The percentage of images with visible ice patterns was quickly determined by eye using the 0−3 labeling method above (Methods, optimization of volume of solution covering monolayers). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2b, substitution periods of 2−4 days showed the most consistent preservation of ice-disrupted structures within the cell nuclei, yielding close to 100% of images with visible ice patterns. At longer substitution periods of 5−7 days, some images displayed much less obvious ice edges, potentially due to the prolonged exposure to organic solvents that dissolved the cell material53. The effect of replacing the substitution solution was assessed by not replacing the solution in other samples for substitution periods of 1, 2, 3, 6, and 7 days. Overall, not replacing the substitution solution resulted in fewer images with completely fixed visible ice patterns, regardless of how long the samples were substituted (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Visual inspection of the 2-day-substitution images showed slightly less clear ice patterns in the cell nuclei compared to the 3-day-substitution images. Therefore, 3 days of immersing the monolayers in the substitution solution at −80 °C with solution replacement on day 1 was chosen to be the best freeze-substitution procedure.

Staining and imaging

Once the samples had warmed up to room temperature, the substitution solution was discarded from the vials. For all of the HUVEC sample conditions, and the PCEC sample HES conditions that contained large molecules in the CPA solution, the samples were first washed with 200 µL of the Hema 3TM fixative solution by gentle agitation. Then, following an adaptation of a published Hema 3TM staining process74, the fixative was discarded, and the samples were immersed in 200 µL of the Hema 3TM solution 1 for 10 min. Solution 1 was then discarded, and the samples were washed with 500 µL of distilled water by gentle agitation and immersing for 1 min for a total of two times. Then, the samples were immersed in 200 µL of the Hema 3TM solution 2 for 10 min, followed by washing with distilled water for 1 min for a total of two times. The stained monolayer was transferred cell-side down onto a glass microscopy slide (12-500-A3, Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Residual water on top of the surface was gently wicked away using a Kimwipe, not disturbing the water layer between the coverslip and glass slide to prevent meniscus artifacts. The samples waiting for imaging were briefly stored for no more than 30 min in 500 µL of distilled water to prevent the samples from drying and causing distortions to the images. For the PC conditions, the samples were first fixed with 500 mL of the freeze-substitution solution at room temperature for at least an hour, then subjected to the same staining process as before.

The monolayer samples were observed under a brightfield microscope (Dialux 22, Leitz, Oberkochen, Germany) at 1000× magnification, with an objective numerical aperture of 1.32. A total of 9 images were captured per sample coverslip using the Infinity3 camera (1392 × 1040, pixel size 6.45 µm2, Lumenera Corporation, Ottawa, ON, Canada) and the Infinity Capture software (Lumenera Corporation, Ottawa, ON, Canada), roughly following a 3 by 3 pattern. Regions with large gaps due to limited cells, heavily stained cell materials, water meniscus edges, or any such artifactual disruptions to the image clarity were avoided. The settings of the imaging software were: contrast 10, hue 0, saturation 100, brightness 0, gamma 1, and gain 1.65. Global white balance and exposure (15–20 ms) were adjusted occasionally to ensure that the no-cell background appeared white. Images were saved in a .tif format and organized in folders based on the condition, replicate number, then cell and surface type in ascending hierarchical order, for ease of access by the algorithm during processing.

Optimization of staining methods

We explored visualizing intracellular ice after freeze-substitution using fluorescence staining with SYTO 13 because this staining method was applied successfully in a previous study to visualize ice in monolayers on a cryomicroscope48. However, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, SYTO 13 resulted in inconsistent and overall weak stain intensity, with random samples yielding completely unviewable images due to intense photobleaching, making them difficult to analyze. Also, as exemplified in the NC 4-day fixation image, some images produced by the SYTO 13 staining yielded ambiguous ice formation, where cells appeared as an amorphous smear rather than with defined edges. Taking into consideration these issues and the more complex and equipment-intensive requirements of fluorescence microscopy, brightfield microscopy imaging using Hema 3TM was chosen instead.

The application of a wash step before staining with the Hema 3TM methanol-based fixative solution was deemed necessary for all of the HUVEC monolayer conditions, and the PCEC monolayer HES conditions that were exposed to CPA solutions with large molecular components. In the original Hema 3TM staining protocol74, the designated fixative solution serves to fix the cell material, a process that was already completed during the freeze-substitution process and thus could be omitted. However, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 4a and b, when the samples were not washed with the Hema 3TM fixative solution pre-staining, the HUVEC monolayer conditions and the PCEC monolayer HES conditions all exhibited heavily stained artifacts that significantly impacted the image clarity. These artifacts were likely due to the cell-type specificity of the staining solution and large HES molecules that had adhered to the cell material, which can be mostly removed by the extra wash step. However, for PCEC monolayer conditions with no HES (e.g., PC, Vitrified, NC, and DMSO conditions), the methanol-wash step resulted in a number of random images displaying very weak staining, which could not be properly analyzed. The exact reason behind this is unclear, but for consistency in image quality, the Hema 3TM fixative wash step was not performed on PCEC monolayer conditions containing no HES.

The magnification of the captured images was chosen by visually comparing the clarity of the cell nuclei among the available options. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4c, the 100× and 250× magnifications were rejected due to the cell nuclei appearing too small to be properly analyzed, especially in determining if there was small ice crystal formation in these nuclei. Between the 400× and 1000× magnification, the 1000× magnification was chosen because it produced the most detailed view of the cell nuclei, making it the best option to distinguish the presence of ice within the cell. While this magnification does have the least number of nuclei captured per image, it still yielded between 10 and 20 nuclei per image, which was deemed statistically sufficient when totaling over the nine images per condition per replicate, and averaging over three replicates.

Image preprocessing

The FIJI software94 was used for processing the images and for developing the nuclei labeling portion of the algorithm. The labeled data organization and analysis portion of the algorithm was developed in Python (Anaconda Software Distribution). Graphical presentation of the results and statistical testing were conducted in Microsoft Excel.

To prepare the captured images for analysis with the ice detection algorithms, the images were first preprocessed in FIJI. The original RGB-colored images were converted to 8-bit grayscale images by splitting the colored channel (FIJI, Split Channel command) and taking the blue-color channel, chosen for its balanced pixel intensity profile. The red-color channel showed low contrast between the cell nuclei and cytoplasm due to overly light pixel intensities in the image overall, making any edge features less distinct. The green-color channel showed high contrast between the nuclei and cytoplasm, but overly dark pixel intensities within the nuclei overall, masking any possible edge features in the nuclei. The resulting blue-color channel grayscale images displayed a white background for regions with no stained cell material and steadily increasing darker pixel intensity from the cytoplasm to nuclei to nuclear contents. Camera noise was then removed from the grayscale images using the “Despeckle” command.

Nuclei segmentation

Identification of the cell nuclei was done using the Cellpose3 AI-guided generalist cell and nuclei segmentation program76 (available at: https://github.com/MouseLand/cellpose). The Google Colab version of the Cellpose3 program was used to speed up the processing time (available at: https://colab.research.google.com/github/MouseLand/cellpose/blob/main/notebooks/run_cellpose3.ipynb); this online version provided the program’s installation codes and training images. The green-color channel grayscale images, split from the original RGB-colored images, were used for nuclei segmentation because they exhibited the highest contrast between nuclei and cytoplasm. The images were first inverted before analyzing with the Cellpose3 program, because Cellpose3 detects black as background. These inverted grayscale sample images were loaded in a Google Drive and then mounted by the Google Colab program following the provided instructions. Our personal code for segmenting the cell nuclei, written following the Cellpose3 algorithm’s coding structure and in accordance with our image storage parameters, was input into the “run denoising and segmentation” window. The Cellpose3 model used was “nuclei” for nuclei segmentation. The diameter of the nuclei was set as 100, determined manually using the Cellpose GUI applied to randomly selected PC and NC sample images. The output regions of interest (ROI) were saved as a .zip file that could be read by the FIJI ROI manager plugin. The Cellpose3 ROIs were then preprocessed in FIJI: (1) ROIs with a size <8000 pixels were rejected, assuming that they were too small to be cell nuclei, typically false detections of large staining artifacts; (2) ROIs with touching borders that were >100 pixels long were merged into one ROI. These were mostly seen in the NC conditions, where a single nucleus can contain two to three large ice crystals, causing the Cellpose3 algorithm to identify it as two to three distinct but bordering nuclei rather than just one; (3) ROIs located at the bottom left and top left corners of the images were rejected, because they contained a black imaging artifact from the microscope’s aperture.

CED algorithm

The preprocessed blue-color channel grayscale images and preprocessed Cellpose3 ROIs were assessed using the Canny edge detection (CED) algorithm to detect nuclei containing visible small ice crystal patterns. First, a Gaussian blur of radius 3 was applied to each image. Then, a FIJI-based CED plugin (available at: https://imagej.net/ij/plugins/canny/index.html) was applied using parameters of kernel radius 1, low threshold 1, and high threshold 2. This produced a binary image with white pixels marking the edges in the image, and black pixels as the background. Then, all white pixel edges that were less than 5 pixels long were removed. The ROIs were then overlaid over the binary image, and the percentage of white pixels within each ROI was determined by calculating the ratio of the number of white pixels to the total number of pixels. For HUVEC sample images, each ROI containing >4% white pixels was labeled as displaying small ice crystal patterns, while for PCEC sample images, each ROI containing >5% white pixels was labeled as displaying small ice crystal patterns.

Ring algorithm

The preprocessed blue-color channel grayscale images and preprocessed Cellpose3 ROIs were assessed using the Ring algorithm to detect nuclei containing visible large ice crystal patterns. The ROIs were first overlaid over the grayscale image. Then, two intermediate threshold values were determined for each ROI based on its pixel intensity histogram measured automatically by FIJI’s “Histogram” function: the intensity range threshold and the pixel count threshold. The intensity range threshold, corresponding to the intensity value set at 40% between the lowest pixel intensity and the highest pixel intensity within the ROI, was calculated following the formula:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rm{Range\; threshold}}=\left({\rm{maximum\; intensity\; value}}-{\rm{minimum\; intensity\; value}}\right)\times 0.4+{\rm{minimum\; intensity\; value}}$$\end{document}Range threshold=maximum intensity value−minimum intensity value×0.4+minimum intensity valueThe pixel count threshold, corresponding to the intensity value where 30% of the total number of pixels in the ROI possessed lower intensity, was calculated automatically by the “threshold” macro in FIJI. The minimum of the two intermediate threshold values was chosen and applied to the image, producing a binary image where all pixels with a lower intensity than the threshold were set as black pixels, corresponding to the dark-stained patterns within the cells. Then, a new ring-shaped ROI was created by merging the original ROI with a smaller ROI set 10 pixels within the original ROI from all sides, accomplished through a combination of the “Enlarge”, “Make band”, and “AND” operations. This ring-shaped ROI was applied over the image, and the percentage of black pixels within the ring-shaped ROI was calculated as before. For HUVEC sample images, each ring-shaped ROI containing >15% black pixels was labeled as displaying large ice crystal patterns, while for PCEC sample images, each ring-shaped ROI containing >20% black pixels was labeled as displaying large ice crystal patterns.

Final label processing

Once all the ROIs had been labeled, the total number of ROIs and their corresponding labels were counted and organized for each image. ROIs with no labels were counted as nuclei with “no ice”, ROIs with only the small ice crystal label were counted as nuclei with “small ice”, ROIs with only the large ice crystal label were counted as nuclei with “big ice”, while ROIs with both small ice and large ice crystal labels were counted as “both”. The “both” result was merged into the “small ice” result, because the Ring algorithm can produce a false positive capture for nuclei exhibiting the multi-edge small ice crystal patterns if those nuclei also happen to exhibit dark staining in their outer borders. In other words, the ice detection by the CED algorithm dominates over the Ring algorithm. For each condition in each replicate, the numbers of nuclei with the “no ice”, “small ice”, and “big ice” labels were summed across the nine images to compute a set of results that were representative of that sample coverslip. The percentages of nuclei with each label were computed for each coverslip by taking the ratio of the summed number of nuclei with each label to the total summed number of nuclei on the coverslip. The percentage results from the three replicates (n = 3) were then averaged to compute the final percentage results of nuclei with “no ice”, “small ice”, and “big ice” labels for each condition, with data represented as mean ± standard deviation.

A detailed step-by-step protocol for operating the image pre-processing, nuclei segmentation, CED and Ring algorithms, and labeling and processing is included in Supplementary Note 1.

Algorithm optimization with the difference-tuning method

To apply the difference-tuning method, images from one replicate were chosen as the training case, and a non-blinded eye counting result was determined for the 20 conditions by manually counting and labeling the nuclei in each image as containing “no ice”, “small ice”, or “big ice”. It is recommended to select both conditions with known ice patterns (negative and positive control) and conditions with unknown but distinctly observed ice patterns. Cell nuclei that contained “no ice” were defined as those that displayed a smooth coloration, with little to no gradients in the pixel colors and overall uniform staining intensity. The PC and vitrified samples were used as the references for determining “no ice” patterns, because they were known conditions that did not contain visible intracellular ice in the nuclei. Cell nuclei that contained “small ice” were defined as those that displayed clear geometric staining patterns, showcasing many edge and border features resulting from the contrast between the displaced, dark-stained cell material and light-stained regions where the ice crystals had formed. Ice formation that resulted in “hole”-like, “branch”-like, or “grain”-like patterns, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 5, was considered to be “small ice” patterns. Cell nuclei that contained “big ice” were defined as those that exhibited only one or two large ice crystals, displaying a singular, heavily stained, ring-like edge on the outer border of the nuclei. The NC sample was used as the reference for determining “small ice” and “big ice” patterns, because it was a known condition that exhibited intracellular ice formation. To apply the difference-tuning method, the percentage values of cell nuclei displaying “no ice”, “small ice”, and “big ice” determined by the eye count were subtracted from the percentage values determined by the algorithms (algorithm − eye count). A positive difference value indicated an over-detection by the algorithm in the number of nuclei with said ice label, while a negative value indicated an under-detection. The algorithms’ processes and parameters were optimized by reducing this difference as much as possible, with a difference of 10% set as the maximum acceptable amount. The algorithms were optimized with the objective of having as little difference in the applied processes and parameters between the two cell types as possible, in order to increase the algorithms’ generality. A higher weight was placed on minimizing differences between the algorithm and eye count for the PC, vitrified, and NC conditions, because the ice formation tendencies in these conditions are known.

Supplementary Figure 6 shows the difference-tuning method applied to three major parameter optimization decisions for the CED algorithm. First was the application of the Gaussian blur to the images before the CED process. As shown in the first data column for each of the five conditions, without the Gaussian blur applied, the CED algorithm greatly over-detected the amount of small ice crystals in the PCEC monolayers due to stain artifacts, especially in the PC and vitrified conditions, where there was no ice formation. Different kernel radii for the Gaussian blur were tested, and radius = 3 was determined to be the one that best balanced the removal of staining artifacts and the preservation of actual ice edges. The percentage of white pixels within an ROI for labeling that nucleus as containing “small ice” was also optimized, as shown in columns 2 and 3. For HUVEC monolayers, selecting >5% of pixels being white within the ROI as indicating “small ice” resulted in >10% under-detection of “small ice” in 3 out of the 20 conditions, while selecting >4% of pixels being white as indicating “small ice” resulted in the minimum difference across all conditions. For PCEC monolayers, selecting >5% of pixels being white as indicating “small ice” displayed an overall better minimization of differences than selecting >4%, especially for the PC and vitrified conditions. For the CED macro, we tested various combinations of values at 0.5 increments for the radius (between 1 and 2), low threshold (between 1 and 2), and high threshold (between 2 and 4) for images both pre-Gaussian blur and post-Gaussian blur to determine the final CED parameters.

Supplementary Figure 7 shows the difference-tuning method applied to three major parameter optimization decisions for the Ring algorithm. First was choosing the final threshold to be the minimum value of the two intermediate thresholds instead of using the intermediate thresholds by themselves. As shown in the first data column, applying just the intensity range threshold resulted in >10% over-detection of large ice crystals for 3 out of the 20 conditions for both HUVEC and PCEC monolayers. Selecting the minimum intermediate threshold from the choice of two greatly reduced the difference. Various values were tested for both the intensity range threshold (increments of 5% between 20% and 40% of the minimum to maximum intensity range) and pixel count threshold (increments of 5% between 20% and 40% of total pixels with lower intensity) to determine the final values for both. The percentage of black pixels within the ring-shaped ROI for labeling that nucleus as containing “big ice” was also optimized, as shown in columns 2 and 3. For HUVEC monolayers, selecting >20% of pixels being black pixels within the ring-shaped ROI resulted in greater than 10% under-detection of large ice crystals in the HES −40 °C glass condition. At >15% of pixels being black within the ring-shaped ROI, the differences between the algorithm and the eye count were reduced to below 10% for all conditions, with the compromise of slightly increased differences in the PC and vitrified conditions. For PCEC monolayers, >20% of pixels being black within the ring-shaped ROI resulted in slightly smaller differences between the algorithm and the eye count for the PC and vitrified conditions when compared to >15% of pixels being black, and similar differences in the other conditions. These percentages of pixels being black were chosen from a range of tested values (increments of 1% between 15% and 20%) and were determined as the best at balancing the difference minimization across all conditions.

Blinded eye counting and statistical analysis

Blinded eye counting of visible intracellular ice in the cell nuclei was conducted by two human participants. All of the nine images per sample in the 20 conditions across the three replicates (a total of 540 images) were randomly scrambled and renamed such that no identifiers to their original conditions could be seen. Participants were first shown 2−4 images from each of the PC, vitrified, and NC conditions with known ice formation tendencies as examples, with chosen images exhibiting clear, representative patterns for the “no ice”, “small ice”, and “big ice” labels. They were then asked to count and label each cell nucleus in the scrambled image set with the corresponding ice pattern label. Once all the images had been counted, the scrambled image set with their corresponding ice label data was reorganized back into their original conditions, and the corresponding percentages were calculated. Statistical analysis using two-tailed Student’s t-tests assuming unequal variances was conducted in Excel to calculate statistical significance between comparisons of the two blinded participants’ ice detection results and the final algorithm’s results; a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The t-test analysis was chosen because of the small sample size and the assumption of normal distribution for biological data95,96. For each t-test comparison, the simple effect size (non-standardized effect size) was calculated by the difference between the means of each compared result to determine the practical scientific importance of the effect. The simple effect size calculation was chosen because the resulting mean difference unit of “percentage of nuclei” provides a meaningful evaluation of practical significance without being influenced by the variations in standard deviation present in unitless standardized effect size calculations97,98. The mean difference confidence interval of 95% was calculated for each t-test comparison, with the lower bound value being equal to or less than zero, supporting no statistical significance, and the lower and upper bounds being close to zero, supporting low practical significance99,100.

In addition, image-specific differences between the algorithm and the averaged blinded manual eye count were calculated as follows. For each of the individual 540 images captured, the percentage of nuclei with no ice, small ice, and large ice crystal patterns, as measured by the two blinded manual eye counts for that image, was averaged and subtracted from the percentage ice patterns as measured by the algorithm for that image. The resulting percentage differences of the three ice patterns were averaged for each condition to show the image-specific differences between the algorithm and the blinded manual eye counts for each of the conditions. The resulting percentage differences for the three ice patterns were also averaged for all images of HUVEC and PCEC monolayers to show the total averaged image-specific differences for each cell type. Data was represented as mean ± standard deviation, with positive difference values representing over-detection by the algorithm and negative difference values representing under-detection by the algorithm. Supplementary Figure 8a shows the total averaged image-specific differences between the algorithm and the averaged blinded manual eye count for both cell types. Supplementary Figure 8b–e shows the image-specific differences for all conditions. For the mean and standard deviation data of the image-specific percentage differences, see Supplementary Table 5.
