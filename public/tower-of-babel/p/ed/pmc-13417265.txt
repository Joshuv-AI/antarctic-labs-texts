Light/NADH Bistimulation ATP Synthesis Artificial Organelles for Nitrogen Assimilation and RNA Transcription in Artificial Cells
Authors: Yongshuo Ren, Weichen Wang, Jingjing Zhao, Yan Zhao, Yingming Zhao, Shubin Li, Yanhao Liu, Xiaojun Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Energy supply is a fundamental requirement for maintaining a complex metabolic network within the artificial cells. RNA transcription is a key step in the genetic inheritance of artificial cells. Targeting these two key issues in the field of artificial cells, a light/NADH bisimulation adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis artificial organelle (LNAO) capable of producing ATP through two distinct pathways is built to power reactions within artificial cells. The LNAO, derived from Escherichia coli expressing rhodopsin, mimics photosynthetic phosphorylation by using rhodopsin and ATP synthase to produce ATP; meanwhile, it mimics oxidative phosphorylation by employing the electron-transport chain modules of E. coli membranes to synthesize ATP from NADH. The produced ATP is used to produce CTP with inorganic nitrogen (NH4 +) assimilation catalyzed by CTP synthase (CTPS). The synthesized ATP and CTP are subsequently used as substrates for RNA transcription. The LNAO, CTPS, and iSpinach RNA transcription pathways are encapsulated into giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs) to construct artificial cells capable of inorganic nitrogen assimilation and RNA transcription. The LNAOs provide flexible energy supply methods for constructing complex metabolic networks in artificial cells. The successful coupling of LNAO, CTPS, and the RNA transcription pathway inside artificial cells steps forward toward autonomous artificial cells.

Introduction

The complexity of living cells is fascinating and inspiring. Artificial cells are designed to replicate partial (or all) cellular functions, − including compartmentalization, protein synthesis, , metabolism, , growth and division, , signal transduction, − motility, , and energy supply. , Among them, constructing energy regeneration modules is particularly critical for maintaining multiple cellular functions within the artificial cells. Among the energy-consuming biochemical reaction processes, nucleotide metabolism is essential for genetic material synthesis, which is a crucial step in the reproduction of artificial cells.

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) serves as the primary energy currency within cells, providing energy for various biological processes through the hydrolysis of its high-energy phosphate bonds. Both natural organelles and artificial organelles were developed to provide ATP inside artificial cells. Thylakoids , and mitochondria successfully produced ATP molecules upon light irradiation and the addition of pyruvate inside artificial cells, respectively. Artificial organelles were constructed to produce ATP by using photophosphorylation or oxidative phosphorylation processes. Photosensitive proteins such as photosystem II (PSII) , and bacteriorhodopsin (BR) , in the vesicle membrane were utilized to generate a proton motive force (PMF) upon light irradiation to drive ATP synthase to convert adenosine diphosphate (ADP) to ATP, mimicking the phosphorylation process. The respiratory electron-transport chain proteins in the vesicle membrane, including complex I , and cytochrome bo3 oxidase, , generated PMF across the lipid bilayer by consuming substrates such as NADH or dithiothreitol, enabling ATP synthase to convert ADP to ATP using the oxidative phosphorylation process. ATP production in cyanobacteria and green plants is achieved via both oxidative phosphorylation and photophosphorylation. This dual-metabolic capability confers energetic plasticity to cells, enabling sustained ATP supply through a dynamic interplay between photophosphorylation and oxidative phosphorylation under fluctuating environmental conditions. While previous studies elegantly demonstrated ATP production using either photophosphorylation via rhodopsin or oxidative phosphorylation − , via respiratory chain modules, these approaches yielded organelles with a single-energy supply pathway. The integration of both pathways within a single artificial organelle would be a critical advance, as it would not be merely the sum of these two pathways but would rather endow the system with an emergent energy plasticity. Such a bistimulation organelle could adapt its ATP output in response to fluctuating environmental conditions (e.g., day/night cycles or varying chemical fuel availability), thereby conferring a resilience unattainable by any single-pathway organelle.

Nitrogen assimilation is the process of transferring inorganic nitrogen elements from nitrate and ammonium into nitrogen-containing biomacromolecules, including proteins and nucleic acids. , Nucleotides are raw materials of nucleic acids. Cytidine triphosphate synthase (CTPS) is the key enzyme of nitrogen assimilation directly from ammonium. Lieberman , obtained CTPS from Escherichia coli and proved that ammonia could be utilized directly for CTP synthesis by CTPS in vitro. Using ammonia as a nitrogen donor, the enzyme reaction kinetics of CTPS were systematically investigated. This nitrogen assimilation process is the main source of CTP inside the cells. RNA was transcribed via an in vitro transcription system inside artificial cells. , Aufinger et al. demonstrated that RNA was transcribed using transcription organelles inside artificial cells. In most cases, the transcription of RNA was coupled to the expression of protein using a cell-free transcription–translation system , with all preadded nucleotides. While these systems demonstrated RNA and protein synthesis within artificial cells, they fundamentally relied on the external supply of organic nutrients, i.e., presynthesized nucleotides. A critical hallmark of a living system, however, is the ability to assimilate inorganic precursors (such as ammonium) into organic biomolecules. Therefore, the coupling of nucleotide synthesis via inorganic nitrogen assimilation and subsequent transcription into RNA within artificial cells is highly challenging.

Herein, to address the issue of a single-energy supply mode, the light/NADH bistimulation ATP synthesis artificial organelle (LNAO) was constructed to produce ATP under light and/or NADH stimulation. LNAOs were confirmed to produce ATP inside artificial cells upon light and/or NADH stimulation. To realize the conversion from inorganic nitrogen to macromolecules within artificial cells, the synthesized ATP was subsequently utilized to assimilate ammonium by converting nucleotides from UTP to CTP. The generated ATP and CTP were successfully employed as substrates for RNA transcription. This work paves the way to building self-powered artificial cells capable of environmental adaptation and reproduction.

LNAOs are used to synthesize ATP in two ways: photophosphorylation and oxidative phosphorylation. In the photophosphorylation process, light-driven rhodopsin pumps protons into the vesicle interior, generating a proton motive force (PMF). The PMF is subsequently utilized by ATP synthase to synthesize ATP (Figure b, green box). In oxidative phosphorylation, NADH is oxidized by NADH dehydrogenase to transfer electrons to coenzyme Q, which is further oxidized by cytochrome bo3 oxidases to generate the PMF. Subsequently, the PMF drives ATP synthesis by ATP synthase (Figure b, blue box). LNAOs are encapsulated into artificial cells to provide ATP for the assimilation of ammonium from UTP to CTP catalyzed by CTPS. As the main raw material for RNA, the produced CTP and ATP are successfully used to synthesize iSpinach RNA (Figure c), which is visualized by combination with DFHBI-1T. Thus, ammonium assimilation and RNA transcription by self-energy supply are realized inside artificial cells.

Light or NADH-Driven ATP Synthesis by LNAOs

Rhodopsin variant from Roseiflexus sp. RS-1 was successfully expressed in E. coli C43­(DE3) due to their color change from brown to pink (Figure S2a) and a molecular weight of about 28 kDa (Figure S2b). This specific rhodopsin variant was selected because it exhibited the highest single-cell proton-pumping activity among 43 kinds of rhodopsins expressed in E. coli, thereby ensuring efficient light-driven ATP synthesis in the resulting artificial organelles. To evaluate the proton-pumping activity of the expressed rhodopsin, E. coli (OD600 value of 3) was irradiated with light intensities of 0, 15, 30, and 60 mW/cm2. The pH decrease rate of the solution exhibited a positive correlation with light intensity (Figure S3), which confirmed that rhodopsin was oriented in the membrane to pump protons outward, consistent with previous studies. After high-pressure homogenization treatment, the E. coli membranes were disrupted and reassembled into LNAOs with inverted rhodopsin orientation. The diameter of LNAOs was measured to be 207.7 ± 3.7 nm with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.361 ± 0.023 (Figure S4), and the ζ potential was −15.7 ± 0.9 mV, which can be attributed to the presence of negatively charged phospholipids in the inner leaflet of E. coli membrane. To quantify the amount of rhodopsin in LNAOs, the retinal residue was measured using the reaction between hydroxylamine and the aldehyde group of retinal to form oxime derivatives (Figure S5), since one rhodopsin covalently links with one retinal residue. The protein content of rhodopsin in LNAOs was calculated to be 14.2 wt %.

LNAOs can synthesize ATP under light conditions (Figure a, left). To evaluate the ATP synthesis capacity of LNAOs over time, 1 mg/mL LNAOs were continuously illuminated with 30 mW/cm2 light for 4 h. The ATP concentration increased during the first 2 h and plateaued at about 55 μM thereafter with 1 mM ADP (Figure b). The observed ATP synthesis plateau indicated the stage with balanced ATP synthesis and hydrolysis by LNAOs. The ATP hydrolysis reaction velocity under steady-state ATP concentration (0.70 ± 0.08 μM/min) was the same as the ATP synthesis reaction velocity under light illumination (0.73 ± 0.05 μM/min). The rhodopsins in the membrane of LNAOs generated PMF upon light irradiation (Figure S6a). No ATP synthesis was observed in the dark or in the presence of carbonyl cyanide 3-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP) with light irradiation because CCCP molecules diminished PMF across the membrane. , The decrease in 9-amino-6-chloro-2-methoxyacridine (ACMA) fluorescence demonstrated that rhodopsin pumps protons inward, opposite to its orientation in the E. coli membrane, confirming the formation of inverted membrane vesicles. This also indicated that the ATP synthases from E. coli were inverted in LNAOs at the desired orientation. The ATP synthesis of LNAOs exhibited a light-controlled manner by switching the light on and off with a 15 min interval (Figure c). The ATP synthesis occurred upon illumination and ceased immediately when the light was turned off. LNAOs maintained activity over four light-on–off cycles. The concentration of LNAOs was directly related to the ATP synthesis rate. The total protein concentration in LNAOs was used to define the concentration of LANOs in this article. Under 30 mW/cm2 illumination, the ATP synthesis rates were increased from 0.18 ± 0.02 to 0.73 ± 0.05 μM/min with LNAO concentration increasing from 0.1 to 1 mg/mL (Figure S7a). However, the ATP synthesis rate per rhodopsin decreased from 0.40 ± 0.05 to 0.17 ± 0.01 min–1 (Figure S7b). The reason for this decrease might be due to the increase in turbidity of the high-concentration LNAO solution, resulting in lower light capture of LNAOs in the solution. To obtain a greater ATP concentration, 1 mg/mL LNAOs were used for subsequent experiments. The ATP synthesis rate increased with light intensities from 0 to 30 mW/cm2 and reached a plateau at 30 mW/cm2 due to the proton pump activity saturation of rhodopsin (Figure S6b), with a maximum ATP synthesis rate of 0.73 ± 0.05 μM/min (Figure d). Therefore, the 30 mW/cm2 light was chosen for subsequent experiments. In addition, the initial concentration of ADP was found to influence the concentration of ATP at equilibrium. The final ATP concentration after 4 h of 30 mW/cm2 illumination demonstrated a positive correlation with the initial ADP concentration, with the concentration of ADP varying from 0.5 to 2 mM (Figure S8a). The concentration ratios of ADP to ATP at equilibrium were all close to the constant of 18 (Figure S8b).

In addition to light-driven ATP synthesis, LNAOs were also capable of NADH-driven ATP synthesis due to the presence of the E. coli membrane proteins involved in oxidative phosphorylation, including NADH dehydrogenase, cytochrome bo3 oxidases, and ATP synthase (Figure a, right). To evaluate the ATP synthesis capacity of LNAOs over time, 0.8 mM NADH was added to 1 mg/mL LNAOs, which led to the formation of PMF (Figure S9). The ATP concentration increased from 0 to 15 min and leveled off afterward, with the concentration of 32.57 ± 3.03 μM at 30 min (Figure e), which confirmed the NADH-driven ATP synthesis capability of LNAOs. The addition of CCCP significantly inhibited the production of ATP molecules. The ATP production of LNAOs was controlled by the sequential addition of NADH with an interval of 20 min (Figure f). The ATP synthesis rate increased with LNAO concentration increasing from 0.1 to 1 mg/mL by adding 0.8 mM NADH, with the maximum rate at 1 mg/mL LNAOs (Figure S10). The concentration of NADH also influenced ATP synthesis. At 1 mg/mL LNAOs, ATP synthesis rates increased with NADH concentrations from 0 to 0.8 mM but decreased from 0.8 to 1.5 mM with the maximum rate of 5.70 ± 0.08 μM/min at 0.8 mM NADH (Figure g). Therefore, 0.8 mM NADH was chosen as the optimal concentration for subsequent experiments. Due to the fluorescent properties of NADH (Figure S11a), we observed that NADH consumption occurred at a steady rate during the first 10 min, after which the consumption rate decreased significantly (Figure S11b). This trend aligned closely with the observed plateau in ATP synthesis (Figure e, red curve). Notably, the ATP synthesis rate in NADH-driven reactions was significantly greater than that in light-driven systems. At a LNAO concentration of 1 mg/mL, the ATP synthesis rate with 0.8 mM NADH was 7.8 times greater than that under 30 mW/cm2 illumination.

Since both light and NADH can drive LNAOs to synthesize ATP, we simultaneously stimulated LNAOs using light and NADH. It was observed that under dual stimulation, the production of ATP (Figure h, green columns) was greater than single stimulation with light (Figure h, red columns) or with NADH (Figure h, blue columns) at 10, 60, and 120 min. Notably, the ATP yield under dual stimulation was less than the simple sum of those under single stimulations. The ultimate driving force for ATP production by LNAOs was PMF. Both light-driven rhodopsin and the NADH-driven electron-transport chain pumped protons into the interior of the LNAOs to generate PMF. When one pathway has already established a high PMF, the consequent electrochemical barrier hinders proton pumping caused by the other pathway.

The light-driven ATP synthesis capability of LNAOs after storing at 4 °C for 12 days remained almost the same as that of freshly prepared LNAOs (Figure S12a), while the NADH-driven ATP synthesis capability of LNAOs remained about 50% after 12 days stored at 4 °C (Figure S12b). This was primarily attributed to the higher stability of rhodopsin compared to that of NADH dehydrogenase and cytochrome bo3 oxidase (Figure S12c,d). In addition, the average size of LNAOs was monitored in the same storage conditions (Figure S13). The slight change of the average size and PDI of LNAOs over 12 days indicated the structural stability of LNAOs. However, under ambient light illumination at 25 °C, both ATP synthesis ability of LNAOs stimulated with light and NADH decreased dramatically (Figure S14a,b); meanwhile, the average size and PDI of LNAOs increased dramatically (Figure S15), indicating both the function and structure stability of LNAOs degraded distinctly.

CTP Synthesis Driven by LNAOs

ATP synthesized by LNAOs was then used to synthesize CTP by CTP synthase (CTPS) using UTP and NH4 + as substrates, following eq . 1 UTP+ATP+NH4+→CTPSCTP+ADP+Pi+H+

To express and purify CTPS, the plasmid pET-15b-CTPS was constructed and transformed into E. coli BL21­(DE3) for overexpression (Figure S16a). Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) analysis of the purified CTPS showed a prominent band close to 66.2 kDa (Figure S16b), consistent with the expected molecular weight of 62.79 kDa. The concentration of Mg2+ has a significant impact on the catalytic activity of CTPS. In the absence of Mg2+, CTPS showed negligible catalytic activity for CTP synthesis (Figure S17). By increasing the Mg2+ concentration from 1 to 10 mM, the concentration of CTP gradually increased from 53.91 ± 3.69 to 440.80 ± 11.50 μM at 10 min (Figure S17a). The enzymatic activity of CTPS increased from 0.018 ± 0.004 to 0.219 ± 0.013 U/mg with Mg2+ from 1 to 10 mM (Figure S17b). 10 mM Mg2+ was chosen in subsequent experiments. The concentration of CTP increased as a function of the concentration of ATP, which was the substrate of CTPS (Figure S18a). The K m of ATP for CTPS was obtained to be 148.43 μM (Figure S18b).

By coupling LNAOs with CTPS in the solution, the CTP was produced upon light irradiation. 1 mg/mL LNAOs, 0.5 mM UTP, 10 mM NH4 +, and CPTS were mixed in a solution (Figure a). Upon 30 mW/cm2 illumination, the production of ATP (Figure b) and CTP (Figure c) was monitored as a function of time. The concentration of CTPS dramatically influenced the production of ATP and CTP. With more concentration of CTPS, ATP concentrations decreased (Figure b), while CTP concentration increased (Figure c) at the same time point. The faster conversion of ATP to CTP with a greater concentration of CTPS explained this result. The concentration of ATP approximately reached its highest at 60 min, which meant that the ATP synthesis rate before 60 min was greater than the CTP synthesis rate. With 0.1, 0.5, and 2 μM CTPS, the concentrations of ATP at 60 min were 27.36 ± 0.92, 19.23 ± 0.88, and 11.16 ± 0.68 μM, respectively. In the absence of light, almost no ATP and CTP were produced with 0.1 μM CTPS, which confirmed that the ATP synthesized by LNAOs promoted the synthesis of CTP. At 0.1, 0.5, and 2 μM CTPS, the maximum concentrations of CTP at 120 min were 20.46 ± 3.46, 35.56 ± 4.05, and 51.97 ± 2.24 μM, respectively.

NADH-stimulated CTP synthesis was investigated (Figure d). The reaction process of NADH stimulation was not the same as that of light stimulation because the light was applied to the light-driven ATP synthesis system throughout the whole process, but in the NADH-driven ATP synthesis system, NADH was added only once at the beginning. The concentration of ATP reached its maximum for about 40 min and then decreased significantly (Figure e) due to the consumption of ATP to produce CTP. An increase in CTPS concentration also led to a decrease in ATP concentration (Figure e) and an increase in CTP concentration (Figure f). The maximum concentrations of ATP were 25.75 ± 1.36, 18.37 ± 0.75, and 11.57 ± 1.23 μM with 0.1, 0.5, and 2 μM CTPS, respectively. At 120 min, the concentrations of CTP were 11.21 ± 2.76, 25.78 ± 3.30, and 32.79 ± 4.90 μM with 0.1, 0.5, and 2 μM CTPS, respectively. For the synthesis of CTP, at both 120 and 240 min, the concentrations of CTP were lower than those upon light irradiation, which was due to the continuous supply of ATP with light irradiation.

The dual stimulation of LNAOs by light and NADH led to greater concentrations of ATP and CTP (Figure g,h,i). During the first 120 min, the concentrations of ATP reached their maximum at about 40 min (Figure h) of 31.71 ± 1.43, 20.65 ± 0.62, and 14.14 ± 0.74 μM with 0.1, 0.5, and 2 μM CTPS, respectively. At 120 min, the concentrations of CTP were 26.59 ± 3.25, 44.66 ± 6.37, and 56.83 ± 5.74 μM with 0.1, 0.5, and 2 μM CTPS, respectively. With another addition of NADH at 120 min, more ATP (Figure h) and CTP (Figure i) were produced.

The turnover numbers of CTPS under light, NADH, and dual stimulation with 0.1 μM CTPS within 2 h were 1.71 ± 0.29, 0.93 ± 0.23, and 2.22 ± 0.27 min–1, respectively. The statistical analysis of ATP production (Figure j) and CTP production (Figure k) at 120 min with 0.1 μΜ CTPS confirmed the advantages of dual stimulation of LNAOs for inorganic nitrogen assimilation.

iSpinach RNA Transcription Driven by LNAOs

The ATP and CTP synthesized by LNAOs using light or NADH were utilized to produce RNA. To prove the concept, a fluorescent RNA aptamer (iSpinach) was chosen as the target RNA because it could bind to DFHBI-1T to emit green fluorescence. The produced ATP and CTP by LNAOs, together with preadded GTP and UTP, served as the substrates for the transcription of iSpinach using T7-iSpinach DNA as the template with the help of T7 RNA polymerase (Figure a). The 92 bp T7-iSpinach DNA was obtained from the plasmid pUC57-T7-iSpinach using PCR (Figure S19). To maximize RNA yield, the concentrations of produced ATP and CTP by LNAOs should be kept at similar levels. Under dual stimulation, the concentration ratios of ATP to CTP at 120 min were 1.19 with 0.1 μM CTPS, 0.39 with 0.5 μM CTPS, and 0.12 with 2 μM CTPS, respectively. Therefore, a CTPS concentration of 0.1 μM was selected for the subsequent transcription studies.

The amount of transcribed iSpinach was monitored using the fluorescence emission at 505 nm from the combination of iSpinach and DFHBI-1T (Figure S20). The concentration of iSpinach was calculated from the calibration curve obtained by using purified iSpinach (Figure S21) in the presence of excess DFHBI-1T (40 μM) (Figure S22). Under 30 mW/cm2 illumination, the synthesis rate and yield of iSpinach increased with T7-iSpinach DNA concentrations ranging from 0 to 10 nM. However, no further increase in rate or yield was observed when the DNA concentration was increased from 10 to 20 nM (Figure b). Therefore, 10 nM T7-iSpinach DNA was chosen for the following transcriptions. To obtain the maximum yield of iSpinach, the concentrations of CTP, UTP, and Mg2+ were optimized. The optimal concentrations of CTP and UTP were determined to be 0.5 mM (Figure c). The optimal Mg2+ concentration was 10 mM (Figure d).

The concentration of iSpinach driven by both light irradiation and the addition of NADH was always greater than that driven by sole light irradiation or the addition of NADH (Figure e), which resulted from the high yield of ATP and CTP by LNAOs stimulated both with light and NADH (Figure j,k). After 180 min, the iSpinach concentrations were 116.49 ± 11.10 nM with sole light irradiation, 46.40 ± 3.04 nM with sole NADH, and 143.00 ± 4.92 nM with both light and NADH, respectively, with the relative ratios being 2.51:1:3.08. With no light irradiation, almost no iSpinach was detected (Figure e). To further confirm the successful transcription of iSpinach, the iSpinach RNA molecules produced under light, NADH, and combined light and NADH conditions, as well as a dark control after 3 h were purified using columns. The purified iSpinach was analyzed by urea-PAGE (Figure f). The RNA band between 50 and 80 bases indicated the expected size of iSpinach at 69 bases, confirming the successful transcription. Using ImageJ to quantify band intensities, the relative grayscale ratios for light, NADH, and combined light and NADH conditions were determined to be 2.86:1:3.92. The RNA yields under these three conditions were consistent with the corresponding ATP and CTP yields, which implied that ATP and CTP concentrations were the limiting parameters of RNA yield. The K m values of T7 RNA polymerase are 33–60 μM for UTP, 23–81 μM for CTP, 31–76 μM for ATP, and 76–190 μM for GTP, respectively. − The added concentration of GTP (0.5 mM) and UTP (0.5 mM) was much higher than their K m values, while the produced ATP (31.60 ± 1.34 μM, Figure j) and CTP (26.59 ± 3.25 μM, Figure k) concentrations under dual stimulation for 120 min without transcription were similar as their K m values (lower point in the K m value range). Therefore, ATP and CTP concentrations limited the transcription rate.

All of the abovementioned results successfully demonstrated that LNAOs provided nucleotides for RNA transcription in solution with the light irradiation, the addition of NADH, or both. It was ready to encapsulate all components into giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs) to fabricate an artificial cell capable of inorganic nitrogen assimilation and RNA transcription.

iSpinach RNA Transcription in Artificial Cells Containing LNAOs

The artificial cells capable of RNA transcription driven by LNAOs via light or NADH (Figure a) were fabricated using the emulsion transfer method (Figure S23a) with an average diameter of 18.86 ± 7.56 μm (Figure S23b). The proteins and nucleotides outside the artificial cells were removed by centrifugation and replacement of supernatants 4 times (Figure S24). The melittin was inserted into the artificial cell membrane to form pores to allow NADH to enter GUVs (Figure a). The equal extracellular concentrations of NTPs, DFHBI-1T, DTT, KCl, and spermidine were supplied to avoid the leakage of those components inside artificial cells. Over 2 h, the artificial cells exposed to light (L, third row in Figure b), NADH (N, fourth row in Figure b), or both conditions (LN, bottom row in Figure b) exhibited a continuous increase of the internal fluorescence intensity over 2 h, whereas almost no fluorescence was found inside the GUVs with no light exposure, no addition of NADH and melittin (D, top row in Figure b), or with melittin but with no addition of NADH and light exposure (M, second row in Figure b), which showed that RNA synthesis inside GUVs could be driven by both light and NADH. The iSpinach RNA concentrations inside artificial cells were 134.93 ± 23.21 nM (with light irradiation), 323.89 ± 46.95 nM (with the addition of NADH), and 410.55 ± 47.61 nM (light + NADH), respectively (Figure c), which were determined from the calibration (Figure S25). Interestingly, the iSpinach RNA concentration under NADH-only conditions was higher than that under light-only conditions, which contrasts with the results observed in the solution. The volume of artificial cells occupied about 0.17% of the total volume of the solution. The NADH outside artificial cells was 0.8 mM, which diffused into artificial cells. Therefore, the NADH concentration was maintained at a high level inside artificial cells for 120 min, which resulted in a high concentration of ATP inside artificial cells.

The iSpinach RNA transcription was measured using flow cytometry over 2 h. FITC-H fluorescence intensity was positively correlated with the concentration of iSpinach RNA (Figure S26). Flow cytometry histograms (Figure S26) indicated the CV of the fluorescence of iSpinach to be about 70%. The average FITC-H intensity after 2 h closely exhibited the same trend as the fluorescence intensity data obtained via confocal microscopy. Under light, NADH, and combined light and NADH conditions, the increases in average fluorescence intensity were 1569.33 ± 710.87, 4120.67 ± 437.36, and 5683.00 ± 513.50, respectively (Figure d), which further confirmed the successful fabrication of artificial cells containing LNAOs capable of inorganic nitrogen assimilation and transcription.

Conclusion

A light/NADH bistimulation ATP synthesis artificial organelle (LNAO) was constructed using the inverted membrane of E. coli to produce ATP. E. coli was engineered with rhodopsin in its membrane, enabling the LNAO to possess the photophosphorylation ability. The NADH dehydrogenase, cytochrome bo3 oxidases, and ATP synthase in the E. coli membrane enabled the oxidative phosphorylation ability of LNAO. Therefore, the LNAOs were able to produce ATP triggered by light, NADH, and both light and NADH. This artificial organelle mimicked two critical ATP production pathways in living organisms, i.e., photophosphorylation and oxidative phosphorylation, by combining the primary functions of mitochondria and chloroplasts into a single organelle. The ATP production triggered by both light and NADH was higher than that triggered by either light or NADH alone.

The inorganic nitrogen (NH4 +) was assimilated into CTP by reacting with UTP and ATP catalyzed by CTPS, which was driven by LNAOs. The iSpinach RNA transcription pathway was built using the produced ATP and CTP, together with T7-iSpinach DNA, preadded UTP and GTP, and T7 RNA polymerase. By encapsulating LNAOs, CPTS, and iSpinach RNA transcription pathways into GUVs, artificial cells capable of inorganic nitrogen assimilation and RNA transcription were successfully built. The bistimulation artificial organelle (LNAO) provides flexible energy supply modes for artificial cells, enhancing their resilience to environmental fluctuations. Furthermore, the establishment of CTP synthesis for RNA transcription holds critical importance for advanced cellular functions, such as self-replication of artificial cells. The success of coupling LNAOs, inorganic nitrogen assimilation, and RNA transcription inside an artificial cell lays the path toward autonomous artificial cells.
