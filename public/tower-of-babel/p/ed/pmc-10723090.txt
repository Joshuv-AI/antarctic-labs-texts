Impact of Clinical Decision Support Within the Electronic Medical Record on Opioid Prescribing and Dispensing
Authors: Michelle Nguyen, Seema Ledan, Ching Cheng, Gregory Khan-Arthur, Sara Mukherjee, Anila Xhixho, Kristen Fink
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
IntroductionClinical decision support tools support prescribers and pharmacists as they select and verify appropriate opioid regimens in efforts to combat the high variability in opioid prescribing. This study seeks to examine the impact of alerts within the electronic medical record and pharmacy system on day supply of initial opioid prescribing and dispensing.MethodsThis retrospective study compared a 6-month pre- and postimplementation of clinical decision support tool alerts at an integrated health care system. Data were analyzed to assess changes in the day supply of an opioid at the point of initial prescribing and dispensing based on alerts.ResultsThe best practice alert in the electronic medical record was associated with a 27% change (p = 0.007) in prescribing by the physician, which resulted in a reduction of average day supply from 12.09 to 6.58 days. The alert in the pharmacy system was associated with a 41.3% change (p < 0.001) in dispensing, which resulted in a reduction of average day supply from 13.46 to 6.96 days.DiscussionTo promote judicious opioid prescribing, the best practice alert in the electronic medical record led to a statistically significant change in prescribing. To support appropriate dispensing, the alert in the pharmacy system led to a statistically significant change in dispensing.ConclusionImplementation of two clinical decision support tools that mirrored Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommendations of prescribing less than a 7-day supply when initiating opioids resulted in a decrease in day supply of the opioid prescription for patients identified as opioid-naïve at the point of prescribing and dispensing.

Introduction

The current opioid epidemic in the US has garnered national attention in the clinical setting and public space, as the mortality rate from opioid overdoses continues to exponentially rise. From 1999 to 2020, there was nearly a 5-fold increase in overdose deaths involving prescription opioids with an estimated total of 263,000 overdose deaths. 1 In 2020, 2.3 million Americans were diagnosed with prescription opioid use disorder and approximately 16,416 Americans died from drug overdoses involving prescription opioids. 1,2 Provisional data for 2021 indicated a 28.5% increase in reported drug overdose deaths with opioids accounting for the highest incidence, which is likely attributable to pandemic restrictions, self-isolation, and difficulty with obtaining treatment. 3 With approximately 45 people dying each day from a prescription opioid overdose and the declaration of a national public health emergency, the opioid epidemic demands action to protect our communities from the dangers of opioid abuse. 1

The overprescribing of opioids continues to prolong the epidemic, as taking prescription opioids in higher doses or for longer durations can increase the risk of opioid use disorder, overdose, and death. 3 Early prescribing patterns for opioid-naïve patients are strong predictors for long-term opioid dependence, forming the rationale for a targeted initiative surrounding judicious prescribing. The likelihood of chronic opioid use rises with each additional day supply of medication beyond 3 days, with the largest incremental increases seen when the first prescription supply exceeds 10 or 30 days. 4 However, large doses (50–90 morphine milligram equivalent/day) at initiation are associated with minimal improvement in mean pain intensity and no difference in functionality compared to lower doses (less than 50 morphine milligram equivalent/day). 5

Across all specialties, primary care clinicians prescribe approximately 45% of all dispensed opioids, with substantial growth in their prescribing rates. 6 Although they prescribe the bulk of opioid prescriptions, many primary care clinicians express concerns of opioid misuse, as they report insufficient training in prescribing opioids, difficulty in managing chronic pain, and strong concern for potential addiction. 7 In addition, many describe increased stress as a barrier to appropriately prescribing opioids for pain management. 8 These attitudes and beliefs, combined with increasing trends of opioid-related overdose, underscore the need for better clinician guidance on opioid prescribing. 9 Proper education and training, coupled with the implementation of clinical decision support tools, has proven to support opioid risk mitigation. 10 When these tools are implemented at the point of prescribing, evidence supports changes in prescribing habits, such as substantially reducing prescribed opioid doses in chronic opioid users. 11–13

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Guidelines for prescribing opioids offers clarity on best practices and recommendations based on literature, expert opinion, and stakeholder and public input. When initiating opioids for chronic pain, the guidelines recommend clinicians select immediate-release agents over extended-release or long-acting opioids to reduce the risk of unintentional overdose. 9 In the treatment of acute pain, clinicians are recommended to prescribe the lowest effective dose of immediate-release agents for no longer than the expected duration of pain, often less than 3 to 7 days. 9 Prescribing less than a 7-day opioid supply when initiating opioids for opioid-naïve patients could mitigate the likelihood for long-term use and dependence.

To promote judicious opioid prescribing consistent with the CDC Guidelines, clinical decision support tools for Kaiser Permanente Mid-Atlantic States electronic medical record were implemented to support prescribers as they select the most appropriate opioid regimen.

Methods

Design

Assessment of the primary outcome was based on a best practice alert (BPA) built in Kaiser Permanente’s electronic medical record to alert prescribers of opioid orders that exceed the maximum recommended 7-day duration for initial opioid prescribing. Once triggered based on established criteria (eg, no opioid dispensed and sold in the last 90 days with a new opioid prescription that has a calculated day supply of over 7 days), the prescriber is prompted to evaluate whether the prescription is appropriate with required actions. Required actions include changing the quantity or frequency of the opioid to not exceed 7 days or selecting an acknowledgment reason (eg, alert is incorrect, continuation of therapy from outside Kaiser Permanente) if the prescriber desires to continue with the initial order that exceeds the maximum 7-day recommendation. A retrospective chart review was conducted on available data pre- and postimplementation of electronic medical record alerts. For the BPA in the electronic medical record, the preimplementation period was defined as July 1, 2020 to October 1, 2020, and the postimplementation period was defined as October 1, 2020 to January 1, 2021.

Assessment of the secondary outcome was based on a pharmacy management software alert implemented in Kaiser Permanente’s pharmacy system for Medicare and Medicaid members to support pharmacists as they review and verify opioid prescriptions that exceed a 7-day supply for opioid-naïve patients, and it occurred simultaneously to prescriber alert. Once the alert appears, the pharmacist is required to verify the opioid history of the patient and review the prescription prior to dispensing. If the patient is truly opioid-naïve (eg, no opioid dispensed in the last 90 days), the pharmacist must either recommend a decrease in day supply to the prescriber for approval or input drug utilization review codes into the pharmacy management system if the prescription is appropriate (eg, patient meets exclusion criteria). A retrospective chart review was conducted on available data pre- and postimplementation of pharmacy management software alerts. For the pharmacy management software alerts, the preimplementation period was defined as December 1, 2020 to March 1, 2021, and the postimplementation period was defined as March 1, 2021 to June 1, 2021.

Collectively, the study sought to examine the impact of two clinical decision support tools that mirrored CDC recommendations on initial opioid prescribing and dispensing. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Kaiser Permanente Mid-Atlantic States.

Participants

The study included participants who were at least 18 years old and were prescribed an opioid by a Mid-Atlantic Permanente Medical Group physician for noncancer pain for greater than a 7-day duration. They were identified as opioid-naïve if the electronic medical record, pharmacy management software system, and prescription drug monitoring program showed no opioid prescription sold within the last 90 days of prescribing in all 3 jurisdictions supported in Kaiser Permanente Mid-Atlantic States (District of Columbia, Maryland, Virginia). Participants were excluded if they were pregnant, deceased, on opioids for cancer-related pain, in hospice, palliative care, or long-term care facilities, on suboxone treatment, or did not have a pharmacy management software triggered alert (eg, commercial plan members). After exclusions, there was a total number of 256 participants that were then categorized into their respective study groups based on the date of initial prescribing of opioid prescriptions (Figure 1).

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was the percent change in the number of opioid-naïve patients prescribed more than a 7-day supply from July 1, 2020 to January 1, 2021 due to the BPA in the electronic medical record. The secondary outcome measure was the percent change in the number of opioid-naïve patients dispensed more than a 7-day supply from December 1, 2020 to June 1, 2021 due to the pharmacy alert in the pharmacy management system. Additional outcomes included the change in the average day supply of the initial opioid prescription before and after the implementation of the alerts.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was set at an alpha of 0.05. A χ2 test was used for analyzing the primary and secondary outcomes.

Results

Based on the pre- and postimplementation dates for both the electronic medical record and pharmacy management software alerts, participants (N = 256) were then grouped to review primary and secondary outcomes. To assess the primary outcome or change in opioid prescribing due to the BPA in the electronic medical record, 110 participants were included; the preimplementation period included 21 participants, and the postimplementation period included 89 participants.

To assess the secondary outcome or change in opioid dispensing due to the pharmacy alert in the pharmacy management software system, 146 participants were included; the preimplementation period included 107 participants, and the postimplementation period included 39 participants.

Most participants within the randomized sample (N = 256) were women (59.4%) with the highest prevalence in the 55 to 64 age group (26.6%) and 65 and older age group (37.9%) (Table 1). Among all randomized participants, the most commonly prescribed opioids were tramadol (42.2%), acetaminophen/codeine (18.4%), and oxycodone/acetaminophen (18.4%). Participants were mostly prescribed opioids for indications of peri-operative pain (22.3%), back pain (20.7%), and joint pain (17.6%). The physician specialties who prescribed the highest percentage of opioids included internal medicine (49.6%), family medicine (10.5%), and ambulatory surgery (10.5%). Outcomes

In the assessment of the primary outcome, 0 out of the 21 participants in the preimplementation phase had the day supply of their opioid prescriptions changed prior to implementation of the BPA in the electronic medical record. After the implementation of the BPA in the electronic medical record, 24 out of 89 opioid prescriptions were changed by the prescriber to a 7-day or less duration. As a result of the BPA, there was a statistically significant 27% difference in day supply prescribed for opioid-naïve patients (p = 0.007) (Table 2). Among the prescriptions changed by the prescriber, the average day supply of the initial opioid prescription was reduced from 12.09 days to 6.58 days with most prescribers choosing to reduce the quantity of the prescription (83.3%), but others decided to change the frequency of use (8.3%) or remove the order altogether (8.3%) (Figure 2).

To assess the secondary outcome, prescriptions were reviewed to see if the pharmacist recommended a decrease in day supply to the prescriber due to the alert in the pharmacy management software system. Prior to the implementation of the alert in the pharmacy management software system, 32 out of the 107 opioid prescriptions were recommended for day supply change with a prescriber approval. After the implementation of the dispensing alert, 28 out of the 39 prescriptions were recommended by the pharmacist for day supply change with a prescriber approval to a 7-day or less duration (Table 2). As a result of the pharmacy management software alert, there was a statistically significant 41.3% difference in day supply prescribed for opioid-naïve patients (p < 0.001). Among the prescriptions recommended by the pharmacist and changed by the prescriber, the average day supply of the initial opioid prescription was reduced from 13.46 days to 6.96 days (Figure 3).

Discussion

The data gathered from opioid-naïve participants and day supply prescribed show the impact of clinical decision support tools within the electronic medical record and pharmacy management software system on initial opioid prescribing and dispensing. To aid the existing education provided to physicians and pharmacists regarding CDC recommendations related to the appropriate prescribing and dispensing of opioids, alerts were designed and implemented to balance the clinical benefits of opioids against their safety risk.

Similar to the current literature surrounding primary care physicians bearing the highest rate of opioid prescribing, internal medicine and family medicine physicians collectively wrote more than 60% of opioid prescriptions in this study. 5–7 To promote judicious opioid prescribing in line with CDC recommendations across all clinical settings, the BPA in the electronic medical record led to a statistically significant change in prescribing after the alert was enacted when compared to the prior timeframe when only education was provided to physicians. Potential rationale behind lack of prescriber intervention despite the alert included continuation of therapy as noted by the prescriber in the BPA’s acknowledgment reason and the as-needed use of opioids that may have allowed previous prescribed opioids to extend beyond a 90-day duration. Additional factors that may have influenced prescriber intervention included whether the prescription drug monitoring program (PDMP) was reviewed prior to prescribing to verify the patient’s opioid history and use of different benchmarks to measure opioid-naïve status (no opioid sold within 180 days vs 90 days).

To support pharmacists as they verify opioid prescriptions for opioid-naïve patients, the alert in the pharmacy management system led to a statistically significant change in dispensing when compared to the preimplementation period, during which pharmacists were only educated on excessive duration beyond 7 days in their required Corresponding Responsibility training. Pharmacists at Kaiser Permanente Mid-Atlantic States are strongly encouraged to check the PDMP prior to verifying and subsequently dispensing any opioid prescription. However, without system alerts prompting the pharmacist to check the patient’s opioid-naïve status, there was potential for missed opportunities. Although the alerts are enacted and proven to be effective, there continues to be potential for overriding alerts, such as insufficient education to understand and define opioid-naïve status and limited time to verify opioid naive status.

There were several limitations to the study despite achieving statistically significant results. This was a retrospective chart review; thus, the intrinsic design of the study limited the available outcomes to evaluate. Additionally, the reported data and electronic medical record data do not identify previous prescriptions that were sold externally, so triggered alerts may not be applicable to some participants. However, the benefits outweigh the risks or limitations, which justified the implementation of the electronic medical record and pharmacy management software alerts. Further, if a prescriber did not use the prepopulated order inputs in the electronic medical record, and instead chose to use free text when placing the order for an opioid, the electronic medical record would not have been able to calculate a day supply for the order, and consequently, the BPA would not have fired even if the prescription was written for an excessive duration beyond 7 days. Another limitation was the phased approach with launching the alerts in the pharmacy management software system depending on the line of business. With the alerts for patients with commercial plans not in place during the study time period, the investigators had to further exclude the participant volume, leading to decreased number of outcomes to evaluate for statistical significance. Additionally, within the study design, there was not a transition period embedded in the timeframe for implementation of the BPA in the electronic medical record to ensure that the alert was accurately firing for the intended patient population based on the established criteria.

Throughout the COVID-19 pandemic, the integrated medical system at Kaiser Permanente Mid-Atlantic States continued to assist and educate physicians and pharmacists in appropriate prescribing. As physicians and pharmacists were seeing many patients during a stressful period, the alerts were not halted but posed as a benefit to alert the health care team for improved patient safety.

Given the compelling efficacy of the alerts and statistical significance, additional trials with larger sample sizes for each line of business could further magnify the impact on both opioid prescribing and dispensing. Additionally, considering that all alerts have been thoroughly implemented in both the electronic medical record and pharmacy system, future studies could evaluate the long-term impact of the alerts (eg, review continued use of opioids after day supply change on initial fill) now that there is preliminary data centered around the timeframe that the alerts were first established.

Conclusion

Implementation of two clinical decision support tools that mirrored CDC recommendations for no more than a 7-day opioid duration for opioid-naïve patients was associated with a substantial decrease in day supply of the initial opioid prescription. The outcomes clearly convey the value of strategic stops at multiple points of the prescribing process for physicians to use their judgment along with evidence-based guidelines, to truly evaluate whether opioid prescriptions are clinically appropriate for those who are considered opioid-naïve. Further studies are needed to evaluate whether the alert would further decrease the continued use of opioids following a decrease in the initial day supply dispensed.
