Effects of a cow’s milk protein–free diet as an adjunct to standard therapy in juvenile idiopathic arthritis: a randomized controlled study
Authors: Seyedeh Fatemeh Hashemi Karouei, Vadood Javadi, Mahboubeh Mansouri, Khosro Rahmani, Niloofar Shashaani, Mahmoud Hajipour, Reza Shiari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundJuvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is a chronic autoimmune disorder, characterized by chronic idiopathic joint inflammation in children under sixteen years of age. Emerging evidence suggests that food allergens- particularly cow’s milk proteins may modulate inflammatory pathways, and that their elimination could potentially ameliorate disease activity. This study aimed to evaluate through a randomized controlled clinical trial whether eliminating cow’s milk protein from the diet influences disease activity and symptom severity in children with JIA.MethodsIn this randomized controlled trial, 120 children with controlled juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) receiving stable standard therapy were randomly allocated to either a cow’s milk protein–free diet (intervention group, n = 60) or an unrestricted diet (control group, n = 60) for one month. Disease activity was assessed at baseline and post-intervention using subjective measures (patient- and physician-reported Visual Analog Scale [VAS]) and objective parameters, including erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), active joint count, morning stiffness duration, fever.ResultsCompared with baseline, the intervention group demonstrated statistically significant improvements in multiple outcomes, including patient VAS (2.9 ± 1.9 to 1.7 ± 1.4; P < 0.001), physician VAS (2.5 ± 1.8 to 1.6 ± 1.3; P < 0.001), ESR (14.6 ± 13.7 to 9.4 ± 7.3 mm/hr; P < 0.001), CRP (7.3 ± 13.7 to 2.5 ± 3.6 mg/dL; P = 0.011), morning stiffness duration (6.1 to 0.6 min; P = 0.002), active joint count (0.55 to 0.11; P < 0.001), and Disease Activity Score (5.53 ± 4.44 to 3.0 ± 2.6; P < 0.001). In contrast, the control group exhibited only minor or non-significant changes.ConclusionIn children with well-controlled JIA, a cow’s milk protein–free diet was associated with modest short-term improvements in pain and disease activity. These findings suggest a potential adjunctive benefit alongside standard therapy, but the short duration, low baseline disease activity, and reliance on parental self-report warrant cautious interpretation.Ethical consideration and trial registrationThe protocol of this clinical trial has been registered in the Iranian Clinical Trial Registration Center (registration code: IRCT20241230064216N1). Registered 28 April 2025, available from https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir/search/result?query=IRCT20241230064216N1, and ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, registered 10 November 2024 (Ethics Code: IR.SBMU.MAP.REC.1403.531).

Background

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is the most common chronic rheumatic disease of childhood, characterized by persistent joint inflammation, pain, and stiffness that significantly impair physical function and quality of life. The global incidence of JIA ranges from 0.8 to 6.22 per 100,000 children per year, with prevalence estimates between 7 and 401 per 100,000 [1–3].

JIA encompasses several subtypes, including oligoarticular, RF-positive and RF-negative polyarticular, systemic, psoriatic, enthesitis-related arthritis, and undifferentiated forms. Despite systemic JIA, which affects boys and girls equally, oligoarticular and polyarticular forms are more common in girls. Each subtype presents with distinct clinical patterns, making early diagnosis and treatment planning challenging [4, 5].

Despite major advances in pharmacologic therapy including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), and biologic agents, many children with JIA continue to experience active disease. Complications including joint damage and psychosocial difficulties, emphasizing the need for complementary strategies alongside standard treatment [6].

In recent years, growing attention has been directed toward the potential role of environmental and dietary factors– including Early-life exposures, feeding patterns, and gut microbiota composition- in the pathogenesis of diseases such as JIA [7–14].

Epidemiological studies have suggested a possible association between cow’s milk protein intolerance and the onset or worsening of autoimmune conditions in children [15]. Gut dysbiosis and mucosal inflammation may represent a shared pathogenic pathway linking autoimmune diseases and food allergies. Proposed mechanisms include immune complex formation, molecular mimicry, and activation of pro-inflammatory pathways via the gut-immune axis [16].

Some experimental and clinical studies indicate that eliminating cow’s milk proteins from the diet may reduce systemic inflammation and improve symptoms in certain inflammatory conditions. Although evidence in JIA remains limited, these findings suggest that a cow’s milk protein-free diet could offer therapeutic benefits [17].

This study aims to address this knowledge gap by evaluating whether eliminating cow’s milk protein as a prototype for major food allergens can influence disease activity, inflammatory markers, and clinical outcomes in children with controlled JIA and pre-existing medical treatment, providing evidence to support dietary interventions as complementary strategies in disease management. The primary clinical question is whether eliminating major food allergens, particularly cow’s milk protein, can contribute to improved control of JIA or not.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study was designed as a randomized controlled clinical trial to evaluate the effects of a cow’s milk protein-free diet on disease activity in children diagnosed with juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA). Blinding in our study was applied to patient allocation not in food preparation. At study entry, all enrolled children had clinically controlled disease and were receiving background therapy. Concomitant medications were recorded at baseline, and no medication changes or dose adjustments occurred in either group during the study period. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Code: IR.SBMU.MAP.REC.1403.531) and the trial was registered prospectively with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT number: IRCT20241230064216N1). Registered 28 April 2025, available from https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir/search/result?query=IRCT20241230064216N1. The research was in line with the Declaration of Helsinki. The parents had given their written consent before their children’s participation.

Study population

Children were recruited from the Pediatric Rheumatology Clinic at Mofid Children’s Hospital in Tehran, Iran, between [December/2024] and [April/2025]. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age under 16 years, (2) confirmed diagnosis of JIA according to the ILAR classification, and (3) the ability to adhere to dietary restrictions. Non-adherence to the diet was an exclusion criterion. There was no patient or public involvement in the design, conduct, or reporting of this trial.

Randomization and allocation

Participants were randomized using a block randomization method with a block size of six to ensure equal distribution across intervention and control groups (N = 60 per group). Randomization sequences were generated using Random Allocation Software (version 2.0). Stratification was applied based on key variables such as age, sex, JIA subtype, disease duration, and presence of complications. Group assignments were concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE) managed by an independent biostatistician who was not involved in clinical procedures, counseling, or analysis. The envelopes were only opened after patient eligibility confirmation and informed consent; therefore, group assignment remained unpredictable.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) to detect a mean difference of 2.5 points in Disease Activity Score, with a standard deviation of 4.0, an alpha level of 0.05, and a power of 80%. The minimum required sample size was 54 per group, and this was increased to 60 to account for potential dropouts.

Laboratory assessors were not aware of group allocation. The dietitian who delivered the intervention had no role in data collection or analysis. Group codes were securely stored and only revealed after the final data analysis was completed (Fig. 1).

Dietary intervention

Children in the intervention group received structured nutritional counseling to follow a diet completely free of cow’s milk protein. Dietary education was delivered at baseline in a face-to-face session by a trained dietitian, under the supervision of a clinical immunologist. Parents were instructed to eliminate all sources of cow’s milk protein, including milk and dairy products derived from cows, goats, or sheep (e.g., milk, yogurt, cheese, butter), as well as processed foods containing milk derivatives. A written list of prohibited foods and acceptable alternatives was provided to all families. Dietary adherence was monitored primarily through parental self-report during bi-weekly structured telephone follow-ups and scheduled clinic visits. During each contact, parents were specifically asked about adherence to the prescribed diet, including any intentional or accidental dietary deviations, exposure to hidden sources of cow’s milk protein, and challenges related to food selection or preparation. Identified difficulties were addressed, and dietary instructions were reinforced as needed. The control group continued their usual diet without dietary restrictions. Concomitant medical care was permitted in both groups and was recorded throughout the study. No diet-related adverse events were reported during the study period.

Blinding

People who measured the outcomes (clinicians and lab staff) did not know which group the patients belonged to. The dietitian providing nutritional counseling was not engaged in the performance of the outcome or in data analysis. Study identifications were kept confidential by the dietitian and revealed only after the final analyses were made.

Owing to the nature of the dietary intervention, blinding of participants and their families was not feasible. Therefore, this study was conducted using a single-blind design. The physician responsible for clinical assessments and all laboratory personnel performing outcome measurements were blinded to group allocation throughout the study. Dietary counseling was provided by a dietitian who was not involved in outcome assessment, follow-up visits, or data analysis. Group allocation codes were securely maintained by the statistical researcher and were not disclosed to the investigators until all statistical analyses had been completed.

Outcome measures

The outcome was disease activity assessed via the Juvenile Arthritis Disease Activity Score-10 (JADAS-10) for oligo and poly arthritis, a validated composite index including active joint count, physician’s global assessment, patient/parent global assessment (using a 10-point Visual Analog Scale), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, duration of morning stiffness (in minutes), and active joint count and Disease Activity Score appropriate for other subtype as systemic form include of systemic features (fever, rash, organ involvement) and considering enthesitis and axial involvement in SPA.

Assessments were performed at baseline and repeated after one month in both groups.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of distribution was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Within-group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-parametric data. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 120 children with juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) were enrolled and successfully randomized into two groups: 60 in the intervention group (cow’s milk protein-free diet) and 60 in the control group (Regular diet). Gender distribution was balanced between the groups (46% male, 54% female; P = 1.00). The majority of participants were of Fars ethnicity, with no statistically significant difference between groups (P = 0.26). The oligoarticular subtype was the most common form of JIA in both groups (intervention: 53.1%, control: 48%) (Table 1).

Pain assessment (patient and physician VAS scores)

Patients in the intervention group reported a significant reduction in pain severity. Self-reported Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores decreased from 2.9 ± 1.9 to 1.7 ± 1.4 after one month (P < 0.001). Physician assessments reflected these improvements, with VAS scores decreasing from 2.5 ± 1.8 to 1.6 ± 1.3 (P < 0.001). In the control group, patient VAS scores showed a slight, non-significant reduction (from 2.8 ± 4.2 to 2.38 ± 4.0; P = 0.3), while physician VAS scores demonstrated a modest yet significant improvement (2.3 ± 0.2 to 1.8 ± 1.6; P = 0.01) (Table 2).

Inflammatory markers (ESR and CRP)

In the intervention group, ESR decreased significantly from 14.6 ± 13.7 mm/hr to 9.4 ± 7.3 mm/hr (P < 0.001). However, the control group showed only a slight decrease, from 15.3 ± 17.1 mm/hr to 11.7 ± 7.7 mm/hr, which was not statistically significant (P = 0.072).

C-reactive protein (CRP) levels improved significantly in both groups. In the intervention group, CRP decreased from 7.3 ± 13.7 to 2.5 ± 3.6 mg/dL (P = 0.011), while in the control group, levels dropped from 9.3 ± 17.5 to 4.2 ± 5.6 mg/dL (P = 0.014). However, the extent of reduction was more pronounced in the intervention group (Table 2).

Morning stiffness and active joint count

Children in the intervention group experienced a significant decrease in the duration of morning stiffness from 6.1 min at baseline to 0.6 minutes’ post-intervention (P = 0.002). The control group also improved, but to a lesser extent (3.4 to 1.0 min; P = 0.028).

Similarly, the number of active joints significantly dropped in the intervention group—from 0.55 to 0.11 (P < 0.001). The control group showed a smaller decrease (0.53 to 0.26; P = 0.05) (Table 3).

Disease activity scores

Disease Activity Score significantly improved in the intervention group, decreasing from 5.53 ± 4.44 to 3.0 ± 2.60 (P < 0.001). In the control group and showed a non-significant decrease from 5.38 ± 5.67 to 4.17 ± 7.28 (P = 0.066) (Table 3).

Overall summary

Compared to the control group, children following the cow’s milk protein-free diet showed greater improvements across all outcome measures. These included subjective assessments of pain, fever, objective inflammatory markers, physical function indicators (morning stiffness and active joint counts including axial joints), entheses and composite disease activity scores. The results suggest that dietary elimination of cow’s milk proteins can lead to meaningful clinical benefits in managing JIA symptoms.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial suggests that a diet free of cow’s milk protein may be associated with modest short-term improvements in selected clinical outcomes, including pain and overall disease activity, in children with juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) who are stable on standard drug therapy. The improvements observed in patient and physician reported visual analog scale (VAS) scores suggest potential effects on subjective symptoms as well as physician-assessed disease activity.

It is important to note that baseline disease activity in our cohort was low, reflecting the controlled disease status of participants at study entry. Accordingly, the observed changes in disease activity parameters represent small absolute reductions, which nevertheless reached statistical significance over the one-month follow-up period. These findings may therefore reflect a modest supportive benefit rather than large clinical effect or disease-modifying activity.

Small reductions in ESR and CRP were observed in the intervention group, which may reflect mild anti-inflammatory effects of dietary modification; however, the low baseline values limit the magnitude of detectable change and a potential floor effect may have constrained the extent of measurable improvement.

Clinically, children in the intervention group demonstrated reductions in morning stiffness duration and the number of active joints, whereas changes in the control group were minimal. Despite these differences, the short duration of follow-up and the mild baseline disease activity warrant cautious interpretation of the clinical relevance of these findings.

Our results are broadly consistent with previous studies proposing that cow’s milk proteins and other dietary antigens may contribute to immune activation in susceptible individuals [18, 19]. Epidemiological evidence, including reports by Arvonen et al. (2017), has suggested associations between cow’s milk allergy and the development of JIA [18]. Experimental and clinical studies have suggested that elimination of cow’s milk proteins might influence pro-inflammatory cytokine production, intestinal barrier function, and systemic immune responses, although these mechanisms were not directly assessed in our study [8, 20–23].

Although the present study did not directly assess gut microbiota composition, intestinal permeability, cytokine profiles, or immunologic markers, several biologically plausible pathways have been proposed in previous literature to explain potential interactions between diet and immune-mediated diseases [8, 18–29].

Increasing evidence suggests that gut dysbiosis may alter immune homeostasis, promote low grade intestinal inflammation, and impair epithelial barrier integrity, thereby facilitating systemic immune activation in susceptible individuals [24–26]. Such mechanisms have been implicated in various autoimmune and inflammatory conditions, including juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) [26]. However, these pathways were not directly evaluated in our cohort and therefore remain speculative in the context of the present findings.

Food-related immune responses may be mediated through both IgE and non–IgE dependent mechanisms. Non–IgE mediated reactions, particularly to cow’s milk proteins, can be clinically subtle and are often identified only through elimination and reintroduction strategies [15, 21]. Some observational and epidemiological studies have suggested potential associations between cow’s milk allergy and the development or activity of JIA [18], although consistent mechanistic evidence remains limited [14, 22, 23].

In addition, immune modulation related to chronic dietary antigen exposure including cytokine-mediated systemic effects or altered IgG subclass responses such as IgG4 has been described in selected allergic and autoimmune conditions [27, 28]. Nevertheless, IgG4 levels and related immunologic pathways were not assessed in this study. Therefore, any proposed involvement of IgG4-related or broader immune-complex mechanisms should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than explanatory of the observed clinical changes.

Taken together, while existing literature provides biologically plausible frameworks linking diet, gut–immune interactions, and systemic inflammation, the present trial was not designed to investigate mechanistic pathways. Consequently, causal inferences cannot be drawn, and further mechanistic studies are warranted to clarify the biological basis, if any, of dietary effects in JIA [19, 29].

Overall, these findings support the hypothesis that targeted dietary interventions, such as a cow’s milk protein–free diet, may offer a complementary short-term approach alongside pharmacological therapy in selected children with well-controlled JIA. However, these preliminary observations require confirmation in larger and longer-term randomized control trials incorporating objective adherence measures and mechanistic assessments.

Clinical implications

The findings of this study suggest that a cow’s milk protein-free diet may be a feasible and safe adjunct to standard therapy in children with well-controlled JIA. While modest improvements in pain and disease activity were observed, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of disease-modifying effects. Dietary modification should be implemented alongside not in place of conventional treatment. These results support further evaluation of nutritional strategies as adjunctive approaches in JIA management [18, 29, 30].

Strengths and limitations

Most previous investigations in this field have been limited to case reports or observational studies. The strengths of the present study include its randomized controlled design, blinded outcome assessment, stable background pharmacologic therapy, the use of multiple validated clinical and laboratory outcome measures (VAS, ESR, CRP, and disease activity scores), and the inclusion of multiple JIA subtypes, which enhances both the comprehensiveness and generalizability of the findings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size and short duration of follow-up preclude the assessment of long-term efficacy or the sustainability of dietary effects. Dietary adherence was assessed solely by parental self-report, without objective verification methods such as biomarkers or detailed food diaries. Additionally, other dietary and lifestyle factors were not systematically controlled, and gut microbiota or immunologic mechanisms were not directly evaluated [31].

Future directions

Future studies should include larger cohorts with longer follow-up to determine the durability and clinical significance of dietary interventions in JIA. The incorporation of objective adherence measures, comprehensive dietary assessments, and mechanistic evaluations—including microbiome and immunologic analyses—would provide deeper insight into the underlying biological pathways. Exploration of combined anti-inflammatory dietary approaches and alternative milk substitutes may further clarify the role of nutrition in modulating disease activity and improving outcomes in JIA [32, 33].

Conclusion

Considering the impact of environmental risk factors such as nutrition on arthritis, in this study we investigated the role of a cow’s milk protein-free diet on disease activity in JIA. A cow’s milk protein-free diet was associated with modest but statistically significant short term improvements in pain and clinical disease activity in children with well-controlled JIA. Although these findings suggest a potential adjunctive benefit of dietary modification in addition to standard therapy, the short duration of the intervention and the mild baseline disease activity and parental self-report to assess dietary adherence, warrant cautious interpretation. Larger randomized controlled trials with longer follow-up and mechanistic assessments are needed to confirm these findings and to clarify the underlying biological pathways [19, 34–36].

Take-home message

Eliminating cow’s milk protein from the diet may lead to modest improvements in disease activity and symptom severity in children with juvenile idiopathic arthritis. Dietary modification could be considered as a safe adjunct to standard therapy, but further research is needed to evaluate long-term clinical benefits.
