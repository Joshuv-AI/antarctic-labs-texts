AFP assistant: a retrieval-augmented generation and large language model-powered multilingual polio chatbot for low-resource language communities
Authors: Gelane Biru, Honey Gemechu, Firanol Teshome, Eyerusalem Gebremeskel, Hundessa Daba Nemomssa, Kokeb Dese, Melaku Tilahun, Karrasaaqii Bulti Wakgari, Nardos Bisrat, Gelan Ayana, Jude Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute flaccid paralysis (AFP) surveillance in Ethiopia remains limited due to hard-to-reach populations, restricted healthcare access, low public awareness, and persistent underreporting within community-based surveillance (CBS) systems. Contributing to this are inadequate training of community reporters, communication barriers, and cultural beliefs and misinformation that further hinder timely case detection. Here, AFP Assistant, a multilingual large language model powered chatbot, is developed to strengthen CBS through improved health communication and real-time reporting. The system supports Amharic, Afaan Oromo, and English to enhance accessibility and inclusivity. A curated dataset derived from literature, guidelines, and community interviews was translated and validated by experts to ensure clinical and cultural accuracy. The system integrates supervised fine-tuning with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to deliver accurate, context-aware responses. Trained on 468 question–answer pairs using pretrained Gemini 2.5-flash, the model achieved an accuracy of 0.90 and a loss of 0.31. The RAG framework improved retrieval relevance, grounding, and response efficiency. Evaluation through in-house testing and human annotation using a structured rubric demonstrated robust performance across tasks and languages, while user feedback confirmed usability and relevance. These findings highlight the potential of AI-driven chatbots to enhance AFP surveillance and equitable health communication in low-resource settings.Subject terms: Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Polio is a highly contagious infectious disease caused by the poliovirus, which can lead to sudden muscle weakness and, in severe cases, permanent paralysis1–4. This paralysis is referred to as acute flaccid paralysis (AFP), defined as the abrupt onset of weakness or paralysis with reduced muscle tone, most often occurring in children, or in any paralytic illness where poliomyelitis is suspected5,6. Although poliovirus is the most notable cause, AFP can arise from a wide range of etiologies, including other infectious agents (e.g., enteroviruses), autoimmune disorders (e.g., Guillain–Barré syndrome, Transverse Myelitis), and neuromuscular conditions such as myasthenia gravis or botulism1,7–10. Its clinical manifestations are diverse, often presenting as facial droop, limb weakness, swallowing or speech difficulties, and respiratory compromise, all of which require urgent medical attention. To eradicate the disease, the Global Polio Eradication Initiative (GPEI) was established and outlined four endgame strategies aimed at supporting eradication efforts while enhancing the detection of silent circulation of vaccine-derived polioviruses (VDPVs)11,12. Among these, high-quality AFP surveillance is recognized as the gold standard for detecting and reporting polio cases13,14. One important approach within AFP surveillance is community-based surveillance (CBS), which leverages community volunteers (CVs) and local leaders to actively identify and report suspected AFP cases through systematic case finding15. CBS for AFP is particularly crucial in hard-to-reach pastoral and semi-pastoral areas, where local engagement plays a vital role in timely case identification and reporting16,17. Despite its advantage, community-based AFP surveillance in many regions faces persistent challenges, including delayed detection and reporting, limited community awareness, inadequate health infrastructure, low immunity in underserved populations, high population mobility, security constraints, and surveillance performance gaps, especially in underserved communities18–21.

In Ethiopia, CBS for AFP serves as a vital mechanism for early detection, reporting, and investigation of poliomyelitis cases22. However, its overall effectiveness remains limited by systemic and contextual barriers, including inadequate public health infrastructure, political instability, weak communication networks, volunteer demotivation, low health literacy, and inadequate access to reliable information23,24. Deep-rooted cultural misconceptions and misinformation further complicate the situation25,26. In many communities, AFP is attributed to supernatural causes such as witchcraft or spiritual afflictions. As a result, parents of paralyzed children often seek help from traditional bone setters or religious institutions rather than formal healthcare facilities. A key barrier to effective detection is the absence of a community-level knowledge base about the disease. This knowledge gap reduces the sensitivity of AFP surveillance, as many community members cannot differentiate between polio and other causes of paralysis.

To address persistent challenges in CBS for AFP, a range of multifaceted interventions have been introduced. These include intensive training and capacity-building programs, alongside the expansion of surveillance activities into hard-to-reach and conflict-affected areas, while maintaining both sensitivity and specificity10. Central to these efforts has been the structured engagement of community members, including volunteers, local leaders, and frontline health workers, which has significantly improved case detection and reporting18. Their participation has fostered trust, heightened community awareness, enhanced the timeliness of notifications, and strengthened the overall effectiveness and sustainability of the surveillance system. In recent years, these community-driven approaches have been complemented by technological innovations designed to bridge systemic gaps within the AFP surveillance framework27–29. Notable examples include the deployment of mobile applications to facilitate timely and accurate data collection30,31, and the use of educational campaigns disseminated via social media and digital platforms to reinforce awareness and sustain community engagement32. Despite these advancements, critical gaps remain, highlighting the need for more comprehensive strategies. In particular, the integration of emerging digital technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI) holds considerable potential for enhancing predictive analytics, automating case detection, and enabling intelligent information dissemination. Leveraging such innovations could further strengthen the sensitivity, timeliness, and responsiveness of AFP surveillance, ensuring greater resilience and effectiveness in diverse settings.

Recent advances in AI, especially in large language models (LLMs), have accelerated the growth of conversational AI, with chatbots emerging as powerful conversational entities in healthcare33. Several studies have explored their development across diverse health applications, including medication support, disease monitoring, mental health counseling, symptom assessment, and patient assistance in accessing trustworthy health information. For instance, one study introduced a specialized chatbot for ophthalmologic consultations, particularly macular degeneration, using a bidirectional encoder representation from transformers (BERT), gated recurrent unit (GRU), and term frequency–inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) based retriever–reader model34, while another work developed an automated medical chatbot to streamline healthcare communication through AI-driven conversational interfaces built on natural language processing (NLP) techniques35. Medibot similarly aims to enhance healthcare accessibility and user interaction by integrating NLP with mapping technology36, and the proposed E-Health Bot employs advanced NLP algorithms to enable more human-like interactions37. In the mental health domain, researchers have integrated models such as efficiently learning an encoder that classifies token replacements accurately (ELECTRA) and BERT into chatbots to improve counseling support38, whereas other work leverages LLMs, particularly generative pre-trained transformer (GPT)-4, to more faithfully replicate patient-provider conversations, thereby enhancing diagnosis and treatment recommendations39. Another research in the same domain explores a consultation chatbot to bridge the lack of awareness and limited knowledge about depression using NLP40. Additionally, cutting-edge NLP techniques using large language model meta-AI (LLaMA) have been applied to build medical chatbots capable of handling a broad range of medical inquiries41. In the African context, emerging research has begun to address the unique challenges of low-resource languages and settings. For example, studies in Afaan Oromo have explored symptom-based specialist recommendation systems, contributing to the development of domain-specific corpora and supervised learning approaches tailored to local linguistic contexts42. Similarly, a Swahili-language medical chatbot (Lueji) adopts a hybrid fine-tuning and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) approach to address low-resource constraints43.

For infectious diseases, particularly in the context of COVID-19, chatbots have been extensively used to disseminate health information on symptoms, precautionary measures, and available medical treatments44–46. Beyond information delivery, these systems have also played a critical role in combating misinformation and limiting the spread of false or misleading health content47. Extending this application to broader public health contexts, tools such as AIDA48, a general-purpose chatbot developed in collaboration with UNICEF has been designed to provide reliable information on childhood vaccination. However, almost all health-sector chatbots are developed for resource-rich languages, particularly English, which limits access for communities in low-resource language settings where language barriers persist. While recent systems often fine-tune LLMs for domain-specific tasks, leveraging their ability to predict and assemble statistically likely word sequences to answer complex questions and follow intricate instructions49, customization for low-resource languages remains challenging due to scarce datasets, high computational costs, and persistent research gaps50,51. The underrepresentation of low-resource languages, which are less studied and less digitized, further hinders advancements in NLP compared to English52–55.

Moreover, though many healthcare chatbots have been developed in recent years, a critical gap persists in addressing infectious diseases such as polio, particularly in low-resource settings where community awareness and early detection are essential. To bridge this gap, this study presents the development of AFP Assistant, a multilingual, AI-powered chatbot built on a hybrid architecture that integrates LLM fine-tuning with RAG to deliver accurate, context-aware, and continuously updated responses. Unlike existing tools, the key contribution of this work is the development of a multilingual assistant supporting Amharic and Afaan Oromo alongside English, enabling access for users in low-resource language settings and promoting health information equity. In addition to general awareness, the system integrates AFP surveillance guidelines, locally relevant myths and misconceptions, and contextual public health information related to polio in Ethiopia. The chatbot provides trusted information on symptoms, vaccination, and prevention, guides users in reporting suspected AFP cases, and supports frontline health workers with on-demand, evidence-based resources. By recording queries anonymously and generating aggregated insights, the system contributes to data-driven public health strategies and awareness initiatives. Beyond its technical innovation, the AFP Assistant represents a purposeful advancement toward equitable healthcare access by delivering vital medical information in underserved languages and strengthening CBS systems. More than a digital information tool, it fosters meaningful community engagement, facilitates early case detection, and enables timely reporting, thereby reinforcing Ethiopia’s public health infrastructure and supporting global polio eradication efforts. Its distinctive value lies in bridging language barriers and addressing digital health inequities through an inclusive platform that empowers both health workers and community members. For example, a mother in a remote village who suspects her child may show signs of AFP can interact with the chatbot in her native language, such as Amharic or Afaan Oromo, to receive accurate, culturally and linguistically accessible guidance on symptoms, next steps, and care-seeking pathways. By enabling real-time, context-sensitive communication, the AFP Assistant demonstrates strong potential to enhance disease surveillance, promote informed decision-making, and strengthen preparedness for future public health emergencies in Ethiopia and beyond.

Results

Results of language model selection

Following the screening of candidate models based on research-specific criteria, a comparative evaluation was conducted on Gemma 3, LLaMA 3.1, and DeepSeek-R1 via Hugging Face, as well as Gemini 2.5-flash via Vertex AI. Each model was tested using five benchmark questions per language in English, Amharic, and Afaan Oromo. Language understanding (LU) was defined as the model’s ability to correctly interpret queries in the target language, while response correctness (RC) was assessed by comparing model outputs against reference documents for semantic and factual accuracy. As shown in Fig. 1, all models perform near-optimally in English; however, performance declines in low-resource languages. Gemini 2.5-flash demonstrates the strongest cross-lingual robustness, resulting in its selection for this work. Gemma 3 and DeepSeek-R1 achieve moderate LU but with reduced RC, and LLaMA 3.1 performs poorly in both Amharic and Afaan Oromo.

Results for retrieval-augmented generation

The evaluation of retrieval performance for this study was conducted using multiple indicators, including retrieval count, relevance score distributions, query response time, and retrieval-specific metrics such as Precision@k and Recall@k. The retrieval count was configured to return seven documents per query, serving as a consistency check for the pipeline. Any deviation from this expected count may indicate inefficiencies or misconfigurations in the retrieval mechanism. The relevance scores, generated by the ChromaDB vector store’s embedding function, measure the degree of similarity between queries and retrieved documents. A distribution skewed toward higher scores reflects stronger retrieval quality, whereas irregularities or lower values may suggest limitations in the embedding model or retrieval process. Furthermore, query response time serves as a key indicator of system efficiency, directly influencing the quality of real-time user interaction. Lower response times indicate smoother performance and enhanced usability, often corresponding to simpler queries that require minimal retrieval or reasoning. In contrast, longer delays may result from more complex or multifaceted queries, increased retrieval depth, or intermittent connection issues, all of which can impact system responsiveness. To evaluate response times across all three languages, ten representative queries were used for each of Amharic, Afaan Oromo, and English. This ensured consistency, comparability, and a reliable assessment of system performance under multilingual conditions. To further strengthen the evaluation of the RAG component, retrieval-specific metrics, precision, and recall at k values were computed using a labeled benchmark that maps user queries to relevant document chunks. As k value increases, Recall improves consistently, indicating better coverage of relevant information as more candidate documents are retrieved. In contrast, Precision shows a slight decline before stabilizing, suggesting that additional retrieved chunks may introduce marginally less relevant results while maintaining overall relevance. This trade-off highlights the importance of selecting an appropriate k value, with moderate values providing a balanced compromise that supports effective and reliable downstream response generation. Comparison with the state-of-the-art models

In the comparative evaluation, the AFP Assistant was benchmarked against three SOTA LLMs, ChatGPT, Grok, and DeepSeek using a standardized set of AFP-related queries designed to reflect the practical information needs of frontline health workers. These queries were administered in English, Amharic, and Afaan Oromo to rigorously assess multilingual performance across both high- and low-resource language settings. To ensure methodological consistency and comparability, the same evaluation framework employed for system assessment was adopted for the comparative analysis. Specifically, a structured rubric (Table 4) was applied, encompassing multiple dimensions of response quality, including factual correctness, completeness, clarity and understandability, level of detail, structure and organization, contextual relevance, domain specificity, and local language response quality. All model outputs were systematically evaluated using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very poor, 5 = excellent) across these criteria. The evaluation was conducted by six domain experts with relevant linguistic and public health expertise, who independently assessed the responses based on the predefined rubric. Final scores were obtained by aggregating expert ratings, and in cases of differing judgments, the final decision was determined through consensus among the experts. This expert-driven evaluation approach ensures a robust and fair comparison across models, while capturing critical differences in domain-specific accuracy and performance in low-resource languages. From the evaluation set, a representative query “What is polio?” posed in both Amharic and Afaan Oromo is presented in Fig. 8 to qualitatively illustrate the comparative behavior of the models.

Discussion

This study introduced the AFP Assistant, a multilingual AI-powered chatbot designed to strengthen the CBS of AFP in Ethiopia. The system addresses critical barriers to effective surveillance, linguistic and informational inequity by providing reliable, culturally appropriate polio-related information in Amharic, Afaan Oromo, and English. Unlike most existing health chatbots, which primarily target resource-rich languages, the AFP Assistant explicitly prioritizes low-resource language contexts, filling a gap in polio surveillance and awareness.

The results demonstrate that the hybrid architecture of SFT combined with RAG effectively improves accuracy, contextual grounding, and multilingual robustness. In particular, the findings highlight the effectiveness of transfer learning and multilingual modeling for low-resource languages such as Amharic and Afaan Oromo. By leveraging a pretrained multilingual model, the system is able to transfer rich linguistic and semantic knowledge from high-resource languages, significantly improving performance despite limited labeled data. The use of a parallel multilingual corpus further supports cross-lingual alignment, enabling the model to benefit from English-language supervision while maintaining semantic consistency across languages. Additionally, the integration of RAG plays a critical role in improving factual grounding by injecting contextually relevant knowledge during inference, thereby reducing hallucinations. The adoption of PEFT within the Vertex AI environment further demonstrates that effective domain adaptation can be achieved with limited computational resources, making the approach practical for real-world deployment in low-resource settings. Finally, Gemini 2.5-flash was identified as the most suitable model due to its superior handling of low-resource languages, outperforming other SOTA LLMs in both RC and LU.

These findings have significant implications for Ethiopia’s surveillance system. By empowering communities to recognize symptoms, report suspected AFP cases, and access prevention guidance in their own languages, the AFP Assistant enhances both the sensitivity and timeliness of CBS. It also supports health workers and volunteers with on-demand resources, thereby strengthening local capacity and bridging critical gaps in communication and trust, particularly in rural and underserved areas where conventional health communication tools often fall short.

A few limitations should be acknowledged. The scarcity of structured datasets in Amharic and Afaan Oromo required extensive translation and validation by native speakers and health experts, a resource-intensive process that may have introduced subtle biases. Computational limitations restricted large-scale experimentation, as even smaller LLMs demanded significant GPU resources, and the cost of fine-tuning limited the use of larger adapters for parameter-efficient training. Evaluation relied primarily on human judgment through query testing, a common practice in generative model research but inherently subjective, especially in the absence of standardized benchmarks for Amharic and Afaan Oromo. Technical challenges arose during preprocessing and data handling, particularly with large JSONL text entries; token limits sometimes caused encoding errors, memory overloads, or data truncation, necessitating careful chunking and strict validation. Furthermore, the system currently depends on stable internet connectivity, which may limit accessibility in the most resource-constrained communities.

Despite these challenges, the AFP Assistant demonstrates the feasibility and value of AI-driven, multilingual health tools in low-resource settings, providing a practical pathway for strengthening CBS. Its modular architecture enables adaptation to other public health contexts by updating the knowledge base and incorporating disease-specific datasets and guidelines without requiring major changes to the core model. The approach can be adapted to high-burden and emerging diseases, such as measles, malaria, tuberculosis, and Mpox, by integrating relevant clinical and surveillance information. In addition, the framework is designed to scale to other low-resource language settings through the use of multilingual models and RAG, enabling accurate handling of linguistically diverse medical content. While careful selection of models and retrieval components remains important, the overall system is highly adaptable. Future work includes expanding language coverage (e.g., Somali and Afar), improving offline and low-bandwidth functionality, integrating with national health information systems for real-time reporting, and supporting structured data collection during community visits. Collectively, this approach has the potential to evolve into a multilingual, culturally adapted, and community-centered AI platform that supports disease prevention, surveillance, and public health resilience across Africa.

Methods

AFP Assistant overview

The AFP Assistant is built on a hybrid architecture that combines fine-tuning LLMs with a RAG framework. Fine-tuning embeds domain-specific knowledge, while RAG enables accurate, context-aware access, and up-to-date responses drawn from a structured knowledge base. To ensure inclusivity, the system incorporates advanced NLP techniques with multilingual support for low-resource languages such as Amharic and Afaan Oromo, enabling equitable access to health information across diverse communities.

AFP Assistant was developed in a structured five-step process. It began with data collection and curation, where polio-specific datasets were obtained from interviews, research articles, reviews, and documents. These were formatted into RAG-compatible files for retrieval and JavaScript object notation lines (JSONL) files for fine-tuning, ensuring the models are trained on reliable and relevant data. The platform design and development phase involved fine-tuning selected LLMs on Vertex AI56, integrating them with a RAG framework and ChromaDB57 for efficient retrieval and context-aware response generation. The fine-tuned model is then deployed to a Vertex AI endpoint, forming the system’s technical backbone. The implementation and development phase focused on multilingual integration and deployment across mobile and web platforms to ensure accessibility and scalability. In the chatbot integration stage, the AFP Assistant was tested and integrated with the fine-tuned NLP model for effective query processing and real-time response generation. A live monitoring dashboard enabled visualization of user interactions and system performance, supporting real-time evaluation and decision-making. Finally, the maintenance and iteration phase ensured continuous improvement and feature enhancement through regular updates and user feedback, strengthening the chatbot’s accuracy, responsiveness, and long-term reliability in supporting polio surveillance and public health communication in Ethiopia. System architecture

The AFP Assistant system architecture is organized into three main layers: data ingestion and storage, query processing and retrieval, and response generation. In the data ingestion and storage layer, relevant data sources, including medical guidelines, research articles, and community insights, are ingested, preprocessed, and stored in a structured knowledge base. The query processing and retrieval layer manages user interactions by first detecting the input language, specifically distinguishing among Amharic, Afaan Oromo, and English, to route queries appropriately. Once identified, the query is embedded into a vector representation and used to retrieve semantically relevant text segments from the knowledge base via a vector database. In the response generation layer, the retrieved evidence is combined with the user’s query and passed to the fine-tuned LLM, which generates outputs that are both medically accurate and culturally appropriate. This multilingual and hybrid design ensures synergy between the two components, with fine-tuning that embeds stable domain knowledge on polio, while RAG enables dynamic access to updated information, together reducing hallucinations, improving cross-lingual consistency, and enhancing factual reliability. Retrieved results, combined with system instructions, are passed to a fine-tuned multilingual LLM trained on structured datasets to generate the final response.

Datasets collection and curation

Comprehensive textual data related to poliomyelitis, covering symptoms, causes, prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and surveillance, were collected through a systematic and reproducible protocol. The data sources included peer-reviewed research and review articles retrieved from academic databases such as Google Scholar, PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, JSTOR, DOAJ, IEEE Xplore, and ResearchGate, as well as authoritative guidelines, policy documents, and educational materials obtained from reputable websites, including the World Health Organization, the GPEI, the Ethiopian Ministry of Health (MoH), the Ethiopian Public Health Institute, the Oromia Health Bureau, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the Mayo Clinic. Database searches were conducted using structured keyword combinations polio, poliomyelitis, AFP, surveillance, epidemiology, vaccination, and community health, with queries adapted to each platform and restricted to English-language publications from 2002 to 2024. Importantly, the record counts reported apply only to research and review articles retrieved from academic databases, while website-based materials were used to supplement and contextualize the dataset. The search yielded 260 records, of which 36 duplicates were removed, leaving 224 records for screening; 94 records were excluded based on date and relevance. Following a full-text assessment of 130 articles, 70 were excluded due to limited relevance, outdated guidance, redundancy, or the presence of subjective, non-evidence-based claims, including repeated textual segments across sources. This process resulted in a final dataset of 60 high-quality research and review articles, complemented by authoritative web-based resources to ensure accuracy, completeness, and contextual consistency.

Additional insights were collected through interviews, employing purposive sampling to engage 15 respondents with direct experience in Ethiopia’s AFP surveillance system, including healthcare professionals, surveillance officers, and CVs. The use of purposive sampling enhanced the contextual relevance and validity of the findings, ensuring that user-centered perspectives informed both the assessment of system readiness and the development of the multilingual chatbot. The interviews served to gather insights that could be directly integrated into the design of the AFP Assistant, ensuring that its content and features addressed real community needs. The interview material is provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Since most available resources were in English, the entire dataset was carefully translated into both local languages (Amharic and Afaan Oromo). To ensure accuracy and cultural relevance, translations underwent rigorous review by language experts, with native speakers and local medical professionals contributing to the translation, transcription, and verification of medical terminology. A parallel corpus was then developed by aligning English sentences with their Amharic and Afaan Oromo equivalents, ensuring linguistic consistency while preserving technical precision.

To support both the retrieval and fine-tuning processes, the dataset consisted of two formats: a document corpus for RAG and a structured question–answer dataset for LLM fine-tuning. The RAG knowledge base was constructed from curated health resources, comprising approximately 215 pages in English, 172 pages in Amharic, and 222 pages in Afaan Oromo. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), a structured JSONL dataset of question–answer pairs was developed, including 268 pairs in English, 100 in Amharic, and 100 in Afaan Oromo. To enhance contextual relevance, around 50 pairs in each of the local languages were obtained from primary data collection, reflecting locally expressed questions and community-level information needs. These were compiled using locally produced materials such as flyers, case definitions, and training resources developed by the MoH, in addition to interviews. Overall, for Amharic and Afaan Oromo, approximately 18.6% of the dataset was derived from primary sources, while the remaining data was obtained through translation. This reflects the limited availability of publicly accessible health information in these languages and underscores the importance of incorporating locally generated content to improve linguistic and contextual relevance. This comprehensive multilingual integration of both primary and translated data provides a robust foundation for the development of the AFP Assistant, enhancing inclusivity, linguistic relevance, and accessibility for Ethiopia’s diverse communities. Large language model selection

Selecting an LLM is a critical step in developing multilingual healthcare applications. For this work, the chosen models needed to provide robust multilingual support, particularly for low-resource languages such as Amharic and Afaan Oromo, while also being adaptable for domain-specific fine-tuning on polio. Model selection was guided by a strategy that considered both open-weight and managed API-based approaches. First, open-weight models, including Meta’s LLaMA 3.1, Google’s Gemma 3, and DeepSeek-R1, were considered based on their accessibility for fine-tuning, compatibility with multilingual tasks, and flexibility for customization. In parallel, a managed API-based model (Google’s Gemini 2.5-flash) was also included to address limitations observed with open-weight approaches and to provide a comparative perspective. This approach enabled a balanced evaluation of customization capabilities alongside performance and deployment considerations. Suitability was evaluated through query-based tests in English, Amharic, and Afaan Oromo, using five benchmark questions per model (queries are provided in Supplementary Material 3). Performance was assessed based on each model’s ability to interpret queries, generate accurate responses in the appropriate language, and demonstrate awareness of polio-related content. Additional evaluation criteria included accessibility and licensing, compatibility with fine-tuning pipelines, pretraining coverage across multilingual corpora, and efficiency in resource-constrained environments.

As summarized in Table 3, several state-of-the-art (SOTA) open-source models, including LLaMA 3.1, Gemma 3, and DeepSeek-R1, offer valuable capabilities but present limitations that affect their suitability for this research. LLaMA 358 provides open weights and supports both full and parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods, such as low rank adaptation (LoRA) and quantized low rank adaptation (QLoRA). However, its language coverage does not explicitly include low-resource languages such as Amharic and Afaan Oromo, and its larger variants demand extensive graphical processing unit (GPU) memory, making it impractical for locally constrained computational setups. Similarly, Gemma 359 is fully open-source, fully tunable, and supports over 140 languages, making it a strong candidate for multilingual applications. Yet, despite its efficiency relative to other large models, Gemma 3 still requires substantial computational resources for effective fine-tuning, which exceed the capacity of the available local infrastructure. DeepSeek-R160, although notable for its advanced reasoning abilities and cost efficiency, primarily supports English and Chinese, exhibiting weak performance in African low-resource contexts. Its extremely large model size and dependence on multi-GPU further limit its practicality for this study. In contrast, Google’s Gemini61 demonstrated clear advantages across multilingual capability, adaptability, and deployment scalability. The Gemini 2.5-flash model, accessed through Vertex AI, explicitly supports Amharic and other low-resource languages and integrates seamlessly with RAG pipelines via managed application programming interfaces (APIs). Although not open-source, its selection was driven by practical considerations, including limited local computational resources, the need for reliable multilingual performance, and ease of deployment in real-world healthcare settings.

Dataset preparation for fine-tuning on Gemini

After data collection, the dataset underwent comprehensive preprocessing to ensure quality and consistency. Initially, all question–answer pairs were manually developed in English. These entries were then translated and culturally adapted into Amharic and Afaan Oromo by language experts, with native speakers and medical professionals verifying terminology and contextual accuracy. The translated data were aligned to form a parallel multilingual corpus across the three languages. Subsequently, duplicate entries, irrelevant content, and formatting inconsistencies were removed, and the curated data were organized into high-quality question–answer pairs. Each pair was tokenized to meet input–output length constraints (up to 5000 tokens per sample), structured into role-based formats, and converted into JSONL format for compatibility with Gemini’s SFT pipeline. Strict validation procedures ensured that each entry adhered to the defined schema, with nonconforming samples discarded to maintain data reliability and structural integrity. These preprocessing and quality assurance steps were particularly critical for low-resource languages, where data precision directly affects model accuracy and fine-tuning performance. Supervised fine-tuning of Gemini 2.5-flash for domain adaptation

SFT is a targeted training approach that adapts a pre-trained LLM to domain-specific tasks using a carefully curated dataset of question–answer pairs or task-oriented examples62. This process adjusts the model’s parameters to internalize specialized knowledge, terminology, stylistic nuances, and reasoning patterns relevant to the target domain. In healthcare, SFT has been effectively applied to tasks such as diagnosis support, clinical note summarization, and medical entity recognition. Its advantages include improved domain accuracy, reduced hallucinations, and lower inference latency, since relevant knowledge is embedded directly into the model. Compared to training an LLM from scratch, SFT is more computationally efficient; however, it requires high-quality labeled datasets, which are costly and time-intensive to prepare.

In this work, SFT was applied to Google’s Gemini 2.5-flash model using Google Cloud’s Vertex AI, with model inference performed in the us-central1 region. Vertex AI provides built-in support for fine-tuning Gemini models, enabling an efficient and scalable training pipeline. Gemini, a transformer-based generative model trained on diverse multilingual corpora, is particularly well-suited for low-resource languages, allowing the system to generate accurate and culturally appropriate responses for polio-related queries. For fine-tuning, the dataset was split into 80% training and 20% validation sets. The distribution of input–output tokens in the SFT dataset, which directly informs the tuning process, is presented in Fig. 13. To enable efficient adaptation, Vertex AI employs PEFT through adapter-based methods, where lightweight adapters are inserted into each transformer layer while the base model weights remain frozen. In this configuration, only the adapter parameters are updated, significantly reducing the number of trainable parameters and enabling training on modest computational resources.

Retrieval-augmented generation principles and implementation

RAG is a robust framework that enhances the capabilities of LLMs by integrating external retrieval mechanisms. This approach enables LLMs to produce responses that are more accurate, contextually relevant, and up-to-date by grounding outputs in a curated knowledge base63. In a typical RAG workflow, a user query is converted into a vector representation, which a retriever uses to search a vector database or other external sources for semantically relevant text segments. These retrieved segments augment the original query, allowing the LLM to generate responses with improved factual accuracy and contextual alignment. RAG is especially valuable in knowledge-intensive and rapidly evolving fields such as healthcare, where it reduces hallucinations, provides traceable citations, and allows knowledge bases to be updated without retraining the entire model, making it scalable and adaptable.

In this study, the RAG pipeline is applied using the collected polio dataset in Amharic, English, and Afaan Oromo as the knowledge base. To support multilingual queries, the dataset in all three languages was prepared and indexed, ensuring accurate and contextually relevant chatbot interactions. Although the system can ingest multiple document formats, such as PDF, Word, and PowerPoint, this research used Word documents. After ingestion, the text from these documents was extracted and divided into multiple chunks, where each chunk contained 500 tokens. To preserve semantic continuity between the chunks, a 25-token overlap was applied across all segments, and a batch size of 100. Each chunk was then embedded using the multi-qa-mpnet-base-dot-v164 sentence transformer and stored in ChromaDB along with relevant metadata for efficient retrieval. The system employs the lightweight embedding model to support English, Amharic, and Afaan Oromo queries. Although the model is primarily optimized for English, automatic language detection and language-filtered retrieval are applied to mitigate multilingual limitations. This design choice ensures effective performance under limited computational resources. As illustrated in Fig. 14, which presents the RAG workflow for this study, the system first detects the language of the user query, retrieves the most relevant chunks, reranks them, and incorporates the top results into the prompt for the polio-tuned model. A similarity-based reranking approach was used based on cosine similarity with a Hierarchical Navigable Small World index. The reranking parameters included a cosine distance metric, a top-k value of 7, and the embedding model. This choice was driven by the small dataset size, strict low-latency requirements, and limited computational resources. This process enables the tuned model to generate fact-based responses related to polio, thereby ensuring both accuracy and effective public health communication.

Multilingual support and detection of languages

To ensure accessibility and maximize impact across Ethiopia’s linguistically diverse polio-affected communities, the AI system provides robust multilingual support in Amharic, Afaan Oromo, and English, eliminating the need for external translation layers that could otherwise increase response time. Query processing begins with automatic language detection, directing user inputs to the corresponding language-specific retrieval components within ChromaDB. A two-stage detection pipeline enhances accuracy and responsiveness: first, FastText65, trained on over 157 languages, performs initial detection after text preprocessing and demonstrates particularly strong performance for Amharic due to its subword-level modeling. When FastText’s confidence is low, the system invokes Compact Language Detector 2 (CLD2)66, which probabilistically reassesses the input and excels in handling shorter or noisier text, especially in Afaan Oromo. If neither detector confidently identifies the input language, the system defaults to English. By combining FastText’s speed and reliability for Amharic with CLD2’s precision for Afaan Oromo, the system efficiently handles multilingual and noisy queries while ensuring accurate, language-specific retrieval. The system further accounts for linguistic variability in real-world health communication. Amharic and Afaan Oromo exhibit rich morphological structures and flexible word formations, leading to diverse query expressions. FastText’s subword representations help capture these variations, while CLD2 enhances robustness to irregular and fragmented inputs. In addition, locally collected question–answer pairs enrich the training data with common community expressions and informal phrasing. Together, these design choices improve the system’s ability to interpret diverse inputs and generate contextually appropriate responses in multilingual community health settings. System evaluation framework

The chatbot’s response accuracy was evaluated using a combination of in-house (developer) assessment and expert review. Generated responses were compared against verified information from the curated dataset used to construct the system’s knowledge base. In this study, response accuracy is defined as a multi-dimensional measure reflecting the extent to which a generated answer aligns with the factual content of the reference dataset while remaining contextually appropriate, clear, and informative. To operationalize this definition, a structured evaluation rubric was applied using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very poor, 5 = excellent) across six criteria: factual correctness, completeness, clarity and understandability, level of detail, structure and organization, and contextual relevance (see Table 4). A predefined multilingual test set (Amharic, Afaan Oromo, and English) was used for evaluation. Six domain experts fluent in the target languages independently assessed the responses, and final scores were computed by averaging their ratings. This framework provides a transparent and reproducible approach for assessing response accuracy across multiple dimensions.

To ensure an unbiased assessment, the evaluation was conducted on a strictly held-out test set that was not used during system development, tuning, or iterative refinement. The test set was constructed based on predefined criteria to ensure diversity and representativeness, including coverage of multiple languages (Amharic, Afaan Oromo, and English), variation in query complexity, and inclusion of common user intents observed in real-world deployment scenarios. This design allows the evaluation results to approximate the system’s true generalization performance and provides a more realistic estimate relative to tuning accuracy. Performance results were further stratified by language and by representative task categories, including AFP symptom-related queries and vaccination information. Although the system is not explicitly designed for task classification, these categories reflect practical usage patterns and enable more granular analysis of system performance across different types of queries.

To complement the expert-based assessment, a preliminary user-centered evaluation was designed as part of the broader Haqila platform deployment. The AFP Assistant is part of Haqila67, a unified AI-based platform designed to support CBS was implemented across eight sites in the Jimma Zone, Ethiopia, involving approximately 32 participants. Using purposive sampling, three sites, Seka, Jimma University General Hospital, and ShGGH, were selected based on proximity to facilitate engagement and feedback collection. Eight representative users were recruited to ensure diversity in gender, professional roles, and language proficiency. Participants interacted with the chatbot within routine workflows and provided structured feedback on usability, clarity, and contextual relevance.

Ethical considerations and data security

Ethical approval for the data collection component of this study was obtained from the Jimma University Institutional Review Board (IRB) (Ref. No. JUIH/IRB/275/25; approved April 12, 2025). All participants involved in the interview process were informed about the purpose of the study, and informed consent was obtained before participation. The questionnaire was designed to assess community awareness and misconceptions regarding polio and did not collect any personally identifiable information or individual health records.

To ensure privacy and secure data management, the system utilizes Supabase as the backend infrastructure, which provides built-in support for authentication, secure data access, and user management. Supabase Auth is used to manage user authentication, with passwords securely hashed and never stored in plain text. Role-based access controls restrict access to user data and system resources to authorized components and administrators only. To further protect user privacy and ensure ethical data handling, the system records user queries in an anonymized manner, capturing only the query content, associated email, and submission date. No personally identifiable information is shared with third parties, and all recorded data is used exclusively for system improvement, aggregated analysis, and public health research purposes. These measures support the responsible handling of user interactions and promote the ethical deployment of AI-based health information systems in community settings.

Experimental settings

This study involved five experiments designed to evaluate the performance of the AFP Assistant and its underlying architecture. The first experiment focused on model selection, where four candidate LLMs (Gemini 2.5 Flash, Gemma 3, LLaMA 3.1, and DeepSeek-R1) were benchmarked using a standardized set of five queries in English, Amharic, and Afaan Oromo. The evaluation examined each model’s ability to automatically detect the query language, generate accurate responses in the same language, and demonstrate semantic correctness in polio-related contexts. The second experiment addressed fine-tuning, where Gemini 2.5 Flash, selected as the base model, was trained on a curated dataset of 468 Q&A pairs (~130,248 tokens), consisting of 268 in English, 100 in Amharic, and 100 in Afaan Oromo. Fine-tuning was conducted on Google Vertex AI using configured hyperparameters (learning rate multiplier of 5, adapter size of 4, and 15 epochs), and performance was tracked through accuracy and loss metrics across training sets to ensure convergence and mitigate overfitting. The third experiment evaluated the RAG pipeline, with performance measured through visualization metrics, including relevance distribution scores, the average number of retrieved documents per query, and the mean query response time. Building on this, the fourth experiment tested RAG-grounded responses by verifying that all retrieved outputs originated exclusively from the curated knowledge base. This evaluation was conducted in Amharic, English, and Afaan Oromo, confirming correct indexing and consistent top-k retrieval with k = 7 in each language. The fifth experiment involved a comparative evaluation of SOTA models ChatGPT, Grok, and DeepSeek against the developed end-to-end functionality of the AFP Assistant through its dedicated user interface, assessing performance within a real-time interactive environment. Using identical polio-related queries, outputs were assessed qualitatively, with particular attention to accuracy, multilingual competence, and factual grounding. Accuracy was evaluated by comparing the system’s responses with verified reference answers, measuring the proportion of correctly identified and contextually relevant responses.
