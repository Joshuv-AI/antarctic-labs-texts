Extremely Low Birth Weight Infant (Gestational Age of 29 Weeks) With Kabuki Syndrome Type I: Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Qi Li, Yuzhu Zheng, Xinyuan Guo, Jiang Xue
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThis paper aimed to investigate the clinical phenotype of Kabuki syndrome (KS) in premature infants.MethodsThis paper presents a case of an extremely low birth weight infant (gestational age 29 weeks) with KS1 caused by a variant in the KMT2D gene. The clinical, pathological, and differential diagnostic findings were comprehensively analyzed. A thorough literature review was also performed to enhance the understanding of KS, revealing its unique features and prognostic significance.ResultsThe infant was a male with a gestational age of 29 weeks and a birth weight of 850 g. He had intrauterine growth retardation, characterized by cleft palate, sacrococcygeal skin depressions, and recurrent metabolic acidosis. Whole‐exome sequencing revealed the c.4267C > T (p.Arg1423Cys) variant in the KMT2D gene, which was absent in his parents. The patient was discharged after 67 days of treatment, and he was followed up to 19 months of corrected gestational age, with growth retardation and expression language delay. Ten previous studies on preterm infants were retrieved, with 10 preterm infants. They all had characteristic facial features, such as long blepharophimosis, sparse and lateral 1/3 eyebrows, and large and prominent/cupped ears. Other manifestations were extrauterine growth delay (7/10), abnormal development of the cardiovascular system (7/10), abnormal development of the nervous system (5/10), and cleft palate (2/10).ConclusionsKabuki syndrome is a rare hereditary disorder involving multiple organs and systems. Genetic assessment for preterm infants with congenital abnormalities is recommended.Keywords: cleft palate, extremely low birth weight infant, Kabuki syndrome, KMT2D gene, premature infants

Introduction

Kabuki syndrome (KS) is a rare hereditary disorder involving multiple organs and systems with five characteristic clinical manifestations (Niikawa et al. 1981), including typical facies (e.g., long palpebral fissure, lower eyelid ectropion, arched eyebrows with sparse lateral 1/3, short nasal columella, flat nasal tip, large prominent/cup‐shaped ears), intellectual disability, postnatal growth retardation, dermal anomaly (e.g., prominent fingertip pads), and mild skeletal abnormalities. Other clinical features (Wang et al. 2019) may include congenital heart disease, genitourinary abnormalities, dental hypoplasia, susceptibility to infections or autoimmune diseases, feeding difficulties, epilepsy, hearing impairment, and cleft lip and palate. The incidence of KS is 1/68,000–1/32,000, affecting all ethnic groups, without gender or race preference (Philip, Meinecke, et al. 1992; Schrander‐Stumpel et al. 1994).

The pathogenic variations of KMT2D (NC_000012.12) and KDM6A (NC_000023.11) genes are the main genetic cause of KS. The KMT2D gene encodes a mammalian histone H3 lysine 4‐methyltransferase widely expressed in embryonic and adult tissues and is essential for early embryonic development. Its pathogenic variation leads to Kabuki syndrome type 1 (KS1, MIM#147920), an inherited autosomal dominant disorder (Froimchuk, Jang, and Ge 2017). The KDM6A gene encodes a histone demethylase that interacts with KMT2D, and its pathogenic variation leads to Kabuki syndrome type 2 (KS2, MIM#300867), which is an X‐linked dominant inherited disorder (Lederer et al. 2012). Pathogenic variants of KMT2D and KDM6A were found in 56%–76% and 5%–8%, respectively, of patients clinically diagnosed with KS (Banka et al. 2015).

The present paper aimed to report an extremely low birth weight infant (gestational age of 29 weeks) with KS1 caused by the variant of KMT2D gene. A literature review was performed on premature infants complicated with KS to timely identify, diagnose, and treat such infants and improve the long‐term outcomes.

Case Presentation

General data. The patient was a male infant with a gestational age of 29 weeks. The mother was gravida 3, para 2 (G3P2). The infant was delivered by cesarean section in 2021 due to preeclampsia and fetal distress. The placenta was calcified in multiple places, and the infarct area of the placenta accounted for about 25%. The amniotic fluid was light yellow with a volume of 300 mL. The infant had a normal umbilical cord. Body length was 36 cm (longer than the 10th percentile of average body length with the same gestational age), head circumference was 24 cm (located in the 3rd percentile of average head circumference with the same gestational age), and birth weight was 850 g (less than the 10th percentile of average body weight with the same gestational age). After birth, the infant had a history of asphyxia resuscitation with an unknown Apgar score, and he was then transferred to the authors' department under tracheal intubation and resuscitative balloon ventilation. Physical examination on admission revealed preterm appearance, poor response, and bluish skin on the extremities but red on the other skin parts and mucous membrane. During intubation, it was found that the soft palate and a part of the hard palate were split (incomplete cleft palate). Breathing sounds were clear with wet rales in both lungs under the resuscitative balloon. Abdominal examination showed no abnormality. Skin depression could be found in the sacrocaudal end without exudate. Hypotonia was identified in the extremities. The father was 39 years old and healthy. The mother was 37 years old and healthy and diagnosed with pregnancy‐induced hypertension syndrome at 26 weeks of gestation. An induced abortion at 1+ month was noted for G1 pregnancy. However, the mother went on to have a successful second pregnancy (G2), resulting in the birth of a healthy girl who was 11 years old.

Diagnosis and treatment. Respiratory support, anti‐infection, intravenous nutrition, and other supportive treatments were performed during hospitalization. Adequate enteral nutrition was carried out at 1 month after birth, and respiratory support was removed at 1.5 months after birth. As the infant was an extremely low birth weight infant with intrauterine growth retardation, cleft palate, sacrococcygeal skin depression, and recurrent metabolic acidosis within 2 weeks after birth, whole‐exome sequencing was conducted with parental consent. The results of whole‐exome sequencing performed at Minlusi Medical Laboratory Academic Center showed the c.4267C > T (p.Arg1423Cys) variant in the KMT2D gene, a suspicious gene that was significantly correlated with clinical phenotype, was a novel variant, indicating KS1 (Figure 1 and Figure S1). After 67 days of hospitalization, the patient was discharged with a corrected gestational age of 38+4 weeks, weighing 2.78 kg. His vital signs were stable, and he tolerated feeding well.

Result: Suspected genetic variants were found to be significantly associated with clinical phenotypes.

Related examinations. Echocardiography showed a patent foramen ovale (< 1 mm). The result of hereditary metabolic disorder detection was not significant. Chromosome karyotype analysis of peripheral blood showed no abnormality. Fundus screening showed immature retinas of the infant (double). Amplitude‐integrated electroencephalogram and cranial magnetic resonance were normal. Brain MRI‐DWI was normal. Brainstem auditory‐evoked response was not passed. Abdominal and urinary ultrasound showed no abnormalities.

Post‐discharge follow‐up. After the hospital discharge, the infant underwent a routine physical examination. When writing those lines, the patient was 22 months (19 months of corrected age) and had no feeding problems. The length is 80 cm, and the weight is 10 kg. He was treated with growth hormone for half a year for short stature. No more details about the hormone treatment are available. The patient has normal hearing and can perform actions on command. However, the patient displays language development delays.

Literature Review

A comprehensive literature search was conducted of studies published between January 1, 2000, and January 1, 2023. The following databases were queried: PubMed, Web of Science, and EMBASE. The following keywords were used in combination with Boolean operators AND or OR for the literature search: “premature” and “Kabuki syndrome” or “Kabuki makeup syndrome” or “Niikawa‐Kuroki syndrome”. The inclusion criteria for qualifying studies were (I) studies that reported premature infants diagnosed with Kabuki syndrome and (II) studies that reported the infant's gestational age, sex, clinical characteristics, and other indicators. Ten premature infants with KS were included. The minimum gestational age was 31 weeks, the maximum gestational age was 36 weeks, the shortest diagnosis time was 2 months, and the longest diagnosis time was 19 months. Only infant #1 had intrauterine growth retardation. All infants had the Kabuki syndrome characteristic facial features, such as long palpebral fissures, arched eyebrows with sparse lateral 1/3, and large prominent/cup‐shaped ears. Infants #4 and #6 explicitly had cleft palate. Prominent fingertip pads were common in these infants. Two infants had sacrococcygeal skin depression, and three infants had skeletal developmental abnormalities of the hands. Five infants had neurodevelopmental abnormalities. Six infants had hypotonia. Seven infants had cardiovascular developmental abnormalities. Two infants had urinary abnormalities. Two infants were born with hyperinsulinemia. Two out of four male preterm infants had cryptorchidism. Seven infants presented with extrauterine growth retardation. Infant #4 died 8 days after birth due to multiple deformities.

Discussion and Conclusions

Notably, KS is a congenital mental abnormality/retardation syndrome. In December 2018, Adam and 10 other experts from the United States, Japan, Europe, and the United Kingdom reported an international expert consensus on the diagnostic criteria for KS (Adam, Banka et al. 2019), which emphasized that patients who met the probable or suspicious diagnostic criteria should be diagnosed with clinical gene detection as soon as possible (Table S1). Facial features could be easily recognized between the ages of 3 and 12 (Adam, Banka et al. 2019). Therefore, it is a great challenge to diagnose KS in newborns and infants.

This infant was born prematurely at 29 weeks gestation, weighing 850 g. The infant had no family history or exhibited typical clinical symptoms. Due to cleft palate, impaired intrauterine growth retardation, sacrococcygeal skin depression, and repeated metabolic acidosis in the early stages, whole‐exome sequencing was conducted 3 weeks postpartum to confirm the diagnosis, revealing the c.4267C > T (p.Arg1423Cys) variant in the KMT2D gene. The c.4267C > T (p.Arg1423Cys) variant has been reported and is presently considered a variant of unknown significance (VUS) (Miyake et al. 2013; Bogershausen, Gatinois, et al. 2016), but it was predicted as likely pathogenic for KS1 (Bogershausen, Gatinois, et al. 2016). The c.4267C > A (p.Arg1423Ser) variant has also been reported and is considered likely pathogenic (Miyake et al. 2013; Nykamp et al. 2017; Margot et al. 2020). KS has no phenotype related to metabolic acidosis. It was considered that repeated metabolic acidosis in this case was attributed to the underdeveloped renal function in a premature infant (especially in extremely low birth weight infants), leading to a poor ability to regulate the acid–base balance and being prone to metabolic acidosis. Secondly, the intake of a large amount of amino acids in early intravenous nutrition increases the amount of non‐volatile acidic products in the body, exceeding the excretion capacity of the kidney. Therefore, the infant no longer suffered from metabolic acidosis with the reduction of parenteral nutrition to adequate enteral nutrition.

Only limited information about the prenatal and perinatal history of KS patients is available. It was reported that a high proportion of excessive amniotic fluid was found in a prenatal examination of KS, which could be secondary to swallowing dysfunction caused by craniofacial structure and/or functional abnormalities (Rosenberg et al. 2020). The gestational age of this infant was 29 weeks, and the birth weight was less than the 10th percentile of the average body weight with the same gestational age. The placenta had calcification and infarction, but the amniotic fluid volume was normal. It was considered that intrauterine growth and development restriction and placental changes were likely to be related to the maternal complications of pregnancy‐induced hypertension.

It has been confirmed in vivo that the knockout of the KMT2D gene could directly affect the differentiation of osteochondral progenitor cells from neuroembryonic cells, leading to cleft palate, mandibular dysplasia, skull base ossification defects, and facial hypoplasia with a reduced frontonasal bone length (Froimchuk, Jang, and Ge 2017; Shpargel et al. 2020). It was reported that the incidence of cleft palate was between 33%–50% (Schrander‐Stumpel et al. 2005). The case presented here and those reviewed in the literature showed that 3/11 infants had a cleft palate, which is in general agreement with Schrander‐Stumpell et al. Moreover, the literature review showed that all 10 preterm infants had characteristic facial features, such as long blepharophimosis, sparse and lateral 1/3 of the eyebrows, and large and protruding/cupped ears. However, in this case, none of the characteristic facial features were recorded by the author, suggesting that greater attention is needed to avoid potential oversight. Notably, the most appropriate time for the operation of the cleft palate is generally 12–18 months to ensure language development. This infant's corrected gestational age is 19 months, and he should be referred to the stomatology department as soon as possible.

Targeted deletion of the KMT2D gene in cardiac precursor cells and myocardial tissue may lead to serious heart defects and embryonic death (Lee et al. 2013; Ang et al. 2016). Clinical reports have shown that congenital heart defects occur in up to 80% of KS patients, with a ventricular septal defect being the most common, followed by aortic and mitral valve constriction (Digilio et al. 2017), which is also consistent with the findings of the present study. Furthermore, clinical phenotypes may be constrained by ascertainment bias and coincidental anomalies not associated with KS.

Sacrococcygeal skin depression is one of the characteristic skin manifestations of tethered cord syndrome. There are no reports of KS complicated with tethered cord syndrome worldwide. There were also two cases of sacrococcygeal skin depression in the retrospective search. Still, no relationship of sacrococcygeal skin depression with KS was further explored in the present study.

Growth retardation, hypotonia, seizures, and intellectual disability are also frequently found in KS patients, of which 10%–39% of KS patients were identified to suffer from seizures (Liu et al. 2015). In addition, some patients also suffer from sleep disorders (Rapp et al. 2022). In the present literature review, more than half of the premature infants showed growth retardation, hypotonia, and abnormal neurodevelopment, but long‐term follow‐up data are lacking.

Growth hormone (GH) deficiency and diabetes are common endocrine complications in KS patients (van Montfort et al. 2021). It is noteworthy that about 1% of newborns with hyperinsulinemia were diagnosed with KS (Yap et al. 2019). In the present study, one premature infant was diagnosed with hyperinsulinemia. However, no physical examination and growth hormone analysis were carried out in the present study, which suggested that GH deficiency should be regarded in the follow‐up of premature KS patients.

Congenital abnormalities of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT) are relatively common in patients with KS, such as horseshoe kidney, pelvic kidney, hydronephrosis, renal insufficiency, and hypospadias, among others. The reported incidence ranges from 20% to 60% (Ming et al. 2003). However, there was only one case of kidney abnormality (pelvic kidney) with a low incidence in the present study. Whether CAKUT can be relevant to a specific population of premature infants remains elusive.

Patients with KS often have immune dysfunction, and about 60% of patients are more susceptible to infections, especially pneumonia and upper respiratory tract infections (Margot et al. 2020). The lack of immunoglobulin, reduced memory cells, and poor vaccine response make severe infections inevitable in patients with KS (Ming et al. 2005). In addition, the KMT2D gene may affect the differentiation of B cells, further leading to humoral immune deficiency and autoimmune diseases, such as hemolytic anemia, idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura, or thyroiditis (Lindsley et al. 2016). These preterm infants were not found susceptible to infection and autoimmune diseases, indicating the necessity of long‐term monitoring. In general, the management of KS focuses on providing support and treating the clinical symptoms. The signs of KS are often not easily recognizable at birth, even in premature infants, aside from any structural abnormalities. Nevertheless, early genetic testing can help rule out other genetic issues requiring immediate intervention, such as metabolic management. On the other hand, a genetic diagnosis can aid in the ongoing monitoring of KS cases and assist in future reproductive planning for cases that have an inherited component.

In conclusion, KS is a genetic disorder that leads to developmental delays and congenital malformations with specific facial features. In developing the disease, the emphasis on multidisciplinary diagnosis and treatment may improve patients' long‐term quality of life. Typical clinical manifestations are not easily recognized in the neonatal period, and data on preterm infants are even rarer. The present case report and literature review suggested that early whole‐exome sequencing may assist in identifying rare diseases and expanding phenotypes for premature infants with developmental delays, malformations, or special features, thereby helping the medical team systematically diagnose and treat infants and provide corresponding guidance to their families. Meanwhile, this study also reminds clinicians to improve and collect comprehensive data on KS cases to provide ideas and evidence for further clinical studies on KS patients.
