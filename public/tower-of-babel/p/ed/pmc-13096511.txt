Cyclophilin A stabilizes the capsid protein p72 to facilitate African swine fever virus replication
Authors: Hui Kong, Liqin Yang, Zhenjiang Zhang, Ying Zhang, Linlin Liu, Wan Wang, Linfei Song, Yuanmao Zhu, Encheng Sun, Fang Li, Zhigao Bu, Xiangpeng Sheng, Dongming Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
African swine fever virus (ASFV) is a large DNA virus that poses a major threat to the global swine industry. Its virion is encapsulated by an icosahedral capsid predominantly composed of the structural protein p72, which constitutes approximately one-third of the total virion mass. Despite its abundance, the mechanisms regulating p72 stability remain poorly understood. Here, we demonstrate that host-mediated stabilization of p72 is essential for efficient ASFV replication. Mass spectrometry of p72 co-precipitates identified host cyclophilin A (CypA, also known as PPIA) as a key binding partner of p72. CypA interacts with p72 both in vitro and in vivo, specifically engaging exposed regions of p72 via its hydrophobic cavity. CypA interaction stabilizes p72 by reducing its ubiquitination and preventing proteasomal degradation, whereas cyclic CypA inhibitors destabilize p72 by disrupting this interaction and promoting its ubiquitination. Importantly, genetic or pharmacological inhibition of CypA markedly impairs ASFV replication. Mechanistically, CypA inhibition disrupts viral factory formation and virion assembly by decreasing p72 protein accumulation without affecting its transcription. Together, our findings uncover a host-dependent mechanism regulating capsid protein stability and highlight host CypA as a promising target for antiviral strategies against ASFV.

Introduction

African swine fever (ASF) is currently the most devastating infectious disease affecting swine, causing substantial economic losses worldwide1,2. It is characterized by high morbidity and mortality rates (reaching up to 100%) in both domestic and wild pigs, and is designated as a notifiable disease by the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH)3. First reported in Kenya in 1921, ASF has since spread globally over the past century, affecting numerous countries and regions4. Despite its severe impact, there are currently no safe and effective vaccines or antiviral therapeutics commercially available for its prevention or treatment, posing significant challenges to ASF control.

ASF virus (ASFV), the causative agent of ASF, is a large double-stranded DNA virus with a complex icosahedral architecture5. ASFV particles, approximately 200 nm in diameter, consist of multilayered structures, including a genome-containing nucleoid, core shell, inner envelope, icosahedral capsid, and an external lipid envelope6. The most abundant component of the ASFV virion is the major capsid protein p72, which constitutes about 33% of the total mass of the virion7. p72 has been well-demonstrated to play a critical role in capsid formation and viral assembly8–11, suggesting that viral or cellular factors regulating p72 may affect ASFV infection. Previous studies have shown that the ASFV-encoded protein pB602L functions as a chaperone facilitating p72 folding12,13. Repression of pB602L expression results in the decreased p72 protein levels and the formation of aberrant zipper-like structures instead of icosahedral ASFV particles14. In contrast, p72 degradation is essential during the early phase of ASFV replication, driving capsid disassembly and enabling the release of core particles containing the viral genome into the cytoplasm following fusion of the viral inner envelope with host endosomal membranes, thereby facilitating viral DNA delivery into host cells15,16. These findings suggest that p72 must be precisely and temporally regulated throughout the ASFV life cycle, likely through host factors that stabilize p72 during later stages of infection. Despite its essential role in virion assembly, the mechanisms governing p72 stability remain largely unknown.

CypA is abundantly expressed and possesses chaperone-like and prolyl isomerase activities that modulate protein folding and function, thereby influencing diverse biological processes, including MAPK signaling, RIG-I pathway, and cytokine production17–19. It has also been implicated in pathogen infections and a wide range of disease processes18. Whether CypA plays a role in ASFV infection remains unclear.

In this work, we identify host CypA as a critical regulator of p72 stability and ASFV infection. Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) combined with mass spectrometry reveals a specific interaction between CypA and p72, which is further characterized by AlphaFold3 structural modeling, mutagenesis, and binding assays. CypA interaction protects p72 from K63-linked ubiquitination-mediated degradation, whereas inhibition or depletion of CypA accelerates p72 turnover, impairing viral replication. Furthermore, CypA inhibition disrupts viral factory formation and impedes virion assembly by reducing p72 protein accumulation during ASFV infection. These findings uncover a host-dependent mechanism controlling p72 stability and suggest that targeting the CypA-p72 interaction could represent a potential antiviral strategy.

Results

Host CypA is a potential binding partner of the ASFV p72 protein

As the major component of the ASFV capsid, the viral p72 protein—encoded by the B646L gene—plays a critical role in ASFV virion assembly8,11. It is well-established that B646L transcription initiates at 5 ~ 6 h post-infection (hpi) and is progressively upregulated during the late stages of ASFV infection, displaying a distinct expression pattern compared to the early genes CP312R and CP204L (encoding p30)20. In this study, porcine alveolar macrophages (PAMs) were infected with the genotype II ASFV HLJ/18 strain (referred to as ASFV unless otherwise noted), and samples were harvested at various time points post-infection for Western blot analysis to examine the expression kinetics of these viral proteins. Consistent with the transcriptional data20, p72 protein levels markedly increased at the later stages, in contrast to the early expression of CP312R and p30 (Fig. 1A). Given that protein abundance is controlled by multiple interconnected processes, including mRNA transcription, post-translational modifications, and protein degradation21, it is plausible that the accumulation of p72 at the late-stage of ASFV replication is also regulated by certain host factors after its synthesis. To explore potential mechanisms regulating p72 stability, we first sought to identify host factors that interact with p72. ASFV-infected PAMs were subjected to a co-IP with an anti-p72 antibody. As expected, viral p72 was specifically enriched by the anti-p72 antibody but not by the control IgG (Fig. 1B). The resulting co-precipitates were then analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), which identified a porcine cyclophilin-derived peptide, “FDDENFILK” (Fig. 1C). This peptide showed strong signal intensity in the p72-enriched sample but was absent in the IgG control (Fig. S1A). Sequence alignment of porcine cyclophilin family members revealed that both CypA (PPIA) and PPIE contain this peptide (Fig. 1D), suggesting p72 may associate with either or both proteins. Because cyclophilin family members share high sequence similarity22, we conducted GST pulldown assays using recombinant GST-tagged porcine cyclophilins and lysates from HEK293T cells expressing p72 to preliminarily assess their interactions. While most cyclophilins showed weak p72 binding, CypA and PPIE exhibited the strongest and comparable ability to recruit p72 (Fig. S1B). To determine the predominant binding partner, we examined the subcellular distributions of p72, CypA, and PPIE. Whereas p72 was mainly cytoplasmic and PPIE was strictly nuclear, CypA was present in both cytoplasm and nucleus (Fig. 1E), indicating that CypA, but not PPIE, has the spatial basis to interact with p72 in the cytoplasm. Together, these results suggest that host CypA may serve as a major binding partner of p72.

CypA is highly conserved across humans and animal species, including Ornithodoros, exhibiting approximately 100% identity between Sus scrofa and Phacochoerus africanus (warthog) (Fig. 1F). Emerging evidence suggests that CypA modulates the stability of different viral proteins, such as the HIV capsid23, the poxviruses C6 protein24, and the SARS-CoV-2 spike25. Therefore, it is reasonable to speculate that host CypA may likewise regulate p72 stability, warranting further investigation.

CypA interacts with the viral p72 protein both in vitro and in vivo

To confirm the interaction between CypA and p72, we performed a series of binding experiments. Recombinant Strep-tagged trimeric p72 protein (referred to as recombinant Strep-p72) (Fig. S2) purified from yeast was subjected to Strep pulldown assays with GST or GST-CypA obtained from E. coli. We found that GST-CypA, but not GST alone, was efficiently recruited by Strep-Tactin resin with the recombinant Strep-p72 (Fig. 2A). In contrast, in the absence of recombinant Strep-p72, Strep-Tactin resin did not capture GST-CypA (Fig. 2B), which indicates that it is p72 that directly interacts with CypA in vitro. Moreover, co-IP in HEK293T cells co-expressing Strep-p72 and CypA-HA revealed that p72 was specifically detected in the CypA-enriched precipitates (Fig. 2C), confirming the formation of CypA-p72 complex in cells. Since intracellular co-localization is often a prerequisite for protein-protein interactions, we further examined subcellular co-localization of CypA and p72. Consistent with Fig. 1E, in plasmid-transfected HEK293T cells, Strep-p72 predominantly localized in the cytoplasm, whereas CypA-HA was present in both the cytoplasmic and nuclear regions (Fig. 2D). Importantly, co-expression of Strep-p72 and CypA-HA in HEK293T cells revealed strong cytoplasmic co-localization of these two proteins (Fig. 2D, E). We further assessed the dynamics of this co-localization in PAMs during the course of ASFV infection. At 6 hpi, only a small fraction of PAMs expressed low levels of p72, which localized to a restricted cytoplasmic region without detectable co-localization with CypA (Fig. 2F). However, as the infection progressed, p72 accumulated and showed pronounced cytoplasmic co-localization with endogenous CypA, particularly within viral factories (Fig. 2F). This co-localization was initially observed at 12 hpi, persisting to 24 hpi. A weaker p72 signal in factories was observed in a small fraction (~10%) of infected cells at late stages, potentially reflecting extensive p72 accumulation and redistribution. Importantly, the production of infectious progeny virions was initiated at 12 hpi and increased over time (Fig. 2G), coincided temporally with CypA-p72 co-localization, suggesting a potential role of CypA in promoting efficient virion assembly. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that host CypA co-localizes with ASFV p72 in host cells, likely contributing to viral replication.

CypA binds to the exposed regions (ERs) of p72 through its hydrophobic cavity

Prompted by the above interaction data, we sought to explore the binding mode between CypA and p72. While the structure of human CypA has been extensively characterized26,27, the porcine CypA structure remains unresolved. Using the AlphaFold3 server, we predicted the structure of porcine CypA, which displayed a high similarity to its human counterpart (Fig. 3A), consistent with their high sequence identity (Fig. 1F). Given the demonstrated accuracy of AlphaFold3 in modeling biomolecular interactions28, we employed it to predict the CypA-p72 binding complex. Considering that p72 exists as a large trimer (~219.5 kDa) and CypA is a relatively small protein (~17.8 kDa), we hypothesized that the p72 trimer may bind CypA at a molar ratio of 1:3. The AlphaFold3-predicted model of a p72 trimer complexed with three CypA molecules exhibited high confidence (pTM = 0.74), with each CypA molecule binding to p72 in an identical fashion (Fig. 3B). Further detailed inspection of the binding interface using PyMOL29 revealed that CypA likely interacts with the apical exposed regions (ERs) of p72 via its 33 amino acid residues (spanning positions 54–153) (Fig. 3C, D). To experimentally verify this prediction, we generated a series of CypA truncation mutants with sequential deletions of 20, 40, 60, or 80 amino acids from either the N- or C-terminus (Fig. 3E). These mutants were expressed in E. coli, purified, and subjected to in vitro binding assays with the recombinant Strep-tagged p72 trimers. Deletion of 20 or 40 residues from either terminus had no significant effect on the binding between CypA and p72. However, removal of 60 or 80 residues from either end nearly abolished the interaction (Fig. 3F). As shown in Fig. 3E, deletions of 60 or more residues from either terminus disrupted key elements within the hydrophobic cavity (spanning residues 54–126), thereby impairing its structural integrity. To directly examine the role of the hydrophobic cavity in p72 binding, we generated a CypA(8A) mutant in which all key residues within the cavity were substituted with alanine (Fig. 3G). This mutant showed a marked loss of p72-binding capacity (Fig. 3H). These results support our structural prediction, confirming that the hydrophobic cavity of CypA serves as the primary binding region for p72.

We next sought to validate the corresponding CypA-binding site on p72. Previous structural studies have shown that p72 monomer adopts a double jelly-roll fold with four exposed regions (ERs) or insertions8,9,11. In our earlier study, we generated five porcine monoclonal antibodies (B1, F11, C9, H3, and G6) targeting p72 and determined their complex structures with p7213. Comparative analysis revealed that the binding epitopes of four of these antibodies (B1, F11, C9, and H3) significantly overlapped with the predicted CypA-binding region, all of which are located in the ERs of p72; Notably, each p72 trimer is capable of simultaneously binding three antibody molecules (Fig. 3I). In vitro binding assays confirmed that these four p72 antibodies effectively competed with CypA for p72 binding, supporting the idea that p72 recruits CypA via its ERs (Fig. 3J, K). In contrast, antibody G6, which binds the central apical region of the p72 trimer at a 1:1 ratio, failed to compete with CypA for p72 binding, similar to the negative control anti-p30 antibody (Fig. 3J, K). Given that the four competing antibodies (B1, F11, C9, and H3) all targeted ER1 on the outer apex of p72, while the non-competing G6 did not bind ER1 (Fig. 3I), we propose that the critical amino acid residues mediating p72-CypA interaction are primarily located within ER1. Collectively, our findings indicate that the CypA-p72 interaction is mediated through the hydrophobic cavity of CypA and the ER1 region of p72, laying a foundation for further elucidating how CypA may modulate p72 stability.

CypA stabilizes viral p72 by reducing its K63-linked ubiquitination

ASFV p72 has been reported to undergo degradation in host cells15,30. To elucidate the major pathway responsible for p72 degradation, we treated p72-expressing cells with different inhibitors. While the lysosomal inhibitor bafilomycin A1 (BafA1) induced LC3-II accumulation, it had minimal impact on p72 protein levels (Fig. 4A). In contrast, treatment with MG132, a classical proteasomal inhibitor, not only enhanced Ub conjugation but also markedly increased p72 accumulation (Fig. 4A), indicating that p72 degradation is predominantly mediated by the proteasome. Furthermore, the stability of p72 was also significantly promoted by TAK-243 (MLN7243) (Fig. 4A), a first-in-class UBE1 inhibitor31 that depletes cellular polyubiquitin chains. Notably, the ASFV pB602L protein is essential for the correct folding and trimer assembly of p72, and the expression of p72 alone leads to the formation of misfolded p72 aggregates11,12,14. To further investigate the regulation of pB602L on p72 stability, we co-expressed p72 and pB602L in cells and conducted inhibitor treatment assays. Consistent with the results above, we observed similar effects of the inhibitors on p72 stability when the experiments were performed in the presence of pB602L (Fig. 4A). These findings indicate that the ubiquitin-proteasome system serves as the primary pathway for p72 degradation, irrespective of the presence or absence of pB602L.

It is noteworthy that co-expression of pB602L upregulated p72 protein levels (Fig. 4B), in agreement with a previous study14. Given that CypA has been reported to regulate the stability of several viral proteins24,25,32, we examined its potential role in modulating p72 levels. Similar to pB602L, CypA expression increased p72 protein levels (Fig. 4B and Fig. S3A). To ensure physiological relevance, p72 was co-expressed with pB602L in all subsequent experiments, unless otherwise specified. Notably, CypA exhibited a dose-dependent effect in enhancing p72 accumulation in cells (Fig. 4C), highlighting its critical role in regulating p72 expression or protein stability. However, CypA co-expression did not change the mRNA level of p72 (Fig. 4D), which indicates that CypA probably regulates the protein folding or stability of p72. Notably, while pB602L promoted p72 trimer formation regardless of CypA expression, CypA alone failed to induce trimerization (Fig. S3B), indicating that CypA does not exert a chaperone-like function in p72 folding. Deletion of endogenous CypA in cells resulted in a decreased p72 protein level, whereas re-expression of CypA restored p72 accumulation (Fig. 4E), further supporting its regulatory role in maintaining p72 stability. To assess p72 degradation rate, we used cycloheximide (CHX) to block protein synthesis and found that CypA expression suppressed p72 degradation (Fig. 4F). These results suggest that CypA enhances p72 protein stability in cells.

Since p72 was reported to undergo ubiquitination-mediated degradation30, we investigated whether CypA affects p72 ubiquitination. To this end, p72 was enriched from cells expressing p72 alone or co-expressing p72 and CypA under denaturing conditions, and its ubiquitination levels were analyzed. While strong ubiquitination of p72 was detected in the absence of CypA, co-expression of CypA markedly reduced its ubiquitination (Fig. 4G, H and Fig. S3C). To further characterize the ubiquitin (Ub) linkage type, cells were co-transfected with p72 and HA-tagged wild-type Ub or a series of Ub mutants, each retaining a single lysine residue at the indicated position. This analysis revealed that p72 is predominantly modified by K63-linked polyubiquitin chains in cells (Fig. 4I, J). Notably, overexpression of CypA resulted in a pronounced reduction of K63-linked polyubiquitination on p72 (Fig. 4K). Although K63-linked ubiquitination is typically associated with signal transduction33,34, several studies have demonstrated that it can also target substrates for degradation35–37, and a recent study specifically implicated K63-linked ubiquitination in promoting p72 degradation30. Together, these findings suggest that CypA enhances protein stability of p72 by suppressing its K63-linked ubiquitination.

CypA inhibitors destabilize p72 by disrupting CypA binding and enhancing p72 ubiquitination

Cyclosporine A (CsA), a cyclic undecapeptide with the highest known binding affinity for CypA, is a well-characterized CypA inhibitor17,38. CsA is an immunosuppressive agent derived from fungi, whereas its non-immunosuppressive analogs, such as NIM811 and Alisporivir (Debio-025), have been developed as therapeutic alternatives39. Structural studies have resolved the complexes formed between CypA and these cyclic undecapeptide inhibitors40–42, revealing that their binding sites overlap with the p72-binding surface of CypA (Fig. 5A). This structural overlap suggests that CsA and its analogs may disrupt the interaction between CypA and p72. To test this, we performed an in vitro binding assay using the recombinant Strep-p72 and GST-CypA proteins in the presence or absence of CypA inhibitors. As shown in Fig. 5B, the addition of CsA to the in vitro binding system effectively blocked the binding of CypA to p72. Similarly, NIM811 and Alisporivir also inhibited CypA-p72 binding (Fig. 5B), confirming that these inhibitors can competitively interfere with this interaction. Given that CypA enhances p72 stability, we hypothesized that inhibitors interfering with this interaction could destabilize p72. To investigate this, p72-expressing cells were treated with CypA inhibitors for 24 h and p72 protein levels were analyzed by Western blot. CsA treatment induced a dose-dependent reduction in p72 protein levels (Fig. 5C, E), with noticeable effects starting from 5 μM and a maximal reduction at 20 μM. NIM811 similarly decreased p72 levels in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5C, D). A CHX chase assay further demonstrated that NIM811 accelerated p72 degradation, shortening the time for the protein intensity to reach 50% of its initial value from 6 h to 2.95 h (Fig. 5F, G). Moreover, analysis of p72 ubiquitination in the cells treated with CypA inhibitors revealed that CsA or NIM811 exhibited a robust ability to increase the ubiquitination of p72 (Fig. 5H, I and Fig. S4), in contrast to the reduction induced by CypA overexpression (Fig. 4K). Together, these findings establish that the cyclic peptide inhibitors of CypA promote p72 degradation by enhancing its ubiquitination.

Inhibition of CypA impairs ASFV infection

The data described above suggest that CypA may play a critical role in ASFV infection. To investigate this, we performed viral infection assays using BK2258 cells, a wild boar-derived cell line known to support ASFV replication45. BK2258 cells were engineered to stably express either a control sgRNA (Ctrl-sg) or CypA-targeting sgRNAs (CypA-sg) via the CRISPR/Cas9 system. Western blot analysis confirmed that CypA protein expression was efficiently ablated in CypA-sg cells (Fig. S5A). Upon ASFV infection, CypA-sg cells exhibited a marked reduction of viral p30 expression compared to control cells (Fig. S5A). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) further revealed that loss of CypA significantly impaired the production of extracellular ASFV virions in the supernatants (Fig. S5B). To validate these findings in a primary cell context, PAMs were transfected with either CypA-targeting siRNAs (siCypA-1/2/3) or a negative control siRNA (NC) prior to ASFV infection. Since cyclic peptide inhibitors of CypA destabilize p72, we next examined whether these inhibitors could impact ASFV infection. PAMs were infected with ASFV in the presence of CypA inhibitors, including CsA, NIM811, and Alisporivir. All three inhibitors significantly decreased virions production in the supernatants of infected cells (Fig. 6C). Western blot analysis revealed that treatment with these compounds decreased the intracellular accumulation of viral proteins (Fig. 6D), and immunofluorescence staining showed a marked decrease in the number of p72-positive cells in the inhibitor-treated groups (Fig. 6E). To further quantify the infection ratio, we employed an artificially attenuated ASFV strain, HLJ/18-7GD, which expresses the fluorescent markers EGFP and mCherry46. CsA and NIM811 caused reduction in fluorescent signals in the infected PAMs (Fig. 6F), in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. S5C, D). Flow cytometry analysis corroborated these findings, showing that treatment with CypA inhibitors decreased the proportion of the infected cells by approximately tenfold or more (Fig. 6G, H and Fig. S5E). Consistently, Western blot analysis revealed a dose-dependent suppression of p72 and p30 expression in the infected PAMs by CypA inhibitors (Fig. 6I, J and Fig. S5F), and qPCR quantification of extracellular virions in the cell supernatants confirmed a dose-dependent inhibitory effect of CypA inhibitors on ASFV infection (Fig. 6K, L and Fig. S5G). Notably, these CypA inhibitors had minimal effects on cell viability at the indicated concentrations (Fig. S6A, B). Collectively, these results demonstrate that CypA inhibition, either through genetic decrease or pharmacological intervention, effectively suppresses ASFV infection.

We next assessed the expression of viral proteins p72 and p30 during ASFV infection. Treatment with CsA or NIM811 markedly inhibited the time-dependent accumulation of both viral proteins (Fig. S7A, B), suggesting that CypA inhibition may finally interfere with the expression of viral genes. To investigate the effect of CypA inhibitors on viral gene transcription further, RNA-seq analysis was performed on NIM811-treated or untreated PAMs, with ASFV infection at 72 hpi (Fig. S7C, D). In line with previous studies20,47, more than 160 viral genes were highly expressed in ASFV-infected cells (Fig. 7E). However, NIM811 treatment obviously reduced the levels of the viral gene transcription (Fig. 7E and Fig. S7E, Supplementary Data 1). qRT-PCR analysis of CP204L (p30) and B646L (p72) mRNA levels further confirmed that although viral gene transcription was elevated at 48 hpi and much higher at 72 hpi, this transcriptional activity was significantly suppressed by CypA inhibitors (Fig. 7F, G). These results demonstrate that CypA inhibitors can impair the transcription of viral genes during multi-cycle replication.

ASFV infection is known to induce hemorrhagic fever, accompanied by exacerbated inflammatory responses in multiple organs, ultimately causing pig mortality48. To determine whether CypA inhibitors can mitigate ASFV-induced host inflammation, we conducted host transcriptomic analyses of the infected PAMs. ASFV infection upregulated numerous host genes (Fig. S8A, Supplementary Data 2), many of which are associated with inflammatory pathways, including cytokine receptor signaling and the PI3K signaling pathway (Fig. S8B, Supplementary Data 3). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed significant activation of inflammation-related processes and notable alterations in plasma membrane components following ASFV infection (Fig. S8C, Supplementary Data 4). Notably, volcano plot analysis showed that NIM811 treatment effectively suppressed the ASFV-induced upregulation of host gene expression (Fig. S8A), a finding further supported by heatmap analysis (Fig. S8D, Supplementary Data 5). Collectively, these data suggest that CypA inhibition not only disrupts ASFV replication but also attenuates virus-induced inflammatory responses, highlighting the therapeutic potential of cyclic peptide inhibitors of CypA as antiviral agents against ASFV replication.

To define the specific stages controlled by CypA, we conducted single-cycle ASFV replication assays in PAMs and collected samples over time to monitor viral DNA replication, as well as the mRNA and protein levels of B646L (p72). As shown, viral DNA replication in a single-cycle infection was not significantly affected by CsA or NIM811 (Fig. S9A). Transcription of B646L began to increase significantly at 8 hpi, peaked at 12 hpi, and remained at high levels thereafter, with no significant differences between inhibitor-treated and control groups (Fig. 8C). In contrast, Western blotting revealed that p72 protein was markedly reduced upon treatment with CsA or NIM811, whereas in control group, p72 became detectable at 8 hpi and accumulated progressively over time (Fig. 8D, E and Fig. S9B). Notably, the levels of another viral protein, p30, were unaffected by CypA inhibition (Fig. 8D, E). These results demonstrate that CypA primarily regulates p72 protein levels, rather than broadly influencing viral genome replication or early viral gene expression.

Given that certain viral proteins, including p72, help retain structural proteins within the assembly area and thus contribute to ASFV virus factory organization14,51, we next asked whether CypA inhibition disrupts this process. BK2258 cells were subjected to immunofluorescence staining for p72 and p54, the latter serving as a marker for viral factory localization52. Treatment with CypA inhibitors markedly reduced p72 signals and disrupted the formation of p54-positive puncta (Fig. 8F). Confocal microscopy further revealed that large cytoplasmic viral factories co-stained with p54 and DAPI were present in DMSO-treated cells, whereas inhibitor-treated cells exhibited substantially smaller factories (Fig. 8G, H), indicating compromised viral factory formation and impaired virion assembly. To directly assess virion assembly, we examined ASFV-infected PAMs treated with DMSO or CypA inhibitors by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Numerous mature icosahedral virions were readily observed within viral factories in DMSO-treated control cells (Fig. 8I, J). In contrast, CypA inhibitor-treated cells contained markedly fewer mature virions and exhibited obvious aberrant tubular structures in viral factories (Fig. 8I), partially resembling previously reported zipper-like assemblies induced by disruption of p72 or pB602L14,53. Consistently, CypA deficiency also promoted the formation of aberrant structures in viral factories of ASFV-infected cells (Fig. S9C). Together, these findings demonstrate that CypA inhibition disrupts ASFV viral factory organization and virion assembly by destabilizing p72 protein, thereby impairing ASFV infection.

Discussion

The capsid protein p72 is the most abundant component of the ASFV particle and plays a pivotal role in virion assembly. However, the mechanisms governing its stability in host cells remained largely elusive. In this study, we identify a regulatory pathway by which the host protein CypA enhances the stability of p72 and promotes ASFV infection (Fig. 9). We demonstrate that host CypA is a bona fide binding partner of p72, both in vitro and in vivo, exhibiting specific and direct interaction. Functional assays further reveal that overexpression of CypA enhances p72 protein stability, whereas silencing of CypA or disruption of the CypA-p72 interaction accelerates p72 degradation. Importantly, inhibition of CypA leads to a significant suppression of ASFV replication. Mechanistically, we show that CypA inhibition disrupts viral factory formation and impairs virion assembly, primarily by reducing the accumulation of p72 protein. Therefore, our work highlights the CypA-p72 axis as a host-virus interaction essential for p72 stabilization and ASFV replication.Fig. 9Proposed model of CypA-mediated regulation of ASFV p72 stability and viral replication.Schematic model summarizes how host CypA regulates the stability and function of the ASFV capsid protein p72. CypA binds to the ER1 region of p72 via its hydrophobic cavity, protecting p72 from ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation. This interaction promotes the accumulation of p72, facilitates efficient virion assembly, and enhances ASFV replication. Proposed model of CypA-mediated regulation of ASFV p72 stability and viral replication.

Schematic model summarizes how host CypA regulates the stability and function of the ASFV capsid protein p72. Newly synthesized p72 is normally subject to ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal turnover in host cells. CypA binds to the ER1 region of p72 via its hydrophobic cavity, protecting p72 from ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation. This interaction promotes the accumulation of p72, facilitates efficient virion assembly, and enhances ASFV replication. In contrast, inhibition of CypA disrupts CypA-p72 binding, resulting in increased p72 ubiquitination and degradation, thereby impairing viral factory formation and virion assembly.

Consistent with prior studies15,30, our findings reveal that p72 undergoes ubiquitination-mediated degradation in host cells. One previous study demonstrated that host protein OAS1 recruits the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM21 to promote p72 ubiquitination and proteasome-dependent degradation30. More recently, another E3 ligase, Stub1, was shown to mediate p72 ubiquitination, leading to its degradation via selective autophagy15. In our experiments, inhibition of cellular ubiquitination with TAK-243 led to a marked accumulation of p72 (Fig. 4A), suggesting that p72 is highly unstable in mammalian cells and is likely targeted by multiple E3 ligases. Furthermore, our findings show that CypA stabilizes p72 by reducing its ubiquitination (Fig. 4), whereas inhibitors blocking the CypA-p72 interaction increase p72 ubiquitination (Fig. 5). These results suggest that CypA binding to p72 may prevent its association with one or more E3 ligases. The specific E3 ligase(s) primarily responsible for p72 ubiquitination and counteracted by CypA remains unidentified and warrants further investigation.

The ASFV-encoded nonstructural protein pB602L (CAP80) functions as a molecular chaperone for p72, ensuring its proper folding and facilitating icosahedral capsid assembly12,13. Repression of pB602L expression results in reduced p72 levels14. Consistent with this finding, our study confirmed that co-expression of pB602L increases p72 protein abundance (Fig. 4). Similarly, CypA overexpression also enhanced p72 accumulation. However, although CypA is a well-known cellular chaperone involved in protein folding17, it failed to efficiently promote p72 trimerization in cells (Fig. S3B), indicating a regulatory role distinct from that of pB602L. This notion is further supported by the lack of CypA co-localization with p72 at the onset of its expression (6 hpi; Fig. 2F), suggesting that CypA predominantly interacts with trimeric p72 rather than participating in its folding or trimerization. Previous work has shown that suppression of pB602L expression severely compromises viral icosahedral capsid assembly14, underscoring that host proteins are unlikely to substitute for pB602L in mediating p72 trimerization. Moreover, disruption of either p72 or pB602L has been reported to induce abundant aberrant “zipper-like” structures in viral factories14,53. Interestingly, we observed similar aberrant tubular structures in ASFV-infected cells treated with CypA inhibitors, most likely arising from p72 degradation upon CypA inhibition. However, the frequency and extent of these aberrant structures in factories under CypA inhibition were lower than the classical phenotypes reported upon complete abrogation of p72 or pB602L14,53. This suggests that CypA inhibition partially impairs p72 stability and capsid assembly, but does not completely eliminate functional p72 trimers, resulting in a mixed phenotype—limited zipper formation together with mature icosahedral virions. Collectively, these findings support the notion that CypA functions as a modulator of p72 stability rather than as an indispensable chaperone for its folding or trimerization.

Emerging evidence indicates that CypA plays a critical role in regulating the infectivity and replication of multiple viruses17. First, CypA associates with HIV-1 virions by specifically binding the Gag polyprotein54,55, stabilizing the capsid against host restriction factors such as ref-1 and the E3 ligase TRIM5α23,56,57. Second, CypA facilitates influenza B virus (IBV) replication by upregulating OTUD4 to stabilize BNS1 and by suppressing MIB1-mediated degradation of BNP58. Third, CypA binds the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, promoting its trimerization on virions and thereby enhancing spike stability and viral infection25. Collectively, these studies reveal a common mechanism whereby CypA stabilizes viral proteins to promote the replication of certain RNA viruses. Beyond RNA viruses, CypA interacts with the orthopoxvirus capsid protein L3 and antagonizes TRIM5α-mediated L3 degradation, highlighting its essential role in DNA virus replication24. In line with this, our study shows that CypA increases ASFV p72 stability by inhibiting ubiquitination-mediated degradation, thereby promoting virion assembly, further supporting its importance in DNA virus infection. Additionally, CypA is also an essential cofactor for HCV59,60, which enhances viral replication by interacting with viral NS5A to activate its RNA-binding activity61. These studies illustrate that CypA can employ distinct mechanisms to modulate virus infection across diverse viruses.

CypA is a ubiquitously expressed protein that participates in diverse biological processes in host cells18, suggesting that, beyond regulating p72 stability, it may influence ASFV infection through additional mechanisms. For instance, CypA activates MAPK signaling pathways, including ERK1/2, p38, and JNK62,63. Given that many viruses exploit MAPK signaling to facilitate replication64,65, and that ASFV infection has been reported to modulate MAPK14/p38 expression and phosphorylation66, it is plausible that CypA-mediated MAPK activation contributes to ASFV proliferation. In addition, secreted CypA interacts with membrane receptor CD14767, triggering NF-κB activation and promoting the secretion of various proinflammatory cytokines68,69, which may provide a mechanistic explanation for the cytokine storm observed during ASFV infection in domestic pigs70. Conversely, CypA has also been reported to act as a positive regulator of RIG-I-mediated antiviral immune responses71,72, suggesting that CypA-dependent antiviral mechanisms may be harnessed by host cells to counter ASFV infection. These potential mechanisms mediated by CypA may critically shape the outcome of ASFV infection and warrant further investigation.

Notably, the cyclophilin family comprises more than ten members that share highly similar amino acid sequences within the conserved cyclophilin-like domain19. Because the hydrophobic cavity of CypA responsible for p72 binding resides within this domain, it is plausible that other cyclophilins may also interact with ASFV p72. Indeed, our results showed that, in addition to CypA, which exhibited the strongest interaction, several other cyclophilins, including PPIB, PPID, PPIE, PPIF, and PPIL1, also displayed p72-binding capacity to varying extents (Fig. S1B). Except for the nucleus-restricted PPIE, most of these cyclophilins are substantially distributed in the cytoplasm73–77, providing the subcellular basis for p72 interaction in the cytoplasm. These findings suggest that other cyclophilins may also contribute to p72 stabilization, albeit likely less efficiency than CypA. Furthermore, because CypA inhibitors exhibit partial activity against other cyclophilin proteins22, their inhibitory effects on ASFV infection are likely stronger than that caused by CypA deficiency alone, consistent with our observations. The precise contributions of individual porcine cyclophilins to ASFV infection merit further investigation in future studies.

The turnover of p72 plays distinct roles at different stages of the ASFV life cycle, including uncoating and virion assembly. After viral entry, ASFV capsid disassembly occurs in late endosomes, where p72 and other structural proteins are degraded to facilitate core release78. This degradation primarily depends on autophagy-lysosome pathways, as shown by BafA1-mediated inhibition of capsid disassembly16. This was further supported by the recent study showing that the E3 ligase Stub1 promotes selective autophagy-mediated degradation of p7215. In contrast, our data show that ectopically expressed p72 in HEK293T cells is primarily degraded via the proteasome, rather than the lysosomal pathway (Fig. 4). A possible explanation for this discrepancy is that overexpressed p72 predominantly localizes to the cytoplasm rather than the lysosomes (Fig. 2), which may direct its degradation via the proteasomal pathway. Notably, p72 protein expressed during ASFV infection also predominantly localizes to cytoplasm (Fig. 2 and Fig. 8), suggesting that proteasome-dependent degradation occurs after p72 synthesis during infection. However, during virion assembly, ASFV-expressed p72 is actively accumulated in the cytoplasm, indicating a shift toward enhanced stability in the late stages of ASFV infection. These findings suggest that p72 degradation is finely regulated depending on the stages of the viral life cycle and subcellular localization. Further studies are needed to elucidate the host and viral factors that coordinate this regulation.

In summary, our findings provide compelling evidence that host CypA plays a critical role in stabilizing the major capsid protein p72, thereby promoting efficient ASFV propagation. Targeting CypA thus represents a promising antiviral strategy to disrupt ASFV replication and virion production.

Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP)

Co-IP assays were conducted using ASFV-infected PAMs or HEK293T cells co-transfected with Strep-tagged p72 and HA-tagged CypA for 36 h. Cells were collected by centrifugation at 500 g for 5 min and lysed in co-IP buffer consisting of 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail. Lysates were subjected to ultrasonication using a non-contact automated ultrasonic disruptor (5 s on, 8 s off, for 60 cycles). After centrifugation at 12,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C, the clarified supernatants were incubated overnight at 4 °C with either anti-p72 or anti-HA antibodies for immunoprecipitation. Immune complexes were washed five times with Co-IP buffer, resuspended in 1× SDS loading buffer, and boiled at 100 °C for 30 min prior to analysis by Western blotting.

Mass spectrometry analysis

Mass spectrometry analysis was performed on control IgG- and anti-p72-enriched samples (one biological replicate each) from PAM lysates, following previously described procedures with minor modifications82,83. Protein bands were excised from SDS-PAGE gels and subjected to in-gel digestion. Gel pieces were destained with 30% acetonitrile in 50 mM NH₄HCO₃, reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol at 56 °C for 30 min, and alkylated with 60 mM iodoacetamide in the dark for 30 min. After dehydration, samples were rehydrated with sequencing-grade trypsin (Promega) in 50 mM NH₄HCO₃ and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Peptides were sequentially extracted with 60–90% acetonitrile containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid, dried, and desalted on C18 columns. The resulting peptide samples were redissolved with 0.1% formic acid and analyzed by liquid chromatography (LC) using an UltiMate3000 Rapid Separation system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to a Q Exactive HF-X Orbitrap (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. Raw MS data were processed with MaxQuant (v2.7.5.0)84 using default and specified parameters, including trypsin digestion allowing up to two missed cleavages, variable modifications of methionine oxidation and protein N-terminal acetylation, and the use of both unique and razor peptides for protein quantification. MS tolerances were set to 20 ppm for FTMS precursors and 0.5 Da for ITMS fragments. Peptide-level false discovery rate (FDR) was set at 1%, with a minimum peptide length of seven amino acids. Database searches were performed against both the Sus scrofa reference proteome (UP000008227) and the ASFV HLJ/18 strain proteome (GenBank: MK333180.1).

Expression and purification of recombinant proteins

Recombinant Strep-tagged p72 trimer protein was expressed and purified in yeast cells as previously described85,86. For bacterial expression of GST-tagged proteins, pGEX-4T1 plasmids containing full-length CypA or its truncation constructs were transformed into E. coli BL21 cells. Transformed bacteria were first grown on LB agar plates containing 100 µg/mL ampicillin at 37 °C. A single colony was then picked and cultured in 5 mL LB medium with shaking at 220 rpm overnight. In the next day, the culture was inoculated into 500 mL fresh LB medium and grown at 37 °C for 2–3 h, until the OD600 reached 0.6–0.8. Protein expression was induced by adding IPTG to a final concentration of 0.5 mM, followed by incubation at 16 °C overnight. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 g for 10 min, washed once with PBS, and resuspended in NETN buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.6), 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% TritonX-100, 1 mM EDTA, 2 mM PMSF, and 1 mM DTT. Cell lysis was performed using a high-pressure cell disruptor. After centrifugation at 12,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected and incubated with Glutathione Sepharose 4B beads for 2 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation. After washing five times with NETN buffer, GST-tagged proteins were eluted using freshly prepared elution buffer containing 200 mM Tirs-HCl (PH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 50 mM reduced glutathione. Eluted proteins were dialyzed overnight at 4 °C in 2 L of dialysis buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.6), 500 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT, and 20% glycerol. After dialysis, proteins were aliquoted and stored in −80 °C refrigerator. Protein purity and concentration were assessed by SDS-PAGE followed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining.

Strep pull-down assay

Recombinant GST or GST-tagged proteins, Strep-tagged p72 trimer, Strep-Tactin resin, and a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) were combined into 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes. The mixtures were supplemented with co-IP buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.6; 150 mM NaCl; 1% TritonX-100; 1× protease inhibitor cocktail; 0.5% Micrococcal Nuclease (M0247S); 1 mM CaCl2; and 2 mM DTT) to a final volume of 800 µL. For the competitive binding assay, 5 µM cyclic undecapeptide CypA inhibitors or 10 µg p72-specific antibodies were added to the reaction. The samples were incubated at 4 °C for 4 h or overnight with gentle rotation. After incubation, the Strep-Tactin resin was collected by centrifugation at 500 g for 1 min and washed five times with pre-chilled co-IP buffer. The bound proteins were eluted by mixing the resin with 1× SDS loading buffer and boiling at 100 °C for 30 min. Samples were subsequently analyzed by Western blotting.

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy

Immunofluorescence was performed to analyze the subcellular localization of the indicated proteins, as previously described87. Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay

Transfected or inhibitor-treated cells were exposed to CHX (100 μM) for the indicated time periods, followed by Western blot analysis of p72. Virus adsorption and internalization assays

ASFV binding and internalization assays were performed as previously described92. For the adsorption assay, BK2258 stable cell lines or PAMs treated with CypA inhibitors were incubated with ASFV (MOI = 5) at 4 °C for 2 h to allow viral binding. Unbound virions were removed by washing the cells five times with ice-cold PBS. Cells were then harvested for total DNA extraction, followed by qPCR quantification of ASFV genomic DNA. For the internalization assay, BK2258 cells or PAMs were first incubated with ASFV (MOI = 5) at 4 °C for 2 h to enable viral attachment. After five washes with ice-cold PBS, cells were shifted to 37 °C and incubated in growth medium for an additional 2 h to facilitate viral internalization, in the presence or absence of CypA inhibitors. The cells were then incubated with trypsin-EDTA for 2 min at 37 °C to remove noninternalized particles. Cells were then collected for total DNA extraction and qPCR analysis of internalized ASFV genomes.

Flow cytometry analysis

PAMs were seeded in 12-well plates and cultured for 6 h prior to infection with ASFV HLJ/18-7GD (MOI = 0.1) for 72 h, in the presence or absence of CypA inhibitor treatment. Infected cells were either examined by fluorescent microscopy or analyzed by flow cytometry. For flow cytometry analysis, cells were harvested by trypsinization, neutralized with complete medium, washed with PBS, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Single-cell suspensions were prepared by passing the cells through a 70-μm cell strainer. Cells were first gated on forward scatter (FSC-A) versus side scatter (SSC-A) to exclude debris and cell aggregates. mCherry-positive infected cells were subsequently identified by gating on mCherry fluorescence versus SSC-A, using uninfected cells as the negative control. Data were acquired on a flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1).

RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

PAMs were infected with or without ASFV (MOI = 0.1) for 72 h in the presence or absence of NIM811. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, and high-throughput RNA sequencing was performed by BMKgene. Raw FASTQ files from RNA sequencing were quality-filtered using fastp93, followed by alignment to the reference genome using HISAT294. Gene-level counts were quantified with featureCounts95, and differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 package96, applying thresholds of |log2(Fold change)| ≥2 and adjusted p-value ≤ 0.01. Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed using clusterProfiler R package97, with reference to the Gene Ontology98 (GO: https://www.geneontology.org) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes99 (KEGG: https://www.genome.jp) pathway databases.

Quantitative RT-PCR analysis

Total RNAs were extracted from ASFV-infected PAMs using TRIzol reagent, and RNA concentrations were measured with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. A total of 1 μg RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Perfect Real Time) (#RR047A, Takara). Quantitative PCR was performed in triplicate in a 96-well plate using 50 ng cDNAs per well, the SYBR® Green Realtime PCR Master Mix (TOYOBO, #QPK-201), and an ABI QuantStudio5 system. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2(–ΔΔCt) method, with GAPDH as the internal reference. The PCR primers used were: ASFV B646L (forward: 5′-ACGGCGCCCTCTAAGGGT-3′; reverse: 5′-CATGGGCAGCTTCAAACGTTTC-3′) and pig GAPDH (forward: 5′-ACCCAGAAGACTGTGGATGG-3′; reverse: 5′-ACGCCTGCTTCACCACCTTC-3′).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

PAMs were infected with ASFV (MOI = 5) for 24 h in the presence or absence of CypA inhibitor treatment. Infected PAMs were harvested and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS (PH 7.4) at 4 °C overnight. Fixed samples were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in epoxy resin, and processed for ultrathin sectioning and staining according to standard protocols. Sections were examined using a Hitachi H-7650 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) operated at 80 kV. Digital images were captured and analyzed using Adobe Photoshop.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Unless otherwise stated, all experiments were independently repeated at least three times with similar results, and representative images are shown. Statistical significance between groups was determined using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test or ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple-comparisons test, as indicated in the figure legends. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0).
