Machine Learning-Based Reconstructions of Historical Daily and Monthly Runoff for the Laurentian Great Lakes
Authors: Rohini S. Gupta, Sungwook Wi, Scott Steinschneider
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-quality regional streamflow datasets are necessary to support local water resources planning and management in the Great Lakes basin. However, the region’s observed streamflow records are limited by the availability of surface water gauges, which go in and out of service over time. In this paper, we present a continuous reconstruction of daily streamflow from 1951–2013 at 656 gauged locations throughout the Great Lakes basin. This dataset is created using a novel regional Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model that integrates local climate data, physical catchment characteristics, and runoff observations from nearby gauged sites. We also use this model to estimate monthly runoff into Lakes Superior, Michigan-Huron, St. Clair, Erie, and Ontario. The daily reconstruction product will equip water managers with information to support local water resources analyses, while the monthly runoff product can help improve historical water balance estimation across the Great Lakes and provide critical context for lake level shifts under a changing climate.

Background & Summary

Accurate long-term hydrological datasets are foundational for effective water systems planning and management. Streamflow records collected through gauging networks provide critical insights into hydrologic variability driven by climate and land-use change. They also serve as key inputs into water resource systems analyses, reduce uncertainty in flood and drought frequency estimates, and support hydrologic model calibration and predictions in ungauged basins. However, these datasets are often compromised by gaps caused by instrument failures and permanent gauge closures linked to funding constraints. This challenge is widespread across North America: since 1980, over 2,500 U.S. gauges and 460 Canadian gauges with over 30 years of data have been discontinued1.

To address these gaps, streamflow reconstructions are often developed to fill historical records or estimate naturalized flows unaffected by upstream human activities. These efforts can be costly and time-intensive and have traditionally been conducted at the river-basin or state scales2–4. More recently, regional-to-global-scale products have begun to emerge5–10. These larger-scale datasets, typically derived from physical models and sometimes assimilating remotely sensed or in-situ observations, are a valuable supplement to gauge data and provide insight into global river flows and trends. However, these global approaches are limited in their ability to support local-scale water systems planning and management. Datasets derived from global-scale hydrological models that are characterized by a coarse spatial resolution and simplified hydrologic representation cannot reliably resolve local hydroclimate dynamics7 and demonstrate limited skill at daily timescales, which are key to capturing extreme events and serving as input into water system models5,6. Datasets created using satellite data and global climate products are often constrained to periods from the 1980’s onward and are limited in the size of river reaches that can be represented8–11. Thus, there remains a critical need for improved, long-term, daily-scale streamflow reconstructions that are both scalable and suitable for diverse water resource management applications.

One region where high-quality, long-term streamflow data are particularly critical is the Laurentian Great Lakes basin. This system contains the largest freshwater resource in the world, holding nearly 20% of global supply. It provides drinking water for 30 million people and supports diverse ecosystems and economic activity12. The region faces growing challenges from anthropogenic climate change, including warmer temperatures, shifting hydrologic seasonality, and intensifying extremes13–16. Robust hydrological datasets are needed to assess and plan for these changes. Furthermore, water levels in the Great Lakes themselves have fluctuated significantly over the last two decades, in part due to variability in runoff. Extreme low levels between 2000–2012 strained water supply, recreation, and shipping industries, followed by record highs from 2017–2020 that caused over $1 billion in shoreline damages17. Future water level changes remain highly uncertain due to variability in the primary components of the lake water balance: over-lake precipitation, over-lake evaporation, and runoff18–20. Improving historical estimates of these components, including runoff, is vital for refining water balance models and understanding emerging trends.

In this study, we focus on developing long-term, basin-wide runoff estimates at daily and monthly scales in the Great Lakes region, a need highlighted in prior work20–22. Creating such products is challenging for three reasons: (1) nearly 30% of the Great Lakes basin is ungauged21,23; (2) the basin is transboundary, which complicates integrated modeling21,24; and (3) it exhibits highly heterogeneous landscapes, including diverse soil types, land uses, and climates. Due to these challenges, existing runoff products have been limited in both spatial and temporal scale. Some studies have developed monthly reconstructions using regression-based23 or physically-based25,26 daily models at 100–200 gauges across the region. However, some of these products are limited in time span and all struggle to produce accurate reconstructions across the domain (e.g., median Nash Sutcliffe or Kling-Gupta efficiencies at or below 0.5).

Other work has focused on monthly, aggregated runoff at the lake basin scale. Examples include the GLM-HMD historical monthly runoff product27, the U.S. Army Corp of Engineers’ Advanced Hydrologic Prediction Service (AHPS) dataset28, and the Great Lakes Seasonal Hydrological Forecasting System (GLSHFS) dataset29. These products rely on methods such as area ratio extrapolation or conceptual rainfall–runoff models (e.g., the Large Basin Runoff Model30) to generate long runoff reconstructions that extend as far back as the 1950s, but these approaches suffer from assumptions of spatial homogeneity between gauged and ungauged basins and stationarity of hydrological processes22. Other monthly runoff products use physically based models like WATFLOOD, SWAT, and NOAA’s National Water Model, but these generally begin after 197931,32 and suffer from challenges in parameterization across large, ungauged regions33.

A promising alternative for long-term runoff reconstruction is deep learning, particularly the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network34. LSTMs can learn hydrologic patterns and long-term dependencies from large datasets, enabling accurate streamflow prediction and extrapolation to ungauged basins35–37. Recent studies have shown that LSTMs outperform traditional models in the Great Lakes basin. For example, Mai et al.38 showed that an LSTM forced with dynamic meteorology and static basin characteristics outperformed 12 other physical hydrologic models in a large runoff intercomparison across the region. Park et al.39 further extended this framework using an entity-aware LSTM to generate daily streamflow estimates for 975 sub-basins across the Great Lakes region from 1980–2013.

Building on these advances, Wi et al.40 demonstrated that incorporating streamflow from nearby donor gauges as additional inputs substantially improves LSTM performance, particularly at target basins with high-quality donor data. This approach highlights the value of leveraging spatial hydrologic coherence to enhance prediction skill beyond what can be achieved using meteorological and static basin information alone. We build on the model introduced in Wi et al.40 to create a streamflow reconstruction product for the Great Lakes basin that exceeds the spatiotemporal extent and accuracy of existing daily and monthly products. Unlike recent LSTMs developed for the Great Lakes38,40, we train multiple LSTMs that (a) use both climate and donor gauge information to generate daily runoff estimates, rather than just meteorological inputs; (b) extend reconstructions back to 1951 for 656 gauges across both the U.S. and Canadian portions of the basin; (c) and aggregate predictions across ungauged catchments to create long-term, lake-wide monthly runoff reconstructions for Lakes Superior, Michigan-Huron, St. Clair, Erie, and Ontario. This dataset constitutes one of the longest and most extensive streamflow reconstructions in the Great Lakes basin and will enable local and regional water managers to better understand historical runoff variability, contextualize emerging trends in runoff, and support adaptive water resources management throughout the region.

To create daily and monthly runoff products, we train separate LSTMs for four time periods between 1950–2013: 1950–1965, 1966–1980, 1981–1995, and 1996–2013. The active streamflow gauges in each period (see Fig. 1) serve as training targets for each of the models. These streamflow data were collected from the USGS and Water Survey of Canada across 656 gauges in total, with individual gauges coming in and out of service over the 1950–2013 period. We fit separate models to each subperiod to capture changes in hydrologic response over time that may not be captured in available input datasets (e.g., poorly documented shifts in human activity that can modify watershed behavior). We use a 15-year subperiod length to reduce the likelihood of blending together distinct hydrological regimes that could emerge from anthropogenic influences such as land use changes and dam operations.

Each model is trained with inputs that include dynamic meteorology, physical catchment characteristics, and runoff from neighboring gauges. Meteorologic forcing data come from Livneh et al.42, a gridded observational dataset (1/16° resolution, or ~6 km) that spans the entire Great Lakes basin. This dataset provides daily precipitation and minimum and maximum temperature from 1950–2013, derived from nearly 20,000 stations within the Global Historical Climatology Network and WSC. From this data, we compute average meteorologic time series for each gauged watershed, along with climate characteristics such as long-term mean temperature, long-term mean precipitation, aridity, overall fraction of precipitation falling as snow, and frequency and duration of high precipitation events (5 times mean daily precipitation) and low precipitation events (<1 mm/day daily precipitation).

Geophysical attributes including basin-averaged elevation and slope are derived from the HydroSHEDS digital elevation model43. Soil properties are extracted from the Global Soil Dataset for Earth System Models and averaged across a depth of 0–0.2 meters (shallow) and 0.2–2 meters (deep)43. Land cover data are obtained from the North American Land Change Monitoring System43. Data Records

Daily runoff predictions for the 656 gauges and monthly runoff into Lakes Superior, Michigan-Huron, St. Clair, Erie, and Ontario for the period of 1951–2013 are available for download at 10.5281/zenodo.16897689. The file size of all the data is approximately 223.12 MB and contains a single CSV file per gauge with predicted and observed streamflow when available. A single CSV file with monthly lake-wide runoff is also included for each of the Great Lakes (approximately 117.5 MB).

Methods

This work extends the LSTM approach introduced in Wi et al.40 to generate runoff products across the Great Lakes region. The original model, LSTMClim+Dnr, combines local climate data with streamflow from nearby donor gauges to estimate streamflow at target sites. To provide a benchmark, we also develop a second model, LSTMClim, which uses only local climate inputs without donor streamflow (Step 1 in Fig. 2). Both models also incorporate static basin features (i.e., geophysical attributes) at the target basin as additional inputs.Fig. 2Schematic of experimental design. First, two types of LSTM models are developed, LSTMClim and LSTMClim+Dnr. Then, the performance of the models is evaluated across a variety of metrics under a 5-fold cross validation scheme. The LSTMClim+Dnr is ultimately chosen to produce a daily runoff product for 656 gauges throughout the Great Lakes basin and a monthly lake-wide runoff product.

Schematic of experimental design. First, two types of LSTM models are developed, LSTMClim and LSTMClim+Dnr. Then, the performance of the models is evaluated across a variety of metrics under a 5-fold cross validation scheme. The LSTMClim+Dnr is ultimately chosen to produce a daily runoff product for 656 gauges throughout the Great Lakes basin and a monthly lake-wide runoff product.

To account for changes in climate, land use, and human activity over time, we train separate LSTMClim+Dnr and LSTMClim models for four historical subperiods: 1950–1965, 1966–1980, 1981–1995, and 1996–2013. We apply a five-fold cross validation to evaluate out-of-sample model performance in time and space and decide which model to ultimately use for runoff reconstructions (Step 2 in Fig. 2).

For the final reconstruction, models are trained on all available data within each subperiod to produce a daily runoff dataset at the 656 gauges across the region. These outputs are then aggregated and combined with additional predictions at ungauged catchments to create monthly runoff estimates for 128 basins draining directly into the Great Lakes. These are subsequently combined to create a monthly lake-wide runoff product for the five lake systems: Superior, Michigan-Huron, St. Clair, Erie, and Ontario (Step 3 in Fig. 2).

Detailed descriptions of each methodological step are provided below.

Long short-term memory networks

We develop two regional LSTM models to predict daily streamflow across the Great Lakes, LSTMClim and LSTMClim+Dnr, both of which are described in detail in Wi et al.40. We provide a brief overview of these models here. The LSTMClim has 34 input features (K = 34): 3 climate variables and 31 catchment attributes for each target basin. The climate inputs include basin-averaged daily precipitation, maximum temperature, and minimum temperature, while the catchment attributes are listed in Table 1. Climate variables vary dynamically across time steps, whereas catchment attributes remain static throughout the input sequence.

The LSTMClim+Dnr model uses additional streamflow inputs provided from five donor gauges that are selected using a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). The MLP is used to estimate the Spearman’s rank correlation for daily streamflow between pairs of streamflow gauges to identify donor gauges that are strongly correlated to target sites. The MLP is trained on streamflow from all active gauges in a designated subperiod and uses the following inputs for each pair of sites: (1) the Euclidean distance of physical characteristics between watersheds (see Table 1), (2) the Euclidean distance between watershed centroids, and (3) the Spearman’s rank correlation of daily precipitation between the two sites40. For each streamflow gauge, M = 5 donor gauges are selected from the training set based on the highest MLP-estimated correlations. Each selected donor gauge contributes two input features to the LSTMClim+Dnr model: (1) MLP-estimated correlation, which indicates the strength of relationship with the donor gauges; and (2) donor gauge streamflow data for the day on which a prediction is generated for the target basin. Thus, the total number of inputs for the LSTMClim+Dnr model is 2 M + 34 = 44.

Before training, all input features for all models are standardized by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation across all training sites in the training period, except for donor gauge streamflow measurements, which are pre-standardized using a quantile mapping procedure. Observed streamflow at the target site is divided by drainage area to produce flow in millimeters.

Cross validation scheme

Separate LSTMClim and LSTMClim+Dnr models are trained for four historical subperiods (1950–1965, 1966–1980, 1981–1995, 1996–2013) to account for climate and landscape changes over the 63-year period. Each subperiod is further divided into a 10-year training interval and a 5-year testing interval (8 years for the last subperiod; see Table 2). Within each subperiod, we implement a five-fold cross validation procedure. Models are iteratively trained on four folds (80% of the gauged sites shown in Fig. 1) using data from the training interval, and predictions are generated for (1) the training sites during the testing interval, and (2) the remaining 20% of gauged sites for both training and testing intervals. This process ensures that out-of-sample spatial predictions are produced for every gauge (i.e., each site is excluded from training in at least one fold). Generation of daily and monthly runoff products

Results indicate that LSTMClim+Dnr consistently outperforms LSTMClim; therefore, we adopt this model to generate the final data products. LSTMClim+Dnr is retrained using all available active gauges within each of the four subperiods to create two products: 1) daily runoff estimates at 656 gauges across the Great Lakes basin; and 2) monthly, lake-wide runoff aggregated from 128 subbasins draining into the five Great Lakes.

Daily runoff predictions throughout the great lakes basin

We train LSTMClim+Dnr using 10 seeds for each subperiod and incorporating all active gauges. Predictions are generated for all 656 gauges, and results are averaged across the 10 seeds to produce a single ensemble estimate. Finally, outputs from the four subperiods are combined to create a continuous daily streamflow record from 1951–2013 for each gauge.

Monthly lake-wide runoff predictions

To estimate monthly runoff draining directly into the five Great Lakes, we apply the trained LSTMClim+Dnr models to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N=128$$\end{document}N=128 subbasin boundaries digitized by NOAA’s Great Lakes Environmental Research Laboratory (GLERL) (see Fig. 2). For each subbasin, drainage areas associated with interior streamflow gauges are excluded, and the LSTMClim+Dnr model is used to generate daily runoff predictions for the remaining ungauged area, averaged across 10 random seeds. Observed daily runoff from the interior gauged areas is then combined with the modeled ungauged contributions to produce total subbasin runoff. When observations are missing at an interior gauge, LSTMClim+Dnr predictions are used instead. In the instance of nested interior gauges, only flows from the most downstream gauge are used in the calculation of total subbasin runoff. Runoff estimates are aggregated across subbasins for each lake and averaged to produce a monthly lake-wide runoff product for 1951–2013.

Technical Validation

Cross validation of daily flows

Figure 3 shows model performance under five-fold cross-validation, focusing on the cumulative distribution of NSE across sites based on spatial (row 1) and temporal (row 2) out-of-sample estimates. Spatial out-of-sample estimates are for testing sites never used in training, while temporal out-of-sample estimates are based on the average streamflow predictions for training sites across folds but for the testing interval. Curves shifted further to the right indicate better performance.

Results demonstrate that both models perform better out-of-sample in time than in space across all subperiods. Out-of-sample spatial performance is similar for both models (Fig. 3, row 1), with median NSE values between 0.67–0.69 across subperiods. However, LSTMClim+Dnr outperforms LSTMClim for temporal validation, with median NSE values between 0.84–0.86, demonstrating that donor gauge information enhances predictions at training gauges during previously unseen time periods. This indicates that LSTMClim+Dnr is particularly effective for extending and gap-filling partially gauged locations, while remaining comparable to the state-of-the-art benchmark (LSTMClim) for ungauged sites.

Figure 4 presents the distribution of additional performance metrics (KGE, PBIAS, FLV, FHV) for both models out-of-sample in time. Each box plot contains a single point per training gauge, derived from averaging streamflow predictions across the four folds in which the gauge was used for training. Overall, LSTMClim+Dnr exhibits higher and less variable KGE values and lower biases (PBIAS, FLV, FHV) compared to LSTMClim. The 1966–1980 subperiod is an exception, perhaps because nearly half of the new gauges activated in that subperiod had limited data (<10 years), reducing the potential to identify useful donor gauges. For LSTMClim, we observe a general improvement over time across most metrics (except FHV), with the largest biases and lowest KGE values occurring in the earliest subperiod (1950–1965). This trend is less pronounced in LSTMClim+Dnr, highlighting the model’s ability to improve performance in the earliest subperiod when gauging density is lowest.

Similar results for spatial out-of-sample predictions are provided in Figure S1, which, like Fig. 3, shows comparable performance between the two models across sites and subperiods.

Focusing on the most recent subperiod (1996–2013), gauges are further classified as natural or regulated (i.e., low or high human impact). Mai et al.38 provided a similar classification, which we adopt for sites used in that study. Sites not included in Mai et al.38 were classified as natural based on the ‘reference’ designation in GAGES-II46 for U.S. sites, or the RHBN-N designation in Canada’s Reference Hydrometric Basin Network47 (RHBN). Figure 5 shows that LSTMClim+Dnr outperforms LSTMClim based on NSE and temporal validation (2005–2013) across both regulated and natural gauges, but the performance gap is larger for natural sites. Here, the difference in median NSE grows to 0.11 between the two models. Out-of-sample in space, LSTMClim performs only slightly worse than LSTMClim+Dnr.

Overall, LSTMClim+Dnr aligns closely with GLM-HMD, AHPS, and GLSHFS, particularly in recent decades. The largest differences for Lakes Erie, Michigan-Huron, St. Clair, and Superior occur during 1951–1965, when gauge density is lowest and observed records are sparse (see Figures S5–58). Lake Ontario monthly runoff estimates show large differences in both the early and modern records (Fig. 9a,c). Our estimates and those from GLM-HMD (both gauge-based) tend to show higher peaks than AHPS and GLSHFS, which rely exclusively on rainfall-runoff modeling (Fig. 9b). The closer alignment of LSTMClim+Dnr estimated monthly peaks with GLM-HMD compared to AHPS and GLSHFS, and the strong alignment between these products in the modern period, is a consistent pattern across lakes (Figures S5–S8). NWM estimates differ the most from other products, particularly for Lake Superior (Figure S8).

The gray shaded L2SWBM estimates are centered closer to GLSHFS and AHPS. If our data were assimilated as an additional input into the model, it would likely increase the L2SWBM estimates (align them closer to GLM-HMD and away from AHPS and GLSHFS), especially during the peak spring runoff season and earlier in the period of record. This would then lead to adjustments in estimates of other water balance components in the L2SWBM model (over-lake evaporation and precipitation), which could influence how trends are estimated for all water balance components across the period of record.

In this study, we create two data products that are valuable resources for regional Great Lakes water systems planning and management. The extended daily runoff reconstruction provides local water managers with longer continuous data to analyze historical hydroclimate variability and contextualize emerging trends that will support planning activities across the Great Lakes basin. These data can also help support calibration of process-based models used for hydrologic and water quality assessments throughout the region. The monthly reconstruction of lake-wide runoff provides opportunities for improved historical water balance estimation across the Great Lakes, with implications for evaluating and predicting future water level variability. The LSTMClim+Dnr model itself holds great potential to be scaled to other regions that would benefit from long-term reconstructions and that have streamflow gauging networks and meteorological data to support training. Unlike global hydrology models or satellite-based approaches, the reconstructions from LSTMClim+Dnr are not temporally constrained by the relatively short timeframe of satellite or remote-sensing data. Furthermore, LSTMClim+Dnr will be able to provide more accurate, localized reconstructions for rivers with smaller reach sizes that are not well represented in global-scale models.

Our study presents a deterministic reconstruction of runoff throughout the Great Lakes. Future work should focus on methods that tailor LSTM ensembles to explicitly quantify uncertainty. Several approaches already exist in the broader machine-learning and hydrologic modeling literature, such as Monte Carlo dropout that produces multiple model configurations or Bayesian LSTMs that treat network weights as probability distributions50–52. Alternatives such as quantile-regression or mixture-density LSTMs feature adjusted loss functions that predict conditional distributions of streamflow rather than single values52,53. Integrating these methods would allow future reconstructions to represent a fuller range of plausible streamflow traces and convey confidence in predictions, which ultimately builds trust among users and supports more comprehensive water systems planning and management.
