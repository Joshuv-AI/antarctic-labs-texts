Gender, indigeneity, and intersectional considerations; The mental health of frontline public safety and healthcare workers in Canada
Authors: Lindsay P. Allen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThere are high prevalences of PTSD and suicide in frontline professional emergency responders; currently in Canada, every two out of three of such frontline workers are suffering at least one mental disorder. Beyond individual psychoeducation, gender and intersectional considerations must be addressed to better support mental health of these workers.MethodsSemi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted over Zoom with 41 frontlines workers from across Canada. Theoretical sampling and inductive analysis led to a focus on the experiences of minorities, namely, women and Indigenous participants.ResultsThe key findings include that the workforce lacks diversity; women face a double- or triple-bind; gender-based bullying and harassment continue to be a problem; gender-based mental health stigma persists; there are issues with male peer supporters who lack gender-based understanding; a cultural of machoism persists; there are good in-roads for Indigenous spirituality and inclusion in therapy; Indigenous Elders' teachings and presence in the workplace are therapeutic; and there are special considerations when working in small communities.ConclusionsAddressing behavioural and institutional barriers to minority mental wellness must become priorities if frontline organizations seek sustainable recruitment and retention among equity-deserving populations.

Introduction

Canadian frontline public safety and healthcare workers (articulated herein as ‘frontline workers’) have higher rates of PTSD, suicide, and other mental disorders than average citizens, due to frequent and accumulative exposures to violent and traumatizing events [1]. Currently, every two out of three frontline workers (FLW) are suffering at least one mental disorder in Canada [1]. There are numerous threats to the mental health of frontline workers, including frequent exposures to potentially psychologically traumatizing events, as well as the overall organizational behaviour and safety climate [2], [3], [4], [5]. The wellbeing of the organization depends on healthy individuals working within it, just as the individual workers' mental health is affected by the larger organizational structures, norms, and processes [2], [6], [7]. It is well-established that certain populations are rendered more vulnerable through power relations operating within hierarchal frontline organizations; yet, there are large gaps in the literature about frontline Indigenous and non-Indigenous women's mental health in regard to organizational behaviour stemming from how power is structured [8]. Gender and indigeneity are risk factors for mental ill-health due to how work-related psychological injuries can be exacerbated by marginalization stress, systemic oppression, and structural disadvantage [8].

Canada is an increasingly pluralistic country. Diversity in the frontline workforce is needed to improve capacity for fittingness and communication with diverse service users, and in turn, more effective service provision [9]. The Canadian healthcare labour force is substantively feminized and racialized (i.e., 80% women, 23% people of colour); however, there remains a glass ceiling effect on policy- and decision-making positions [10], [11]. Public safety is men-dominated at all levels of paramilitarily structured organizations [12].

In Canada and worldwide, gender-based violence has been evidenced in policing, healthcare, and correctional service organizations, and this type of phenomena was exacerbated under the stressful circumstances of the COVID-19 pandemic [12], [13], [14], [15]. Women who are Indigenous are at heightened risk of gender-based violence [8]. Women in public safety face more discrimination, sexual harassment, and bullying – with less support from superiors and job satisfaction – than men [8], [16], [17].

FLW are potential agents of change in employing organizations, and it is important to support workers' agency [18]. Psychoeducation, one of the most common interventions for FLW mental health, can empower workers in their self-management [19]. At the same time, a criticism of psychoeducation is the inherent risk of exclusively placing responsibility on individuals to stay well or get well, without interrogating their environments, including policies, systems, institutions, social structures, and social determinants of health [7]. Systemic issues must be addressed in tandem with individual level mental health initiatives to support mental well-being comprehensively [7].

This study arose out of COVID-19 pandemic recovery initiatives to address high levels of FLW mental ill-health, including an evaluation of a psychoeducation program that discovered participants were desperate to discuss more than program feedback. Participants emphasized organizational behaviour issues, and this led to further research questions. This article pertains to the research questions: Did gender, indigeneity, and other intersectional considerations emerge as important factors in minorities' lived experiences of organizational behaviour affecting this sample of frontline public safety and healthcare workers, during and directly following the COIVD-19 pandemic? If so, how?

Methods

Paradigm and design

A pragmatist paradigm, a qualitative design, and a critical constructivism approach to grounded theory methodology were used, drawing on feminist, intersectionality, marginalization, and decolonization theories [20], [21], [22]. Grounded theory is well-suited to this study because it recognizes the influence but incompleteness of extant theories ability to comprehensively represent participants' real-world settings; the methodology remains close to participant data to not muddy discovery and pre-empt justification of an extant theory [20], [21], [22]. The motivation for the study was to honour what participants shared about their mental health thoroughly and accurately aligns well with grounded theory. Further, critical constructivist grounded theory attends to the role of power and participants' social locations, congruent with the research questions to understand socio-political factors contextualizing mental health [20], [21], [22].

Recruitment, screening, and sampling

Approximately 70,000 FLW who received mental health training through a federal funding initiative in 2022 were eligible to participate in the study. They were screened before enrollment into the mental health training program by the provider agency, and in any case of acute mental distress, they were referred immediately to a relevant treatment program or crisis center. Though unusual for grounded theory, but due to the larger program size (this choice as due to the study being carved out of a prior dataset involving 70,000 participants, as well as pragmatics of operating within a large interdisciplinary mixed-methods team), random selection was used to draw from different batches of registrants from different regions, organizations, and job categories. 1155 potential participants were emailed an ethics-approved recruitment letter between August and December of 2022 in batches of up to one hundred people at a time in order to manage responses responsibly and promptly. Of the 71 people who expressed interest, 41 completed the full study process. Scheduling was a substantial barrier in this time-strapped population (e.g., chronic staffing shortages, mandatory overtime, numerous funerals), and there were also technical barriers for those working in prisons. The sample characteristics are similar to the larger workforce, more or less depending on location, which is remarkable given the mixed-methods and pragmatic sampling strategy was not designed to be an exact statistically representation. Further sampling was not possible due to funding and time constraints in the original study, with which the research approvals were also enmeshed. Far more than recruitment, theoretical sampling is the gathering, coding, and analysing of data toward a theory [21], [23]. Within the (large for qualitative) sample size of 41, theoretical sampling can be understood as the focus on minority participants. This led to the subsample of focus being those who were women and/or Indigenous/visible minority women (there were no Indigenous men). Inclusion criteria through theoretical sampling thus became gender and ethnicity.

Positionality in relation to data generation

Positionality is the acknowledgement of the researcher's social locations in relation to the research [20]. I am a cisgender woman with Canadian and European ancestry who has worked in Indigenous community health in South, Central, and North Americas since 1999 and as a formal health researcher since 2018. At the time of the study, I was a Research Associate and PhD Candidate, with credentials including a Master of Science (MSc); I currently hold a PhD in Medicine. I conducted all the interviews. For participant privacy and safety, the only people present were the participant and I. We were not previously known to each other; participants knew I was working to support a larger program of research at the Canadian Institute of Public Safety Research and Treatment. I was personally invested in supporting mental health research in Canada because during the COVID-19 pandemic, several of my family and community members succumbed to mental unwellness (including anxiety disorder, psychosis, self-harm, and suicide), leading to a genuine desire to respond to those in need of help. Frontline emergency responders were identified as a priority population by the Government of Canada [24].

Verbal and written consent processes were completed prior to each interview. Zoom Pro videoconferencing software was used to record each interview, located wherever participants were most comfortable. The interview guide was developed based on a pilot study, then pre-tested with the larger research team [20], [21], [22]. The interview guide asked questions regarding participant demographics, experiences of the mental health training, and what helped or hindered implementing the training at work and home.

In-depth, semi-structured interviews lasted between 28 and 120 min long, typically just over an hour, with open-endedness built-in, depending on how much the participants wanted to discuss. There were no repeat interviews. An accuracy-checked, deidentified transcript was returned to each participant, who was then given two weeks to review the document and redact any comment or section at their discretion [25]. Participants typically had a lot to say in the interviews, often unburdening themselves far beyond the question guide. They indicated that they were content with the findings via wrap-up emails. I believe my experience helped the study (e.g., via well-developed empathetic active listening skills, qualified naiveté, ongoing and thorough reflexive self-questioning) more than hampered the study.

Data analysis

The data was first analyzed for the purposes of evaluating the mental health training program with the research team at the Canadian Institute of Public Safety Research and Treatment, including template analysis coding and interdisciplinary discussions. For a new and separate doctoral study using a novel research question, objective, and approach, I performed a new analysis of the data.

A separate, second (or third or more) analysis of data is common in quantitative research but less so in qualitative research; however, there are important benefits to revisiting and reusing data for more than one study [20]. In-depth interviews often produce overwhelming amounts of rich, detailed data too voluminous to be analyzed, and it is ethical to honour participants' contributions through more analyses [20]. Given that the population of inquiry is a difficult to recruit population, I sought to respectfully maximize what participants had shared by returning to the data for a new analysis [20], [26], [27]. The previous analysis was completely different, focused on how to improve a program, rather than structural, sociological, and contextual phenomena. While scholars suggest that it is ideal to have numerous coders and testing of inter-coder alignment, the leading researcher often defines the codebook and corrects alignment that way [20]. I recoded the entire dataset, informed by previous iterations of analysis and discussions.

Using the most recent version and updates of Dedoose qualitative and mixed-methods analysis software, the textual data was coded line-by-line, then section-by-section, starting with open coding, then more focused coding to identify the main themes and subthemes related to the research questions [20], [21], [22]. I was very familiar with the data because I had been a part of the previous team analysis. Memo-writing helped to highlight linkages across themes, as well as for my reflexivity practice [20], [21], [22]. Charmaz's and Levitt's methodological guides, research team members, and an academic advisory committee (five subject/methods experts) were consulted throughout the process to ensure rigour [20], [21], [22]. Data saturation, though often reported as a checkbox without much discussion, is in actuality difficult to discern [23], [28], [29], [30]. The thematic saturation was reached for gender-related issues far sooner than we agreed to end the interview stage, while the same could not be said of Indigeneity; however, recruitment of this subsample proved very challenging and is acknowledged as a limitation of this current study and flagged as an area where more research is needed. Studies specific to Indigenous frontline workers would require more time and resources; this would be a very worthy endeavor.

I tested for differences across other categories as noted in the coding structure, viewable in Fig. 1. Compare and contrast methods (e.g., across job categories, between genders) were used via the software has tests to explore many analysis (e.g., code frequency by descriptor, code co-occurrence). The strongest by far of emerging differences were along the axes of gender and indigeneity. I also used negative case analysis, so when there were exceptions to the theme, these are included in the results. Ensuring rigour

Prior to and throughout the study, discussions peers and advisors at the university helped to guide and clarify the process. I maintained an audit trail, including sampling procedure results in Excel, email communications with participants and potential participants, dated and detailed interview notes, coding structure iterations, and drafts of interpretation [20], [25]. I used reflexivity journaling and the Dedoose memo-writing function to examine linkages, uncertainties, and underlying processes that emerged during analysis [20], [25]. Member checking occurred via participants providing feedback and approval on their transcripts, with opportunity to make changes and provide direction [20], [25]. Analytical and member checking processes included iterative literature reviews and discussions with academics, stakeholders, community members, and presentation audiences [20], [25].

Research ethics

The study received approval from the University of Regina Research Ethics Board (UR REB# 2024–676) and the University of Manitoba Bannatyne Campus Health Research Ethics Board (UM HREB #HS26350(H2024:076)) in April of 2024. Reporting of study data adheres to the Consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative studies (COREQ) [31].

While there were no Indigenous Nations or communities involved in the research, but rather a small number of self-declared and self-selected autonomous individuals, it is still useful to address Indigenous research ethics. Given that it was not community-level research, there was no community-level engagement; done properly, the latter requires years of relationship-building and community leadership resources [32], [33], [34]. Making the contributions of Indigenous participants available through open access publishing contributes to improving Indigenous access to relevant research as per the Indigenous research principles such as OCAP [35]. This facilitates access without requiring every community to have the resources, capacity, and infrastructure to maintain their own data repository. Cultural safety was maintained through a culturally-responsive, trauma-informed, participant-centred approach, using ongoing relationship-building and consent-checking processes [36], [37], [38]. The de-identification stages attended to any risks of identifiability [20]. Further, it is important to include Indigenous voices and to resist institutional, bureaucratic research barriers [32], [33], [39], [40].

Results

The core themes that emerged were included that: 1) FLW women have substantial additional stressors affecting mental health (including subthemes of women's double- or triple-bind, bullying, lack of supports, and machoism) and, 2) Indigenous women's insights provide guidance to improve FLW mental health going forward (including subthemes of spirituality and inclusion in therapy, Elders teachings and presence as therapeutic, and the implications of relationality in small communities).

‘We have a huge issue with diversity, there is none’.

Firstly, an illustrative excerpt provides context on how diversity is limited in frontline workplaces. As a paramedic-firefighter said:My service has a huge issue with diversity. We don't have any. […] We have the one hundred and eighty frontline staff. We have five females. The first one was hired [number of decades] years ago now. Then there was no other female hired for almost [number over twenty-five] years. There are no openly gay or no openly LGBTQ members in our service. We are all very much the white, male, alpha personality, brown haired, brown eyes individuals. Nobody could tell the difference between us unless they saw our name tags. Same haircut even. […] Yeah, it's pretty bad, we are working on trying to improve that, and it needs to be improved, but it's hard to get there. [P16].

As an example of negative case analysis, only two public safety personnel expressed they have worked with more diverse groups, offering some exception to the prevalent sense of a ‘monolithic white male culture’ in these services. Healthcare participants characterized their workplaces as somewhat more diverse, depending on their unit.

Theme 1: FLW women have substantial additional stressors affecting mental health.

Working, mothering, and supporting spouses: ‘I feel like a shitty mom’.

Full-time working mothers shouldered more responsibility for their children's many needs, including practical (e.g., ensuring nutrition, clothing, hygiene, school supplies), logistical (e.g., scheduling appointments, childcare, transportation), emotional and mental health (e.g., problems with friends, self-esteem, substances, social media, screen time), and educational (e.g., supporting homework, volunteering) than their spouses did. In couples with two FLWs, women felt they gave more support than they received from spouses, additionally if their spouse had an injury or disability. With few breaks at work (‘it's a meat grinder,’ ‘it's just call after call,’ ‘it's hard to get a bathroom break’) or at home (‘sometimes I just need 15 minutes to myself’), FLW mothers had difficulty finding time to do mental health self-management practices.

Women participants acknowledged overt and internalized sexism and misogyny, feeling they are being set up to fail because they are ‘never enough.’ Participants explained the strain of trying to be a ‘good mother’ even when they come home from work exhausted and psychologically vulnerable. A law enforcement officer with a PTSD discussed:I felt like a bag of shit after […] we were talking about how PTSD affects our ability to connect with other people, and how it can affect kids. […] I feel like a shitty mom. […] Its's mom guilt. [P31].

I felt like a bag of shit after […] we were talking about how PTSD affects our ability to connect with other people, and how it can affect kids. […] I feel like a shitty mom. […] Its's mom guilt. [P31].

Fathers may have felt similarly but none mentioned these strains. Fathers were more likely to express regret about missing out on their children's upbringing or about their children seeing them at their worst (e.g., when using alcohol or substances as coping strategies).

Participants advocated for more and stronger gender parity policies in frontlines workplaces to distribute parenting workloads and expectations more evenly. Participants advocated for greater supports, such as flexible hours and mental health promoting practices built into the workday.

Gendered bullying: ‘He really destroyed my mental health’.

Bullying and harassment emerged as a subtheme. Coworkers make insinuations that women FLW are given easier career entry tests, despite the same standards (e.g., tests of knowledge, weightlifting, fitness) applying to everyone. Delayed promotions were reported by women, such as this firefighter: “I passed the testing to be a captain years ago, but I didn't get the position […despite that] I've been qualified for a while” [P29]. Women participants reported years of bullying from superiors, peers, inferiors, and sometimes multiple people ganging up. While her aggressor was granted a promotion, this police officer continued to have to work under him in the same department. She reflected:He really destroyed my mental health. […] It's hard right, because I'm a female, and he's a male. I stand up for myself, which is different than a lot of people. So the perception is that I'm a bitch, right. But I don't care… that's taken me a little bit of time. [P6].

He really destroyed my mental health. […] It's hard right, because I'm a female, and he's a male. I stand up for myself, which is different than a lot of people. So the perception is that I'm a bitch, right. But I don't care… that's taken me a little bit of time. [P6].

Women facing PTSD, anxiety, depression, etc., then also are forced to contend with bullying and harassment; this compounds trauma from frontlines work exposures into larger issues (e.g., institutional betrayal trauma, sanctuary trauma). One police officer who was diagnosed with breast cancer felt relieved because she thought the bullying for that diagnosis would at least be better than mental health bullying.When I was diagnosed with a very aggressive form of breast cancer, I thought, Oh, good! They're going to understand, this past year, that's why everything has been so out of whack for me […]. I was treated abysmally because of their perception of my mental health. [P11].

Participants advocated for mental health training for leadership and throughout organizations, promoting trauma-informed mental health champions and women, and watchdogs and corrective mechanisms to address misuses and abuses of power.

Gendered stigma: ‘Females don't come forward because they're looked at as weak’.

Women participants experienced misdiagnoses, as well as not being believed about their health symptoms by coworkers and physicians. Older women participants suffered undiagnosed PTSD until more recently, a holdover from women's exclusion from the military − the context in which PTSD as a diagnosis was first developed. Participants inhabiting two or more categories of marginalization (e.g., gender, age, disability, Indigeneity) faced serious adversity at work when trying to seek help and to manage medical issues. A woman police officer who incurred work-related physical and mental disabilities said:Looking after mental health − we don't. I realized that through last year, I kept on trucking, and I should have gone off for a month to get myself straightened out. […] I'd been so worried about the perception, what people would think. And is it because I'm a woman? Yeah, 100%. We have more to prove, in my opinion, on this job than men do. […] A lot of females don't come forward because they're looked at as weak. [P6].

Women participants advocated that workplaces reduce mental health stigma and become trauma-informed including regarding sexual trauma which is very prevalent in populations of incarcerated women.

Male peer supporters: ‘Do you think if you wore makeup and a dress?

While one the main sources of mental health support in FLW is formally trained peer supporters, women participants experienced subpar services. Peer support can be less accessible for women due to gendered stigma, mandatory organizational reporting requirements, and problematic advice. One participant, when she was suffering depression and substance dependency had a peer supporter respond: “He said, ‘Do you think if you wore makeup and maybe a dress, and did your hair, do you think you might feel better about yourself? Do you think people might be more accepting of you?’” [P11]. This was unhelpful and counterproductive to addressing the psychological injuries which were entwined with chronic workplace bullying and harassment. This type of gendered attitude reproduces a barrier to women's help-seeking: “We didn't talk about calling peer supporters. We didn't trust them” [P11].

Machoism: “Rage is much more acceptable, crying is not acceptable”

Many participants commented that anger was the only acceptable emotion in these sectors (e.g., ‘anger is acceptable,’ ‘rage is acceptable,’ ‘crying is not acceptable,’ ‘crying is a weakness’) in the sense of general cultural norms. While sadness is a normal human emotion in response to witnessing the horrors and traumas prevalent in frontline work, FLW are extremely guarded. A woman in corrections advocated for this to change for FLW mental health:I see a lot of tears in people's eyes, and the holding back, as this is seen as weakness. It's terrible. Because it needs to be let out actually. I always tell people, crying is the same as laughing. If you hold in your laughter, it's not good, same as crying. If you hold it in eventually, it's, you're dying from the inside. [P13].

I see a lot of tears in people's eyes, and the holding back, as this is seen as weakness. It's terrible. Because it needs to be let out actually. I always tell people, crying is the same as laughing. If you hold in your laughter, it's not good, same as crying. If you hold it in eventually, it's, you're dying from the inside. [P13].

Gendered acceptable expressions of emotions are gatekept to maintain group membership and because “You have to because otherwise […] you are a target from everybody. You are being a target for management, a target from your co-worker, and a target of the inmates” [P13]. Participants advocated for emotional intelligence in leadership and the initiation of comprehensive and proactive mental and emotional health supports.

Theme 2: Indigenous women's insights provide guidance to improve FLW mental health.

There were several things important to Indigenous participants despite that there were not many Indigenous people who participated. Dure to the richness of the data elicited in the in-depth interviews, there were enough data to highlight important elements that can inform the current results and future research.

Spirituality and inclusion in therapy: ‘When can we say, my spirit name is?’

In initial introductions in psychoeducation groups, Indigenous participants advocated for a more spiritual understanding of their purpose in coming together. For example, in Indigenous ceremonial and healing spaces, there are customary ways of introducing oneself that indicate spiritual ontologies: “When can we say? ‘Hi, my parents gave me the name […], my Spirit name is […].’ My Spirit name is important” [P17]. Indigenous women longed for culturally safe therapeutic spaces inclusive of – or even centered in – Indigenous spirituality and spiritual understandings of health. They also wanted clinician-facilitators to facilitate discourse to address group membership and belonging given the historic and ongoing processes of social exclusion. Indigenous participants found it welcoming when there was an acknowledgement of Indigeneity when starting a therapeutic relationship in one-to-one therapy and in a facilitated group.

Indigenous participants noted that diversity (e.g., Indigeneity, spirituality, culture, ethnicity) is important not to ignore because people are multi-faceted and that there is therapeutic benefit to embracing complexity and adding depth to group therapy discussions. They advocated for practicing grace, curiosity, and approachability around these topics which can be polarizing if not well-facilitated. Bringing diversity into psychoeducation and group therapy sessions can help people who ontologically equate mental health with spiritual health.

Elders teachings and presence as therapeutic: ‘Invite my Spirit with me’

Participants found that, as alternative to stoicism and machoism, the functional disconnect functional reconnect (FDFR) technique (from psychoeducation for frontline workers) resonated with their experiences and helped empower them. Indigenous participants highlighted connections between FDFR and spirituality. For example, when a Cree woman working in correctional services struggled with work-related psychological injury, her Elder taught her how to intentionally invite her Spirit to come home with her with her at the end of a shift and to help her Spirit not get lost or left behind at the prison. She explained:I didn't want to leave my Spirit there. What was taught to me about from an Elder about that was that if I leave my Spirit there, then in the night, I'm going to be worrying and thinking about how I can solve the problems that are happening at work. I was stressed out, and I knew that I was thinking too much about things all the time, and that's what she had said to me that had helped her. […] When I had a stressful day, coming home from work, I would invite my Spirit with me. And that was my reconnection. That definitely fit with disconnect, reconnect [technique]. It was very helpful. [P14].

A key positive and mentally steadying factor for Indigenous FLWs is the Elder-in-Residence at the organization because the presence of an Elder is inherently calming and therapeutic. Similarly, access to Indigenous medicines (e.g., sage, cedar, and sweetgrass for smudging, tobacco for praying with) and to Indigenous practitioners of psychology, psychiatry, and other branches of western medicines are important: “I was so happy that she was able to give me the connection to get the Indigenous psychologist that I have” [P14].

Working in a small relational community; ‘It hits home harder’.

Many FLW worked in a different neighbourhood or jurisdiction than where they lived. Working in one's own community, particularly in small, rural, remote, isolated, or ‘where everyone knows everyone else’ can increase psychological stress, associations, and personalization compared to anonymous callers.From an Indigenous perspective, when you're a probation and parole officer in your own community, you know the people, you have relationships with the people […] because Indigenous communities are close-knit communities. When one of those members that you're serving in the community dies by suicide for example, it hits home a little harder. [P22].

From an Indigenous perspective, when you're a probation and parole officer in your own community, you know the people, you have relationships with the people […] because Indigenous communities are close-knit communities. When one of those members that you're serving in the community dies by suicide for example, it hits home a little harder. [P22].

Several Indigenous and non-Indigenous participants had experience working in isolated Indigenous communities. They described additional stressors arising from having to provide multiple types of emergency response simultaneously and under-resourced. They were frustrated with perennial governmental jurisdictional issues that caused inequitable funding for Indigenous communities and trying to respond to calls without adequate resource allotments to meet the needs of the population and land base.Because it is [rural and remote] First Nations policing, and the way that's gone for so long where it's not actually funded based on needs, it's basically a flat rate check of ‘take it or leave it’ – but ‘it’ is nowhere near what's actually needed. […] We're making do with about half as much as we should. And so that's just extreme strain on everyone […] You're constantly working short where you're covering multiple communities. And just a lot more danger because you don't have another cruiser for another like three hours. [P41].

Participants were too often short-staffed or completely alone without back up when responding to ‘very bad calls,’ a systemic issue that exacerbates FLW mental health and impacts services to Indigenous communities.

Other intersectional considerations

There were no individuals in the sample who were openly 2SLGBTQI+ participants which is unfortunate because such people could provide key insights into experiences of majority culture and minority subcultures. There were some participants who drew parallels between stigma around mental unwellness to stigma around sexual orientation or gender identity; for example, the psychologically and socially meaningful process of coming out as 2SLGBTQI+ was sometimes understood as similar to or repeated in the process of disclosure of mental health status. A few participants expressed confusion and aggression regarding the practice of stating and using pronouns. For example, I received occasional rebuke and pushback when asking participants how they self-identified their gender at the end of the interview. I observed a lack of education around gender parity, gender diversity, and gender identity issues (e.g., “There's blue jobs and pink jobs,” as per Participant 18), including in the psychoeducation program we were discussing. As summarized by Participant 30: “There is so much more room for what we could do” and Participant 4 “There's a long way to go.”

Discussion and conclusions

A novel contribution of the article was to bring FLW from different job categories into dialogue about mental health and intersectional considerations in Canada in the pandemic recovery era. Very few studies consider Indigenous and non-Indigenous women frontline workers' mental health as pertaining to their organizational behaviour, to my knowledge. Few studies offer direct quotes, insight, and directions forward from Canadian frontline workers' lived experiences.

This study found that women FLW experience multiple additional psychological strains and stressors. Social expectations around division of labour create the unique double- or triple-bind pressures placed disproportionately on FLW working mothers, and there can be mental health implications that should not be ignored. Employed women are more likely to perform higher intensity caregiving, to experience more role conflict, to sacrifice opportunities, and to operate with fewer supports than men, resulting in statistically significant health and financial disadvantages that compound over time [41]. FLW described these interrelated issues and added that there is further mental health strain as spouses of frontline workers or as frontline workers themselves (i.e., already managing PTSD, anxiety, etc.). Where work-related policies are both pro-mothers and pro-equality, these pressures can be reduced [42]. Where policies support more equitable fatherhood participation, fathers report more satisfaction with parenting, more engagement with their children, and stronger bonds with their children and spouses [42]. Cultural recognition, along with monetary and public support, for the value of care labour is needed; without these shifts, such labour will remain formally unvalued and feminized, and “women and mothers will continue to be disadvantaged, stigmatized, and stressed” [42] p.265).

Women and other minorities in men-dominated workplaces benefit from proactive anti-bullying policies and management, including consistent response to complaints [43], [44]. Respectful workplace policies are important but not enough on their own and must be enacted by strong transformational leadership [44]. Conventional wisdom dictates that victims of bullying should speak up, directly challenge their bullies, and tell higher authorities, but too often victims who complain to managers are told to take it as a joke, that they should be able to handle it on their own, and that they are hypersensitive [45], [46]. Nursing literature demonstrates that women operate under heightened moral, cultural, and societal expectations and stereotypes of an ‘angelic force’ (e.g., nurses, paramedics) despite working within hospitals (and similar institutions) based on a military model that perpetuates structural violence; further, women are socialized not to complain about their work conditions, and to expect to have to deal with violence, trauma, and harassment [47]. Organizations penalize whistle-blowers, perpetuating the normalization of violence and toxic environments in which sanctuary trauma can exacerbate other types of occupationally incurred traumas [7]. Women and Indigenous workers are often perceived by communities to have more cultural competency, insight, empathy, trustworthiness, and skills for de-escalating violence; there are fewer officer-involved shootings where there are more women police officers [16]. The largest barriers to increasing women's participation are behavioural and institutional (i.e., the rampant ‘sexual harassment, hostility, and undermining’ of women by an ‘all boys club’) [16]. This study found that FLW need mechanisms to confront bullying at all levels of their organizations if they are going to meet goals of improving diversity in the workforce, including having influential voice in decision-making.

Drawing on Indigenous cultural and spiritually safe practices can be beneficial and even integral for mental health and well-being. Little has been written about the use of Indigenous spiritual practices in the context of group therapy for Indigenous and non-Indigenous frontline workers. This study contributed connections between some western psychology and Indigenous practices, signalling an important area to explore further.

There were limitations to sample diversity, and future studies should sample for Indigenous frontline workers specifically. Less diversity in the sample could have provided more category-specific knowledge; more sample diversity could have more external validity and transferability to a larger variety of settings. Readers may find certain aspects (but not necessarily all aspects) transferable to other settings. Research is needed to discern how best to support women, Indigenous people, 2SLGBTQI+, and other minority FLW, including in ways not limited to individual-level interventions, such as organizational behaviour and transformation.
