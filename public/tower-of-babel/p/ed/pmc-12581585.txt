A rare case report of activated PI3K delta syndrome (APDS): diagnostic pitfalls
Authors: Mohamad Qazreen Ahmad Shawaludin, Zarina Thasneem Zainudeen, Fahisham Taib, Azlan Husin, Adli Ali, Intan Juliana Abd Hamid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionActivated PI3Kδ Syndrome (APDS) is a rare inborn error of immunity characterized by recurrent infections, lymphoproliferation, and autoimmunity. It results from mutations in the phosphoinositide 3-kinase delta (PI3Kδ) signalling pathway, leading to either gain-of-function (APDS1) or loss-of-function (APDS2) phenotypes. Clinical presentation ranges from asymptomatic to severe, depending on the underlying genetic defect. Malignancy, particularly lymphoma, represents the most frequent and life-threatening complication. Due to overlapping features with other primary immunodeficiencies, APDS is often misdiagnosed as combined immunodeficiency. Early molecular testing, particularly genetic analysis, is therefore crucial for accurate diagnosis. Although management remains complex and heterogeneous, there is growing interest in targeted approaches, particularly PI3Kδ-specific therapy.Case reportWe describe a 12-year-old boy with recurrent respiratory and ear infections since infancy, accompanied by persistent lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, and thrombocytopenia. Initial immunological evaluation suggested combined immunodeficiency, and prophylactic antibiotics were initiated, but genetic testing was deferred due to mild clinical features and parental reluctance. Following multiple years of enduring symptoms, genetic investigation identified a PIK3CD mutation leading in a missense alteration p.Glu1021Lys, confirming APDS 1. The patient was subsequently commenced on immunoglobulin replacement therapy and consulted for consideration of immunosuppressive and targeted pathway blocker therapy.ConclusionThis case highlights the diagnostic challenges of APDS, arising from both overlapping clinical features with other immunodeficiencies and external factors such as cost and parental decision-making. Early genetic testing facilitates accurate diagnosis, and timely recognition of APDS enables appropriate management, including immunosuppressive, targeted therapies and, in selected cases, potentially curative hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.Keywords: Activated PI3K delta syndrome, Inborn error of immunity, Recurrent infections, Hyper IgM syndrome, Primary immunodeficiency

Introduction

Activated PI3K Delta Syndrome (APDS) is a rare autosomal dominant inborn error of immunity (IEI) characterized by a broad spectrum of clinical phenotypes, primarily including recurrent infections, lymphoproliferation, autoimmunity, and failure to thrive [1, 2]. It was first described as a combined immunodeficiency resulting from a gain-of-function (GOF) or loss of function (LOF) mutation in the phosphoinositide 3-kinase delta (PI3Kδ) gene signalling pathway [3, 4]. These mutations lead to overactivation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR/S6K pathway in leukocytes, resulting in excessive cellular proliferation and immune dysregulation. APDS is categorized into two subtypes based on the specific gene affected. APDS1 is caused by heterozygous pathogenic gain-of-function variants in the PIK3CD gene. In contrast, APDS2 results from heterozygous pathogenic loss-of-function variants in the PIK3R1 gene [2, 5]. The true prevalence of Activated PI3K Delta Syndrome (APDS) remains unknown and it was estimated to be approximately 1–2 affected individuals per million [1, 5]. The severity and clinical manifestations of APDS may vary widely among affected individuals. Symptoms may present as early as infancy, typically beginning with recurrent sino-pulmonary infections, and may later progress to include features of lymphoproliferation and autoimmunity [6]. The initial clinical manifestations of APDS may overlap with those of other inborn errors of immunity and are often misdiagnosed as Common Variable Immunodeficiency (CVID) or Hyper-IgM syndrome. A molecular genetic study should be conducted to confirm the diagnosis, which identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant indicative of Activated PI3K Delta Syndrome. Conventional management of Activated PI3Kδ Syndrome (APDS) includes prophylactic antibiotics, immunoglobulin replacement therapy (IRT), immunosuppressive agents, and in selected cases, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). More recently, there has been growing interest in novel therapeutic approaches and trial, particularly PI3Kδ-specific targeted therapy. In this article, we report a case of Activated PI3Kδ Syndrome (APDS) that illustrates the diagnostic challenges arising from overlapping clinical features with other immunodeficiencies, variable disease severity, and external factors such as cost and parental decision-making. We emphasize that early genetic testing is crucial for establishing an accurate diagnosis, while timely recognition of APDS enables appropriate management, including recent targeted therapies and, in selected cases, potentially curative hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

Case study

We report the case of a 12-year-old boy, born to non-consanguineous parents, with no previous family history of immunodeficiency. He was born at 37 weeks with no significant antenatal and postnatal history. He is the fifth out of seven siblings, all of whom are reportedly healthy. Since the age of 9 months, he experienced recurrent hospital admissions for respiratory tract infections, requiring antibiotic therapy and oxygen support. He also suffered from frequent symptomatic otitis media, approximately 3 to 4 episodes annually, alongside recurrent rhinosinusitis, occasionally complicated by epistaxis. These infections typically responded to antibiotics, ear drops, and nasal sprays. In addition, he had a history of delayed recovery of shingles complicated with hypertrophic keloid over the left scapula and frequent flares of eczematous rashes, particularly involving the neck, flexural areas, and bilateral hands. These rashes were pruritic and often triggered by environmental factors. Over time, he exhibited faltering growth, with positive findings of lymphadenopathy and hepatosplenomegaly. Notably, there were no reports of recurrent abscesses or joint infections. Upon clinical evaluation, he exhibited no dysmorphic characteristics and lacked phenotypic indicators of neurodevelopmental disorders. His developmental milestones were appropriate for age and comparable to those of his siblings. No other evidence of neuropsychiatric manifestations was observed.

He was first evaluated at the Immunology Clinic at the age of 2 years and 5 months for recurrent infections and suspected primary immunodeficiency. His initial investigations during the first clinical immunology review in 2014 revealed lymphopenia with thrombocytopenia, and peripheral blood film showed the absence of blast cells (Table 1). The serum IgM levels were elevated at 2.92 g/L (normal range: 0.31–1.79), accompanied by normal IgA and IgG levels, diminished B cells counts at 283 (normal range: 1800–3000) and T cells counts at 907 (normal range: 1200–2600). Additionally, there was a suboptimal response to the specific antibody response to the pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23) (Table 2), suggesting a differential diagnosis of Combined Immunodeficiency. Due to the persistent thrombocytopenia and hepatosplenomegaly, Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis was contemplated` as a differential diagnosis. However, the parent declined further investigations, including bone marrow aspiration, trephine biopsy and genetic testing. They also did not consent to immunoglobulin replacement therapy despite multiple counselling sessions provided by the attending clinical immunologist. The patient was commenced on oral azithromycin prophylaxis at a dose of 5 mg/kg per dose, administered three times weekly. During prophylactic antibiotic treatment, his infections remained partly controlled (2–3 episodes per year), manifesting only as mild episodes of rhinosinusitis and respiratory tract infections, none of which required hospital admission.

Unfortunately, the patient defaulted on follow-up from 2020 to 2023 due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Follow-up resumed in 2024, when the family sought relocation to an alternative immunology centre due to logistical difficulties. Clinically, he continued to experience persistent rhinosinusitis, with recurrent ear infections occurring two to three times per year, as well as mild lower respiratory tract infections, all of which were managed at home. Serial monitoring demonstrated persistent cytopenia, lymphopenia, lymphadenopathy, and hepatosplenomegaly. Repeated follow-up investigations consistently revealed persistent cytopenia without blast cells, along with persistently low B- and T-cell counts (Tables 1 and 2). Whole-exome sequencing was subsequently performed at a commercial laboratory, funded by his parents’ employer. The test attained a mean coverage of 156.95x with a depth exceeding 20x at the variant locus and identified a heterozygous missense variant in PIK3CD, reported as NM_005026.5:c.3061G >A, resulting in NP_005017.3(p.Glu1021Lys). The variant is located in exon 24, at the genomic coordinate GRCh38 NC_000001.11:g.9726972G >A. The E1021K variant was classified as pathogenic according to ACMG/AMP criteria [5]. This variant aligns with a molecular diagnosis of Activated PI3K Delta Syndrome type 1 (APDS1) and has been documented in previously affected individuals [3–5, 7].

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy was initiated at a dose of 0.4 g/kg every four weeks, following detailed discussions with the parents. He tolerated the infusions well, with no adverse events, and achieved a target trough level of 8 g/L. He remained small for his age but continued to demonstrate growth, with both height and weight at the 25th centile. Since initiation of IVIG therapy, the frequency of symptoms has decreased, with no recent hospital admissions or episodes of invasive infection. School attendance also improved, with no prolonged absenteeism reported. Ongoing surveillance and monitoring for complications included a CECT thorax, which showed no dilated or thickened airways and no signet ring sign, indicating no evidence of bronchiectasis. A formal hearing assessment with pure tone audiometry was normal, while a peripheral blood film demonstrated no evidence of blasts or lymphoma. The patient was also referred to a psychologist for further assessment of intellectual functioning. The parents were counselled regarding the potential use of immunosuppressive agents and recently available targeted therapy, however, they remained undecided due to financial constraints and limited access to such treatment options. Parents received genetic counselling and health education regarding APDS; however, genetic testing for family members was not conducted due to inadequate resources.

Discussion

Activated PI3K Delta Syndrome (APDS) is a rare autosomal dominant inborn error of immunity (IEI) caused by overactivation of the PI3K–AKT–mTOR pathway, leading to impaired T- and B-cell differentiation, progressive lymphopenia, hypogammaglobulinemia, and elevated IgM [1, 2]. Clinically, it presents with early-onset recurrent infections, chronic lymphoproliferation, and autoimmunity, though severity varies widely, from severe childhood disease to asymptomatic adults [5]. The phenotype overlaps with other IEIs such as CVID, CTLA-4 deficiency, NFKB1 deficiency, and STAT3 gain-of-function, often leading to misdiagnosis. Indeed, Rivalta B. et al. described cases initially diagnosed as hyper-IgM syndrome, CVID, or unspecified hypogammaglobulinemia, underscoring the diagnostic challenges [6].

The suspicion of inborn errors of immunity (IEI) and the diagnosis of APDS are usually delayed [8]. Typically, symptoms of APDS present in early childhood with recurrent infections, particularly involving the lungs, sinuses, and ears. In the most recent cohort reported by Barzaghi F. et al., the classical onset was characterized by recurrent respiratory infections, often mild but frequent, and associated with airway remodelling [8]. Coulter et al. reported that recurrent respiratory tract infections were the most common presentation, observed in 98% of patients, with onset before the age of 7 years. This was followed by recurrent otitis media (48%) and rhinosinusitis (45%) [9, 10]. Similarly, Jamee et al. found that the most frequent infections in APDS patients were pneumonia (43.6%), otitis media (28.8%), sinusitis (25.9%), diarrhoea (16%), and eye infections (10.7%) [2]. A genotype–phenotype correlation has also been suggested, with sinusitis more commonly reported in APDS1, while pneumonia is more frequently observed in APDS2. Notably, bronchiectasis, a marker of chronic pulmonary damage, has been reported in 28–60% of patients across various cohorts, with a mean age at diagnosis of 8.6 years [1, 2].

Overactivation of PI3Kδ leads to abnormal proliferation of white blood cells, resulting in non-neoplastic lymphoproliferative conditions such as recurrent chronic lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, and hepatomegaly. Non-clonal lymphoproliferation is observed in up to 89% of APDS cases, with lymphadenopathy is more common in APDS2, whereas hepatomegaly is reported more frequently in APDS1 [8, 9]. Besides benign lymphoid proliferations, increased B-cell proliferation combined with genetic instability in lymphocytes contributes to the development of malignancy, which typically occurs later in the course of APDS [10]. Lymphoma develops in approximately 12–25% of APDS patients, most often diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (5.7%), classical Hodgkin lymphoma (3.7%), or marginal zone B-cell lymphoma (2.0%), typically presenting in adolescence or early adulthood, and is more frequent in the context of chronic Epstein-Barr virus infection [2, 11–13].

Immunophenotypically, APDS is characterised by reduced B- and T-cell counts, elevated IgM, and low IgG/IgA. Elevated IgM level, reported in 82% of patients, supports PI3Kδ mutation screening even when CD40 ligand expression is normal [13, 14]. Nonetheless, cases without IgM elevation have been misdiagnosed as combined immunodeficiency, emphasizing the importance of early genetic testing in patients with combined immunodeficiency and immune dysregulation [15].

Management of APDS depends on disease severity and includes observation, prophylactic antibiotics, immunoglobulin replacement therapy (IRT), immunosuppressive agents, targeted therapy, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) [14, 16]. Prophylactic antibiotics (commonly azithromycin or trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole) and IRT reduce recurrent bacterial infections, though they offer limited protection against viral, autoimmune, and lymphoproliferative complications. Cohort studies by Coulter et al. (APDS1) and Elkaim et al. (APDS2) show that up to 89% of patients require long-term IRT with significant bacterial infection, starting at 0.4 g/kg and increasing to 0.6 g/kg in those with bronchiectasis [14, 17]. Combination therapy is beneficial in patients with established lung disease. In the reported case, prophylactic azithromycin alone controlled infections, but IRT may provide added benefit for sinusitis and long-term respiratory protection [14, 17]. Immunosuppressive agents and targeted therapies such as sirolimus (rapamycin), rituximab, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine, and cyclosporine have shown benefit in managing autoimmunity, lymphoproliferative disease, and complications following HSCT. Sirolimus, an mTOR inhibitor and T-cell–directed therapy, reduces ribosomal protein S6 phosphorylation and has been shown to diminish hepatosplenomegaly and lymphadenopathy effectively. According to the European Society for Immunodeficiency (ESID) APDS registry cohort, sirolimus was the most frequently prescribed immunosuppressive medication. In this cohort, eight patients achieved complete remission with sirolimus treatment, however, five patients discontinued therapy due to adverse effects, most notably severe oral ulcers [11, 18].

More recently, targeted therapies such as Leniolisib, a selective p110δ inhibitor, have been introduced, with the potential benefits of reducing the risk of lymphoma. A randomized controlled trial of leniolisib administered orally at 70 mg twice daily for 12 weeks in APDS patients demonstrated significant clinical benefits, including reduction in lymphadenopathy, increased naïve T-cell populations, improved spleen size, normalization of lymphocyte subsets, and correction of cytopenia, with no serious adverse effects reported [19]. However, Conti et al. described a case of Hodgkin lymphoma that developed during leniolisib therapy, which was interrupted due to multifactorial causes, including supply shortages during the COVID-19 pandemic; nevertheless, the treatment resulted in notable improvements in lymphadenopathy, spleen volume, and naïve B-cell proportion [20]. The Italian cohort analysed treatment outcomes and found that Rapamycin, used in 36% of patients, effectively controlled lymphoproliferation, whereas the selective PI3Kδ inhibitor leniolisib, given to 32% of patients, proved advantageous for both infections and immune dysregulation [8]. Overall, leniolisib appears to offer greater therapeutic efficacy and a more favourable safety profile compared to sirolimus, which is frequently associated with severe side effects such as oral ulceration, and shows a less satisfactory response, particularly in intestinal disease and cytopenia, thereby, significantly affecting patients’ quality of life [17].

Surveillance in APDS should focus on bronchiectasis and lymphoma, the major long-term complications. Bronchiectasis monitoring includes clinical evaluation for chronic cough or recurrent infections and confirmation with high resolution CT-scan (HRCT), the diagnostic gold standard [21]. Lymphoma surveillance requires regular clinical examination, blood tests (FBP, LDH, EBV load), and imaging (ultrasound, MRI, or CT/PET-CT if suspicious). Early biopsy is essential for atypical nodes. In the absence of formal guidelines, most experts recommend 6–12 monthly specialist reviews.

In this case, the patient presented with recurrent sinopulmonary infections and thrombocytopenia from early childhood, but the disease lacked the severity that typically prompts urgent genetic evaluation. The delay in achieving a molecular diagnosis was influenced by the unconventional disease course, parental initial hesitance, and financial limitation restricting access to genetic testing, which in our context necessitates on out-of-pocket payment. This highlights a frequently under-recognized barrier in paediatric care including the influence of family readiness, cultural beliefs, and perceived clinical urgency in shaping diagnostic trajectories. While supportive therapies such as prophylactic antibiotics provided partial infection control, the absence of a confirmed diagnosis restricted access to more targeted management strategies, including mTOR inhibition, PI3Kδ-targeted therapy, or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) where appropriate. Importantly, this case underscores the critical role of ongoing surveillance and monitoring, particularly for bronchiectasis and lymphoma, which represent significant long-term complications of APDS. Structured follow-up with regular clinical review, laboratory evaluation, and imaging where indicated is therefore essential to optimize outcomes and guide timely therapeutic interventions.

Conclusion

APDS is a rare primary immunodeficiency that is difficult to recognize due to overlapping features with other syndromes. Genetic testing is essential for timely diagnosis, as delays can lead to complications such as bronchiectasis, autoimmunity, and lymphoma. While supportive therapy may help, targeted treatments and structured surveillance are critical to improve long-term outcomes.
