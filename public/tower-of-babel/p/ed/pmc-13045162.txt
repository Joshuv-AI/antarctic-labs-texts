Association between C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index and risk of stroke in different renal function status: a national cohort study
Authors: Wei Xian, Tong Wu, Xin-Xin Zhao, Ye Yang, Xiao-Ying Li, Ya-Li Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index (CTI), a novel biomarker integrating inflammation and insulin resistance, has been linked to cardiovascular disease. However, its association with stroke risk across varying levels of renal function remains unclear.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 8,808 participants aged ≥ 45 years from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), covering the period 2011 to 2018. CTI was calculated as 0.412×Ln (CRP [mg/L]) + Ln (TG [mg/dL]×FPG [mg/dL])/2. Multivariable Cox models and restricted cubic splines were employed to assess the association between CTI and incident stroke. We stratified the analysis by estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and introduced interaction terms between CTI and eGFR levels to evaluate potential effect modification.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 59.48 (9.41) years, and 4098 (46.5%) were male. During a median follow-up of 7 years, 404 (4.6%) stroke events occurred. In the fully adjusted model, each 1-unit increase in CTI was associated with a 15% higher stroke risk [hazard ratios (HR) = 1.15, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 1.00-1.32]. A significant positive linear dose-response relationship was observed (P overall <0.001, P non−linear= 0.289). Notably, we observed significant interaction between CTI and eGFR levels on stroke risk (P = 0.037). The association was markedly stronger in individuals with mildly reduced renal function (eGFR 60–89 mL/min/1.73 m²), where the highest CTI quartile (Q4) had an HR of 2.73 (95% CI: 1.16–6.40) compared to the Q1. Conversely, no significant associations were observed in participants with preserved (eGFR ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m²) or moderately-to-severely reduced renal function (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²).ConclusionElevated CTI levels are associated with an increased risk of stroke in middle-aged and older Chinese adults, particularly among individuals with mild renal impairment. By combining inflammatory and metabolic markers, CTI might offer potential clinical value for risk assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03135-x.

Introduction

Stroke represents a leading cause of death and long-term disability, especially in low and middle income countries [1, 2]. In 2021, there were 93.8 million prevalent and 11.9 million incident strokes globally, with a concerning rise in incidence among younger populations [1, 3]. It is predicted that by 2050, the mortality rate and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) due to stroke will reach 9.7 million and 189.3 million, respectively [3]. In China, stroke is the foremost cause of death and associated with the highest DALYs lost of any disease, with over 2 million new cases diagnosed annually [4, 5]. Although the age-standardised incidence and mortality rates have decreased, the overall burden of stroke is expected to increase further as a result of population ageing, an ongoing high prevalence of risk factors (e.g., hypertension), and inadequate management [2, 4]. Therefore, the identification and management of modifiable risk factors are crucial for primary prevention of stroke.

Insulin resistance (IR) is a pathophysiological state characterized by diminished or impaired sensitivity of target organs or tissues to insulin that affects glucose uptake and utilization [6–8]. Accumulating evidence has highlighted that IR is a significant risk factor for a spectrum of metabolic diseases, cardiovascular diseases (CVD) and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [9–18]. The hyperinsulinemic euglycemic clamp (HEC) test (gold standard) and homeostatic model assessment of IR (HOMA-IR) are methods for assessing IR. However, the expensiveness, intrusiveness, and complexities of the HEC test, as well as the requirements for fasting insulin of the HOMA-IR have limited its extensive use in clinical practice and epidemiological research [6, 19, 20]. Consequently, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, a simple indicator derived from fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and triglycerides (TG), has emerged as a practical alternative. Emerging evidence has confirmed that the TyG index is not only associated with the risk of CVD [21–23] but also demonstrates a higher area under the curve (AUC) than HOMA-IR [24]. Furthermore, inflammation contributes to stroke pathogenesis by promoting atherosclerosis, damaging the function of vascular endothelium, and increasing thrombosis [25–27]. C-reactive protein (CRP), an indicator of systemic inflammation, is closely related to the risk of stroke [28–30]. Therefore, the composite C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index (CTI), developed by Ruan et al. [31], simultaneously reflects IR and inflammation status and has been linked to poor prognosis [31] and mortality of cancer [32], as well as to the incidence and mortality of CVD [33, 34].

Given that renal dysfunction, as indicated by the reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), is an established risk factor for stroke and can exacerbate both inflammation and metabolic disturbances, it may modify the relationship between CTI and stroke risk [35, 36]. However, there are few large-scale epidemiological studies that investigated the association between CTI and stroke risk, particularly examining potential effect modification by renal function. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the association between CTI and the risk of stroke under different eGFR levels utilizing data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS).

Methods

Study design and subjects

The CHARLS is a national longitudinal survey designed to collect comprehensive information ranging from socio-economic status to health conditions of the population aged 45 years and older in China (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/). The study employed a multi-level stratified sampling method and covered 150 counties or regions of 28 provinces in China with data collection waves in 2011– 2012 (baseline, wave 1), 2013 (wave 2), 2015 (wave 3), and 2018 (wave 4). Biological samples were collected during waves 1 and 3. Detailed information has been described previously [37]. The Ethics Committee of Peking University approved the CHARLS study (IRB00001052-11015) and all participants signed informed consent forms.

The participant selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. The 11,847 participants who provided blood information in baseline survey were identified. We excluded 973 participants who were aged < 45 years or had a history of stroke or cancer at baseline. Then, 2,066 participants were further removed due to missing data on CRP, FPG, TG, eGFR, or gender. We also excluded participants with extreme values of systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP) and body mass index (BMI), defined as values less or more than 3 standard deviations (SD) from the mean. Finally, 8,808 participants were included in the final analysis.

Calculation of CTI

The TyG index was calculated as Ln (TG [mg/dL]×FPG [mg/dL])/2 [38]. The CTI index was calculated by the following formula [31]: CTI = 0.412×Ln (CRP [mg/L]) + Ln (TG [mg/dL]×FPG [mg/dL])/2.

Assessment of incident stroke

The outcome of this study was the incidence of stroke, which was assessed by asking the question: “Have you ever been diagnosed with a stroke by a physician?” Survival time was calculated from the interval between the last interview and the one in which the stroke was first recorded or the date of last follow-up for subjects who did not suffer from stroke.

Assessments of covariates

The structured questionnaires were used by trained interviewers to collect the sociodemographic and health-related data. Demographic information included gender, age, residence, education level, and marital status. The health-related data encompassed lifestyle information, such as smoking status and alcohol consumption, as well as disease history of heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Information on medication use for hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes was also recorded. Laboratory tests were analyzed by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, including FPG, total cholesterol (TC), TG, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), uric acid, creatinine, and glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c).

Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, or taking antihypertensive medications, or self-reported hypertension diagnosed by a doctor. Diabetes was defined as FPG ≥ 126 mg/dL, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or current use of antidiabetic medications, or self-reported diabetes diagnosed by a doctor. Dyslipidemia was defined as TG ≥ 150 mg/dL, TC ≥ 240 mg/dL, HDL-C < 40 mg/dL, LDL-C ≥ 160 mg/dL, current use of lipid-lowering drugs, or self-reported dyslipidemia diagnosed by a physician. A history of heart disease was based on self-report. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) / height2 (m2). The eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m2 ) was calculated according to the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) [39].

Statistical analysis

The extent of missing data in this study is detailed in Table S1. To mitigate the potential bias caused by missing values in data analysis, we assume that the missing is random. We utilized the multiple imputation by chained equations method based on baseline characteristics to establish a complete dataset. Mean with SD were used for normally distributed continuous variables and median with interquartile range were utilized for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Variance analysis and Kruskal–Wallis test were used to assess the differences between groups, respectively. Categorical variables are presented as numbers (percentages), with statistical evaluations conducted by the Chi-square test. Participants were categorized into four groups according to the quartiles of the CTI: Q1 ≤ 8.15; 8.15 < Q2≤8.68; 8.68 < Q3≤9.25; Q4 > 9.25. The CTI was also modeled as a continuous variable to enhance the robustness of the results. Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests were employed to evaluate the incidence of stroke across groups. To assess potential collinearity between the CTI and other covariates, tolerance values and variance inflation factors (VIFs) were examined. The analysis indicated that the VIFs for TC (VIF = 16.305), TG (VIF = 7.982) and LDL-C (VIF = 13.33) exceeded a value of 5, which led to their exclusion from the multivariate model (Table S4). Multivariable Cox regression models were used to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) with the lowest quartile as the reference group. Three models were developed to facilitate a comprehensive analysis: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for gender, age, marital status (married or other), education level (no formal education, elementary school or below, middle or high school, or college or above), smoking status (never, current, or former), and drinking status (never or current); and Model 3 contained additional adjustments for hypertension (no or yes), dyslipidemia (no or yes), diabetes (no or yes), heart disease (no or yes), hypertension medications (no or yes), dyslipidemia medications (no or yes), diabetes medications (no or yes), BMI, and eGFR. The specific coding of categorical variables is provided in Table S2. Fully adjusted restricted cubic splines (RCS) analysis was performed to explore the dose–response relationship between CTI and stroke risk. The receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curves and the AUC were established to assess the predictive value of CTI, CRP and TyG index on the incidence of stroke. Furthermore, the relationship between the CTI and stroke risk across different eGFR levels (< 60, 60–89, and ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m²) was assessed. Interactions were analyzed applying multivariable-adjusted Cox models through including cross-product terms between CTI and eGFR levels.

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on gender (male and female), age (< 60 years and ≥ 60 years), smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, heart disease and BMI (< 24, 24–28, and ≥ 28 kg/m2 ). Several sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the results by excluding participants with hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, heart disease and missing data. Logistic regression models were also used to explore the relationship between CTI and stroke incidence. Additionally, to address potential over-adjustment bias, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using Model 3 without adjustments for drugs. All analyses were conducted by R software (version 4.5.1) and SPSS (version 21.0). A two-sided alpha level of 0.05 was chosen for the statistical significance of all the analyses.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the population

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the 8,808 participants stratified by CTI quartiles. The mean (SD) age of participants was 59.48 (9.41) years, and 4098 (46.5%) were male. The number (proportion) of participants across eGFR categories (< 60, 60–89, and ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m²) were 200 (2.3%), 2253 (25.6%), 6355 (72.2%), respectively. Compared with participants in the lowest quartile (Q1), individuals with higher quartiles of the CTI were more likely to be female, never smokers and drinkers, have higher BMI, SBP and DBP values, and tended to have hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia and heart disease (all P < 0.05). Association between the CTI and the risk of stroke

During a median follow-up of 7 years, 404 (4.6%) participants experienced incident stroke. The incidence of stroke increased progressively across CTI quartiles: 61 (0.7%) in Q1, 94 (1.1%) in Q2, 118 (1.3%) in Q3, and 131 (1.5%) in Q4. The incidence of stroke increased from Q1 to Q4 groups in the Kaplan – Meier cumulative incidence curve (log-rank test P < 0.001, Fig. 2A). In the full-adjusted multivariate model (Model 3), a 1-unit increase in CTI was associated with a 15% higher risk of stroke (HR = 1.15, 95% CI = 1.00-1.32) (Table 2). Compared to Q1, the risk of stroke was significantly elevated in Q2 (HR = 1.42, 95% CI = 1.02–1.97) and Q3 (HR = 1.53, 95% CI = 1.10–2.13). The risk estimate for Q4 was also higher but not statistically significant (HR = 1.37, 95% CI = 0.96–1.97), which may suggest a threshold effect or statistical fluctuation at the highest exposure level (Table 2). The RCS analysis revealed a significant positive linear relationship between CTI and stroke risk in total participants (P for non-linear = 0.289) (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, the ROC curves indicated that baseline CTI demonstrated the highest diagnostic efficacy of stroke incidence (AUC = 0.583, 95% CI = 0.556–0.611), followed by TyG index (AUC = 0.571, 95% CI = 0.544–0.599), and CRP (AUC = 0.558, 95% CI = 0.529–0.586) (Figure S1).

Association between CTI and stroke risk according to eGFR levels

As shown in Table 3, we identified significant interaction between CTI and eGFR levels on stroke risk (P for interaction = 0.037). Stratified analysis revealed a significant association between CTI and stroke risk in individuals with mildly reduced renal function (eGFR 60–89 mL/min/1.73 m²), where participants in the highest CTI quartile (Q4) had a significantly elevated risk compared to Q1 (HR = 2.73, 95% CI = 1.16–6.40). However, no significant associations were found in individuals with preserved renal function (eGFR ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m² ) or those with moderately-to-severely reduced renal function (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) (all P > 0.05). The cumulative incidence of stroke was significantly different across CTI quartiles in the eGFR ≥ 90 and 60–89 mL/min/1.73 m² stratum as shown by the Kaplan–Meier curves (all log-rank test P < 0.05, Fig. 2B and C), but not in the eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² stratum (log-rank test P = 0.387, Fig. 2D). And a significant linear relationship between the CTI and stroke events was also found in RCS analysis (P for non-linear > 0.05) (Fig. 3B, C and D).

Subgroup analysis

To further explore the relationship between CTI and stroke risk, subgroup analysis stratified by demographical and disease history was carried out (Fig. 4). The direction of the majority of subgroup analyses aligned with the primary results, with most results were statistically significant. An increase in CTI levels was significantly associated with risk of stroke among females, never smokers and drinkers, individuals with a BMI ≥ 28 kg/m², and those with hypertension (all P < 0.05). Additionally, in individuals without dyslipidemia, the HR and corresponding 95% CI was 1.42 (1.10–1.83).

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the findings, we conducted multiple sensitivity analyses and the results remained largely consistent. Firstly, after excluding participants with missing data, the positive association between CTI (continuous and categorical quartiles) and stroke risk persisted (HR = 1.42 95% CI = 1.02–1.97 for Q2, HR = 1.53 95% CI = 1.10–2.13 for Q3) (Table S5). Secondly, after removing those with heart disease, hypertension, diabetes (HR = 1.61 95% CI = 1.13–2.29 for Q3), and dyslipidemia (HR = 1.68 95% CI = 1.09–2.61 for Q3), results did not materially change (Table S6, S7, S8, and S9). Thirdly, when using Logistic regression models, the results remained stable (Table S10, S11, S12, S13, and S14). Finally, in the multivariable Cox models without including adjustments for drugs, the results also remained consistent with the main analysis (HR = 1.54 95% CI = 1.11–2.14 for Q3) (Table S15 and S16).

Discussion

Summary of main results

This large prospective cohort study demonstrated that a higher CTI was associated with an increased risk of stroke in middle-aged and older Chinese population, and the RCS analysis revealed a linear correlation between CTI and the occurrence of stroke. This association was substantially stronger and statistically significant in individuals with mildly reduced renal function (eGFR 60–89 mL/min/1.73 m²), with a significant interaction between CTI and eGFR levels on stroke risk.

Comparison to prior studies

CTI was initially developed to assess the prognosis of cancer patients [31]. This index synthesizes the biomarkers of inflammation (CRP) and IR (TyG), both of which have been broadly proven to be associated with the occurrence and progression of stroke [40–43]. Previous studies have shown that higher CTI levels were linked to an increased risk of stroke, particularly in individuals with normal glucose regulation (HR = 1.33, 95% CI = 1.11–1.59) and prediabetes mellitus (HR = 1.20, 95% CI = 1.04–1.39) [44]. And in hypertensive patients, an increase with each CTI unit was associated with a 21% increase stroke risk (HR = 1.21, 95% CI = 1.08–1.37) [45]. In our study, the point estimate suggests a biologically plausible 15% increase in stroke risk per unit CTI (HR = 1.15), which is consistent with the fact that the CTI may elevate stroke risk through the effects of inflammation and IR on thrombosis and atherosclerosis [46]. Moreover, inflammation and IR interact bidirectionally [47]. However, the 95% CI (1.00-1.32) was of borderline statistical significance, with the lower bound equal to the null value. This limits the robustness of the evidence and further investigation in independent well-powered cohorts is essential to clarify the reliability and magnitude of this association.

Additionally, elevated CTI levels were significantly associated with CVD incidence and mortality in 8,679 adults from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey [33] as well as with recurrent cardiovascular risks in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention [48]. Furthermore, higher levels of cumulative CTI also increase the stroke risk. Those subjects with consistently high CTI levels exhibited an even greater risk [49, 50]. These results indicate that CTI can serve as an indicator for identifying patients at high risk of stroke and ROC analysis in our study demonstrated a modest but superior predictive capacity for stroke incidence compared to CRP or TyG index alone.

A key finding of our study is the significant association between CTI and stroke risk being amplified in participants with mild reduction in renal function (eGFR 60–89 mL/min/1.73 m²). Although we did not observe a statistically significant association between CTI and stroke risk in the subgroup with moderately-to-severely reduced renal function (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²), the direction of the HR (1.63) was consistent with the overall findings. This lack of significance is likely attributable to the limited sample size in this stratum, which resulted in insufficient statistical power to detect an association. Future studies with larger cohorts of patients with advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) are warranted to confirm or refute our findings in this specific population. Previous study has revealed a positive linear association between TyG-BMI index and CVD incidence in individuals with Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrom stage 0–3 [51], and decreased eGFR has been shown to mediate 29.6% of the associations between the TyG index and CVD [52]. Xu et al. reported that elevated CTI was significantly associated with stroke risk in individuals at CKM stages 2 (HR 1.27, 95% CI 1.04–1.56) and 3 (HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.04–1.50) [46]. Additionally, a significant dose-response relationship has been observed between CTI and hypertension with long-term stroke in CKM stage 0–3 populations, with the highest CTI-hypertension group exhibiting a fully adjusted HR of 3.19 (95% CI 2.62–3.88) for stroke compared to the lowest group [53]. CKD is characterized by a state of chronic inflammation and profound metabolic disturbances [54, 55]. Meta-analyses of cohort studies and trials indicate that reduced eGFR increases the risk of stroke by 40% [56], and even mild reductions in eGFR are associated with substantial increases in cardiovascular risk [55]. This might be attributable to a cluster of shared cardiovascular and metabolic risk factors, and the impaired renal function may potentiate the deleterious effects of the metabolic-inflammatory axis measured by CTI, leading to accelerated vascular damage and atherosclerosis. In contrast, no significant association was found in those with preserved renal function (eGFR ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m²), this may be attributable to the relative absence of significant metabolic or cardiovascular risk factors in this group, or it may indicate that a pathological threshold has not yet been reached.

It is worth noting that the subgroup analyses showed a significant association between CTI and stroke risk in individuals without dyslipidemia. This might be attributed to the fact that the chronic low-grade inflammation indicated by the C-reactive protein component within the CTI emerges as a core non-lipid pathway driving stroke risk in individuals without dyslipidemia [57]. In this subgroup, IR, synergizing with concomitant systemic inflammation [58], might drive cerebrovascular injury by inducing endothelial dysfunction, promoting a pro-thrombotic state, and exacerbating atherosclerotic plaque instability [59–61]. In contrast, among individuals with pre-existing dyslipidemia, the potent atherogenic effects of lipotoxicity may overshadow the independent contribution of the inflammatory and metabolic components reflected by CTI, potentially explaining the attenuated association [62, 63]. This finding underscores the value of CTI as an integrative marker, whose incorporated information on IR and inflammation provides significant independent prognostic utility for stroke risk stratification in specific patient phenotypes.

Mechanisms

Several interconnected pathophysiological mechanisms may underlie the complex correlation between IR, inflammation, renal dysfunction, and cardiovascular damage. First, IR can lead to an imbalance in glucose metabolism, and the excessive intracellular glucose flux triggers mitochondrial superoxide production, which intensifies inflammation and oxidative stress. In addition, IR impairs endothelial function by inducing an increase in the production of glycosylation products and free radicals, leading to nitric oxide inactivation, and by inducing an overproduction of reactive oxygen species, which in turn may contribute to the development of atherosclerosis [51]. Furthermore, in the early stages of atherosclerosis, IR may lead to an increase in atherosclerotic plaques through the downregulation of the insulin receptor-Akt1 signalling pathway, causing a decrease in the activation of endothelial nitric oxide synthase in arterial endothelial cells and an increase in the expression of vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 [51]. On the other hand, IR also plays an important role in metabolic disease-induced chronic kidney disease, potentially driven by chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and post-translational modifications of signal-transduction proteins. Insulin causes vasodilation by enhancing endothelial nitric oxide production through activation of the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase pathway. Conversely, under insulin-resistant states, the mitogen-activated protein kinase pathway stimulates vasoconstriction and causes kidney vascular damage [52].

Of note, a critical finding of this study is the effect modification by renal function on the association between CTI and stroke. The amplified association between CTI and stroke in mild renal insufficiency stems from a synergistic pathophysiology. First, renal insufficiency exacerbates the CTI-indicated metabolic dysfunction by impairing insulin clearance and compounding hyperinsulinemia [64], while concurrent uremic toxicity and mineral bone disorders directly aggravate endothelial injury and vascular calcification [65]. Second, renal insufficiency transforms the chronic inflammatory state reflected by CTI into a severe, sustained burden by impairing the renal clearance of cytokines and acute-phase proteins, leading to their pathological accumulation [66]. This dual impairment—simultaneously potentiating the metabolic-toxic and inflammatory pathways—creates a profoundly vasculopathic and prothrombotic milieu [67]. Consequently, the convergence of these amplified mechanisms within individuals with renal dysfunction explains the amplified association between CTI and stroke.

Highlights and limitations

There were several notable advantages of the present study. Firstly, this study explored the correlation between CTI and the incidence of stroke according to eGFR levels for the first time. Secondly, the prospective, nationwide longitudinal design of this study, combined with its substantial sample size, enhances the reliability of our findings. Thirdly, we performed subgroup analyses to assess the consistency of results across various population characteristics, which provides valuable insights for clinical practice.

However, several limitations should be considered. Firstly, given the observational study design of the present analysis, we could not confirm the causal association among the CTI and stroke risk. Secondly, stroke diagnosis was based on self-report and the subtypes of stroke were not available, which is also one of the limitations. Thirdly, although potential confounding factors were adjusted in multivariate models, we still cannot exclude the possibility that residual or unmeasured confounders may influence the results. However, we calculated the E-value to quantify the minimum strength of association that unmeasured confounder would require to explain the observed associations [68]. The E-value for CTI based on Model 3 is 1.57, which indicates that our findings are robust. Fourthly, the study does not consider changes in CTI over time, and future research should consider the dynamic impact of function status and CTI level over time to more comprehensively assess this relationship. Fifthly, the number of participants with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² was relatively small, limiting the statistical power to detect associations in this subgroup. Therefore, it is necessary to further verify our results in this group of individuals in the future. Finally, the sample of this study is derived from general Chinese participants aged 45 years and older, further studies are needed for validation the predictive effect of CTI on stroke in other populations and health conditions.

Conclusions

In conclusion, elevated levels of CTI are associated with an increased risk of stroke, especially in individuals with mildly reduced renal function. Its modest predictive performance over CRP or TyG index alone underscores the potential clinical value in integrating inflammatory and metabolic markers for comprehensive risk assessment. These findings indicate that CTI could serve as a practical tool for improving stroke risk stratification. Future studies are needed to validate these observations in CKD patients, explore the impact of dynamic CTI changes overtime, and investigate its potential utility in guiding targeted interventions to mitigate metabolic-inflammatory risk.
