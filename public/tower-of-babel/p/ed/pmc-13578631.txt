Seen and Heard: Gender Identity and Concordance Shapes Caregiver Experiences in Pediatric Orthopaedics
Authors: Lee S. Haruno, Eliza Buttrick, Raghav Badrinath, Sulagna Sarkar, Coleen S. Sabatini, Jaysson T. Brooks, Sarah Coufal, Christine M. Goodbody, B. David Horn, John T. Lawrence, Kathleen J. Maguire, Lawrence Wells, Brendan A. Williams, Apurva S. Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric care poses unique challenges in communication and ensuring satisfaction as parents/caregivers are often the primary decision-makers. Previous studies have explored the influence of physician and patient race, ethnicity, and gender on clinical encounters. We aimed to examine the impact of physician and caregiver gender on caregiver satisfaction during pediatric orthopaedic clinic visits.MethodsAnonymous surveys were distributed to caregivers following visits with nine pediatric orthopaedic surgeons (six male, three female) at two tertiary-care children's hospitals from 2022 to 2024. Surveys included 16 questions across five domains sourced from the Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems (CAHPS) survey (4-point Likert scale). Caregivers also provided an overall physician rating (scale of 10) and demographic data. Surveys were designated as concordant or discordant (same or different caregiver–physician gender). Responses were analyzed by gender and between matched concordant-discordant visits. Validated measures and numerical scores were compared using Mann-Whitney U tests. Nonvalidated questions were compared using Fisher's tests.ResultsOverall, 482 surveys were completed, the majority by female caregivers (N = 353, 73%); male caregivers (N = 129, 27%). Household incomes differed significantly, with most female caregivers reporting an annual income between $25,000 and $50,000, while most males reported an annual income greater than $100,000 (P < .001). Overall, caregivers rated their physician highly with no significant difference between genders. Significantly more female caregivers negatively perceived physician listening quality and time spent with their children (P = .03 and .02, respectively). Female caregivers felt less involved in decision-making than male caregivers (P = .04). Analysis of 376 surveys matched by gender demonstrated that discordant encounters had a significantly higher rate of dissatisfaction (4% vs 1%) regarding discussion of care options and involvement in decisions compared to concordant visits (P = .02 and .04, respectively). There was also a significant difference (P < .01) in satisfaction between concordant and discordant visits when addressing questions.ConclusionsOverall, caregivers were satisfied with their experience. However, female caregivers reported lower satisfaction with physicians in several domains compared to males. Gender concordance improved patient satisfaction in these domains, supporting the need for improved representation of women in orthopaedic surgery.Key Concepts(1)Caregiver satisfaction was high overall in the ambulatory setting for pediatric orthopaedic surgery.(2)Caregiver/physician gender concordant clinical encounters resulted in higher reported satisfaction compared to discordant encounters.(3)Surgeons should focus on empathetic listening, understanding the impact of the condition on the patient, and the nuances of surgical discussion to build rapport and improve caregiver satisfaction.(4)Continued efforts are needed to achieve a demographic of pediatric orthopaedic surgeons that is reflective of the community and patient population at large.Level of EvidenceIV

Introduction

Previous studies have explored the influence of physician race, ethnicity, and gender on patient experience during clinical encounters. Studies have also found that female physicians better adhere to clinical guidelines and more often use preventative medicine techniques and patient-centered care approaches [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [16]. Female physician–patient gender concordance reportedly increased compliance with clinical recommendations and resulted in higher physician ratings in nonoperative adult specialties [6]. Female surgeons performing procedures on female patients also tended to result in fewer postoperative complications, irrespective of surgical specialty [4]. However, studies suggest that physician–patient gender concordance does not always affect the patient's level of provider trust [7,8].

These findings may be particularly pertinent to orthopaedic surgery, which remains among the least gender diverse specialties in medicine [9]. A recent study by Singleton et al. in pediatric orthopaedic surgery identified perceived physician empathy as the primary driver of patient satisfaction during clinical encounters. Additionally, it was reported that female surgeons were significantly more likely to achieve a higher (≥9/10) patient satisfaction score than their male counterparts [10]. Improving the percentage of females in orthopaedic surgery may thus have significant implications in advancing overall population health, strengthening patient-physician relationships, and ensuring patient access to a more representative orthopaedic workforce. Recent data suggest that women, particularly from backgrounds underrepresented in medicine, are disproportionately impacted by attrition during orthopaedic surgery residency, which may further exacerbate the availability of female surgeons [11]. Women in orthopaedics are also underrepresented academically, as they are significantly less likely to have meeting abstracts lead to successful publication [12]. Evidence supporting the value of female physicians in improving patient care underscores the importance of recruiting, retaining, and promoting women in orthopaedics.

Pediatric specialties in particular pose unique challenges in communication, building rapport, and ensuring satisfaction as parents/caregivers are often the primary decision-makers. There is scarce surgical literature on communication between physicians and pediatric patients/caregivers and the impact of gender concordance in clinical encounters [13]. The first aim of this study is to explore the different perspectives and experiences of pediatric orthopaedic surgery patient caregivers based on gender. The second aim is to examine the impact of physician/caregiver gender concordance during pediatric orthopaedic outpatient visits. Understanding any perceived gender-based differences in communication, clinical experience, or physician/patient relationships will ultimately help better inform and direct care for our patients.

Materials and methods

This prospective investigation was approved by our Institutional Review Board. Anonymous surveys were administered to patient (aged less than 18 years old) caregivers following their outpatient clinic visit with a board eligible/certified fellowship–trained pediatric orthopaedic surgeon between 2022 and 2024. Nine surgeons participated in the study: six male and three female. All worked at one of the two tertiary-care pediatric institutions in both urban and suburban outpatient locations. Patient caregiver was defined as the parent, legal guardian, or decision-maker accompanying the patient on the day of their visit. Anonymous electronic surveys were provided to caregivers to complete while in clinic on their own devices or on a clinic provided device. Participation was voluntary, and those wishing to proceed were informed of their inclusion in the study. Patients were included on a list in the electronic medical record if they completed a survey to prevent duplicate participation. Surveys were administered after both new consultation and follow-up visits. Each question in the survey was considered voluntary. There was on average a 13.5% missing response rate per question. Patients were excluded if they did not speak English and/or if they had already undergone surgery with the attending surgeon, which the authors felt would introduce possible bias based on a previous clinical episode or enhanced physician-family relationship.

Surveys included 16 questions drawn from the Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems (CAHPS) survey, validated for use in an outpatient clinical setting [14,15]. Questions were organized into five ‘domain’: communication, shared decision-making, partnership, information-giving, and support and based on a 4-point Likert scale [15]. Caregivers were also asked to rate their physician overall on a numerical scale from 0 (worst) to 10 (best) and provide demographic data, including household socioeconomic status, race, ethnicity, and education level. An example of the survey is depicted in Appendix 1. Based on self-identified caregiver gender, surveys were designated as concordant (same caregiver/physician gender) or discordant. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture, a secure, web-based software platform hosted at our institution) [17], [18].

According to an a-priori power analysis to identify a medium effect size in overall physician ratings using analysis of variance with fixed effects and interaction at 80% power, a minimum of 158 surveys with an equal number of concordant and discordant surveys per physician were required.

We compared survey responses by caregiver gender and between physician/caregiver gender concordant and discordant visits. Validated measures and numerical scores were compared using Mann-Whitney U tests. Nonvalidated questions were compared using Fisher's tests. Statistics were performed with SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA) with a significance level of P < .05.

Results

Surveys were more often completed by female caregivers (N = 353, 73.0%) than male caregivers (N = 129, 27.0%). There was no significant difference in the race, ethnicity, or educational background of male and female caregivers. In a comparison of survey results based on new-patient encounters vs follow-up visits, we found that female caregivers gave statistically significantly higher overall rating to physicians during follow-up visits (P = .01), but comparable scores to males for first-time office encounters. Similarly, new consultation visits were not found to have a significant gender difference in caregiver perception of time spent with child and involvement in treatment decisions, as was found in the general cohort analysis. There was a statistically significant difference between how new-patient caregivers perceived discussion about ‘reasons to not have surgery’ (P = .042) and ‘how the problem impacts your life’ (P = .013) as compared to follow-up encounters, in which caregivers answered more favorably.

In total, 376 surveys matched by caregiver–physician gender (188 concordant and 188 discordant) were analyzed. Overall, gender-discordant encounters had a significantly higher rate of dissatisfaction (4% compared to 1%) with physician discussion of treatment options and involvement in decision-making (P = .02 and .04, respectively, Table 3). Discussion

This study aimed to explore the potentially different perspectives and experiences of pediatric orthopaedic surgery patient caregivers based on gender during outpatient clinical encounters. We found that more female caregivers accompany their children to pediatric orthopaedic surgery clinical visits and that there was a difference in reported socioeconomic status (based on household income) between caregiver genders. Clinical experiences were generally positive, with caregivers of both genders providing high marks for physician rating (9.7/10) on average. Female caregivers were significantly more likely to report feeling the physician did not listen as carefully, spend as much time, or involve them as much in treatment decisions, regardless of physician gender. In addition to the above gender-specific feedback, physician focus on improving surgical discussions (domain with the highest ‘No’ percentage) and understanding how the child is impacted by the clinical condition could serve to increase caregiver satisfaction and improve physician-patient relationships in general. These findings corroborate those reported by Singelton et al., highlighting the importance of perceived surgeon empathy on caregiver satisfaction in pediatric orthopaedic surgery [10].

Gender concordant clinic visits positively impacted caregiver experiences with respect to involvement in treatment decisions/options and providing understandable explanations. There were no significant caregiver reported negative effects noted in concordant visits. Both male and female caregivers were significantly more satisfied with their involvement in treatment decisions when seeing a physician of the same gender. Considering these positive effects of physician/caregiver gender concordance, particularly when it pertains to potentially complex decisions regarding treatment and the fact that most patients are accompanied by a female caregiver, the importance of having female pediatric orthopaedic surgeons available at institutions and in the community is significant.

Orthopaedic surgery remains among the least diverse (both in gender and in racial/ethnic background) of all medical fields, and these results illustrate the importance of creating, maintaining, and promoting a diverse and representative physician census in pediatric orthopaedics. A prior study from our institution showed that Black/African American caregivers reported worse communication with providers, regardless of provider race, during pediatric orthopaedic outpatient visits [19]. A study from Diaz et al. in 2024 reported that patient-physician language and cultural concordance were found to be significantly important for Hispanic and African American patients, a demographic historically marginalized in national healthcare [20]. Increasing access to physicians that patients preferentially seek based on a shared background or culture may serve to improve overall population health. Though patient satisfaction in clinical encounters is only one element of delivering effective and quality care, accounting for patient comfortability and engagement is an undoubtedly important process of building trust and rapport. Physician gender, race/ethnicity, language, and culture have all been shown to influence patient experience and potentially improve outcomes.

Limitations of this study included those inherent to primarily survey-based data collection. Due to varying levels of resources available at the different clinical sites, we excluded parents/caregivers with a primary language other than English. In addition, while the survey did have an ‘other’ gender category, due to significantly lower numbers, we were unable to adequately capture patients/caregivers whose self-reported gender was nonbinary or not male/female. We acknowledge that this limits representation and fails to capture the unique perspectives of these individuals. In addition, we attempted to match concordant and discordant surveys so that both cohorts would be equally influenced by each surgeon. However, the number of surgeons included in the study (nine) was small. We also did not control the type of outpatient visit, as some surveys were administered following preoperative counseling or surgical discussions, whereas others were routine follow-up of an established condition. Furthermore, some surveys occurred after initial consultations (first time meeting the physician) as opposed to follow-up visits with a familiar individual. This could have skewed the complexity and nuances of communication and family decision-making in some instances. We also recognize the confounding factor of caregivers/patients preferentially choosing their physicians based on a variety of reasons, which could include gender. Furthermore, due to the unbalanced sample size, we are unable to credibly perform a multivariate analysis to control for caregiver income and relationship, or account for any physician specific effects.

Future directions of study include a closer examination of clinical encounters at a higher risk of patient/caregiver perceived deficiencies in communication or physician engagement to identify methods of improving the experience for all involved. This becomes especially important if the shortcomings during clinic visits are found to directly translate to suboptimal patient care or outcomes. Further exploration of the results including linking caregiver perceptions to clinical metrics including length of visit, ultimate adherence to follow-up plan, or decision to pursue surgery would also be of interest. We also recognize that physician gender is only one of a multitude of factors that may influence patient perceptions and experiences, and the results though analyzed by gender, may not be uniformly attributed to it.

Ultimately, the findings in this study show that most caregivers were overall satisfied with their physician encounters and while statistically significant differences in some satisfaction measures were identified, the overall numbers and percentages remained small, suggesting physicians are doing an excellent overall job of meeting family needs during clinic visits. We believe that the results of this study allow a better understanding of the portions of the visit that are most prone to cause frustration or lack clarity, thus encouraging enhanced focus on these components of the encounter when establishing a physician-patient/caregiver relationship.
