Outcomes of Mandible Reconstruction Using Surgeon‐Specific, Patient‐Specific, and Conventional Plates
Authors: Nina Patel, Autefeh Sajjadi, Andrea Park, Rahul Seth, Chase Heaton, Mary Jue Xu, Katherine Wai, Ilya Likhterov, P Daniel Knott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo compare operative metrics, postoperative complications, and radiographic osseous union among surgeon‐specific plates (SSPs), patient‐specific plates (PSPs), and conventional un‐bent plates (CPs) in microvascular mandibular reconstruction.MethodsA retrospective review was performed of 171 patients who underwent osseous mandibular free flap reconstruction from January 2020–January 2025. Patients were stratified by plating technique: SSP (n = 52), PSP with computer‐aided design/manufacture (CAD/CAM) (n = 58), and CP (n = 61). Primary outcomes included operative time, hospital length of stay, and postoperative complications. Secondary outcomes included osteotomy characteristics, CT‐based radiographic osseous union, and time‐to‐Grade 2 union. Multivariate analysis evaluated factors associated with nonunion.ResultsBaseline demographics, defect characteristics, and radiation exposure were comparable across cohorts. Secondary reconstruction procedures were more frequent in the SSP cohort compared with the PSP cohort (p = 0.05), and SSPs were more commonly used in complete (angle to angle) mandibular reconstructions. Operative time, hospital length of stay, and ischemia time did not differ significantly by plating method. Postoperative complication rates were low and similar across groups, with no significant differences in infection, hematoma, or reoperation; however, low event rates may have limited power to detect small but clinically meaningful differences between cohorts. Radiographic nonunion was uncommon and did not differ by plating. Time‐to‐Grade 2 osseous union was similar across plating strategies. Multivariate analysis demonstrated a significant association between nonunion and postoperative complications. Inter‐rater reliability for union grading was high.ConclusionMicrovascular mandibular reconstruction demonstrates comparable clinical and radiographic outcomes regardless of plate selection. Plate selection and CAD/CAM use should be driven by surgeon choice and reconstructive complexity.Level of Evidence3.Keywords: mandibular reconstruction, surgeon‐specific plates, surgical outcomes, VSP plates

Introduction

Osseous microvascular mandibular reconstruction is critical for restoring function and esthetics following oncologic resection, repair of traumatic fractures, or repair of bony congenital deformities. Free flap mandible reconstruction, as demonstrated in current literature, is characterized by high success rates and favorable functional outcomes [1, 2, 3].

A critical decision in mandible reconstruction is the method of locking mandibular reconstruction plate (LMRP) fixation. Conventional un‐bent plates (CPs) require intraoperative manual bending to bridge the mandibular contour defect. This offers flexibility but also introduces variability in contouring and potentially affects plate stability, occlusion, surgical time, and complication rates [4]. Surgeon‐specific plates (SSPs) use LMRPs pre‐bent according to general demographic normative mandibular contour data, guided by the surgeon's experience, aiming to enhance predictability while permitting some intraoperative decision‐making flexibility. Patient‐specific plates (PSPs) leverage virtual surgical planning (VSP) and computer‐aided manufacturing and design (CAD/CAM) to create prefabricated hardware and associated cutting guides tailored precisely to the patient's bony anatomy, which has shown in some studies to improve reconstruction accuracy, reduce operative time, and potentially decrease hardware complications [5, 6].

Although these fixation strategies differ in design and planning, comparative data on complication rates, osseous union, and postoperative outcomes are scarce. Existing studies have primarily focused on technical feasibility or operative efficiency rather than systematically evaluating union rates, hardware failure, infection, or need for revision surgery across all three plating strategies [7, 8]. Moreover, evaluation of advanced imaging through graded metrics allows quantitative assessment of osseous union, yet few studies have applied this uniformly to compare SSPs, PSPs, and CPs [6].

Comprehensive evaluation of clinical outcomes and complication profiles across SSPs, PSPs, and CPs is essential to guide evidence‐based selection of fixation strategy in mandibular reconstruction. This retrospective single‐institution study compares postoperative complications, surgical revisions, and radiologic union among these three plating approaches to provide critical insights into their relative performance and inform surgical decision‐making, optimizing reconstruction success and minimizing complications.

Methods

This study was designed as a retrospective review of patient outcomes following the use of SSPs (Stryker Inc., Portage, MI) compared to PSPs designed with proprietary VSP (Stryker Inc., Portage, MI) and CPs (Stryker Inc., Portage, MI) within a single tertiary care institution. The study protocol was approved by the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF) Institutional Review Board (IRB) (study #24‐42268), ensuring compliance with ethical standards for research involving human subjects. All patient data were anonymized to maintain confidentiality. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, informed consent was waived by the IRB.

Study Population

The patient cohort consisted of 171 individuals who had undergone mandibular reconstruction with either SSPs, PSPs, or CPs between January 2020 and January 2025. Free flap reconstructions were performed by 7 head and neck reconstructive surgeons and 10 microsurgical fellows. Of these, 7 surgeons utilized PSPs and CPS, including 2 surgeons who also employed SSPs. Inclusion criteria included patients aged 18 years or older who received mandibular plates as part of their surgical treatment. Patients with incomplete medical records or follow‐up data were excluded from the analysis.

Data Collection

Patient data were extracted from electronic medical records and included demographic information (including age and sex) and clinical indications for surgery. Operative details, including the type of defect, plate design, and surgeon‐specific modifications, were recorded. Postoperative outcomes were assessed using follow‐up records and clinical notes.

Osseous union was assessed using a 3‐point CT‐based grading scale: grade 0 indicated absent callus formation, grade 1 indicated complete callus formation on either the labial or lingual cortex, and grade 2 indicated complete callus formation on both cortices [7, 8]. Two independent reviewers (N.P. and A.S.) evaluated union at mandibular subsites corresponding to the plated region (condyle, ramus, angle, body, or symphysis). A.S. is a clinically trained, board‐certified otolaryngologist, and N.P. is an otolaryngology research fellow, both with experience in head and neck reconstruction and outcomes research.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcomes that were measured included operative time, hospital length of stay (LOS), and postoperative complications such as flap failure, infection, hematoma, surgical takebacks, and readmission rates. Operative time was recorded from incision to closure, while LOS was measured from the day of surgery through discharge. Postoperative complications were defined as any event requiring intervention, including flap compromise, infections requiring antibiotics or debridement, and hematomas necessitating evacuation. Differences in these outcomes among SSPs, PSPs, and CPs were analyzed to assess if plating techniques affect surgical efficiency and patient recovery.

Comparative and Data Analysis

Outcomes resulting from the use of the SSPs were compared with those resulting from the use of PSPs and CPs. Comparative metrics included complication rates, functional outcomes, radiographic union, and patient satisfaction. Statistical analyses were performed on the collected patient data to determine differences in outcomes between groups.

The quantitative data were analyzed using R statistical software (R Studio Team, 2024, PBC, Boston, MA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics and clinical characteristics. Complication rates were calculated as proportions. Patients were stratified according to plating method (SSP, PSP, or CP). Continuous variables were compared using unpaired t‐tests or one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using chi‐square or Fisher exact tests depending on expected cell counts. Pairwise comparisons between cohorts were subsequently performed when overall group differences were identified. A two‐tailed p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Oncologic resection was the most common indication for reconstruction (n = 127, 74%), followed by osteonecrosis, dehiscence, or infection (n = 29, 17%). Defect localization most frequently involved the mandibular body (47%) and ramus (24%). While most clinical characteristics including defect size and location did not differ significantly between plating methods, the distribution of ramus defects was slightly lower in the PSP group (p = 0.04), suggesting a modest association with plating choice. Finally, no significant differences were observed in radiation history, adjuvant radiation therapy, or combined radiation exposure across groups.

Fibula free flaps were the predominant reconstructive modality (n = 138, 80%). There was greater use of the osteocutaneous radial forearm free flaps in the CP group and increased use of alternative free flap donor sites (scapula, DCIA, latissimus‐serratus‐rib) in the SSP group (p < 0.05). Plate selection also varied with the anatomic extent of mandibular reconstruction, categorized as hemi‐mandible versus complete (angle‐to‐angle) mandibular reconstruction, with PSPs and conventional plates more frequently used for hemi‐mandible reconstructions and SSPs more commonly used for complete mandibular reconstructions (p < 0.05). Need for secondary reconstruction, defined as any unplanned return to the operating room for reconstruction‐related revision including hardware revision or additional reconstructive procedures, were more frequent in the SSP group compared with the PSP group (p = 0.05).

Table 2 summarizes the operative times and hospital LOS across plate types. Mean operative time did not differ significantly between plating systems (SSP group: 668.4 ± 130.4 min; PSP group: 705.4 ± 113.6 min; CP group: 672.5 ± 108.5 min). LOS was similar across all cohorts, with a mean duration of 9.4–9.6 days. Although mean ischemia time was longer in the PSP and CP groups (SSP: 158.2 min; PSP: 160.1 min, p = 0.06; CP: 186.3 min, p = 0.16), these differences were not statistically significant.

Radiographic nonunion at ≥ 6 months was rare and did not differ significantly by plate type (≤ 3.3% across groups). There were no statistically significant differences in average time to Grade 2 osseous union across plating methods. On multivariate analysis, plate type was not independently associated with radiographic nonunion. However, the presence of any postoperative complications was significantly associated with nonunion (adjusted OR 4.2, 95% CI 1.4–12.6; p = 0.01).

Discussion

This study compares outcomes of SSPs, PSPs and CPs in mandibular free flap reconstruction, focusing on operative times, length of hospital stay, postoperative complications, and osseous union. We found no statistically significant differences in complication rates, average time to grade 2 osseous union, or overall clinical outcomes across plating methods. Descriptive trends demonstrated that PSPs were more frequently utilized in primary reconstructions with less common flap types (including scapula and other complex osseous flaps), whereas CPs were more commonly used for osteocutaneous radial forearm flaps, secondary surgeries, and total mandibular reconstruction requiring full‐length (angle‐to‐angle) plates. These patterns suggest that plate selection reflects reconstructive complexity and surgeon preference rather than differences in clinical effectiveness [9]. Importantly, multivariate analysis demonstrated a statistically significant association between nonunion and postoperative complications, underscoring the clinical relevance of achieving reliable osseous integration independent of fixation strategy. This finding is mirrored in previous studies which underscore that biologic and wound‐related factors play a greater role in osseous healing than plate selection alone [10].

While outcomes were similar across plating strategies, these findings should be interpreted in the context of relatively modest cohort sizes and low event rates for several complications and radiographic nonunion. The study may therefore be underpowered to detect small but clinically meaningful differences between plating methods, particularly for infrequent outcomes such as flap failure, hardware‐related complications, or nonunion. Accordingly, the absence of statistically significant differences should not be interpreted as definitive equivalence between fixation strategies.

Mandibular free flap reconstruction has become increasingly reliable, with high rates of flap survival and functional restoration reported across institutions and reconstructive approaches [11, 12, 13, 14]. The introduction of VSP and CAD/CAM has expanded fixation options, particularly with PSPs, which allow for precise preoperative contouring and osteotomy planning [15, 16]. Several studies suggest that PSPs may improve surgical accuracy and efficiency, especially in complex or revision settings where anatomy is distorted [5, 17, 18, 19].

Interestingly, secondary reconstruction procedures were more frequent in the SSP cohort compared with the PSP cohort. This finding may reflect underlying reconstructive complexity and case selection rather than plate performance alone, as SSPs were more frequently utilized for complete mandibular reconstructions and more extensive defects requiring intraoperative flexibility. These data further support that plating strategy in contemporary mandibular reconstruction is often driven by defect characteristics and surgeon preference rather than standardized selection criteria [19, 20].

Despite these theoretical advantages, our study did not demonstrate a reduction in complication rates with PSPs compared with SSPs or CPs. This contrasts with select reports suggesting improved outcomes or reduced hardware‐related complications with customized plates [20, 21]. However, other studies have similarly shown no clear difference in complication rates when comparing VSP‐assisted and CPs, highlighting that postoperative outcomes are influenced by a continuum of factors beyond plate design, including radiation exposure, defect size, flap selection, and patient comorbidities [10, 22, 23]. Our findings suggest that the clinical outcomes associated with SSPs were comparable to those observed with alternative plating strategies in this cohort.

The application of SSP for full mandibular reconstruction compared to hemi‐mandible reconstruction infers potential utility and benefit of SSP in large complex defects. Prior work has shown that CPs offer intraoperative flexibility when addressing unexpected anatomic variation, albeit at the cost of longer operative times and increased manipulation of soft tissues [24]. Complete mandibular reconstructions require rigid fixation across multiple subsites with limited tolerance for cumulative alignment error and symmetry deviation, which may explain the preference for SSPs in these scenarios [16]. Conversely, the greater use of osteocutaneous radial forearm flaps in PSP and CP cohorts likely reflects selection of these techniques for smaller defects where preoperative planning or conventional plating is sufficient.

Finally, the observed association between nonunion and postoperative complications is consistent with prior studies correlating delayed or incomplete osseous union to plate exposure, infection, and need for revision surgery [23, 25, 26]. The use of a CT‐based grading system allowed for objective assessment of union across mandibular subsites and reinforces the importance of osteosynthesis as a key determinant of reconstructive success, regardless of fixation method [23, 25, 27].

The radiographic grading system used in this study also has important clinical implications. Grade 2 osseous union represents complete cortical bridging across both cortices and is generally associated with improved long‐term biomechanical stability and lower risk of hardware‐related complications. In contrast, incomplete union or persistent Grade 0–1 healing patterns may predispose patients to plate exposure, infection, malocclusion, or need for revision surgery. Therefore, consistent progression toward Grade 2 union remains an important reconstructive goal regardless of plating strategy.

Limitations of this study include its retrospective design, relatively modest cohort size, and potential selection bias, as plate choice was influenced by surgeon preference, defect complexity, and reconstructive context. Because fixation strategy was selected at the discretion of the reconstructive surgeon rather than through randomization, residual confounding related to defect complexity, prior surgery, radiation exposure, or flap selection may persist despite multivariate analysis. In addition, low event rates for outcomes such as radiographic nonunion, flap failure, and hardware‐related complications may have limited statistical power to detect clinically meaningful differences between plating cohorts. Long‐term functional outcomes, such as occlusion, mastication, speech, and swallowing, were not systematically assessed. Variable follow‐up duration and imaging intervals may have limited detection of delayed complications (i.e., fistula formation, surgical revision) and precision in determining precise time to development of osseous union. Future studies should prospectively evaluate long‐term functional and patient‐reported outcomes, perform cost‐effectiveness analyses of PSPs, SSPs, and conventional plates, and investigate risk factors for nonunion to optimize postoperative care. Larger sample sizes and standardized long‐term follow‐up will further clarify the durability and clinical benefits of different plating strategies.

In this cohort, SSPs, PSPs, and CPs demonstrated generally similar complication profiles, osseous union outcomes, and overall clinical performance in mandibular free flap reconstruction, although the study may have been underpowered to detect small but clinically meaningful differences between fixation strategies. SSPs were more commonly associated with complete mandibular reconstruction and secondary reconstruction procedures, suggesting that plate utilization reflected reconstructive complexity and surgeon preference rather than the inherent superiority of a single fixation method. Achieving reliable osseous union remains critical, as nonunion is significantly associated with postoperative complications.

Conclusion

Among patients who underwent mandibular free flap reconstruction, no significant differences were observed between the utilization of surgeon‐specific versus both patient‐specific and conventional plates among operative times, length of hospital stay, or postoperative complications. All three plating techniques yield similar clinical outcomes. SSPs were more commonly associated with complete mandibular reconstruction and higher rates of secondary reconstruction procedures, likely reflecting greater reconstructive complexity. These findings suggest that all three methods can be used effectively in mandibular reconstruction, and the choice of technique should be guided by other factors such as surgical preferences, patient characteristics, and resource availability. However, given the relatively modest sample size and low event rates, larger prospective studies are needed to better determine whether clinically meaningful differences exist between fixation strategies.
