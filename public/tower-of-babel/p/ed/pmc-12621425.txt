The impact of smartphone-based monitoring on reducing National Health Service visits for cardiothoracic patients with surgical site infections
Authors: Melissa Rochon, Angila Jawarchan, Jamie Croker, Anna Thompson, Glorea P Rajakumar, Nnadiebere Ayogu, Karen Cariaga, Ana Alves, Judith Tanner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundSurgical site infections (SSIs) are a leading cause of postoperative morbidity, contributing to significant healthcare resource utilisation and economic burden. This study evaluates the impact of smartphone-based remote monitoring of surgical wounds on reducing healthcare visits for cardiothoracic patients with SSIs within the National Health Service (NHS).MethodsA retrospective, multi-site service evaluation was conducted at Guy's and St Thomas' NHS Foundation Trust (GSTT), encompassing three specialised cardiac hospitals in London. Adult patients having cardiothoracic surgery were monitored for SSI using the Isla digital platform. SSI rates and associated NHS visits were analysed and compared against national cardiothoracic data.Results1,248 patients enrolled onto the Isla wound monitoring platform over a six-month period, showed an SSI rate of 6.7% and an overall response rate to Isla of 88%. 77 patients with an SSI at GSTT used 295 NHS visits, significantly fewer healthcare resources across nearly all visit types compared to national cardiothoracic SSI patients, including general practitioner (GP), nurse, outpatient and Accident and Emergency attendances (P< 0.05). The only exception was readmissions for SSIs that developed after hospital discharge, where there was no significant difference between GSTT and national patients (P = 0.9197). The median wound healing time for patients with digital monitoring was 19 days (range 2–156 days), while the national data reports a mean time to heal of 91.7 days ±67.5.ConclusionSmartphone-based remote wound monitoring significantly reduced healthcare visits while maintaining effective SSI management, aligning with NHS objectives to enhance resource efficiency and patient convenience. These findings underscore the potential of remote monitoring in postoperative care and warrant further studies to assess its broader applicability.Keywords: Surgical site infection, Surveillance, Smartphone, Surgery, Photography, Healthcare visits

Introduction

Surgical wound complications (SWCs) occur when the normal healing process of a surgical incision is disrupted, resulting in delayed or impaired healing. These complications are a major cause of postoperative morbidity globally, with mortality rates ranging from 1% to 4% among surgical patients. [1] Among SWCs, surgical site infections (SSIs) are the most common, and impose a significant burden on healthcare systems. In Europe, SSIs are reported to affect over 500,000 patients each year, costing healthcare systems up to 20.05 billion euros (adjusted to 2025 inflation). [2] Similarly, in the United States, approximately 158,000 SSIs occur annually, translating into an estimated economic burden of $8.5 billion to $25.19, adjusted for 2025 inflation. [3] These infections not only prolong hospital stays and increase antibiotic usage but also contribute to severe complications such as sepsis, resulting in substantial clinical burdens. [4].

Many of the risk factors for SSI, such as obesity, smoking, and diabetes, are prevalent within the cardiac surgery population. [5] The incidence of SSI after cardiac surgery varies, with reported rates ranging from 3.5% to 26.8%, depending on patient profiles and the type of procedure. [6] Cardiothoracic patients with an SSI have a mean hospital stay of 2.3 times longer than their unaffected counterparts. [7] Notably, in a seminal study examining costs of SSI in England, cardiothoracic surgery incurred the highest additional costs due to SSIs, with an average of £27,795 per infection, adjusted for 2025 inflation. [8].

Notably, a significant proportion of SSIs, around 85%, develop after patients are discharged, necessitating frequent follow-up care in the community, which is often managed by primary care nurses. [9] Approximately 16% of cardiothoracic patients with SSIs require hospital readmission, and 72% visit their general practitioner, adding considerable pressure on primary and secondary healthcare services. [9] Hospital readmissions due to SSIs are the primary cost driver, accounting for up to 47% of the total healthcare costs associated with managing these infections. [2] This highlights the impact of SSIs on the healthcare service capacity, and the urgent need for effective prevention and monitoring strategies.

The use of patient smartphones for remote surgical wound monitoring is a promising approach to mitigate these challenges. This approach facilitates early detection and treatment, potentially reducing SSI severity while improving patient outcomes. For example, a randomized controlled trial demonstrated that remote monitoring of surgical wounds reduced community healthcare visits by 50% without increasing hospital visits. [10] Such reductions in ambulatory care align with the NHS Long Term Plan, which aims to enhance out-of-hospital care and reduce the burden on emergency and community health services. [11].

Guy's and St Thomas' NHS Foundation Trust (GSTT), London has implemented a smartphone-based remote wound monitoring across various surgical specialisms, including caesarean sections, vascular, gastrointestinal, breast, thoracic surgery and cardiac (adult and paediatric) procedures, as part of its broader digital health strategy. The technology is provided by Isla, a progressive web platform which does not need to be installed or downloaded. [12] In order to manage the increasing number of wound submissions (>1,000 per week), a ‘Central Digital Wound Hub’ (CDWH) was established. The CDWH is a rationalised and reprofiled workforce solution to help reduce the pressure of new additional staffing workloads introduced by new digital tools. [13].

The primary objective of this service evaluation was to assess the influence of smartphone-based remote monitoring on healthcare resource utilisation for patients with SSIs following cardiothoracic surgeries within the NHS. Specifically, it aimed to quantify differences in healthcare visits, including those to general practitioners, practice nurses, district nurses, hospital outpatient clinics, accident and emergency (A&E) departments, and hospital readmissions, comparing patients monitored via the Isla digital platform to national benchmarks.

Methods

Setting and study design

This retrospective, multi-centre service evaluation was conducted within the GSTT Clinical Care Group of three London-based hospitals specialising in cardiothoracic surgery: St Thomas' Hospital, Royal Brompton Hospital, and Harefield Hospital. GSTT serves a local population of approximately one million people, with additional patients from across the UK referred for specialised cardiothoracic care. The study was designed to evaluate healthcare resource utilisation by examining NHS visits among patients who developed SSIs following cardiothoracic surgeries conducted between April and September 2024. Institutional approvals were obtained for the service evaluation (CIRIS ID: 009466). In accordance with institutional and national guidance, it did not require research ethics committee approval.

Participants

The evaluation included adult patients (≥18 years) discharged home or to another care facility after undergoing coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery and/or cardiac procedures involving the valves or septum. Patients unable to engage with the digital monitoring through disability, inability to speak English or access to technology, but had a relative or contact who agreed to assist were able to take part. Eligible patients were automatically enrolled on the Isla digital platform, a tool supporting remote wound monitoring and patient engagement, where they received surveillance as per the national surveillance protocol. [13] Patients who were registered with the National Data Opt-Out were excluded from the evaluation.

Intervention - Isla wound monitoring and management

Patients are enrolled onto the Isla platform after surgery and prior to hospital discharge and they, or their nominated contacts, are automatically scheduled to receive pre-programmed weekly SMS texts or emails until 30 days after surgery requesting wound images and information on healing. Additionally, patients can submit information for review anytime they have a concern. All submissions receive review and individualised responses from a surveillance nurse tailored to their wound healing. Patients whose wounds are healing as expected are informed of this. If concerns are noted, patients are either contacted for further information, provided with self-care guidance, or referred for clinical follow-up as appropriate. Self-management support includes hygiene recommendations, wound protection, and dressing guidance. Where antibiotics are prescribed, surveillance nurses follow up with the Microbiology Department to adjust treatment based on culture results, if required. At the Royal Brompton Hospital and Harefield Hospital, surveillance nurses work within the Central Digital Wound Hub (CDWH) which provides a dedicated digital wound monitoring post-discharge surveillance service and operates seven days a week. At St Thomas Hospital, Advanced Nurse Practitioners (ANPs) manage patient submissions daily, following the same standardised protocols as the CDWH. All eleven staff (six in the CDWH and 5 ANPs) reviewing Isla are trained and there are clinical guidelines and standard operating procedures in place.

Comparison data: national SSI and NHS visit data

This study compared local GSTT Isla data against national data. [9] National data is reported in a study that employed a retrospective cohort analysis to characterise SSIs within the UK's NHS. [9] Guest et al. studied anonymised electronic patient records from the Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) linked with Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) databases. [9] CPRD is a real world evidence research service provided by the UK's Medicine and Healthcare Products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) with support from the National Institute for Health and Care Research that includes the electronic records of 18 million current patients provided by general practices across the UK. HES data includes secondary care information such as hospital admissions, outpatient visits and accident and emergency visits. A sample of 50,000 adult patients coded as having open surgery between 2017 and 2022 were included, of which 4,260 patients had cardiothoracic surgery, of which 14% (n=596) developed an SSI. Wound infections were observed and documented in the patient's record by a clinician, any definitions used are unknown. A wound was considered to be unhealed if it was not recorded as healed and was supported by continued professional visits for wound care. No details were provided on SSI classification (superficial, deep, organ/space) or anatomical site.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the utilisation of NHS visits (consultations with general practitioners, practice nurses, hospital outpatient departments, accident and emergency attendances, hospital readmissions) of cardiothoracic patients using Isla digital monitoring who developed an SSI compared against national data for cardiothoracic patients who developed an SSI. Utilisation data is presented for Isla patients who developed an infection, as opposed to all patients, to be comparable with the national data.

Secondary outcomes were SSI rates and time to wound healing for patients on the Isla digital wound monitoring compared with national data, patient Isla engagement rates, and the time between patient/carer submission to Isla and receiving a reply from staff.

SSIs were defined using the criteria from the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) SSI surveillance protocol for clinician-reported SSIs and patient-reported SSIs [13]. The UKHSA Surgical wound healing post discharge questionnaire does not ask the site of the wound infection, for this reason axillary wounds such as groin or drain site infections were also recorded, identified in the images (see Supplementary Material). Wound healing was defined as no longer requiring dressings, therapies or clinical review. Isla engagement was defined as patients submitting at least one wound photo and wound healing questionnaire.

Data collection

For patients using Isla digital monitoring, data on NHS acute and primary care visits were collected retrospectively from EPIC Systems Corporation (Version: 100.2408.1.0). This is an electronic health record system used by several NHS trusts that integrates hundreds of different systems onto one platform, including acute and primary care data. For patients whose community care information was incomplete, GP practices were contacted via secure NHS email to ensure comprehensive documentation of wound management. Patient responses on healthcare visits were cross-verified against EPIC visit records.

30-day post-discharge SSI data for Isla patients obtained prospectively by trained surveillance staff according to the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) SSI surveillance protocol were taken from the Isla database. [14] In-patient and re-admission SSI data were collected prospectively, by trained surveillance personnel using the same UKHSA protocol for up to one year. Wound healing times were assessed over a six-month follow-up period, using EPIC, GP records, and activity on Isla. Isla engagement and staff response times were taken from the Isla database.

Data analysis

All analyses of NHS service use were restricted to the patients who developed an SSI. Percentages reflect the proportion of these SSI patients who used each service type, and mean values are calculated across the full cohort of SSI patients, including those who did not access a given service. Statistical comparisons between the national and local cohorts for resource use were carried out using chi-squared tests or Fisher's exact tests, depending on the size of the groups being compared. Fisher's exact test was used when the number of patients in any group was very small, to ensure the results remained reliable. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant, with no adjustments made for multiple comparisons.

Staff response time was calculated as the median time between patient submission of data to the Isla platform and healthcare provider review and response. NHS visit frequencies were compared with national cardiac patient data (Guest et al., 2023), with descriptive statistics calculated using Microsoft Excel, Python (version 3.12.4) and the Pandas library (version 2.2.2) to summarize the dataset's key characteristics.

Results

Between April and September 2024, 1,248 patients underwent cardiothoracic surgery at one of the three participating hospitals and were discharged home or to another care facility. Twelve patients did not have a mobile contact number or email address and were followed up via phone call at 30 days, with the remaining 99% of patients (1,236/1,248) enrolled onto the Isla wound monitoring platform. The median staff response time to patient submissions was 44 minutes within the CDWH, and 16 hours and 8 minutes for patients monitored by ANPs. During the study period, the average patient response rate to Isla was 88% (range 86%–89%). See Figure 1.

The overall combined SSI rate across the three hospital sites was 6.7% (83/1,248), significantly lower than the national average of 14% (P < 0.0001). This included 0.9% (n = 11) inpatient SSIs, 0.9% (n = 11) readmission SSIs, and 4.9% (n = 61) patient-reported SSIs. Three patients developed two SSIs but are only counted once for the purposes of the NHS visits analysis (number of total patients = 80); all associated visits were counted. In the national dataset, 596 out of 4,260 cardiothoracic surgery patients (14%) developed an SSI. Twenty-three percent of these SSIs developed during the inpatient stay, while 77% occurred post-discharge.

Three of the 80 patients who developed at least one SSI were excluded from the NHS visit data collection due to NHS data opt-out registration. The remaining 77 patients in the Isla group who developed an SSI recorded a total of 295 NHS visits. Of these, 190 (64%) were in the community: 55 GP appointments (used by 27 patients), 112 practice nurse appointments (attended by 36 patients), and 23 district nurse visits (used by 6 patients). Seventy-seven visits (26%) were to hospital outpatient departments. Eleven patients (14%) attended A&E on 15 occasions, with a mean of 1.36 visits per user. Twelve patients were readmitted to hospital for a total of 13 admissions due to an SSI. Among those who used each service, the mean number of visits per patient with an SSI was significantly lower compared to national cardiothoracic SSI patients. Statistically significant reductions were observed for both acute (hospital outpatient and A&E visits) and community-based services (GP, practice nurse, and district nurse visits), with the exception of readmissions for SSIs that developed post-discharge, where usage rates were similar.

The median time to wound healing for patients with an SSI using the Isla platform was 19 days (range 2–156 days). In contrast, the national dataset reported a mean time to heal of 91.7 days (±67.5), with 93% of infected wounds healed by six months.

Discussion

In this study, the Isla wound monitoring platform was evaluated against external published national data for SSI focusing on health resource utilisation. This is important as other studies have shown that cardiothoracic SSIs result in significant healthcare resource utilisation and costs. [15] These complications are associated with pain, anxiety, reduced quality of life, and financial burden. [16] At a system level, post-cardiac surgery SSIs cost the UK an estimated £15 million annually, [8] placing substantial strain on healthcare resources. A national survey reported that 42.5% of cardiothoracic patients require reoperation for SSI, sepsis was reported in 41.4% of infections that developed and a mortality rate of 6.8% was recorded with SSI after cardiothoracic surgery. [17] Wong et al. reported SSI led to delayed discharges in 43.8% of the cardiothoracic SSI cases surveyed. The mean length of stay was 17 days during the primary admission when diagnosed with an infection. In 16.5% of cases of cardiothoracic SSI, readmissions with a mean length of stay of 16.5 days were also reported. [17] There is increasing pressure on surgical wound care resource utilisation as our personal experience, supported with anecdotal reports from some areas within the UK, finds that funding to support post-operative surgical wound care in primary care is being withdrawn.

This study found that 99% of patients were eligible to use Isla and that over 85% of patients demonstrated engagement with Isla. The SSI rate for patients using Isla digital wound monitoring (6.7%) was significantly lower than the national SSI rate published by Guest and colleagues (14%), and of the patients who had an infection, resource utilisation was substantially lower across all acute care and community care services among patients who used Isla, except for readmissions.

Similarly, another study by McLean et al. using patient smartphones for SSI surveillance after abdominal surgery demonstrated a reduction in community visits. [10] McLean et al. found that 14.3% of patients in the smartphone intervention group attended healthcare services, versus 22.3% of patients in the standard care group. However, while community visits declined (OR: 0.57, 95% CI: 0.34−0.94, P= 0.030), hospital attendance did not significantly differ between the groups (OR: 0.76, 95% CI: 0.28−1.96, P= 0.577).

There are several possible reasons for a reduction in resource utilisation among patients who have digital wound monitoring. These include early identification of healing concerns, increased patient reassurance and easy access to clinicians.

Patients having Isla digital monitoring are invited to submit information about their wound weekly. This allows emerging wound complications to be detected early before the wound worsens and becomes harder and more expensive to treat. McLean found complications were 3.7 times higher odds of diagnosis within 7 post-operative days (95% CI: 1.02−13.51, P =0.043). Wound infections and complications that are ‘less severe’ will require fewer visits from district and practice nurses or wound clinic appointments. Remote monitoring through a digital tool can effectively triage patients, allowing healthcare professionals to identify and address complications such as SSI earlier, reducing the need for in-person consultations. Additionally, giving patients advice on self-management where appropriate, enabled patients to resolve minor issues remotely without needing to visit healthcare facilities.

Patients are anxious about looking after their wounds at home [18] and are unsure what normal wound healing look like, therefore the aim of some patients in seeking GP visits is to provide reassurance. Weekly monitoring with feedback via digital platforms provides ongoing reassurance for patients and removes the need to seek GP reassurance, as shown in the reductions in both this study and the McLean study.

A key reason for high patient satisfaction with digital wound monitoring is easy access to healthcare services. [19] Studies in other healthcare settings demonstrate that remote health technologies improve access to care, particularly for patients in rural or underserved areas, by minimising travel and associated barriers, [20] with comparable outcomes with face-to-face visits. The time for patients in this study to receive feedback was quicker for the CDWH than with ANPs. The quicker clinician response times for the hub can be explained through pooling staff resources to create a dedicated team who are available from 8 am until 8 pm and at weekends. This model of having regional hubs with staff to conduct digital review is used successfully in the UK with radiology services. [21] Our approach with the CDWH focuses on meeting patients' needs and preferences by using digital technology to make healthcare more accessible and to enable two-way communication between patients and healthcare providers. [19] Additionally, it includes a workforce solution to effectively use the emerging technology. [13] In future, this work will be supported by artificial intelligence to assist clinicians review and prioritise the increasing volume of patient submissions [22]. This work contributes a growing foundation for further studies on the scalability, cost-effectiveness, and general applicability of smartphone-based wound monitoring systems across various surgical specialisms.

Being able to detect wound complications is dependent on patient engagement with the digital monitoring platform. The high patient engagement with Isla over a six-month period reflects the user-friendly design of the platform. Isla was developed in collaboration with patients and their input shaped many aspects of the interface, including the photo function, text message contents, sequencing of patient information, no need to download any software, weekly submissions, tailored (as opposed to automated) responses and a patient-initiated feedback option. [15].

Accessing healthcare services for wound complications is a major challenge for patients [22]. Typically, patients' primary concern is post-operative incision disruption rather than SSI, yet this still leads to significant use of healthcare resources [23]. Minimising the frequency of patient visits can contribute to the NHS's goal of reducing outpatient appointments by up to a third over the next five years, potentially saving over £1 billion annually while improving patient convenience and resource utilisation. [11] Further work is needed to quantify other savings made i.e. reduced travel time and reductions in carbon dioxide emission from clinicians and patients not travelling.

The aim of this study was to compare the local burden of SSIs with a national benchmark, using real-world data to assess their incidence and impact on healthcare resource use. By capturing data on SSIs, the study offered valuable insights into their effects on patient outcomes and the healthcare system, strengthening the case for targeted prevention and management strategies—particularly those involving technology-enabled supported self-care. We postulate that the Isla platform incorporates key functionalities that likely contributed to earlier detection and reduced healthcare utilization. For example, real-time uploads allowed trained clinicians to promptly review and respond to patient submissions—typically within a median time of under one hour at the CDWH and within 24 hours for ANPs. This enabled the delivery of tailored self-care advice or escalation of care when necessary, supporting early triage and reducing unnecessary in-person visits. The ability to detect wound complications depends on patient engagement with the digital monitoring platform. In this study, Isla demonstrated high engagement levels—85% and above—among 99% of patients who underwent surgery over a six-month period. This reflects the user-friendly design of the platform. Isla was developed in collaboration with patients, whose input shaped many aspects of the interface, including the photo submission feature, text message content, the sequence of patient prompts, the ability to participate without downloading software, the frequency of weekly submissions, and the use of tailored (rather than automated) clinical responses. [15].

There are several limitations to this work. While the local and national datasets are broadly comparable in terms of the population and outcomes measured, the broader scope of the national dataset, which encompasses diverse hospitals and patient demographics, contrasts with the more limited GSTT dataset, potentially hindering comparability. Regional differences in healthcare delivery and resource availability may limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, differences in data collection method (retrospective analysis of electronic health records in the national dataset versus prospective monitoring via the Isla digital platform at GSTT) may influence the accuracy and completeness of reported outcomes. Differences in case mix, care pathways, and documentation standards between sites may confound the observed differences. Furthermore, variations in how SSIs and healthcare resource utilisation are defined and measured, including length of follow-up, between the datasets may introduce inconsistencies. The local data is relatively small and may underrepresent healthcare use if patients sought care outside the recorded systems. Our patient reported SSI rates were lower than the post-discharge SSI rates reported by Guest et al., 2023, however continuous prospective surveillance by trained personnel was undertaken across our sites, and we have previously published a confirmatory analysis of patient reported outcomes [24]. Additionally, stratifying SSIs into superficial, deep, and organ/space categories can offer valuable clinical and epidemiological insights. However, no data on the severity of SSI (superficial or deep incisional, organ or space) was available in the national dataset so was not considered here, which could affect case severity comparability. These factors should be considered when interpreting the comparative results and drawing conclusions about the impact of remote wound monitoring on healthcare resource utilisation. Lastly, this work focused on patients with an SSI rather than all patients, so we do not know the general impact of remote wound monitoring on NHS visits for the patient population as a whole.

Conclusions

This study highlights the potential of smartphone-based remote monitoring of surgical wounds to reduce healthcare utilisation within the NHS for patients with SSIs. By leveraging smartphone technology for remote wound assessment, with standard operating procedures, our service was able to decrease the frequency of in-person visits, especially in community settings, thereby relieving some of the pressure on general practitioners and hospital outpatient services. The significantly lower rates of hospital and community visits among digital wound monitoring patients compared to national data underscore the effectiveness of this approach in early detection and treatment of wound complications. Although further studies, including randomised controlled trials, are needed to validate these findings across broader patient populations, the results support the integration of digital wound monitoring as a scalable solution that aligns with NHS objectives to enhance resource efficiency and patient convenience.
