Sphenoclival fibrous dysplasia with cyst formation causing abducens nerve palsy in an older patient: illustrative case
Authors: Masayuki Nakamura, Hirotaka Hasegawa, Yoichi Yasunaga, Yudai Nakai, Motoyuki Umekawa, Nobuhito Saito
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDFibrous dysplasia (FD) is typically asymptomatic but is known to occasionally cause neurological symptoms such as visual disturbances. However, FD-associated abducens nerve palsy is extremely rare.OBSERVATIONSA 76-year-old woman had a left abducens nerve palsy due to mass effect on the abducens nerve at Dorello’s canal caused by FD and its associated cyst, which was resolved by resection of the cyst through an endoscopic transnasal route.LESSONSAlthough FD is generally an asymptomatic lesion occurring in younger patients, it can occasionally cause abducens nerve palsy even in older patients. Fibrous dysplasia (FD) is a benign bone lesion characterized by the replacement of normal bone tissue with fibro-osseous connective tissue, accounting for 5%–10% of all bone tumors.1–3 FD primarily affects adolescents and young adults without sex-based differences, and the majority of affected patients are 30 years or younger.4 While FD can arise throughout the skeleton, the craniofacial region is one of the most commonly affected areas, with the distribution of cases being maxilla (41%), frontal bone (22%), mandible (20%), sphenoid bone (18%), ethmoid bone (14%), and zygomatic bone (14%).5 The disease can manifest in a single bone (monostotic variant), affect multiple bones (polyostotic variant), or occur in association with hyperfunctioning endocrinopathies such as precocious puberty and hyperthyroidism, a combination known as McCune-Albright syndrome.6, 7 The symptoms are widely variable depending on the site of the lesion; according to a meta-analysis, the common symptoms are nonneurological, including facial asymmetry (34%), facial pain (16%), proptosis (12%), and orbital dystopia (8%).8, 9 On rare occasions, however, FD can also cause neurological symptoms; among them, vision loss, mainly due to optic canal involvement, has been well described in the literature.8–10 On the other hand, other isolated cranial nerve palsies have been very rarely reported; in particular, little is known about abducens nerve palsy due to FD, and there is only a single case in the literature.11 Here, we share our recent experience with a case of skull base FD that presented with abducens nerve palsy.

Illustrative Case

History, Examination, and Imaging

A 76-year-old woman was referred to us for evaluation and treatment of a skull base mass and diplopia. Neurological and ophthalmological examinations, including a Hess chart, revealed a left partial abducens nerve palsy. Her cranial imaging revealed a skull base mass centered in the sphenoid bone and clivus with cyst formation compressing the left abducens nerve (Fig. 1). Since the differential diagnoses were diverse, including FD, chordoma, primary sinonasal malignancy, and metastatic tumor, a transnasal biopsy was first attempted, which did not reveal any tumor components. Assuming that the lesion was, although extremely rare, a symptomatic FD with cyst formation, we performed endoscopic transnasal surgery to decompress the abducens nerve. Surgery

Under general anesthesia, the patient was secured in a supine position on the surgical bed with her head slightly tilted to the left and fixed in a 3-pin head holder. Straight and 30° 4-mm rigid endoscopes (Visera 4K UHD, Olympus) held in a robotic arm (UniARM, Mitaka Kohki) were used. The sphenoid bone was exposed through a transseptal route (Fig. 2A). It was hypertrophic, composed of a mixture of osseous components and relatively soft fibrous tissues. Drilling of the anteromedial part of the hypertrophic sphenoid bone took us to the cyst, which was covered with a fibrous capsule and filled with xanthochromic fluid (Fig. 2B and C). After removing the capsule, the affected left abducens nerve was confirmed through the dura at the petrous apex (Fig. 2D and E). As for the reconstruction, the mucosa of the posteroinferior nasal septum was elevated to cover the inferior and posterior aspects of the cyst cavity, to protect the exposed clival dura as well as prevent re-accumulation of the cyst by facilitating drainage to the nasal cavity (Fig. 2F). Outcome

The patient experienced an uneventful recovery following the surgery, with no postoperative complications. Her postoperative magnetic resonance imaging study showed expected partial removal of the mass in the sphenoclival region and disappearance of the cyst. Her abducens nerve palsy subjectively improved right after surgery, which was objectively confirmed on ophthalmological examination. The histopathological examination of the osseous components and fibrous tissues demonstrated proliferation of spindle-shaped cells with plump nuclei against a background of fibrous tissue, with bony trabeculae formation and sclerosis, leading to a diagnosis of FD, whereas the cyst wall was primarily composed of sparse fibrous tissues with signs of inflammatory cell infiltration, granulation, and hemorrhage (Fig. 3). Discussion

Observations

We report the case of a 76-year-old woman who had diplopia due to compression of the abducens nerve at the petrous apex by a cystic lesion within FD, which was restored with endoscopic transnasal surgery. FD is predominantly an asymptomatic condition characterized by hyperostosis, generally manifesting in younger patients. FD-associated cranial nerve dysfunctions are uncommon, and isolated abducens nerve palsy triggered by FD is exceptionally rare, with our case being only the second recorded instance in the literature.11, 12 The other case was in a 7-month-old boy with monostotic sphenoid FD, leading to temporary abducens nerve palsy and esotropia. In that case, it was suggested that inflammation and the associated bone remodeling were likely related to the abducens nerve palsy. In our case, although nerve damage secondary to hemorrhage or inflammatory changes could be the underlying mechanism, direct compression of the abducens nerve would be a more plausible cause, given the rapid improvement after decompression as observed in our patient. However, given the scarcity of cases, the pathophysiological process and causative factors of FD-associated cranial nerve dysfunctions remain to be elucidated.

Lessons

Among all the cranial nerves, the optic nerve is the most frequently involved in FD, with several meta-analyses emphasizing its vulnerability. Dalle Carbonare and Manisali and Amit et al. reported that the incidences of optic nerve involvement are 8% and 23.3%, respectively.3, 8 Given that visual deficits have been documented in up to 80% of patients who had FD with radiological evidence of optic nerve compression, it can be said that the presence of physical compression is deeply associated with neurological disorders.13–15 The fact that the optic nerve runs through the optic canal, a bony tunnel within the sphenoid bone, likely explains why it is the most affected nerve. On the other hand, the abducens nerve runs through the prepontine cistern and enters the lateral wall of the cavernous sinus after traversing Dorello’s canal, which is not a bony tunnel but a bow-shaped bony enclosure at the tip of the petrous part of the temporal bone with the petrosphenoidal (Gruber’s) ligament forming its roof. Notably, the temporal bone is the least commonly affected in FD.16 These facts likely explain why FD-associated abducens nerve palsy is extremely rare.8, 9

Cyst formation in FD has been sporadically reported in the literature.17–24 Cysts can develop in an acquired fashion over several years or a shorter time span, and the underlying mechanisms are predominantly speculative.18 There are two prevailing hypotheses: 1) benign fibro-osseous lesions precipitate vascular and hemodynamic alterations conducive to cyst formation; and 2) cystic lesions emerge from an intraosseous vascular anomaly, resulting in intramedullary hemorrhage.21, 22, 25 Notably, a mucocele, a radiological mimic of a true cyst, can also occur in association with FD and cause symptoms.26 According to the literature, the incidence of cyst formation ranges from 5% to 8% among patients with FD.19, 20 It remains unclear whether there is a specific location of FD that is more likely to develop cysts, but cyst formation in a variety of craniofacial FDs has been reported to date.18, 23, 24, 27, 28 Of note, one important clinical feature of cystic degeneration of FD is a possible rapid increase in cyst size and resultant neurological deficits.17, 18 Although the natural course of such symptomatic FD-associated cysts remains unclear, surgical intervention usually results in good neurological recovery and thus should be considered in such situations. A reasonable duration of postoperative follow-up should be provided due to the lack of data on recurrence.

In conclusion, FD-associated abducens nerve palsy is extremely rare but can occur. A cyst can rarely form inside FD, which can subsequently cause cranial nerve dysfunction mainly due to its mass effect. Decompression surgery is likely to improve the condition and thus should be considered.
