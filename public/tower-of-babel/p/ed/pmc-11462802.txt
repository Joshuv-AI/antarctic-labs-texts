Exploring the influence of spiritual leadership, leader – member exchange, and traditionality orientation on employee voice behavior
Authors: Yue Yu, Sheena Pitafi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research builds on the idea that the exchange of resources between leaders and followers can influence the behavior of employees. Specifically, the study investigates how leaders can develop strong leader-member exchange (LMX) with their followers, and how this exchange can lead to increased employee voice behavior. The study analyzed data collected from 365 individual employees working in Chinese organization. The findings indicate that LMX acts as a mediator between spiritual leadership and employee voice behavior. The strength of this mediation, however, depends on the followers' level of traditionality orientation. Notably, the findings indicate that the effect is significant only among individuals who exhibit low traditionality. Theoretical contributions and implications for practice are discussed in later sections.Keywords: Spiritual leadership, LMX, Employee voice behavior, Traditionality orientation

Introduction

Employee voice behavior, characterized by the proactive sharing of insights, suggestions, and opinions to improve organizational performance, is essential in fostering innovation, addressing challenges, and promoting a culture of continuous improvement [1, 2]. Indeed, employee voice is considered critical for organizational survival in rapidly changing markets and severe competitions [3]. Among the various factors influencing voice behavior, leadership style stands out as a pivotal element that can either motivate or stifle employee contributions [1, 4]. Notably, spiritual leadership has emerged as a key precursor to voice behavior. This leadership style goes beyond conventional managerial duties, encouraging employees to exceed mere compliance with job requirements, fostering their development and inspiring them to reach their fullest potential [5]. While extensive research has highlighted the positive effects of transformational, paternalistic, ethical, servant, and empowering leadership on employee voice behavior [6–8], spiritual leadership remains largely underexplored in this context [5]. This gap is significant given that moral and altruistic factors, often rooted in spirituality, have been shown to promote voice behavior [9], and that a sense of calling, a core component of spiritual leadership, can positively influence employee voice [10].

While, we acknowledge the role of leadership styles in promoting employee voice, the theoretical model of Morrison [9] emphasizes that the primary driver of voice behavior is prosocial or other-oriented behavior. This means that individuals are motivated to speak up to benefit others, such as colleagues, leaders, units, or organizations. In understanding how spiritual leadership influences employee voice behavior, we focus on leader-member exchange (LMX) as a mediator. LMX theory focuses on the quality of the relationship between a leader and their subordinates, emphasizing that high-quality exchanges are marked by mutual respect, trust, and obligation [11] and attention to positive change [12, 13]. Despite this, previous studies that have linked spiritual leadership with voice behavior [5] have primarily concentrated on self-oriented motives while neglecting the other-oriented motives. This is unfortunate, considering Morrison [4] argument that voice research should prioritize other-oriented motives and LMX. Hence, the principal aim of this research is to examine how spiritual leadership can foster employee voice by augmenting their other-oriented motives.

Our research specifically centers on individual-focused spiritual leadership, which encompasses the leader's conduct aimed at enabling individual followers enhance the spiritual well-being, self-awareness, and self-regulation [14]. As per the social exchange theory [15], this type of behavior is conductive to bolster employee voice behavior because it creates a sense of obligation for followers, as a gesture of reciprocity for the supportive behavior and supportive and thoughtful treatment by the leader. The impetus behind other-oriented motives can be diverse, however, aiding the leader is an imperative factor for employee voice behavior. The current research examines the concept of LMX, which characterizes the nature of the connection between a leader and their subordinates at the individual level [16]. Our proposition is that individual-focused spiritual leadership may enhance employees' voice behavior through the mediating effect of LMX.

Additionally, we posit that employees' traditionality orientation represents a crucial boundary condition that affects the mediating impact of LMX in the association between spiritual leadership and employee voice behavior Employee traditionality refers to an individual's acceptance of traditional Confucian values such as upholding authority, prioritizing safety and righteousness, securing one's future, honoring filial and ancestral duties, and recognizing male superiority [17]. Initially, we suggest that the strength of the association between spiritual leadership and LMX will be contingent on the level of employee traditionality, where a more pronounced link between the constructs will be evident when the follower's traditionality is low as opposed to high. Additionally, we contend that employee traditionality will determine whether a follower decides to express their LMX to the leader through voice behavior [17]. Consequently, we hypothesize that the relationship between LMX and voice will be moderated by employee traditionality, such that it will be stronger when employee traditionality is low rather than high. Essentially, we suggest a double-stage moderation framework, whereby employee traditionality moderates both the spiritual leadership -LMX relationship and the LMX-voice relationship.

The model of this study presented in Fig. 1 offers several contributions to the current research studies. Firstly, we enhance the knowledge on voice behavior by recognizing the mediating role of LMX that links spiritual leadership to voice behavior. By investigating LMX as a mediator, we respond to the need for more studies that explore the underlying mechanisms that drive voice behavior in organizations [18]. As such, our findings have the potential to provide valuable insights into how organizations can foster a culture of open communication and active employee engagement, leading to improved organizational outcomes.

Conceptual model

Secondly, this research study contributes to the existing literature is the introduction of employee traditionality as a significant moderator in the relationship between spiritual leadership and LMX, which provides deeper insights into the distinctive reaction followers may have towards their leader's spiritual leadership behavior. Prior studies have employed social exchange theory to elucidate the positive effects of spiritual leadership on follower behavior and perception [19, 20]; nevertheless, none have integrated potential moderators in their model, thus indicating that the favorable social exchange between the spiritual leader and the follower applies universally to all individuals. Our study, however, suggests that employee traditionality may shape the social exchange between the leader and the follower, weakening the positive influence of spiritual leadership on LMX.

Thirdly, current study also contributes to social exchange theory by shedding light on the conditions under which employees choose to express their exchange orientation to their leaders through voice behavior. Prior research has suggested that followers may demonstrate their exchange orientation by engaging in helping behavior, citizenship behavior, or creativity [18, 21, 22]. Nevertheless, the connection between LMX and voice behavior is complex due to the potentially challenging nature of voice. Exchange-oriented employees may be hesitant to express their exchange orientation through voice behavior, as it may be seen as a challenge to the leader's authority [23]. As such, we posit that the association between LMX and voice behavior is contingent upon followers' traditionality, with those possessing lower rather than higher levels of employee traditionality being more likely to express exchange through voice behavior.

Theory and hypotheses

Individual focused spiritual leadership

The present investigation is centered on the antecedent of LMX in the form of spiritual leadership behavior (SL). Spiritual leadership is leadership behavior that is directed towards the individual development and growth of followers. It involves helping followers to develop their spiritual selves, achieve their potential, and find meaning and purpose in their lives [24]. The targeted impact of this behavior on the unique characteristics and abilities of each individual follower, with coaching tailored accordingly [25]. Therefore, the spiritual leadership behavior is likely to vary across followers, depending on their unique qualities and needs.

The current investigation centers on the personal interaction that occurs between the leader and the individual follower, and observes the mediating effect of LMX (LMX). In accordance with previous research [24–26], our study employs individual-focused spiritual leadership as the independent construct. Individual-focused spiritual leadership is indicative of the leader's empathetic and supportive treatment of the follower, taking into account the distinctive strengths, requirements, and aspirations of each follower [27].

Individual-focused Spiritual Leadership is characterized by four dimensions [26, 28]. The first dimension is vision, which refers to the leader's ability to inspire and motivate the followers by articulating an attractive and compelling future. The second dimension is hope/faith, which involves the leader's ability to instill a sense of optimism, trust, and confidence in the followers. The third dimension is compassion, which refers to the leader's ability to demonstrate care and concern for the well-being of the followers, and to create a supportive and nurturing environment. Finally, the fourth dimension is "unconditional love," which refers to the leader's ability to create a sense of belonging and acceptance among the followers.

Leader-member exchange

Social exchange theory argues that LMX is a key mechanism underlying the relationship between leaders and followers [16]. Specifically, LMX is thought to represent a social exchange relationship between leaders and followers, where leaders provide resources, support, and opportunities for personal growth to their high-quality followers in exchange for their loyalty, commitment, and high levels of performance [16, 29]. Previous study has shown that higher levels of LMX are associated with various positive outcomes, such as higher job satisfaction [16], organizational commitment [29], and task performance [30]. Dienesch and Liden [31] argued that reciprocity is an essential element in the social exchange process between leaders and followers. LMX is built on the mutual exchange of resources, support, and loyalty between leaders and followers, with both parties engaging in behavior that benefits the other [16, 32]. Under LMX, leaders and followers involve in a continuous exchange of resources, with both parties contributing to and benefiting from the relationship [33]. This exchange process is driven by the sense of obligation and commitment that is built through the high-quality exchange of resources and support between leaders and followers [33]. In turn, this sense of obligation and commitment is strengthened by the leader’s demonstration of spiritual leadership behaviors, which foster the development of positive emotions, trust, and respect within the LMX relationship [34]. Consistent with arguments of Eisenberger numerous studies rooted in social exchange theory have centered on the concept of LMX as the underlying mechanism in the exchange process between leaders and followers. For instance, Gupta and Sharma [35] suggested that high-quality relationships between leaders and followers can facilitate positive work outcomes. Shahi and Bhatti [36] found that LMX mediated the link between transformational leadership and employee creativity. In another study Avolio, Walumbwa [37] indicated that when leaders are authentic, it promotes a positive relationship with followers, leading to greater job satisfaction. Hence, building on social exchange theory's theoretical framework [38], and the results of previous empirical studies [35–37].

Drawing on social exchange theory, Eisenberger, Karagonlar [33] defined LMX as a mechanism that represents the quality of the social exchange relationship between a leader and a follower, characterized by mutual obligations and expectations. LMX refers to the degree to which a follower perceives a high-quality personalized relationship with the leader that is based on trust, respect, and mutual support. We argue that LMX can be conceptualized as a form of social capital that the follower can draw upon in terms of access to resources, information, and support from the leader, as well as the leader's willingness to invest time, effort, and resources in the follower's development and well-being. In accordance with the principles of social exchange theory, LMX reflects a reciprocal and mutually beneficial relationship between the leader and the follower, where both parties perceive that they are receiving more benefits than costs from the exchange relationship. Hence, we hypothesize:

H1: Leader member exchange mediates the relationship between spiritual leadership and employee voice behavior.

Spiritual leadership and leader-member exchange: The moderating role of employee traditionality orientation

Our proposed model posits that the effectiveness of individual-focused spiritual leadership in enhancing LMX is moderated by the employee's traditionality orientation. Traditionality, which reflects an individual's adherence to conventional values, norms, and customs in their work-related attitudes and behaviors [17], can significantly influence how followers perceive and respond to leadership [39]. This construct includes aspects such as acceptance of authority, respect for hierarchical structures, and adherence to traditional norms of social conduct [40]. Traditionality encompasses cognitive attitudes, value orientations, and behavioral dispositions [41]. Prior research suggests that individuals with high traditionality are more inclined to accept hierarchical social orders and exhibit greater deference to authority. In contrast, those with low traditionality tend to seek more egalitarian and personalized relationships with their leaders [42].

Previous studies have indicated that employee traditionality is a consistent personal attribute that is unlikely to be impacted by situational factors such as the behavior of leaders [42]. Additionally, there is evidence indicating that traditionality plays a crucial role in determining how followers respond to specific leader behaviors [17, 39, 42]. Liu, Kwan [43] found that the relationship between abusive supervision and followers' psychological health and job satisfaction was stronger for those with lower traditionality, suggesting that traditionality may moderate the impact of certain leader behaviors on followers. Chen and Li [44] found that the connection between spiritual leadership and followers' perceptions of procedural justice was moderated by followers' traditionality, with a more substantial positive relationship for individuals with lower traditionality. Expanding on prior studies, we suggest that the association between individual-focused spiritual leadership and LMX may vary depending on followers' traditionality. We hypothesize that individuals with low traditionality will exhibit a stronger positive relationship between spiritual leadership and LMX for following reasons.

Employees with low traditionality value reciprocal relationships and view the leader–follower relationship as an exchange of resources, guided by the norm of reciprocity [45]. They expect personalized treatment from their leaders, including support, feedback, and autonomy [41]. In this context, the leader offers resources such as support, feedback, and autonomy, while the follower reciprocates with effort, performance, and citizenship behaviors [43]. Consequently, such followers are particularly sensitive to the quality of the treatment they receive from the leader, which could impact their overall perception of the relationship [46]. According to Fry and Cohen [34], spiritual leadership, which emphasizes treating followers as unique individuals and investing in their personal growth, aligns well with these expectations. Consequently, low traditionality followers are likely to experience a stronger positive relationship between spiritual leadership and LMX. High-quality LMX is fostered when leaders show genuine concern for followers' personal and professional development, leading to enhanced follower engagement and performance [16].

In contrast, followers with high traditionality expect top-down communication and maintain a greater distance from their leaders. They are accustomed to more role-constrained interactions and hierarchical structures [41]. Despite this, high traditionality individuals may still respond positively to spiritual leadership due to their inherent respect for authority and loyalty. However, their adherence to traditional norms may buffer them against negative impacts when spiritual leadership is lacking [41, 47]. High traditionality followers are less likely to be influenced by the absence of spiritual qualities and may demonstrate a relatively stable LMX even without strong spiritual leadership [48]. In support, prior research has indicated that compared to low traditionality followers, high traditionality followers demonstrate a weaker positive relationship between spiritual leadership and their perception of procedural justice [40, 49]. Additionally, Liu, Kwan [43] found that abusive supervision has a weaker negative effect on supervisory interpersonal justice ratings for high traditionality individuals. This could attribute to employee adherence to traditional roles and expectations of deference, respect, and loyalty to authority figures. Hence, we hypothesize:

H2: Employee traditionality weakens the positive relationship between spiritual leadership and leader member exchange, such that the relationship is weaker for followers with high traditionality, rather than low traditionality.

Leader-member exchange and employee voice behavior: The moderating role of employee traditionality orientation

In line with social exchange theory, the quality of the LMX) relationship is a pivotal determinant of employee voice behavior [16]. Employees with high-quality LMX relationships often experience greater psychological closeness and mutual trust with their leader, motivating them to engage in voice behavior [50, 51]. Empirical research consistently supports this notion, showing that high-quality LMX relationships are positively associated with proactive behaviors, including voice behavior [52, 53]. However, the relationship between LMX and voice behavior is complex and influenced by various factors, one of which is employee traditionality.

While high-quality LMX relationships can foster open communication and cooperation, leading to positive outcomes like increased job satisfaction and organizational commitment, they can also create a paradox [13, 54]. Employees in these relationships might feel pressure to conform to their leader's expectations, potentially inhibiting them from voicing dissenting opinions [11, 55]. As such, this paradox highlights the importance of exploring boundary conditions, such as employee traditionality, that can affect the extent to which LMX translates into voice behavior.

We posit that employees with low traditionality are characterized by a willingness to challenge the status quo and engage in open, two-way communication with their leaders. These employees view voice behavior as a critical mechanism for fulfilling their part of the social exchange in a high-quality LMX relationship [56] and are motivated to establish a durable interpersonal relationship with the leader [31, 57]. We assert that these employees believe that their leaders value their input and are receptive to feedback, which encourages them to express their thoughts and concerns candidly. These employees are more likely to perceive that their leaders welcome and appreciate their voice behavior, viewing it as integral to their role in the decision-making process [58]. They hold the belief that the leader values their input and feedback, and as such, they feel that they should candidly share their thoughts and concerns with their leader [59]. Consequently, the positive relationship between LMX and voice behavior is expected to be stronger for employees with low traditionality. They leverage voice behavior as a means to not only contribute to organizational improvement but also to solidify their standing and rapport with their leaders.

Conversely, employees who exhibit high levels of traditionality are less inclined to view voice behavior as a suitable approach to cultivate high-quality exchange relationships with their leaders. This is due to their tendency to adhere to the leader's instructions without question, expecting all decisions to be made solely by the leader [60, 61]. These employees are obligated to demonstrate deference and obedience to their leader, rather than expressing their opinions and ideas, as required by their exchange role [41, 62]. High traditionality followers may perceive voice behavior as a violation of the hierarchical structure, or even an affront to the leader's authority [43]. Consequently, despite their desire to build exchange relationships with their leaders, high traditionality employees are less likely to engage in voice behavior to establish such relationships. In conclusion, it is reasonable to anticipate that employees with low rather than high traditionality will be more inclined to utilize voice behavior in creating high-quality exchange relationships with their leaders.

H3: Employee traditionality weakens the positive relationship between LMX and voice, such that the relationship is weaker for employee with high traditionality, and stranger for employee with low traditionality.

In summary, our proposition is that the moderating influence of employee traditionality is not only applicable to the path from spiritual leadership to LMX, but also to the path from LMX to voice. The hypotheses suggest that a moderated mediation process occurs, as employee traditionality plays a crucial role in determining the validity of the mediation effect of LMX. For followers with low traditionality, who are more receptive to their leader’s conduct and are more likely to view voice behavior as a proper means of establishing an exchange relationship, LMX is more likely to transmit the beneficial effect of individual-focused spiritual leadership on voice behavior. Conversely, for followers with high traditionality, whose LMX is less affected by their leader's behavior, and who may consider voice behavior to be disrespectful towards their leader, the mediation effect of LMX is less pronounced. This model of moderated mediation provides an explanation of why and when individual-focused spiritual leadership behavior can motivate follower voice behavior.

H4: Employee traditionality weakens the indirect relationship between spiritual leadership and follower voice via LMX, such that the indirect relationship is weaker for followers with higher, rather than lower, employee traditionality.

Research methods

Data collection procedures

Following the procedure of previous studies [63–66], we used a survey approach to collect data from Chinese employees working in different industries. This approach provides for the capturing of the essence of the fieldwork, hence it is considered to be an effective way for carrying out this study [67, 68]. Data were gathered from workers employed in developing region. In the initial stage, 20 organizations in the region were contacted through academic industrial collaboration. After making phone calls to senior executives and sending them an email invitation outlining the objective of this research and seeking their voluntary participation. The managers were convinced in the confidentiality of the data acquired from their workers. Managers from seven different firms decided to take part in the survey as a result of this approach. Before conducting survey, author has discussed the purpose of study with leading scholars working on innovation projects. Several suggestions and recommendations have been adopted in the survey items. Author has designed the entire survey in three distinct sections. The first section consists a cover letter that describes the objective of research. Second part included the basic concept of the study. The third part covered the information related to participants that included demographics, education, age, and their job experience. Final section focused on survey items of all the constructs. Before data collection, author invited the different participants from different industries to take part in this research study. Nevertheless, prior initiating the final data collection procedure, a questionnaire pre-testing was done on 50 respondents and results was found suitable.

During the period of November 2022 to December 2022, we have sent the 475 questionnaires to participant and 400 has been received back. Some responses were eliminated because they were inaccurately answered out or left blank. Consequently, 365 responses were recognized as acceptable for the final analysis.

Research instruments

To construct the questionnaire of this study, we first constructed an English version of the questionnaire using pre-existing measurement items. As the respondents of this study were Chinese, therefore we translated the English version of questionnaire into Chinese version. Following previous research [69–71], to check the similarity of questionnaire, the Chinese version of questionnaire was translated back into English language. Finally, by comparing both the versions of questionnaire it was observed both have same meaning. Finally, Chinese version of questionnaire was used for data collection procedure. Further, following the procedure of previous studies entire questionnaire was measured using 5-point Likert scale, as scholars observed that 7-point and 5-point scale generates similar results [72]. The detail of measurement items is shown below.

Spiritual leadership: Spiritual leadership was measured using 9 items of Pawar [48]. Sample item of this scale is “Through his/her behaviors toward subordinates, my leader expresses my leader concern for subordinates.”

Leader-member exchange: LMX was measured using 7 items of Wayne, Shore [29]. Sample item of this scale is “My working relationship with my manager is effective”.

Employee voice behavior: The employee voice behavior was measured using 5 items of Van Dyne and LePine [73]. Example item of this scale is “This employee communicates opinions about work issues to others in this group even if his/her opinion is different and others in the group disagree with him/her”.

Employee traditionality: The employee traditionality was measured using 5 items of Farh, Earley [60]. Example item of this scale is “The best way to avoid mistakes is to follow the instructions of senior persons”.

Control variable: Following previous studies on leadership and voice behavior, age, gender, education and experience are used as a control variable in this study [74–77].

Analysis of common method bias

Since we employed a single instrument to obtain cross-sectional data, common method bias (CMB) may have been involved in the response [81]. Scholars noted that common method bias (CMB) occurs when the same method is used to collect data in a research study [81, 82]. As a result, we employed a multifaceted approach to assess the possible issue of CMB in the data set. Initially, the survey was developed with the objective of reducing CMB at the responder stage. One backward item was employed to capture participants' attention while they completing the questionnaire. Secondly, Harman's single-factor assessment was utilized to evaluate the possible issue of CMB in the dataset. This technique revealed that there is no CMB issues since first factor represent exactly 34.63% of the overall variation, which is significantly below the threshold value of 50% [81, 83]. Additionally, the authors examined the variance inflation factor (VIF), and the findings revealed that VIF values were less than the minimum values 3.3 [62, 84], implying that CMB, and multicollinearity is not a serious problem in the current investigation. As a consequence, all the analysis results suggests that this study is free from CMB.

Discussion, implications, limitations

Discussion

In order to effectively respond to the changing business landscape, foster creativity, and proactively identify issues, organizations require input and information from employees across all levels. Leaders face the challenge of motivating and incentivizing their followers to express their opinions, ideas, and concerns related to work. The present study aimed to investigate how employee traditionality orientation serves as a boundary condition in the relationship between spiritual leadership, LMX, and voice behavior. Our findings indicate that employee traditionality weakens the relationship between LMX and voice behavior, and in line with our hypothesis it does impact the relationship between spiritual leadership and LMX. Moreover, employee traditionality moderates the indirect relationship between spiritual leadership and voice behavior via LMX, with the indirect relationship being positive only in cases of low employee traditionality.

Theoretical implications

Our research findings contribute to the current literature in the several ways. Firstly, building upon Morrison [9] theoretical framework, which posits that voice behavior is largely driven by other-oriented motives, we posit that follower LMX can act as a mediator that links spiritual leadership to employees’ voice behavior, particularly in relation to the spiritual leadership dimensions of communicating high expectations and promoting follower development. In essence, when leaders display confidence in their followers' abilities to achieve high levels of performance and provide guidance and feedback to facilitate their growth and development, followers may feel indebted to their leader, which, in turn, prompts them to engage in voice behavior as a way of reciprocating the leader's investment in them. Also, we highlighted the crucial role of employees’ traditionality orientation as a boundary condition in the mediating role of LMX in the association among spiritual leadership and voice behavior. Moreover, our research addresses Griffin, Neal [93] call for an integrative approach to explore how leadership behavior influences voice behavior through work motivation variables, while also considering how this relationship is moderated by work-related beliefs. In this study, we respond to this call by examining the intricate processes and contextual factors that underpin the effectiveness of spiritual leadership in promoting follower voice behavior.

Secondly, consistent with our hypothesis, employee traditionality does moderate the link between spiritual leadership and LMX. Results show that the relationship between spiritual leadership and LMX is weaker with high employee traditionality orientation and stronger with low level of employee traditionality among followers. It highlights the importance of considering followers' cultural values and beliefs when examining the effects of leadership behavior on work outcomes. The results suggest that traditional values and norms may influence how followers perceive and respond to spiritual leadership behavior, particularly in terms of their relationship with the leader. This has implications for cross-cultural leadership research, as it suggests that the effectiveness of spiritual leadership may vary across different cultural contexts. Leaders who aim to promote voice behavior through spiritual leadership may need to consider the cultural values and beliefs of their followers, and adjust their leadership style accordingly. This highlights the need for culturally sensitive leadership practices that take into account the diverse backgrounds and perspectives of followers.

Thirdly, our findings have important theoretical implication regarding the conditions under which follower voice behavior is likely to occur in the context of the employee-employer exchange relationship. Specifically, our results suggest that the connection between LMX and voice behavior is contingent upon employee traditionality orientation. High levels of employee traditionality may lead followers to view voice behavior as an inappropriate means of fulfilling their obligations to the leader, as it may conflict with traditional values and norms that prioritize deference to authority. As a result, followers with high employee traditionality may choose alternative strategies to fulfill their obligations, such as complying with the leader's directives, expending additional effort on their tasks, or completing their assigned work to a high standard.

Practical implications

The present study has managerial implications that are worth considering. Firstly, managers should bear in mind the employee traditionality orientation while attempting to motivate or engage with their subordinates. Given the fact that the traditionality orientation of the employee is a critical determinant in shaping their reaction to leadership behavior, managers must strive to comprehend the traditionality orientation of each subordinate. For subordinates who exhibit low levels of employee traditionality, leaders can cultivate positive social exchange by exhibiting spiritual leadership behavior, specifically by demonstrating high expectations and a commitment to the development of subordinates [5, 94]. This will generate a sense of obligation among subordinates, motivating them to participate in voice behavior.

Secondly, study findings demonstrate that followers with high levels of employee traditionality exhibit a reluctance to voice their opinions in the presence of their leaders, even if they feel a sense of obligation to help their leaders. Given the potential benefits of voice behavior, including the provision of creative ideas, valuable information, and the identification of critical problems, organizations may need to establish alternative communication channels for these followers. This may involve the implementation of unidentified voice systems, such as online forums or suggestion boxes that to secure the identification of the individual who speaks up. Such a system can foster a sense of psychological safety among employees with high levels of traditionality, enabling them to feel secure in sharing their thoughts and concerns with their organization. This approach is consistent with the research of Detert and Burris [95], who argue that creating alternative voice channels can enhance the overall effectiveness of an organization's voice mechanism, leading to more positive outcomes for both employees and leaders.

Limitations and directions for future research

This study presents certain limitations that should be addressed while evaluating the findings. First, the cross-sectional research design used in this study does not allow us to draw any conclusions regarding the causal relationships between spiritual leadership, LMX, and voice behavior. Therefore, future scholars should use a longitudinal or experimental design to establish the causal effects of spiritual leadership on LMX and voice, respectively. Additionally, the link between spiritual leadership and LMX or voice may be reciprocal, as the exchange between follower and leader may lead to higher levels of voice and other proactive behaviors. This, in turn, may increase the level of trust from the leader and enhance the spiritual leadership behavior. Future research should investigate this potential reciprocal relationship using panel or longitudinal designs. Moreover, it is important to examine whether the mediation effect of LMX remains significant even after controlling for other previously studied mechanisms in the spiritual leadership-voice relationship, such as psychological safety and voice role perception.

A second limitation of this study is that our data was collected exclusively from Chinese employees. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings to other cultural contexts is limited. Future research should replicate our study in different countries with varying levels of employee traditionality, to examine whether the relationship between employee traditionality and the spiritual leadership-LMX-voice pathway is universal or culture-specific. Another methodology issue is that we have collected all data from single source at a same time, it will be more interesting if future scholars should gather data from multiple sources by applying longitudinal approaches. Furthermore, it may be worthwhile for future studies to examine whether employee traditionality has an impact on the perception of voice behavior as a personal responsibility, also known as voice role perception [73]. Given the emerging sub-dimensions of voice behavior such as promotive vs. prohibitive voice [45] or challenge vs. supportive voice [96], it is intriguing to explore whether employee traditionality moderates the effects of leadership on different voice dimensions. Thus, future research should investigate whether employees with varying levels of traditionality hold different perceptions of their voice roles in different types of voice behavior.

The third limitation of this study is that we only focused on the mediating role of LMX in the relationship between spiritual leadership and voice behavior, while ignoring other potential mechanisms. Prior research suggests that the positive effects of spiritual leadership on voice behavior may be attributed to different factors, such as psychological safety [97], perceived supervisor support [33], and perceived organizational support [98]. Although we controlled for psychological safety in our analysis, it is possible that the effect of spiritual leadership on voice behavior may be fully or partially mediated by these other mechanisms. Thus, future research should explore the relative importance of these different mechanisms and examine whether they operate in parallel or sequentially in the spiritual leadership-voice relationship.

Furthermore, we did not investigate the potential boundary conditions for these other mechanisms. For example, previous research has suggested that the effect of psychological safety on voice behavior may depend on employee personality traits, such as openness to experience [50] or neuroticism [99]. Thus, it is possible that the effect of spiritual leadership on voice behavior via psychological safety or other mechanisms may depend on follower individual differences, such as personality traits, values, or cultural orientation. Future research should explore these potential boundary conditions to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying the spiritual leadership-voice relationship. Overall, this limitation suggests that the mediating role of LMX in the spiritual leadership-voice relationship is just one piece of a larger puzzle. To fully understand the complex processes underlying the spiritual leadership-voice relationship, future research should adopt a more integrative approach that considers multiple mechanisms and boundary conditions.
