Effect of vitamin D deficiency on pregnancy outcomes in obese and non-obese pregnant women
Authors: Omer Bardak, Oguzhan Gunenc, Emre Uysal, Sibel Ozler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity and vitamin D deficiency are significant risk factors for adverse pregnancy outcomes. While lower vitamin D levels are common in obese individuals, their combined effect on perinatal outcomes remains controversial.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the association between vitamin D deficiency and adverse perinatal outcomes in obese and non-obese pregnant women.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted at SBU Konya Education and Research Hospital between June 2016 and December 2018. The study included 80 obese (BMI ≥ 30) and 80 non-obese (BMI < 30) term pregnant women without major comorbidities. Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels were measured using chemiluminescence. Group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test and chi-square test. Logistic regression analysis was used to identify risk factors for adverse perinatal outcomes.ResultsVitamin D levels were significantly lower in obese pregnant women compared to non-obese women (10.72 ± 8.24 ng/mL vs. 15.03 ± 9.83 ng/mL, p = 0.003). Higher vitamin D levels were independently associated with a reduced risk of low birth weight in the entire cohort (OR: 0.869, 95% CI: 0.747–1.012, p = 0.046), indicating a protective effect. In obese pregnant women, increased waist circumference (OR: 1.065, 95% CI: 1.000-1.134, p = 0.049) and elevated HbA1c levels (OR: 8.869, 95% CI: 1.420-15.411, p = 0.020) were identified as independent risk factors for fetal macrosomia.ConclusionMaternal vitamin D deficiency is an independent risk factor for low birth weight. In obese pregnancies, abdominal adiposity and poor glycemic control are significant predictors of fetal macrosomia. These findings highlight the potential importance of vitamin D supplementation and meticulous metabolic monitoring in obese pregnant women to improve perinatal outcomes.Keywords: Obesity, Vitamin D, Perinatal outcomes, Low birth weight, Fetal macrosomia, Pregnancy complications

Introduction

Obesity, a global public health concern, is highly prevalent among women of reproductive age [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), more than 650 million adults worldwide live with obesity, and the rate is particularly high among pregnant women [2]. Severe maternal and fetal problems, including gestational diabetes, hypertension, fetal macrosomia and an elevated chance of cesarean birth, have been linked to obesity, particularly during pregnancy [3, 4].

During pregnancy, the hormone vitamin D is essential for the health of the mother and the fetus [5]. Recent research has shown that vitamin D has immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory and metabolic benefits in addition to its traditional function of maintaining calcium homeostasis [6]. Preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, preterm birth and low birth weight are among the negative pregnancy outcomes linked to maternal vitamin D deficiency (< 20 ng/mL) [7].

Obesity and vitamin D deficiency have a complicated relationship. One of the primary causes of low serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] levels in obese people has been identified as the sequestration of vitamin D in adipose tissue and decreased bioavailability [8]. Furthermore, it has been noted that the long-term low-grade inflammation that accompanies obesity affects the metabolism of vitamin D [9]. However, the impact of vitamin D deficiency on perinatal outcomes in obese pregnant women remains a subject of debate, with contradictory findings in the literature. Although some research indicates that vitamin D deficiency and obesity enhance prenatal risks in concert [10], other studies contend that this relationship is independent [11].

While the individual effects of obesity and vitamin D deficiency on perinatal outcomes are well known, the perinatal effects of vitamin D deficiency in obese pregnant women remain unclear. While some existing studies in the literature suggest that vitamin D deficiency further increases obesity-related risks [10], other studies argue that such a relationship is not present [11]. These inconsistencies contribute to a lack of complete understanding of the clinical impact of vitamin D deficiency in obese pregnant women. Furthermore, studies on this topic are limited in societies with a high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency due to cultural and geographical factors, such as the Turkish population [12]. This retrospective cohort study aims to examine the association of vitamin D deficiency with specific perinatal outcomes, such as low birth weight and fetal macrosomia, independently of obesity status by simultaneously evaluating obese and non-obese pregnant women. Our study hypothesis is that vitamin D deficiency, which is more pronounced in obese pregnant women, further increases the risk of perinatal complications in addition to obesity itself. This study aims to fill a conceptual gap in the literature by providing evidence-based insights into the importance of vitamin D supplementation and metabolic monitoring, particularly in obese women, a high-risk group.

Materials methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study at the SBU Konya Education and Research Hospital’s Obstetrics and Gynecology Clinic between June 2016 and December 2018. The study included 80 pregnant women who were obese and 80 who were not, as long as they were delivered at term and had no other illnesses. Informed consent was obtained from all participants for the use of their medical records in scientific research. Laboratory data came from the hospital automation system, while patient and infant data came from their files. The project received local ethics approval (Date: 03.01.2019, No: 01–10).

Study group

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using participants’ pre-pregnancy weight and height. Accordingly, those with a BMI below 30 were considered non-obese and those above 30 were considered obese [13].

If the 5-minute APGAR score was < 7, the newborn was a near-miss candidate and these babies were closely monitored. Postnatally, even if the newborn was placed next to the mother, they were closely monitored for respiratory distress, such as moaning or retractions. Newborn exhibiting these symptoms were promptly evaluated. Any newborn who met any of the above criteria (low APGAR or respiratory distress) or whose pediatrician’s clinical assessment determined that additional monitoring and treatment were necessary was admitted to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU).

The following laboratory measurements were selected for investigation based on their proven pathophysiological links to obesity, vitamin D metabolism and prenatal outcomes.

Because of their established roles in maternal metabolic health and possible effects on fetal growth and placental function, vitamin D, HbA1c and lipid profile (total cholesterol, HDL, LDL and triglycerides) were evaluated [8, 15].

Parameters unrelated to the central hypothesis linking obesity, vitamin D status and important perinatal outcomes (e.g., specific micronutrients or advanced inflammatory markers) were not evaluated in this study because they are not routinely measured in our clinical setting.

Definition of adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes

Preeclampsia, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and cesarean delivery due to fetal distress or failure to progress in labor were considered poor maternal outcomes for the purposes of this study. These outcomes were selected because of their clinical relevance and their correlation with vitamin D levels and obesity [3, 7].

Adverse perinatal outcomes were defined as low birth weight (< 2500 g), fetal macrosomia (birth weight > 4000 g) and NICU admission. Criteria for NICU admission were based on the Neonatal Near Miss criteria detailed in the ‘Study Group’ section.

Laboratory measurements

Participants’ serum 25(OH)D levels were measured using the chemiluminescence method with the Siemens ADVIA Centaur XP Immunoassay System at or after the 37th week of gestation.

The cutoff value for defining vitamin D deficiency was set at < 20 ng/mL. This threshold was chosen based on a 2021 American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) committee opinion, which is a widely accepted and clinically practical guideline in obstetric practice [3, 16]. Importantly, this cutoff value is supported by more recent meta-analyses and expert reviews, which have shown that levels below 20 ng/mL continue to increase the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes such as preterm birth and low birth weight [17, 18]. While there is ongoing debate about optimal vitamin D levels, the threshold of < 20 ng/mL remains a conservative and well-established benchmark for defining deficiency in pregnant populations.

Considerations regarding measurement timing

It is important to measure vitamin D levels once in late pregnancy. This timing was chosen because it reflects maternal vitamin D levels closest to the time of birth and may be most important for immediate perinatal outcomes such as birth weight. However, this single measurement cannot capture vitamin D status during critical periods for fetal development, such as placental implantation in the first trimester or peak fetal skeletal mineralization in the second trimester. In conclusion, our findings primarily demonstrate the relationship between vitamin D status in late pregnancy and perinatal outcomes. The impact of deficiency in early or mid-gestation may be different and should be investigated in future prospective studies with serial measurements.

Statistical analysis

The IBM-Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM-SPSS) 22.0 software was utilized in this study’s data analysis. The data’s conformance to a normal distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. The mean ± standard deviation was used to express descriptive analyses for variables that were normally distributed. For values that were regularly distributed, the parametric test (Sample t-test) was employed. Pearson’s chi-square test was used to examine the categorical data, which were then displayed as n (number) and percentages (%). Risk factors for poor maternal and perinatal outcomes in pregnant women who were obese and those who were not were identified using single and multi-category logistic regression analysis. Results were deemed statistically significant if p < 0.05 and data were analyzed at 95% confidence intervals.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the association between maternal vitamin D deficiency and perinatal outcomes in obese and non-obese pregnant women. The results offer crucial details about the metabolism of vitamin D during pregnancy and the impact of obesity on the health of the fetus. When compared to data already available in the literature, the primary findings of this study show both notable similarities and some discrepancies.

First, it was shown that pregnant women who were obese had far lower vitamin D levels. This finding supports the hypothesis that vitamin D is sequestered in adipose tissue, and the higher fat mass in obese individuals reduces its bioavailability [8, 16]. Similarly, in the study conducted by Karampela et al., it was shown that serum 25(OH)D levels were low in obese individuals and vitamin D was stored in fat tissue [8]. This situation once again emphasizes the importance of vitamin D supplementation in obese pregnant women.

In this study, the finding that low birth weight was associated with vitamin D deficiency suggests that vitamin D may have a potential role in embryonic development, independent of obesity status. Numerous routes could mediate this connection. First, proper placental development and function, including trophoblast invasion and angiogenesis, depend on vitamin D [19, 20]. Fetal growth may be limited in conditions of vitamin D deficiency, potentially through mechanisms such as reduced placental blood flow and nutrition transport. Second, the intrauterine environment may be impacted by vitamin D’s immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory qualities [21, 22]. A pro-inflammatory state that has been linked to unfavorable pregnancy outcomes, such as low birth weight, may be predisposed to by a deficient vitamin D level. The findings of the current study are consistent with a meta-analysis by Fang et al., which reported a consistent association between maternal vitamin D deficiency and an increased risk of low birth weight [23]. However, they contradict a study by Vivanti et al., which found no significant association [18]. This discrepancy could be attributed to differences in study populations, the timing of vitamin D measurement, or variations in the adjustment for confounding factors such as maternal nutrition and socioeconomic status.

In the current cohort study of obese pregnant women, increased waist circumference and HbA1c levels emerged as independent risk factors for fetal macrosomia. These observations support the concept of a metabolic pathway linking maternal obesity to fetal overgrowth. Abdominal obesity is a strong marker of insulin resistance [24]. This relationship is likely mediated by maternal hyperglycemia, which facilitates increased glucose transfer across the placenta. This, in turn, could stimulate fetal pancreatic beta cells, leading to fetal hyperinsulinemia (a key growth hormone in the uterus), which promotes excessive fetal fat accumulation and macrosomia [15, 25]. This study supports the notion that glycemic control and central obesity are critical targets for interventions aimed at preventing macrosomia in obese pregnancies. These results suggest that evaluating waist circumference in addition to BMI and ensuring tight glycemic control, even in the absence of overt gestational diabetes, may be valuable in the follow-up of obese pregnant women who are at highest risk of delivering a macrosomic baby.

Interestingly, regression analysis did not find any specific demographic, clinical, or laboratory variables to be significant and independent risk factors for NICU admission in the study population. This may be attributable to the relatively homogeneous and healthy cohort, which excluded women with major chronic comorbidities. Furthermore, the underlying causes of NICU admission in the current study may have been related to transient conditions, such as respiratory distress or low Apgar scores, according to the Near Miss criteria and this may have multifactorial causes not fully captured by the maternal variables we measured. Additionally, it is important to acknowledge that we observed extremely wide confidence intervals for some variables in the regression models, particularly waist-to-hip ratio. This is a known problem in logistic regression and is often due to partial/complete separation, where an outcome is perfectly or nearly perfectly predicted by a variable in a subset of the data, or to limited sample size for certain outcomes [26]. Consequently, these specific results should be interpreted with caution. However, this methodological consideration does not diminish the robustness of our primary findings, which are based on stable and precise estimates of the associations between vitamin D, waist circumference, HbA1c and key perinatal outcomes.

Unlike some previous studies, this study found no significant association between vitamin D deficiency and maternal complications such as gestational diabetes or preeclampsia [7, 27]. However, this finding is consistent with other well-conducted studies [28]. The lack of association observed in this study may be influenced by the retrospective design of the study, the measurement of vitamin D at a single time point in late pregnancy, or the possibility that the pathophysiological effect of vitamin D on these conditions is more pronounced in the first or second trimester. Focusing on healthy women without comorbidities in this study may have also limited the variability in adverse maternal outcomes and reduced the power to detect a significant association.

Strengths of this study include the well-characterized use of obese and non-obese pregnant women with comparable demographic characteristics and the exclusion of major comorbidities, which reduced confounding factors. Furthermore, a comprehensive set of laboratory and clinical parameters relevant to the research question were assessed.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The single-center, retrospective cohort design inherently limits the generalizability of our findings and precludes the establishment of causality. One major limitation is the potential for unmeasured confounding factors. Although we matched our groups according to baseline criteria, we lacked data on potential confounding factors known to influence vitamin D status, such as socioeconomic status, precise dietary intake, physical activity and detailed measurements of sunlight exposure or seasonal variation. Additionally, vitamin D was measured only once in late pregnancy, providing no insight into vitamin D status during critical early periods of fetal development, such as the first or second trimester. Finally, as noted in our statistical analysis, some logistic regression models for variables such as waist-to-hip ratio produced unstable estimates with wide confidence intervals, which should be considered when interpreting these specific results.

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, its single-center, retrospective cohort design inherently limits the generalizability of our findings and precludes the establishment of causality. Second, a major limitation is our inability to account for seasonality and individual sun exposure, which are primary determinants of vitamin D status. Seasonal variation in ultraviolet B radiation profoundly affects endogenous vitamin D synthesis; if the distribution of blood sampling across seasons differed between obese and non-obese groups, this could have introduced significant bias. Additionally, we lacked data on other potential confounders such as precise dietary vitamin D intake, physical activity, and cultural clothing practices that affect sun exposure. Third, vitamin D was measured only once in late pregnancy, providing no insight into vitamin D status during critical early periods of fetal development such as the first or second trimester. Fourth, as noted in our statistical analysis, some logistic regression models for variables such as waist-to-hip ratio produced unstable estimates with wide confidence intervals, which should be considered when interpreting these specific results.

Based on these limitations, we suggest several directions for future research. To move from association to causation and assess the effectiveness of intervention, future research should prioritize randomized controlled trials investigating the impact of vitamin D supplementation on perinatal outcomes in obese pregnant women. Prospective cohort studies with serial measurements of vitamin D throughout pregnancy (ideally starting in the first trimester) are needed to determine the critical timing and effects of deficiency, and these studies must be designed to systematically collect and adjust for seasonal variation, sun exposure, dietary intake, and physical activity. Furthermore, investigating the interactions between vitamin D, insulin resistance, specific inflammatory markers and adipokines may elucidate the underlying mechanisms. Finally, our findings should be validated in larger, multicenter and more diverse populations and complemented by molecular studies of the role of vitamin D in placental function and fetal growth.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that maternal vitamin D deficiency is independently associated with an increased risk of low birth weight. In obese pregnant women, abdominal adiposity and poor glycemic control were significant risk factors for fetal macrosomia. In light of these observed associations, vitamin D supplementation and metabolic monitoring, particularly in obese pregnant women, warrant further investigation as potential strategies to improve perinatal outcomes. However, more comprehensive studies, ideally randomized controlled trials, are needed to establish causality and elucidate the precise mechanisms of these relationships to guide clinical practice.
