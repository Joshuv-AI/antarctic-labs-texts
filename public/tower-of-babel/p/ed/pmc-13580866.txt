PEGylated Chitosan Nanoparticles: Engineering Multifunctional Platforms for Targeted Cervical and Lung Cancer Treatment
Authors: Prashant Kesharwani, Nazim Nasir, Shadma Wahab, Garima Gupta, Khang Wen Goh, Amirhossein Sahebkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPolymer‐based nanocarriers have emerged as transformative platforms in cancer nanomedicine, enabling controlled and site‐specific delivery of therapeutic agents. Among polysaccharide‐derived materials, chitosan stands out for its biodegradability, biocompatibility, and intrinsic mucoadhesive properties that promote efficient drug encapsulation and enhanced cellular internalization. Surface PEGylation the conjugation of polyethylene glycol (PEG) chains further augments the physicochemical and biological performance of chitosan nanoparticles by enhancing colloidal stability, extending systemic circulation, and reducing opsonization by the reticuloendothelial system. In addition, ligand‐mediated functionalization facilitates active tumor targeting and selective intracellular delivery, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off‐target toxicity. This review critically summarizes recent advances in the design, synthesis, and functional modification of PEGylated chitosan nanoparticles, with emphasis on their pharmacokinetic behavior, drug release mechanisms, and anticancer performance. Comparative insights into other polymeric and lipid‐based nanocarrier systems are also provided. Finally, current challenges, safety considerations, and translational perspectives are discussed, underscoring the promise of PEGylated chitosan nanoplatforms as next‐generation biomaterials for precision oncology.

Introduction

Chitosan nanoparticles (NPs) unique properties and capabilities have shown promise in cancer treatment [1]. Chitosan, a biopolymer that originates from chitin present in crustacean exoskeletons, exhibits biodegradability and biocompatibility, making it particularly suitable for drug delivery application [2]. It was observed that the body can easily absorb chitosan, and it degrades gradually without causing significant harm. The engineering of chitosan NPs could facilitate the direct delivery of anticancer drugs to tumor sites, thereby reducing systemic toxicity associated with conventional chemotherapy [3]. Furthermore, chitosan NPs may be modified with specific ligands or antibodies to achieve selective targeting of cancer cells [4]. The cationic nature of chitosan allows for complexation with negatively charged therapeutic agents, and chitosan NPs have the potential to enhance the solubility and stability of poorly soluble drugs [5]. Additionally, chitosan NPs can carry a wide range of therapeutic agents such as drug molecules, genes, and imaginary agents, enabling the combination of therapeutic and diagnostic procedures.

A novel approach to cancer drug delivery is the use of PEGylated chitosan NPs, which integrate the characteristics of chitosan with PEG [6]. PEGylation is the process of immobilizing PEG chains onto chitosan NPs to enhance their stability in biological environments and prolong their circulation time in the bloodstream [6]. The PEG provides protection to NPs against recognition and elimination by the immune system, therefore allowing them to exist in the bloodstream for prolonged durations [7]. The process of PEGylation may reduce the reticuloendothelial system's capacity to identify and effectively remove NPs, and the bioavailability and efficacy of chitosan NPs are enhanced by the use of PEG as a protective coating. PEG enhances the solubility of chitosan NPs in biological fluids, thereby enabling them to readily penetrate target tissues, including tumor sites [8]. Additionally, the PEG layer can be functionalized to enable the conjugation of targeting moieties such as antibodies, peptides, and small molecule ligands for active tumor targeting [4]. The encapsulation of anticancer drugs by PEGylated chitosan NPs has the potential to inhibit degradation and control the release process. This facilitates extended drug delivery at the tumor site while minimizing systemic side effects.

PEGylated chitosan NPs can exploit the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect for preferential tumor accumulation through leaky tumor vasculature and impaired lymphatic drainage [9]. However, it is important to recognize that EPR‐mediated accumulation alone may not ensure therapeutic efficacy, as successful outcomes also require efficient cellular internalization, endosomal escape and intracellular drug release. It is noteworthy that this principle is clinically evidenced by PEGylated liposomal doxorubicin (Doxil), the first clinically approved PEGylated nanomedicine, whose therapeutic outcomes have been reported to not significantly differ from free doxorubicin despite enhanced tumor accumulation, primarily attributed to exclusive reliance on passive EPR‐mediated targeting without active receptor recognition and poor intracellular drug release. In contrast, PEGylated chitosan nanoparticles may offer distinct advantages over liposomal systems, including inherent endosomolytic properties that facilitate intracellular drug release, versatile surface functionalization with active targeting ligands thereby enabling receptor‐mediated endocytosis, and pH‐responsive drug release behavior, thereby ensuring preferential drug liberation in the acidic tumor microenvironment. This limitation necessitates complementary strategies such as active targeting through ligand functionalization to enhance intracellular drug delivery. The conjugation of targeting ligands to PEG chains enables receptor‐mediated endocytosis, thereby facilitating intracellular drug delivery beyond passive accumulation mechanisms. The PEG coating mitigates the interaction between the NPs and the reticuloendothelial system, therefore enhancing their localization inside the tumor [10] This customized approach enhances the effectiveness of the therapy by targeted delivery of the medication directly to cancer cells.

Controlling the plasma concentration of anticancer drugs is critical for achieving therapeutic efficacy while minimizing side effects [11]. The use of PEGylated chitosan NPs is an innovative approach of drug delivery that may help in the maintenance of optimal plasma concentrations of anticancer medicines for prolonged duration. According to recent studies, the drug release rate from PEGylated chitosan NPs can be customized by modifying their composition including the degree of PEGylation, the formulation of the anticancer treatment and the design of the NP matrix [12]. This permits exact customization of the plasma concentration profile to meet treatment needs. PEGylation improves the intrinsic stability of chitosan NPs once they reach the circulatory system and thereby prevents early breakdown of the NPs and the anticancer treatment they carry, ensuring the medication's long‐term therapeutic effectiveness [13]. The ability to maintain plasma concentration for an extended duration allows for the optimization of dose regimens. It is possible to achieve a harmonic balance between efficacy and safety by adjusting the drug's release and circulation time, perhaps reducing the frequency of administration [14]. Ensuring the maintenance of appropriate plasma concentrations is critical for achieving and maintaining drug levels within the therapeutic range, which is critical for maximizing efficacy while minimizing adverse effects [14].

Enhanced stability, reduced immunogenicity, and customisability for targeted drug administration make PEGylated chitosan NPs a promising advancement in cancer therapy. Nevertheless, challenges in formulation, manufacturing, and regulatory approval continue to exist. Notwithstanding specific challenges, ongoing research and development are expected to expand their applications and effectiveness in the treatment of cancer and other diseases. In this review we have analyzed in detail the applications of PEGylated chitosan NPs in treating and targeting cervical and lung cancers with emphasis on their therapeutic efficacy, anticancer mechanisms, drug delivery dynamics and pharmacokinetic behavior. Furthermore, current challenges, translational perspectives, and future directions are discussed to underscore the promise of PEGylated chitosan nanoplatforms for precision oncology (Figure 1).

Method of Producing Drug‐Incorporated PEGylated Chitosan NPs

The production of drug‐incorporated PEGylated chitosan NPs involves many essential processes, each of which plays a vital role in ensuring the activity, stability, and controlled release of the medication. The material undergoes a chemical modification when PEG chains are combined with chitosan [15]. Typically, coupling agents like carbodiimides (e.g., EDC or DCC) and cross‐linking agents achieve this. The reaction parameters, namely pH and temperature, are meticulously fine‐tuned to achieve the optimal degree of PEGylation [16]. Initially chitosan has to be dissolved in an acidic solution like acetic acid, then PEG and a coupling agent has to be added into the solution. Next, the solution is mechanically stirred and maintain at suitable temperatures to facilitate the PEGylation reaction [17]. Next, the PEGylated chitosan could be refined by dialysis or precipitation to remove any unreacted PEG and coupling agents. To incorporated drug into the PEGylated chitosan NPs, PEGylated chitosan is completely dissolved in an aqueous solvent [18]. On the other hand, drug is either dissolved or disperse in an appropriate solvent, separately. Then chitosan solution is combined with drug solution under controlled conditions like stirring or sonication to generate NPs. Occasionally, self‐assembly techniques are employed as it easy and scalable for bulk production of NPs. Hydrophobic effects and electrostatic forces control the self‐assembly of PEGylated chitosan and the drug into NPs. When the medicine and chitosan have opposite charges, electrostatic assembly can be employed. The pharmaceutical compounds are introduced into a PEGylated chitosan solution, which leads to ionic interactions that produce NPs. Next dynamic light scattering (DLS) is utilized to figure out how the NPs' sizes are spread out, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) or transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis is done to look at their shape. The extent of drug incorporation into the NPs can be quantified using HPLC or UV–Vis spectroscopy (Mourdikoudis, M. Pallares, and K. Thanh 2018). Furthermore, in vitro drug release analyses is performed to determine the drug temporal release characteristics from the NPs [19]. Apart from these properties, the physical and chemical stability of the NPs under various conditions (such as temperature and light) has to be studied to verify their integrity and effective drug release properties. Next, produced NPs' biocompatibility is evaluated by conducting cell viability tests and in vivo studies to ensure their safety for use. In short, the process of making drug‐incorporated PEGylated chitosan NPs includes PEGylating chitosan, adding the drug to the NPs through nanoprecipitation, self‐assembly, or electrostatic assembly, and then carefully characterizing the NPs to make sure they work as well as possible. Each phase of the process is crucial for generating NPs with the required drug delivery properties and therapeutic efficacy.

Chitosan as a Nanocarrier to Treat Various Cancers

The ability of chitosan and its derivatives to improve absorption, stabilize medication components, and stimulate drug release makes them preferred as drug carriers in cancer treatment. Several anticancer drugs that are conjugated with chitosan and its derivatives are much localized at the cancer site and may slowly release the drug from the conjugation, which leads to better anticancer activity with fewer side effects than before [20]. New research has demonstrated that integrating nanotechnology with chitosan and its derivatives has the potential to overcome medication transport barriers, boosting therapeutic effectiveness [21]. PEGylated Chitosan NPs for Cervical Cancer Treatment

Methotrexate (MTX), functioning as an antimetabolite and antifolate, can be transported to the cytoplasm where it interacts with dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR). This interaction hampers folic acid (FA) metabolism, thereby obstructing the synthesis of nucleic acids [46]. Conversely, Mitomycin (MMC), a bio‐reductively activated compound, can enter the nuclei and interact with DNA, thereby inhibiting DNA synthesis and preventing nuclear division. By combining the drugs, Jia and their team synthesized PEGylated chitosan NPs (PEG‐CH‐NPs) to encapsulate both MTX and MMC, while MTX additionally served as a tumor‐targeting ligand. The particle size measured 215.0 ± 7.1 nm with globular morphology and zeta potential of 32.33 ± 1.68 mV. The MMC drug loading content was 37.54% ± 0.03%, and MTX drug loading content was 10.40% ± 0.03%. It was observed that most drugs remained encapsulated during circulation. In vitro drug release demonstrated that without proteases, no more than 15% MMC and 10% MTX were released within 120 h, whereas with proteases, approximately 40% MMC and 30% MTX were released within 24 h, indicating preferential intracellular drug release mediated by endo/lysosomal proteases abundant in tumor cells. When HeLa cells were treated with FITC‐conjugated (MTX + MMC)‐PEG‐CH‐NPs, fluorescence intensity was significantly increased, indicating that MTX modification enhanced cellular uptake via folic acid receptor‐mediated endocytosis. Cell viability tests showed concentration and time‐dependent cytotoxicity. The (MTX + MMC)‐PEG‐CH‐NPs exhibited significantly enhanced cytotoxicity against HeLa cells compared to free MTX + MMC, attributed to improved cellular uptake via receptor‐mediated endocytosis and sustained intracellular drug release. In H22 tumor‐bearing Kunming mice administered at 4 mg/kg (MMC‐eq dose) every 2 days for 2 times, tumor weights after 7 days were 0.283 ± 0.039 g for (MTX + MMC)‐PEG‐CH‐NPs versus 0.693 ± 0.054 g for controls, achieving a 59.2% tumor inhibition rate (p < 0.05) compared to 27.8% for MMC‐PEG‐CS‐NPs and 8.24% for free MTX + MMC. Importantly, no significant body weight loss or organ toxicity was observed in the NPs group, while free drugs showed pronounced toxicity. Histopathological examination revealed extensive necrotic regions in (MTX + MMC)‐PEG‐CH‐NPs treated tumors, demonstrating notably higher efficacy in promoting cell apoptosis and inhibiting cell proliferation [22] (Figure 2).

Methotrexate (MTX) is also clinically utilized as a stoichiometric inhibitor of dihydrofolate reductase and is commonly prescribed for cancer therapy. The hydrophilic segment of polymeric NPs minimizes recognition by the RES, enabling long‐lasting circulation in the blood plasma [47]. PEG‐drug conjugates provide numerous benefits, such as extended presence in the body and decreased breakdown by metabolic enzymes, and minimized protein immunogenicity. In an instance of research, Chen and colleagues developed methotrexate NPs utilizing chitosan (CH) and methoxypoly (ethylene glycol) (mPEG) as a carrier system to enhance targeting and prolong plasma circulation time. The NPs, with a diameter of 210 nm and a surface charge of +40 mV, exhibited a narrow size distribution. This positive surface charge facilitated enhanced cellular association and internalization through electrostatic interaction with negatively charged cell membrane. Additionally, PEGylation reduced the zeta potential by shielding the positive charges with PEG chains. According to flow cytometry analysis, it was observed that MTX@mPEG‐CH NPs exhibited significantly enhanced intracellular uptake with geometrical mean fluorescence intensity of 16.68 ± 0.03, which was approximately 4.7‐fold higher than mPEG‐CS NPs (3.53 ± 0.02) and 1.7‐fold higher than FA‐mPEG‐CH NPs (9.81 ± 0.04), suggesting superior targeting and sustained intracellular uptake. This superior uptake was attributed to MTX‐mediated folate receptor targeting. The presence of MTX@mPEG‐CH NPs had a notable impact on cell viability, showing concentration‐dependent anticancer activity. The results demonstrated markedly enhanced cytotoxicity compared to free MTX at equivalent drug concentrations, with cell viability decreasing significantly at concentrations of 5, 10, and 20 μM (p < 0.01). Furthermore, the superior cytotoxic performance was attributed to enhanced cellular internalization via folate receptor‐mediated endocytosis and sustained intracellular drug release. The number of MTX‐loaded NPs transferred into HeLa cells was approximately twice that observed with FA‐mPEG‐CH NPs. These findings demonstrated that MTX@mPEG‐CH NPs effectively entered tumor tissues, showing superior performance compared to FA as a targeting ligand for nanocarriers [23].

To further validate the Janus role of MTX, in which MTX simultaneously functions as both a cancer‐specific targeting ligand and therapeutic anticancer agent, Luo et al. systematically investigated this dual functionality in PEGylated chitosan delivery systems. The (MTX + PEG)‐CS‐NPs, with a particle size of 213.4 nm and drug loading content of 7.23% ± 0.11%, exhibited protease‐mediated intracellular drug release characteristics. It was observed that less than 10% of MTX was released within 48 h under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), thereby minimizing premature drug release during circulation. Importantly, at a 10 μg/mL drug concentration, the formulation demonstrated markedly selective cytotoxicity, reducing HeLa cervical cancer cell viability to approximately 50% while maintaining MC 3T3‐E1 normal cell viability at 95%–100% (p < 0.05). In contrast, free MTX reduced cell viability to approximately 60% for both cell types, confirming that the MTX‐based nanoparticle system achieved cancer‐selective drug delivery through folate receptor‐mediated targeting [25] (Figure 3).

Mechanism of Janus role of the (MTX + PEG)‐CS‐NPs. Once intravenously administrated, it was anticipated that the (MTX + PEG)‐CS‐NPs were accumulated at the tumor site by the EPR effect. Prior to the cellular take, the (MTX + PEG)‐CS‐NPs served similarly as a targeted drug delivery system, in which MTX can function as a targeting moiety and selectively transport the NPs to the target cells. Once internalized into the target cells, the (MTX + PEG)‐CS‐NPs served similarly as a prodrug system, in which MTX would be released inside the cells and function as a therapeutic anticancer agent. Additionally, the protease‐mediated drug release could ensure that MTX timely changes its role from targeting (via FA receptor‐mediated endocytosis) to anticancer (inhibiting DHFR activity and stop FA cycle). This work systematically revealed the unanticipated targeting coordinated with anticancer efficiency of Janus‐like MTX in vitro. (Reproduced with permission from [25]).

Doxorubicin (Dox) is a type of anthracycline antibiotic frequently employed in chemotherapy treatments. Nanocarriers modified with PEG and poloxamer are increasingly being recognized for their potential to enhance the efficacy of cancer treatments [48]. In the current research, Scheeren et al. engineered pH‐responsive chitosan nanoparticles incorporating a lysine‐based surfactant (77KS) to achieve stimuli‐triggered doxorubicin release in the acidic tumor microenvironment. The formulations, including PEGylated (PEG‐DOX‐CS‐NPs) and poloxamer‐modified (Polox‐DOX‐CS‐NPs) variants, exhibited particle sizes ranging from 197.5 to 226.4 nm with an encapsulation efficiency of 62%–67%. In vitro release studies demonstrated distinct release behavior across different pH conditions. At physiological pH 7.4, approximately 71% of doxorubicin was released within 24 h, whereas accelerated release patterns were observed under acidic conditions, with 97% and 100% cumulative release achieved at pH 6.6 and pH 5.4 within 6 h, respectively. This pH‐triggered behavior was attributed to the ionization properties of 77KS combined with chitosan's pH‐sensitive swelling mechanism, which increased particle diameter from 178 nm to 310 nm upon exposure to pH 5.4.

The cytotoxicity evaluation in HeLa cervical cancer cells revealed concentration‐dependent antitumor activity. At both tested drug concentrations (1 and 10 μg/mL), DOX‐CS‐NPs, PEG‐DOX‐CS‐NPs, and Polox‐DOX‐CS‐NPs generally demonstrated enhanced cytotoxic efficacy compared to free doxorubicin, indicating improved therapeutic potential of the encapsulated formulations. Importantly, unloaded chitosan nanoparticles maintained cell viability above 85% at concentrations up to 200 μg/mL. These findings confirmed minimal inherent carrier toxicity and validated that the observed cytotoxicity originated from the encapsulated therapeutic agent [24].

PEGylated Chitosan NPs for Lung Cancer Treatment

The high levels of transferrin receptor (TfR) expression on cancerous cells make this glycoprotein an appealing and efficient target for the targeted delivery of anti‐cancer drugs to rapidly dividing cells [49]. Nag and their team designed paclitaxel‐loaded PEG‐chitosan nanoparticles, which were then conjugated with transferrin for efficient delivery in treating lung carcinoma cells. The mean particle sizes of PTX‐NP‐PEG‐Tf and PTX‐NP‐PEG were 340.7 ± 2.7 nm and 309.5 ± 4.1 nm respectively. In vitro drug release studies demonstrated sustained release characteristics, with 67.14% ± 0.9% paclitaxel released from PTX‐NP‐PEG‐Tf within 48 h under physiological conditions, thereby minimizing premature drug release during systemic circulation. It was observed that the steric PEG barrier effectively inhibited rapid uptake by mononuclear phagocyte systems, thus prolonging circulation time.

In vitro cytotoxicity assays against HOP‐62 human non‐small cell lung cancer cells revealed concentration‐dependent anticancer activity. The IC50 value of PTX‐NP‐PEG‐Tf was 0.3 μM, representing approximately 11.7‐fold enhancement in potency compared to free paclitaxel (IC50 = 3.5 μM). Furthermore, the transferrin‐targeted formulation exhibited superior cytotoxicity compared to PTX‐NP‐PEG (IC50 = 2.0 μM). The transferrin ligand bound to overexpressed TfR on lung cancer cells, facilitating receptor‐mediated endocytosis and enhanced intracellular drug delivery.

In vivo biodistribution studies in rats confirmed lung accumulation. At 8 h post‐administration, PTX‐NP‐PEG‐Tf achieved drug concentrations of 519.7 ± 4.3 μg/g in lung tissue, representing approximately 2.65‐fold higher accumulation compared to free paclitaxel (196.1 ± 2.6 μg/g). Additionally, pharmacokinetic analysis revealed extended circulation half‐life (8.29 versus 5.81 h) with a 41% increase in systemic exposure (AUC: 5650.0 versus 4006.4 μg·h/mL). These findings confirmed that transferrin‐conjugated PEGylated chitosan nanoparticles effectively combined passive EPR‐mediated accumulation with active receptor‐targeted delivery for enhanced lung cancer therapy [32].

The αvβ3 integrins are highly expressed in angiogenic endothelial cells and tumor cells compared to healthy cells, which makes them attractive targets for siRNA administration in cancer treatment [50]. Ragelle and their team developed αvβ3 integrin‐targeted PEGylated chitosan–poly (ethylene imine) hybrid nanoparticles for siRNA delivery to non‐small cell lung cancer cells. The nanoparticles were functionalized with arginine–glycine–aspartate (RGD) peptide or RGD peptidomimetic (RGDp) ligands to exploit αvβ3 integrin overexpression on tumor cells. The formulations exhibited mean diameters ranging from 165 to 211 nm with polydispersity indices below 0.20, indicating monodisperse size distributions. Poly (ethylene imine) was incorporated to enhance the transfection efficiency of the chitosan‐based carrier system.

The gene silencing evaluation in H1299 human non‐small cell lung cancer cells demonstrated concentration‐dependent therapeutic efficacy. At DsiRNA concentrations of 150 nM and above, it was observed that both RGD‐targeted and RGDp‐targeted nanoparticles achieved approximately 90% EGFP silencing. In contrast, non‐targeted nanoparticles showed minimal gene knockdown regardless of applied concentration. Quantitative analysis using stem‐loop reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction was performed to determine intracellular siRNA levels. The results demonstrated that at 100 nM and above, targeted nanoparticles delivered approximately two‐fold higher amounts of intracellular siRNA compared to non‐targeted formulations. It was observed that approximately 2.7 × 105 DsiRNA copies per cell resulted in approximately 80% EGFP inhibition for targeted nanoparticles. The enhanced silencing efficiency was attributed to αvβ3 integrin‐mediated endocytosis, which facilitated superior intracellular delivery compared to non‐specific uptake mechanisms. Importantly, cell viability assays confirmed minimal cytotoxicity. These findings validated that observed gene silencing was RNA interference‐dependent rather than cytotoxicity‐mediated [33].

Future Perspective and Conclusion

While PEGylation enables tumor accumulation through the enhanced permeability and retention effect, recent evidence indicates that EPR‐mediated localization alone does not ensure therapeutic efficacy. It has been demonstrated that successful cancer treatment requires overcoming additional barriers including cellular internalization, endosomal escape, and intracellular drug release. The studies presented in this review validate this principle, as formulations demonstrating both enhanced tumor accumulation and active targeting mechanisms achieved superior therapeutic outcomes compared to passively targeted systems. For instance, the αvβ3 integrin‐targeted nanoparticles developed by Ragelle et al. achieved approximately 90% gene silencing, whereas non‐targeted formulations showed minimal therapeutic effect despite comparable tumor accumulation. Similarly, the MTX‐conjugated nanoparticles investigated by Luo et al. exhibited selective cytotoxicity toward cancer cells while maintaining minimal toxicity to normal cells. These findings underscore the importance of multifunctional nanoparticle design that integrates passive targeting via the EPR effect with active targeting ligands and stimuli‐responsive drug release mechanisms to achieve clinically relevant therapeutic outcomes.

Beyond these fundamental scientific considerations, the clinical translation of PEGylated chitosan nanoparticles faces substantial industrial and regulatory challenges. The scale‐up of manufacturing processes while maintaining uniform particle size, morphology, and drug‐loading efficiency presents significant technical hurdles. Industrial‐scale production requires optimization of processes such as ionic gelation, spray drying, and microfluidic synthesis to ensure batch‐to‐batch reproducibility and cost‐effectiveness. Comprehensive physicochemical characterization is essential to verify size distribution, surface charge, encapsulation efficiency, and release kinetics. The implementation of robust quality assurance systems is necessary to meet regulatory requirements established by authorities such as the FDA and EMA, which demand extensive documentation demonstrating safety, efficacy, and manufacturing consistency before granting approval. It is important to note that strict adherence to guidelines for preclinical testing, clinical trial design, and product labeling is mandatory for successful market authorization.

The biocompatibility of chitosan nanoparticles and the absence of adverse immunological responses are critical safety considerations that require comprehensive evaluation through preclinical and clinical studies. The long‐term effects of PEGylated chitosan nanoparticles on human health, including potential accumulation and biodegradation profiles, remain incompletely understood. Moreover, it has been suggested that anti‐PEG antibodies may develop following repeated administration of PEGylated formulations, potentially compromising therapeutic efficacy and necessitating alternative surface modification strategies. The development of immunogenicity represents a significant concern for chronic therapeutic applications, as pre‐existing or treatment‐induced anti‐PEG antibodies can accelerate nanoparticle clearance and reduce drug bioavailability. Therefore, thorough investigation of immunogenicity, long‐term biocompatibility, and the development of alternative surface modification strategies including zwitterionic polymers, natural polysaccharides, or biomimetic cell membrane coatings is essential for successful clinical translation of chitosan‐based nanocarriers in precision cancer therapy.
