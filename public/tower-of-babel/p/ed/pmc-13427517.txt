Programmable continuous gradient bioprinting for engineering spatially heterogeneous microenvironments
Authors: Taekyung Choi, Hyungseok Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNative tissues exhibit spatial heterogeneity in mechanical, cellular, and biochemical properties, yet reproducing such gradients in extrusion-based bioprinting remains challenging. Existing multi-material bioprinting approaches can create spatial variations in material composition, but often rely on discrete material transitions rather than continuous gradients. Here, we present a dual ball-valve mixing module that generates continuous gradients through real-time control of the mixing ratio between two precursor inks within a single-nozzle configuration. Complementary actuation of paired ball valves continuously adjusts the mixing ratio while maintaining constant extrusion conditions, transforming gradient formation into a programmable feature of the printing process. The versatility of the platform was demonstrated through gradients in mechanical stiffness, cell density, and biochemical cues. Continuous mixing enabled gradual transitions in material properties, reducing abrupt interfacial changes associated with discrete material deposition. Spatially defined cell-density distributions and graded two-population cellular interfaces were achieved while maintaining cell viability comparable to conventional extrusion bioprinting. Furthermore, a TGF-β1 gradient induced location-dependent epithelial–mesenchymal transition responses, demonstrating the ability to translate programmed biochemical gradients into spatially regulated cellular behavior. Consequently, this work establishes dynamic bioink mixing as an effective strategy for generating continuous and spatially programmable gradients, enabling the integration of material composition, cellular organization, and biochemical signaling within a single extrusion-based bioprinting process.Keywords: Functionally graded bioink, Extrusion bioprinting, Ball valve flow control, Tissue microenvironment engineering

Introduction

Biological tissues are heterogeneous structures with composition, microstructure, orientation, density, and mechanical properties that continuously vary with spatial location [1,2]. Recreating such spatial continuity in engineered constructs is critical for controlling stress distribution and transport, as well as spatially patterned cellular behaviors, yet it remains challenging to implement reproducibly during extrusion-based fabrication [3]. In this regard, functionally graded materials (FGMs) have attracted significant attention as a manufacturing paradigm that designs and implements material properties as explicit functions of position [4].

In tissue engineering, graded materials are most commonly implemented using hydrogels, where graded hydrogel systems are widely used to provide spatially heterogeneous physicochemical and mechanical microenvironments [5]. Such graded hydrogel implementations are often discussed under the broader concept of functionally graded hydrogels (FGHs) [6]. However, the central focus of this study is not only the graded hydrogel as an end product, but also the extrusion process that generates it. We define functionally graded bioink (FGB) as a bioink generated by continuously varying the mixing ratio of two precursor inks during extrusion, with the resulting printed structures termed FGB constructs. This work establishes a framework for programming bioink composition during extrusion to create spatially defined gradients within printed constructs.

3D bioprinting has emerged as a promising approach for fabricating graded biological constructs [7,8]. In particular, extrusion-based bioprinting is the most widely used approach because it can process inks across a wide viscosity range and accommodate high cell densities [9,10]. In extrusion bioprinting, the spatial organization of multiple materials is largely determined by printhead design. For example, coaxial extrusion enables independent delivery of multiple bioinks and is widely used to fabricate core–shell, tubular, vascular, and other compartmentalized structures [[11], [12], [13]]. However, because the constituent bioinks remain physically separated throughout deposition, coaxial systems are not inherently suited for generating functionally graded materials with continuously varying composition [11,14,15]. Multi-nozzle extrusion provides a straightforward means of spatially organizing multiple materials through selective deposition from independent printheads. However, continuous gradients are typically approximated by sequential deposition of discrete material compositions, resulting in stepwise transitions rather than smooth compositional variation [16]. Alternative approaches based on post-fabrication processing can be used to generate property gradients, particularly in mechanical stiffness. However, extending these strategies to biologically relevant gradients, such as cell-density distributions or biochemical signaling cues, remains challenging [17,18].

These limitations have motivated the development of mixing-based extrusion strategies, in which gradients are generated by continuously varying the composition of bioinks during deposition rather than by discrete material placement or post-fabrication modification. Previous studies have demonstrated several key capabilities of this approach. Hofmann et al. developed customizable passive static-mixer printheads that enabled continuous compositional blending, stiffness-gradient fabrication, and extrusion of cell-laden bioinks with high post-printing viability [19]. Moser et al. further demonstrated that reliable gradient generation depends not only on mixing strategy but also on precise and responsive control of material delivery [20]. In addition, Kuzucu et al. used an extrusion-based dual-feed mixing platform to fabricate gradients in stiffness, cell density, and immobilized cell-adhesion peptide [21]. Previous work has therefore established the feasibility of continuous mixing, reliable gradient generation, high cell viability, and multimodal gradient fabrication in extrusion bioprinting.

Despite these advances, several process-level challenges remain. Many gradient-printing systems regulate composition by dynamically adjusting the flow rates of multiple material streams, requiring precise coordination between material delivery and print-path progression [20]. Previous studies have demonstrated axial gradients and predefined composition profiles; however, systematic control over gradient onset, transition sequence, direction, reversal, and two-dimensional spatial organization remains comparatively limited [[19], [20], [21]]. Furthermore, although gradients in stiffness, cell density, and immobilized biochemical cues have been successfully fabricated, construct-level validation of programmed gradients, including interfacial deformation and location-dependent cellular responses, remains comparatively limited [21].

To address these challenges, we developed a dual ball-valve flow-control module for extrusion bioprinting that generates gradients through time-programmed regulation of the mixing ratio between two precursor inks. By complementarily actuating two servo-controlled ball valves, the system modulates bioink composition without directly altering the extrusion conditions of the upstream reservoirs. Synchronization of valve actuation with the printing path enabled control over gradient onset, direction, reversal, and spatial organization, allowing the fabrication of linear, bidirectional, square, and radial gradient architectures. The same control strategy was applied to gradients in mechanical properties, cell density, and biochemical cues, and was evaluated through interfacial deformation, cell-density patterning, graded cellular-interface formation, and location-dependent epithelial–mesenchymal transition in A549 cells. Consequently, this study establishes programmable bioink mixing as a strategy for linking time-dependent composition control with spatial gradient design and construct-level biological function.

Materials and methods

Materials and bioink preparation

Gelatin–methacryloyl (GelMA) was synthesized in-house using Type A porcine skin gelatin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, G2625-500G). Gelatin (10% (w/v)) was fully dissolved in Dulbecco's PBS (Corning, USA, 21-031-CV) at 60 °C, after which methacrylic anhydride (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, 276685) was added at 0.6 g per gram of gelatin and reacted at 50 °C for 1 h. The reaction was quenched by adding 200 mL of DPBS, and unreacted MA was removed by centrifugation followed by decantation. The supernatant was dialyzed against deionized water for 7 days with daily water changes using dialysis tubing (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, D9652; MWCO, 12-14 kDa), then frozen at −80 °C and lyophilized for 1 week to obtain GelMA. For bioink formulation, lyophilized GelMA was dissolved in 1× PBS at 37 °C for approximately 12 h to prepare a 5% (w/v) GelMA precursor solution. Lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) was added at 0.1% (w/v; 0.01 g per 10 mL) (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck, Germany, 900889). For cell-laden printing, the precursor solution was sterile-filtered through a 0.22 μm PES syringe filter (JetBiofil, F204.030N) prior to cell incorporation. Unless otherwise specified, two precursor inks were used as end members for gradient generation. A baseline ink consisted of 5% (w/v) GelMA. A reinforced ink for stiffness modulation was prepared by mixing 5% (w/v) GelMA with PEGDA photoink at a 4:1 (v/v) ratio (GelMA:PEGDA = 4:1). All printed constructs were photopolymerized for 30 s using a 405 nm LED light source (Skycares, Prime 100 365C).

Cell culture and labeling

C2C12 (mouse myoblast) and A549 (human alveolar epithelial) cells were used. Both cell types were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM, Corning, USA, 10-013-CV) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Corning, USA, 35-015-CV) and 1% (v/v) penicillin (Corning, USA, 30-002-CI). Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator (Thermo Fisher, USA, 51023121), and the medium was refreshed every 2 days. For fluorescent labeling, CellTracker dyes (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher, USA) were diluted in culture medium at 1 μL/mL and incubated with cells for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by PBS washing prior to use in printing experiments.

Design and fabrication of the dual ball-valve extrusion module

The dual ball-valve mixing module consisted of two independent flow-control units (Valve A and Valve B), followed downstream by a Y-shaped mixing nozzle incorporating an internal static mixer. Each flow control unit consists of a split housing and internal ball-stem components, designed such that rotation of the stem causes the ball to rotate, thereby continuously altering the effective flow cross-sectional area. The Y-shaped nozzle features two inlet branches positioned at approximately 45°, with an inlet diameter of Φ4.00 mm, a retention section of approximately 2 mm, a downstream straight mixing channel length of 30.00 mm, and a channel inner diameter of Φ2.80 mm. A Kenics-type static mixer (KSM) was inserted inside the straight channel to improve mixing uniformity after convergence. KSM was constructed by connecting 10 helical blade elements with a cross-section of approximately 2.5 mm × 0.5 mm in series. The module components were printed using an SLA 3D printer (Formlabs, USA, Form 3+) with Standard Clear Resin (Formlabs, USA) at a layer thickness of 25 μm. Printed parts were washed in isopropyl alcohol, post-cured using a Form Cure system (Formlabs, USA, Form Cure), and assembled using a screw-fastening method. For cell experiments, the module was rinsed with 70% ethanol and UV-sterilized in a biosafety cabinet for at least 6 h.

System integration and servo-motor control

The module was mounted beneath the ink reservoir of the extrusion-based 3D bioprinter (EDmic Bio, Korea) and aligned with the printer extrusion axis. Each valve was independently actuated by a servo motor (MG996R) controlled using an Arduino UNO with the Servo.h library. The servos were powered by an external 4 × AA battery pack, and control signals were delivered via digital pins 8 (Valve A) and 9 (Valve B). The commanded servo input (servo.write) was programmed using a nominal 1:1 mapping to the valve rotation angle (θ). The valve angle was defined as fully open at θ = 0°, and valves were operated within θ = 0–60°.

FGB construct printing and gradient programming

FGB constructs were fabricated using a pneumatic extrusion-based 3D bioprinter (EDmic Bio, Korea). Bioinks were loaded into 10 mL syringes (EST, Korea, PS10) and connected to each inlet of the module. Before printing, the downstream straight mixing channel of the Y-shaped nozzle was pre-filled to minimize composition lag arising from the initial dead volume. Printing was performed at 80–150 kPa with a printing speed of 20–50 mm/min, using a 14G nozzle (EST, Korea, PP14G-1/2). During FGB printing, the two valves were actuated in a complementary manner to program the relative contributions of the two precursor inks while the upstream pressure settings were maintained.

Validation of valve-programmed mixing and spatial gradients

For validation of valve-programmed mixing, a 5% (w/v) GelMA ink supplemented with FITC–dextran (average molecular weight 70 kDa, Sigma–Aldrich, Merck, Germany, 46945) at 0.05 mg/mL was used. For each condition, three independently prepared samples were measured, and each sample was tested in three technical replicates. Validation of programmable gradient positioning and direction

To validate programmable control of gradient onset and direction, a dyed ink was prepared by adding Trypan blue (Gibco, USA, 15250-061; 0.4% stock) at 400 μL per 10 mL of 5% (w/v) GelMA. For 2D onset control, Early/Mid/Late conditions were implemented along an identical printing path by delaying the valve-opening start time of the dyed-ink channel in 20 s increments. For 3D direction control, constructs were fabricated by reversing only the inlet order of the dyed and undyed inks. All images were acquired under fixed settings using a USB-type digital microscope (Dino-Lite, Korea, AM4113-FVT) and quantified in ImageJ/Fiji. Images were converted to 8-bit grayscale (0 = black, 255 = white), and mean gray values were calculated from line- or region-based ROIs. For 2D prints, each entire printed line was selected as an ROI and organized as Segment 1–6. For 3D prints, cross-sectional images were divided into nine ROIs. Extracted values were visualized as heatmaps in Python (Google Colab) consistent with the figure annotations. For toolpath-synchronized two-dimensional gradient layouts, linear patterns were fabricated using continuous raster paths, the square pattern using a continuous center-to-edge concentric-square path, and the radial pattern using a continuous edge-to-center spiral path. The temporal valve-actuation sequence was synchronized with normalized path progression. Monotonic A→B patterns were generated by progressively increasing the contribution of Ink B, whereas the A→B→A pattern was generated by reversing the valve sequence at the midpoint of the raster path. For the square pattern, application of the A→B valve sequence along the outward concentric-square path produced an Ink A-rich center and an Ink B-rich outer region. For the radial pattern, application of the same A→B valve sequence along the inward spiral path produced an Ink A-rich outer region and an Ink B-rich center. Representative constructs were imaged under fixed microscope settings; one representative construct was prepared and presented for each pattern.

Cell-laden FGB printing and biological characterization

For cell-laden printing, C2C12 cells were suspended in 5% (w/v) GelMA at 5 × 106 cells/mL and printed as FGB constructs. At 2 h post-printing, viability was assessed using a Live/Dead kit (Invitrogen, USA, L3224) and imaged with a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan, Eclipse TS2). Live percentage was calculated as Live/(Live + Dead) × 100. Unless otherwise stated, n indicates the number of quantified images/measurements as specified in the figure captions.

Two-dimensional cell numbers were quantified from bright-field images using ImageJ/Fiji (Analyze Particles). The printed construct image was divided into six regions (Parts 1–6) along the printing direction, and particles were counted under fixed settings. Analyze Particles parameters were: size = 6–80 pixel2, circularity = 0.20–1.00.

Three-dimensional cell distributions were evaluated by acquiring confocal z-stacks of constructs containing cells labeled with CellTracker Green CMFDA (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, C2925) using a confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan, AX). Mean gray values were extracted from 8-bit images (0–255) for each z-slice. For visualization, intensity was normalized by min–max scaling: Normalized intensity (%) = (I − I_min)/(I_max − I_min) × 100. Z-slices were further grouped into six depth bins (Layer 1–6), and bin-wise values were summarized as mean ± SD.

Graded cellular interfaces were generated by printing two differently labeled C2C12 populations labeled with CellTracker Green CMFDA (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, C2925) and CellTracker CM-DiI (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, C7001), followed by confocal imaging and channel-ratio quantification via RGB channel splitting in ImageJ/Fiji. Three ROIs (Uniform Yellow, Gradient Interface, and Uniform Green) were analyzed, and mean intensities were measured for each channel. The green fraction was calculated as: Green fraction (%) = I_Green/(I_Green + I_Yellow) × 100, and the yellow fraction was defined as 100 − Green fraction.

Mechanical characterization of compositional gradients

Rectangular specimens (35 × 4 × 2.5 mm) were prepared using 5% (w/v) GelMA ink and 5% (w/v) GelMA:PEGDA (4:1, v/v) ink. Step-transition specimens were fabricated with a discrete interface between the two inks, whereas gradient-transition specimens were produced via ball-valve-enabled continuous mixing to generate a gradual transition zone. All specimens were photopolymerized for 30 s under a 405 nm LED. Tensile evaluation was performed by fixing both ends of the specimen with tweezers and applying manual uniaxial deformation in air, rather than using a mechanical testing instrument. Representative frames were extracted to compare deformation localization and final fracture location.

Rheological characterization

Viscoelastic properties were measured using a rheometer (Anton Paar, Austria, MCR 72) with a 25 mm parallel-plate geometry. The 5% (w/v) GelMA ink and 5% (w/v) GelMA:PEGDA (4:1, v/v) ink were mixed at 10:0, 8:2, 6:4, 4:6, 2:8, and 0:10 (v/v; GelMA: GelMA:PEGDA (4:1)) ratios, cast in 35 mm Petri dishes, and prepared as 1 mm-thick specimens. After photopolymerization for 30 s under a 405 nm LED, frequency sweeps (1–100 rad s−1, strain amplitude = 1%) were performed at 25 °C with a 1 mm gap to obtain the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″).

TGF-β1 gradient substrate fabrication and EMT response mapping

A TGF-β1 gradient substrate was printed using two inks, 5% (w/v) GelMA and 5% (w/v) GelMA containing human recombinant TGF-β1 (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, 100-21C-10UG) at 10 ng/mL. After printing, constructs were photopolymerized for 30 s under a 405 nm LED and surface-coated with 0.1% gelatin for 30 min. A549 cells were seeded onto the substrate at 5 × 105 cells in 100 μL medium and evenly distributed. For immunofluorescence, samples were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (T&I BioPrince, Republic of Korea, BPP-9004) for 15 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Biosesang, Republic of Korea, TR1020-500-00) for 5 min, and blocked with 2% BSA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, 37525) for 1 h. Primary antibody incubation (6 h), PBS washing (12 h), secondary antibody incubation (6 h), and final washing (12 h) were performed at 4 °C. Antibodies and dilutions are provided in Table 1. Following immunostaining, confocal fluorescence images were acquired from five predefined regions (Parts 1–5) along the printing direction corresponding to the programmed TGF-β1 gradient. Samples were analyzed at 24, 48, and 72 h after seeding. For each time point, three independent gradient substrates were prepared (n = 3). Imaging and quantitative analysis procedures are described in Section 2.12.

Microscopy and image quantification

Onset and direction control images were acquired using a Dino-Lite digital microscope. Live/Dead images were obtained using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan, Eclipse TS2). CellTracker and EMT immunofluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan, AX). For EMT marker imaging, three-channel confocal z-stacks (DAPI, Vimentin, and ZO-1) were acquired from each region under identical acquisition settings across all regions and time points (objective, pixel size, z-step, laser power, detector gain/offset, and pinhole). Quantitative analysis was performed in ImageJ/Fiji. Confocal z-stacks were converted to 2D images by average-intensity projection prior to ROI generation and intensity extraction. Nuclei were segmented from the DAPI projection by thresholding and, when required, watershed separation to split touching nuclei. Nuclear ROIs were registered in the ROI Manager using particle-based detection (Analyze Particles). The same nuclear ROIs were applied to the Vimentin and ZO-1 images to extract per-nucleus mean fluorescence intensities under fixed analysis settings. To minimize bias from variable numbers of detected nuclei across images and regions, an equal number of nuclei (150 ROIs) was randomly subsampled per image when more than 150 nuclei were detected; when fewer than 150 nuclei were detected, all nuclei were used. For each substrate, three fields of view were acquired per region and averaged to obtain a substrate-level mean value for each region. The EMT index was calculated as the ratio of the region-mean Vimentin intensity to the region-mean ZO-1 intensity (Vimentin/ZO-1) and summarized for each time point and region. Heatmaps were generated in Python (Google Colab).

Results

Gradient design and experimental validation workflow for programmable FGB fabrication

Gradient design can be defined by the combination of a gradient profile, gradient target, and the spatial dimensions or layout of the gradient [22,23]. Fig. 1A shows that property changes with position can be implemented as discrete, stepwise, or continuous. In actual manufacturing processes, gradients are often approximated in a stepwise manner due to constraints such as the minimum unit of material switching or the mixing response time [24]. Because stepwise transitions can introduce abrupt interfaces and limit controllable transition width and slope, continuous gradients are an important target for evaluating process-level gradient fidelity [3,25]. Additionally, Fig. 1B summarizes that gradient implementation is not limited to simple compositional ratio changes but encompasses a broad range of design targets such as composition, structure, orientation or anisotropy, and density. In this study, these general design concepts were translated into four experimentally implemented modalities: composition, stiffness, cell density, and biochemical cues (Fig. 1C).

Spatially varying microenvironmental cues play important roles in regulating cellular behavior and tissue organization. Among these, biochemical-factor gradients can modulate signaling activity, gene expression, and directional migration such as chemotaxis [[26], [27], [28], [29]], whereas stiffness gradients can influence mechanobiological responses, including cell adhesion, cytoskeletal organization, migration, and differentiation [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. Cell-density gradients can further regulate tissue zonation, cellular organization, and cell–cell interactions by spatially varying local cell numbers and population ratios [21,[34], [35], [36]]. Together, these gradients provide spatially encoded microenvironmental information that governs cellular responses and tissue organization within native and engineered tissues.

Based on this gradient-design framework, the programmable outputs shown in Fig. 1C were connected to the experimental validation workflow summarized in Fig. 1D. Compositional gradients were first evaluated through FITC-dextran-based fluorescence analysis, and spatial programmability was assessed through control of gradient onset, direction, reversal, and geometric layout. Cell-density gradients were assessed through cell viability, spatial cell distribution, and graded cellular-interface formation. Mechanical gradients were evaluated through mixing-ratio-dependent rheological modulation and interfacial deformation behavior, whereas biochemical gradients were examined through location-dependent A549 epithelial–mesenchymal transition responses.

Ball-valve-controlled extrusion system for continuous FGB fabrication

Fig. 2 provides an overview of the ball-valve-based dual-flow control module, its key components, and the overall printing setup used to generate FGB and deliver it along the printing path. To enable continuous compositional programming during extrusion, we developed a plug-in dual ball-valve mixing module fabricated by SLA 3D printing and mounted it onto a conventional extrusion bioprinter (Fig. 2A). The module comprises two independently actuated ball-valve flow-control units (Valve A and Valve B) followed by a Y-junction and an in-line static mixer, allowing two precursor inks to be continuously combined before extrusion (Fig. 2B). After merging at the Y-junction, the two streams pass through the static mixer, which promotes cross-sectional homogenization [37,38]. As a result, the time-varying mixing ratio is translated into a spatial composition profile along the printing path, producing a continuous gradient within the printed construct (Fig. 2C) [39]. This architecture converts a time-domain input into a spatial composition profile along the toolpath, establishing FGB as an in-process material state rather than a stepwise multi-material outcome. The transparent SLA-printed housing enables direct visual inspection of the flow path and the valve rotation mechanism, supporting intuitive validation of the operating principle during setup and testing.

Continuous FGB generation is achieved by programming the valve rotation angles as a function of time. As the valve rotates, the overlap between the through-hole of the ball and the flow channel changes smoothly. This produces continuous modulation of the effective open area and results in a gradual flow response rather than an on–off switching behavior (Fig. 2D). The two valves were driven in a complementary manner so that a decrease in the opening of one valve was balanced by an increase in the opening of the other. This complementary actuation provides a programmed shift in the relative contributions of the two precursor inks while the upstream extrusion settings remain unchanged during printing (Fig. 2E). As a result, the temporal valve sequence can be synchronized with nozzle travel to define gradient onset position, transition sequence, direction, and spatial layout as programmable process variables. Accordingly, the same valve-control principle was applied to composition, stiffness, cell density, and biochemical-cue gradients in the following sections.

To support the operating principle that valve rotation provides continuous (rather than on–off) flow regulation, we quantified the geometric relationship between the servo rotation angle and the effective aperture area by modeling the overlap between the inlet cross-section and the rotating ball-port. As the ball rotates, the overlap between the through-hole and the flow channel decreases smoothly, yielding a monotonic reduction in the effective opening area. Discrete valve states extracted from the programmed actuation sequence (Supplementary Movie S2) were reconstructed as representative snapshots and plotted together with the full angle–area curve (Supplementary Fig. S1). Real-time aperture evolution during single-valve actuation is shown in Supplementary Movie S1, and dual-valve complementary actuation sequences under Arduino control are demonstrated in Supplementary Movie S2. Together, these results define an interpretable transfer relationship in which the valve rotation angle determines the effective aperture area, providing a quantitative geometric basis for using valve angle as a continuous control variable for programmable mixing in FGB printing.

Quantitative validation of valve-programmed mixing and spatial gradient formation

To quantitatively validate valve-angle–controlled mixing, FITC-dextran (70 kDa, 0.05 mg/mL) was used as a fluorescent tracer and the mixing response was evaluated using five complementary valve-angle combinations (A–E) (Fig. 3A). For each condition, extrudates were collected and sampled using an identical cylindrical stamp to ensure consistent volume and geometry, then transferred to a 96-well plate for fluorescence measurement (Fig. 3B).

Fluorescence values were converted into a comparable mixing metric by plate-matched normalization using GelMA-only (0%) and GelMA + FITC–dextran (100%) controls measured on the same plate (normalization equation in Supplementary Fig. S2). This procedure removes plate-to-plate variability and background offsets, allowing valve-angle-programmed mixing to be interpreted as a normalized tracer fraction rather than raw RFU. Accordingly, valve-angle combinations were compared on a common 0–100% compositional scale. The normalized intensity decreased stepwise across valve-angle combinations and exhibited a strong linear relationship (R2 = 0.969), confirming that mixing is quantitatively tunable by valve-angle programming alone (Fig. 3C). Specifically, Fig. 3C validates fixed-angle mixing control by maintaining each complementary valve-angle pair at a constant setting during extrusion, thereby confirming that distinct outlet compositions can be generated across the programmed valve conditions.

Next, we tested whether the programmed mixing state at the nozzle outlet was preserved as a spatial compositional gradient after printing. A gradient construct was printed into a PDMS mold to maintain a constant cross-sectional geometry, and the specimen was sectioned into six equal segments along the printing direction (Part 1 to Part 6) (Fig. 3D). Segment-wise fluorescence quantification showed a monotonic decrease from Part 1 to Part 6 with a strong linear trend (R2 = 0.9537) (Fig. 3E). Together, these results demonstrate that the ball valve-based extrusion system provides quantifiable angle-dependent mixing control and translates the programmed mixing sequence into a spatial composition gradient within the printed construct. In contrast to the fixed-angle validation in Fig. 3C and 3D–E demonstrate dynamic in-process control, in which valve angles were continuously adjusted during a single print to translate programmed inputs into a spatial gradient.

Validation of programmable gradient positioning and direction

To visually validate that the ball valve–controlled dual-flow system can program both gradient onset position and direction during printing, a Trypan blue–dyed GelMA ink was used as a high-contrast tracer (Fig. 4). Representative real-time printing is provided in Supplementary Movie S3. For two-dimensional constructs, the toolpath was kept identical while the actuation start time for the dyed ink was delayed to generate Early, Mid, and Late onset conditions (Fig. 4A), producing a clear shift in the first appearance of the color transition. Quantification was performed by dividing each print into six segments (Segment 1–6), converting images to grayscale, and computing segment-wise mean intensity to generate a heatmap (Fig. 4B). Because the analysis uses fixed segmentation and relative grayscale intensity along the print direction, it provides a robust readout of transition location that is less sensitive to global illumination or absolute intensity differences. This analysis confirms that the dominant transition boundary shifts systematically with programmed valve timing (Early: 2 → 3; Mid: 3 → 4; Late: 5 → 6). The largest intensity change occurred at segment boundaries 2 → 3 (Early, Δ = 78.9), 3 → 4 (Mid, Δ = 86.3), and 5 → 6 (Late, Δ = 46.3). To summarize the most prominent transition location per condition, we defined the dominant transition boundary as the adjacent segment pair exhibiting the maximum absolute intensity change (max |Δ|), and reported the resulting Condition–Boundary–max |Δ| table and bar plot in Supplementary Fig. S3, highlighting a systematic shift with programmed valve timing.

To extend this concept to three-dimensional constructs, stacked printing was performed and the inlet order of dyed and undyed inks was switched to reverse gradient direction while maintaining the same geometry (Fig. 4C); representative fabrication is shown in Supplementary Movie S4. Cross-sectional images were divided into nine regions (Segment 1–9) and analyzed by mean grayscale intensity (Fig. 4D). The Clear→Dyed condition increased from low intensity in early segments (Segments 1–3, 38.8–41.1) to higher intensity in later segments (Segments 7–9, 85.3–102.0), whereas the Dyed→Clear condition exhibited the opposite trend (Segments 1–3, 96.4–82.1; Segments 7–9, 38.2–37.4). Together, these results demonstrate that valve timing and inlet assignment enable programmable gradient placement in both onset position and direction across 2D and 3D prints. Because this experiment is intended to visualize programmable gradient placement rather than quantify mixing fidelity, representative constructs are shown under fixed imaging settings (n = 1 per condition), while quantitative reproducibility of valve-programmed mixing is independently established in Fig. 3.

Toolpath-synchronized programming of two-dimensional gradient layouts

The preceding experiments demonstrated that valve-actuation timing and inlet assignment can be used to control gradient onset position and axial direction within predefined two- and three-dimensional geometries. To further examine whether valve programming could be extended beyond a single unidirectional transition, we synchronized the temporal valve-actuation sequence with the spatial progression of different printing paths and generated four representative two-dimensional composition layouts (Fig. 5).

(B) Square pattern showing an Ink A-rich center and an Ink B-rich outer region generated along a continuous center-to-edge concentric-square path. (C) Radial pattern showing an Ink A-rich outer region and an Ink B-rich center generated along a continuous edge-to-center spiral path. Within each panel, the target composition pattern, corresponding printing path, and representative printed construct are presented sequentially. Yellow–orange and red indicate Ink A-rich and Ink B-rich regions, respectively. Black circles indicate the starting positions, and arrows indicate the direction of nozzle travel. The programmed layouts were organized into linear, square, and radial pattern groups, with each group presenting the target composition field, corresponding printing path, and representative printed construct. The linear layouts included a monotonic transition from Ink A to Ink B and a non-monotonic transition from Ink A to Ink B and back to Ink A. For the linear A→B pattern, the relative contribution of Ink B was progressively increased along the raster printing path. For the A→B→A pattern, the valve-actuation sequence was reversed after reaching the central region, producing an Ink B-rich center surrounded by Ink A-rich regions (Fig. 5A).

For the square pattern, printing began at the center and progressed outward along a continuous concentric-square path. During this center-to-edge progression, the contribution of Ink A was gradually decreased while that of Ink B was increased, producing an Ink A-rich center and an Ink B-rich outer region (Fig. 5B). In contrast, the radial pattern was printed from the outer edge toward the center along a continuous inward spiral path. Application of the same A→B valve-actuation sequence along this edge-to-center path generated an Ink A-rich outer region and an Ink B-rich center (Fig. 5C). Thus, the final spatial orientation of the composition gradient was determined by the combination of the programmed valve sequence and the direction of toolpath progression.

The representative printed constructs exhibited visually distinguishable linear, bidirectional, square, and radial composition distributions corresponding to the programmed transition classes. These findings demonstrate that valve-programmed gradient formation is not restricted to a single axial profile but can be coupled with different toolpath geometries and travel directions to generate composition fields with distinct spatial layouts and transition profiles.

Mechanical gradient implementation and interfacial deformation behavior

To demonstrate the feasibility of stiffness-graded FGB constructs, we used two precursor inks, 5% (w/v) GelMA and a reinforced formulation prepared as 5% (w/v) GelMA mixed with PEGDA photoink at a 4:1 (v/v) ratio (GelMA:PEGDA = 4:1) (Fig. 6). We first examined the rheological properties of the two end-member formulations, 10:0 and 0:10 (GelMA ink:GelMA/PEGDA ink). Frequency-sweep measurements showed a clear difference in storage modulus (G′) between the two end members over the angular-frequency range of 1–100 rad s−1 (Fig. 6A). We then prepared intermediate mixtures at programmed ratios of 8:2, 6:4, 4:6, and 2:8 to determine whether programmed compositional variation could generate intermediate mechanical states. As shown in Fig. 6B, the G′ values of these intermediate formulations were distributed between the two end-member reference curves and generally increased with the fraction of the PEGDA-containing ink. These results confirm that programmed compositional variation generates a graded series of intermediate rheological states between the two precursor formulations, thereby establishing the material basis for generating a stiffness gradient through time-programmed mixing. Using the same ink pair, we then compared uniaxial deformation behavior between constructs with a discrete step transition and those with a continuous gradient transition (Fig. 6C–F). To isolate the effect of transition continuity, the overall geometry and end-member formulations were kept constant while only the transition scheme (step vs gradient) was varied. The step-transition construct contained an abrupt interface between the PEGDA-reinforced and GelMA regions (Fig. 6C), whereas the gradient-transition construct contained a broader transition zone generated by continuously varying the mixing ratio along the printing direction (Fig. 6D). In the step-transition specimens, deformation progressively concentrated near the sharp interface, and fracture occurred at or near the boundary region (Fig. 6E and Supplementary Movie S5). In contrast, gradient-transition specimens showed deformation distributed over a broader region, with the construct remaining integrated throughout the imaged deformation sequence and without abrupt separation at the nominal interface (Fig. 6F and Supplementary Movie S6). Together, these observations suggest that a continuous stiffness transition can reduce localized deformation associated with an abrupt property mismatch and may enhance the mechanical integrity of graded interfaces [[40], [41], [42]].

Cell compatibility and programmable cell-density distributions

To assess the applicability of the ball valve-based dual flow control system to cell-laden bioprinting, C2C12 myoblasts were suspended in GelMA and printed to evaluate cell viability, the spatial distribution of a programmed cell-density gradient, and the feasibility of forming a continuous graded cellular interface (Fig. 7). First, to determine whether the added valves and mixing path impose additional cell damage, Live and Dead staining was compared between prints produced using a conventional nozzle and the ball valve nozzle (Fig. 7A). Both conditions exhibited predominantly live signals, and the live cell percentages were 88.15 ± 4.19% and 86.74 ± 3.48% for the conventional and ball valve nozzles, respectively, with no significant difference between groups. These results indicate that the module can be operated without a measurable loss of cell viability under the tested conditions.

(B) Representative image-based cell counting along a single 2D gradient print (Parts 1–6). (C) Confocal z-stack analysis of a 3D construct with a programmed cell-density gradient, showing a depth-dependent fluorescence intensity profile. (D) Two-color interface printing using C2C12 cells labeled with CellTracker dyes, generating uniform, mixed-gradient (interface), and uniform regions; channel contribution was quantified by the intensity-based fraction metric described in Methods. (For interpretation of the references to color in this figure legend, the reader is referred to the Web version of this article.)

Next, we examined whether valve ratio control is translated into a two-dimensional cell density gradient within a single print. The printed construct was segmented along the printing direction into six regions (Parts 1–6), and cell numbers were quantified in each region (Fig. 7B). Cell counts increased monotonically from Parts 1–6 with an approximately 72-fold change across the construct, confirming that continuous modulation of the inflow ratio of the cell suspension ink yields a reproducible spatial density gradient. We further examined whether the same concept could be extended to three-dimensional constructs by printing a stacked construct and analyzing the depth-dependent signal using confocal z-stack imaging (Fig. 7C). The mean intensity profile along the z-axis showed a consistent monotonic trend, supporting that the programmed density gradient can be extended from planar prints to volumetric constructs. For compact visualization of the full z-stack trend, z-slice intensities were min–max normalized and grouped into six depth bins (Layer 1–6) (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Finally, to evaluate whether the platform can support interface-like tissue patterning, two C2C12 populations labeled with different CellTracker dyes were printed under uniform and gradient conditions (Fig. 7D). Uniform Green and Uniform Yellow prints showed dominance of a single population across the construct (Green 77.1%, Yellow 92.7%). In contrast, the Mixed Gradient condition exhibited colocalized signals from both channels, indicating a gradual compositional transition rather than an abrupt boundary. These results demonstrate that ball-valve-controlled mixing enables cell-compatible printing, programmable cell-density gradients in both two- and three-dimensional constructs, and continuous cellular transition zones applicable to tissue-interface engineering.

TGF-β1 gradient substrate enables spatially resolved EMT responses

To examine whether ball-valve-programmed FGB printing can spatially pattern chemical cues within a single construct and translate this pattern into location-specific cellular phenotypes, we printed a TGF-β1 gradient substrate and quantified EMT responses of A549 cells across space and time (Fig. 8). A continuous gradient was generated by co-printing two precursor inks, 5% GelMA without TGF-β1 and 5% GelMA containing TGF-β1 at 10 ng/mL, and the resulting construct was divided into five regions (Parts 1–5) along the printing direction, corresponding to nominal TGF-β1 levels of 0, 2.5, 5, 7.5, and 10 ng/mL (Fig. 8A and B). After A549 seeding, ZO-1 and Vimentin immunofluorescence was quantified at 24, 48, and 72 h to map EMT progression along the printed concentration coordinate (Fig. 8C). Vimentin increased toward the high-concentration end of the gradient at all time points (Fig. 8C(i–iii), corresponding to 24, 48, and 72 h, respectively), and the difference between the 0 and 10 ng/mL regions was significant at 24, 48, and 72 h. In contrast, ZO-1 exhibited comparatively weak and non-monotonic variation across the concentration axis under the current intensity-based quantification scheme at 24, 48, and 72 h (Fig. 8C(iv–vi), respectively), indicating that Vimentin provided a more sensitive spatial readout than ZO-1 under the present experimental and image-analysis conditions. Among the evaluated time points, the most pronounced region-dependent EMT response was observed at 72 h, particularly toward the high-TGF-β1 end of the printed gradient. Representative single-channel images acquired from Parts 1–5 of a gradient substrate at 72 h are provided in Supplementary Fig. S5 to visualize the spatial distributions of Vimentin and ZO-1 along the programmed concentration coordinate. The representative images were provided for spatial visualization, whereas region-wise comparisons were based on the quantitative per-nucleus fluorescence analysis shown in Fig. 8C.

To integrate these EMT-associated trends into a single metric, an EMT index was defined as the Vimentin-to-ZO-1 ratio. For intuitive visualization of region-specific EMT tendency on a single substrate, the EMT index values are displayed as a heatmap in Fig. 8C(vii). The complete EMT index dataset and statistical evaluation across concentrations and time are provided in Supplementary Fig. S6. The EMT index increased toward higher TGF-β1 regions, reaching a maximum value of 2.557 at 72 h in the 10 ng/mL region (Fig. 8C(vii) and Supplementary Fig. S6). The resulting concentration–response map demonstrates that the printed concentration coordinate can serve as a common spatial reference for evaluating region- and time-dependent EMT-associated responses within a single culture platform. By encoding dose as position within one construct, this approach may reduce variability associated with separately prepared treatment groups and enable interrogation of intermediate transition regions that are difficult to capture with discrete, batch-wise dosing [[43], [44], [45]]. Accordingly, ball-valve-controlled FGB printing provides a practical platform for generating programmed biochemical gradients and linking them to spatially and temporally resolved phenotypic responses in vitro.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that the plug-in dual ball-valve module enables continuous, single-nozzle gradient extrusion with programmable spatial placement without requiring major reconfiguration of an existing extrusion platform (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7, Fig. 8). Importantly, continuous compositional mixing itself is not unique to the present study, as previous gradient-bioprinting systems have already established continuous blending, stable material delivery, and multimodal gradient fabrication [[19], [20], [21]]. Rather, the principal process-level contribution of this work is the use of complementary downstream ball-valve aperture modulation as a toolpath-synchronized, in-process control variable while the upstream extrusion settings remain unchanged. FITC–dextran tracer experiments established a quantitative relationship between valve-angle input and normalized outlet composition and demonstrated its translation into a spatial composition profile within the printed construct (Fig. 3). This relationship supports the use of valve angle as a predictable process parameter, rather than solely as an empirically adjusted operating condition. Compared with stepwise multi-material switching or coaxial delivery, which respectively produce discrete transitions or maintain physically separated material streams, the present approach generates a continuously mixed composition within a single deposition pathway [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. Valve-actuation timing and inlet assignment further enabled relocation of the gradient onset along an identical toolpath and reversal of the gradient direction in three-dimensional stacked constructs (Fig. 4). Such placement control is relevant to gradient-based microenvironment design, in which biochemical, cellular, or mechanical cues may need to begin at specific positions or extend in predefined directions within a common geometry [28,29,46,47].

This temporal control was further translated into geometry-dependent spatial programming through synchronization with the printing path. Raster, concentric-square, and spiral paths generated monotonic and non-monotonic linear gradients, an Ink A-rich center surrounded by an Ink B-rich outer region, and an Ink A-rich outer region surrounding an Ink B-rich center, respectively (Fig. 5). Notably, the square and radial patterns were produced using the same A→B valve-actuation sequence but opposite directions of toolpath progression. This result indicates that the final composition field is determined jointly by the temporal valve program, toolpath geometry, and direction of nozzle travel, rather than by the valve sequence alone. Accordingly, downstream aperture control can be coupled with toolpath design to program not only the presence of a gradient but also its transition order and spatial orientation. This capability extends continuous gradient extrusion beyond predefined one-dimensional axial profiles and provides a basis for encoding composition into geometry-specific printing paths.

Application of the same mixing strategy to GelMA and GelMA–PEGDA formulations generated intermediate storage-modulus responses between the two end-member formulations (Fig. 6A and B), supporting composition-dependent modulation of the elastic behavior of the mixed materials. The step-transition construct exhibited localized deformation and failure near the abrupt material interface, whereas deformation in the gradient-transition construct was distributed across a broader region without abrupt separation at the nominal interface throughout the recorded deformation sequence (Fig. 6E and F). This observation is consistent with graded-interface concepts in which gradual property transitions can reduce strain localization and stress concentration associated with abrupt material mismatch [40,41,48]. However, these findings should be interpreted as qualitative evidence of altered deformation behavior rather than as a quantitative demonstration of enhanced mechanical performance. Because the comparison was based on manual uniaxial deformation, instrumented force–displacement measurements, local strain mapping, and replicate-based mechanical testing will be required to quantify the mechanical effect of the graded transition.

The module also supported cell-laden extrusion while maintaining spatial control over cellular organization. Cell viability after passage through the complete flow pathway—including the ball valves, Y-junction, static mixer, and downstream nozzle—was comparable to that obtained using conventional nozzle extrusion under the tested conditions (Fig. 7A). This result indicates that incorporation of the additional valve-control and mixing pathway did not produce a measurable reduction in short-term cell viability under the selected printing conditions. Valve-ratio programming was further translated into cell-density distributions in both two- and three-dimensional constructs (Fig. 7B and C). In addition, two-color printing of differently labeled C2C12 populations produced a gradual cellular transition rather than an abrupt boundary (Fig. 7D). Because the experiment used two labeled populations of the same cell type, this result is more appropriately interpreted as evidence of graded two-population patterning than as the formation of a fully heterotypic interface. Nevertheless, the finite transition zone is consistent with interface-tissue-engineering concepts in which biological boundaries are represented by controllable spatial transitions rather than idealized discontinuities [[1], [2], [3],42,48]. Further studies using distinct cell types and bioinks with different rheological properties will be required to determine whether similar transition control can be maintained in more complex multicellular interfaces.

Biochemical-gradient printing further showed that programmed material composition could be translated into location-dependent cellular responses. The printed TGF-β1 gradient produced spatially resolved EMT-associated phenotypes in A549 cells (Fig. 8). Vimentin expression increased toward the high-TGF-β1 region, whereas ZO-1 exhibited a weaker and non-monotonic response across the gradient, consistent with the concentration-dependent but marker-specific nature of TGF-β1-induced EMT responses [49,50]. The different response patterns of Vimentin and ZO-1 suggest that the two markers exhibited different sensitivities to the concentration range and observation period evaluated in the present study. The printed concentration coordinate provided a common spatial reference for mapping region- and time-dependent cellular responses [51], and the integrated EMT index reached its highest value at 72 h in the nominal 10 ng/mL region. By encoding biochemical dose as spatial position within a single construct, the platform enables intermediate treatment regions to be evaluated alongside the two end-member conditions and may facilitate direct within-construct comparison of concentration-dependent cellular responses [[43], [44], [45]]. However, the regional TGF-β1 concentrations were assigned nominally from the programmed mixing ratios and were not directly measured within the photocrosslinked substrate. Direct quantification of local factor concentration, diffusion, and temporal retention will therefore be necessary to relate the programmed input more precisely to the biological response.

Across the mechanical, cellular, and biochemical experiments, the same downstream valve-control principle was used to convert temporal changes in precursor contribution into spatially organized material or biological outputs. Different gradient targets could therefore be implemented by changing the functional contents of the two precursor inks without redesigning the overall extrusion mechanism. The modular configuration may also facilitate adaptation to existing extrusion-based systems with limited hardware modification. Nevertheless, the control parameters established for one ink pair cannot be assumed to apply directly to another formulation because the resulting flow distribution depends on viscosity, pressure response, aperture-dependent resistance, and mixing behavior.

Several limitations should therefore be considered. The achievable transition width and spatial resolution are constrained by the residence-time distribution and axial dispersion within the static mixer and downstream nozzle. In addition, the valve-angle-to-composition relationship is expected to vary among ink systems and operating conditions. Future work should establish material-specific calibration curves and quantitatively characterize transition width, slope, spatial lag, and temporal response. Replicate-based quantitative evaluation of the geometry-specific gradient layouts will also be necessary to establish pattern reproducibility, while instrumented mechanical testing will be required to validate the proposed interfacial benefit. For biochemical gradients, direct measurement of local factor concentration and retention should be incorporated to distinguish the programmed nominal gradient from the gradient experienced by the cells. Addressing these limitations will be essential for advancing the platform from proof-of-concept gradient fabrication toward quantitatively predictive and application-specific gradient engineering.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a plug-in dual-ball-valve extrusion module to generate continuous and spatially programmable gradients within a single-nozzle bioprinting process. By controlling the downstream valve openings in a complementary manner, we were able to adjust the relative mixing ratio of two precursor inks without changing the upstream extrusion settings. Synchronization of valve actuation with nozzle movement allowed control over gradient onset, direction, reversal, transition order, and two-dimensional spatial layout. The same process strategy was applied to mechanical, cellular, and biochemical gradients. Continuous compositional transitions produced intermediate rheological states and distributed deformation across a broader interfacial region, while cell-laden printing maintained short-term viability and enabled graded cellular distributions. In addition, a printed TGF-β1 gradient induced spatially and temporally resolved EMT-associated responses in A549 cells. These results demonstrate that ball-valve-controlled mixing provides a versatile framework for integrating material composition, cell organization, and biochemical signaling into spatially heterogeneous bioprinted structures.
