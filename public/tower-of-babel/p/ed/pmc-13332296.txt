The Development of Telelearning as an Effort to Improve Nurses’ Readiness to Adopt Artificial Intelligence
Authors: Reni Asmara Ariga, Fatwa Imelda, Herriyance Herriyance, Edianto Edianto, Fajar Amanah Ariga, Selviani Ariga, Sri Budi Astuti, Hijrah Purnama Sari Ariga, Gabriela Marline Br Pasaribu, Tiki Anugraini Pasaribu, Ghina Faras Syifa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The digital transformation of healthcare is driving the integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) into clinical practice. Although telelearning is widely used in digital learning strategy, empirical evidence regarding its relationship with nurses’ readiness to adopt AI remains limited. This study aims to identify the level of telelearning development and nurses’ readiness to adopt AI, as well as to analyze the relationship in a Ministry of Health Hospital. A quantitative cross-sectional study involved 219 clinical nurses through census sampling. Univariate analysis and Spearman’s rho were used. Most respondents fell into high category for telelearning development (58.9%) and readiness to adopt AI (59.4%). There was a significant positive correlation between telelearning development and readiness to adopt AI (ρ=0.485; p=0.001). These findings indicate that a well-developed telelearning environment is associated with higher levels of AI readiness among nurses. Study limitations include the cross-sectional design and the use of self-report instruments. The recommendations should evaluate AI literacy modules integrated into telelearning, digital mentorship, AI policy co creation, and nursing curriculum revisions incorporating AI ethics and digital competency standards.

1. Introduction

Digital transformation is bringing significant structural changes to the global healthcare system. The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that over 70% of countries have national digital health strategies and are developing large-scale digital transformation programs. 1 Digital transformation demands a transition through digital systems to a more complex stage, namely the utilization of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in clinical data analysis, risk prediction, and decision-making support. However, the development of this digital infrastructure is often not accompanied by adequate human resource readiness, potentially leading to resistance, misuse of technology, and suboptimal utilization. 2

As a developing country, Indonesia is in the midst of an increasingly intensive digital health transformation, yet this process is not without challenges. The WHO indicates that the nurse-to-population ratio has reached 33 per 10,000 people. Additionally, the 2024 Indonesia Health Profile reports that there are 376,569 active nurses working in hospitals across Indonesia. 3 This figure underscores the significant potential of the nursing workforce in supporting the national digital transformation agenda. However, this potential has not yet been fully matched by digital literacy readiness given that nurses’ digital literacy levels remain varied. In fact, the success of digital transformation is determined not only by infrastructure readiness but also by healthcare workers’ ability to adapt to technological changes. This digital literacy gap directly impacts nurses’ readiness to understand, adopt, and optimally utilize AI-based technologies in clinical practice. 2

Telelearning serves as a technology-based learning approach tailored to nurses’ competency needs, offering flexible, interactive, and continuous access. Various findings indicate that telelearning is associated with digital literacy and healthcare workers’ ability to adapt to technological changes, thus conceptually holding the potential to influence nurses’ readiness to adopt AI. 4

Indonesian Ministry of Health hospitals, which function as both teaching hospitals and national referral hospitals, have begun integrating telelearning into their nurse competency development systems. This telelearning implementation is realized through Case Reflection Discussions (DRK) conducted on a regular basis. Through DRKs, nurses are encouraged to discuss real-world cases, conduct critical analysis, and reflect on ethical aspects and clinical decision-making within a structured forum. 5

Previous research has explored nurses’ attitudes, knowledge, and readiness regarding AI, as well as factors influencing the acceptance of this technology, such as knowledge, self-efficacy, and hospital support.6-9 Additionally, several studies have examined nurses’ readiness for telehealth, telemedicine, and telenursing, while research on telelearning remains limited to assessing improvements in digital literacy and learning effectiveness.10,11 and no studies have tested the development of a telelearning system in hospitals in relation to nurses’ readiness to adopt AI, which a gap in this research.

This research gap is further supported by a preliminary study conducted by the researchers through interviews with five unit heads and fifteen clinical nurses, revealing that most nurses have used AI-based technology during the COVID-19 pandemic, although its use remains limited to simple applications such as ChatGPT and MetaAI for retrieving clinical information. These preliminary findings indicate initial exposure to AI; however, it remains unclear to what extent existing telelearning initiatives contribute to nurses’ readiness to adopt AI in nursing practice.

The study aims to identify the level of telelearning development and nurses’ readiness to adopt AI, as well as to analyze the relationship between telelearning and readiness in the digital transformation of healthcare services in hospitals. The research hypothesis is that there is a significant positive relationship between the level of telelearning development and nurses’ readiness to adopt AI in the digital transformation of healthcare services in hospitals.

2. Method

2.1. Study Design

This study used a quantitative correlational design with a cross-sectional approach.

2.2. Location and Participants

The research was conducted from August to September 2025 at an Indonesian Ministry of Health hospital, which is a tertiary referral hospital and academic medical center located in an urban center. This hospital demonstrates a commitment to digital transformation, with a Hospital Information System (HIS) implementation rate of 37.55%, exceeding the national target of 25%. Additionally, telelearning development is implemented through digital platforms and Case Reflection Discussions (DRK), which are part of the professional performance indicators for nurses. 5

Sample selection began with the identification of the population based on personnel data, which indicated a total of 219 nurses. This was followed by a screening phase involving verification of inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criterion was having previously participated in a distance-learning course. The exclusion criteria were nurses holding managerial positions and not involved in clinical practice, as well as incomplete questionnaires or missing consent forms. Thus, using census sampling of all eligible nurses, 219 nurses were identified, and there were no internal or external dropouts among the respondents. The use of this sampling technique allows for data collection from all members of the population who meet the criteria, thereby providing a more accurate representation of each subgroup within the population and minimizing sampling error.

2.3. Potential Bias

Potential bias was controlled by ensuring the target population was clearly defined from the outset based on measurable inclusion criteria, as well as by conducting data collection independently through a structured questionnaire. To maintain objectivity and data quality, the researchers provided direct explanations regarding the questionnaire’s content and supervised the completion process in person to ensure respondents understood each statement item. Additionally, the completion time was limited to 10–20 minutes and adjusted to fit nurses’ work schedules to minimize cognitive load and avoid bias resulting from work fatigue that could affect the accuracy of respondents’ answers.

2.4. Research Instrument

The research instrument was developed by the researchers based on a literature review and adapted to the Indonesian language and hospital policies in Indonesia. The instrument consists of 3 sections, namely:1. Demographic characteristics, including age, gender, highest level of education, length of service, telelearning training, and experience using AI.2. Telelearning development, defined as nurses’ perceptions of the effectiveness of online learning systems in hospitals. It consists of 3 dimensions: a) Training, to measure training accessibility, training flexibility, relevance of learning content to clinical practice, improvement of professional competence, effectiveness of interactive media, and improvement in literacy and digital skills. b) Organizational and institutional support, to measure hospital organizational support. c) Infrastructure and policy, to measure technology infrastructure readiness and institutional policies and regulations. The instrument was adapted from the literature with adjustments to language and policies in Indonesia. The instrument underwent validity testing with 3 experts (I-CVI=1.00) and reliability testing using Cronbach’s Alpha (α=0.928).3. Nurses’ readiness to adopt AI is defined as the individual nurse’s level of readiness to understand, accept, and integrate AI technology responsibly into nursing practice. It consists of 5 dimensions, namely: a) Knowledge, to measure understanding of AI functions in nursing and perception of the importance of AI literacy. b) Attitude, to measure moral and ethical values and awareness of the risk of bias in decision-making. c) Skills, to measure reflective thinking skills and awareness of analytical skills. d) Self-efficacy, to measure readiness to learn and perception of limitations in ethical reflection skills. e) Organizational support, to measure organizational readiness. The instrument was validated by 3 experts (I-CVI = 1.00) and its reliability was tested using Cronbach’s Alpha (α = 0.913).

Each variable consists of 9 positive statements using a Likert scale: 1 (Strongly Disagree), 2 (Disagree), 3 (Neutral), 4 (Agree), and 5 (Strongly Agree). Total scores were categorized as low (9–21), moderate (22–33), and high (34–45). This categorization, based on dividing the score range into three equal intervals, was done to facilitate the interpretation of respondents’ perception levels.

3. Ethics and Data Collection Procedures

The study received approval from the Health Research Ethics Committee. Prior to distributing the questionnaires, the researchers provided verbal and written explanations regarding the study’s objectives, procedures, benefits, and respondents’ rights, including the right to refuse or withdraw at any time without consequences. Respondents who were willing to participate signed an informed consent form. Questionnaires were distributed directly to nurses in their respective units and completed independently outside of their shift hours to avoid disrupting patient care, with an estimated completion time of 15–20 minutes. Researchers were present to provide clarification if needed. All questionnaires were checked for completeness on the same day, and there were no missing data in the analyzed dataset.

4. Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software. The analysis stages included: (1) Univariate analysis to describe the frequency distribution of respondent characteristics and the scores of each variable; (2) Bivariate analysis using Spearman’s rho test to examine the relationship between telelearning development and nurses’ readiness to adopt AI.

5. Results

5.1. Demographic Characteristics

The frequency distribution and percentage of respondents showed that the majority of nurses were aged 46–55 years (35.2%), female (80.4%), held a diploma in nursing as their highest level of education (70.8%), and had more than 20 years of work experience (39.3%). All respondents had participated in telelearning-based training (100%). However, only 147 nurses (67.1%) had ever used AI (Table 1).

5.2. Univariate Analysis

5.2.1 Telelearning Development

The frequency and percentage of telelearning development variables indicate that the majority of nurses assessed distance learning as providing good accessibility (33.8%) and training flexibility (33.3%), with content relevant to clinical practice (37.9%). Indicators of improved professional competence and the effectiveness of interactive media also received strong approval (32%), accompanied by improvements in digital literacy and skills (39.7%). From the perspective of organizational and institutional support, 31.5% of nurses assessed that hospitals provided adequate support. Additionally, regarding infrastructure and policies, technological readiness (39.7%) and policies supporting the implementation of distance learning (35.6%) were rated as good (Table 2).

The development of telelearning was categorized as low, moderate, and high. The majority of respondents indicated a high level (58.9%), followed by the moderate category (35.6%). These findings indicate that the development of telelearning in hospitals has been proceeding optimally and has received a positive response from the majority of nurses.

5.2.2 Nurses’ Readiness to Adopt AI

The frequency and percentage of variables related to nurses’ readiness to adopt AI indicate that the majority of nurses understand the functions of AI (34.7%) and recognize the importance of AI literacy (35.6%). In terms of attitudes, nurses agree with the application of moral and ethical principles (38.4%) and are aware of the potential for bias (32.9%). In terms of skills, 35.6% of nurses are highly capable of engaging in reflection, and 31.5% state they are ready to apply analytical skills. Additionally, self-confidence is reflected in the willingness to continue learning (33.8%) and the ability to recognize the limitations of ethical reflection (34.7%). Organizational support is considered quite good, with 33.8% of nurses stating that their institutions have provided facilities and support for AI competency development (Table 3).

Nurses’ readiness to adopt AI was categorized as low, moderate, and high. The majority of nurses fell into the high-readiness category (59.4%), followed by the moderate-e category (32.4%). These results indicate that the majority of nurses already possess the knowledge, attitudes, skills, and self-efficacy that support the AI adoption process.

5.3. Bivariate Analysis

Results of the bivariate analysis using the Spearman test showed a significant relationship between the development of telelearning and nurses’ readiness to adopt AI (ρ=0.485; p=0.001). The positive correlation coefficient indicates that the better the implementation of telelearning, the higher the level of nurses’ readiness to use AI technology. Thus, the hypothesis is accepted.

6. Discussion

The research results show that the majority of nurses fall into the high category in both telelearning development (58.9%) and readiness to adopt AI (59.4%). These findings indicate that the digital learning environment and individual nurses’ readiness for the integration of AI technology have developed quite well in the hospitals studied.

Bivariate analysis revealed a significant positive relationship between telelearning development and nurses’ readiness to adopt AI (ρ = 0.485; p = 0.001). The correlation coefficient, which falls into the moderate category, indicates that the better the development of telelearning within the organizational environment, the higher the nurses’ readiness to use AI in clinical practice. Thus, the results of this study demonstrate a meaningful association between these two variables.

The novelty of this study lies in filling a research gap by specifically examining the relationship between the development of telelearning systems in hospitals and nurses’ readiness to adopt AI, whereas previous studies tended to be limited to evaluating digital literacy or the effectiveness of telelearning. This novelty is demonstrated through an analysis of the relationship between the Case Reflection Discussion (DRK) telelearning method and improvements in nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and skills regarding AI.

7. Telelearning as an Enabler of Digital Literacy and AI Readiness

The development of telelearning in hospitals is associated with nurses’ readiness to adopt AI and aligns with the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), where the effectiveness of structured and flexible digital learning systems can enhance nurses’ perceived usefulness and self-efficacy regarding new technologies. When nurses perceive that telelearning facilitates access to information and is relevant to clinical practice, it reduces resistance and strengthens behavioral intentions to integrate AI into daily nursing care.12,13

The success of telelearning in this study is also consistent with Adult Learning Theory, where learning for clinical nurses is more effective when it is contextual and oriented toward solving real-world problems. Through the telelearning method in Case Reflection Discussions (DRK), nurses not only receive passive knowledge transfer but are also engaged in critical analysis that strengthens digital literacy and adaptive skills. This approach, relevant to professional needs, ensures that digital transformation in hospitals is supported by HR that is cognitively and operationally prepared.14,15

The integration of telelearning strengthens nurses’ readiness across the domains of knowledge, attitude, and skill. Findings indicate that nurses with high exposure to telelearning demonstrate a better understanding of AI functions (knowledge), stronger ethical values regarding the risk of bias (attitude), and sharper reflective and analytical abilities (skill). This aligns with the literature on digital competencies and AI literacy, where active engagement in self-directed learning and academic progress serve as strong predictors of increased technological readiness in healthcare. 16

8. Organizational and Infrastructural Facilitators and Barriers

The success of the transition toward AI adoption depends heavily on the maturity of the digital infrastructure and hospital support. The implementation of digital systems in hospitals helps nurses become familiar with the data ecosystem. However, research findings indicate a participation gap; although all respondents (100%) have completed telelearning-based training, readiness for AI adoption remains uneven. This suggests that technological availability alone is insufficient without strong organizational readiness, including adaptive hospital policies and adequate facility support.

The work environment plays a decisive role in determining the outcomes of this digital transformation. Referring to the literature on the relationship between the work environment and professional quality, an environment lacking in infrastructure and managerial support can hinder the effectiveness of the training received. Digital leadership is key to bridging this gap by fostering innovation and providing stable resources. Additionally, hospital policies must focus on reducing administrative workload through automation so that nurses can adopt new technologies without feeling overwhelmed.17,18

Hospitals need to ensure their support extends beyond merely providing hardware to strengthening support systems. Digital infrastructure readiness, coupled with clear policies, will minimize technical barriers that are often the primary cause of nurse resistance. By strengthening digital leadership and improving the quality of the work environment, hospitals can ensure that investments in telelearning translate into tangible AI adoption readiness, which ultimately enhances the efficiency of nursing human resources and the quality of patient care.19,20

9. Age, Education, and Work Experience as Moderating Factors

Research findings reveal challenging demographic dynamics, with the majority of nurses falling within the 46–55 age range (35.2%) and having over 20 years of work experience (39.3%). This phenomenon indicates a generational digital divide, where senior nurses must adapt to the rapid pace of digital transformation that has only recently become widespread in Indonesia over the past few years. Long tenure is often correlated with resistance to change, due to comfort with conventional care methods. In Indonesia’s work culture, which highly values seniority, senior nurses’ perceptions of AI significantly influence adoption rates within work units; if senior nurses express skepticism, this can hinder technological transformation even when the technology is widely available. 21

The educational landscape, dominated by diploma graduates (70.8%), has implications for digital literacy and exposure to technologies like AI. Referring to the literature on healthcare workforce competencies, formal education levels are closely linked to the ability for self-directed learning and the application of scientific literature in practice. The dominance of diploma graduates in Indonesia’s national healthcare system demands telelearning strategies that are more practical and less theoretical. This digital literacy gap poses a strategic barrier because previous vocational education curricula have not deeply integrated AI competencies, necessitating interventions to bridge these academic background limitations. 22

10. Ethical and Professional Implications of AI Integration

The integration of AI into nursing care presents ethical challenges. One of the main obstacles is the concern over the dehumanization of care, where the use of algorithms is feared to diminish the human touch and empathy that are at the core of the nursing profession. According to studies, nurses often feel anxious about the risk of algorithmic bias, which could exacerbate service disparities if the data used is not representative. Therefore, AI readiness must be redefined as a holistic competency that includes ethical understanding to ensure this technology functions as a support, not a replacement, for nurses’ clinical judgment.23,24

In addressing this complexity, the development of self-efficacy and professional resilience becomes a key factor. Nurses need strong self-confidence to interact with AI systems without losing their professional authority. Resilience helps nurses remain adaptable amid technological changes, but this must be accompanied by mechanisms for moral reflection. Critical reflection allows nurses to continually evaluate whether recommendations from AI systems align with nursing ethical values and patient safety. Without this capacity for reflection, there is a risk of blind reliance on technology that could threaten professional integrity. 25

11. Implications

This research has implications for patient experience: nurses’ readiness for AI is a critical prerequisite for creating safe experiences for patients and families in the digital age. Nurses with digital literacy, self-efficacy, and an ethical understanding of AI are better able to leverage the system for accurate clinical decision-making, expedite patient information analysis, and support documentation. The use of AI supported by nurses’ readiness also has the potential to improve patient safety through earlier risk detection and reduced documentation errors. Furthermore, when nurses understand the technology being used, communication with patients and families regarding the care process becomes clearer, more transparent, and data-driven, thereby increasing trust in digital health services. On the other hand, optimizing digital systems has the potential to reduce repetitive administrative burdens, allowing nurses to have more time for direct therapeutic interactions with patients.

The findings of this study also have implications for policymakers, hospital management, and nurses. For health policymakers, strengthening nurses’ AI literacy and digital competencies must be integrated into national competency standards and nursing education curricula, including ethical aspects of technology use. For hospital management, investment in telelearning is not sufficient if it focuses solely on providing a platform; it must be accompanied by consistent policies, strengthened digital infrastructure, continuous supervision, and leadership that fosters a culture of innovation. Organizational readiness is a key factor in ensuring that individual readiness can be implemented systemically. For nurses, these findings underscore the importance of continuous learning, reflection on the use of AI, and the strengthening of professional integrity in the face of digital transformation.

12. Study Limitations

This study has several limitations, namely: 1) The cross-sectional design only allows for the identification of an association between telelearning development and AI adoption readiness, so it cannot be used to infer a causal relationship. 2) The study was conducted at a single hospital, so the organizational characteristics, work culture, and digital infrastructure at that location may limit the generalization of findings to other hospitals with different levels of digital readiness. 3) The use of self-report instruments has the potential to introduce perceptual bias and social desirability bias, where respondents may provide more positive answers regarding technological readiness. Additionally, the inclusion all of whom had participated in telelearning and the majority of whom had been exposed to AI potentially leads to an overestimation of the actual readiness level when compared to a population of nurses who have not had similar exposure. 4) The absence of qualitative data limits a deeper understanding of the experiences, resistance, or contextual dynamics influencing AI adoption readiness.

13. Conclusion

This study indicates that the majority of nurses fall into the high category regarding telelearning development and readiness for AI adoption, and there is a significant positive relationship with a moderate correlation strength between these two variables. These findings suggest a meaningful association between digital learning experiences and professional readiness to address AI integration in clinical settings.

Future research should employ longitudinal or experimental designs to more robustly test the directional relationship between telelearning and AI adoption readiness. The development of AI literacy modules integrated into telelearning, including AI-based ethical decision-making simulations, could be evaluated as structured interventions. Studies are also recommended to explore organizational strategies such as digital mentorship and co-creation of AI policies with nurses. Cross-national research and mixed-method approaches will broaden generalizability and deepen understanding of the professional cultural dynamics in AI adoption, while supporting the strengthening of digital competency and AI ethics standards in nursing curricula.
