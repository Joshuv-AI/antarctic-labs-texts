Agreement in Qualitative and Quantitative Assessments of Disease Severity: Evidence from Pharmaceutical Reimbursement in Sweden
Authors: Martin Henriksson, Lars Sandman, Jonathan Siverskog
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesPublicly funded healthcare systems that consider a trade-off between efficiency and equity by allowing a higher cost per patient benefit in patients with more severe conditions must somehow assess disease severity. Some countries employ quantitative measures of shortfall, whereas others rely on qualitative assessments. Despite its importance in pharmaceutical reimbursement and pricing, the operationalisation of disease severity in real-world decision making has rarely been scrutinized. The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship and agreement between qualitative disease severity assessments and quantitative measures of disease severity in Swedish pharmaceutical reimbursement.MethodsInformation from 36 pharmaceutical reimbursement decisions made by the Dental and Pharmaceutical Benefits Agency (TLV) in Sweden from 2018 to 2023 was extracted, including the qualitative assessment of disease severity (moderate, high, or very high). Based on publicly available decision documents from the agency, we calculated absolute QALY shortfall (AS) and proportional QALY shortfall (PS). Linear regression was used to describe the mean shortfall across severity classifications. Ordinal logistic regression was used to analyse the role of AS and PS as predictors of TLV’s qualitative disease-severity assessments and the predictive ability of both measures was compared using the coefficient of discrimination (D’).ResultsThe mean AS and PS was 12.2 and 0.796, respectively, in the very high disease severity category, which was approximately twice the mean shortfall observed in the moderate and high severity categories (Moderate: AS = 6.0, PS = 0.340; High: AS = 6.2, PS = 0.405). When the quantitative measures of severity were used as predictors of the qualitative assessments, PS was better able to discriminate between TLV’s severity classifications than was AS (D’ = 34.6% vs 22.3%). However, both measures frequently predicted low probabilities of the qualitative assessments that were observed and there was both substantial variation in shortfall for diseases with the same qualitative assessment (AS, R2 = 35.8%; PS, R2 = 61.0%) and overlaps in observed shortfall across different severity classifications.ConclusionProportional QALY shortfall agrees more closely than AS with qualitatively assessed disease severity applied in the Swedish reimbursement system but there are large variations in the qualitative assessments that cannot be explained by either measure. Further investigation is warranted to understand if this is an intended and desired outcome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40258-026-01040-8.

Key Points

Different uses of the concept of disease severity for priority setting in healthcare will likely lead to different resource allocations and decision makers must understand the implications of using a particular approach in real-world decision making.Whether an algorithmic quantitative approach or a qualitative judgement shall form the basis for the implementation of a disease severity decision modifier should be based on careful deliberation as the empirical evidence provided in this work suggests there are large variations between the two approaches.

Introduction

Publicly funded healthcare systems with population health responsibilities that consider efficiency and equity face, and must operationalise, an inevitable trade-off between health maximisation and the distribution of health across individuals in society [1–3]. For example, Swedish legislation stipulates that patients with higher disease severity have a greater claim on collective healthcare resources, but that these resources should still be allocated so that there is a reasonable relationship between cost and effect [4]. Although not explicitly mentioned in the legislation, it has been argued that such an approach is rooted in prioritarianism, a theory of distributive justice recognising that health gains in patients with severe conditions have a higher moral importance than similar gains in patients with less severe conditions [5]. The Dental and Pharmaceutical Benefits Agency (TLV) in Sweden operates under this principle when making pharmaceutical reimbursement decisions and is thus forced to not only define and assess disease severity but also determine how to prioritise patients belonging to the high severity groups [6]. Currently, the agency uses a qualitative approach to assess disease severity, and for patients belonging to a high-severity category a higher costs per health gain is accepted.

In recent years the qualitative approach employed by TLV has been criticised for lacking transparency potentially leading to arbitrary reimbursement decisions [7], and it has been proposed that TLV should move in the direction of a more quantitative approach [8]. Other countries considering severity when deciding how to prioritise treatments use quantitative approaches. For example, Norway and the Netherlands employ absolute and proportional shortfall, respectively [9, 10]. Recently, the same avenue was pursued by NICE in the UK where a change in the technology appraisal manual introduces a decision modifier related to the severity of the condition under consideration, using a combination of absolute and proportional shortfall, replacing the end-of-life modifier [11]. The implications for real-world decision making of choosing a particular approach is of particular interest in pharmaceutical reimbursement and pricing policy where the operationalisation of disease severity has rarely been scrutinised even though different approaches may influence the severity assessment of conditions eligible to be treated with new pharmaceuticals. Motivated by the suggestion to move toward a quantitative assessment of severity in drug reimbursement in Sweden, the aim of this study was to explore how the qualitative approach to severity assessment used in Sweden compares to quantitative approaches used in other healthcare jurisdictions in Europe. The study adds to the current literature by providing a comparison of qualitative and quantitative assessments of disease severity in a jurisdiction where disease severity is of major importance for pharmaceutical reimbursement and pricing. Furthermore, our findings allow us to elaborate on the implications of using different approaches to disease severity as a priority setting criterion in healthcare decision making.

Efficiency and Equity Trade-Offs

Within a budget constrained healthcare sector, allocating resources to an intervention or health programme implies the resources were not used for a competing health-generating activity. These lost opportunities, or health forgone, are the health opportunity costs of spending healthcare resources in a particular way [3, 12]. Economic evaluations typically compare the costs and health outcomes of two or more interventions to assess whether a particular health intervention would provide positive health effects, i.e., if the health gained by spending the resources on the intervention is higher than the health lost from making the resources available. However, prioritisation decisions often take several aspects into account of which cost effectiveness is merely one [13]. Many jurisdictions employ a multi-criteria approach in the allocation of scarce healthcare resources, often considering a trade-off between efficiency (cost effectiveness) and distributive justice (e.g., giving priority to patients with more severe conditions) [14]. Such considerations imply that some redistribution of resources to patients considered worse off may be accepted, albeit at a cost of lower total health than the maximum, and as noted by Wiliams [2] more than 25 years ago, we must decide how big a sacrifice in the overall health of the population we are prepared to accept to eliminate disparities in health.

In Sweden, TLV’s practice has been to base drug reimbursement decisions primarily on the cost per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained and a case-by-case assessment of disease severity. Accepting a higher cost per QALY for treatment of severe disease has been a way to operationalise the needs-solidarity principle in the ethics platform [6] that is part of the overarching legislation for healthcare in Sweden outlined in more detail in Siverskog and Henriksson [3]. Although the implementation of policy differs across all health systems, similar approaches to the trade-off between efficiency and equity can, as noted above, be found in for example Norway, the Netherlands, and the UK [9–11].

Measuring Disease Severity

Disease severity is a multifaceted concept and its role in healthcare priority setting has been debated [14]. In this study, we take as point of departure two quantitative measures of disease severity that have been suggested in the literature and are applied in other countries and compare these with the qualitative approach to disease severity used by TLV.

A patient’s absolute QALY shortfall (AS) is defined as the difference between the remaining quality-adjusted life expectancy of a healthy individual and the remaining quality-adjusted life expectancy of the patient. For example, assume that a 60-year-old patient is expected to accrue 12 QALYs with the disease, and that the same figure for a 60-year-old healthy individual is 16 QALYs. In this case, AS would be 4. In practice, the number of QALYs that patients would be expected to experience over the remainder of their lives under current care can be derived from the economic evaluation informing a reimbursement decision and then be compared with the number of QALYs expected in the general population [15]. Proportional QALY shortfall (PS) is instead defined as the fraction of QALYs that people lose relative to those experienced by a healthy individual. It is calculated as a patient’s AS divided by the quality-adjusted life expectancy of the general population [15, 16]. In the above example, PS would be 0.25.

The qualitative approach by TLV categorises disease severity on a 4-grade scale as mild, moderate, high, or very high. The process of categorisation involves a qualitative assessment based on a supporting tool with 4 aspects to be considered. The first is how disease is expected to impact health-related quality-of-life, the second and third are duration and frequency of illness, and the final is the effect of the disease on life-expectancy [8]. There is no explicit guidance regarding how these aspects should be weighed against each other to arrive at a severity category, but given that all four criteria (particularly the first and the last) are measurable in terms of an impact on quality-adjusted life expectancy, AS and PS are reasonable competing candidates both for approximating how TLV currently assess disease severity and for formalising TLV’s qualitative approach.

Methods

Data Extraction

Data were extracted from publicly available decision documents published by TLV [17]. We reviewed all decisions made from 2018 up to include 2023, where full reimbursement (the full indication) or restricted reimbursement (a subgroup of the indication) had been granted. Decisions prior to 2018 were excluded as the background information to the reimbursement decisions made by TLV was sparse, implying that the relevant calculations could not be carried out.

Cases approved by a cost-minimisation analysis (n = 173) and decisions from reassessments not involving any new health economic data (n = 15) were not relevant for this work. Of the 109 decisions involving a cost-effectiveness assessment, 7 provided a qualitative assessment, i.e., it was argued that the incremental costs were low in relation to the gain in QALYs without presenting any calculations of cost effectiveness. Of the 102 remaining decisions that could potentially be used for shortfall calculations 36 had complete data. Reasons for exclusion were no assessment of disease severity made by TLV (n = 3), the expected QALYs in the comparator arm from the economic evaluation were lacking (n = 43), the relevant time horizon of the economic evaluation or the start age of the evaluated cohort was missing (n = 20). Comprehensive data were not extracted from excluded decisions, but the distributions of disease severity categorisations were similar in included and excluded decisions. It should be noted that TLV commonly provides two documents for a decision: a formal, rather brief decision document summarising the decision, and a more extensive document with details regarding clinical information and the health economic assessment. If all required information was not available in the brief document the more extensive assessment report was reviewed.

Shortfall Calculations

For the shortfall calculations we used standard mortality rates from statistics Sweden to calculate gender-specific probability of survival at different ages for starting ages ranging from 0 to 100 years. The data were combined with age- and gender-specific QALY-weights of the Swedish population [18] to estimate the expected quality-adjusted survival for all starting ages. This was performed for men and women separately, and for a combined estimate assuming an even gender split. The estimates of life expectancy and quality-adjusted life expectancy are shown in Fig. 1 using an equal gender split. In the calculation of shortfall, we used the QALY estimate presented as TLV’s base case in the assessment reports for the comparator strategy and applied the standard formulas of shortfall presented above. The comparator strategy, rather than the QALY estimate of the pharmaceutical under assessment, was assumed to best represent the patient group’s expected QALYs at the time of the reimbursement decision. This is the same approach as outlined by Wailoo [15]. As TLV reports discounted QALYs in their assessments, we used the discounted expected QALYs of the general population in the calculations (solid line in Fig. 1). If a TLV assessment reported 10 expected discounted QALYs for the comparator strategy in their assessment, and the mean age of the evaluated cohort was 65 years AS would be 3.20 QALYs (13.20-10) and PS would be 0.24 (3.20/13.20) as the expected discounted quality-adjusted survival for 65-year-old individuals is 13.20.

If the patient population comprised men or women only, we used the expected QALYs of the general population for the relevant gender. In cases where the patient population of interest was of mixed gender, the expected QALYs of a 50/50 gender split in the general population was used because the gender composition of the analysed cohorts was often lacking in the TLV documentation. It should be noted that the differences between the expected QALY estimates across gender compositions were small. The starting age of the analysed cohorts was rounded to the nearest integer.

Statistical Analysis

Linear regression was used to describe how average AS and PS differed across TLV’s severity classifications. The association between TLV’s qualitative assessment of disease severity (as the dependent variable) and the quantitative measures of QALY shortfall (as predictors) was estimated by ordinal logistic regression. As a robustness check, estimation was also done by multinomial logistic regression and linear regression (with one model for each category). Furthermore, estimation was done with and without adjustment for patient age and an indicator for cancer disease to explore the role of AS and PS as independent predictors. Goodness of fit was compared using the coefficient of discrimination (D’) [19, 20], which measured the ability of our models to predict TLV’s severity classifications on a scale from 0% (null model with no predictive ability) to 100% (perfect model with complete predictive ability). The relevance of adding patient age, cancer disease, or a measure of QALY shortfall as predictors to more parsimonious models was assessed with the likelihood-ratio (LR) test. Hypothesis tests were conducted for significance at 5%.

The LR-tests (online supplementary material, Table S2) showed that both AS (p < 0.001) and PS (p < 0.001) were significant predictors of TLV’s severity classifications when added to a model including only patient age and cancer disease. Cancer disease was a significant predictor of higher disease severity classification in both the AS model (p < 0.001) and the PS model (p < 0.001). Age on the other hand only helped to significantly predict disease severity in combination with AS (p = 0.024) where it was positively associated with higher severity classification. The same inferences were drawn when estimation was done by multinomial logistic regression instead of ordinal logistic regression (online supplementary material, Tables S1 and S2). Finally, the addition of patient age and cancer disease as predictors led to a large improvement in goodness of fit and made the difference in predictive ability between the AS model and the PS model smaller (AS: D’ = 55.0%; PS: D’ = 56.9%, online supplementary material, Table S3). Further model testing and model parameters are available in the online supplementary material (Tables S4–S6).

Discussion

Collecting publicly available data in Sweden enabled us to study real-world health policy decisions with an explicit objective that more resources should be given to patients who are worse off in terms of disease severity. The reimbursement decisions studied operationalised this principle by allowing a higher cost per QALY if the disease severity was higher. The mean shortfall estimates were similar for diseases categorised as moderate and high severity by TLV. For diseases categorised as very high severity the shortfall measures were twice as high as for diseases categorised as moderate or high. The shortfall measures varied substantially within the qualitative severity categories employed by TLV. For example, AS for chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, metastatic prostate cancer, and haemophilia type A was 3.48, 7.98, and 25.14, respectively, with predicted probabilities of very high severity at 15.1%, 45.2%, and 99.7%, respectively, but all 3 diseases were categorised as very severe by TLV, and therefore the same approval threshold of SEK 1 million was employed for treatments of these diseases. Furthermore, diseases with similar shortfall measures were often categorised in different severity categories by TLV. For example, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, breast cancer (HER2-positive), and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (low-density lipoprotein (LDL)-cholesterol > 2.0 mmol/L) had similar AS measures ranging from 3.35 to 3.91 with predicted probabilities of moderate severity around 50%; still, disease severity was categorised by TLV as very high, high, and moderate, respectively, and therefore different approval thresholds were applied for treatments of these diseases. Previous work suggests that these thresholds are SEK 1 million, 750,000, and 500,000, respectively, implying that different opportunity costs are being accepted for treatments of conditions equally severe according to a measure of QALY shortfall [3, 21].

We found that both higher PS and AS positively predicted TLV making higher disease severity classifications. Cancer disease was also a predictor of higher disease severity categorisation, whereas age only contributed to the prediction of a very high severity classification together with AS. One explanation for the latter finding may be related to the emphasis on time to death as one aspect to reflect the life-expectancy impact in the qualitative assessment of disease severity as time to death is similarly neutral to age as PS. We can only speculate if this is an indication that the qualitative assessments may be drawn closer to PS to avoid potentially perceived age discrimination against the elderly as prioritising based on chronological age is ruled out by the healthcare legislation in Sweden [4].1 All else equal a year lost will result in a higher PS the older the patient population, whereas all else equal a decrement in health-related quality of life of 0.1 will yield the same PS regardless of age.

It should be noted that the purpose of this paper was not to determine whether TLV’s categorisations of severity were ‘correct’, rather our data provide a way to illuminate some of the criticism raised around TLV’s approach to assess disease severity and point to the implications of choosing different approaches to disease severity [7]. Given the lack of in-depth discussion and study of real-world implications of different approaches in the jurisdictions mentioned above, this should stimulate more discussion of whether the implications are acceptable or not. Based on the material published by TLV, it is difficult to disentangle the reasons for the observed differences between the shortfall measures and the qualitative assessments. Although the support tool employed by TLV appears to mimic a qualitative assessment of the shortfall measures, it is not ruled out that other aspects were considered in their assessments [8]. It is therefore not possible to assess if the discrepancies observed are due to erroneous qualitative assessments of quality of life, duration of symptoms, and life expectancy, or whether other aspects were, either intentionally or unintentionally, considered in the severity assessments. For example, our findings indicate that if two diseases are associated with the same measurable QALY shortfall, but one is cancer and one is not, TLV is more likely to assess disease severity for the cancer as very high. Whether this is an intended and ethically motivated outcome is something that would seem to warrant further inquiry. It is also important to acknowledge the small sample limitation of our study. Although it should be noted that if the difference between the qualitative categorisations and the quantitative measures is a concern, this is still true for the sample we were able to investigate. However, one should be careful in drawing general statistical conclusions as we cannot rule out that the decisions that we excluded due to lack of data would have revealed a different association. Our study should primarily be viewed as a first attempt to provide rare insights into the operationalisation of disease severity in real-world decision making.

Given the importance of disease severity for drug reimbursement and pricing in several countries we believe further efforts should be devoted to problematising the concept of severity and the methods used for its assessment; our results provide a first attempt in doing so. Recent research from both Sweden and Norway indicates that different approval thresholds are indeed applied for more severe diseases in real-world decision making [21, 22]. The Netherlands and the UK also explicitly state that higher approval thresholds should be applied for more severe diseases, although we are not aware of any studies investigating how decision makers have applied this in practice. The approach to disease severity differs between countries with Sweden being the only country applying a qualitative approach. Norway use AS, the Netherlands PS, and the UK a combination of AS and PS. Norway and the Netherlands do not discount severity whereas the UK does. Furthermore, all countries use different weighting schemes (i.e., approval thresholds) for different disease severity categories implying that the impact on potential drug pricing of the same disease may be different in different countries. This may be due to differences in inequality aversion or the perceived opportunity costs associated with paying more for QALYs in high-severity patient populations, or both. It may also be the case that these policies are more due to path dependence and that further scrutiny of how disease severity is operationalised in priority setting may lead to policies being re-evaluated. The purpose of our present work is primarily to stimulate such a discussion. In terms of policy implications from a Swedish point of view, we believe that the intention by TLV to add quantitative shortfall measures to the qualitative assessments would be a welcomed move. A challenge remains as to how these should be combined and on what grounds the quantitative shortfall measures should potentially be weighted with the qualitative component. We conjecture that a quantitative approach would have added information that potentially could have changed some of the decisions that stand out as potentially inconsistent, i.e., that the same shortfall measure led to different disease severity categorisations by TLV or that diseases within the same disease severity categorisation were associated with widely varying shortfall estimates.

In conclusion, the qualitatively assessed disease severity applied in the Swedish reimbursement system correlates with average quantitative shortfall measures, with PS predicting the TLV categorisation better than AS. There are large individual variations that warrant further investigation to understand if this is an intended and desired outcome.
