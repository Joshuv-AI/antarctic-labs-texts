Silicified strontium-curcumin chelated nanospheres mitigate inflammatory vicious cycles to accelerate diabetic wound healing
Authors: Zhiwei Zhao, Youjun Ding, Guangjin Gao, Yepeng Zhang, Zhaowenbin Zhang, Min Zhou, Ye Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In diabetic wounds, the overexpression of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the imbalance of the immune microenvironment surrounding immune cells, such as macrophages, can trigger a vicious cycle of inflammation in the wound area, a key factor contributing to impaired wound healing. To effectively address this challenge, this study innovatively harnessed the chelation-synergistic effect between strontium (Sr) ions, which possess immune-regulating functions, and curcumin (Cur), which exhibits antioxidant properties, to develop and design silicified strontium-curcumin chelated nanospheres (Sr-Cur NPs) with dual functions of anti-ROS and immune regulation. Subsequently, these nanospheres were formulated into a composite hydrogel (Sr-Cur@Alg) using sodium alginate. Sr-Cur@Alg successfully blocks the inflammatory vicious cycle formed by macrophages and endothelial cells under diabetic pathological conditions through TLR4/MyD88/NF-кB pathway. Specifically, it prevents macrophages in the M1 polarization state from further inducing endothelial cells to secrete large amounts of ROS, while also blocking the adverse effects of endothelial cells in this state on the continuous M1 polarization of macrophages. This innovative strategy accelerates the healing of diabetic wounds, ultimately presenting a promising therapeutic avenue for their management.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus has emerged as a global health challenge, affecting nearly 6 % of the adult population worldwide [1]. Diabetic wounds, a common complication of diabetes, have a lifetime incidence ranging from 19 % to 34 %. Based on this, it is estimated that the number of patients with diabetic wounds globally ranges from 112 million to 194 million, a substantial figure. Among these patients, the amputation rate for diabetic foot exceeds 20 %, and the mortality rate within five years post-amputation reaches a concerning 50 %–70 % [2,3]. Amputation has a devastating impact on patients’ daily functioning and quality of life. Moreover, it is strongly linked to an increased incidence of complications such as cardiovascular disease and depression. Consequently, the treatment of diabetic wounds represents a critical clinical challenge that requires urgent resolution. Delving into the pathogenesis, it becomes that the primary cause of impaired healing in diabetic wounds is the persistent inflammatory response of the wound. This sustained inflammation further leads to the loss of viability in skin-related cells in the wound area, such as endothelial cells and smooth muscle cells, thereby slowing down the rate of angiogenesis. The impaired angiogenesis, in turn, results in insufficient oxygen and nutrient supply to the wound site, which delays wound healing or may lead to non-healing wounds [4]. Further research has revealed that the overexpression of reactive oxygen species (ROS) at the wound site and the imbalance in the immune microenvironment of immune cells, such as macrophages, are the primary culprits behind the excessive inflammatory storm in the wound region [[5], [6], [7]]. In response to this challenge, numerous researchers have conducted studies and developed a range of wound dressings with ROS-scavenging capabilities [5,8] or immunomodulatory effects [9]. However, due to the interplay between ROS and the imbalance in the immune microenvironment, which mutually enhance each other’s capacity to mediate the inflammatory storm, a vicious cycle of inflammation is established [[10], [11], [12]]. This implies that wound dressings with a single function of either anti-ROS or immunomodulation are ineffective in breaking the vicious cycle of inflammation in diabetic wounds in practical applications. Considering the current challenges, the creation of a wound dressing that combines antioxidant and immunomodulatory functions represents a promising therapeutic strategy to break the persistent inflammatory cycle and facilitate the repair of diabetic wounds.

Strontium ions (Sr), known as trace elements with immune-regulating capabilities, are critically involved in modulating immune responses. They are capable of inhibiting the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines by macrophages, such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), while simultaneously promoting the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines, including transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) and IL-10. This process induces the expression of M2-type markers in macrophages and facilitates their polarization toward the M2 phenotype, thereby effectively alleviating the immune microenvironment imbalance mediated by macrophages [[13], [14], [15]]. Curcumin (Cur), on the other hand, is a naturally occurring flavonoid compound. The hydrogen atoms on the phenolic hydroxyl groups in its molecular structure exhibit high reactivity, conferring a certain degree of acidity. When encountering ROS, such as superoxide anion radicals (·O2−) and hydroxyl radicals (·OH), curcumin donates hydrogen atoms (H·) to react with ROS, thereby efficiently scavenging them [16,17]. More intriguingly, flavonoid compounds like curcumin are capable of undergoing unique chelation reactions with divalent metal ions such as Sr ions. The resulting chelates significantly enhance the original biological functions. Currently, such chelation systems have been widely applied in the repair of burn wounds and other types of refractory wounds [[18], [19], [20]]. Based on the aforementioned properties, if a wound dressing containing Sr-Cur chelates can be designed, it may simultaneously achieve anti-ROS and immunomodulatory functions, thereby blocking the vicious cycle of inflammation and significantly promoting the repair of diabetic wounds.

However, Sr ions face the challenge of limited long-term retention at the wound site. They are rapidly excreted from the wound site through body fluids, limiting their sustained efficacy. Therefore, it is essential to establish a microenvironment at the wound site that allows for the sustained release of Sr ions [21]. Meanwhile, Cur is limited by poor water solubility and low bioavailability. When used alone, cells in the wound site have difficulty absorbing Cur, preventing it from exerting its intended effects [[22], [23], [24]]. Given these circumstances, it is necessary to modify the system in which Sr and Cur coexist to more efficiently deliver the Sr-Cur chelate to the wound site. Research has revealed that silicon (Si) nanospheres can incorporate various trace elements during their self-assembly process, thereby achieving long-term delivery of these elements [20,25]. Interestingly, when Cur is thoroughly mixed with Si nanoparticles, this flavonoid compound undergoes silicified modification. The Si ions released from the Si nanoparticles can create a bioactive alkaline microenvironment, thereby significantly enhancing the solubility and bioavailability of Cur [26]. Consequently, if silicified strontium-curcumin chelate nanospheres are constructed during the self-assembly process of Si nanospheres and then fabricated into a wound dressing, it is promising to achieve the long-term delivery of the Sr-Cur chelate.

Building on this foundation, this study innovatively adopted a strategy of simultaneously introducing Cur and Sr during the self-assembly process of Si nanospheres, successfully constructing silicified strontium-curcumin chelate nanospheres (Sr-Cur NPs). Furthermore, in-depth exploration through in vitro cell experiments revealed that Sr-Cur NPs could modulate the immune response, stimulate the migration and proliferation of functional cells, and enhance the formation of new blood vessels. This was achieved through the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway, which exerts anti-inflammatory effects on one hand and scavenges ROS on the other.

Subsequently, sodium alginate (Alg), a commonly used wound dressing matrix with excellent biocompatibility, was selected to load these nanospheres, resulting in the preparation of a composite hydrogel (Sr-Cur@Alg) (Fig. 1). To validate the efficacy of this composite hydrogel, experiments were conducted using a mouse diabetic wound model. The results demonstrated that Sr-Cur@Alg could significantly exert anti-ROS effects and effectively promote the immunomodulation of macrophages, successfully inhibiting the vicious cycle of inflammation. Materials and methods

Preparation and characterization of Sr-Cur NPs

Sr-Cur nanospheres (NPs) were prepared via the sol–gel method. Specifically, 3.00 g of ammonium fluoride (NH4F) and 1.82 g of cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) were dissolved in 0.5 L of deionized water, than stirred for 1 h at 80 °C. Next, 0.1 g strontium nitrate was added to 1 mL absolute ethanol and subjected to ultrasonic treatment until complete dissolution was achieved. The solution was then thoroughly mixed with 9.0 mL of tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS). The thoroughly mixed sample was slowly added dropwise into the previously prepared reaction solution using a 10 mL syringe, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for an additional 4 h. After the reaction was complete, the reaction vessel was removed from the water bath maintained at 80 °C, allowed to cool to ambient temperature, and left to stand overnight. The mixture was then centrifuged for 25 min at 8000 rpm, and the resulting precipitate was sequentially rinsed with absolute ethanol and deionized water. Finally, the washed sample was ultimately freeze-dried and subsequently calcined in a muffle furnace for 6 h at 600 °C to produce Sr NPs. Subsequently, 0.2 g of Sr NPs was added to ethanol (100 mL) containing curcumin (Cur, 2 mg/mL) and stirred intensively for 48 h to facilitate complete chelation between Cur and Sr. The resulting mixture was centrifuged for 5 min at 8000 rpm, followed by three ethanol washes to eliminate excess Cur adsorbed on the nanoparticle surface, and then freeze-dried for 24 h to obtain the final Sr-Cur NPs. Sr-Cur NPs were visualized via scanning electron microscopy (SEM, model S-4800) with a sample concentration of 50 μg/mL. Their ultrastructure was subsequently investigated using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100F). The particle sizes of Sr NPs and Sr-Cur NPs were quantitatively analyzed using Image J software. Meanwhile, the release of Sr ions was assessed using ICP - AES (Thermo Fisher X Series 2), and the amount of Cur released was determined by UV–Vis spectrophotometer. The Cur-loaded Si NPs (Si-Cur NPs) and Sr NPs was used as control groups.

Characterization of the chelation capacity of the released products from Sr-Cur NPs

To evaluate the presence of chelates in Sr-Cur NPs, the Sr-Cur NPs were soaked in 75 % ethanol for 24 h, and their ultraviolet absorption spectra were determined using a UV–visible spectrophotometer. A control solution was prepared using the chelate of Sr ions and Cur. The specific preparation steps for the Sr ion-Cur chelate solution are as follows: First, 0.1 mmol (36.8 mg) of Cur was weighed and dissolved in 1 mL of DMSO, then diluted to a concentration of 0.050 μmol/mL with 75 % ethanol. An SrCl2 solution with a concentration of 0.050 μmol/mL was prepared using 75 % ethanol as the solvent. Subsequently, the Cur solution and Sr ion solution were mixed at a volume ratio of 1:1 to prepare the Sr-Cur chelate solution. Following this, the ultraviolet absorption spectra were measured using a UV–visible spectrophotometer.

Preparation and characterization of Sr-Cur@Alg

The synthesis procedure of the bioactive hydrogel is outlined as follows. Initially, 3 g of sodium alginate (CAS: 9005-38-3; Sigma) was accurately weighed and dissolved in deionized water (100 mL). Then, Sr-Cur NPs were added to 10 mL of a 3 % (w/v) sodium alginate solution and mixed thoroughly. The obtained suspension was immersed in a calcium chloride solution for 10 min to allow cross-linking, resulting in the formation of the bioactive hydrogel (Sr-Cur@Alg). Images of the hydrogel were captured using a digital camera (Canon). For further analysis, the bioactive hydrogel was subjected to freeze-drying for 24 h. The microstructure of the bioactive hydrogel was observed using SEM, and the dispersion of ceramic powder within the hydrogel was examined using energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS).

Cell culture

RAW 264.7, HDF, and HUVEC were all obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from the femurs and tibiae of 8–12 week old C57BL/6J mice [27]. These cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10 % (v/v) FBS.

Cell viability assay

HUVEC (4 × 103 cells) and RAW 264.7 (4 × 103 cells) were seeded into 96-well and incubated overnight. The cells were then treated with varying doses of Sr-Cur NPs and incubated for 24 h. Afterward, CCK-8 (10 μL) was added per well and incubated for another 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was recorded to determine cell viability. All tests were performed in triplicate.

Live/dead staining assay

HUVEC (4 × 104 cells per well) and HDF (2 × 104 cells per well) were seeded into 24-well plates and subsequently incubated with 20 μg/mL of Sr-Cur NPs for 24 and 48 h. After incubation, a live/dead staining kit (Calcein-AM/PI) was used. Each well received 500 μL of staining solution and incubated for 40 min at 37 °C, and then visualized under a fluorescence microscope.

In vitro wound-healing assay

HUVEC (1 × 106 cells per well) cells were seeded into 6-well plates. When the cells reached approximately 80 % confluence, a linear scratch was introduced into the monolayer using a 200 μL pipette tip. Detached cells were carefully removed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), simulating a wound. Cells were then cultured for 24 h in serum-free DMEM containing PBS, Sr NPs, or Sr-Cur NPs. Cell migration was monitored at 0 and 24 h under an optical microscope.

Tube formation assay

Matrigel (50 μL) was added to the 96-well plate of each well and incubated at 37 °C for 45 min to promote gelation. HUVEC (2 × 104 cells/well) cells were plated on the gel and treated with serum-free DMEM containing PBS, Sr NPs, or Sr-Cur NPs. After 6 h of incubation, 500 μL Calcein-AM was added for 40 min to stain live cells. Tube structures were observed under a fluorescence microscope and analyzed with ImageJ.

In vitro antioxidant activity

To evaluate the ROS scavenging ability of Sr-Cur NPs, HUVEC and RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in 24-well plates for 12 h. Oxidative stress was induced by treating cells with 100 μM H2O2 for 24 h, with co-incubation of Sr NPs or Sr-Cur NPs. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with DCFH-DA for 20 min. Fluorescence microscopy was used to observe intracellular ROS, and ImageJ software quantified fluorescence intensity to assess ROS neutralization.

Macrophage polarization

RAW 264.7 (2 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured overnight. The cells were then exposed to 100 ng/mL LPS for 24 h, followed by Sr NPs or Sr-Cur NPs treatment for another 24 h. Total cellular proteins were extracted using a protein extraction kit. Western blot (WB) assays were conducted following established protocols [28]. The antibodies applied included Arg-1 (Proteintech, Cat. No. 16001), iNOS (HUABIO, HA721583), CD206 (HUABIO, HA722892), TNF-α (Wanleibo, WL01581), VEGF (HUABIO, SP07-01), and GAPDH (HUABIO, H661060013).

Indirect Co-culture assay

BMDMs (2 × 106 cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates a were first stimulated with LPS 24 h and subsequently treated with Sr-Cur NPs for another 24 h, while PBS treatment served as the control. The expression of macrophage polarization markers was analyzed by flow cytometry, including CD86 (M1 marker) and CD206 (M2 marker). Immunofluorescence staining was further performed to assess inducible nitric oxide synthase iNOS (M1 marker) and CD206 (M2 marker). In addition, the intracellular ROS clearance capacity of macrophages was quantified by flow cytometry. Following these treatments, the culture supernatants of the two BMDMs groups were collected and indirectly co-cultured with HUVEC (2 × 105 cells/well) for 24 h. Flow cytometry was then employed to evaluate ROS clearance in HUVECs. Subsequently, the supernatants from HUVEC cultures were harvested and indirectly co-cultured with naïve BMDMs (M0 phenotype) for an additional 24 h. Flow cytometry was again performed to determine the expression of CD86 and CD206, as well as the ROS clearance capacity in BMDMs.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

mRNA expression was analyzed by real-time qPCR using primers listed in Table S1. Total RNA was extracted with a standard RNA isolation kit, and RNA purity and concentration were assessed by the 260/280 nm absorbance ratio. cDNA was synthesized from the purified RNA by reverse transcription and used as a template for qPCR.

Diabetic wound healing experiment

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (License No.: 2024AE01087) and followed ethical guidelines for animal care. Male C57BL/6 mice (7 weeks old) from Suzhou SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Male C57BL/6 mice were acclimated for one week, fasted for 12 h, and then intraperitoneally injected with streptozotocin (60 mg/mL) to induce diabetes [[29], [30], [31]]. Mice were acclimatized and subsequently induced with STZ to develop diabetes. Mice with blood glucose above 16.7 mmol/L were considered diabetic and used for subsequent experiments. Mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 12/group): DM, Alg, Sr@Alg, and Sr-Cur@Alg. Hydrogels were fixed to the wound areas of animals by first dropping them and then spraying with CaCl2 solution to promote rapid gelation. The rapid gelation process of the hydrogel was recorded using photos and movies. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (5 %) and subjected to a 10 mm full-thickness skin wound on their dorsal area. The wound area was treated, and images were captured on days 0, 3, 7, 11, and 13. Wound healing was quantified using ImageJ software.

Histological analysis

On days 7 and 13, animals were euthanized, and their wound tissues were collected. The tissues were fixed in paraformaldehyde, dehydrated through ethanol, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and subjected to dewaxing and hydration. Histological analysis was performed using H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining. Vascularization was assessed by CD31 (Abcam, ab281583, 1:1000) and α-SMA (Abcam, ab7817, 1:4000) staining. Immunofluorescence staining for F4/80 (CST, 70076, 1:200), IL-1β (CST, 12242, 1:200), and CD206 (Abcam, ab64693, 1:2000) was used to evaluate inflammation at the wound site.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. The P-values in this study were calculated using common statistical tests available in the Analysis function of GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. The statistical tests employed were Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Significance was set at ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Preparation, characterization, and biocompatibility evaluation of Sr-Cur NPs

In this study, Sr-Cur NPs and Sr NPs were successfully synthesized. From a macroscopic perspective, Sr-Cur NPs appeared as yellow powder, while Sr NPs were white powder, visually indicating the successful conjugation of Cur with Sr NPs (Fig. S1). In-depth observations using SEM and TEM images revealed that the introduction of Cur did not significantly alter the overall morphology of the nanoparticles. Additionally, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis demonstrated the uniform distribution of strontium (Sr), silicon (Si), and oxygen (O) elements in both Sr-Cur NPs and Sr NPs (Fig. 2a). Further quantitative statistical analysis reveals that the average particle size of Sr-NPs is 336.36 ± 43.27 nm, and that of Sr-Cur NPs is 333.17 ± 44.44 nm. This finding clearly indicates that the incorporation of Cur does not affect the particle size of the nanoparticles (Figs. S2 and S3). Regarding ion release characteristics, both Sr-Cur NPs and Sr NPs exhibited the ability to release Sr ions over a 7-day period. Notably, Sr-Cur NPs not only released Sr ions within 7 days but also achieved sustained release of Cur (Fig. 2b and c). Subsequently, we further conducted a comparative analysis of the Cur release capacity between the Cur-loaded Si NPs (Si-Cur NPs) and Sr-Cur NPs. Our findings revealed no significant disparity between the two (Fig. S4). When inorganic metal cations such as Sr ions and flavonoid compounds like Cur coexist in a solution, a unique chelation reaction occurs [[32], [33], [34], [35]]. It can be found that the ultraviolet absorption peak of the released products from Sr-Cur NPs shifted from Cur’s original 425 nm to a longer wavelength. This shift aligns perfectly with the maximum absorption peak of a solution directly prepared with Sr ions and Cur in a 1:1 ratio (Fig. S5). By integrating this observation with prior studies demonstrating that the combination of quercetin with Cu ions, Cur with Fe ions, and Cur with Cu ions all result in a redshift of the ultraviolet absorption peak and the subsequent formation of new chelates [18,36,37], we can conclude that Sr-Cur NPs indeed contain Sr-Cur chelates. This chelate compound possesses higher biological activity, which can significantly enhance cell viability [19,25,26,38].

Based on the above findings, to further evaluate the cytotoxic effects of Sr-Cur NPs, CCK-8 assays and live/dead staining experiments were conducted. First, the toxicity of Sr-Cur NPs on HUVEC was assessed using different concentration gradients. Compared to the control group, Sr-Cur NPs showed no significant toxicity at any tested concentration. Additionally, cell proliferation was highest at 20 μg/mL of Sr-Cur NPs (Fig. 2d). To verify the reliability of the results, the 20 μg/mL concentration was further tested on RAW 264.7 cells, with no apparent toxicity observed (Fig. 2e). Therefore, 20 μg/mL was selected for subsequent experiments. Furthermore, live/dead staining experiments conducted on HUVEC and HDF at 24 and 48 h revealed an extremely low number of dead cells in the field of view (Fig. 2f and g). Subsequently, we compared the effects of Sr NPs, Cur NPs, and Sr-Cur NPs on the viability of HUVEC. The results revealed that the viability of HUVEC treated with Sr-Cur NPs was significantly higher than that of those treated with Sr NPs and Cur NPs alone, indicating that the Sr and Cur in Sr-Cur NPs indeed exert a unique synergistic effect (Fig. S6). In conclusion, these results confirm the excellent biocompatibility of Sr-Cur NPs with the cells. The biocompatible concentration of Sr-Cur NPs also provides an appropriate experimental concentration basis for the subsequent further evaluation of its inhibitory capacity against ROS and its regulatory effect on macrophage polarization.

Sr-Cur NPs Attenuate ROS and modulate macrophage polarization

The sustained accumulation of ROS is among the major contributors to the impaired healing of diabetic wounds. This accumulation triggers excessive oxidative stress, leading to metabolic disorders, cell apoptosis, and even tissue damage [39,40]. Therefore, the elimination of ROS is crucial for the treatment of diabetic wounds. To validate the antioxidant capacity of Sr-Cur NPs, this study employed H2O2 to stimulate HUVEC and Raw264.7 cells, simulating in vitro oxidative stress injury. Intracellular ROS levels were evaluated by DCFH-DA staining. Both the Sr NPs and Sr-Cur NPs groups showed weak green fluorescence compared to the control group (PBS), indicating their antioxidant effects (Fig. 3a–c). Notably, the Sr-Cur NPs group displayed the weakest fluorescence, suggesting its superior ability to scavenge ROS. Subsequently, flow cytometry was utilized to further detect the ROS-scavenging capacity of Sr-Cur NPs, and the results were consistent with the fluorescence quantification results (Fig. S7). An additional key contributor to impaired healing in diabetic wounds is the imbalance in the immune microenvironment, where macrophages serve as central regulators of both tissue damage and regeneration [[41], [42], [43]]. The phenotypic switch of macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 type to the anti-inflammatory M2 type plays an essential role in promoting wound healing [44,45]. To investigate the regulatory effect of Sr-Cur NPs on macrophages, this study analyzed the quantitative results of WB. In comparison to PBS group, both Sr NPs and Sr-Cur NPs groups showed significantly reduced protein expression of M1 pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and iNOS, while the expression of M2 anti-inflammatory cytokines Arg-1 and CD206 was significantly increased [46,47]. These results indicate that both the Sr NPs and Sr-Cur NPs groups promote macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype. Moreover, the Sr-Cur NPs group exhibited the lowest protein expression of the inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and iNOS, and the highest expression of the M2 polarization markers Arg-1 and CD206 (Fig. 3e, h, i and Fig. S8). To further validate its anti-inflammatory effect, qPCR experiments were conducted, and the results were consistent with those of the WB experiments (Fig. S9). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining results revealed that, compared to all other groups, Sr-Cur NPs group resulted in the lowest intensity of green fluorescence for iNOS (M1 macrophage indicator) and the highest red fluorescence intensity for CD206 (M2 macrophage indicator) (Fig. 3d, f and g). Furthermore, this study also used flow cytometry to further verify the effect of Sr-Cur NPs on the M2 phenotype transition of macrophages. Results demonstrated that the Sr-Cur NPs group induced the most pronounced CD206 expression (Fig. 3j and k). The above results indicate that Sr-Cur NPs exert a strong anti-inflammatory capacity and regulate macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype, thereby accelerating the healing of diabetic wounds. Further, we utilized BMDMs to assess the regulatory effects of Sr-Cur NPs on their polarization. The findings indicated that the efficacy of Sr-Cur NPs on BMDMs was consistent with that on Raw264.7 cells, as they both significantly suppressed LPS-induced M1 polarization and promoted M2 polarization (Fig. S10). Additionally, we found that Sr-Cur NPs exert significant regulatory effects on the interaction between BMDMs and HUVEC. Firstly, we observed that the culture medium of BMDMs in the M1 polarization state contains high concentrations of inflammatory cytokines. These cytokines prompt HUVEC to generate a large amount of ROS (Fig. S11). In turn, HUVEC in this state further influence BMDMs, driving them towards M1 polarization (Fig. S12). Interestingly, leveraging its free radical scavenging capability as well as anti-inflammatory regulation, Sr-Cur NPs can significantly disrupt this inflammatory vicious cycle formed between BMDMs and HUVEC, thereby effectively preventing the further exacerbation of the inflammatory storm (Figs. S10, S11, and S12).

Previous studies have linked the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway to excessive ROS accumulation, macrophage immune imbalance, and the subsequent inflammatory response [[48], [49], [50], [51]]. Given the strong anti-inflammatory effects of Sr-Cur NPs observed in this study, we hypothesized that these nanospheres may exert their effects by inhibiting the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway. To test this, WB analysis was conducted, revealing that Sr-Cur NPs significantly reduced the protein levels of TLR4, MyD88, and NF-κB compared to the PBS group (Fig. S13), indicating the inhibition of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway. These findings imply that Sr-Cur NPs may alleviate the inflammatory microenvironment of diabetic wounds through modulation of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway, potentially contributing to the promotion of diabetic wound healing.

Sr-Cur NPs promote functional activation, migration, and tube formation of HUVEC

In the complex process of diabetic wound healing, excessive ROS accumulation and macrophage immune imbalance are closely linked to vascular growth [52,53]. The migratory capacity of cells and their angiogenic potential are indispensable key factors for diabetic wound healing [54]. Notably, Sr-Cur NPs demonstrate significant potential for vascular remodeling. In the in vitro wound healing scratch assay, the migration rate of cells treated with Sr-Cur NPs reached 77.7 %, in stark contrast to the 34.2 % observed in the PBS control group (Fig. 4a and b), highlighting a substantial improvement in cell migration. Furthermore, the tube formation assay revealed that Sr-Cur NPs significantly enhanced the formation of larger tubular structures by HUVECs compared to the control and other groups (Fig. 4d and f), providing strong evidence of their pro-angiogenic effects. To further validate the angiogenic capacity of Sr-Cur NPs, we examined the expression of VEGF, a key angiogenic factor, at both the gene and protein levels. Both WB and qPCR results were highly consistent, demonstrating that Sr-Cur NPs markedly upregulate VEGF expression (Fig. 4c, e and g). Preparation and characterization of Sr-Cur@Alg hydrogel

Given the remarkable functional properties of Sr-Cur NPs in inhibiting the excessive ROS accumulation, regulating the macrophage immune microenvironment, and promoting the proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis of HUVEC, we hypothesize that they have the potential to significantly disrupt the vicious cycle of inflammation in diabetic wounds, thereby accelerating the repair of hard-to-heal wounds. Based on this, to apply Sr-Cur NPs in the treatment of diabetic wounds, we selected Alg as a carrier for loading them, successfully preparing a composite hydrogel. The relevant photographs clearly show that pure Alg exists in a flowing fluid state. However, after loading with Sr-Cur NPs or Sr NPs and cross-linking with calcium chloride [55], non-flowing hydrogels are formed (Fig. 5a). Further observation using SEM images reveals that all hydrogels, after freeze-drying, display a porous structure (Fig. 5b). This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the inherent properties of Alg: its molecular chains exhibit a certain degree of flexibility and entanglement. In solution, the Alg molecular chains entangle with each other, forming a three-dimensional network structure [56]. The distribution of Sr and Si elements confirms that Sr-Cur NPs or Sr NPs are uniformly incorporated within the Alg hydrogel (Fig. 5b).

To further evaluate the antioxidant properties of the composite hydrogels in vitro, the experimental results demonstrate that Sr-Cur@Alg exhibit the highest scavenging ability against ABTS and DPPH (Fig. 5c and d). Delving into the underlying mechanism of their antioxidant properties, we found that this may be closely related to the enzyme-like catalytic activity of Sr-Cur NPs. Consequently, the Sr-Cur@Alg composite hydrogel also displays significant superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like and catalase (CAT)-like catalytic activities, showing the most prominent effects in scavenging hydroxyl radicals (·OH, reflecting SOD-like activity) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, reflecting CAT-like activity) (Fig. 5e and f). It is noteworthy that we discovered that Sr@Alg hydrogel also possesses slight free radical scavenging capacity, as well as SOD and CAT enzyme-mimicking activities. This phenomenon can be explained by the preparation process of Sr NPs, where Sr is incorporated into the -Si-O-Si- framework of MSN to form a-Si-OSr-hybrid framework. This process introduces oxygen vacancies (OV) into the material, leading to the formation of multivalent Sr ions (Sr2+/Sr1+), which in turn confer weak nanozyme catalytic activity to the hydrogel [57,58]. These findings suggest that Sr-Cur NPs may serve as a nanozyme material capable of substituting for biological enzymes in tissues, effectively eliminating ROS and thereby exerting antioxidant effects [[59], [60], [61]].

Next, we observed the therapeutic effects of Sr-Cur@Alg and other hydrogels. On the 13th day of treatment, significant improvements in wound healing was observed in all groups (Fig. 6b). However, no significant difference in the wound healing rate was observed between the PBS group and the Alg group. Among them, the Sr-Cur@Alg group exhibited the best healing outcome, with a healing rate as high as 95.76 %, followed by the Sr@Alg group with a healing rate of 83.68 % (Fig. 6c). It is worth mentioning that we further observed the long-term therapeutic efficacy of Sr-Cur@Alg by extending the wound healing period to 24 days. Quantitative statistical analysis revealed that Sr-Cur@Alg exhibited superior therapeutic effects compared to both Sr@Alg and Cur@Alg (Fig. S17). Moreover, no wound recurrence was observed in any of the groups. This outcome demonstrates that Sr-Cur@Alg exhibits favorable long-term therapeutic efficacy for diabetic wounds. In particular, we focused on evaluating the therapeutic effect of Cur@Alg on diabetic wounds here. It can be observed that the wound area treated with Cur@Alg showed no significant difference compared to the Alg group alone and was smaller than that treated with Sr-Cur@Alg (Fig. S17). The reasons for this phenomenon may be attributed to two aspects. On one hand, it could be due to the water-insolubility of Cur itself, making it difficult for cells in the wound area to absorb. On the other hand, the chelate formed by Sr ions and Cur is likely to possess better biological activity than Cur alone. It is worth mentioning that Sr-Cur@Alg exhibits universality for complex wounds, owing to the remarkable antibacterial ability of Sr-Cur NPs themselves (Fig. S18). Therefore, Sr-Cur@Alg containing Sr-Cur NPs can significantly promote wound healing in mice infected with Staphylococcus aureus and also demonstrates long-term therapeutic efficacy for over 24 days (Fig. S19).

To further comprehensively evaluate the therapeutic effect of Sr-Cur@Alg, we employed histological analysis using H&E staining and Masson’s trichrome staining to assess the thickness of the epidermis and granulation tissue, wound width, and skin morphology. H&E staining images revealed that on the 13th day of treatment, the Sr-Cur@Alg group displayed typical skin layer structure and morphological characteristics, with an epidermal thickness close to that of normal skin (Fig. 6d and e). In addition, compared with other groups, the wound tissue width in the Sr-Cur@Alg group was significantly reduced (Fig. 6f). To evaluate collagen deposition during wound tissue remodeling, Masson,s trichrome stain was applied to visualize collagen deposition within the granulation tissue. Compared to the other treatment groups, the Sr-Cur@Alg group exhibited significantly higher deposition of blue-stained collagen fibers within the dermal tissue, indicating abundant well-structured collagen (Fig. 6g and h). These findings further confirm that Sr-Cur@Alg accelerates wound healing in vivo by promoting tissue proliferation and collagen deposition.

Sr-Cur@Alg hydrogel enhances diabetic wound healing by inhibiting the inflammatory cycle and promoting angiogenesis

To further explore whether the therapeutic effect of Sr-Cur@Alg on diabetic wound healing is associated with the suppression of the inflammatory feedback loop, we performed immunofluorescence staining on the skin tissues after 7 days of treatment. CD206 is a marker of M2 macrophages, which contribute to tissue repair and wound healing. Using F4/80 and CD206 to identify macrophages, we found that M2 macrophage prevalence was highest in the Sr-Cur@Alg group, according to quantitative analysis (Fig. 7a and b). Inflammatory accumulation in wounds is promoted by the expression of key pro-inflammatory factors, such as IL-1β, which disrupt the inflammatory microenvironment and hinder wound healing. Immunofluorescence results on day 7 revealed a decrease in IL-1β fluorescence intensity in the Sr@Alg group compared to the PBS and Alg groups, with the lowest signal observed in the Sr-Cur@Alg group, suggesting a marked reduction in local inflammation and indicating that inflammation at the wound site was effectively alleviated (Fig. 7c and d). Moreover, angiogenesis is essential for efficient tissue regeneration during wound healing. We assessed vascular regeneration through immunofluorescence analysis of CD31 and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA). CD31, a transmembrane protein specific to endothelial cells, is typically expressed at the initial stages of angiogenesis. Meanwhile, α-SMA serves as a marker for vascular smooth muscle cells and is commonly used to assess neovascular formation. On day 13, Sr@Alg and Sr-Cur@Alg treatments significantly promoted vascularization of the wound tissue. Among all groups, the Sr-Cur@Alg group wounds tissue exhibited the greatest number of blood vessels based on quantitative analysis (Fig. 7e and f). This indicates that Sr-Cur@Alg can accelerate diabetic wound healing by promoting angiogenesis.

Additionally, we used WB to investigate the effect of Sr-Cur@Alg on macrophage polarization. The results showed that, following hydrogel treatment, the protein levels of the pro-inflammatory markers inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) decreased, while the protein expression of the anti-inflammatory markers arginase-1 (Arg-1) and CD206 significantly increased (Fig. 7g–k), consistent with the immunofluorescence results. According to the results, Sr-Cur@Alg treatment appears to induce the conversion of M1 macrophages, which generate pro-inflammatory cytokines, into M2 macrophages that secrete both growth factors and anti-inflammatory cytokines. On day 13 of treatment, no inflammatory abnormalities were found in major organs (such as the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney), as confirmed by histopathological analysis in all groups (Fig. S20), indicating that Sr-Cur@Alg has good biocompatibility in vivo. To better facilitate the clinical translation of Sr-Cur@Alg-based wound dressings in the future, we further evaluated the blood compatibility of Sr-Cur NPs, as well as the levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) after treatment with Sr-Cur NPs and Alg hydrogel. The results preliminarily confirm the biosafety of Sr-Cur NPs and Alg (Fig. S21). Nevertheless, further large animal experiments and preclinical trials are required, which will contribute to the future clinical application of this wound dressing capable of inhibiting the vicious cycle of inflammation.

In conclusion, Sr-Cur@Alg can regulate the imbalance of the immune microenvironment caused by macrophages and eliminate the excessive accumulation of ROS, thereby inhibiting the inflammatory vicious cycle. Simultaneously, it can significantly promote diabetic wound healing and tissue regeneration by providing sufficient nutrients to cells in the wound area through promoting angiogenesis.

Conclusion

In diabetic wounds, the overexpression of ROS and the imbalance of the immune microenvironment surrounding immune cells such as macrophages often trigger a vicious cycle of wound inflammation. To address this challenge, this study meticulously designed and developed Sr-Cur NPs nanospheres with dual functions of anti-ROS and immunomodulation. Subsequently, we utilized Alg as a carrier to fabricate these nanospheres into a composite hydrogel (Sr-Cur@Alg). Experimental findings demonstrate that Sr-Cur@Alg can significantly release bioactive Sr-Cur chelates, thereby inhibiting the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway. This process exhibits dual effects: on one hand, it effectively scavenges excessive ROS within the wound, thereby reducing oxidative stress levels; on the other hand, it modulates macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype, thus regulating the immune response. Through these dual mechanisms, Sr-Cur@Alg significantly inhibits the vicious cycle of inflammation, promotes epithelial tissue regeneration, markedly alleviates inflammatory responses, and significantly enhances angiogenesis. Ultimately, this innovative material accelerates the healing of diabetic wounds, offering a novel approach to their treatment.
