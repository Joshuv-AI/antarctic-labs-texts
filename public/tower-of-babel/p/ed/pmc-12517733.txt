Harnessing innate immunity: natural killer cells and innate immune responses in reservoir clearance
Authors: Katharina Schiering, Maya Beiersdorfer, Angelique Hoelzemer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of reviewAs the HIV pandemic persists in the absence of an effective vaccine, this review summarizes promising recent strategies harnessing natural killer cells as part of the innate immune system for HIV cure.Recent findingsCurrent HIV cure approaches aim to enhance natural killer (NK) cell function by reversing inhibition, cytokine-mediated activation and targeting reservoir tissues. Additionally, HIV-specific NK cell therapies include broadly neutralizing antibody-mediated antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, bispecific antibodies and adoptive NK cell transfers. To date, none of the strategies by themselves showed sufficient efficacy, highlighting the importance of integrated approaches combining multiple NK cell-based strategies as well as considering the interplay between the innate and adaptive immune response to HIV-1.SummaryEncouraging findings from in vitro studies, animal models and clinical trials studying innovative NK cell-based therapies demonstrate their potential for cure strategies. Different strategies may need to be combined to create synergy to overcome the major challenge represented by the latent viral reservoir.Keywords: adoptive natural killer cell therapy, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, bispecific antibodies, HIV reservoir, natural killer cells

INTRODUCTION

More than 40 years after its recognition, the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) pandemic remains a public health threat with 1.3 million new infections and 9.3 million people living without HIV treatment in 2023 [1]. Although the introduction of antiretroviral therapy (ART) has increased life expectancy significantly [2] it does not result in a cure. Implications include life-long adherence to ART and drug-related toxicities [3]. Intensive research into HIV prevention has not yet resulted in an approved HIV vaccine [4]. This emphasizes the need for a functional cure which aims to control HIV replication without ART. Latently HIV-1-infected cells harbor replication-competent provirus that can reactivate upon ART cessation, which presents the main obstacle to HIV-1 cure. Davenport et al. outlined two main strategies to induce a functional cure: decreasing the frequency of successful HIV reactivation events to delay the time to rebound, and promoting post-rebound viral control by removing susceptible HIV targets and enhancing host immune control [5]. Natural killer (NK) cells, as innate effector cells, may especially contribute to surveilling reactivating reservoir cells (Fig. 1). Evidence for the importance of NK cells in control of HIV infection comes from studies associating the genetic presence of specific NK cell receptors and certain HLA alleles with delayed progression to AIDS [6,7] and NK cell driven viral evolution [8]. In addition, the HIV vaccine trial RV144, which was the only trial to show moderate success, demonstrated a positive correlation between one of the main NK cell effector functions, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), and protection against HIV infection [9,10]. There are a number of excellent articles reviewing the role of NK cells and innate immunity in HIV infection [11–14] and approaches to developing a cure [15–17]. Therefore, this review will focus on exciting new advancements and promising NK cell strategies targeting the viral reservoir.

Strategies to harness innate immune surveillance of reactivation events. Following analytical treatment interruption (ATI), block-and-lock strategies aim to silence the latent reservoir. Nonetheless, individual reservoir cells may successfully reactivate causing local viral replication. In the case of rapid immune control these events remain spatially confined, opening up a window of opportunity for immune surveillance before reactivation events result in viral rebound detectable in blood. In this model, we summarize approaches to harness NK cells to kill reactivated cells during this window, contributing to HIV-1 reservoir decline over time and prevention of viral rebound. Approaches that enhance NK cell function include cytokine treatments, blocking of inhibitory receptors and targeting to sanctuary sites. In addition, bNAb and bispecific antibody therapies that improve NK cell-mediated ADCC and gene-modified NK cell transfusions represent promising HIV-specific therapies. Lastly, NK cells can control T cell activation, influencing adaptive immune responses and thereby potentially limiting the number of available cells vulnerable to HIV reservoir re-seeding. TIPPING THE SCALE TOWARDS ACTIVATING NK CELL CYTOTOXICITY AGAINST THE HIV-1 RESERVOIR

Chronic untreated HIV infection causes continuous innate immune activation [18]. While the frequency of cytotoxic NK cells decreases, a highly impaired NK cell subset expands in response to persistent viremia [19–21]. NK cell activation and dysfunction remain even under effective ART with undetectable viral load [18,22,23]. Expression of PD-1 on NK cells during chronic HIV-1 infection hints at exhaustion of these cells that persists in ART-treated individuals [24]. To leverage NK cells for killing reservoir cells, efforts are directed towards reversing NK cell inhibition and improving their cytotoxic function. Astorga-Gamaza et al. identified a functionally impaired NK cell population expressing the inhibitory receptor KLRG1 whose expression positively correlated with viral transcription in treated HIV infection [25]. Anti-KLRG1 antibodies enhanced the killing capacity of NK cells and in parallel induced viral reactivation in latently infected CD4+ T cells given that KLRG1 is enriched in cells harboring intact provirus [26]. Another inhibitory receptor, TIGIT, is upregulated on NK cells of people with HIV (PWH) and can suppress NK cell function [27]. Wang et al. recently demonstrated the positive correlation of TIGIT expression on NK cells with reservoir size and uncovered the mechanism for TIGIT-mediated decrease of interferon gamma (IFN-γ) production in NK cells [28▪]. Their results also confirmed previous findings that TIGIT blockade enhanced NK cell-mediated elimination of reactivated latently infected CD4+ T cells [29]. However, a recent study of an anti-TIGIT monoclonal antibody (mAb) in a nonhuman primate model of HIV infection did not show improvement of viral control as the antibody was unable to consistently reverse exhaustion of NK and T cells [30▪]. Data from both nonpathogenic and pathogenic Simian Immunodeficiency Virus (SIV) infections in nonhuman primates revealed a distinct HLA-E–restricted NK cell suppressive activity that depends on the interaction of HLA-E with its inhibitory receptor NKG2A. In nonpathogenic SIV infection, this inhibitory effect is overcome through NK cell differentiation and the emergence of a highly functional NKG2Alow NK cell subset [31]. Ultimately, it may be necessary to block multiple inhibitory receptors to maximize NK cell effector function.

Next to reducing NK cell inhibition, other strategies such as cytokine treatments aim to activate NK cell function. Interleukin (IL)-15-treated NK cells from PWH exhibited enhanced effector functions as well as improved clearing of HIV-infected cells [32,33]. In a humanized mouse model, IL-15 superagonist (N-803) treatment inhibited acute HIV infection in an NK cell-dependent manner [34]. Furthermore, broadly neutralizing antibody (bNAb) therapies might also benefit from integrating cytokines, as IL-15 has been shown to increase NK cell-mediated ADCC activity [35–37]. Additionally, N-803 administration drove NK cell migration to B cell follicles in SHIV-infected, ART-suppressed macaques [38]. A phase I study in PWH suggests N-803 administration to be safe and reported proliferation of NK cells as well as decreased frequencies of inducible provirus-harboring cells for up to 6 months [39]. The comprehensive impact and observed safety profile make IL-15 treatments, specifically N-803, a promising approach that is currently being tested in clinical trials aimed at reducing HIV persistence in lymph nodes [40] or in combination with bNAbs [41,42].

The ability of NK cells to clear reactivated reservoir cells is contingent upon their access to HIV reservoir sites. However, in humans, B cell follicles in lymph nodes represent a sanctuary site with follicular helper T (Tfh) cells being a major source of infectious virion production and active virus transcription, persistent even under long-term ART [43,44]. In contrast, in nonpathogenic SIV infection of African green monkeys, lymph nodes contained only low levels of virus accompanied by increased numbers of CXCR5+ NK cells and depletion of NK cells was linked to elevated viral RNA and DNA levels in lymph nodes [45]. Further supporting evidence comes from a study of chronically infected rhesus macaques; expansion of CXCR5+ NK cells in lymph nodes was associated with reduced plasma viral loads [46]. Therefore, strategies to enhance NK cell cytotoxicity should include directing NK cells toward major reservoir sites.

BROADLY NEUTRALIZING ANTIBODY THERAPIES AND NATURAL KILLER CELLS

The Fc-region of antibodies can directly activate NK cell cytotoxicity against antibody-labelled target cells by triggering the FcγRIIIa-receptor CD16 on NK cells, even in the presence of inhibitory NK cell signals. The RV144 HIV-1 vaccine trial demonstrated a role for ADCC in controlling HIV-1, showing that the vaccine induced potent ADCC responses that correlated with protection against infection in recipients with low levels of anti-Env immunoglobulin A (IgA) antibodies [10,47–49]. Additionally, vaccine-induced ADCC correlated with significantly lower viral loads during acute infection of rhesus macaques [50]. In humans, naturally occurring NK cell-mediated ADCC activity reduced the viral set point during primary HIV-1 infection [51]. Likewise, broader ADCC responses are associated with slowing progression of HIV infection [52] and elite controllers have higher levels of Env-specific ADCC than viremic individuals [53,54]. Another clinical correlate of ADCC function are higher peripheral blood CD4+ T cell counts [55,56]. Together, this evidence highlights the importance of ADCC in anti-HIV immunity. Accordingly, ADCC is explored as part of several HIV treatment approaches, such as bNAb therapies. Fc-mediated effector functions of anti-HIV antibodies significantly increased protection against HIV challenge as well as therapeutic activity in vivo [57,58]. bNAb therapy appears to reduce the intact proviral reservoir in PWH [59], and although its mechanism is not clear, improved ability of bNAbs to recruit innate immune effectors may enhance reservoir clearance. In humanized mice, bNAbs hindered the establishment of the HIV-1 reservoir via Fc-FcR-mediated mechanisms and, in combination with inducers of viral transcription, reduced viral rebound [60]. Importantly, Fc-mediated effector functions include both ADCC and antibody-mediated cellular phagocytosis (ADCP). Nonetheless a study focusing exclusively on ADCC activity of bNAbs reported NK cell-mediated killing of reactivated reservoir cells [61].

Improving Fc-dependent functions of bNAbs is therefore of high interest. A recent study showed that combining afucosylation with protein engineering led to improved NK cell activation and killing [62]. Improved NK cell-mediated ADCC for most afucosylated bNAbs has been reported even in the HIV-related context of partially exhausted NK cells and low antigen density [63] as well as against both T cells and macrophages [64]. However, bNAbs with potent ADCC failed to improve protection against SHIV/SIV challenges in vivo [65,66]. Additional macaque studies using the second-generation bNAb PGT121 reported Fc-mediated functions to be dispensable for protection and viremia reduction [67,68]. Nevertheless, the significance of Fc functions may differ depending on the specific bNAb, as both wild type and afucosylated PGT121 demonstrated weaker ADCC activity in vitro than other antibodies [63,69]. Another approach to improve ADCC activity was employed by Marchitto et al.; adding a small CD4-mimetic to a cocktail of CD4-induced antibodies resulted in potent ADCC activity against CD4+ T cells as well as macrophages [70].

Additionally, several strategies activating NK cells and bringing them close to target cells have been developed to improve NK cell responses. “Bispecific Killer cell Engagers” (BiKEs) are immunomodulators consisting of a scFv directed against a target cell antigen (such as the HIV-1 envelope) linked to a domain that binds CD16 to induce ADCC [71,72]. Recently, promising results of two single-chain diabodies (scDbs), incorporating the scFv of a bNAb and a CD16 antibody, were reported by Board et al. [73▪▪]. In vitro testing confirmed increased NK cell-mediated killing of HIV-1-infected cells in the presence of the scDbs compared to the original unmodified bNAbs. Combined with potent latency reversal agents (LRA) the scDbs were able to reduce the frequency of intact provirus harboring CD4+ T cells of people on long-term ART and in a humanized mouse model. This study highlights the potential of bispecific antibodies to reduce the latent reservoir in combination with LRAs while under ART. Another recent study by Sánchez-Gaona et al. of a bispecific antibody targeting Env and CD16, Bi-Ab32/16, revealed conflicting results [74▪▪]. Bi-Ab32/16 was able to increase ADCC activity in NK cells from PWH in vitro and, for some PWH, reduced intact proviruses after ex vivo reactivation of latently infected CD4+ T cells. However, Bi-Ab32/16 treatment of humanized mice did not delay the time to viral rebound after ART discontinuation, possibly due to the observed reduced percentages of NK cells. The use of a bNAb in the study by Board et al. compared to a nonneutralizing antibody in the study by Sánchez-Gaona et al. might have contributed to the in vivo performance discrepancy, suggesting a combination of both neutralizing and ADCC functions might be necessary to control early reactivation events.

ADOPTIVE NATURAL KILLER CELL THERAPY FOR HIV-1 CURE

Adoptive NK cell therapies have shown great success in the field of cancer treatment (reviewed in [75]) and have since been explored for HIV cure. Administration of allogeneic human NK cells to HIV-1-infected humanized mice delayed viral rebound after ART interruption [76]. Recently, Miller et al. conducted a phase 1 study of single haploidentical NK cell infusions in six PWH, accompanied by either IL-2 or N-803; the treatment was generally well tolerated [77▪▪]. Donor NK cells persisted in the blood for a week and in lymph node and gut tissues in one participant for 28 days. The persistence of transfused NK cells was especially notable as the recipients received no lymphodepleting pretreatments. The study focused in particular on HIV reservoirs in sanctuary tissues. Lymphatic and gut tissue samples taken >1 week after the last cytokine dose showed no increase in NK cell numbers; however, the frequency of cells positive for viral RNA consistently declined in all four analyzed lymph node samples. Although follow-up studies are needed, Miller et al. have set a precedent for adoptive NK cell immunotherapies in HIV cure.

Other adoptive NK cell approaches employ modifications to enhance anti-HIV activity. Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-engineered NK cells demonstrated cytolytic activity in vitro but so far have not mediated improved HIV suppression in vivo [78,79]. Nevertheless, engineering strategies are evolving. Lim et al. have developed a universal CAR NK cell that is able to target multiple HIV epitopes by using a range of HIV-specific 2,4-dinitrophenyl (DNP)-conjugated antibodies that are then recognized by the anti-DNP CAR NK cells [80].

A new strategy presented by Tomescu et al. links adoptive immunotherapy with ADCC [81▪]. Gene-modified NK cells expressing the high affinity Fc-receptor CD64, preloaded with bNAbs were able to retain bNAbs for at least a week. These engineered NK cells displayed superior HIV-specific ADCC activity compared to control NK cells. The authors further suggest the possibility of combining their approach with strategies to direct NK cells specifically to HIV reservoir sites. Although in vivo efficacy still needs to be demonstrated, Tomescu et al. offer intriguing novel concepts to achieve synergy by integrating multiple NK cell-based strategies to target the reservoir.

HOW DO NATURAL KILLER CELLS SHAPE THE ADAPTIVE IMMUNE RESPONSE TO HIV-1? BENEFICIAL OR DETRIMENTAL?

Notably, in PWH, those who develop bNAb activity demonstrated a higher degree of NK cell dysfunction compared to those without bNAbs [82]. The authors suggested that impaired NK cell function allows augmented expansion of Tfh cells, thereby promoting bNAb development. In contrast, in nonpathogenic SIV infection, NK cell accumulation in mesenteric lymph nodes and subsequent viral control protected IgA+ memory B cells and preserved IgA production [83]. Additional research is needed to investigate NK cell mediated regulation of Tfh cells and the balance between eliminating HIV-infected cells without compromising antibody responses. NK cells also modulate adaptive antiviral T cell immunity by controlling T cell inflammation, as first demonstrated in a murine LCMV infection model [84]. The results highlight that by killing activated CD4+ T cells, NK cells indirectly influence CD8+ T cell function, either preventing or facilitating fatal pathology (depending on the infectious dose). This regulatory role is an emerging area of interest in HIV-1 research. According to a recent preprint, an expansion of adaptive CD57+FcεRγ-NKG2C+ NK cells early in infection was associated with optimal CD8+ T cell activation and long-term viral control [85]. In another recent preprint, we report that NK cells killed bystander CD4+ T cells activated in the context of HIV-1 infection by targeting HLA-F – the ligand for the activating KIR3DS1 receptor on NK cells [86]. In the IciStem cohort (a cohort studied for HIV-1 cure following hematopoietic stem cell transplant), T cell inflammation posttransplant was associated with vulnerability to HIV reservoir re-seeding [87]. Thus, NK cell regulation of T cell responses may play a role in post-rebound viral control, when NK cells reduce the number of activated CD4+ T cells available for reinfection. Taken together, these findings suggest that NK cells shape the response to HIV infection in multifaceted ways.

CONCLUSION

Controlling the viral reservoir constitutes a major challenge on the path toward HIV cure. Mathematical modelling by Davenport et al. predicts that for many therapies over 99.9% efficacy would be necessary for life-long remission if used individually [5]. As current therapies are still far from reaching the needed efficacy, a combination of synergistic strategies will be most promising. The encouraging outcome of haploidentical NK cell infusions positions this approach as a solid starting point with a wide range of possible modifications, such as introducing CARs, gene modifications to preload NK cells with bNAbs, blocking NK cell inhibitory receptors and targeting NK cells toward tissue sanctuaries. Additionally, given the inherent antiviral potential of NK cells, bNAb therapies could benefit from strategies engaging NK cells, such as Fc-engineering or bispecific antibodies. Open research questions remain regarding the potential of HIV-1 peptide-driven expansion of adaptive NK cells for vaccine studies [88,89]. A better understanding of the differentiation and function of adaptive NK cells in the context of HIV-1 infection will be essential to harness them for cure strategies [31,90–93]. Importantly, NK cell-based cure strategies should also be analyzed from an integrated perspective, accounting for interactions with the adaptive immune system for a concentrated effort to target the viral reservoir.
