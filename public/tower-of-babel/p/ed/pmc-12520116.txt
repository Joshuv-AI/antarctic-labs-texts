Improving dual targeting selectivity in T-cell engagers via synapse-gated and affinity-tuned trispecific antibody design
Authors: Peng Zhao, John Schardt, Chi-I Chiang, Pooja Shah, Gee Sung Eun, Jan Martinek, Matthew Cyr, Yoshimi Johnson, Bismark Amofah, Xiaoying Ye, Samuel Edwards, Xiaoru Chen, Mark Penney, Wenhai Liu, Chunning Yang, Keith Rickert, Amber Lee, Sterling Payne, Hanzhi Zhang, Garrett Kelly, Chunlei Wang, Allison Gerber, Kathy Mulgrew, Rajat Varma, Jonathan Boyd, Xiuling Li, John D Bagert, Even Walseng, Yariv Mazor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTT-cell engagers (TCEs) represent a powerful drug modality for redirecting a patient’s own T cells to recognize and eradicate cancer cells. Although TCEs have been effective in treating hematological cancers, their broad application for solid tumors has been more challenging due to the absence of tumor-specific antigens. This often leads to on-target, off-tumor toxicities and a low therapeutic index (TI). Strategies for dual-antigen targeting of double-positive cancer cells over single-positive normal tissue may improve the TI of TCEs. In this study, we report the development and characterization of a conditional dual tumor-associated antigen (TAA)-targeting trispecific antibody (TriMab) TCE composed of a non-active anchoring arm (i.e. anti-TAA1), deficient in mediating an active immunological synapse, and an affinity-tuned active arm (i.e. anti-TAA2), paired with an anti-CD3 domain to drive AND-gated targeting and elimination of dual-TAA tumors while sparing single-TAA healthy cells. Using an anti-receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 1 (ROR1) mAb as a proof-of-concept anchoring arm and an array of affinity-modulated variants of the anti-epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) GA201 mAb as active arms, we show in vitro conditional engagement and elimination of double-positive human NCI-H358 non-small cell lung cancer cells over single-positive, non-target NCI-H358.ROR1.KO cells by affinity-modulated TriMab TCEs. In vivo, the TriMab TCE exhibits selective targeting and eradication of ROR1/EGFR double-positive tumors in a mouse xenograft model. We further demonstrate the generality of the anchoring arm in TriMab using anti-HER2 mAbs targeting different binding epitopes and discuss the interplay of factors regulating immunological synapse formation. Lastly, we demonstrate that the TriMab modality exhibits a favorable developability profile and mAb-like pharmacokinetic properties in human neonatal Fc receptor transgenic mice. Overall, this work presents a generalizable approach to utilizing the TriMab modality by leveraging avidity effects and molecular geometry to achieve conditional AND-gated dual TAA-targeting with a significantly improved TI.KEYWORDS: Dual-targeting, therapeutic index, TriMab, trispecific

Introduction

T-cell engagers (TCEs) represent a prominent immunotherapeutic modality, as demonstrated by the approval of several FDA-approved drugs for both hematologic and solid tumor indications.1–3 Notwithstanding these successes, the limited availability of tumor-specific extracellular antigens has significantly hindered the extensive application of TCEs in the treatment of solid cancers.4 This constraint often results in on-target, off-tumor toxicity, thereby compromising the therapeutic index (TI).5 Effective strategies to selectively target and eliminate tumor cells while sparing healthy cells remain a critical challenge, which has spurred intense interest in the engineering of next-generation TCEs with enhanced tumor selectivity.6–9 A promising approach to surmount the challenge of TCE specificity is to target more than one tumor-associated antigen (TAA) on the tumor cell.4,10 However, the endeavor of dual TAA targeting is formidable, as each target binder entails its own set of on-target, off-tumor liabilities.11

A variety of strategies for developing dual TAA-targeted TCEs with AND-gated conditionality, which require both targets to be expressed on tumor cells to activate TCE function, have been reported. One such AND-gated approach is the ‘split TCE,’ which uses a two-molecule system where CD3 activity is prevented by separating the anti-CD3 VH and VL domains and fusing each to different TAA binding domains (e.g., TAA1-αCD3VH and TAA2-αCD3VL). The split TCE only induces cytotoxic activity when both molecular components concurrently bind the same target cell, resulting in the association and functional reconstitution of the anti-CD3 VH and VL domains.8,12 Although the split TCE approach has demonstrated a notable reduction in cytokine release in vitro compared to conventional TCE control,12 the translational potential of split TCEs remains to be established. This strategy presents considerable challenges, including the risk of reduced bioactivity due to insufficient anti-CD3 reconstitution. Additionally, predicting this mechanism of action in vivo is complex, and there are further manufacturing and regulatory hurdles to consider with the development of what is essentially a combination therapy.

As an attractive alternative, dual TAA-targeted trispecific TCEs represent an emerging AND-gated strategy to enhance selectivity and limit on-target, off-tumor toxicity within a single molecule drug modality. To date, several dual TAA-targeted trispecific TCEs have leveraged the principle of cross-arm avidity to enhance target selectivity.2,4,10,13–15 This principle favors antibody engagement with double-positive tumor cells over single-positive healthy cells, such that a weak monovalent binding domain (e.g., anti-TAA1) necessitates the co-engagement of another binding partner (e.g., anti-TAA2) to enhance apparent affinity through avidity effects, thereby triggering potent bioactivity.2,4,10,13–15 For example, Tapia-Galisteo et al. have reported the development of a single-chain trispecific TCE molecule designed to co-engage the TAA targets EGFR and EpCAM, which demonstrated up to 100-fold selectivity for antigen double-positive target cells over single-positive cells.16 In another study, Dicara et al. recently demonstrated the influence of molecular geometry and anti-CD3 affinity on the capacity of a dual TAA-targeted trispecific TCE antibody to preferentially eliminate Ly6E/B7-H4 double-positive target cells.13 These foundational studies underscore the significant potential of dual TAA-targeted trispecific TCEs in selectively targeting tumor cells over normal tissues. However, it remains possible that current strategies may not completely mitigate on-target, off-tumor toxicity. This is particularly concerning given the high potency of TCE modalities, which heightens the risk of substantial damage to single-positive normal tissues. Therefore, further advancements are warranted to establish straightforward and reliable strategies for generating dual TAA-targeted TCEs with a widened TI for treating solid tumors. We assert that by incorporating an element of conditionality and harnessing cross-arm avidity, we can markedly enhance target selectivity. This strategy aims to prevent TCE activity when interacting with single TAA-expressing cells and ensure that killing occurs only upon dual TAA binding. To realize this objective, careful molecular engineering considerations are required to safely and effectively integrate conditionality into a dual TAA-targeted TCE modality.

In this study, we report the development of a single-molecule, conditional AND-gated dual TAA-targeted trispecific TCE (TriMab) designed for selective elimination of double-positive cancer cells while sparing single-positive healthy cells. The conditional TriMab features: (1) a high-affinity TAA1-targeted ‘anchoring arm’ that cannot facilitate synapse formation between TAA1-positive cells and effector T cells, (2) an affinity-attenuated TAA2-targeted ‘active arm’ that promotes efficient synapse formation exclusively in the presence of TAA1 and TAA2 double-positive target cells and (3) a CD3 recruiting moiety. We sought to establish a generalizable blueprint for the reliable generation of conditional AND-gated TriMab through affinity tuning and synapse gating. Further, we examined the influence of molecular parameters, including epitope and affinity, on the selectivity of TriMab at the platform level.

As proof-of-concept, we selected receptor tyrosine kinase like orphan receptor 1 (ROR1) as the anchoring arm target and epidermal growth-factor receptor (EGFR) as the active arm target and demonstrated enhanced selectivity for an optimized ROR1/EGFR TriMab in vitro, including the selective elimination of double-positive NCI-H358 target cells over NCI-H358.ROR1.KO. We further corroborated the enhanced selectivity of TriMab in vivo using an NCI-H358 tumor xenograft model. Mechanistically, we show that TriMab requires concurrent binding of ROR1 and EGFR to mediate functional immunological synapse (IS) formation, as assessed by confocal imaging. The TriMab exhibited monoclonal antibody-like developability and pharmacokinetics (PK), suggesting the suitability of this modality for further development. To establish the generality of the TriMab approach, we used anti-HER2 as an anchoring arm and observed a similar significant improvement in selectivity for the optimized HER2/EGFR TriMab. Overall, the conditional TriMab platform shows promise for broad application in addressing the substantial unmet need for effective solid tumor treatments, which have largely been limited by on-target, off-tumor toxicity.

Results

Design and production of ROR1/EGFR dual-targeting TriMab TCE with orthogonal Fab pairing

The development of dual TAA-targeted TCEs requires the engineering of trispecific molecules that can simultaneously engage TAA1, TAA2, and CD3. Toward this goal, we leveraged and expanded upon our clinically validated bispecific antibody approach, the DuetMab platform, integrating the engineering features outlined in Bagert et al. 17–19 Briefly, heterodimerization of two distinct heavy chains (HC) is achieved by knob-into-hole (KiH) mutations at the constant heavy 3 (CH3) domains.20 Cognate heavy and light chain (LC) pairing is achieved via an engineered interchain disulfide bond in one of the constant heavy 1 (CH1)-constant light chain (CL) domains combined with electrostatic steering mutations at the interface of both CH1-CL domains.19,21

Building upon the DuetMab scaffold, the TriMab modality consists of three unique antigen-binding fragment (Fab) regions (Figure 1a). The Fab region linked to the hole crystallizable fragment (Fc), referred to as (Fab 1), carries a wild-type interchain disulfide bond and electrostatic steering mutations (S183K in CH1 and V133E, EU convention) in kappa constant light chain domain (Cκ), to promote correct LC-HC pairing. For the Fabs connected to the knob Fc, the N-terminal Fab (Fab 2) features an engineered interchain disulfide bond (F126C, C220V in CH1 and S121C, C214V in Cκ) and electrostatic steering mutations (S183E in CH1 and V133K in Cκ) at the CH1-Cκ interface. Meanwhile, the inner Fab (Fab 3) possesses an engineered interchain disulfide bond (F126C, C220V in CH1 and S122C, C212V in lambda constant light chain domain (Cλ)) and electrostatic steering mutations (A141D in CH1 and T117R in Cλ) at the CH1-Cλ interface. In this study, the KiH Fc of TriMab was further engineered to carry the triple mutations (L234F, L235E, and P331S) designed to reduce Fc – mediated immune effector functions.22

To apply AND-gated conditionality to the TriMab, we incorporated three fundamental molecular design features: (1) a high affinity anchoring arm that binds tightly to single-positive cells but lacks cytotoxicity due to epitope and/or geometry constraints, thereby preventing the formation of an active immunological synapse between TAA1-positive cells and effector T cells; (2) an affinity-attenuated TAA2-targeted ‘active arm’ capable of forming an active immunological synapse only upon simultaneous engagement with the anchoring arm; and (3) an anti-CD3 component for T cell recruitment. For optimal synapse formation and appropriate spacing from the anchoring arm, the anti-CD3 moiety was positioned proximally under the active arm (Figure 1a).

As proof-of-concept, we selected ROR1 and EGFR as the target pair, given the significant interplay of these two receptors in various solid tumor settings.23,24 For the constructions of ROR1/EGFR TriMab, we utilized the anti-ROR1 (clone ERR1-TOP43)25 as the anchoring arm, the anti-EGFR (clone GA201)26 as the active arm, and the anti-CD3 (clone 20G6)27 as the T cell targeting moiety. The anti-ROR1 clone was selected from a panel of anti-ROR1 clones as the anchoring arm due to its high affinity (9.5 nM) and its inability to mediate T cell-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (TDCC) on ROR1 single-positive cells when tested using a conventional 1 + 1 T cell engager DuetMab (TED)28 format (Fig. S1). The deficiency of ERR1-TOP43 in forming an active immunological synapse between ROR1-positive cells and effector T cells is a fundamental feature for its role as an anchoring arm.

To evaluate the influence of the intrinsic binding affinity of the active arm on enhancing target cell selectivity, we used a panel of anti-EGFR affinity variants, where v1 corresponds to the wild-type GA201 sequence with a binding affinity of 0.7 nM. The affinity-attenuated variants, v2 and v3, exhibit approximately 8-fold (8 nM) and 30-fold (32 nM) reductions in EGFR-binding affinity, respectively.29 Within the TriMab format, the anti-CD3 T cell recruiting moiety exhibited an affinity of 47.5 nM. The binding affinities of the individual arms are summarized in Table S1. Control TriMab molecules were constructed by replacing either the anchoring arm or the active arm with an isotype control Fab domain (clone R347).30 A total of three control/active arm TriMab (R347/EGFR[v1-v3]) molecules were generated, in addition to one anchoring/control TriMab (ROR1/R347), and three dual TAA-targeted TriMab variants (ROR1/EGFR[v1-v3]) as shown in Figure 1b.30

For transient production of TriMab constructs, all five antibody chains were co-transfected into a Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) expression host. This process yielded TriMab molecules with robust and consistent titers exceeding 200 mg/L, which correlated well with the expression levels of the parental IgGs (Table 1). The molecules were purified using MabSelect SuRe columns, eluted at acidic pH, and analyzed for monomer content and light chaining pairing. All molecules exhibited monomer content greater than 95% as determined by size exclusion chromatography (SEC) as shown in Table 1. The LC ratios after dithiothreitol (DTT) reduction were analyzed via bioanalyzer and indicated that all ROR1/EGFR TriMab and corresponding control molecules displayed kappa: lambda LC ratios close to 2, consistent with the expected value (Figure 1c and Table 1). Secondary affinity purification or preparative SEC purifications were not necessary to achieve the desired purity. Stable CHO clones expressing TriMab constructs have demonstrated volumetric productivities ranging from 2–3 g/L (data not shown). This productivity is comparable to standard IgG processes, indicating that TriMab titers of multiple grams per liter are achievable in stable CHO lines. Overall, these data indicate the high efficiency of TriMab assembly and suitable manufacturability.

Advanced biophysical characterization of TriMab demonstrates robust chain pairing and stability

To rigorously assess the integrity of the TriMab molecules, we used liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analyses. First, LC-MS of the intact, non-reduced TriMab after purification were consistent with the expected molecular mass of the TriMab molecules (Figure 2a and Table S2). Further, middle-level LC-MS analysis after IgdE digestion was used to interrogate the individual Fab arms of TriMab, which confirmed chain pairing at high resolution (Figure 2b and Table S2). LC-MS analysis of deglycosylated TriMab upon DTT reduction confirmed the mass of individual chains (Table S3). Finally, the molecule integrity was further corroborated via in-depth analysis of GlySERIAS- and/or IgdE-digested TriMab fragments after separation by reversed-phase ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-UHPLC) (Fig. S2 and Table S4).

The thermal stability of TriMab molecules was determined by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) (Figure 2c) All test articles exhibited manufacturable thermal stability properties, with onset temperatures (Tonset) and melting temperatures (Tm) exceeding 50°C and 61°C, respectively, which are significantly higher than physiological and accelerated storage assessment temperatures.31

Peptide mapping confirmed the formation of all predicted inter-chain disulfide bonds, with no evidence of disulfide scrambling between the heavy and light chains of ROR1/EGFR[v3] TriMab (Figure 2d).

TriMab demonstrates mAb-like developability

To further interrogate the developability of TriMab, we evaluated the molecules for (1) accelerated stability under stress conditions, (2) self-association, and (3) non-specific binding. For accelerated stability tests, we assessed the aggregation and fragmentation propensities of the molecules using SEC, both before and after exposure to heat stress (40°C for 14 days) and photo stress (chromatic white light for 7 days). We observed no significant increase in aggregation or fragmentation for any of the TriMab molecules. Regarding antibody self-association propensity, all TriMab molecules exhibited low or no self-association, as measured by affinity-capture self-interaction nanoparticle spectroscopy (AC-SINS) (Table S5).32 In terms of nonspecific binding, the ROR1/EGFR TriMab and control molecules exhibited very low binding to human embryonic kidney (HEK) cells (Table S5), indicating that the TriMab format did not introduce substantial hydrophobic or positively charged regions that could adversely affect its pharmacokinetic (PK) profiles. Collectively, these data underscore the favorable developability profile of the TriMab modality.

TriMab demonstrates concurrent binding to ROR1, EGFR, and CD3

To assess the ability of ROR1/EGFR TriMab to simultaneously bind to ROR1, EGFR, and CD3, we conducted a sandwich biolayer interferometry (BLI). Briefly, biotinylated recombinant ROR1 extracellular domain (ECD) was first captured on streptavidin sensors, followed by sequential loading of TriMab, recombinant EGFR ECD, and recombinant CD3εδ. As shown in Figure 3a, all ROR1/EGFR TriMab molecules demonstrated concurrent binding to ROR1, EGFR, and CD3 antigens, indicating the potential suitability of TriMab for TCE applications.

To evaluate the capacity of TriMab to concurrently engage ROR1 and EGFR in a physiological context, and to examine the influence of anti-EGFR affinity on TriMab’s cross-arm avidity, we conducted on-cell concurrent binding analysis to ROR1/EGFR double-positive PA1 ovarian cancer target cells using flow cytometry. In terms of assay design, TriMab, at varying concentrations were first incubated with target cells. Subsequently, unbound EGFR on the cell surface was measured using fluorescently labeled, low-concentration anti-EGFR IgG (GA201), such that TriMab engagement with EGFR is inversely proportional to the fluorescent signal. As shown in (Figure 3b), the negative ROR1/R347 control TriMab did not exhibit a concentration-dependent decrease in EGFR IgG binding signal, confirming the assay’s specificity. We then quantified the differences in half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50), by comparing each ROR1/EGFR TriMab variant with its corresponding R347/EGFR control TriMab (Table S6). To facilitate comparative analysis of the data, we introduced a “fold-change parameter” (FCP) to measure target selectivity. An FCP value greater than 1 indicates selective TriMab binding to dual TAA-expressing cells. This metric is determined by dividing the IC50 value obtained for the R347/EGFR TriMab by the IC50 of the ROR1/EGFR TriMab. Notably, TriMab variants with reduced EGFR active-arm affinity demonstrated greater FCP.

EGFR affinity-attenuated TriMabs exhibit selective in vitro cytolysis toward double-positive tumor cells and favorable cytokine release profile

To determine whether cross-arm avidity binding enhances target cell selectivity, we aimed to assess the ability of TriMab to preferentially eliminate double-positive NCI-H358 cells over NCI-H358.ROR1 knock-out (KO) cells. To that end, we generated an isogenic variant of the NCI-H358 cell line by using CRISPR/Cas technology to knock out ROR1, resulting in the NCI-H358.ROR1.KO cell line. The isolated clone was selected based on lack of ROR1 staining confirmed by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis. Further characterization revealed that the NCI-H358.ROR1.KO cell line exhibited a comparable growth rate and EGFR receptor density to the parental cell line (Fig. S3 and Table 2). Additionally, the NCI-H358.ROR1.KO cell line retained sensitivity to EGFR-mediated TDCC by R347/EGFR[v1] control TriMab and exhibited similar T cell activation compared to parental NCI-H358 with a half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) of 1.8 nM for NCI-H358 and 0.8 nM for NCI-H358.ROR1.KO cells.

To gain a deeper understanding of the potential TI of TriMab, we analyzed T cell activation and cytokine release stemming from the TDCC assay. Consistent with the cytotoxicity findings, the ROR1/EGFR[v3] TriMab exhibited the most significant differential in selective T cell activation between NCI-H358 and NCI-H358.ROR1.KO cells (Figure 4a, lower panel). Remarkably, the ROR1/EGFR[v3] TriMab induced approximately 70% T cell activation in the presence of NCI-H358, while only baseline activation (2–6%) was observed at 6.25 nM in NCI-H358.ROR1.KO cells, as indicated by CD69/CD25 double-positive pan-T cell populations at the end of the 3-day TDCC assay. In terms of cytokine release, we measured the concentrations of the proinflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, IFN-γ, and IL-2 in cellular supernatants by ELISA. As shown in Figure 4b, attenuation of the TriMab active arm significantly widened the TI. Specifically, For ROR1/EGFR[v1] TriMab, when cytotoxicity reached approximately 90% of maximum TDCC, the cytokine release of TNF-α and IL-6 was nearly 500 pg/mL. Conversely, for ROR1/EGFR[v3] TriMab, cytotoxicity reached approximately 90% of maximum TDCC with only baseline levels of TNF-α and IL-6. Similar effects were observed for IFN-γ and IL-2 (Fig. S6). Overall, these findings demonstrate the ability of TriMab with affinity-tuned active arm to induce robust potency coupled with low cytokine release, thereby significantly enhancing the TI.

ROR1/EGFR[v3] TriMab exhibits improved targeting selectivity across multiple cancer cell lines

To corroborate the enhanced TI of ROR1/EGFR[v3] TriMab across a range of cancer cell lines, we conducted TDCC assays on a diverse panel of tumor cells featuring varying expression levels of ROR1 and EGFR antigens (Table 2). To assess target selectivity and calculate the FCP values, we used the corresponding control TriMab molecules with either anchoring-arm or active-arm only to simulate the engagement of ROR1/EGFR[v3] TriMab with single-positive cells, expressing ROR1 or EGFR antigens. The control TriMab molecules serve as a useful tool, particularly when KO cell lines are unavailable (Fig. S7). Accordingly, the FCP was calculated by dividing the EC50 value obtained for ROR1/EGFR[v3] TriMab by the EC50 value obtained for R347/EGFR[v3] TriMab. As shown in Figure 5 and Table S7, ROR1/EGFR[v3] TriMab demonstrated a significant degree of target cell selectivity, with FCP values ranging between 800–1000 across various tumor cells (Figure 5, corresponding EC50 values are listed in Table S7). Similar to the observations with NCI-H358 cells, in the absence of effector cells, the ROR1/EGFR[v3] TriMab exhibited minimal cytotoxic effects on the additional tumor cell lines tested (Fig. S5b). These results collectively highlight (1) the suitability of leveraging control TriMab molecules as an alternative strategy for selectivity studies as the data align well with our analyses using knockout cells, and (2) the limited impact that receptor density ratios have on the target cell selectivity of TriMab. Overall, these findings underscore the robustness of the TriMab approach in eliciting highly selective TCE activity across a spectrum of ROR1/EGFR double-positive cancer cells.

ROR1/EGFR[v3] TriMab does not exhibit sink effects driven by the high affinity ROR1 anchoring arm

To determine whether high-affinity engagement of the TriMab anchoring arm with single-positive normal cells could lead to target-mediated drug disposition (TMDD) due to a sink effect, we examined the cytotoxic potency of ROR1/EGFR[v3] against NCI-H358 double-positive cells, both in the presence and absence of CA46 ROR1 single-positive cells. As shown in Figure 6 and Table S8, ROR1/EGFR[v3] TriMab exhibited consistent bioactivity, with similar EC50 values across various cell ratios ranging from 1:1 to 20:1. These results were in line with the control group, which only included double-positive target cells at a 0:1 ratio. Additionally, we demonstrated that the engagement of ROR1/EGFR[v3] TriMab and its corresponding ROR1/R347 and R347/EGFR[v3] control TriMabs with NCI-H358 cells resulted in minimal TriMab internalization, as determined by IncuCyte (Fig. S8). In conclusion, these findings mitigate concerns regarding potential sink effects induced by the high-affinity engagement of the ROR1 anchoring arm.

To examine the conditions under which TriMab could induce functional immunological synapse (IS) formation via EGFR:CD3, we used an optical microscopy assay. TriMab utilizing HER2 as an anchoring arm demonstrates the generality of the approach

To further validate the generality of the TriMab approach beyond ROR1/EGFR, we chose to evaluate HER2 as an additional anchoring arm. Previously, we leveraged a dual TAA targeting HER/EGFR DuetMab, in which the anti-HER2 clone trastuzumab exhibited excellent anchoring-arm activity, effectively inhibiting HER2-dependent EGFR signaling both in vitro and in vivo.29 Given the requirement for the anchoring arm to possess limited TAA-guided TCE activity on its own, we hypothesized that a membrane-distal HER2 binder may result in reduced anti-HER2-mediated TDCC due suboptimal synapse formation.

To test this hypothesis, we evaluated two well-characterized anti-HER2 clones as TriMab anchoring arms: Trastuzumab and clone 4380. These clones recognize two distinct HER2 domains: domain IV (membrane-proximal domain) and domain I (membrane-distal domain), respectively (Figure 8a).34 Both antibodies exhibit comparable cellular binding affinities (EC50 = 0.62 nM and 0.53 nM, respectively) when evaluated in the HER2/R347 TriMab format (Fig. S9a). Additionally, both HER2 clones demonstrated negligible internalization in NCI-H358 cells when tested in the TriMab format (Fig. S9b).

The control TriMab HER2/R347 bearing clone 4380 anchoring arm showed limited TDCC (less than 25%) on all four cell lines tested. Overall, careful selection of the anchoring arm binding epitope is a pivotal design decision, as it significantly influences the target selectivity of TriMab. Upon demonstrating the generality of the approach, subsequent studies herein focused on evaluation of ROR1/EGFR TriMab as proof of concept.

TriMab exhibits mAb-like PK profile

To assess the pharmacokinetic (PK) properties of TriMab in vivo, we used a human neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) transgenic Tg32 mouse model. The mice were injected with a single intravenous dose at 5 mg/kg, and the PK profiles were monitored over a period of 35 days in comparison to the constituent IgGs. Half-life (t1/2) of the TriMabs ranged from 4.2 to 5.8 days (Figure 9 and Table 3). Notably, among the parental IgGs, ROR1 exhibited a prolonged t1/2 of 12.6 days, whereas EGFR IgG had a t1/2 of 5.9 days. Given that EGFR clone GA201 lacks cross-reactivity with mouse EGFR, the reduced PK of EGFR IgG compared to ROR1 IgG is likely due to the intrinsic properties of its sequence. When incorporated into TriMab molecules, the reduced PK of EGFR GA201 appears to be inherited by the TriMab constructs, suggesting that the PK of TriMab is influenced by the antibody sequences used in its construction. This has been reported for both IgGs and bispecific antibodies.19,21 Overall, these data underscore the mAb-like PK of TriMab and further emphasize the significant impact that clone selection can have on PK profile.

Discussion

In this study, we report the engineering of TriMab, a trispecific dual TAA-targeted TCE modality. By using synapse gating and affinity modulation, we show that the conditional AND-gated TriMab achieves a remarkable 500-fold improvement in selectively targeting double-positive cells over single-positive cells, within the ROR1/EGFR context. We further demonstrate the broad applicability of our approach through the testing of additional antigen pairs, including HER2/EGFR and several undisclosed pair combinations. Our results highlight the exceptional selectivity of the optimized TriMab across multiple target cancer cell lines with diverse target expression profiles. In vivo, we successfully validated the selective TGI of an optimized ROR1/EGFR TriMab against double-positive NCI-H358 cells in a mouse xenograft model. Overall, we have developed an innovative strategy that harnesses synapse gating and cross-arm avidity for the engineering of dual TAA-targeted TCEs with robust AND-gated target cell selectivity, effectively addressing the challenge of on-target, off-tumor toxicity.

The TriMab strategy is differentiated from existing dual TAA-targeted, trispecific TCEs currently in early development. Dicara et al. have reported a dual TAA-targeted, AND-gated TCE that targets Ly6E and B7-H4. While they leverage cross-arm avidity to achieve dual targeting, the incorporation of high-affinity moieties for both anti-TAA arms significantly elevate the risk of eliminating single-positive cells. More specifically, the authors strategically placed the higher-affinity anti-TAA1 (B7-H4) binder proximally (in cis) to the anti-CD3 domain to facilitate tight synapse formation directed by TAA1, thereby enhancing potency. However, despite this preferential binding to double-positive cells, such strategies may not sufficiently mitigate the risk of eliminating single-positive healthy tissues.13 Similarly, Shen et al. describes the design of a class of trispecific T cell engagers (TriTCEs) that adopt a logic-gated dual tumor-targeting strategy.35,36 These TriTCEs are designed with an “AND” gate mechanism, which selectively induces apoptosis in cancer cells only when both target antigens, EGFR and the HLA-A *02:01-presented NY-ESO-1 peptide, are co-expressed on the cell surface. However, the study also reveals that single-targeting control molecules were able to provoke a significant level of T cell activation, as evidenced by the upregulation of CD69 and CD25 and the release of granzyme. This finding raises concerns about the safety profile of TriTCEs and the potential for off-tumor toxicity. In another notable example, Carretero-Iglesia et al. describe ISB 2001, a trispecific antibody targeting both BCMA and CD38 for the treatment of multiple myeloma (MM). This antibody is designed to overcome immune escape and enhance cytotoxicity against MM cells. Notably, ISB 2001 mediates the killing of MM cells even in the absence of BCMA expression. The authors optimized the configuration and spatial arrangement of the antibody’s Fab domains to maximize tumor cell killing while minimizing T cell activation and cytokine release on CD38 single-positive healthy cells.

Beyond dual TAA-targeted approaches, other strategies for engineering TCEs with reduced on-target, off-tumor toxicity have also been explored through the development of single TAA-targeted ‘masked TCEs.’ These conditional TCE prodrugs remain inactive in circulation or healthy tissues and are designed to become active only upon trafficking to the tumor microenvironment (TME).7 Masked TCEs often incorporate masking domains linked via protease-cleavable spacers, which subsequently enable the restoration of TCE activity upon encountering proteases upregulated in the TME. In recent years, masked TCEs have been extensively investigated for the treatment of solid tumors, with several masked TCE prodrugs currently in Phase 1 clinical development. Notable examples include VIR5500 (PSMAxCD3 from Vir Biotechnology), JANX007 (PSMAxCD3 from Janus), and JANX008 (EGFRxCD3 from Janus), all of which utilize unique CD3 masking technologies.7 Despite the potential of masked TCEs in limiting peripheral T cell activation and directing cytolytic activity within the TME, this strategy presents several inherent challenges that complicate clinical translation.6 For instance, the degree of masking may be insufficiently robust to fully mitigate on-target, off-tumor toxicity. Additionally, many masking technologies require discovery and optimization campaigns for each target binder, a process that is both challenging and time consuming.

With TriMab, we introduce a pioneering approach in the context of a TCE by utilizing a high affinity ‘anchoring arm’ positioned in trans to the anti-CD3 domain. This arm is characterized by its inability to direct a functional immunological synapse due to the combination of its epitope and geometry. Additionally, we position the attenuated active arm in cis relative to the anti-CD3 domain to facilitate tight functional immunological synapse, which requires the engagement of the active arm, but remains dependent on the high-affinity anchoring arm to drive cross-arm avidity. Overall, our synapse-gated trispecific TCE represents a novel antibody engineering approach that may uniquely address the challenge of on-target, off-tumor toxicity.

Future extensions of this work can foreseeably build on the TriMab engineering framework established in this study. Specifically, the selection of TAA pairs is fundamental to the TriMab approach. Co-expression and proximity of these pairs on the same target cell is paramount and must be thoroughly validated. While traditional methods such as RNA sequencing and immunohistochemistry offer detailed insights for accurate TAA pair assessment, advancement in single-cell genomics are expected to provide the quantitative resolution necessary for efficiently identifying optimal TAA combinations present on tumor cells, that lack co-expression on healthy cells.37 Furthermore, choosing the appropriate target for the anchoring arm is a critical design decision. We posit that not all receptor targets are suitable for the anchoring arm, as suitability may be influenced by factors such as extracellular domain size, molecular architecture, and other relevant considerations.

TriMab represents an attractive and differentiated next-generation TCE platform, but the strategy is not without potential liabilities when considering clinical translation. Notably, sink effects driven by the high affinity anchoring arm present a potential risk. In this study, we demonstrated that TriMab retains comparable potency, even in the presence of excess single-positive target cells, which serve as a model for healthy tissue. Furthermore, TriMab is not readily internalized upon target engagement. We propose that ideal anchoring arm targets should lack broad expression across circulation and healthy tissues to mitigate challenges such as poor PK, TMDD, and high dosing requirements. Additionally, caution is warranted when selecting TAA targets with a high probability of antigen loss or tumor antigen escape, as TriMab requires dual TAA targeting for potent TCE activity. Further in vivo studies using transgenic mice or non-human primate models are required to investigate the full impact of TMDD on PK with the TriMab format.

Evaluating the toxicity to normal cells is paramount for determining the safety profile of TriMab. Although the use of normal cell types would be ideal for safety testing, in vitro assays with these cells present considerable challenges due to difficulties in their culturing and maintenance. To mitigate potential toxicities toward EGFR-expressing cells, we incorporated an affinity-attenuated EGFR binding arm into the TriMab framework. We utilized multiple cell lines derived from lung, breast, and colon tissues, each selected for their varying EGFR expression levels to reflect relevant tissue profiles. The ROR1/EGFR[v3] TriMab demonstrated an improved TI across these models. Looking ahead, it will be advantageous to assess the TI using advanced cell models that more accurately recapitulate tissue-specific receptor density and sensitivity to T cell-mediated cytolysis. Such models include three-dimensional cell cultures, spheroids, organoids, and cells or tissues derived from human-induced pluripotent stem cells. Additionally, studies using human receptor knock-in mice can provide insights into tissue-specific toxicity, while investigations in non-human primates may further inform preclinical toxicology predictions.

We anticipate that dual-antigen targeted TCEs will continue to evolve as an attractive and versatile solution for enhancing target cell selectivity across various therapeutic applications. Recent studies have highlighted the early development of AND-gated TCEs for hematological malignancies, with the objective of mitigating on-target, off-tumor toxicity.8,12 By extension, these studies suggest that potential applications for TriMab may extend beyond solid tumor indications. Moreover, the deployment of TCEs has recently gained traction beyond oncology, particularly in targeting CD19 and CD20 to deplete B cells as a treatment for systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, and other autoimmune diseases.38 Given the lower tolerance for toxicities in the treatment of autoimmune diseases, dual-antigen targeting is foreseeably an attractive approach due to its increased selectivity, potentially resulting in a safer and more effective therapeutic strategy.

Overall, by building upon our clinically validated DuetMab as the backbone for the TriMab design, we have successfully engineered and validated a developable trispecific TCE. This innovative design is capable of selectively targeting and eliminating dual-positive cells while providing an expanded TI. This platform holds the potential to address outstanding medical needs across various therapeutic areas.

Materials and methods

Expression and purification of bispecific antibodies

Bispecific antibodies were produced by co-transfecting three mammalian expression vectors: (1) a bicistronic plasmid containing two heavy chains (HCs), (2) a bicistronic plasmid containing two light chains (LCs) of Fab 2 and 3, and (3) a plasmid containing the LC of Fab 1.

For the HC plasmid (1), the first operon contained a HC with ‘hole’ mutations (T366S, L368A, and Y407V), heterodimer disulfide (Y349C), and RF mutations (H435R/Y436F) in the immunoglobulin constant domain 3 (CH3) domain, while the second operon contained ‘knob’ mutations (T366W) and heterodimer disulfide (S354C) mutations in CH3.20 The immunoglobulin constant domain 1 (CH1) domains have V12 disulfide and/or electrostatic steering mutations.19,39

The LC vector (2) contained LCs of the knob-side Fabs (Fab 2 and 3) with the lambda constant light chain domain (Cλ) in the second operon. The LC constant domains have V12 disulfide and/or electrostatic steering mutations.

The LC vector (3) contains the LC of the hole-side Fab 1. The LC constant domain has wild-type disulfide and electrostatic steering mutations.

Antibodies were produced by transient co-transfection of the three plasmids at a 1:1:1 molar ratio into CHO cells using PEI-MAX (Polysciences) as a transfection reagent.40 CHO cells were maintained in a proprietary culture medium. Cell supernatants were collected 12 days after transfection and filtered through 0.22 μm sterile filters. Antibody concentrations were determined using protein A sensors (Sartorius) on an Octet384 instrument, following the manufacturer’s recommended protocols. Antibodies were purified using HiTrap MabSelect SuRe affinity chromatography (Cytiva), all in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The antibodies were subsequently buffer exchanged into phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.2).

The purity and oligomeric state of purified molecules were assessed by microfluidics-based electrophoresis and analytical SEC using a previously established protocol with minor modifications.19 Microfluidics-based electrophoresis was performed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Version B.02.11) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. In this analysis, despite having similar molecular weights, we observed that kappa and lambda LCs are efficiently resolved.

Analytical SEC-HPLC was performed on an Agilent 1260 Infinity HPLC system using a TSK-gel G3000SWxL column (Tosoh Biosciences). Concentrations of purified antibodies were determined by absorbance measurements at 280 nm.

Biolayer interferometry

Binding measurements were conducted using an Octet RED384 instrument (Sartorius). Streptavidin biosensors were first loaded with biotinylated antigens (ACRO Biosystems) in PBS (pH 7.2) containing 1 mg/mL bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.05% (v/v) Tween-20. The loaded biosensors were then washed in the same buffer before performing association and dissociation measurements with various antibodies at specified times. Kinetic parameters were determined using a non-linear fit of the data with Octet software (v.12.2.1.24).

Mass spectrometry

LCMS experiments were conducted using a previously established protocol with minor modifications.19 Proteins were deglycosylated using PNGase F (Promega V4831) before intact mass analysis or digested using IgdE (Genovis, A0-AG1-020) and/or GlySERIAS (Genovis, A0-GS1-020) before subunit mass analysis.

For in-depth analysis of selected molecules, protein mass was acquired using a UPLC coupled to a Xevo G2-XS QTof mass spectrometer (Waters). A BEH C4 column (300 Å, 1.7 µm, 2.1 × 50 mm; Waters) was used for reversed-phase separation. Mobile phases A and B consisted of 0.02% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) in water and acetonitrile, respectively. Mass spectra were collected within a mass-to-charge ratio range of 800–4,500. Molecular mass was determined by deconvolution using the MaxEnt I algorithm within Waters MassLynx (version 4.2).

For routine analysis of all the molecules used in this study, protein mass was acquired using an Agilent 1200 series HPLC coupled to an Agilent 6520 Accurate-Mass Q-TOF LC/MS with an electrospray ionization source. Two micrograms of reduced antibody were loaded onto a Poroshell 300SB-C3 column (2.1 × 75 mm; Agilent) and eluted at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min using a step gradient of 60% B after 6 minutes (mobile phase A: 0.1% formic acid in water; mobile phase B: 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile; JT Baker). The data were collected and processed using Agilent’s Mass Hunter software (v.13.0).

Differential scanning calorimetry analysis

DSC experiments were conducted using a previously established protocol with minor modifications.19 DSC was conducted using a MicroCal VP-DSC scanning microcalorimeter (Malvern Panalytical). All solutions and samples used for DSC were filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and degassed before loading into the calorimeter. Antibodies used for the DSC studies were confirmed to be over 98% monomeric, as determined by analytical SEC. Prior to DSC analysis, all samples were exhaustively dialyzed (at least three buffer exchanges) in 25 mM histidine-HCl (pH 6.0). The buffer from this dialysis was used as the reference buffer for subsequent DSC experiments. Before measuring the samples, baseline measurements (buffer versus buffer) were subtracted from the sample measurements. Dialyzed samples at a concentration of 1 mg/mL were added to the sample well, and DSC measurements were performed at a scan rate of 1°C/min. Data analysis and deconvolution were carried out using the Origin DSC software (version 7.0, Malvern Panalytical). Deconvolution analysis was performed using a non-two-state model, and the best fits were obtained using 100 iteration cycles. The Tm and Tonset is defined as the qualitative temperature at which the thermogram first exhibits a non-zero slope. The Tm is defined as the temperature at which half of the molecules in a set are unfolded, calculated as the temperature value corresponding to each peak maximum on the thermogram.

Lys-C peptide mapping

Lys-C peptide mapping experiments were conducted using a previously established protocol with minor modifications.21 Free thiol groups in the sample were initially capped using 1 mM N-ethylmaleimide. The sample was then denatured in a solution of 6 M guanidine in phosphate buffer at pH 7.0 and 37°C for 30 minutes. The denatured solution was diluted 2.5-fold with 100 mM phosphate buffer containing 0.06 mM EDTA at pH 7.0. Endoproteinase Lys-C (New England Biolabs P8109S) was added at a 1:10 enzyme-to-protein ratio, and the reaction mixture was incubated at 37°C for 16 hours. Additional Lys-C was then added at the same ratio, and the mixture was incubated for an additional 4 hours at 37°C.

Following Lys-C digestion, half of each reaction mixture was reduced by adding DTT to a final concentration of 30 mM and incubating at 37°C for 15 minutes. The other half of the reaction mixture was prepared without reduction. The digested peptides were separated by reverse-phase HPLC on a BEH300 C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 150 mm) and analyzed using a UV detector and an online LTQ Orbitrap mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher). The RP-UPLC mobile phase A consisted of 0.02% TFA in water, and mobile phase B consisted of 0.02% TFA in acetonitrile. Samples were eluted using a gradient with increasing buffer B.

Peptides were identified and analyzed by comparing results from the non-reduced and reduced peptide maps. The sequence of each peptide was identified using MS and confirmed using MS-MS (peptide mass sequencing), based on the known sequence of the protein. Disulfide-bonded peptides were confirmed by MS data as peptides present only in the non-reduced sample. Free thiols were calculated based on the areas of the selected ion chromatogram (SIC) for N-ethylmaleimide-labeled peptides divided by the areas of the corresponding reduced peptides.

Accelerated stability heat stress study

Experiments were conducted using a previously established method with minor modifications.41 Samples were diluted to 1 mg/mL in PBS (pH 7.2) and incubated for two weeks at either 4°C or 45°C. High-performance size exclusion chromatography (HP-SEC) was used to analyze the samples and determine the percentages of monomers, aggregates, and fragments based on curve integration using OpenLab CDS software (version 2.7, Agilent). The changes in monomer, aggregate, and fragment content were calculated by comparing the differences in each sample incubated at 45°C versus 4°C.

Photostability assay

Experiments were conducted using a previously established method with minor modifications.32 For photostability testing, antibodies were formulated at 1 mg/mL in PBS (pH 7.2), filled into 1cc SCHOTT glass vials, stoppered/sealed, and placed into an ICH-compliant photostability chamber (Caron Model 6545–2). Samples were exposed to cool white light at 3000 lux over the course of 1 week, for a total exposure of approximately 500,000 lux hours. Samples were analyzed by HP-SEC.

HEK binding assay

Experiments were conducted using a previously established method with minor modifications.41 Nonspecific HEK cell binding was measured using a Mirrorball Fluorescence Cytometer (SPT Labtech). Initially, 10 μL of Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti-human IgG (H + L) reporter antibody (Invitrogen A-21445, RRID:AB_2535862) was diluted to 16 nM in Mirrorball buffer (Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution with 0.5% BSA) and added to the wells of a 384-well, clear-bottom plate. Each test antibody was then serially diluted in Mirrorball buffer, and 10 μL of each dilution was mixed with the reporter antibody. Finally, 20 μL of HEK293F cells (ThermoFisher A14527), diluted to 250 cells/μL in Mirrorball buffer, were added to the wells. The plate was incubated at room temperature for 2 hours, and the fluorescence of each well was measured using the Mirrorball Fluorescence Cytometer. The experiment was repeated in triplicate.

AC-SINS

Experiments were conducted using a previously established method with minor modifications.41 Briefly, both whole goat IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch 005–000-003, RRID:AB_233698) (non-capture) and polyclonal goat anti-human IgG Fc (Jackson ImmunoResearch 109–005-098, RRID:AB_2337541) (capture) antibodies were transferred into a 20 mM potassium acetate (pH 4.3) buffer and then conjugated to 20 nm gold nanoparticles (Innova Biosciences 3201–0100) at a 3:2 ratio of capture to non-capture antibodies. The antibodies were incubated with gold nanoparticles at a 9:1 ratio for 1 hour at room temperature, then subsequently blocked by the addition of 0.1 μM polyethylene glycol methyl ether thiol (2000 MW; Sigma-Aldrich 729,140) for 1 hour. The coated and blocked nanoparticles were concentrated by centrifugation to an OD535 of 0.4 and stored at 4°C. To assess self-association, 5 μL of nanoparticles were mixed with 45 μL of purified antibody at 50 μg/mL in PBS (pH 7.2) or HA (20 mM histidine, 200 mM arginine, pH 6) in a 384-well plate. Nanoparticles mixed with buffer only (no antibody) served as a control. Absorbance was measured on a SPECTROstar Nano UV/vis plate reader from 490 to 700 nm. The wavelength of peak absorbance was calculated using MARS data analysis software and used to determine the wavelength shift compared to the nanoparticle-only control. The experiment was repeated in triplicate.

Cytotoxicity and T cell activation assay

In vitro cytotoxicity assays

Experiments were conducted using a previously established method with minor modifications.28 PBMCs from healthy donors were used as effector cells. These PBMCs were isolated from lymphocyte-enriched buffy coat preparations obtained from Miltenyi Biotec. Human tumor cell lines from American Type Culture Collection were cultured in the recommended growth medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and L-glutamine at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

A total of 10,000 tumor cells were incubated overnight in 96-well, flat, opaque plates. Human PBMCs were thawed and then added to the assay plates at an effector-to-target (E:T) ratio of 10:1, followed by 4-fold serial dilutions of TriMab molecules.

After a 72-hour incubation, T cells and supernatant were collected first. The wells were then gently washed twice with PBS, and Promega CellTiter-Glo luminescent substrate solution was added to quantify the number of viable cells present in the wells. The mean signal from all non-treatment wells was calculated and used to determine the cytolysis percentage in the treatment wells ((1-(Treatment Signal/Mean of Non-Treatment Signal)) × 100 = % cytolysis). The percentage of cytolysis was plotted by concentration.

For T cell activation, collected cells were washed with FACS buffer composed of PBS (pH 7.2), 2% heat inactivated FBS, 2 mM EDTA, and 0.01% sodium azide. The cells were then stained with a master mix consisting of 4,’6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Life Technologies), anti-CD4-FITC (clone RPA-T4, Biolegend, RRID:AB_314074), anti-CD8-PE-Cy7 (clone RPA-T8, Biolegend, RRID:AB_314130), anti-CD25-PE (clone M-A251, Biolegend, RRID:AB_2561861), and anti-CD69-APC (clone FN50, Biolegend, RRID:AB_314844) in FACS buffer. After staining, the cells were washed and acquired on a FACSymphony A3 (BD Biosciences). FlowJo v10.9 was used to analyze the data (BD Biosciences). T cell activation was calculated as (number of live CD25+CD69+CD4+ (or CD8+) cells)/(number of live CD4+ (or CD8+) cells).

Cytokine release assay

The concentrations of inflammatory cytokines in the coculture supernatant were determined using a Quantikine ELISA kit (R&D Systems) for the detection of IL-2, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6, following the manufacturer’s protocol. A 1:10 dilution was made in assay buffer provided in the kit for all the samples before assay. The ELISA plate was coated with a capture antibody specific to cytokine-of-interest and incubated overnight at 4°C. Following washing to remove unbound antibodies, a blocking buffer was added to prevent nonspecific binding. Samples and standards were then added to the appropriate wells and incubated for 2 hours at room temperature. A detection antibody was added post-wash, followed by another incubation for 2 hours at room temperature. Substrate solution was added to each well and incubated for 15 minutes at room temperature. The reactions were then stopped with a stop solution, and the absorbance is measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The cytokine-of-interest concentration in the samples was determined by comparing their absorbance values to a standard curve generated from the standards provided in the kit.

Internalization assay

10,000 NCI-H358 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate to achieve ~70% confluence at the time of assay. TriMab molecules were labeled by mixing IncuCyte Human Fabfluor-pH dye (Sartorius, RRID: AB_3714617) at 1:1 ratio (both at 100 nM) in serum-free medium. This was subsequently incubated while protected from light for 30 minutes at room temperature. The antibody – dye mix was then added to cells in complete medium and incubated for 24 hours. Fluorescent signals were quantified by IncuCyte analysis software with normalization to cell count/confluence (Sartorius). Anti-transferrin receptor IgG (in-house expressed anti-CD71 clone 15G11.v5 in human IgG1 isotype, RRID: AB_3714623) was used as the positive control for uptake.

Receptor density analysis

Experiments were conducted using a previously established method with minor modifications.29 Receptor density studies were performed by flow cytometry on a FACSymphony A3 (BD Biosciences) as described. Briefly, anti-EGFR GA201 and anti-ROR1 ERR1-TOP43 IgGs were labeled using an Alexa Fluor 647 labeling kit (Invitrogen) according to the supplier’s recommendations. Antibody concentration and fluorochrome-to-protein (F:P) ratio were determined using an ND-1000 spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher).

The cell lines to be tested were resuspended at approximately 4 × 106 cells/mL and washed with ice-cold FACS buffer (PBS pH 7.2, 2% FBS, 2 mM EDTA, and 0.1% sodium azide), followed by incubation with the conjugated antibodies at saturating concentrations (≥20 μg/mL) for 30 minutes at 4°C. After washing with FACS buffer, the detection of bound antibodies was performed on the FACSymphony A3 (BD Biosciences). Results were analyzed using FlowJo analysis software (version 10.9, BD Biosciences).

For quantification of EGFR and ROR1 receptor density on cells, Quantum Alexa Fluor 647 Molecules of Equivalent Soluble Fluorochrome (MESF) beads (Bangs Laboratories) were analyzed on the flow cytometer using similar settings to establish a standard curve. Using the QuickCal program (Bangs Laboratories), the calculated MESF was divided by the antibody F:P ratio to provide a corrected antibody binding capacity.

Competition flow cytometry with anti-EGFR GA201 IgG

Experiments were conducted using a previously established method with minor modifications.42 PA1 cells were detached from the culture plate using Accutase, then resuspended in FACS buffer composed of PBS (pH 7.2), 2% heat inactivated-FBS, 2 mM EDTA, and 0.01% sodium azide. The cells were pelleted, washed twice, and 50 µL of cells were added to 96-well plates (Corning 3799). In a separate 96-well plate (Corning 3879), TriMabs were added to wells starting at 4000 nM and titrated using threefold dilutions (working concentrations were 2×).

Fifty µL of TriMab dilutions were incubated with PA1 cells for 10 minutes on a plate shaker at 1000 rpm. Alexa Fluor 647-labeled anti-EGFR GA201 IgG was then added to the TriMab-cell mixture to a final concentration of 5 nM. The mixture was returned to the plate shaker and incubated for an additional 30 minutes at 1000 rpm. After incubation, the cells were washed three times before resuspension in FACS buffer with DAPI and subsequently analyzed on an iQUE3 flow cytometer (Sartorius).

Cellular binding study

Experiments were conducted using a previously established method with minor modifications.21 HEK cells overexpressing human HER2 were detached from the culture plate using Accutase, then resuspended in FACS buffer composed of PBS (pH 7.2), 2% heat inactivated-FBS, 2 mM EDTA, and 0.01% sodium azide. The cells were pelleted, washed twice, and 50 µL of cells were added to 96-well plates (Corning 3799). In a separate 96-well plate (Corning 3879), TriMabs were added to wells starting at 100 nM and titrated using threefold dilutions.

Fifty µL of TriMab dilutions were incubated with HER2 HEK cells for 30 minutes on a plate shaker at 1000 rpm. Then, after washing twice by centrifugation at 500 g for 5 min, Alexa Fluor 647-labeled anti-human Fc (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 109–607-008, RRID:AB_2632454) was then added to the TriMab-cell mixture to a final concentration of 2 μg/mL. The mixture was returned to the plate shaker and incubated for an additional 30 minutes at 1000 rpm. After incubation, the cells were washed three times before resuspension in FACS buffer with DAPI and subsequently analyzed on an iQUE3 flow cytometer (Sartorius).

Immunological synapse imaging

Experiments were conducted using a previously established method with minor modifications.33 Liposomes were prepared using standard extrusion equipment (Avanti Polar Lipids) to form a SLB on clean-room grade cleaned glass (SCHOTT). The SLB consisted of a combination of 91.75% 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (850375), 6.25% DGS-NTA(Ni) (790404), and 2% Biotinyl Cap PE (870273) lipids purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids. Purified fluorescently labeled antigen molecules (ROR1, EGFR ECD, human leukocyte antigen (HLA) SCT) with a C-terminal 10x histidine tag (His-tag) was incubated on the SLB for 25 minutes at room temperature to achieve a protein density of approximately 250 antigens/µm2 on the SLB. The HLA SCT is single chain trimer consists of HLA-A *02:01, beta-2 microglobulin, and Wilms tumor protein peptide RMFPNAPYL (PDB 7SR5) with a C-terminal 10x His-tag.43

After washing twice to remove the His-tagged molecules, a casein blocking buffer was incubated for 25 minutes at 37°C and washed twice with HBS-BSA buffer containing 1% BSA.33 Prior to imaging, 1 nM TriMab molecules were mixed with 7.5 × 106/mL unstimulated CD3 T cells and introduced into the lipid bilayer chamber, incubated for 25 minutes at 37°C, and then fixed with a 2% paraformaldehyde solution. Imaging was performed using spinning-disk confocal microscopy with a Nikon W1 SORA equipped with a Hamamatsu sCMOS camera. The number of EGFR cluster-positive cells (488 channel) was quantified and divided by the total number of cells (bright field channel) in the field of view. The experiments were conducted with human primary T cells from two different healthy donors.

Pharmacokinetic analysis

Experiments were conducted using a previously established method with minor modifications.19,21 Female Tg32 mice with human FcRn, aged between 8 and 12 weeks (Jackson Laboratory), were injected with antibodies intravenously via the tail vein at a dose of 5 mg/kg, with 6 animals per group. Serial submandibular bleeds were obtained from the mice at 1 hour, 6 hours, 24 hours and after 2, 4, 7, 11, 14, 21 and 28 days. Terminal bleeds were taken via cardiac puncture on day 36. After 30 minutes at room temperature, blood samples were centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 15 minutes, and sera were collected for analysis. All PK studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at AstraZeneca.

The concentration of antibodies in mouse serum samples was determined using a universal ELISA assay. The procedure followed a stepwise format where wash steps followed each incubation step. Microtiter plates were coated with a goat anti-human IgG Fc antibody (RRID:AB_2337541). A standard curve for each antibody was prepared in 10% pooled naïve mouse serum. Three levels (high, medium, and low) of quality control samples were prepared in 100% pooled naïve mouse serum. Following a blocking step with casein buffer, standard curve calibrators, quality controls, and samples diluted to the method minimum required dilution of 1:10 were added to the microtiter plates and incubated for 1 hour with shaking at room temperature. After a wash, HRP-labeled goat anti-human IgG H+L antibody (RRID:AB_2337584) was added to the plate and incubated for 1 hour with shaking. Reactions were stopped with acid, and the plate was read on a spectrophotometer at 450 nm. Data were analyzed with SoftMax Pro, version 7.1. The standard curve was established using a 4-parameter logistic curve fit model without weighting. The quantitative range of this assay was 15.625 ng/mL (lower limit of quantitation) to 2000 ng/mL (upper limit of quantitation). PK parameters were estimated via non-compartmental analysis using Phoenix WinNonlin version 8.2 (Certara).

Tumor growth inhibition study

The tumor growth inhibition studies were conducted by Crown Bioscience using animal protocols CBSD-ACUP-001 approved by the IACUC at AstraZeneca and Crown Bioscience. Animal welfare for this study complied with the U.S. Department of Agriculture’s Animal Welfare Act (9 CFR Parts 1, 2, and 3) as applicable. During the study, the care and use of animals were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and in compliance with the accreditation requirements of the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care.

In vivo experiments were conducted using a previously established method with minor modifications.29,44 NCI-H358–derived xenograft models were developed by subcutaneous injection of 5 million NCI-H358 cells in Cultrex (R&D Systems) into the flanks of 5- to 7-week-old female NSG MHC class I/II double-knockout (NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid H2-K1b-tm1Bpe H2-Ab1g7-em1MvwH2-D1b-tm1BpeIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ) mice (The Jackson Laboratory). Resulting tumors were measured twice per week, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: ((longest diameter (mm)*shortest diameter(mm)2))/2. Mice were randomized when tumor volumes reached approximately 80–100 mm3, and each mouse was implanted intraperitoneally with 5 × 106 human PBMCs in 0.1 mL of PBS (day 0). Starting from day 1, TriMab molecules were injected at 0.5 mg/kg along with anti-CD16 (Fc block, RRID:AB_282954) at 20 mg/kg (for all groups) via intraperitoneal injection every 3 days.

Tumor growth and body weight were monitored at least twice weekly. Where statistics are provided, growth rate comparisons were conducted as previously described. The body weight of all animals was monitored throughout the study, and animals were euthanized if they lost more than 20% of their body weight compared to their weight on the day of randomization.

Data and statistical analysis

Data and statistical analyses were performed using Prism 9.5.1.733 (GraphPad). Curve fitting and EC50 value determination was performed by linear regression. Octet kinetic analyses were performed using Octet 384 software (v.7.2) with a global fit and 1:1 binding model, with Pearson’s Chi-squared test less than 5.
