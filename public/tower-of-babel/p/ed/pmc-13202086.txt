Potential stratification of TAFRO syndrome by anti-SS-A antibodies status and therapeutic response to rituximab
Authors: Tomohiro Suzuki, Saeko Yamada, Kanto Toriumi, Masanori Kono, Haruka Tsuchiya, Keishi Fujio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThrombocytopaenia, anasarca, fever, reticulin fibrosis, renal dysfunction and organomegaly (TAFRO) syndrome has been reported to be associated with anti-SS-A antibodies, which activate type I interferon (IFN) signalling. We propose a subclassification of TAFRO syndrome based on the anti-SS-A antibody status, with the aim of identifying effective treatment strategies.MethodsWe reviewed TAFRO syndrome cases from the PubMed/MEDLINE databases from January 1977 to October 2024. The information on concomitant autoimmune diseases, the presence of anti-SS-A antibodies, serum IgG levels, treatment courses and clinical outcomes was collected and retrospectively analysed. Patients were stratified according to anti-SS-A antibody status, and response to tocilizumab (TCZ) or rituximab (RTX) was evaluated.ResultsAmong 212 cases, 27 had concomitant autoimmune diseases, with Sjögren’s disease (SjD) being the most common. Anti-SS-A antibody positivity was observed in 37 cases, of which 12 fulfilled the classification criteria for SjD. RTX was significantly more effective in anti-SS-A antibody-positive patients than in antibody-negative patients (88.9% vs 16.7%, p=0.011), whereas therapeutic response to TCZ was not affected by anti-SS-A antibody status. Serum IgG levels were significantly higher in anti-SS-A antibody-positive patients than in antibody-negative patients (p=0.020).ConclusionsTAFRO syndrome shows clinical heterogeneity and anti-SS-A antibody status may represent one potential factor associated with treatment response. Further studies are needed to confirm these findings and clarify the underlying mechanisms.Keywords: Autoimmune Diseases, Antibodies, Immune System Diseases

Introduction

TAFRO syndrome is a rare inflammatory disease characterised by a constellation of symptoms: thrombocytopaenia, anasarca, fever, reticulin fibrosis, renal dysfunction and organomegaly, and was first described in Japan in 2010.1 This syndrome is considered a variant of idiopathic multicentric Castleman disease (iMCD), a spectrum of disorders involving systemic inflammation, characteristic lymph node histopathologic findings and multiorgan dysfunction resulting from pathologic hypercytokinaemia.2 It has been reported that approximately one-third of patients with TAFRO syndrome die within the first 24 months, with survival rates declining sharply during this period.3 To improve the survival rate of patients, prompt diagnosis and therapeutic intervention are essential. According to current diagnostic criteria,4 the diagnosis of TAFRO syndrome requires the exclusion of autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), Sjögren’s disease (SjD) and ANCA-associated vasculitis. On the other hand, TAFRO syndrome is clinically heterogeneous, and a group of patients has been reported to develop the syndrome against a background of autoimmune mechanisms. In particular, it is noteworthy that TAFRO syndrome with anti-SS-A antibodies has been reported,5 which are known to be associated with type I interferonopathy6 7 and it has been suggested that TAFRO syndrome may be an extremely severe clinical manifestation of SjD.8 The clinical endotype of TAFRO syndrome, including its therapeutic response, may differ depending on the underlying pathology, but this has not been fully investigated. Moreover, it has been suggested that increased activation and differentiation of class-switched B cells by interferon (IFN) may cause hypergammaglobulinaemia in a subgroup of SLE patients,9 but it is unclear whether type I IFN signalling activation and elevated serum IgG levels are also associated with TAFRO syndrome.

Here, we performed a retrospective literature-based exploratory analysis of TAFRO syndrome cases to examine differences in treatment responses based on anti-SS-A antibody status or concomitant autoimmune diseases and investigated whether serum IgG levels were higher in a group of anti-SS-A antibody-positive TAFRO syndrome patients, in whom type I IFN signalling is thought to be activated, compared with antibody-negative patients.

Methods

Cases of TAFRO syndrome associated with autoimmune diseases were identified through a literature search using the PubMed/MEDLINE databases from January 1977 to October 2024 (figure 1). Details of the search strategy are described in the online supplemental text. In this analysis, we included cases meeting the 2016 proposed diagnostic criteria for iMCD-TAFRO2 or the 2019 updated diagnostic criteria4 as cases of TAFRO syndrome. Note that while autoimmune diseases such as SLE or SjD are listed as conditions to exclude in both criteria, this analysis also examined the coexistence of autoimmune diseases. Therefore, cases with concomitant autoimmune diseases were not excluded.

Search strategy used for the review of anti-SS-A antibody status or concomitant autoimmune diseases in TAFRO syndrome. TAFRO, thrombocytopaenia, anasarca, fever, reticulin fibrosis, renal dysfunction and organomegaly.

A total of 212 cases were evaluated for anti-SS-A antibody status, concomitant autoimmune diseases, serum IgG levels, treatment course and clinical outcome, and stratified based on these indicators. Reference lists of 212 cases and details of the statistical analysis are also described in the online supplemental text. All statistical analyses were performed for exploratory purposes only, given the retrospective, literature-based nature of the data and the substantial heterogeneity across reports.

Results

We found that 37 of the 212 patients tested positive for anti-SS-A antibodies, 43 tested negative for anti-SS-A antibodies, and 132 had no record of anti-SS-A antibodies (online supplemental table S1). Of the 37 seropositive patients, 12 fulfilled the 1999 revised Japanese criteria10 or the 2016 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism classification criteria11 for SjD. There were no significant differences observed between anti-SS-A antibody-positive and anti-SS-A antibody-negative patients in terms of baseline clinical characteristics, except for serum IgG levels and prevalence of SjD (online supplemental table S2). Additionally, among the 212 patients, 27 patients had concomitant autoimmune diseases. Of the 27 autoimmune disease patients, 13 had SjD, 5 had SLE, 2 had chronic thyroiditis, 2 had ulcerative colitis (UC), 1 had both UC and type 1 diabetes mellitus and 1 patient each had mixed connective tissue disease, immune thrombocytopenic purpura, rheumatoid arthritis and polymyalgia rheumatica. In summary, based on our findings, SjD appeared to be the most common autoimmune disease associated with TAFRO syndrome.

The therapeutic response to tocilizumab (TCZ) or rituximab (RTX) based on anti-SS-A antibody status or coexisting autoimmune diseases is summarised in table 1. As a side note, the definitions of ‘effective’ and ‘refractory’ in treatment response are detailed in the online supplemental text. Cases that resulted in death due to infection were either cases where neither TCZ nor RTX was effective, or cases where neither TCZ nor RTX was used. We revealed that anti-SS-A antibody-positive cases had significantly higher RTX response rates than antibody-negative cases (88.9% vs 16.7%, p=0.011), and there was no significant difference in RTX response rates depending on whether patients had concomitant autoimmune diseases (60.0% vs 66.7%, p>0.99). Similarly, there was no significant difference in TCZ response rates depending on whether patients were positive for anti-SS-A antibodies (37.5% vs 55.0%, p=0.34) or patients had concomitant autoimmune diseases (28.6% vs 53.0%, p=0.26). Furthermore, serum IgG levels were significantly higher in patients with positive anti-SS-A antibodies than those with negative antibodies (1695.2±599.5 mg/dL vs 1283.2±672.4 mg/dL; p=0.020) (figure 2). No significant difference was observed in the response rate to RTX between the elevated IgG group and non-elevated IgG group (50.0% vs 60.0%, p>0.99), as well as TCZ (25.0% vs 61.5%, p=0.12); however, the non-elevated IgG group tended to respond more readily to TCZ than the elevated group (online supplemental table S3).

Discussion

Based on the results of this study, in which cases of TAFRO syndrome with concomitant autoimmune diseases were not intentionally excluded to examine the coexistence of both conditions, we hypothesise that TAFRO syndrome may be stratified according to anti-SS-A antibody status and RTX may be particularly effective in patients with anti-SS-A antibody-positive TAFRO syndrome. Anti-SS-A antibody positivity is suggested to be associated with enhanced type I IFN signalling which regulates B cell activation and survival, and diseases in which type I IFN activity is high include SjD and SLE.6 7 The persistent activation of the type I IFN pathway induces a characteristic transcriptional profile known as the IFN signature.6 7 Anti-SS-A antibodies may serve as a surrogate marker for the activation of type I IFN signalling in TAFRO syndrome, which could enable the efficient use of RTX in patients who respond well to the treatment.

As a background, in fact, emerging evidence suggests that type I IFN signalling is also upregulated in certain patients with TAFRO syndrome independent of autoimmune comorbidities.12 Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis showed a marked increase in the type I IFN response gene expression in clusters of monocytes, natural killer cells and CD8+ T cells from patients with TAFRO syndrome during disease exacerbation.12 This finding suggests that type I IFN responses may play a central role in the pathogenesis of the subpopulation of TAFRO syndrome, and the presence of a strong type I IFN gene signature suggests that type I IFN inhibitors, such as anifrolumab, could be considered as a treatment option. On the other hand, the degree of IFN activation varies substantially across autoimmune diseases. As an example, while SjD and SLE are diseases with increased type I IFN activity, UC is not associated with increased type I IFN activity compared with SjD or SLE. Tnaive MX1, which is a naive T cell in which IFN signalling is activated, is abundant in SjD and SLE but not in UC.13 In other words, there are autoimmune diseases, such as UC, where type I IFN activity is not considered active. Since it is difficult to assert that interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) expression is elevated in all patients with autoimmune diseases, it is inferred that there is no difference in responsiveness to TCZ and RTX. Conversely, the presence of anti-SS-A antibodies may be closely associated with type I IFN signalling activation, suggesting a difference in responsiveness to TCZ and RTX.

Moreover, there was no significant difference in response rate to RTX between the elevated IgG group and non-elevated IgG group in this study. This discrepancy may be due to the fact that serum IgG is a nonspecific marker affected by multiple factors, such as inflammation and immune activation. In contrast, anti-SS-A antibodies are qualitative markers that acutely reflect type I IFN signal activation. In other words, the presence of B cells involved in autoantibodies production is directly related to the response to RTX, suggesting that serum IgG levels alone are insufficient to determine the response to RTX. Although serum IgG levels were significantly higher in patients with positive anti-SS-A antibodies than those with negative antibodies, it should be noted that autoimmune diseases, particularly connective tissue diseases such as SjD, are inherently associated with hypergammaglobulinaemia. Therefore, the higher serum IgG levels observed in anti-SS-A antibody-positive patients may be partly attributable to the presence of concomitant autoimmune diseases rather than anti-SS-A antibody positivity itself.

Importantly, Yoshimura et al proposed a conceptual stratification of TAFRO syndrome into IL-6-dominant and IL-6-recessive subgroups, suggesting that the latter may involve type I IFN-related pathways.14 This clinically oriented stratification complements previous mechanism studies focused on TAFRO syndrome with elevated ISGs levels and enhances the practical significance of pathophysiology-based treatment selection.

This study has some limitations. First, the number of cases and the study design are limited. This literature review is a retrospective analysis of cases from case reports or case series only. Therefore, this study merely constitutes hypothesis-generating analysis, formal assessments of publication bias and statistical heterogeneity were considered unreliable and were not performed. Moreover, since autoimmune diseases are listed as conditions to exclude in the diagnostic criteria for TAFRO syndrome,2 4 the case of TAFRO syndrome with concomitant autoimmune diseases included in this analysis does not strictly meet the diagnostic criteria, and such cases are rare and tend to be reported more frequently. Thus, the actual rate of autoimmune diseases comorbidity may be lower than reported here. Limitations also exist regarding the definition of treatment response. In fatal cases, we predefined that a case would only be classified as ‘effective’ if the author explicitly stated in their clinical judgement that the cause of death was not primarily attributable to uncontrolled TAFRO syndrome. However, definitively asserting that TAFRO syndrome itself did not contribute to the death may be difficult. There is also a limitation regarding anti-SS-A antibodies. Since not many reports describe anti-SS-A antibody status and the background of treatment with therapeutic agents is not standardised, it is unclear whether the difference in treatment response rate is due to anti-SS-A antibody status. Additionally, since we did not directly measure ISGs expression or IFN signature, the presence of anti-SS-A antibodies does not definitively indicate an enhanced IFN signature in TAFRO syndrome. The stratification model presented in this study is purely hypothesis-generating and requires validation through large-scale prospective studies with direct assessment of the IFN pathway, and therefore, we cannot simply link anti-SS-A antibodies positivity to IFN activation. Moreover, not only is the method for measuring anti-SS-A antibodies inconsistent across individual cases, but anti-SS-A antibodies can also target two distinct antigens, Ro52 and Ro60. However, most of the reports we reviewed did not distinguish between the two, and this distinction was not made in this analysis either.

We demonstrated that stratifying patients based on anti-SS-A antibody status may be useful for predicting response to RTX through a retrospective literature-based exploratory analysis. Stratification of patients with TAFRO syndrome and enhanced understanding of its aetiology and pathophysiology are expected to improve the prediction of treatment response and prognosis and to inform the selection of optimal therapeutic strategies.
