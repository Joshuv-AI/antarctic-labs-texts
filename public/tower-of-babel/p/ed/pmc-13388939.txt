Experimental and predictive analysis of pile raft foundation in clayey soils using machine learning and MATLAB tools
Authors: Channabasavaraj Wollur, T. Aruna, S. Yashaswini, Bitan De, S. Yallappa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study demonstrates the performance of piled raft foundations in a variety of clay soils collected from four states in southern India. Comprehensive physical, chemical, mineralogical and geotechnical characterisation of 12 different soil samples were performed. The testing on 12 soils reviles that soil has 54 to 70% clay content with predominantly montmorillonite mineral, which leads to high FSI values between 75 to 200%, and low dry densities in the range of 13.5 to 16.9 g/cc. Experimental loading tests were executed with different piled raft configurations such as no pile (R), 4 piles (R4), 6 piles (R6), and 9 piles (R9) raft. These studies extracted data for the calculation of the Load Sharing Ratio (LSR) and Settlement Reduction Ratio (SRR) in relation to the standard shallow foundation on homogeneous clayey soil. A 64% increase in LSR and 75% reduction in settlement values were observed as the number of piles increased from R4 to R9 sequentially. Machine learning (ML) models were developed to predict LSR and SRR based on soil parameters such as clay percentage, soil type, Free Swell Index (FSI), and Maximum Dry Density (MDD). The trained ML models successfully predict the LSR and SRR and for higher configurations with 12 (R12), 15 (R15), 18 (R18), and 21 (R21) piles with an accuracy of more than 90%. Finally, the LSR and SRR values obtained from ML models of experimental data for the test were compared via MATLAB. This verification process confirmed the high (> 95%) accuracy of the ML models in all scenarios. This study provides a broad direction on the designing of pile raft in a cost effective manner for a wide range of clayey soils.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53172-5.

Introduction

When soil strength is insufficient to support structural loads, deeper foundations, such as pile groups, are often used to transfer loads to more competent soil layers1. The piled raft foundation (PRF) integrates the load-bearing abilities of both the raft and piles, making it a widely adopted system in recent years2. LSR and SRR of PRF are critical parameters in foundation design, particularly in soft soil conditions3. The LSR indicates how loads are distributed between the raft and the piles4, while the SRR reflects the effectiveness of the piles in minimising total settlement5. The LSR is influenced by factors such as pile spacing and stiffness6. A study found that spacing greater than three times the pile diameter (3D) enhances load sharing efficiency7. In disconnected piled raft systems, the load distribution is optimised through gravel and geogrid layers, which help achieve a more uniform pressure distribution8. Increasing the distance between piles can improve raft load sharing by up to 11.5%9. Pile length significantly affects settlement reduction; doubling the pile length can reduce settlement by up to 9%9,10. However, increasing the number of piles beyond a certain point yields diminishing returns. The introduction of piles can lead to a total settlement reduction of up to 60.82% when strategically placed11. The effectiveness of piles in reducing settlement is also contingent on soil conditions, with varying results observed in different soil types12.

The application of AI and ML in predictive modelling for pile raft foundations has shown significant advancements in accurately assessing settlement, axial stress, and bearing capacity13–16. These models leverage various algorithms to enhance prediction accuracy, thereby improving foundation design and performance17. ML models, particularly Extreme Gradient Boosting, have been effectively utilised to predict settlement in micropile-supported raft foundations, using a dataset of 350 instances with 13 features18. The models were evaluated using metrics such as root mean square error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE), demonstrating high predictive accuracy. Explainable AI techniques have been employed to predict axial stress in piled rafts affected by adjacent tunnelling, revealing that stress reduction varies with pile location and soil density19,20. Tree-based algorithms showed superior performance in modelling these stress changes, underscoring the importance of feature engineering17. ML methods, including Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), have been applied to estimate the ultimate bearing capacity of bored piles, achieving high correlation coefficients (R > 0.93)21. These models utilise engineering parameters from multiple test sites, showcasing their robustness in foundation design. According to22, the study employs ANN models using the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm and non-linear multiple regression (NMR) to predict load sharing and interaction factors in PRF, thereby enhancing design strategies and estimating the factor of safety. While AI and ML models provide enhanced predictive capabilities, traditional methods still hold value, particularly in complex scenarios where empirical data is crucial for validation.

This paper evaluates the performance of raft and pile raft foundations on clayey soil by examining two key parameters: the LSR between the raft and piles, and the SRR. The LSR quantifies the distribution of the total structural load between the raft and the piles23. For instance, an LSR of 30% means the raft bears 30% of the total load, while the piles carry the remaining 70%. This indicates that the piles are the primary load-bearing elements, with the raft providing secondary support, aiding load transfer and improving overall foundation stability. The optimal LSR varies depending on factors such as soil conditions, the geometry of the piles and raft, and the magnitude and distribution of the applied loads24. The SRR measures how effectively piles reduce the overall settlement of a PRF compared to a raft foundation without piles. PRFs are specifically designed to minimise settlement, especially in soft soils significantly. This is achieved because the piles transfer a substantial portion of the load to deeper, stronger soil layers, thereby reducing the settlement experienced by the raft23. A higher SRR (e.g., 80%) indicates that the piles are highly effective at minimising settlement, which is critical for ensuring the long-term serviceability and structural integrity of the supported structure, particularly in areas prone to significant settlement25. Conversely, a lower SRR (e.g., 20%) indicates that the piles have a lesser impact on reducing settlement.

This paper also highlights the development of predictive models for pile-raft foundation behaviour using experimentally obtained soil parameters. Key input parameters considered include percentage of clay, soil type, free swell index (FSI), and maximum dry density (MDD), as these significantly influence the performance of pile raft foundations. These values were used as inputs to ML models15,16,26 to predict foundation performance in terms of LSR and SRR. The predictions were validated in MATLAB, confirming a high accuracy exceeding 95% across all scenarios.

Raft and pile

Laboratory tests utilised a 100 mm × 100 mm × 10 mm flexible steel raft. This raft was used with 4, 6, and 9 pile configurations, as depicted in Figs. S1–S3. Mild steel piles, 10 mm in diameter and 100 mm long, were chosen for their high stiffness, as shown in Fig. S4. Steel has a high modulus of elasticity, allowing smaller pile sections than wood or aluminium. This stiffness is crucial for accurate load transfer in the experiments. The experimental setup aimed to investigate pile group behaviour under controlled conditions. The steel raft and piles provided a consistent, reliable foundation for the study. The chosen dimensions and materials were selected to simulate realistic foundation elements. The research focused on analysing the performance of these pile-raft systems.

Methodology

This study investigates the performance of pile raft foundations on clayey soil. A 500 mm × 500 mm square test tank was used to prepare a controlled soft clay bed using Black Cotton (BC) soil. The soil was compacted in 8 cm layers. Soil samples are meticulously prepared by weighing the exact quantity of oven-dried soil, determined by its MDD. This soil is then thoroughly mixed with water equivalent to its OMC, as established by a compaction test. The homogenous mixture is compacted in three layers within a square test tank, ensuring uniform density using a conventional rammer across all layers. The top surface is then levelled with a ruler. Following compaction, the soil bed was allowed to consolidate for two days. Once the soil sample is ready, the pile and raft arrangement is carefully pushed into the compacted soil, taking great care to minimize disturbance to the surrounding area. After an initial penetration depth, a hydraulic jack is used to push the pile and raft into the soil slowly.

The experimental setup incorporates a loading frame (Fig. 2) capable of applying a maximum compressive load of 500 kN. This strain-controlled equipment offers six constant strain rates. A circular base plate (32 mm thick, 250 mm diameter) is attached to a threaded vertical stem connected to a worm wheel arrangement. The soil-filled tank is positioned on this base plate, pressing against the upper rigid arm of the loading frame via a proving ring. As the base plate moves vertically, it induces a compressive load on the soil, which is measured by the proving ring. The proving ring has a maximum capacity of 50 kN and can measure compressive loads as small as 0.063 kN. Pile and raft dimensions and influencing factors

The length-to-diameter (L/D) ratio is a key factor influencing the load-carrying capacity, load distribution, and settlement characteristics of pile-raft foundations. According to38, higher L/D ratios increase total settlement but reduce differential settlement between the pile and the surrounding soil. Optimising pile stiffness through rational adjustments in length and diameter enhances overall stability. In this study, an L/D ratio of 5 was adopted for all tests. Raft size and stiffness strongly affect foundation performance. Increasing raft width from 4 to 7 m can improve load transfer by about 75%39. Thicker and stiffer rafts reduce differential settlement and improve load sharing between the raft and piles, depending on stiffness characteristics40. The raft used in this study measured 100 mm × 100 mm and was 10 mm thick. Model pile dimensions were selected based on soil type, test tank size, and foundation depth. Pile spacing directly affects group efficiency and settlement in clayey soils41. Studies suggest an optimal spacing of 3Dh for enhanced ultimate capacity42,43, with minimal change in displacement beyond 6D44–46. Increasing spacing from 3 to 10D may reduce the proportion of load shared by piles from 42 to 23%47. Accordingly, pile spacing was maintained at 30 mm for four piles and 40 mm for six and nine pile groups (Figs. S19, S21 and S22). The experimental program comprised four test sets: R (raft only), R4 (raft with four piles arranged in a 40 mm square grid), R6 (raft with six piles in a 40 × 30 mm rectangular grid), and R9 (raft with nine piles in a 30 mm square grid). After placing the foundation in the compacted soil (Fig. S20), a hydraulic jack applied gradually increasing loads measured by a 50 kN proving ring with 0.063 kN precision. Testing continued until the raft settlement reached 25 mm. The study evaluates pile-raft performance on clayey soils through LSR and SRR. The load corresponding to 25 mm settlement, considered safe per IS 1904, forms the basis for LSR. SRR comparisons are made at a load of 2 kN across all twelve soils. Factors such as loading rate, soil drainage, and moisture variations were excluded from the LSR and SRR evaluation.

This study employs model scale tests in a 500 mm × 500 mm tank with L/D = 5 piles, effectively simplifying soil-structure interaction analysis while accounting for scale effects to support reliable prototype applications. The 1g testing conditions produce appropriately scaled stress levels, and clayey soils stress dependent behavior ensures deeper shear zone activation aligns well between lab and full scale scenarios. Proper drainage path scaling enhances realistic consolidation timelines in clays, while similitude principles matching stress, strain, and length scales via elastic laws enable accurate representation. These model tests provide valuable qualitative trends and mechanisms, complemented by validation through centrifuge scaling, numerical back analysis, or field data for robust quantitative design insights.

This paper highlights the performance of pile raft foundations on clayey soil, focusing on LSR and SRR, which are critical parameters for evaluating foundation behavior. For the LSR evaluation across various soils, the load corresponding to a 25 mm settlement, a value considered safe and well within acceptable limits (as per IS 1904) for most building foundations, is used as the benchmark. Similarly, for SRR assessment, the settlement at a load of 2 kN is used to compare all 12 clayey soils. It is important to note that other factors, such as loading rate, soil drainage conditions, and soil moisture content, were not considered in the evaluation of LSR and SRR values.

Results and discussion

The specific gravity (G) of the soils ranged from 2.4 to 2.8. At the same time, the water content (w) varied between 60 and 80%, the soils were classified as Medium Plastic Silt (MH, six samples), High Plastic Clay (CH, three samples), and Medium Plastic Clay to Silt (MH-CH, three samples), exhibiting a high water-holding capacity (53% to 76%) and a very high swelling potential (75% to 200%). The organic content remained low (below 15%) and negligible for analysis, with all soil samples indicating a basic pH (> 7), and chemical analysis showing a majority composition of Silica, Alumina, and Magnesium, notably including 10% to 15% iron content, which likely contributed to the slightly higher specific gravity and imparted strength. The absence of a single dominant chemical constituent meant its influence on pile raft foundations was minimal and could be ignored in further analysis to estimate LSR and SRR values. However, the compaction curves revealed that all 12 clayey soils exhibited low MDD values (13 to 17 kN/m3) and high Optimum Moisture Content (OMC) values (28% to 38%), findings supported by the FSI value and primarily attributed to the presence of highly swelling clay minerals. XRD analysis confirmed a diverse mineral range, including typical soil minerals like Quartz, mica, montmorillonite, illite, Calcite, and muscovite, and several non-soil minerals (tridymite, mascagnite, lyonsite, pyroxo magnetite, ornorate, anorthite, and olivine), possibly resulting from varied chemical composition or contamination.

As observed from the curves in the figures S23 to S25, the PRF consistently carries a higher load than the plain raft, regardless of the settlement magnitude. In the initial stages of loading, the settlement of the piled raft is significantly less than that of the plain raft. This indicates that adding piles makes the foundation system much stiffer from the outset. For instance, in clayey soil 9, at a settlement of 9 mm, the 9-pile raft combination supported a load of 2.88 kN, which is a remarkable 140% higher than the load carried by the plain raft at the same settlement. Here, the piles primarily function as settlement reducers, and the synergistic interaction among the piles, the raft, and the soil allows the raft to bear a greater load while experiencing minimal settlement. However, as the load increases, the settlement of the piled raft also rises. This behaviour suggests a progressive reduction in the soil-pile stiffness at higher loads. Consequently, the ratio between the load carried by the piled raft and the plain raft progressively decreases with increasing settlement. This trend suggests that pile provision is most effective when settlements are low, particularly when settlements are less than 2% of the tested raft size. Furthermore, as loading continues to increase, the difference in load-bearing capacity between the plain raft and the piled raft diminishes, indicating that the influence of the piles reduces at higher loads and eventually becomes constant beyond a certain loading level.

LSR value increases with the number of piles; this trend suggests that, while adding more piles increases the total load capacity, the proportionate contribution of each pile to the total load increases. This implies that as more piles are added, the raft itself becomes more effective in sharing the load, and the overall system stiffness increases, leading to a more efficient distribution of stress. The piles primarily act as settlement reducers, engaging the soil more effectively and stiffening the overall system, allowing the raft to carry a larger absolute load, even if the proportion of load on each pile is lower. Table 4 demonstrates that combining piles with a raft foundation significantly increases the load-carrying capacity at a settlement of 25 mm. Notably, the LSR increases with the number of piles in the piled raft system, thereby enhancing overall load capacity and reducing raft settlement. This behaviour is typical of PRFs, in which piles and rafts work together to support structural loads efficiently. For design purposes, optimising the number and arrangement of piles can improve foundation performance while effectively controlling settlement.

Table 3 clearly illustrates the effectiveness of adding piles to a raft foundation in reducing settlement under a constant load of 2kN across various clayey soil conditions. Under all soil conditions, the addition of piles dramatically reduces settlement compared to the unpiled raft. Machine learning tools for predictive models

To develop predictive models for estimating the Load Sharing Ratio (LSR) and Settlement Reduction Ratio (SRR), machine learning algorithms were trained using the experimental dataset obtained from laboratory tests conducted on twelve clayey soils under four foundation configurations (R, R4, R6, and R9). Each dataset entry consisted of soil properties such as clay fraction, soil classification, FSI, and MDD along with experimentally measured foundation responses.

The dataset consisted of 48 experimental observations derived from the combinations of soil types and pile configurations. Although the dataset size is relatively small compared to typical machine learning studies, it represents controlled laboratory experimental data with high measurement consistency. Prior to model training, the dataset was pre-processed by organizing input features and output variables. The input features included soil parameters and configuration parameters, while the target outputs were the experimentally determined LSR and SRR values. The dataset was divided into training and testing subsets (as depicted in table S3 to S6) using a 70:30 split48, where 70% of the data was used to train the models and 30% was reserved for testing predictive performance. To reduce overfitting and improve model generalization, k-fold cross-validation (k = 5) was employed during model training.

Selection of predictive models

Predictive modelling is crucial for estimating LSR and SRR for various pile-raft combinations for a given soil, using input variables without performing extensive physical tests for every combination. To achieve this, Several machine learning algorithms were implemented, in this current study including; RF, XGBoost, Decision Tree (DT), SVM, K-Nearest Neighbour (KNN) and ANN47.

Hyperparameter tuning for tree-based models such as Random Forest and XGBoost was performed using grid search optimization, where parameters such as the number of estimators, tree depth, and minimum samples per split were systematically adjusted to obtain optimal predictive performance.

The predictive capability of the models was evaluated using standard regression performance metrics including: MAE, MSE, RMSE and Coefficient of Determination (R2). These metrics quantify the deviation between predicted and experimentally observed values and provide a robust assessment of model accuracy. Due to the limited dataset size, ensemble tree-based algorithms such as Random Forest and XGBoost demonstrated superior predictive performance, as these methods are known to perform well with small datasets and nonlinear relationships.

A simple linear regression model is used, assuming a linear relationship between one independent variable (e.g., Maximum dry density) and the dependent variable (e.g., load carried by the pile-raft). However, several ML models can be used to evaluate pile–raft performance. This study uses a few of these ML technologies. DT and RF method. These models partition the data based on decision rules to predict outputs. Random Forests use multiple trees and average their results, often leading to more robust predictions than a single DT. SVM: this can model complex relationships between input variables and the target, particularly for non-linear patterns. ANNs are appropriate in situations where LSR and SRR rely on complex combinations of input factors, such as percentage of clay, free swell index, and soil type, because they can capture intricate correlations and interactions among variables. XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting): A powerful ensemble learning technique that builds models iteratively to minimise prediction errors. It has been widely used in material science for predicting material properties due to its high performance and accuracy.

Trend comparison using MATLAB curve fitting

Uncertainty often affects settlement prediction, as in many geotechnical engineering problems. Precise settlement estimates are essential for design, but this uncertainty frequently leads to inaccurate results51,52. For pile foundations in clayey soil, prediction uncertainty arises directly from variations in input data and the settlement calculation approach used. Therefore, validating ML regression predictions is crucial to address these uncertainty challenges53,54.

MATLAB is excellent for developing predictive models for pile foundations; it is equally powerful for validating these models. Validation is the process of assessing how well a model’s predictions align with real-world observations or trusted experimental data. In the course of understanding the variation of Q(R+n) and S(R+n), we conduct a detailed curve-fitting analysis in MATLAB version 2018a using the Curve Fitting Toolbox. (Note that n stands for the pile number). In both analyses, we take n = 4, 6 and 9 as the experimentally validated data and curve-fitting is done in two ways: (a) power-law variation and (b) exponential variation. Since Q(R+n) and S(R+n) are monotonically increasing and decreasing functions of pile count, we disregard other fitting techniques, such as parabolic fitting and spline methods, which yield a mixture of monotonically increasing and decreasing variations. In the case of power-law variation, Q(R+n) and S(R+n) are fitted as functions of AXnB, where A and B are arbitrary constants. Each soil sample will have distinct values of A and B. Similarly, for exponential variation, Q(R+n) and S(R+n) are fitted as a function of C X exp (n X D). Note that, for load sharing and settlement analysis, B and D can only have positive and negative values, respectively. On the other hand, the values of A and C depend on the initial value set by the experimental data.

All occasions predict a monotonic increase in LSR percentage, however, ML-based predictions closely match with power-law variation, while the exponential fitting overshoots both ML and power-law. Figure S28 exhibits similar prediction of SRR variation as a function of pile count via same three methods. Contrary to the previous case, it predicts a monotonic decrease in SRR percentage in all occasions. In this case, three predictions differ in their final values, although the ML-based prediction is closer to the exponential fit and the power-law falls apart.
