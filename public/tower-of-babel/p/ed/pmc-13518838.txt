Compaction characteristics and lump crushing behavior of homologous gangue powder grouted uncemented backfill
Authors: Zilong Zhang, Xiang He, Xiaowu Feng, Longqiang Wei, Zhen Liu, Pei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The homologous material system is a green backfill technology that utilizes mine-derived associated solid wastes to prepare filling materials in situ, with minimal or no introduction of exogenous components, thereby achieving a closed-loop “mining–beneficiation–backfill” paradigm. Backfill systems using gangue powder as the primary material can provide effective support for underground caving zones while offering significant advantages in cost and sustainable mining. In this study, an integrated mold for filling, curing, and loading was independently designed, and confined compression and acoustic emission (AE) tests were conducted on gangue powder slurries with varying particle sizes and concentrations. The stress–strain responses under confined compression, AE ring-down counts and spatial localization, and fractal dimensions of crushed gangue were systematically analyzed, and the cause of the qualitative transformation in slurries around 100 mesh was discussed. The results show that: (1) At 6 MPa, the backfill specimens reach 83%–88% of their maximum strain, which can be regarded as the structural yield critical point of this material. (2) The maximum strain depends solely on the post-filling porosity and is inversely proportional to both the slurry concentration and the mesh size (i.e., finer particles lead to lower strain). (3) The backfill strength is influenced by multiple interacting factors; generally, finer particle sizes and higher concentrations yield higher strength. However, when the concentration exceeds 50%, slurries with particle sizes larger than 100 mesh suffer from reduced fluidity, resulting in decreased backfill strength. (4) The 100 mesh threshold represents the point of qualitative change in gangue powder slurries. The underlying mechanism is the competition between force fields: as particle size decreases, the gravitational force on particles diminishes following a power-law, while short-range repulsive forces increase exponentially. Research on homologous material systems is an essential step toward addressing increasingly complex backfill environments and provides valuable guidance for backfill scheme selection and strength prediction.

Introduction

With the advancement of green mining, grouting backfill has become an important means of mitigating surface subsidence and preventing secondary disasters. Moreover, coal gangue, as a bulk industrial solid waste, can cause waste of land resources and environmental damage if stockpiled on the surface for extended periods. Homologous material system grouting can simultaneously meet the requirements for backfill strength and green utilization of solid wastes, offering a significant cost advantage. Taking gangue powder grouting as an example, its cost is approximately one-fifth that of cement-based grouting backfill and one-thirty-fifth that of chemical backfill1. Furthermore, the use of gangue powder as a backfill material not only alleviates environmental pollution caused by coal gangue accumulation on the surface but also realizes the cyclic utilization of mine solid wastes. As gangue powder and loose gangue aggregates constitute a homologous system, they exhibit superior chemical compatibility and synergistic deformation capacity. Research on homologous material backfill systems is therefore closely related to the economic interests of enterprises and plays a vital role in protecting the natural environment and safeguarding people’s lives and property.

Internationally, scholars have conducted extensive research on grouting backfill. From the perspective of backfill materials, grouting backfill can be primarily classified into chemical material backfill, cement-based material backfill, and uncemented backfill, distinguished according to the source of backfill strength. Regarding chemical backfill, Wang Dongxing et al.2 and Fernández-Jiménez et al.3 investigated the microscopic mechanisms of alkali-activated fly ash, establishing chemical models and solidification mechanisms under various alkali-activation methods. Duxson et al.4 approached geopolymerization reactions to investigate the chemical structural characteristics of geological polymerization products with multiple materials. Regarding cement-based backfill, Sun Yu et al.5 focused on the damage evolution, failure modes, and size effects of cylindrical cemented backfill specimens, providing a basis for backfill parameters in experimental studies. Wang Jun et al.6 and Yang Ke et al.7 employed machine learning combined with acoustic emission systems to investigate the influence of fly ash proportion, offering references for predicting cemented body failure. Wei Zhenxin et al.8 conducted confined compression tests to study the influence of different curing periods on goaf roof control. Regarding uncemented backfill, as no chemical reaction contributes to strength generation, research emphasis has shifted to factors such as gangue gradation, fractal dimension, and aggregate particle size. Li Wei et al.9, Liu Xiangfeng et al.10, Wu Hao et al.11, and Zhang et al.12 investigated the influence of gangue aggregate gradation in confined compression tests, obtaining conclusions regarding particle distribution characteristics, fractal dimensions, and energy evolution under various conditions. Additionally, Dong et al.13 utilized nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) technology to scan the pore structure of coal gangue concrete, elucidating the strong correlation between porosity and specimen compressive strength from a mesoscopic perspective. Zhang Aiqing et al.14 established a mechanical model for the water head height of uncemented backfill bodies in segmented cemented backfill methods and derived theoretical solutions. Wan Ze’en15 selected low-cement, high-aggregate inert slurries to study synchronous grouting in shield tunneling through water-rich strata, thereby satisfying on-site construction requirements. Wang Yongfu et al.16 employed the steady-state permeability method to conduct industrial–academic–research collaborations and seepage experiments on four different gangue gradations, obtaining important parameters such as porosity, fractal dimension, and Euler number.

Current mainstream research focuses on multi-source material backfill, such as grouting backfill using cement as the binding agent, gangue powder as the aggregate, and fly ash as the supplementary material. Compared with pure chemical backfill and uncemented backfill, the aforementioned schemes balance operational cost and backfill strength. However, for mining areas that consider only solid waste utilization, pure gangue powder homologous material backfill systems combined with an underground “mining–beneficiation–backfill” system can achieve greater economic benefits. Nevertheless, existing research has primarily addressed the pre-filling crushing characteristics of loose gangue, while studies on the post-grouting failure characteristics of gangue powder in caving zones remain scarce. Therefore, investigating the influencing factors of homologous material backfill systems under confined compression is of paramount importance.

Experimental programme

Test materials

The coal gangue used in this study was sourced from a top coal caving face at a mine in Linfen City, Shanxi Province, China. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed to determine the mineralogical composition of the coal gangue, and the XRD pattern is shown in Fig. 1. The principal mineral constituents are quartz, kaolinite, and potassium feldspar, accounting for 63.66%, 20.13%, and 7.49% of the total composition, respectively. The analysis results indicate that this material is a typical high-silica–alumina gangue. Owing to the extremely high hardness and compressive strength of quartz, it can serve as a “load-bearing skeleton” within the backfill matrix. Meanwhile, the high content of kaolinite (a clay mineral) can produce excellent suspension and lubrication properties after being ground into powder17.

Test apparatus

To simultaneously satisfy the requirements of grouting backfill and confined compression testing, a specially designed integrated “filling–curing–loading” apparatus was fabricated. To ensure equipment stability, the circular steel cylinder (Q235 steel) has internal dimensions of Φ125 × H180 mm, fixed with upper and lower double-layer circular bands. The outer wall features a 2.5° taper design; as the axial loading stress increases, the friction between the circular bands and the cylinder wall progressively increases, maintaining the stability of the steel cylinder. To simulate water seepage in caving zones, 2 mm diameter holes were reserved at the bottom of the cylinder, and a nanoscale polypropylene fiber (PP cotton) filter element was placed on the apparatus base18. This design ensures that excess water exuded during the specimen curing period drains through the base holes, while aggregates and reaction products are retained within the specimen pores. To overcome the drawback of conventional integral circular steel cylinders in which internal aggregates are difficult to remove after confined compression testing, the test mold was designed as two semi-cylindrical halves that close along a vertical seam. By disassembling the external tightening bands, the post-loading state of the internal specimen can be fully revealed. An MTS 816 servohydraulic testing machine was employed as the confined compression loading platform. A DS5-16B multi-channel full-information acoustic emission analyzer manufactured by Soft Island Technology Co., Ltd. was adopted, with four sensors distributed evenly around the steel cylinder. The signal sampling frequency of the acoustic emission system was set to 3 MHz, and the signal recording threshold was set to 80 mV.

Test procedure

The homologous material system is an emerging academic concept in recent years. It refers to a green backfill system in which mine-derived associated solid wastes are used in situ to prepare filling materials during underground backfilling, with minimal or no introduction of exogenous materials, thereby realizing a closed-loop “mining–beneficiation–backfill” paradigm during extraction operations18. Taking gangue powder grouting as an example, the factors influencing backfill effectiveness primarily include the mass concentration of the slurry, the strength and rheological properties of the gangue powder, and the particle size and fineness of the aggregate. Therefore, the experimental design comprised a comprehensive matrix of concentration and particle size. The concentration factor was set at four levels: 35%, 40%, 45%, and 50%. The particle size factor was set at three levels: 40–70 mesh, 70–100 mesh, and 100–130 mesh. As shown in Table 1, a total of 12 test groups (S1–S12) were arranged.

Considering size effects and slurry fluidity19,20, the gangue aggregate adopted a gap gradation of 5–25 mm. Prior to filling, the apparatus was fully assembled, and the nanoscale filter element was placed at the bottom; loose gangue aggregate was then uniformly poured in until the apparatus was filled to the top. To ensure identical slurry filling volumes, mechanical grease was first applied to seal the base holes before pouring the slurry. The mold was then placed on a vibrating table to remove internal air until no bubbles emerged. After filling, the mechanical grease at the base was removed to allow excess water to permeate out, and the specimens were cured for 21 days to achieve their long-term strength. During confined compression, acoustic emission sensors were first installed around the cylinder, and displacement-controlled loading was applied at a rate of 5 mm/min until the stress reached 10 MPa. Finally, the stress–strain curves, acoustic emission wave signals during loading, and the post-test gangue crushing conditions were collected and analyzed.

Experimental results and analysis

Compression characteristics of homologous material backfill

Under the homologous material system employing gangue powder as the backfill material, the specimens were loaded at a constant rate to 10 MPa, after which the specimens entered a stable stage. The characteristic of this stage is that the axial strain remains almost invariant as the axial stress progressively increases. Figure 3 presents the stress–strain curves of the specimens backfilled with slurries of different particle sizes. Taking Fig. a (40–70 mesh) as an example, the strain rises rapidly before 6 MPa, and after 6 MPa the strain remains essentially constant at approximately 0.25. At this stage, the specimen has been fully compacted, representing the structural yield critical point. Because the specimen is fully compacted, the strain is directly proportional to the volume of internal voids and inversely proportional to the volume of the backfill aggregate. The patterns shown in the figure reveal that, for slurries of identical particle size, higher concentrations maintain lower maximum strain levels on the stress–strain curves; as illustrated in Fig. 3, the maximum strain follows the order 35% concentration > 40% concentration > 45% concentration > 50% concentration. Integrating the data from Fig. 3a and b, and 3c, the average strain for each group follows the order: 40–70 mesh > 70–100 mesh > 100–130 mesh. In summary, when the specimen reaches the stable stage, the axial strain under confined compression is inversely proportional to both the slurry concentration and the mesh size (i.e., finer particles lead to lower strain) and directly proportional to the specimen porosity.

Acoustic emission response characteristics of homologous material backfill

The acoustic emission (AE) response characteristics of each specimen during confined compression are shown in Fig. 4. The green curves represent ring-down counts, and the red curves represent the cumulative energy received by the sensors. A rise in ring-down counts is typically accompanied by a sharp increase in cumulative energy, indicating the generation of internal fracture within the specimen6,21. It is noteworthy that the peak value of the ring-down count reflects the concentrated effect of fracturing; when the ring-down count of a specimen exhibits its initial peak, it can be understood as the critical point at which the backfill body transitions from displacement reorganization or elastic deformation to plastic failure6,17.

Observing backfill specimens of a single particle size, it was found that both the average ring-down count and the peak ring-down count increase with increasing concentration. Taking specimens S1–S4 of the 40–70 mesh particle size as an example, the peak ring-down counts were 45 at 35% concentration, 151 at 40% concentration, 178 at 45% concentration, and 262 at 50% concentration; other groups also generally follow this trend. The underlying reason is that, as the concentration increases, the number of particles in the voids increases, leading to a greater number of particle contact points under pressure. Consequently, multiple microseismic sources are simultaneously activated during loading, manifesting as an overall increase in ring-down counts.

Observing backfill specimens of different particle sizes, it was found that, for the same concentration, the initial peak of the ring-down count progressively delays as the particle size decreases. Taking specimens S1, S5, and S9 at the same 35% concentration as examples: specimen S1 (40–70 mesh) exhibited a uniform distribution of ring-down counts, and the cumulative energy showed a small-amplitude, stepwise increase22. Specimen S5 (70–100 mesh) exhibited its peak at approximately 47% of the total loading time, with cumulative energy rising from approximately 10,000 mV·ms to 15,000 mV·ms. Similarly, specimen S9 (100–130 mesh) exhibited its peak at approximately 80% of the total loading time, with cumulative energy rising from approximately 5500 mV·ms to 12,000 mV·ms. On the one hand, as the particle size decreases, the number of particles per unit concentration increases, making it easier to form high-density network force chains that collectively support the specimen during the initial stable stage. On the other hand, finer slurries possess better fluidity, which can prevent structural weaknesses at the specimen edges caused by insufficient filling.

Spatial fracture characteristics of homologous material backfill

In this study, the acoustic emission spatial positioning system developed by Soft Island Technology was employed. Data were received using four evenly distributed acoustic wave sensors, and the spatial positions of fracture points were calculated via an exhaustive method. To obtain the most accurate values possible, we first performed pencil‑lead break tests on the backfill specimens to determine the wave velocity. Subsequently, the curing molds were completely filled during the experiment to minimize the formation of large voids.It is noteworthy that, because the apparatus base was designed with open holes and excess water drained out after curing, certain voids inevitably existed between the gangue aggregates and between the gangue and particles. Consequently, the specimen as a whole was in a non-uniform state. When the system back-calculates the spatial position of fracture points using a fixed wave velocity, experimental errors are unavoidable; however, this study does not require completely precise positioning but rather aims to investigate the morphological characteristics of fractures from a macroscopic perspective.

Figure 5 shows the spatial fracture point localization maps of the grouted specimens during confined compression. The center of each sphere represents the spatial position of a fracture point, the sphere size maps the magnitude of fracture energy (larger spheres indicate greater energy), and the sphere color maps the temporal sequence of fracture occurrence (colors gradually transition from red to blue as time progresses). First, analyzing the number of fracture points under different concentrations: as the concentration increased from 35% to 50%, the numbers of fracture points for specimens S1–S4 were 35, 27, 22, and 35, respectively; for specimens S5–S8, the numbers were 119, 52, 31, and 50; for specimens S9–S12, the numbers were 80, 39, 16, and 14. Analysis shows that, for specimens filled with 40–70 mesh and 70–100 mesh slurries, the number of fracture points exhibits a trend of first decreasing and then increasing, with the inflection point occurring between 45% and 50% concentration. In contrast, specimens filled with 100–130 mesh slurry exhibit a continuous decreasing trend. Combined with the test procedure, the reason for this difference can be reasonably attributed to slurry fluidity: 40–100 mesh slurries have poor fluidity, and when the concentration is increased to 50%, the slurry cannot flow into all voids, resulting in uneven filling and consequently reduced backfill strength and an increased number of fracture points23.

To characterize the staged fracture behavior of the backfill during confined compression, two concepts are introduced based on the structural yield critical point (6 MPa) identified from the stress–strain curves. The stage before 6 MPa, during which the specimen primarily undergoes slip reorganization of gangue aggregates, is defined as the initial bearing capacity stage. The stage after 6 MPa, dominated by crushing reorganization and the formation of a dense interlocking skeleton, is defined as the ultimate structural strength stage. As shown in Fig. 5, the AE source localization spheres are color-coded by time (red: early; blue: late) and scaled by energy. For 40–100 mesh backfills, large spheres are typically blue (ultimate stage), while red spheres are generally small and represent low‑energy events. This indicates that major crushing predominantly occurs after the aggregates have reorganized. In contrast, for slurries finer than 100 mesh, large spheres appear in red. This shift arises because the extremely fine gangue particles cannot provide sufficient initial bearing capacity; instead, they act as a lubricant that facilitates aggregate rearrangement. Consequently, the reorganization time is shortened, and the high‑energy crushing events that normally belong to the ultimate stage are advanced to the initial stage. Fracture points directly attributable to gangue powder particles are largely absent, and the overall fracture process is accelerated21.

Fractal dimension characteristics of crushed specimens under gangue powder backfill

Unlike the fractal dimension of loose gangue aggregates after crushing, the fractal dimension after grouting backfill requires more complex treatment. First, considering size effects, overly fine particles would severely affect slurry fluidity; therefore, the gangue aggregate could not adopt the Talbot gradation and instead adopted a hierarchical gradation excluding the 0–5 mm fraction. Second, after confined compression, powdered particles adhere to the surfaces of crushed gangue, introducing errors in the measured particle sizes of the crushed gangue. In summary, for particle sizes above 100 mesh, slurry fluidity is significantly affected by concentration, and backfill strength decreases when the concentration exceeds 50%. However, for slurries with particle sizes below 100 mesh, excellent fluidity is maintained, and backfill strength increases steadily with increasing slurry concentration26.

Discussion

The above experiments investigated the influence of gangue powder grouting backfill on the crushing of loose aggregates from the perspectives of concentration and particle size. The results reveal that when the gangue powder particle size is smaller than 100 mesh, the properties of the slurry undergo a qualitative transformation27. Regarding the stress–strain curves, the final strain of backfill specimens below 100 mesh exhibits a significant increase. From the perspective of acoustic emission signal response, the fracture stage of backfill specimens below 100 mesh is delayed. Considering both spatial localization and fractal dimension, the strength evolution of backfill specimens below 100 mesh exhibits altered regularity, with specimen strength continuing to increase as the concentration rises to 50%. This qualitative transformation does not originate from a single factor but encompasses multi-scale, multi-perspective influences. Its core lies in the fact that when particles are smaller than a certain size, their corresponding stress state, motion logic, and physical properties all undergo qualitative changes28.

When the particle diameter d is at the 100 mesh threshold (0.15 mm), the gravitational force on the particle decreases following a power-law relationship, whereas the repulsive force between particles increases exponentially, sufficient to maintain transient stability of the slurry.

At this point, the slurry transforms from a “mixture of water and aggregate particles” into a “continuous colloidal substance”; macroscopically, this manifests as “water-retaining suspension”28.

Conclusions

Homologous material backfill is a system that primarily utilizes solid wastes from the same mine stratum, employing virtually no exogenous materials, resulting in relatively low backfill strength but also low backfill cost. Moreover, the backfill material and the backfill zone share identical physical properties, such as elastic modulus, Poisson’s ratio, and thermal expansion coefficient, thereby ensuring the long-term stability of the backfill body. This study employed gangue powder slurries of various particle sizes and concentrations, and conducted confined compression experiments on the backfill specimens. The following conclusions were drawn:

Under in-situ grouting backfill with gangue powder as the primary material, the specimens reach 83%–88% of maximum strain at a stress of 6 MPa, and 92%–96% at 8 MPa; therefore, the structural yield critical point of this material can be considered to be at 6 MPa. For slurries with particle sizes above 100 mesh, when the concentration reaches 50%, the backfill strength instead decreases.
