Changes in daily living dependency and incident depressive symptoms among older individuals: findings from four prospective cohort studies
Authors: Juxiang Yang, Gang Song, Minheng Zhang, Hongwei Liu, Miaomiao Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith rapid population ageing, depressive symptoms in older adults have become a pressing public health concern. While functional dependency is a known risk factor, the impact of changes in dependency over time remains unclear.ObjectiveTo examine the association between changes in daily living dependency and incident depressive symptoms in older adults across international cohorts.MethodsWe used data from 46 327 adults aged ≥50 years across four longitudinal ageing studies: China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (China), Health and Retirement Study (USA), English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (England) and Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (Europe). Daily living dependency was classified into three levels based on difficulties in activities of daily living (ADLs) and instrumental ADLs (IADLs). Change in dependency was assessed using baseline and 2-year follow-up data. Depressive symptoms were measured using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies depressive symptoms Scale or the European Depression Scales (EURO-D). Cox proportional hazard models estimated HRs and 95% CIs for incident depressive symptoms over a median follow-up of 4.2–5.1 years.FindingsA total of 12 902 new depressive symptom cases occurred during follow-up. Compared with participants whose dependency status remained unchanged, those who recovered to independency had a significantly reduced risk of depressive symptoms. Functional deterioration, including transitions from independency to ADL or IADL dependency, was associated with increased risk of depressive symptoms (both pooled HRs 1.55), while functional improvement, from ADL or IADL dependency to independency, was linked to reduced risk (HRs 0.83 and 0.80, respectively).ConclusionsImprovement in ADL dependency is linked to a lower risk of depressive symptoms, while worsening dependency significantly increases depressive symptoms risk.Clinical implicationsRoutine assessment of functional status and early interventions to maintain or restore daily living independency may help prevent depressive symptoms in older adults. Targeted rehabilitation and support services could play a key role in reducing the mental health burden of ageing populations.Keywords: Depression

Background

The global population is ageing at an unprecedented rate, with the proportion of individuals aged 60 and older expected to double by 2050. However, longevity does not guarantee good health.1 Depression, affecting approximately 300 million people worldwide,2 is a leading cause of disability and poses serious health risks for older adults. It not only reduces quality of life but also increases the risk of chronic diseases and mortality.3 As population ageing accelerates, addressing the risk factors for late-life depression is essential for promoting healthy ageing.4

Physical and mental health in later life are closely intertwined, with functional independency playing a critical role in psychological well-being.5 Independency supports autonomy, social engagement and a sense of purpose, while functional decline can lead to helplessness, frustration and diminished self-worth.6 Difficulties with basic self-care (eg, bathing, dressing) contribute to emotional distress, whereas instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) impairments (eg, managing finances, shopping) can limit social participation and increase loneliness.7 Cognitive and emotional pathways further mediate this relationship, as declining physical function can reinforce negative self-perceptions and perpetuate psychological decline.8 Additionally, reliance on caregivers, while necessary, may induce feelings of burden and loss of control, further elevating depressive symptoms risk. Conversely, regaining independency may enhance self-efficacy and resilience, promoting mental well-being.9 Despite this critical relationship, few studies have explored how changes in daily living dependency—both deterioration and recovery—impact depressive symptoms risk, as most research has focused on static associations rather than the dynamic nature of functional decline.

Objective

To fill the critical gap in understanding how functional transitions influence mental health in later life, we conducted a multinational, prospective cohort study using harmonised data from four large-scale ageing studies: the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), and the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE).

Unlike prior studies that relied on static assessments of daily living dependency, our research focused on dynamic changes in functional status—capturing both recovery to independency and progression to greater dependency—and examined their associations with the onset of depressive symptoms. By leveraging longitudinal data from over 46 000 older adults, this study offers robust cross-national evidence on the mental health implications of functional decline and recovery, providing novel insights into inform prevention and intervention strategies for late-life depression.

Methods

Study design and participants

We utilised data from four ageing cohort studies: the CHARLS, the HRS, the ELSA and the SHARE. We extracted data from the most recent five waves (2010–2020) for each cohort. The baseline waves included: CHARLS wave 1 (2011), HRS wave 11 (2012), ELSA wave 5 (2010–2011) and SHARE wave 4 (2011). Subsequent follow-up surveys were used to track outcomes until the latest available waves: CHARLS wave 5 (2020), HRS wave 15 (2020), ELSA wave 9 (2018–2019) and SHARE wave 8 (2020). As illustrated in figure 1, we applied the following exclusion criteria: (1) <50 years old; (2) participants with missing data on daily living dependency at baseline or in the second survey; (3) participants with depressive symptoms at baseline and the second survey or no follow-up data. After applying these criteria, a total of 46 327 participants were included in the final analysis (CHARLS: 6957 participants, HRS: 10 683 participants, ELSA: 5998 participants, SHARE: 22 689 participants).

Variables

Depressive symptoms

Each cohort assessed depressive symptoms using different scales: CHARLS employed the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies depressive symptoms Scale (CES-D10), HRS and ELSA used the 8-item CES-D (CSE-D8) and SHARE applied the 12-item EURO-D scale. Although CES-D and EURO-D differ in structure, previous studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between these measures.10

The CES-D scale is widely used as a rapid screening tool for depressive symptoms in older adults, demonstrating high reliability and validity. The recommended cut-off points for identifying clinically significant depressive symptoms are: CES-D8 ≥3, CES-D10 ≥10. The EURO-D scale, designed for cross-country comparisons in Europe, has been validated for internal consistency and external validity (with a cut-off score of ≥4).11 12

Daily living dependency and dynamic nature

Daily living dependency was defined as a participant’s inability to perform the basic ADLs and IADLs on their own.13 ADLs represent the essential physical tasks required for daily functioning. In HRS, ELSA and SHARE, these included: dressing, bathing, eating, getting in and out of bed, using the toilet and walking inside a room. However, in CHARLS, “walking inside a room” was not assessed; instead, controlling urination and defecation was included. IADLs involve more complex tasks necessary for independent living. In this study, these included: housework, cooking, shopping, managing finances and taking medication. Across all cohorts, ADLs included six activities, and IADLs included five activities.

Following prior research,13 participants were categorised into three daily living dependency levels: (a) independency (no reported difficulties in either ADLs or IADLs); (b) IADL dependency (having any difficulty with five IADL items but no difficulty with ADLs); (c) ADL dependency (having any difficulty with six ADL items). To clarify this concept for readers, we present figure 2, which illustrates the baseline distribution of daily living dependency across cohorts.

To characterise the dynamic nature of functional status, we examined transitions in daily living dependency levels between baseline and the second survey. As shown in figure 3, participants were stratified into three baseline groups based on their initial level of dependency, with each group encompassing three distinct transition trajectories: group I (baseline: ADL dependency): stable ADL dependency, ADL dependency to independency and ADL dependency to IADL dependency. Other variables

Additional covariates included demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, current employment status and chronic disease status. Demographic variables comprised age, gender (male or female), marital status (married or partnered vs single) and harmonised educational attainment categorised as below high school, high school or college and above. For sensitivity analysis 3, we included additional non-harmonised sociodemographic variables specific to each cohort. In CHARLS, Hukou status (urban vs rural) and ethnicity (Han vs non-Han) were considered. HRS included race and residential location (urban, suburban, ex-urban). ELSA included race (white vs non-white). SHARE included country and household location (urban vs rural). Lifestyle factors included engagement in physical activity (vigorous or moderate, weekly), current drinking (yes or no) and smoking status (never, former or current smoker). Information on chronic conditions was obtained from self-reports in response to the question, ‘Has a doctor ever told you that you have any of the following conditions?’ To ensure cross-national comparability, we focused on seven common chronic diseases consistently assessed across the four cohorts: hypertension, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, heart disease, stroke, psychiatric disorder and arthritis.

Following previous cross-national studies that addressed missing covariate data using multiple imputation methods,14 we performed multiple imputation by chained equations using the mice package in R (V.4.3.2). The imputation model incorporated all relevant covariates and the outcome and exposure variables. Twenty imputed datasets were created using predictive mean matching, and results were combined using Rubin’s rules. Participants with missing data on exposure or outcome were excluded prior to imputation.

Statistical analysis

For descriptive statistics: normally distributed continuous variables were reported as Mean±SD, with t-tests used for comparisons. Categorical variables were expressed as n (%), with χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests used for group comparisons. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For each participant, follow-up time was calculated from the second survey (ie, after the assessment of changes in daily living dependency) to the first report of depressive symptoms. Participants who did not develop depressive symptoms were censored at the date of their last available follow-up. We used Cox proportional hazards models to estimate HRs and 95% CIs, assessing the association between changes in daily living dependency (from baseline to the second survey) and the subsequent risk of incident depressive symptoms.

In addition, following prior studies,15 we conducted pooled analyses by combining individual-level data from all four cohorts. This approach allowed us to maximise statistical power, ensure consistent covariate adjustment and evaluate overall associations across diverse populations. Cluster-robust SEs were used to account for within-study correlation.

To test the robustness of our findings, we performed three sensitivity analyses. First, we redefined ADL using five core items common to all cohorts and repeated the Cox proportional hazards models. Second, we combined ADL and IADL into four levels and derived 16 transition types. Due to limited cases in some groups, this analysis was conducted only in the pooled sample using Cox proportional hazards models. Third, we repeated fully adjusted models within each cohort adding cohort-specific social variables to check for residual confounding.

All statistical tests were two-sided, with p<0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R (V.4.3.2; www.r-project.org).

Findings

Baseline characteristics of the study population

This study utilised data from four ageing cohorts: the CHARLS, the HRS, the ELSA and the SHARE. Across five waves (2010–2020), the median follow-up duration was 4.2 years for CHARLS, 5.1 years for HRS, 5.1 years for ELSA and 4.5 years for SHARE. Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 46 327 participants aged 50 years and older were included, with 12 902 incident cases of depressive symptoms recorded. Specifically, the final analysis included 6957 participants from CHARLS (female: 45.5%, mean age: 61.7 years), 10 683 from HRS (female: 58.7%, mean age: 65.5 years), 5998 from ELSA (female: 51.8%, mean age: 65.9 years) and 22 689 from SHARE (female: 51.9%, mean age: 64.9 years). Baseline characteristics and daily living dependency status are presented in online supplemental Table 1) and figure 2. A similar trend was observed across cohorts: individuals who developed depressive symptoms during follow-up were more likely to be female, have a lower level of education, abstain from alcohol consumption and exhibit dependency in ADL.

Association between changes in daily living dependency and the risk of incident depressive symptoms

Online supplemental table 2 presents the association between changes in daily living dependency and the risk of incident depressive symptoms across four longitudinal cohorts: CHARLS, HRS, ELSA and SHARE. Across all four cohorts and in the pooled analysis, transitions from independency to functional dependency were consistently associated with an increased risk of incident depressive symptoms.

Compared with individuals who remained independent, those who developed IADL limitations had significantly higher risks of depressive symptoms (pooled HR 1.55, 95% CI 1.42 to 1.69), as did those who progressed to ADL dependency (pooled HR 1.55, 95% CI 1.44 to 1.68). These associations were consistent across CHARLS, HRS, ELSA and SHARE, with the largest effect sizes observed in ELSA (independency to IADL: HR 1.73, 95% CI 1.30 to 2.30; independency to ADL: HR 1.78, 95% CI 1.42 to 2.23).

In contrast, improvement in functional status was associated with reduced depression risk. Older adults who recovered from ADL dependency to independency had a significantly lower risk of developing depressive symptoms (pooled HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.75 to 0.92), with consistent protective effects observed in CHARLS, ELSA and SHARE. Recovery from IADL dependency to independency also significantly reduced the risk of depressive symptoms (pooled HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.69 to 0.93), with statistically significant associations found in all four cohorts.

The pooled HRs suggest that changes in daily living dependency have clinically meaningful implications for depression risk. Regaining independency from ADL or IADL limitations was associated with a 17%–20% relative risk reduction in depressive symptoms, corresponding to an estimated absolute risk reduction of approximately 6–8 percentage points. Conversely, the onset of IADL or ADL dependency led to a 55% higher relative risk (HR 1.55), equating to an average absolute risk increase of 11–12 percentage points. These findings indicate that for every 100 older adults who lose functional independency, approximately 11 additional cases of depression may occur, while 6–8 cases might be prevented for every 100 who regain independency.

These findings underscore the mental health impact of physical functioning trajectories in later life. Preventing functional decline and promoting recovery may yield meaningful reductions in depression burden among ageing populations.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the results, three additional sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, we harmonised the definition of ADL dependency using five core items common across cohorts (dressing, bathing, eating, getting in and out of bed and using the toilet). Results remained consistent: functional deterioration was associated with increased risk of incident depressive symptoms, while recovery to independency was linked to reduced risk (online supplemental table 3).

Second, we reclassified functional status into four levels by combining ADL and IADL dependency, generating 16 transition patterns. This analysis, restricted to the pooled dataset, showed a graded association: transitions to more severe dependency, especially combined ADL+IADL dependency, had the highest risk (HR=1.85), while improvements—particularly from combined dependency to independency—were linked to reduced risk (figure 4). These findings further support the impact of functional change on depressive symptom risk.

Sensitivity analysis 2. ADL, activities of daily living; IADL, instrumental ADL.

Third, to account for potential residual confounding by cohort-specific social determinants, we repeated the fully adjusted models within each cohort including additional variables unique to that study: CHARLS, Hukou status and ethnicity; HRS, race and residential location; ELSA, race; SHARE, country and household location. Results were essentially unchanged, with effect estimates remaining consistent in direction and magnitude compared with the main analyses (online supplemental table 4).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first multinational cohort study to evaluate the dynamic changes in daily living dependency and their association with incident depressive symptoms among older adults. We found that individuals transitioning from independency to dependency exhibited a significantly increased risk of depressive symptoms, while those who regained independency had a reduced risk. These findings were consistent across four major ageing cohorts, underscoring the critical importance of maintaining or restoring functional independency to mitigate mental health risks in later life. A key strength of this study is its multicohort design, which integrates harmonised longitudinal data from four large-scale ageing studies across. This approach enhances generalisability across diverse sociocultural and healthcare contexts, increases statistical power for estimating risks associated with less common functional transitions. In the context of global population ageing, a multicohort framework provides a stronger and more representative basis for identifying intervention targets that are relevant across settings while also accounting for local variation.

As global ageing trends continue, the prevalence of depressive symptoms among older adults is rising, imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems and representing a growing public health concern.16 The strong link between mental and physical health has been well documented,17 and prior studies have shown that ADL and IADL impairments are highly correlated with depressive symptoms in older adults. Yang et al employed a random forest model to identify key predictors of depressive symptoms in cross-sectional data from CHARLS and HRS, ranking ADL and IADL among the top five factors associated with depressive symptoms.5 Wang et al further found that functional disability increases the risk of depressive symptoms, particularly when IADL functions are impaired.7 However, these studies relied on cross-sectional analyses or static measures of functional dependency, failing to capture the dynamic nature of functional changes over time. By integrating longitudinal data and assessing changes in dependency status, our study provides novel evidence that both maintaining and regaining independency are associated with a lower risk of depressive symptoms. Several pathways may explain the observed association between functional transitions and depressive symptoms. From a biological perspective, loss of physical function can trigger chronic systemic inflammation, characterised by elevated proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), which have been widely linked to depression pathophysiology.18 Additionally, functional impairment may dysregulate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, resulting in abnormal cortisol secretion patterns that contribute to mood disturbances and depressive symptoms.19 Increased oxidative stress and neuroendocrine imbalances associated with physical decline may further disrupt neurotransmitter systems involved in mood regulation, such as serotonin and dopamine pathways.20 Psychosocial mechanisms also play a crucial role in this relationship. Declines in daily functioning often lead to reduced autonomy and loss of independency, which can cause feelings of helplessness, low self-esteem and diminished sense of purpose.21 The consequent limitation in social participation, including inability to engage in previously enjoyed activities or maintain social networks, may exacerbate social isolation and loneliness.22 Moreover, increased dependence on caregivers can generate psychological strain for both parties, leading to caregiver burden and increased interpersonal stress, which further impacts mental health.23 Together, these biological and psychosocial pathways likely interact in complex ways to increase vulnerability to depressive symptoms. Conversely, regaining independency may enhance self-efficacy, encourage greater engagement in social and physical activities and reduce feelings of helplessness, thereby lowering depressive symptoms risk.

A critical challenge in evaluating the relationship between ADL/IADL and depressive symptoms is the potential for bidirectional causality. On one hand, depressive symptoms can reduce motivation and energy levels, making it more difficult for older adults to perform daily activities.24 On the other hand, functional limitations may lead to depressive symptoms, creating a vicious cycle that exacerbates health deterioration and reduces quality of life.25 To mitigate the risk of reverse causation, we excluded participants with baseline depressive symptoms from our primary analyses. Furthermore, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding individuals who developed depressive symptoms during the period of dependency change and redefined the second survey as the new baseline, further minimising the risk of reverse causality. The consistency of our findings across different cohorts reinforces the robustness of these associations and highlights the importance of interventions aimed at maintaining or restoring functional independency as a strategy for depressive symptoms prevention in older adults.

Our study has several strengths. First, we leveraged four internationally representative ageing cohorts, enhancing the generalisability of our findings across diverse cultural and healthcare contexts. This cross-national, multicohort approach provides a comprehensive understanding of the relationship between functional dependency and depressive symptoms risk across different social and medical systems. Second, we innovatively explored the dynamic changes in ADL status—specifically, recovery or deterioration—and their impact on depressive symptoms risk, addressing a key gap in prior research. By moving beyond traditional cross-sectional analyses, our study provides new evidence supporting the mental health benefits of functional recovery.

Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, depressive symptoms were assessed using different instruments across cohorts, which may have introduced measurement variation; however, cohort-specific analyses helped to reduce this risk. Second, functional transitions may have continued beyond the third wave, potentially leading to underestimation of long-term associations. Third, although we adjusted for a broad set of covariates, key psychosocial factors such as cognitive function, personality traits, social networks and participation in social activities were not consistently measured across cohorts. These factors are likely to influence both changes in functional status and depressive symptoms, and their omission may have introduced residual confounding, potentially biasing the observed associations. Fourth, due to study design constraints, sample weights were not applied, limiting national representativeness; nonetheless, the inclusion of four large international cohorts improves the external validity of our findings. Finally, attrition due to death or severe illness, common in older populations, may have introduced selection bias and led to underestimation of the mental health burden associated with functional decline.

Our findings have significant implications in the broader context of global ageing and the pursuit of healthy ageing. As life expectancy continues to rise, ensuring not only longevity but also quality of life has become a critical public health priority. The strong association between changes in ADL dependency and depressive symptoms risk underscores the urgent need for policies and interventions that support functional maintenance and recovery in older adults. By promoting strategies that facilitate independency, societies can enhance the well-being of ageing populations, reduce the burden of late-life depressive symptoms and contribute to the overarching goal of ageing with dignity and resilience.

Clinical implications

Dynamic changes in dependency in daily living are closely associated with depressive symptoms risk in older adults. Recovery from dependency to an independent state significantly reduces the risk, whereas the transition from independency to dependency markedly increases it. Reversing dependency is as crucial as preserving independency in daily living, both of which can effectively mitigate depressive symptoms risk among the elderly. These findings highlight the critical importance of public health interventions aimed at maintaining and restoring functional independence among older adults. By focusing on strategies that promote recovery from dependency and prevent deterioration in daily living activities, such interventions can effectively reduce the risk of depressive symptoms. Implementing community-based rehabilitation programmes, enhancing access to supportive services and promoting physical and social activities are practical approaches to preserving independence and improving mental health outcomes. Given the increasing ageing population and the significant burden of depression, prioritising functional independence in public health policy can contribute to reducing depression prevalence, enhancing quality of life and decreasing healthcare costs associated with mental health conditions. Our study provides strong evidence to guide the development of targeted interventions that support healthy ageing and address the growing challenge of late-life depression on a population level.
