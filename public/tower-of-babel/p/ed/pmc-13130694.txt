Targeting TPX2-dependent lineage plasticity by CDK4/6 inhibition reverses therapy resistance in neuroendocrine bladder carcinoma
Authors: Zehua Chen, Shengmeng Peng, Shi Fu, Shizhi Lin, Yiwen Su, Xinxin He, Yangjie Zhang, Junlin Lu, Sen Liu, Hengji Zhan, Jianmei Zhong, Baolin Ye, Chengjunyu Zhang, Chu Liu, Bolin Pan, Haonan Dong, Hongqiong Li, Xinni Ye, Jun Wang, Siting Chen, Baoqing He, Jingdie Wu, Hui Li, Qiang Zhang, Haifeng Wang, Xu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNeuroendocrine bladder carcinoma (NEBC) is an aggressive and therapy-resistant cancer with poor prognosis. Although lineage plasticity drives urothelial-to-neuroendocrine transdifferentiation, intermediate states remain poorly characterized. Through transcriptomic profiling of public datasets, we define a lineage plasticity-associated signature in bladder cancer. We analyze 64,756 in-house and 201,720 public single-cell transcriptomes from NEBC and urothelial carcinoma (UC), identifying TPX2high bladder cancer cell subpopulation with increased lineage plasticity, supported by multi-cohort histological validation. TPX2high cells lack canonical neuroendocrine markers and are associated with adverse clinical outcomes. TPX2high cells exhibit dysregulated cell cycle progression and occupy a suppressive niche associated with CD8+ T cell exhaustion. In vitro and in vivo, TPX2 promotes lineage plasticity and induces T cell exhaustion. In preclinical patient-derived organoids (PDOs) and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, CDK4/6 inhibition plus immune checkpoint blockade demonstrates potent antitumor efficacy. Overall, our findings suggest this combination therapy as a promising therapeutic strategy for TPX2high and NEBC patients.

Introduction

Neuroendocrine (NE) bladder carcinoma (NEBC) is a rare but highly aggressive malignancy, characterized by rapid progression and poor survival.1 More than 60% of the patients are diagnosed with locoregional or distant metastases. In cases where metastasis is present, the median survival duration ranges from 6 to 9 months, whereas patients with localized disease have a median survival of less than 3 years.2,3 Although NEBC initially responds to cisplatin and etoposide-based chemotherapy regimens, relapse is common and is typically accompanied by treatment resistance, posing a significant therapeutic challenge.4 At the molecular level, NEBC is characterized by recurrent inactivation of TP53 and RB1, dysregulation of cell cycle checkpoints, and activation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition pathways.5,6 Notably, NEBC exhibits a profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), with markedly reduced immune infiltration and over 50% enriched pathways linked to the suppression of T cell-mediated adaptive immunity.7 Therefore, there is an urgent need to further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying NEBC and to identify new therapeutic targets and strategies.

Lineage plasticity enables tumor cells to switch phenotypic identities, thereby promoting therapeutic resistance and immune evasion.8,9 Current evidence suggests that NEBC often arises from basal-type urothelial carcinoma (UC) through a process of lineage plasticity driven by genetic alterations such as TP53 and RB1 inactivation, along with MYC overexpression.10,11 Molecular pathological studies have further revealed that mixed NEBC and adjacent UC components frequently share clonal genetic alterations, suggesting a common evolutionary origin.12 Notably, among the 20 patients with NE-subtype bladder cancer in the TCGA-BLCA cohort, only three exhibited classic small-cell morphology, while the remaining 17 retained the conventional histologic features of UC.6 This observation implies that NE traits may emerge at the molecular level prior to overt morphological manifestations. Collectively, these findings support a model in which a subset of UCs undergoes lineage plasticity to acquire NEBC phenotypes. However, transcriptional programs defining pre-NE transitional states and their functional impact on tumor progression and the immune microenvironment have not yet been defined.

In this study, we combined bulk and single-cell transcriptomic analyses across multiple clinical cohorts, including 2,694 transcriptomic profiles, 64,756 cells from in-house scRNA-seq (single-cell RNA sequencing) cohort, and 201,720 cells from public scRNA-seq cohort, to identify a transitional cell population within the lineage plasticity continuum of bladder cancer. This subpopulation exhibited high plasticity and was marked by elevated expression of TPX2. We further demonstrated that these TPX2high cells displayed dysregulated cell cycle progression and were localized within immunosuppressive niches, contributing to T cell exhaustion. Functional validation confirmed the role of TPX2 in driving G1/S transition, NE differentiation, and CD8+ T cell exhaustion. Finally, we demonstrated that pharmacologic CDK4/6 inhibition exerts potent antitumor effects in patient-derived models, supporting its potential as a promising therapeutic strategy for both TPX2high patients and NEBC.

We identified 14 distinct gene modules, whose full gene list for each module is provided in Table S1. We then assessed the correlations between modules and clinical traits, such as luminal (lu) and basal (ba) markers, cell cycle, stemness, and NE lineage plasticity features.13,14 Notably, MEbrown, MEdarkgreen, and MEgrey60 were strongly associated with lu and ba signatures, which may have originated from canonical UC cells. MEgreenyellow was strongly correlated with NE signatures but was negatively correlated with lu/ba markers, indicating canonical NE differentiation (Figure 1B). Strikingly, MEmidnightblue demonstrated NE differentiation without direct NE correlation, but it showed significant positive associations with lineage plasticity features, including DNA repair, HIF activation, and EZH2 pathway activation, suggesting that cells expressing this module probably acquired NE differentiation potential. Additionally, high expression of MEmidnightblue was a risk factor in UC (Figure 1C).

To identify key genes of NE lineage plasticity, we defined the signature of 315 genes exhibiting high correlations in both the MEmidnightblue module and the NE differentiation module as the NE-like signature (NE-like Sig) (Figure 1D). Conversely, genes meeting equivalent thresholds in the MEgreenyellow module were defined as the NE signature (NE Sig) (Figure S1A). Key gene constituents of the NE-like Sig, which are implicated in lineage plasticity, included EZH2, TPX2, AURKA, BIRC5, CCNB1, and AURKB,15,16 whereas canonical NE markers (ACSL1, INSM1, SYP, and CHGA) defined the core NE Sig. This molecular congruence validates the approach for identifying lineage-specific drivers of plasticity. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genome (KEGG) analysis further demonstrated that the NE-like Sig was predominantly associated with spliceosome regulation, cell cycle progression, and DNA replication mechanisms (Figure S1B), while the core NE Sig was enriched in neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions and calcium-mediated signaling pathways (Figure S1C). Cross-dataset validation across 20 bladder cancer transcriptomic cohorts including 2,650 samples confirmed that the NE-like Sig consistently correlated positively with NE differentiation and the activation of lineage plasticity pathways and concomitantly exhibited broad negative associations with established lu and ba lineage markers (Figure 1E). Additionally, NE-like Sig showed robust associations with aggressive phenotypes, including TP53 mutations, tumor grade, muscle-invasive status, and advanced tumor stage in bladder cancer (Figures S1D–S11K). Together, our results revealed that the NE-like Sig is a molecular hallmark of NE lineage plasticity in bladder carcinogenesis, functionally linking transcriptional dysregulation with clinically aggressive disease progression.

Single-cell transcriptomics reveals heterogeneity of the cell cycle dynamics underlying NE lineage plasticity

The plasticity of epithelial cells toward NE differentiation has been documented across multiple malignancies15,17; however, the existence of cells with NE lineage transition potential in bladder cancer remains undefined. To assess whether the NE-like Sig identifies tumor cells undergoing NE lineage plasticity at single-cell resolution, we performed in-house scRNA-seq on 5 UC and 5 NEBC samples from 2 hospitals (clinical details in Table S2). Additionally, we analyzed published scRNA-seq from 9 independent datasets, encompassing 49 UC and 2 NEBC tumor samples (clinical details in Table S3). Following quality control, 64,756 cells from the in-house cohort and 201,720 cells from the public cohort underwent dimensionality reduction and clustering (Figures 2A, 2B, and S2A). We identified major cell types including T cells, NK cells, B cells, plasma, myeloid cells, mast cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and epithelial cells based on their marker genes (Figures S2B and S2C; Tables S4, S5, and S6). To further characterize NE-like cells, epithelial cells were isolated for AUCell analysis, with NE-like Sig and NE Sig scores assigned to each cell (Figures S2D and S2E; Tables S7 and S8). Upon reclustering and signature scoring, cells with elevated NE-like Sig expression were identified as NE-like cells, representing approximately 13% of the epithelial cells in our in-house cohort and approximately 10% in the public cohort. Notably, within the in-house cohort, NEBC samples consistently exhibited a higher proportion of NE-like epithelial cells than UC samples (Figure S2F; Table S9). Gene expression profiling revealed that NE-like cells share with NE cells a transcriptional program associated with proliferation, stemness, and lineage plasticity. Importantly, NE-like cells simultaneously retain expression of canonical lu/ba features, while lacking NE-specific markers, thereby excluding their classification as an NE subpopulation. (Figure 2C). Notably, compared with lu/ba epithelial cells, NE-like cells exhibit higher stemness scores, together with elevated activation of TP53 loss-associated transcriptional program and multiple lineage plasticity-related pathways (Figures S2G–S2L). To further elucidate the functional characteristics of NE-like cells, we conducted KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, which demonstrated significant enrichment of the cell cycle pathway in both NE-like and NE cells relative to lu/ba cells (Figure 2D), emphasizing their role in cellular proliferation and associated biological activities. Additionally, monocle3 pseudotime and slingshot analysis of epithelial cells derived from in-house scRNA-seq cohort further indicated that NE-like cells differentiate toward NE states (Figure S2M).

Cell cycle acceleration driven by TP53/RB1 inactivation is common in NE-differentiated tumors,18,19 yet how cell cycle checkpoint dysregulation changes during the differentiation of NE-like cells into NE cells remains unclear. To identify distinct cell cycle programs that drive lineage transitions within NE-like cells and NE cells, we applied nonnegative matrix factorization (NMF) to epithelial cells from the in-house and public scRNA-seq cohorts. We identified 7 correlated meta-program (MP) clusters (Figure 2E); each epithelial cell was assigned to its dominant MP by the maximum module score and visualized with uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) embeddings (Figure 2F). Crucially, two MPs displayed divergent cellular tropisms; MP1 specifically enriched in NE-like cells, whereas MP2 showed preferential associations with terminally differentiated NE cells (Figures 2G and 2H). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that MP1 and MP2 represented distinct phases: MP1 was biased toward the G2/M phase, whereas MP2 aligned with the G1/S phase (Figure 2I). Cell cycle analysis across both in-house and public scRNA-seq cohorts revealed progressive S-phase expansion during urothelial-to-NE differentiation (Figure 2J). These results highlighted distinct cell cycle signatures between NE-like and terminally differentiated NE cells, with accelerated S-phase progression during NE differentiation.

Mapping reveals TPX2 as a representative and functionally relevant driver of NE-like cells

Although NE-like cells have been transcriptionally profiled, their precise definition remains elusive. Differential expression analysis pinpointed TPX2 as a tumor cell-enriched marker exhibiting pronounced upregulation in NE-like cells, with the strongest correlation with the NE-like Sig across multiple transcriptomic cohorts (Figures 3A and S3A–S3C). Additionally, deconvolution of bulk RNA-seq data using BayesPrism revealed that TPX2 levels strongly correlated with estimated NE-like cell proportions (Figure S3D). Spatial transcriptomics further confirmed co-enrichment of TPX2high spots and elevated NE-like Sig score (Figures 3B and 3C). Clinically, high TPX2 expression was associated with poor prognosis, muscle-invasive bladder cancer subtypes, and advanced tumor stage (Figures S3E–S3K). mIF analysis of 179 UCs from three independent cohorts, comprising samples from Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital (SYSMH), the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (KMMU), and the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (NMU), revealed that TPX2high tumor cells increased with tumor stage and muscular invasion (Figures 3E, 3F, S3L, and S3M). These findings collectively demonstrated that NE-like cells are prevalent in UC specimens, with TPX2 as a robust marker.

To mechanistically demarcate whether the distinct cell cycle modalities observed are governed by TPX2 and are functionally linked to NE differentiation, we employed two independent small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) to knock down TPX2 and lentiviral transduction to overexpress TPX2 in T24 and UM-UC-3 cell lines (Figures S4A–S4D). Importantly, TPX2 knockdown induced significant G1/S phase arrest (Figures 3G and 3H), whereas ectopic TPX2 overexpression (TPX2-oe) markedly increased the S-phase population (Figures 3I and 3J). Furthermore, both TPX2 knockdown and overexpression resulted in significant modulation of lineage plasticity markers (EZH2 and SOX2), indicating TPX2-dependent NE differentiation (Figures 3K, S4E, and S4F). Therefore, TPX2 accelerates the G1/S transition and initiates the transdifferentiation to NEBC.

TPX2 orchestrates CD8+ T cell exhaustion to establish an immunosuppressive niche during NE differentiation

While NE tumors are conventionally considered to possess an immunosuppressive TME,20,21,22 it remains unclear whether immune alterations precede or accompany the initial lineage transition toward NE-like cells rather than merely emerging with terminal NE differentiation. To dissect immune remodeling associated with NE lineage plasticity, we concentrated on lymphocyte-mediated immunity given its critical role in tumor immune surveillance. Among the lymphocyte subsets, differential expression analysis revealed that CD8+ T cells exhibited the most prominent transcriptional alterations (Figure S5A), prompting us to focus subsequent analysis on CD8+ T cells. Notably, in our in-house cohort, CD8+ T cells were annotated and reclustered into three distinct subpopulations (Figures 4A and S5B; Table S10). Cluster 0 displayed the highest exhaustion signature (Figure S5C), and R o/e analysis revealed that Cluster 0 was predominantly enriched in NEBC samples (Figure 4B), consistent with higher exhaustion score in NEBC compared to UC samples (Figure 4C). Having observed that CD8+ T cell exhaustion was most pronounced in NEBC, we next explored whether this CD8+ T cell exhaustion phenotype arises concomitantly with lineage plasticity. We stratified public scRNA-seq samples by the proportion of NE-like cells (Figure S5D). To further characterize CD8+ T cells heterogeneity, we annotated major T cell lineages and subsequently reclustered CD8+ T cells into five distinct subpopulations (Figures S5E and S5F; Table S11). R o/e analysis revealed that clusters 1 and 3 were enriched in the NE-like high group, whereas clusters 2 and 4 were predominant in the NE-like low group (Figure S5G). Next, mIF was performed on UC (n = 182) and NEBC (n = 23) samples from three independent cohorts. UC patients with NE-likehigh showed higher infiltration of PD-1+TIM-3+CD8+ T cells than those with NE-likelow, with the highest levels observed in NEBC (Figures 4D and 4E). To further delineate the spatial context of this exhausted T cell accumulation, spatial proximity analysis via QuPath in NE-likehigh patients (n = 91) further demonstrated that TPX2high tumor cells were preferentially localized adjacent to PD-1+TIM-3+CD8+ T cells, indicating a potential spatial interaction that may facilitate the enrichment or maintenance of exhausted CD8+ T cells (Figures 4F–4H), supporting the presence of a TPX2-dependent immunosuppressive niche associated with NE differentiation.

To further validate our findings, we performed in vitro co-culture of activated peripheral CD8+ T cells with TPX2-overexpressing (TPX2-oe) T24 or UM-UC-3 cell lines and found that TPX2-oe tumor cells significantly induced CD8+ T cells exhaustion (Figures 4I–4L and S8). In vivo, we ectopically overexpressed TPX2 in MB49 cells and established xenograft tumors in C57BL/6 mice (Figure 5A). Compared with vector control group, the TPX2-oe group exhibited significantly accelerated tumor growth and tumor weight (Figures 5B–5D), confirming that TPX2 drives tumor progression. Furthermore, TPX2-oe promoted NE differentiation, enhancing cellular plasticity and driving a more aggressive NE-like phenotype (Figures 5E and 5F). Flow cytometry as well as mIF both revealed a higher proportion of PD-1+TIM-3+CD8+ T cells in the TPX2-oe group, indicating enhanced T cell exhaustion (Figures 5G–5J and S8). Notably, spatial proximity analysis demonstrated a close co-localization between TPX2+ cells and PD-1+TIM-3+CD8+ T cells, reinforcing the role of TPX2 in forming an immunosuppressive niche (Figures 5K and 5L). Combined CDK4/6 inhibition and immune checkpoint blockade enhances antitumor efficacy in NEBC

Despite initial tumor control by platinum-based chemotherapy,23 intrinsic or acquired resistance ultimately drives disease progression in most NEBC patients, contributing to persistently poor outcomes. Although extensive exploration of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) combined with chemotherapy in NE carcinomas, current clinical evidence unfortunately indicates suboptimal therapeutic outcomes for both ICI monotherapy and combination regimens.24,25,26,27 To identify potential therapeutics targeting NE differentiation, we conducted small-molecule drug screening using signatures from the NE-like vs. lu/ba and NE vs. lu/ba comparisons, which prioritized palbociclib (Pal) as a leading candidate (Figures S6A and S6B). Furthermore, Pal was predicted to exhibit higher sensitivity in NE-like and NE cells than in lu/ba epithelial cells (Figure S6C).

To functionally validate whether NE-like cells exhibit selective sensitivity to CDK4/6 inhibition, we generated patient-derived organoids (PDOs) from TPX2low and TPX2high tumors defined by immunohistochemistry (IHC) following our published studies, and treated them with vehicle, cisplatin (Cis) + gemcitabine (Gem), PD-1 blockade (αPD-1), Cis + Gem + αPD-1, or αPD-1 plus Pal (Figures S6D and S6E). The combination of αPD-1 and Pal induced a significant, preferential loss of viability in TPX2high, but not in TPX2low PDOs, with no significant difference in response to chemotherapy between the two groups, indicating a CDK4/6-dependent vulnerability that emerges early during NE acquisition (Figures 6A, 6B, and S6F). To extend these findings to fully developed NE tumors, we established PDOs from fresh NEBC tissues, as confirmed by histological and IHC analyses (Figures 6C, S6I, and S6J). PDOs exhibited resistance to Cis + Eto alone or in combination with αPD-1. In contrast, Pal significantly reduced PDO viability, with the lowest activity observed in the Cis + Eto + αPD-1 + Pal group, which was accompanied by a significant reduction in p-RB levels (Figures 6C, 6D, S6K, and S6L). In addition, we successfully established a patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model from the NEBC tissues (Figure 6E). Strikingly, quadruplet therapy (Cis + Eto + αPD-1 + Pal) elicited the most potent antitumor efficacy, inducing significant tumor regression with markedly suppressed growth kinetics (Figures 6F and S7A–S7C). Notably, Pal treatment led to a significant reduction in p-RB levels (Figures S7D and S7E). mIF analysis of PDX tissues further demonstrated that the combination of Pal can reduce PD-1+TIM-3+CD8+ T cells in the TME (Figures 6G and 6H) and S-phase proliferating cells (Figures 6I, 6J, S7F, and S7G) as well as increase the number of apoptotic tumor cells (Figures S7H and S7I). These results indicate that Pal is a promising therapy across the NE spectrum, from NE-like states to NEBC, by restraining tumor proliferation and remodeling the immunosuppressive TME.

Discussion

Lineage plasticity has emerged as a fundamental mechanism enabling cancer progression, therapy resistance, and immune evasion.28,29,30 However, the molecular events and intermediate cellular states underlying NE differentiation remain poorly understood. In this study, we identified and functionally characterize a previously unrecognized transitional cellular state within the spectrum of urothelial-to-NE differentiation in bladder cancer. This state, defined by TPX2 overexpression and heightened transcriptional plasticity, lacks canonical NE markers but exhibits strong associations with cell cycle dysregulation and localized immune suppression. Our findings suggest that TPX2 is not only a robust biomarker of this plastic intermediate but also a functional regulator driving G1/S transition and facilitating CD8+ T cells exhaustion through spatial niche formation.

Previous molecular studies of UC and NEBC have delineated their key genetic and transcriptional architectures.7,31,32 Yet bulk transcriptomic profiling and static histopathological assessments have yielded only endpoint snapshots, hindering fine-scale delineation of the dynamic continuum and intermediate states underlying the UC-to-NEBC transition. In this study, by integrating transcriptomic data across multiple stages of bladder cancer progression at a large-scale single-cell resolution, we achieved a high-resolution view of the cellular hierarchy and identified a previously unrecognized tumor cell population with pronounced lineage plasticity. Transcriptionally distinct from canonical NE markers, this population was enriched for plasticity-associated pathways, including DNA repair, EZH2 signaling, TP53 regulatory networks, and stemness programs.13,31,33 Trajectory analyses further positioned these cells in an intermediate state along the differentiation continuum, marked by heightened stemness, attenuated lu/ba identity, and frequent TP53 alterations, with a gradual trajectory toward NE differentiation. Collectively, these findings established a comprehensive framework for understanding lineage plasticity in bladder cancer and revealed a transitional state with potential implications for tumor evolution and therapeutic resistance.

Established drivers of lineage plasticity in aggressive tumors include RB1 and TP53 codeletion, as well as transcriptional and epigenetic regulators such as EZH2, SOX2, and MYCN, which collectively orchestrate cell-state reprogramming and stemness acquisition.33,34 However, these canonical pathways do not fully account for the molecular heterogeneity observed during lineage transitions. Here, TPX2 emerged as a previously unrecognized driver of lineage plasticity. Beyond its documented roles in proliferation and therapeutic resistance,35,36 TPX2 functions as a critical upstream regulator of the G1/S checkpoint, integrating cell cycle control with lineage reprogramming. Distinct stage-specific cell cycle patterns—G2/M bias in NE-like cells and aberrant G1/S regulation in NE-differentiated cells—underscore the dynamic coordination between TPX2 activity and the plasticity continuum. These insights expand the mechanistic framework of lineage plasticity and position TPX2 as a potential therapeutic target for plastic, transitioning cell states during NEBC progression.

NEBC is widely recognized as an immunologically “cold” tumor, characterized primarily by limited immune-cell infiltration.37 Extending this paradigm, our analyses uncovered a previously uncharacterized dimension of immune suppression: a lineage-plastic TPX2high subpopulation that establishes an immunosuppressive niche early in the plasticity process, driving progressive CD8+ T cell exhaustion proportional to the degree of plasticity. Immunofluorescence analyses across multiple independent clinical cohorts validated the close spatial proximity between TPX2high plastic cells and exhausted CD8+ T cells, underscoring the pivotal role of this transitional state in initiating immune evasion. Collectively, these findings indicate that immune suppression in NEBC is not a feature restricted to terminal NE differentiation but is instead initiated during lineage reprogramming, shaping a suppressive TME from the earliest stages of tumor evolution and providing a mechanistic rationale for the heightened sensitivity of these plastic states to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, consistent with clinical reports of partial responses in NEBC patients.38,39

The concurrent dysregulation of the G1/S checkpoint and pronounced CD8+ T cell exhaustion in high-plasticity NEBC prompted us to explore a targeted therapeutic strategy. Guided by small-molecule drug prediction analyses, Pal was prioritized as the most promising CDK4/6 inhibitor for disrupting plasticity-driven cell cycle dysregulation. Notably, CDK4/6 inhibitors have also been shown to enhance tumor antigen presentation and alleviate immunosuppressive cues within the TME,40,41,42 providing a mechanistic rationale for combining cell cycle inhibition with immunotherapy. In both PDOs and PDX models, the combination of CDK4/6 inhibition with αPD-1 achieved robust tumor suppression and a synergistic antitumor immune response. Collectively, these findings not only demonstrate preclinical proof of concept for this dual-targeted approach but also establish a compelling rationale for its clinical translation and future trial development aimed at overcoming plasticity-driven therapeutic resistance.

In summary, our study delineated the intrinsic link between lineage plasticity, immune evasion, and cell cycle dysregulation in NEBC. We identified TPX2 as a central regulatory node connecting transcriptional reprogramming, G1/S transition abnormalities, and CD8+ T cells exhaustion. Furthermore, we propose a therapeutic strategy involving combined inhibition of CDK4/6 and blockade of PD-1, providing mechanistic insight and preclinical evidence for overcoming plasticity-driven therapeutic resistance in NEBC.

Limitations of the study

While our findings provide valuable insights into the role of lineage plasticity in NEBC, the absence of a lineage-faithful model for NE differentiation in bladder cancer limits our ability to fully capture the transition from UC to NEBC. In addition, the number of patient samples profiled by scRNA-seq was limited, which may reduce power to detect subtle differences across conditions and may not fully capture inter-patient heterogeneity. We also acknowledge that the dynamic changes in cell cycle states during lineage plasticity were not fully delineated in the current study. Moreover, our mechanistic exploration remains preliminary, and further studies are warranted to delineate the molecular circuits governing lineage plasticity and its contribution to tumor progression. Nevertheless, our results highlight the importance of tumor plasticity in shaping disease evolution and therapeutic response. Future efforts should focus on developing clinically relevant models that faithfully recapitulate NE differentiation, expanding independent patient cohorts, and enabling deeper mechanistic investigations of the pathways driving this transformation. Importantly, the proposed therapeutic strategy requires further validation in clinical trials to fully assess its potential for therapeutic application.

Experimental model and study participant details

Clinical samples

Fresh primary tumor tissues were collected from eight patients diagnosed with UC or NEBC by urologists, and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was performed using the 10x Genomics Chromium platform. Specifically, there were five patients with UC and five with NEBC, with ages ranging from 48 to 87 years and a mean age of 66 years. In addition, clinical specimens from 182 patients with UC and 23 patients with NEBC collected from Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital (SYSMH), the Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University (KMMU), and the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (NMU), were used for multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) staining to validate the transcriptomic findings. Detailed clinical and pathological information for this cohort is provided in Table S12. Written informed consent was obtained from all the patients prior to sample collection. The study protocols were approved by the Research Ethics Committee (Approval No. SYSKY-2023-396-2), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional regulations.

Cell lines

The T24, UM-UC-3, and MB49 cell lines were procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA).

Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) culture and drug treatment

Fresh tumor tissue was mechanically minced into 1–3 mm3 fragments and enzymatically digested with collagenase at 37°C for 30 min (Worthington Biochemical Corporation, Cat#LS004174).54 The digestion was terminated by adding 10% FBS, and the resulting suspension was filtered through a 100 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min. The cell pellet was resuspended in ice-cold Matrigel (Corning, Cat#356231) and seeded as droplets into 24-well plates. The plates were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 30 min to allow Matrigel polymerization, after which complete organoid medium (AimingMed, Cat#10-100-542) was added. Organoids were passaged every 7–10 days.

Once the cultures reached stable growth, the organoids were cocultured with activated PBMCs at a ratio of 1:6 (organoids:PBMCs). Activated PBMCs were prepared as described above. The treatment groups received the indicated drugs, either as single agents or in combination, at specified concentrations (e.g., 20 μM Cisplatin [Cis; Selleck, Cat#S1166], 10 μM Gemcitabine [Gem; Selleck, Cat#S1714], 10 μM Etoposide [Eto; Selleck, Cat#S1225], 5 μM Palbociclib [Pal, Selleck, Cat#S1116], or 10 μg/mL αPD-1 (Selleck, Cat#A3039) for a total of 72 h. At the end of treatment, SYTOX dye (KeyGEN, Cat#KGE2504) was added to assess cell death based on its ability to penetrate compromised membranes and bind nucleic acids in nonviable cells. Organoids were imaged via a Leica SP8 STED 3X superresolution live-cell confocal microscope, and the fluorescence intensity or SYTOX-positive area was quantified with image analysis software to compare cell death across treatment groups.

Establishment of an NEBC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model

This animal experiment was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval No. IACUC-AEWC-F250115015) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and national regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals. Female 5-week-old NOD SCID mice (RRID: IMSR_RJ:NOD-SCID) were used to establish a PDX model of NEBC.

Fresh tumor tissue was obtained from a surgical specimen of a patient with NEBC at Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital and immediately placed in chilled DMEM. The primary tumor was minced into 2–3 mm3 fragments and subcutaneously implanted into the flanks of the NOD SCID mice. Prior to therapeutic intervention, the mice were humanized via intravenous injection of activated PBMCs. PBMCs were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin-streptomycin, and 30 U/mL recombinant human IL-2. T cell expansion and activation were induced by stimulating the cultures with Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 Dynabeads, followed by incubation at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 5 days. Activated cells were harvested and injected into the mice before initiating therapy. When the tumor volume reached approximately 50–100 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to five treatment groups (n = 6 per group) and treated for 3 weeks: (1) Vehicle group, administered an equal volume of saline; (2) Cis + Eto group, treated with Cis (7 mg/kg) once weekly and Eto (10 mg/kg) once daily on days 1–3 of each week; (3) Cis + Eto + αPD-1 group, treated with the same chemotherapy regimen combined with αPD-1 (200 μg/mouse, twice weekly); (4) Cis + Eto + Pal group, treated with Cis, Eto, and Pal (2 mg/mouse, oral gavage, twice weekly) following the same dosing schedule; and (5) Eto + Cis + Pal + αPD-1 group, treated with Eto, Cis, Pal, and αPD-1 following the same schedule.

Tumor size was measured every 3 days via digital calipers. Owing to the staggered nature of tumor engraftment and individual growth variability in the PDX model, the mice were euthanized at different time points following completion of the treatment schedule. Tumors were excised, photographed, weighed, and subjected to histopathological analysis.

Tumor xenograft model

This animal experiment was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval No. IACUC-AEWC-F250115016) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and national regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals. Female 5-week-old C57BL/6 (RRID:IMSR_JAX:000664) mice were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Sun Yat-sen University and housed under pathogen-free conditions. Briefly, 5 × 105 MB49 cells from either the vector or TPX2 overexpression group were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of the mice (5 mice per group). Tumor growth was monitored by measuring tumor volume and body weight at regular intervals. Upon reaching the experimental endpoint, determined by tumor volume and ethical considerations, all mice were euthanized. Tumor tissues were collected for flow cytometric analysis to assess the proportion of PD-1+TIM-3+CD8+ T cells within the tumor. Additionally, a portion of the tumor was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent immunohistochemistry and multiplex immunofluorescence analysis.

Method details

ScRNA-seq

Fresh tumor specimens were processed immediately after resection. The tissues were mechanically dissociated and subjected to enzymatic digestion to generate single-cell suspensions, followed by filtration through a 40-μm strainer and red blood cell lysis. Cell viability was assessed prior to library preparation. Single-cell libraries were constructed using the 10x Genomics Chromium platform according to the manufacturer’s instructions and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. The raw sequencing data were processed with Cell Ranger to generate gene‒cell count matrices for downstream analysis.

Collection and processing of bulk transcriptomic datasets

We collected transcriptomic data related to bladder cancer from multiple public databases, including 21 datasets and 2,694 samples. All bulk RNA-seq datasets were normalized to transcripts per million (TPM) to ensure consistent quantification of gene expression levels. In subsequent analyses, a subset of these RNA-seq datasets was used to construct a WGCNA discovery cohort, and the remaining datasets were included in downstream validation analyses to evaluate the consistency of the expression patterns of key modules and candidate biomarkers across different cohorts. Given the substantial differences in the dynamic range, batch effects, and platform-specific characteristics between bulk RNA-seq and microarray technologies, all datasets were analyzed independently within their respective platforms to avoid systematic bias from cross-platform integration.

WGCNA analysis

We performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) on 7 bulk RNA-seq datasets comprising bladder cancer samples (n = 889) to identify co-expression modules associated with NE differentiation.44 The batch effect of different datasets was removed using the sva package (v3.48.0) based on transcriptomic profiles transformed to transcripts per million (TPM). First, a signed hybrid gene network was constructed by calculating pairwise Pearson correlations and applying soft-thresholding powers ranging from 12 to 20 by 2, which are candidate thresholds to balance scale-free topology fitting (R2) with network connectivity and ensure the robustness of the resulting network. The adjacency matrix was subsequently computed via the selected soft threshold (β = 8) and transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM) to minimize noise and improve the accuracy of the network. Hierarchical clustering was then performed on the TOM and gene modules were identified via dynamic tree cutting (minimum module size = 30; deepSplit = 1). Finally, module eigengenes (MEs), which represent the first principal component of each module’s gene expression profile, were computed to capture the overall expression pattern of each module, providing a comprehensive characterization of the modular structure of the network. Among these MEs, those that were too close, as measured by the correlation of their eigengenes, were merged. By inspecting the correlation of these modules with the sample molecular information, MEmidnightblue was strongly correlated with NE differentiation, and MEgreenyellow was correlated with NE. For each module, membership genes with both module membership and gene significance higher than 0.5 were retained as signature genes.

Collection and processing of public scRNA-seq datasets

ScRNA-seq data were collected from 51 bladder cancer samples across 9 publicly available datasets.37,55,56,57,58,59,60,61,62 Preliminary filtered data generated via the CellRanger (RRID:SCR_023221) pipeline were used for downstream analyses.63 As part of quality control, low-quality cells were removed based on the following criteria: (1) fewer than 300 or more than 6000 detected genes, (2) total UMI counts >25,000 or <1,000, and (3) mitochondrial gene content exceeding 20%.64

Putative doublets were further identified and excluded via the DoubletFinder algorithm with the top 20 principal components and parameters set to pN = 0.25 and pK = 0.09, thereby improving cell-level accuracy.65 The qualified raw count matrix was normalized via the NormalizeData function with default settings in the Seurat package (v5.3.0, RRID:SCR_016341).43 Highly variable genes (HVGs; n = 2,000) were identified with FindVariableFeatures, and the data were subsequently scaled with ScaleData to mitigate technical variation.

To correct for batch effects across donors, we applied the Harmony integration algorithm with the following parameters: batch_key = “patient”, σ = 0.1, and λ = 1.52 Dimensionality reduction was performed via principal component analysis (PCA), and unsupervised clustering was carried out via the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was used to visualize the low-dimensional structure. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted via the FindAllMarkers function, and cell populations were annotated via the integration of canonical markers with gene signatures reported in previous studies.

Identification of the cell states of epithelial cells

To identify the cell states, two signature gene sets from WGCNA, i.e., NE-like Sig and the NE Sig, were used to assess the signature score with AUCell (v1.22.0). We calculated the ‘AUC’ for each geneset in each cell and obtained the preliminary cell identities via the L_k2 threshold. Together, the final identities of these epithelial cells were determined via signature score comparison. The number of epithelial cells with double-negative signature scores was set to lu/ba.

To better characterize the transcriptional traits of the AUCell-identified NE-like cells, we derived an NE-like cells signature by performing differential expression analysis within this cell population. Genes with an adjusted p < 0.05 and log2FC > 1.5 were selected as marker genes, yielding a refined and representative gene set that captures the core molecular features of NE-like cells.

Survival analysis

Survival analysis was performed with the R packages survival (v3.5.7) and survminer (v0.4.9). The optimal cut-off for continuous variables was identified via the surv_cutpoint function, after which variables were dichotomized to enable group-based comparisons. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated with survfit and visualized through ggsurvplot to assess survival differences between groups.

Bayesian-based cell composition inference

To estimate the abundance of NE-like cells in the bulk RNA-seq samples, we employed the BayesPrism algorithm (v2.2.2) for Bayesian transcriptomic deconvolution.47 The reference expression matrix was derived from the integrated scRNA-seq dataset described above, which comprised multiple cell types. Epithelial cells were annotated into three subtypes: lu/ba, NE-like, and NE. Prior to modeling, reference data were subjected to stringent quality control: genes with excessive noise, low detection rates, or known technical bias (e.g., mitochondrial and ribosomal genes) were excluded. Only protein-coding genes expressed in at least five cells were retained to ensure robust model training.

BayesPrism was subsequently applied to multiple bulk transcriptomic cohorts to infer posterior estimates of cell state proportions for each sample. The predicted abundance of NE-like cells served as a key variable for downstream analyses. All analyses were performed in R (v4.3.1).

Meta-program inference via NMF

To further dissect the transcriptional programs driving the transition from NE-like to NE phenotypes, we performed nonnegative matrix factorization (NMF, v0.26) analysis on epithelial cells.48 Initially, we extracted the MEmidnightblue module genes identified via WGCNA as lineage plasticity-related signatures and constructed a gene expression matrix accordingly.

NMF was independently applied to each sample across a range of factorization ranks (k = 4–9) to identify robust coexpression programs (i.e., those observed using different ranks in the same sample), referring to the 3CA pipeline in Gavish et al. (https://www.weizmann.ac.il/sites/3CA). For each sample, robust programs were selected, redundancy due to multiple ranks was removed, and a filter based on the similarity to programs from other samples was applied. To capture recurrent transcriptional modules across samples, we computed pairwise Jaccard similarity between individual NMF components and performed hierarchical clustering. Highly overlapping programs were merged into 12 consensus meta-programs (MPs). For each MP, we assessed the correlation between module scores and gene expression profiles and selected the top 30 genes with the highest correlation as representative driver genes to refine the core gene sets. The cells were subsequently assigned to their dominant MP based on the maximum module score, reflecting their primary transcriptional identity.

Trajectory inference and differentiation potential analysis

Monocle3

To infer the differentiation trajectory of NE-like and NE cells, we utilized monocle3 (v1.3.4).49 The single-cell expression matrix was converted into a CellDataSet object and preprocessed with preprocess_cds, retaining 50 principal components (num_dim = 50) for dimensionality reduction. We then applied reduce_dimension with UMAP to generate low-dimensional embeddings. Pseudotime ordering was performed via order_cells, and trajectory plots were visualized with plot_cells to illustrate the developmental continuum of the NE-like and NE populations.

Slingshot

To further validate the developmental hierarchy, we applied the slingshot algorithm to the UMAP embeddings.50 The cells were clustered via Gaussian mixture modeling, and a minimum spanning tree (MST) was constructed via Slingshot to connect the cluster centroids and infer the global lineage architecture. Slingshot fitted principal curves along the inferred lineage structure to reconstruct the smooth differentiation trajectories. The getCurves function was then used to extract smoothed pseudotime values for visualization and downstream analyses.

Spatial transcriptomics data analysis

Spatial transcriptomics data were obtained from Yu et al. (2019). Analysis was performed in Seurat (v5.3.0).66 Low-quality spots, including low UMIs and high mitochondrial transcript fractions, were removed on the basis of standard quality control (QC) metrics. Gene expression normalization was performed using SCTransform. After normalization, PCA was performed to reduce dimensionality, followed by clustering using FindNeighbors and FindClusters. UMAP was employed for visualization on the basis of the first 30 principal components. Spatial gene expression patterns were visualized via SpatialDimPlot. The correlation between the NE-like Sig and TPX2 expression was assessed by calculating module scores with AddModuleScore. Finally, SpatialFeaturePlot was used to map the distribution of NE-like Sig and TPX2 across the tissue section.

Cell culture

T24 (RRID:CVCL_0554) and UM-UC-3 (RRID:CVCL_1783) human bladder cancer cell lines, as well as the MB49 (RRID:CVCL_7076) mouse bladder cancer cell line, were cultured under standard conditions at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.67 T24 cell line was maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Cat#C11875500BT), while UM-UC-3 and MB49 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Cat#C11965500BT). Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Cat#A5256701) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Cat#15140163, final concentration: 100 U/mL penicillin and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin). Mycoplasma contamination was routinely tested via conventional PCR.

Western blotting

Protein lysates were prepared from cultured cells or tissue samples and quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo Fisher, Cat#TFS-23227). Equal amounts of protein were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore, Cat#IPVH00010). After blocking, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against TPX2 and GAPDH (Abcam, Cat#ab8245, RRID:AB_2107448), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies against mouse IgG (H + L) (Ray antibody, Cat#RM3001, RRID:AB_3096964) or rabbit IgG (H + L) (Ray antibody, Cat#RM3002, RRID:AB_3096965). Protein signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system and visualized with the Smart-Blot Imaging System (Beijing Smart Biotech).

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm and mounted onto poly-L-lysine-coated slides. Prior to staining, the slides were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 90%, and 70%). Antigen retrieval was performed in EDTA buffer (pH 8.0) via heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with hydrogen peroxide, and nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Beyotime, Cat#ST023) to reduce the background content and enhance antibody specificity.

Multiplex immunofluorescence was performed via the PANO 7-plex TSA kit (Panovue, Cat#0004100100) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, enabling simultaneous detection of up to six protein markers along with nuclear counterstaining with DAPI. Briefly, sequential rounds of staining were conducted, with each target detected via a specific primary antibody, including rabbit anti-human TPX2 (Proteintech, Cat#11741-1-AP, RRID:AB_2208895; 1:1000), rabbit anti-human CD8 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#85336, RRID:AB_2800052; 1:400), rabbit anti-human pan-cytokeratin (ZSGB-Bio, Cat#ZM-0069, RRID:AB_2941997; 1:200), mouse anti-human PD-1 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#43248, RRID:AB_2728836; 1:100), rabbit anti-human TIM-3 (Abcam, Cat#ab241332, RRID:AB_2888936; 1:1000), rabbit anti-human PCNA (ServiceBio, Cat#GB11010, RRID:AB_3714654; 1:500), rabbit anti-human/mouse Ki-67 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#34330, RRID:AB_2942026; 1:1000), rabbit anti-mouse EZH2 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#5246, RRID:AB_10694683; 1:50), rabbit anti-mouse SOX2 (Abcam, Cat#ab92494, RRID:AB_10585428, 1:100), rabbit anti-human/mouse Rb (phospho S807) (Abcam, Cat#ab184796, RRID:AB_2923400, 1:500). Each primary antibody was followed by incubation with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody, and signal amplification was performed via tyramide signal amplification (TSA). Microwave-mediated antigen retrieval was repeated between each TSA round to remove antibody complexes. After all antigen detection was complete, nuclear counterstaining was performed with DAPI. Finally, the stained slides were scanned via the Vectra Polaris imaging system (Akoya Biosciences).

Immunofluorescence-based spatial proximity analysis

After immunofluorescence staining, the definition of coexpressing cells and spatial proximity analysis were performed with QuPath (v0.5.1, RRID:SCR_018257). Initially, cells were identified through QuPath’s automated segmentation algorithm, followed by the selection of target populations, including TPX2+panCK+ and PD-1+TIM-3+CD8+ cells, based on predefined fluorescence intensity thresholds. The spatial proximity between TPX2+panCK+ and PD-1+TIM-3+CD8+ cells was evaluated by measuring the intercellular distance, with a 25 μm threshold applied to define proximity.68 The spatial distribution of these populations was quantified with QuPath’s spatial analysis tools, enabling the assessment of their relative positioning within the tissue microenvironment. All the results were exported from QuPath and subjected to further statistical analyses in R.

TUNEL staining

TUNEL Assay Kit-FITC (Servicebio, Cat#G1504) was used to detect apoptosis.69 Briefly, FFPE tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through a series of graded ethanol washes. The sections were then treated with Proteinase K at room temperature for 20 min to permeabilize the cells. After washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture, which contains terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase and fluorescein-labelled dUTP, at 37°C for 2 h. After the reaction, the sections were washed again with PBS and counterstained with DAPI for nuclear labeling. The stained sections were observed under a fluorescence microscope, and apoptotic cells were identified by the green fluorescence signal.

Cell cycle analysis by flow cytometry

Cell cycle analysis was conducted via a Beckman CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer.70 Cells were transfected with TPX2 siRNA (or control siRNA), and cell cycle profiling was performed at 48 h post-transfection. The cells were washed with cold PBS and resuspended in 0.5 mL of prechilled 70% ethanol, followed by overnight fixation at 4°C. After fixation, the cells were centrifuged, washed with PBS, and incubated with 1 mL of staining solution from a commercial cell cycle analysis kit (MultiScience, Cat#CCS012) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Finally, the samples were analyzed via flow cytometry in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted via TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Cat#5596026CN). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized via HiScript II Q RT SuperMix (Vazyme, Cat#R222-01).71 RT-qPCR was performed on a LightCycler 480 system (Roche) using gene-specific primers and ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Cat#Q711-02). Each reaction was performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. β-actin was used as the internal reference gene for normalization.

CD8+ T cell isolation and stimulation

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from whole blood via lymphocyte separation medium (TBD, Cat#LTS1077). CD8+ T cells were subsequently isolated via a CD8+ T cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat#130-096-495) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity of the isolated CD8+ T cells was confirmed by flow cytometry and was typically greater than 90%. To activate the CD8+ T cells, Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher, Cat#11132D) was added according to the manufacturer’s instructions, along with 30 U/mL recombinant human IL-2 (Sigma, Cat#SRP8034), and the cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS.

Coculture experiment

Activated CD8+ T cells were cocultured with tumor cells in 12-well plates at a 1:4 ratio of tumor cells (105 cells) to T cells for 24 h in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS. After the coculture period, the cells were collected and stained with Ghost Dye Red 780 (Tonbo Biosciences, Cat#13-0865-T500) for viability, followed by staining with antibodies specific for CD8 (BioLegend, Cat#980908, RRID:AB_2888883), PD-1 (BioLegend, Cat#379209, RRID:AB_2922607), and TIM-3 (BioLegend, Cat#364819, RRID:AB_3683393). The cells were incubated on ice for 30 min and then analyzed by flow cytometry to assess the exhaustion status, specifically measuring PD-1 and TIM-3 expression on CD8+ T cells.

Small molecule drug screening

In this study, we conducted computational drug screening using the Expanded CMap LINCS 2020 Resource, specifically leveraging the level 5 beta compound treatment dataset (level5_beta_trt_cp_n720216x12328.gctx) obtained from CLUE.io (https://clue.io, RRID:SCR_016204). We focused on compounds with MOA (Mode of Action) including CDK inhibitors and cell cycle inhibitors tested in urinary tract-derived cell lines. For NE-like and NE cells, the query signature consisted of the top and bottom 150 highly expressed and differential genes compared to lu/ba epithelial cells, indicated by log2 fold change. Connectivity scores were measured as Pearson similarity between the signature and compound-induced profiles. For each compound, the scores were averaged across all experimental conditions and cell lines to prioritize potential therapeutic candidates.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times, yielding consistent and reproducible results. All the statistical analyses were performed via R software (v4.3.1). For group comparisons, appropriate tests were applied, including the two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test, two-sided Student’s t test, one-way ANOVA, and two-way ANOVA, were conducted as indicated in the figure legends. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation coefficients, depending on data distribution and variance homogeneity. Unless otherwise stated, a two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant, with adjustments for multiple comparisons applied where appropriate using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Sample sizes were determined empirically rather than by statistical calculation. No data points were excluded, and neither randomization nor blinding was implemented during the experimental procedures or outcome assessments.
