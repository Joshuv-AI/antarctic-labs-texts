Early Electroencephalographic Background Evolution as a Predictor of Airway Outcomes in Severe Traumatic Brain Injury: A Pilot Study
Authors: Adnan Qadri, Soumya N Maiti, Eman A Khan, Immia Bukhari, Duha Wani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsDetermining the optimal timing for extubation or tracheostomy in patients with severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) remains challenging. Standard weaning criteria often fail to account for neurological readiness, leading to high rates of extubation failure. This study evaluated whether the evolution of electroencephalography (EEG) background activity over the first 96 hours could predict airway outcomes better than standard clinical assessments.Patients and methodsWe conducted a prospective observational study on 43 patients with severe TBI requiring mechanical ventilation. Electroencephalography recordings were obtained on admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) and at 96 hours. Background activity was classified as improving (alpha/beta dominance) or non-improving (theta/delta dominance). The primary outcome was successful extubation vs extubation failure (defined as re-intubation or need for tracheostomy).ResultsOf the 43 patients, 25 (58%) were successfully extubated, while 18 (42%) failed. Dominant EEG frequency at 96 hours was a powerful predictor of outcome. Patients with improving alpha/beta backgrounds demonstrated a positive predictive value (PPV) of 92.9% for successful extubation. Conversely, persistent theta/delta slowing was significantly associated with extubation failure [odds ratio (OR): 18.41; 95% confidence interval (CI): 2.1–160.3; p = 0.008], regardless of the patient's age (p = 0.42).ConclusionEarly improvement in EEG background activity is a robust indicator of airway competence in severe TBI. A non-improving EEG warrants caution, while an improving background suggests that tracheostomy may be judiciously deferred to allow for successful extubation. Integrating serial EEG into weaning protocols may prevent unnecessary surgical interventions and reduce extubation failure rates.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury remains a leading cause of mortality and long-term disability worldwide, placing a significant burden on intensive care resources.1 In patients with severe TBI, airway management is a critical determinant of outcome. While the initial focus is often on securing the airway to prevent secondary insults, the subsequent decision to liberate a patient from mechanical ventilation is equally challenging. Extubation failure in this population is associated with high rates of ventilator-associated pneumonia, prolonged ICU stay, and increased mortality.2

Decisions regarding extubation are traditionally guided by clinical assessments of the GCS and hemodynamic stability, alongside physiological parameters such as the spontaneous breathing trial (SBT).3,4 However, these conventional markers primarily assess pulmonary mechanics and cardiovascular reserve, often failing to capture the “neurological competence” required for airway protection. Consequently, a significant proportion of patients who pass standard weaning criteria still fail extubation due to inadequate central respiratory drive or persistent encephalopathy.3,5

Electroencephalography provides a direct, real-time assessment of cerebral cortical function. While its primary application in neurocritical care has been seizure detection, emerging evidence suggests its background features hold significant prognostic value. Preservation of EEG background reactivity is a powerful predictor of survival and favorable functional outcome in comatose patients, suggesting that the integrity of cortical networks is essential for recovery.1 Similarly, in the Indian critical care setting, researchers have highlighted that EEG provides critical diagnostic and prognostic clues in patients with altered sensorium, serving as a valuable extension of the clinical examination.2

Despite this, the utility of early EEG background evolution—specifically as a predictor of airway readiness—remains underutilized. Most centers focus on ruling out seizures, potentially overlooking the rich prognostic information embedded in the background rhythm. This study aimed to evaluate whether the evolution of EEG background activity over the first 96 hours predicts airway outcomes in patients with severe TBI, independent of seizure occurrence.

By utilizing serial limited-duration recordings, we sought to validate a pragmatic, accessible tool for prognostication in resource-limited neurocritical care settings, which can be used to differentiate true neurological airway readiness from simple respiratory stability, thereby allowing clinicians to delay tracheostomy in patients with alpha/beta rhythm while judiciously optimizing tracheostomy timing for those with persistent background slowing.

Patients and Methods

This prospective observational study was conducted in the ICU of a tertiary care hospital from January 2024 to July 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board and registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India. Written informed consent was obtained from the legally authorized representative of each patient before enrollment.

Adult patients (aged ≥18 years) with severe TBI, defined as an admission GCS score of ≤8, who were admitted to the ICU in the postoperative period, were eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria included a pre-existing history of seizure disorder or epilepsy, known hypersensitivity to antiepileptic drugs, severe metabolic encephalopathy precluding EEG interpretation, pregnancy or lactation, polytrauma (including but not limited to chest injury and spine injury), and reactive airway disease. Patients requiring deep sedation were excluded from the study, as it could falsely be interpreted as a non-improving EEG. Similarly, neuromuscular blockade was avoided so that it would not mask clinical seizures.

Patients received seizure prophylaxis with either phenytoin [(15 mg/kg followed by 100 mg thrice a day (TID)] or levetiracetam [(500 mg twice daily (BD)] as decided by the neurosurgical team; however, EEG interpreters remained blinded to drug allocation. Sedation was maintained using fentanyl (100–200 µg/hour), according to the protocol of our ICU. All patients received N-acetylcysteine (600 mg TID and nebulization BD) for secretion management per the ICU protocol. To avoid confounding variables affecting sedation and awareness, the only drugs utilized that could effectively interfere with these parameters were fentanyl as a sedative-analgesic and phenytoin or levetiracetam as antiepileptic agents in the doses specified. Notably, no neuromuscular blocking agents were administered to any study patients.

Clinical decisions regarding extubation and tracheostomy were made by the treating ICU team based on standard clinical criteria and were independent of EEG findings. The ICU team making decisions regarding extubation and tracheostomy was also blinded to the EEG findings.

Consent was taken from the relative/attendant of the patient.

All patients underwent limited-duration EEG recordings (1 hour) at two predefined time points: ICU admission postoperative day 1 (POD-0) and day 4 (POD-3). To avoid bias because of fentanyl, we would make sure the EEG was performed after 8–10 hours of stopping fentanyl, to allow time for the drug to be removed from the system. Also, daily sedation breaks were given to limit the accumulation of the drug in the body.

Recordings utilized the standard international 10–20 electrode placement system using cup electrodes or EEG caps, depending on wound condition. Strict impedance checks were performed to ensure signal quality remained consistent regardless of the recording interface. Recording parameters included a low-frequency filter of 1 Hz, a high-frequency filter of 70 Hz, a notch filter of 50 Hz, and a sensitivity of 7 μV/mm. A consultant neurologist, blinded to clinical outcomes and therapeutic allocation, interpreted all EEGs.

Electroencephalography background activity was evaluated across four domains: Continuity (continuous, discontinuous, and burst suppression), dominant frequency (delta, theta, alpha, and beta), interhemispheric symmetry, and reactivity. EEG background evolution was categorized based on changes observed from POD-0 to POD-3.

Improving EEG: Defined as the presence of two or more favorable changes (e.g., improvement in continuity, shift toward faster frequencies, improved symmetry, or restoration of reactivity) without deterioration in any other domain.

Improving EEG: Defined as the presence of two or more favorable changes (e.g., improvement in continuity, shift toward faster frequencies, improved symmetry, or restoration of reactivity) without deterioration in any other domain.

Non-improving EEG: Defined by the presence of one or more adverse changes, such as loss of continuity, shift toward slower frequencies, new asymmetry, loss of reactivity, or no meaningful change in EEG.

Non-improving EEG: Defined by the presence of one or more adverse changes, such as loss of continuity, shift toward slower frequencies, new asymmetry, loss of reactivity, or no meaningful change in EEG.

The primary outcomes were airway management endpoints: Extubation success or failure, and the requirement for tracheostomy. Secondary outcomes included seizure occurrence, ICU length of stay, and early mortality.

The decision to proceed with primary tracheostomy without an extubation attempt was based on the treating team's assessment of neurological airway protection and was analyzed as part of the composite airway failure outcome.

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, United States of America). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Formal sample size calculation was not performed as it was a pilot study, and a total of 43 patients with complete data were included. Because our classification utilizes a mutually exclusive binary structure (where a patient without an improving alpha/beta background trajectory is classified as having a non-improving theta/delta profile), a univariate logistic regression yields perfectly symmetrical, mirrored odds ratios (ORs) for both parameters (OR = 18.41 vs OR = 0.05). Due to the sample boundaries of this pilot study (n = 43), expanded multivariate logistic regression modeling was deferred to prevent statistical overfitting (Fig. 1).

Results

Patients assessed for eligibility (January 2024–July 2025) (Fig. 1).

A total of 43 patients with severe TBI were included in the final analysis. Based on the evolution of EEG background activity between ICU day 1 and ICU day 4, patients were categorized into two groups: Improving EEG (n = 16) and non-improving EEG (n = 27). There were no statistically significant differences in mean age, admission GCS, or the frequency of Marshall CT Score ≥4. However, early EEG background evolution demonstrated a significant association with clinical airway endpoints. Patients displaying a non-improving EEG background trajectory experienced a significantly higher rate of airway failure—encompassing both failed extubation attempts requiring re-intubation and the necessity for a primary tracheostomy—compared to those with an improving background. The unadjusted odds of experiencing airway failure were more than 10 times higher in the non-improving cohort (Table 2).

Airway failure comprised both failed extubation requiring re-intubation and primary tracheostomy. Electroencephalography background evolution demonstrated consistent predictive performance across these clinical pathways, indicating that it identifies neurological airway incompetence rather than a specific procedural outcome. An improving alpha/beta background demonstrated an unadjusted OR of 0.05 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.006–0.47] for extubation failure, with a positive predictive value (PPV) of 92.9% for extubation success. Conversely, a persistent theta/delta background carried an unadjusted OR of 18.41 (95% CI: 2.1–160.3; p = 0.008) for failure, demonstrating a PPV of 58.6% for absolute airway failure.

We compared various clinical and physiological variables between patients with successful extubation (n = 25) and those with airway failure (n = 18). Conventional predictors such as age, admission GCS, ventilator-associated pneumonia, and PaO2/FiO2 ratio did not show statistically significant differences between the outcome groups (Table 3). Excessive airway secretions showed a borderline trend toward failure but did not cross the threshold for strict statistical significance.

The dominant cortical background frequency recorded on POD-3 served as a significant predictor of airway endpoints (Table 4). The manifestation of fast-wave activity (alpha/beta dominance) yielded an exceptionally high predictive probability for successful extubation. In contrast, the continuation of slow-wave activity (theta/delta dominance) significantly increased the hazard of airway failure or subsequent tracheostomy.

Detailed analysis of procedural transitions revealed that while a fraction of patients with improving profiles failed an initial extubation trial, the vast majority were successfully liberated later without surgical intervention (Table 6 in supplementary files). Conversely, the majority of patients in the non-improving slow-wave cohort who failed required subsequent tracheostomy, with a subgroup transitioning directly to tracheostomy without an extubation attempt due to failure to meet basic clinical weaning criteria (Table 6 in supplementary files). Furthermore, an unfavorable, non-improving EEG trajectory tracked closely with a longer mean ICU length of stay and a higher incidence of ICU mortality compared with the favorable, improving cohort.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the early evolution of EEG background activity over the first 96 hours following severe TBI is a robust predictor of airway outcomes. We observed that patients with a “non-improving” EEG trajectory had a greater than 10-fold increased risk of airway failure compared to those with improving backgrounds. These findings suggest that serial limited-duration EEG offers a pragmatic window into global cerebral recovery and successful predictor of extubation, significantly outperforming traditional clinical predictors.

Extubation failure in neurocritical care is frequently driven by “neurological incompetence”—an inability to maintain airway patency and central respiratory drive—rather than pulmonary dysfunction. Our finding that the emergence of alpha or beta activity predicts extubation success is consistent with the work of Oddo et al., who demonstrated that preserved EEG background reactivity is among the strongest predictors of favorable functional outcome in comatose patients.1

Furthermore, the utility of EEG in the Indian critical care setting has been well-established by Kapadia et al., who observed that specific EEG patterns provide critical diagnostic clues in patients with altered sensorium. Our study extends these observations by demonstrating that EEG background features may provide clinically relevant information regarding airway readiness and extubation success.2

While older age is known to predict poor long-term recovery in TBI patients, our study found it did not predict extubation failure (p = 0.42). This suggests a key difference: Long-term recovery depends heavily on age, but keeping the airway open depends on immediate brain function. Our findings show that in the early stages of care, a patient's actual brain activity—measured by EEG—is a much better guide for safe extubation than their age alone.

Our observation that the theta/delta ratio strongly differentiates airway outcomes aligns with quantitative EEG (qEEG) research by Leon-Carrion et al. They identified the “delta-alpha ratio” (DAR) as a critical metric for functional recovery, where delta dominance reflects persistent cortical differentiation failure.3

Recent advancements in neurocritical care have increasingly focused on the use of qEEG to automate and enhance prognostication in severe TBI. Literature published between 2024 and 2025 emphasizes that specific qEEG features—most notably spectral power parameters like the DAR, beta/delta power, and alpha variability—are robust predictors of functional recovery and ICU outcomes. While our study relied on visual interpretation rather than automated software, our classification system serves as a pragmatic clinical analog to these quantitative metrics. By manually tracking the shift from slow-wave (theta/delta) to fast-wave (alpha/beta) dominance, we effectively captured the same fundamental neurophysiological shifts identified by automated DAR and spectral power algorithms. Furthermore, while high-resource centers are transitioning to continuous qEEG, recent evidence suggests that repeated, limited-duration EEGs yield comparable prognostic value regarding functional outcomes and mortality in TBI patients when compared to continuous monitoring. This strongly supports the validity and continued relevance of our serialized, limited-duration approach in resource-constrained environments where advanced computational infrastructure remains unavailable.

We utilized a composite definition for “EEG improvement,” requiring favorable changes in at least two domains (e.g., frequency, continuity, or reactivity). While this criterion is stringent, it was chosen to maximize specificity. In severe TBI, isolated fluctuations in a single domain (e.g., transient frequency acceleration) can occur due to changes in sedation or metabolic status. By requiring concordant improvement across multiple domains, our classification likely captured true structural reintegration of thalamocortical networks rather than transient physiological variability. This multi-parametric approach aligns with the American Clinical Neurophysiology Society (ACNS) guidelines, which emphasize that background assessment involves the synthesis of voltage, frequency, and reactivity.6–8

A unique and clinically significant finding of our study is the disconnect between hemodynamic stability and extubation success. Standard weaning guidelines prioritize hemodynamic independence (e.g., absence of vasopressors) as a key readiness criterion. However, we found that the absence of vasopressor support was identical between success and failure groups (p = 0.64). This highlights a critical limitation in applying general ICU weaning protocols to neurotrauma patients. As noted by Namen et al., neurocritical patients frequently fail extubation despite passing SBTs because these tests assess pulmonary mechanics, not neurological protection.5 Our data suggest that in TBI, standard physiological readiness (respiratory and hemodynamic stability) without concomitant EEG improvement is essentially a “false positive” for extubation readiness.

Our analysis also reinforced the distinction between epileptogenicity and global cerebral dysfunction. Seizure incidence was low and did not differ between improving and non-improving groups (p = 0.83). This is consistent with findings by Foreman et al., who noted that while seizures contribute to secondary injury, the background architecture reflects the overall integrity of the thalamocortical networks.9 A patient may be effectively treated for seizures yet exhibit a malignant, non-improving background that precludes safe extubation.

The timing of extubation attempts did not differ significantly between our groups (11.2 vs 12.3 days; p = 0.23), yet the outcomes were vastly different. Coplin et al. have previously shown that delayed extubation in brain-injured patients is associated with higher rates of pneumonia and longer ICU stays.10 Hence, early tracheostomy is preferred to reduce ICU length of stay. However, our findings challenge this approach for patients with improving neural activity. Since the presence of alpha/beta dominance at 96 hours demonstrated a 92.9% PPV for successful extubation, clinicians may defer the decision for early tracheostomy in this subgroup. This allows for a period of expectant management, potentially avoiding unnecessary surgical interventions in patients destined for successful extubation in the subsequent days.

In the Indian context, Zirpe et al. have highlighted the importance of early tracheostomy in neurotrauma to reduce ICU length of stay, although patient selection remains challenging.11 Our findings imply that a non-improving EEG on POD-3 could serve as the objective biomarker needed to refine this selection. Identifying these “non-improvers” early could prompt earlier tracheostomy, thereby avoiding the morbidity of prolonged, futile intubation attempts.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study has several key strengths that distinguish it from previous research. First, we addressed a critical gap in neurocritical care: The dissociation between respiratory mechanics and neurological airway readiness; we highlighted the unique value of EEG as a measure of “central drive” rather than just pulmonary function. Second, our study utilized a pragmatic, accessible methodology—serial limited-duration EEG—rather than resource-intensive continuous EEG (cEEG). This increases the generalizability of our findings to centers in resource-limited settings where cEEG may not be feasible. Third, we employed a stringent composite definition for “EEG improvement” requiring concordance across multiple domains (frequency, continuity, and reactivity), thereby reducing the risk of misclassifying transient physiological fluctuations as true neurological recovery. Finally, by excluding patients with severe metabolic encephalopathy and those requiring heavy sedation, we minimized major confounders that often complicate EEG interpretation in the ICU.

However, our study also has limitations that merit consideration. First, this was a single-center study with a modest sample size. While we identified a strong signal, the limited number of events precluded a robust multivariable logistic regression analysis to fully adjust for all potential clinical confounders. Second, although serial EEG provided valuable snapshots, it lacks the temporal resolution of continuous monitoring, potentially missing intermittent subclinical seizures or fluctuating mental states between recording sessions. Third, we acknowledge the wide CI associated with our primary OR for airway failure (95% CI: 2.1–149.3). While the lower bound confirms a statistically significant association, the wide upper bound reflects limited statistical precision due to our small sample size. Consequently, while the direction of the predictive signal is strong, the exact magnitude of the effect remains uncertain. These findings are therefore hypothesis-generating, underscoring the need for larger-scale validation studies. Fourth, our cohort was restricted to severe TBI (GCS < 8), and these findings may not be generalizable to patients with moderate head injury or other etiologies of coma. Fifth, the decision to tracheostomize was ultimately clinical; while we blinded the treating team to the specific EEG details, some degree of implicit bias in clinical assessment cannot be entirely ruled out. Sixth, since our EEG had only one neurologist to interpret the EEG, interobserver variability, which is a known problem with EEG interpretation, cannot be completely ruled out. Finally, long-term neurological functional outcomes such as Extended Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOSE) scores were not assessed, limiting evaluation of the relationship between EEG evolution and long-term neurological recovery.

Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate these findings and refine the predictive algorithms.

Conclusion

In patients with severe TBI, the evolution of EEG background activity over the first 96 hours appears to be a valuable indicator of neurological airway readiness and readiness for extubation. Non-improving EEG background is associated with a significantly higher risk of airway failure, even in patients who are seizure-free and hemodynamically stable. Conversely, the high predictive value of an improving background suggests that tracheostomy may be judiciously deferred in these patients to allow for natural airway recovery. These results require validation in larger cohorts.
