ROS-scavenging microspheres loaded with extracellular vesicles for intervertebral disc degeneration therapy
Authors: Chunping A., Haozhe Cheng, Guangzi Chen, Chuang Huang, Lice Wang, Kai Cao, Tao Xu, Zhong Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD), primarily driven by oxidative stress and inflammation, significantly impacts patient quality of life. Current therapies lack efficacy, highlighting the need for novel treatment strategies. This study investigates the protective effect of antioxidant hydrogel microspheres containing black phosphorus (BP) nanosheets and extracellular vesicles (EVs), fabricated using a microfluidic technology-based delivery system, designated as EVs@BPMS. In vitro analyses of EVs@BPMS revealed that BP nanosheets enhanced the antioxidant capacity of the hydrogel microspheres. The EVs@BPMS system functioned through the sustained release of extracellular vesicles. These vesicles collectively mitigated oxidative damage by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS), reducing oxidative stress, suppressing cellular senescence, and ultimately restoring extracellular matrix homeostasis in nucleus pulposus cells. Transcriptomic analysis further elucidated that the microspheres inhibited inflammatory responses via the IL-17-ferroptosis pathway, providing a theoretical basis for the development of targeted therapeutic interventions. In vivo studies confirmed the protective efficacy of EVs@BPMS in a rat model of IVDD, demonstrating substantial attenuation of disc degeneration. The findings underscored the innovative capacity of antioxidant hydrogel microspheres in regulating oxidative stress and maintaining cellular homeostasis. Moreover, they highlighted the potential of these microspheres for clinical application in degenerative disc disease.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04740-4.

Introduction

Intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) has emerged as a predominant contributor to low back pain, a significant global health concern. Moreover, IVDD not only impairs individuals’ quality of life but also imposes a considerable burden on healthcare systems worldwide [1, 2]. Epidemiological studies suggest that IVDD affects a substantial portion of the population, particularly those aged 30 and above, with prevalence rates ranging from 30% to 80%, depending on the specific demographic and diagnostic criteria used [3, 4]. As the global population continues to age, the incidence of low back pain associated with IVDD is expected to escalate. This trend necessitates a thorough understanding of the underlying mechanisms of IVDD and the development of effective treatment strategies.

Current research underscores that the pathophysiology of IVDD is multifactorial, involving genetic, mechanical, and biochemical factors. Among these, oxidative stress has been identified as a critical mechanism contributing to the degenerative process [5]. Elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, apoptosis of nucleus pulposus (NP) cells, and degradation of the extracellular matrix, which collectively exacerbate pathological disc deterioration [6, 7]. Furthermore, the inflammatory microenvironment within the intervertebral disc plays a pivotal role in promoting degeneration; inflammatory cytokines can perpetuate a cycle of damage and cell death [8]. Consequently, therapeutic strategies that both inhibit inflammatory signaling and reduce oxidative stress have become a major research focus in managing IVDD.

Significant efforts have been made to develop therapeutic interventions targeting IVDD. These efforts focus particularly on regenerative medicine approaches, such as the application of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), gene therapy, and biomaterials designed to enhance disc repair[9]. MSCs generate substantial quantities of extracellular vesicles (EVs) that mediate critical intercellular communication [9, 10]. Transplantation of MSC-derived EVs presents significant advantages over whole MSC therapy, notably including their low immunogenicity; lack of tumorigenic potential; and ease of storage and transportation compared to whole MSCs [11, 12]. Thus, MSC-derived EVs emerge as a highly promising cell-free alternative to traditional MSC-based regenerative strategies. While MSC-derived EVs have shown promise in preclinical studies for their regenerative potential, the clinical translation of these therapies is hindered by challenges such as the hostile microenvironment of degenerated discs and limitations in effective delivery systems [13, 14].

Black phosphorus (BP) is a stable allotrope of phosphorus that possesses a layered crystal structure analogous to graphite, where individual layers are stacked via van der Waals forces [15]. The weak interlayer interactions in multilayer BP enable the production of single-layer or few-layer two-dimensional BP nanosheets through liquid-phase exfoliation [16]. These two-dimensional BP nanosheets have been extensively investigated in nanomedicine due to their unique physicochemical properties and exceptional biocompatibility [17–19]. Furthermore, the zero-valent state of phosphorus—which refers to phosphorus atoms in their elemental form—and the large specific surface area afforded by its 2D structure endow BPNS with remarkable ROS scavenging capability, enhancing its efficacy in treating inflammation-related diseases [20, 21]. A recent preclinical study demonstrated that BPNS could effectively eliminate excess ROS and promote subchondral bone regeneration, thereby alleviating osteoarthritis [22].

In light of these considerations, this study seeks to explore the therapeutic potential of combining EVs derived from MSCs with BP nanosheets through physical integration within a gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel matrix. This innovative construct, termed EVs@BPMS, is designed to continuously deliver anti-inflammatory and antioxidant therapeutic agents within the intervertebral disc while simultaneously reducing oxidative stress and inflammation. The antioxidant properties of black BP present a novel approach to addressing oxidative stress in IVDD. Additionally, the regenerative capabilities of MSC-derived EVs offer promising potential for tissue repair in IVDD. The development of this system-level design aims to provide a synergistic effect by targeting the key pathological features associated with disc degeneration.

Materials

Cell culture

Bone marrow MSCs were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (Guangzhou, China). The cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium and Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM-F12; Gibco, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Boster Bio, China). The cells were cultured at 37 °C under a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Additionally, the medium was refreshed every 48 h.

NP cells were isolated from the caudal intervertebral discs of 8-week-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, weighing 200 ± 20 g. Following euthanasia by carbon dioxide inhalation, the skin of the tail was disinfected sequentially with 75% ethanol and povidone-iodine. Under aseptic conditions, NP tissues were carefully excised and isolated using microsurgical instruments. The tissues were then sequentially digested with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by 0.25% type II collagenase for an additional 3 h. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm strainer, centrifuged at 300 ×g for 5 min, and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The cell pellet was resuspended in complete culture medium (DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin), then incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The medium was replaced every 48 h, and cells were passaged when they reached 80–90% confluence.

Isolation and characterization of EVs

MSCs were cultured in EVs-depleted medium for 24 h, after which the conditioned medium was collected. The conditioned medium was sequentially centrifuged at 300 ×g for 10 min and 2,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cells. It was then centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 30 min, at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The resulting supernatant was then subjected to ultracentrifugation at 100,000 ×g for 90 min, at 4 °C, to collect the EVs. The EVs were washed with a large volume of sterile, ice-cold PBS. It was then recovered by a second ultracentrifugation under identical conditions (100,000 ×g; 90 min; 4 °C). For morphological characterization, the isolated EVs were visualized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Hitachi H-800, Japan). Their size distribution and the presence of specific protein markers (CD81, CD63, TSG101) were analyzed by Western blotting. To investigate cellular internalization of EVs, purified EVs were fluorescently labeled with the lipophilic dye DiI (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Labeled EVs were then co-cultured with NP cells for 24 h. Following incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS. The internalization of DiI-labeled EVs was then visualized and photographed using a fluorescence microscope.

Fabrication and characterization of EVs@BPMS

Fabrication of GelMA hydrogel microspheres co-loaded with BP and EVs via Microfluidics. BP nanosheets were purchased from Ruixi biological technology. Mineral oil containing 5% Span 80 was used as the continuous phase. For the dispersed aqueous phase, a 10% GelMA solution was diluted with sterile PBS containing BP nanosheets and EVs, supplemented with 0.5% LAP photoinitiator. This yielded a 5% GelMA solution with final BP nanosheets and EV concentrations of 10 mg/mL and 1 mg/mL, respectively. Both phases were introduced sequentially into a cross-junction microfluidic device; the flow rates were maintained at 50 µL/min (continuous phase) and 5 µL/min (dispersed phase), respectively. The resulting emulsion was collected into a Petri dish and exposed to UV light (405 nm) for 2 min to initiate photo-crosslinking, yielding solid hydrogel microspheres. Following UV exposure, the microspheres were thoroughly washed with a surfactant-based demulsifier to remove residual surfactant and mineral oil. All procedures for the preparation of the microspheres were performed under light-protected conditions at room temperature.

ROS scavenging efficiency

The antioxidant properties of BPMS were evaluated using ABTS•+, DPPH, and hydroxyl radical scavenging assays. The samples were incubated with the respective radical solutions, and the changes in absorbance were measured using a microplate reader. Scavenging efficiency was calculated using standard formulas based on the absorbance differences before and after incubation.

Biocompatibility assessment

The biocompatibility of NP cells with EVs@BPMS was assessed using the Calcein AM/PI Cell Viability Assay Kit (Beyotime, China).

Western blotting and RT-qPCR

Total protein from cell lysates was extracted with RIPA lysis buffer and subjected to Western blot analysis to determine protein expression levels. RNA was isolated using RNAiso reagent from the samples, reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA), and subsequently analyzed by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). The relative mRNA expression of target genes was calculated using the ΔΔCT method.

RNA-seq and bioinformatic analyses

To compare the effects of H₂O₂ treatment alone versus the combined treatment of EVs@BPMS and H₂O₂ on the transcriptome of NP cells, we performed RNA sequencing analysis. Cells were cultured under the specified experimental conditions for 24 h. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Yeasen, China). Subsequent transcriptome sequencing with 150 bp paired-end reads was performed by Majorbio Biotech (China) using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, followed by preliminary data analysis. Bioinformatics analyses were performed using the Majorbio Cloud Platform. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were then identified using DESeq2, applying a screening threshold of |log₂FC| ≥ 1 and p-value < 0.05. Subsequently, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted on significant DEGs.

Mitotracker staining

After treatment, the cells were stained with MitoTracker Green FM (Beyotime, China) at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by nuclear counterstaining with Hoechst 33,342 (Solarbio, China) for 10 min at room temperature. After triple washing with PBS, the samples were immediately imaged using a confocal microscope (Nikon A1R HD25, Japan).

Immunofluorescence (IF)

The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, then permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and then blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin for 30 min. The cells were then incubated overnight (approximately 12–16 h) at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-MMP13 (Proteintech, China), anti-Aggrecan (Proteintech, China), and anti-GPX4 (Proteintech, China). Following three washes with PBS, the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Yeasen, China). Samples were then observed, and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope. Quantitative analysis of the fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ software.

ROS staining

ROS levels were quantified using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe (Beyotime, China). After treatment, the NP cells were incubated with serum-free medium containing 10 µM DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 30 min, in the dark. Samples were then observed, and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope. Quantitative analysis of the fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ software.

Senescence β-galactosidase staining

SA-β-Gal activity was assessed using a commercial Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (Beyotime, China) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The number of senescent cells was then quantified under bright-field microscopy.

Mitosox red mitochondrial superoxide detection assay

Mitochondrial ROS levels in NP cells were detected with the Mitosox Red mitochondrial superoxide indicator (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Images were captured with a fluorescence microscope.

Intracellular iron evaluation

Intracellular iron was quantified with the FerroOrange fluorescent probe (Dojindo, Japan), adhering to the supplier’s guidelines. Imaging was performed on a fluorescence microscope to capture the fluorescence signal.

Mitochondrial membrane potential measurement

Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed with the JC-1 Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Following 24-h H₂O₂ exposure, cells were stained with JC-1 solution and incubated for 20 min at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. After two washes with JC-1 buffer at room temperature, cells were covered with PBS. Images were captured on a fluorescence microscope and analyzed quantitatively using ImageJ.

Establishment of a rat IVDD model

All animal experimental procedures were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Animal Research Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No. 4949). The design and implementation of the animal experiments adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines. A 21-gauge needle was inserted vertically through the annulus fibrosus into the NP. It was then continuously rotated 360° and held in place for 60 s to induce caudal intervertebral disc injury. Five experimental groups were established in this study: (i) a sham group with no needle puncture; (ii) an IVDD group with needle puncture followed by PBS injection; (iii) an MS group with needle puncture followed by injection of microspheres; (iv) a BPMS group with needle puncture followed by injection of microspheres loaded with BP nanosheets; and (v) an EVs@BPMS group with needle puncture followed by injection of microspheres co-loaded with BP nanosheets and EVs. All microsphere injections were performed immediately following needle puncture using a micropump. The injection volume for each disc was 10 µL. Caudal IVDD was assessed at 8 weeks post-operation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Results are reported as mean ± standard deviation unless specified differently. To compare multiple groups, we used one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA); when the ANOVA indicated statistical significance, pairwise comparisons were further examined with Tukey’s post hoc test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Characterization of BP nanosheets

Two-dimensional BP nanosheets were employed to assess their biological effects. The morphology of the BP nanosheets was examined by TEM and SEM (Fig. 1A). TEM analysis indicated an average lateral size of 121.23 ± 20.44 nm for the BP nanosheets (Fig. 1B-C). AFM revealed the corresponding two- and three-dimensional topographical features (Fig. 1D). Raman spectroscopy displayed three characteristic peaks at approximately 359.1, 434.3, and 461.6 cm⁻¹, corresponding to the A₁g, B₂g, and A₂g vibration modes, respectively (Fig. 1E), which is consistent with previous reports. Energy-dispersive X-ray elemental mapping demonstrated a homogeneous distribution of phosphorus (P), oxygen (O), and nitrogen (N) in the BP nanosheets (Fig. 1F). These results collectively confirm the successful acquisition and basic physicochemical properties of the BP nanosheets used in this study.

Fabrication and characterization of EVs@BPMS hydrogel microspheres

We further prepared antioxidant-containing hydrogel microspheres, fabricated using a microfluidic-based delivery system, which contain BP nanosheets and EVs (Fig. 2A). To determine the optimal loading of BP nanosheets in the microspheres, four formulations were prepared with BP nanosheet concentrations of 0, 2.5, 5, and 10 mg/mL, labeled as MS, BPMS2.5, BPMS5, and BPMS10, respectively. The antioxidant capacity of these microspheres was assessed using ABTS•⁺ and DPPH radical scavenging assays. ABTS•⁺ scavenging activity increased in a concentration-dependent manner, reaching a maximum of 51.4% for BPMS10 (Fig. S1). Likewise, DPPH assay results showed enhanced radical scavenging with higher BP nanosheet content, where BPMS10 exhibited 47.68% scavenging efficiency (Fig. S2). Degradation studies revealed similar erosion profiles across all formulations, indicating that the inclusion of BP nanosheets did not adversely affect the cross-linking stability or degradation rate of the hydrogel network (Fig. S3). These results confirmed the successful preparation of antioxidant-functionalized microspheres, which hold promise for ROS-responsive therapeutic and EVs delivery. Western blotting confirmed the presence of classical EVs markers CD63, TSG101, and CD81 in EVs, validating the successful isolation of EVs from MSCs supernatants (Fig. S4). TEM images displayed the characteristic vesicular structure of EVs (Fig. 2B), while SEM revealed the surface morphology of the lyophilized EVs@BPMS (Fig. 2C). EVs@BPMS microspheres had an average particle size of 111.46 ± 6.73 μm (Fig. S5). Radical scavenging assays further indicated that EVs@BPMS possessed efficient ROS scavenging capabilities (Fig. 2D). EVs@BPMS exhibited a sustained release profile, characterized by an initial burst release of approximately 55% of EVs within the first five days, followed by a gradual release over the subsequent 15 days (Fig. 2E). Fluorescence imaging confirmed that the released EVs were effectively internalized by NP cells (Fig. 2F). Additionally, EVs@BPMS showed no significant adverse effects on cell proliferation or viability, indicating favorable cytocompatibility (Fig. 2G-H).

EVs@BPMS alleviate oxidative stress-induced ECM degeneration and inflammation in NP cells

We evaluated the therapeutic effect of EVs@BPMS on H₂O₂-induced extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation and inflammation in NP cells [23]. NP cells were treated with 500 µM H₂O₂ for 24 h to establish an oxidative stress model. As shown in Fig. 3A–B, Western blot analysis revealed that BPMS markedly reduced the expression of MMP3, MMP13, COX-2, and iNOS, while increasing the levels of Collagen II and Aggrecan compared to the H₂O₂ group. Notably, EVs@BPMS exhibited more pronounced efficacy in restoring ECM homeostasis and suppressing inflammatory responses. Consistent with the Western blot data, quantitative PCR analysis further confirmed that EVs@BPMS more effectively attenuated H₂O₂-induced ECM degradation and inflammatory responses compared to BPMS alone (Fig. 3C). ELISA was used to quantify the secretion of key inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. Treatment with EVs@BPMS significantly reduced the levels of these cytokines in the culture supernatant compared to the H₂O₂ group, highlighting the system’s strong anti-inflammatory effects (Fig. S6). In subsequent high-density NP cells cultures, histological staining (safranin O and toluidine blue) revealed that H₂O₂ stimulation markedly suppressed proteoglycan expression. EVs@BPMS treatment effectively reversed this inhibition, demonstrating its protective role in maintaining ECM integrity (Fig. S7). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that EVs@BPMS treatment more effectively reduced MMP13 expression and enhanced Aggrecan levels in H₂O₂-stimulated NP cells, as compared to treatment with BPMS (Fig. 3D-E). These results collectively demonstrate that EVs@BPMS conferred significant protection against ROS-triggered damage in NP cells under an oxidative stress microenvironment.

EVs@BPMS inhibit oxidative stress-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and cell senescence

Oxidative stress plays a key role in the pathogenesis of IVDD. Current evidence indicates that degenerated intervertebral discs exhibit excessive ROS generation, which establishes a pathological oxidative microenvironment. This redox imbalance induces NP cells senescence and facilitates ECM degradation, thereby accelerating IVDD [5]. Accordingly, H₂O₂ was employed to generate excessive ROS, mimicking oxidative stress conditions in vitro, in order to investigate the protective effects of this microsphere complex on NP cells. Intracellular ROS levels were assessed using DCFH-DA and Mitosox fluorescent probes. The results revealed that H₂O₂ treatment markedly elevated intracellular ROS levels relative to the control group. By contrast, all treatment groups exhibited significant ROS reduction, with the EVs@BPMS group exerting the most potent antioxidant effects (Fig. 4A–D). The results demonstrate the robust ROS-scavenging and antioxidant capacities of EVs@BPMS. To assess associated changes in mitochondrial structure, morphology was evaluated using MitoTracker staining and confocal microscopy. Control NP cells presented intact mitochondrial networks. In contrast, H₂O₂ stimulation resulted in significant damage, substantially reducing both network complexity and mitochondrial number, which is consistent with the autophagic clearance of damaged mitochondria. Both BPMS and EVs@BPMS treatments restored mitochondrial networks and increased mitochondrial number, with EVs@BPMS demonstrating superior efficacy in recovering mitochondrial length and density (Fig. S8). Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed with JC-1 staining. H₂O₂ treatment markedly decreased the red fluorescence intensity of JC-1 aggregates while enhancing the green fluorescence of its monomers (Fig. 4E–F), a pattern consistent with the early stages of apoptosis in NP cells. Treatment with BPMS or EVs@BPMS significantly elevated JC-1 aggregate formation and concurrently reduced monomer levels. Notably, EVs@BPMS induced a markedly higher aggregate-to-monomer ratio compared to the BPMS group. Additionally, SA-β-gal staining revealed that all treatment groups effectively attenuated NP cells’ senescence. The EVs@BPMS group demonstrated the most potent anti-senescence effect, as evidenced by the lowest percentage of SA-β-gal-positive cells (Fig. 4G–H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that EVs@BPMS effectively mitigated oxidative stress-induced apoptosis and cellular senescence in NP cells.

EVs@BPMS protect NP cells by inhibiting IL-17-ferroptosis axis

To uncover the mechanism through which EVs@BPMS ameliorates the oxidative microenvironment of intervertebral discs, we performed RNA-Seq transcriptomic analysis on NP cells treated with or without EVs@BPMS following H₂O₂ exposure. Comparison between the H₂O₂ and H₂O₂ + EVs@BPMS groups revealed 195 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), comprising 61 upregulated and 134 downregulated genes (Fig. 5A). GO analysis indicated that these DEGs were significantly enriched in the intracellular ferritin complex and cellular iron ion homeostasis (Fig. 5B). Meanwhile, KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in the IL-17 signaling pathway and ferroptosis (Fig. 5C). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed significant enrichment of genes associated with the IL-17 signaling pathway (Fig. S9). Based on these results, we hypothesize that the IL-17 signaling pathway and ferroptosis represent key underlying mechanisms by which EVs@BPMS ameliorate the oxidative microenvironment in intervertebral discs. To verify the RNA-seq findings, we further investigated the specific regulatory effects of EVs@BPMS on the IL-17 signaling pathway and ferroptosis. As shown in Fig. 5D–E, H₂O₂ stimulation markedly elevated the levels of key pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-17, IL-6, and IL-1β, suggesting that oxidative stress induces a robust inflammatory response. However, EVs@BPMS treatment notably suppressed the activity of the IL-17 signaling pathway. Moreover, it upregulated GPX4 expression while downregulating ACSL4 expression in NP cells. This regulatory pattern was partially reversed by EVs@BPMS treatment (Fig. 5F–G). Consistent with the Western blot results, PCR analysis further demonstrated that EVs@BPMS markedly rescued the H₂O₂-induced dysregulation of key genes associated with ferroptosis (Fig. S10). Furthermore, immunofluorescence analysis revealed significantly reduced GPX4 intensity in the H₂O₂ group, whereas EVs@BPMS treatment markedly increased its expression (Fig. 5H and S11). As shown in Fig. 5I and S12, H₂O₂ treatment triggered intracellular iron accumulation, whereas EVs@BPMS intervention effectively attenuated this iron overload. Cellular lipid peroxidation is a hallmark of ferroptosis, where the metabolite levels of reduced glutathione (GSH) and malondialdehyde (MDA) serve as central biochemical indicators of this process [24]. EVs@BPMS treatment markedly reversed the H₂O₂-induced elevation in MDA and reduction in GSH levels in NP cells (Fig. 5J). We used recombinant IL-17 protein to further validate the effect of EVs@BPMS on IL-17 signaling pathway. As shown in Fig. S13, western blot results suggested the inhibition of IL-17 signaling and ferroptosis by EVs@BPMS was also reversed following the addition of IL-17 protein. These findings suggested that EVs@BPMS specifically targeted the IL-17 signaling pathway and ferroptosis to remodel the oxidative microenvironment of intervertebral discs.

EVs@BPMS alleviate the progression of IVDD in vivo

To evaluate the therapeutic effect of EVs@BPMS in a rat model of IVDD, a needle puncture injury was performed in the caudal disc to induce the condition. Imaging and histological analyses of the surgical site were carried out eight weeks after surgery. Micro-CT was used to evaluate disc height changes. Compared with the sham group, the IVDD group showed a marked decrease in intervertebral disc height (Fig. 6B and C). However, varying magnitudes of disc height restoration were observed across all treatment groups (MS, BPMS, and EVs@BPMS), with the EVs@BPMS group exhibiting the most significant recovery. T2-weighted MRI revealed a significant decrease in signal intensity in the acupuncture-treated group. Conversely, the BPMS and EVs@BPMS groups exhibited increased signal intensity relative to the IVDD control group. Assessment via the Pfirrmann grading system demonstrated that the EVs@BPMS group exhibited the most pronounced signal intensity recovery (Fig. 6D-E). H&E staining revealed severe structural degeneration within the IVDD group, manifesting as NP atrophy, annulus fibrosus disruption, and loss of normal disc architecture (Fig. 6F). Additionally, SO&FG and Alcian blue staining demonstrated substantial proteoglycan depletion in the NP. Histological scoring demonstrated varying degrees of therapeutic efficacy across treatment groups. Notably, the EVs@BPMS group exhibited the lowest scores, indicating superior efficacy in alleviating IVDD progression relative to other groups (Fig. 6G). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed dysregulation of GPX4 and Collagen II expression in the IVDD group, suggesting extracellular matrix degradation characteristic of IVDD. Conversely, EVs@BPMS treatment promoted a significant restoration of GPX4 and Collagen II expression (Fig. 6H-I). Histological analysis via H&E staining revealed no signs of inflammation, necrosis, or other pathological alterations in the major organs, corroborating the favorable in vivo biosafety profile of EVs@BPMS (Fig. S14). In conclusion, the integration of radiological and histological results demonstrates that the EVs@BPMS hydrogel possesses significant efficacy in alleviating IVDD progression in the rat model.

Discussion

IVDD is a prevalent condition that significantly contributes to chronic low back pain, affecting millions of individuals worldwide. This degenerative process is characterized by the deterioration of the intervertebral discs, leading to a cascade of pathological changes, including loss of hydration, matrix degradation, and inflammation [25]. The interplay of mechanical and molecular factors, particularly oxidative stress, plays a crucial role in the progression of IVDD. Consequently, understanding the underlying mechanisms and exploring innovative therapeutic strategies are essential for effectively managing this debilitating condition, ultimately improving the quality of life for affected patients.

This study investigates the potential of antioxidant hydrogel microspheres as a novel therapeutic approach for IVDD, specifically focusing on their capacity to mitigate oxidative stress and restore NP cells functionality. By employing microfluidic technology to deliver EVs and BP nanosheets, the study aims to develop a sustained release system that can effectively modulate the inflammatory microenvironment and enhance matrix synthesis in degenerated discs. The subsequent findings underscore the therapeutic potential of these microspheres regarding cellular behavior and matrix regeneration, laying the groundwork for further discussion on their implications for intervertebral disc therapy.

BP nanosheets combine potent ROS reactivity with favorable biocompatibility, the latter attributed to their degradation into benign phosphate ions. This unique combination of properties positions BP nanosheets as an ideal functional component for engineering advanced antioxidant hydrogels [26, 27]. Our results showed that hydrogel microspheres loaded with BP nanosheets exhibit remarkable antioxidant properties. These results validate the successful engineering of BP nanosheet-based antioxidant microspheres. Hydrogel microspheres loaded with BP nanosheets exhibited potent antioxidant properties, confirming the successful engineering of these BP-based antioxidant microspheres. This design positions them as an ideal vehicle for EVs delivery, while concurrently endowing the system with ROS-responsive therapeutic capabilities. Furthermore, EVs derived from MSCs have demonstrated established therapeutic efficacy in the treatment of IVDD [28, 29]. A recent investigation revealed that MSC-EVs mitigate apoptosis triggered by endoplasmic reticulum stress via the activation of the AKT and ERK signaling pathways [29]. Our results showed that the release behavior of EVs from EVs@BPMS exhibited a distinct biphasic pattern, characterized by an initial burst release of approximately 55% of the encapsulated EVs within the first 5 days, followed by a sustained slow-release phase. The initial burst is designed to rapidly deliver a high concentration of EVs to the degenerated disc tissue, which can immediately counteract acute inflammatory responses and promote early cellular repair. However, the limitations of such burst release for long-term therapeutic applications are also evident, as it may lead to insufficient EV availability during later stages of the chronic disease. Subsequently, the therapeutic efficacy of the EVs@BPMS system against IVDD was evaluated. Notably, EVs@BPMS were found to effectively preserve extracellular matrix homeostasis in NP cells under conditions of excessive ROS activity.

Intriguingly, transcriptomic sequencing analysis revealed a potential mechanism whereby EVs@BPMS mitigate IVDD via the modulation of ferroptosis and the IL-17 signaling pathway. Ferroptosis is a novel form of regulated cell death that is distinct from apoptosis, autophagy, and necrosis [24, 30]. Mechanistically, ferroptosis is initiated by the excessive accumulation of intracellular Fe²⁺, which drives the overproduction of ROS and lipid peroxidation, ultimately leading to cell death [31, 32]. More importantly, emerging evidence indicates that ferroptosis is a primary mechanism contributing to IVDD. In parallel, IL-17 exerts its biological effects by binding to its cognate receptor, subsequently leading to the activation of downstream NF-κB and MAPK signaling cascades. These pathways, in turn, drive the transcriptional upregulation of mediators that promote inflammation and extracellular matrix catabolism. As a pivotal pro-inflammatory cytokine operating within an intricate network, IL-17 is recognized as a central player in autoimmune and inflammatory pathogenesis, underpinning a spectrum of conditions including psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, and ankylosing spondylitis [33–35]. Furthermore, IL-17 can exacerbate oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, thereby promoting ferroptosis [36]. Zhao et al. found that IL-17 A treatment significantly increased intracellular ROS levels and suppressed the expression of Collagen Ⅱ and GPX4, whereas the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 reversed the IL-17 A-induced ROS elevation and restored the expression of Collagen Ⅱ and GPX4 [37]. Our results suggested the inhibition of IL-17 signaling and ferroptosis by EVs@BPMS was also reversed following the addition of IL-17 protein. In the present study, we demonstrated that EVs@BPMS mitigate oxidative stress-driven IVDD, potentially by targeting the IL-17-ferroptosis axis.

In contrast to traditional antioxidant therapies, the mechanistic insights gained from this study reveal a novel strategy for harnessing the antioxidant capacity of BP, representing a significant advancement in the field of regenerative medicine. The innovative application of EVs-loaded antioxidant hydrogel microspheres not only enhanced the antioxidant defense of NP cells but also effectively restored extracellular matrix homeostasis within an inflammatory microenvironment.

However, several limitations of the present study merit consideration and highlight directions for future research. First, intervertebral disc is the body’s largest avascular organ, and the degradation behavior and long-term safety of BP nanosheets in low-metabolic environment remain unclear. In follow-up studies, we will conduct a comprehensive evaluation of the in vivo biosafety profile of BP nanosheets to facilitate its potential clinical translation. Second, although the therapeutic efficacy of EVs@BPMS has been validated in a rat IVDD model, its performance in large animal models and potential for clinical translation require validation. Accordingly, future studies will incorporate extended observation periods and comprehensive immunotoxicity profiling to advance translational development.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings demonstrated the significant therapeutic potential of EVs-loaded antioxidant hydrogel microspheres in mitigating IVDD both in vitro and in vivo. By effectively targeting oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways, this innovative strategy offered a novel therapeutic avenue for disc degeneration. The EVs@BPMS system effectively restored matrix homeostasis and reduced oxidative stress induced by H₂O₂. Mechanistically, the EVs@BPMS inhibited the progression of IVDD by suppressing the IL-17-Ferroptosis signaling axis. Future investigations should focus on validation in large animal models to pave the way for clinical translation, ultimately improving patient outcomes and advancing the field of regenerative medicine.
