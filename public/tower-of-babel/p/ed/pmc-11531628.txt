Assessing the energy system's greenhouse emissions via the health of Smart Territories and Cities
Authors: Lou Martinez Sancho, Gilles Maignant, Laurence Vanin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this paper is to understand how the main drivers of air pollution and greenhouse emissions impact public health in a Smart Territory — meaning a technologically integrated city or neighborhood — as well as how to design an optimized energy system model beyond only data by including the holistic experience of its people through phenomenology and ethics. We understand that a Territory and a City is a complex system where mathematical tool modeling known as Design of Experiment (DOE) and its optimization solutions are required to establish causality and identify the variables that have a broader impact on public health so that mortality rates due to air pollution are reduced. DOE's statistical branch is a novel methodology when applied to energy systems and the design of Smart Territories and Cities. Because of it, we have been able to demonstrate how energy emissions, capacity and Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE) affect the mortality rate and create the foundations for building fully decarbonised energy systems enhanced by mathematical solutions. However, the Cartesian approach of extrapolating from models which align themselves with economic growth alone will not solve the energy dilemma. In fact, the current energy transition project is based on outdated archetypes that lead to scary futures. To course-correct the problematic energy model of a Smart Territory, phenomenologists and creative imagination need to be cultivated and put into action together with mathematical modeling, leading to vectors of positive change and adaptation. Only then might the energy models designed to be reproducible could became ethically virtuous and therefore accepted by the Territory's co-dwellers within the chain of causalities.Keywords: Smart city, Health city, Low carbon energy, Phenomenology, Public health, Air pollution, Smart Territory, Design of experiments

Introduction

Energy systems have a significant impact on the atmosphere of a Territory and Cities, both in terms of air pollution and the overall outcomes of public health [1]. The combustion of fossil fuel in power plants, transportation and buildings is a major source of pollution and greenhouse gas emissions [2]. At the same time, we need energy to maintain a high development index, while also reducing inequalities and fostering prosperity. Due to that, energy systems have a defining role in the future health of Territories and tomorrow's cities. We understand health on several different levels, including personal, environmental, economic and social [3]. While technological research and disruptive innovations are being developed and implemented to reduce the need for fossil fuels in tandem with more environmental protection, this form of future urbanization has a strong influence on local health as well as the health of the entire globe, with high-level impact on mortality rates.

On one hand, cities attract millions of people with new jobs, potential prosperity and other benefits offering abundance. But on the other hand, millions more people worry that humanity is losing touch with nature and endangering our own species' survival alongside the planet's, with the claiming degrowth to be the only solution [4]. Proposed climate change innovations tend to be driven by technology and science. Clearly, environmental issues need to be addressed. The question is: how do we best implement these new energy sources to reduce harmful impacts on people, their cities and the most vulnerable in Territories? What are the parameters impacting the largest mortality rates per energy system? Is it more critical the impact of the capacity of production itself, CO2 emissions, or the cost of electricity? Is it possible to engineer an ideal energy system that supports growth, and reduces mortality rates and health problems, while simultaneously keeping the benefits of access to low-carbon, low-cost energy?

Even if science and technology enable us to design and propose an ideal energy system for a given Territory or City, the underlying question is: how will people living in the Territory, City perceive a clean atmosphere? What ethical implications will we encounter?

To answer the energy dilemma described above, we have conducted a quantitative study to identify the key high-level variables impacting mortality rates in a given Smart Territory, using the energy production data of Tennessee, USA, as a use case. The objective is to understand which energy production systems have the biggest impact on local mortality due to pollutants, and if factors such as cost, and energy capacity production have a cause-effect relationship with yearly mortality rates by given type of energy — fossil fuel versus low-carbon.

In the first part of this study, we have analyzed the correlation and cause-relations of three parameters within mortality rates stemming from energy production. A DOE has been performed to understand causality through mathematical means [5].

Once the key variables of causality have been determined, an ideal energy system was modeled for the same Territory. Energy modeling and engineering sciences are not always familiar to the city's researchers and leaders but are critical in comprehending the impacts of proposed solutions. Contrarily, the link between quantitative and qualitative research in the fields of energy and health in smart Territories and Cities remains relatively poor when compared to the knowledge and operational solutions presented by environmental questions, and even more particularly by those posed about energy or the next urban theory in decrement of the health of cities [6]. Our approach is aligned with the mathematical abstraction specialists generating energy models and with the organizational thinkers in charge of urban health used to a sanitary or spatial analysis of Territories and Cities.

Therefore, in the third part of this study, we have identified first steps to tackle the question of how the atmosphere and perceptions of a Territory thanks to epistemology, ethics, phenomenology, and urban planning could be consider beyond solely quantitative approaches.

The challenge

The World Bank Development Report of 2010 pointed out that Smart Territories and Cities produce over 50 % of the world's CO2 emissions [7]. From 2010 to 2050, the number of buildings will likely double, and, unfortunately, air pollution and climate change are two interrelated environmental problems, both caused by human activities. We define air pollution as the release of harmful substances such as particulate matter, nitrogen oxides, sulfur dioxide and ozone into the atmosphere. In conjunction, air pollution contributes to climate change by releasing greenhouse gasses, such as carbon dioxide and methane, into the atmosphere [8,9].

Furthermore, air pollution leads to the formation of smog, which exacerbates the effects of climate change by trapping more heat, and directly impacts public health through conditions such as asthma. A well-known example of this has been described by Christine Corton about the disaster of killer smog in 1950's London, highlighting the difficulties of gaining attention from the population and public policymakers despite hard data indicating that a sample cubic inch of air contained 340,000 sooty particles [10].

The difficulty of clean-air politics was that unlike sewage or water, air pollution was never a candidate for major public engineering projects. Until the 21st century, the answer has only been regulation, which positioned legislators against two of the largest pillars of the Capitalist economic model (i) the free market and (ii) private property, both of which are embedded in the sacred of hearth and home [11,12]. Human development and the abundance model have brought value to society. However, it has been based on fossil fuels and incredibly cheap coal, with those smokiest of fuels burning right in people's own firesides. That meant every historic attempt at properly enforcing anti-smoke legislation was doomed until the Clean Air Acts of the 1950's and 1960's. Even after the so-called “Great Killer Fog” of 1952, the government was unconvinced it had a role to play. Despite 12,000 additional deaths, Harold MacMillan — then a Conservative minister — grumbled about public expectations and suggested to a committee, “We cannot do very much, but we can seem to be very busy, and that is half the battle nowadays” [13].

The 21st century concept of a Smart City, combined with the awareness of a city having quantifiable health, had led to a variety of utopian technological solutions which promised to improve the efficiency and sustainability of our Territories [14]. The range of the technologies and strategies available are wide, from air quality sensors that monitor pollution levels, to providing real-time info to residents in a Territory to promoting sustainable transportation options like public transit and cycling, and walkable neighborhoods as envisioned by the 15 Minute City concept, which helps reduce vehicle emissions [15]. Promoting green spaces, improving building efficiency and reducing the use of energy-intensive technologies are also part of the arsenal available to solve air pollution in Smart Territories.

Despite any technical solutions and policies available, air pollution and public health issues are still growing worse. Consistently we observe the lack of an holistic approach that would address this energy dilemma. Maria Neira, Director of the Department of Public Health, Environmental and Social Determinants at the WHO highlights the impacts of air pollution on public health: “Air pollution is a silent public health emergency. It is time to act decisively to reduce the toll of human lives from this invisible killer. The world cannot afford to continue with business as usual” (2022) [16]. Her words clearly emphasize the urgency of the situation.

From a global perspective, Territories and Cities are already transforming as they move towards a low carbon future, which will, in turn, transform public health organizations. The new paradigm shifts — built on the pretext that the previous ones were polluting — are geared towards the guarantee of a model that will improve the health of individuals and optimize the Territories’ environments. Therefore, understanding the key variables impacting mortality rates linked to any designs of an ideal energy system will better support policy makers undertaking systemic transformations with a scientific base. Some of them are currently under specific deadlines to decarbonize and have already started consultations.

However, exploring viable techno-economic-social solutions integrating ethics and philosophy are the not-to-be-missed approach. Aristotle, who formulated one of the most influential virtue ethics theories, viewed virtues as achieving eudaimonia — or well-being — which can be more deeply understood as humans flourishing towards the ultimate goal of a good life [17]. A techno-scientific and phenomenological analysis of a Smart City's health would allow us to construct an ideal energy system that needs to be enhanced by ethics in order for it to be accepted and sustained “Well-behaved citizens will find in Ethics a systematic account of principals by which they hold that their conduct should be regulated,” (Russell, 1945) [18].

Statistical analysis

Designed experiments (DOE) are a useful branch of statistics in establishing cause-and-effect relationships for a given data set, which, in turn, help optimize the response variable. There is extended literature about using the DOE method for industry, but less so for energy, public health or sustainability [[21], [22], [23]]. In our case, the Y (output) stands for the mortality rate due to greenhouse gas emissions and consequent air pollution related to a given energy production source. The experiment includes PV (Photovoltaic) non-tracking, onshore wind, PV tracking with storage, CCGT (Combined cycle gas turbine), CHPO (Combined head and power), coal, advanced nuclear, OCGT (Open cycle gas turbines), Hydro Large, Hydro Pumped, PV non-tracking with storage, utility-scale battery (1 h), utility-scale battery (4 h) and offshore wind with and without storage (see Table 1). The variables considered are to showcase the interactions amongst them and the outputs of greenhouse emissions, capacity production and the cost of the energy systems.

DOE will be used in this context to understand how to optimize variables of the energy system in terms of mortality rate. In this case, it is very costly to perform a scalable quantifying experiment in a Smart Territory, therefore comprehending the optimal configuration of an energy system and its supporting causality is made possible through DOE techniques. Here once more, we employ engineering tools to assess a complex question related to the health of Smart Territories [6]. If we understand the variables and energy sources that produce a higher impact, it will help support the design of an optimized energy system and therefore a healthier Territory, which will lead to a better impact made by policymakers in their decision process.

Before creating the design and collecting data, we have defined the clear objective as understanding how several factors — energy capacity factor, CO2 emissions and LCOE (levelized cost of electricity) per energy solution — affect the mortality rate and identify the variables that are more impactful before designing and modeling an ideal decarbonized energy model. Phenomenological analysis

According to Merleau-Ponty, “The world is there prior to any analysis I could have done of it” [29] which implies a phenomenological study beyond the mere analysis of data. The Smart City and Territory and its transition to a healthier, less polluted system offers an incredible opportunity to phenomenologists for learning through practical experience about the energy flow of a city.

Developing modern energy projects requires not just technical expertise but also a deep understanding of the people impacted. This is especially true for projects utilizing novel technologies like advanced nuclear reactors or any novel energy solution. It's important to recognize that some communities might be hesitant to participate due to past experiences with research projects. They may feel “researched-out" and undervalued (Clark, 2008) [30].

This case study explores a phenomenology approach. The phenomenology strategy emphasizes a clear vision from the outset, leveraging a “phenomenological framework" as defined by Merleau-Ponty [29]. What I am doing as someone that experiences the city and the world is the experience as is. Phenomenology plays an essential role by sensing, describing before any scientific and technological knowledge could take place. That perceptional knowledge needs to be put in place into a form that is relevant and addresses the challenge faced by the community and its policy. The scale of impacts starts with the individual body as Merleau-Ponty teaches us “Everything that I know about the world, even though science, I know form a perspective that is my own”.

A crucial step is identifying stakeholders. We began with a “bottom-up" approach, engaging individuals within the community, while simultaneously connecting with local authorities at all levels (federal, state, and county).

Energy projects are often subject to both political agendas and regulations. Boxelaar et al. (2006) and Whitman (2008) provide a foundation for phenomenology engagement and its role in managing natural resources [43], [31,32].

However, it's important to remember that “community" is a multifaceted concept. It includes people who share a location, a common interest, or even a specific identity (Harrington et al., 2008) but communities are constantly evolving, shaped by interactions between individuals, societal structures, and the environment. Recognizing this fluidity is essential (Cleaber et al., 2018) [33].

Therefore, our approach began by focusing on individuals within the community. This emphasis on individual perspectives proved to be a pivotal element of the overall strategy. Once the phenomenologist and perception of individuals perceptions are known it is time to interconnect them with the relevant science and technology.

Consequently, we are learning from engagement with the Territory, and we need to acknowledge that we are personally involved in the study, that “we build perception out of the perceived” [29]. The momentum of imagination [34] [35] might open the door to discovering and proposing epistemological breakthroughs that can solve the energy dilemma, moving from a top-down approach issued by policymakers and/or technological solutions, towards a grass-roots approach rooted in the human experience and its imagination as its starting point.

The correlogram in Fig. 4 and the 3D correction plot in Fig. 3 show a positive correlation between carbon intensity and mortality rate, a low positive 0.18 correlation between LCOE and mortality rate, and the lowest negative correlation of −0.11 between capacity production and mortality rate. Therefore, we will focus our analysis of linear regression on the highly correlated variant to better understand the relationship between mortality rate and carbon intensity.

However, care must be taken to not mix correlation and causality [36,37]. To Kant, the concept of causality requires that an A is such that another B derives from the first variable following an absolutely universal rule [37]. In this case study as presented in Fig. 5 and Table 5, we observed a low correlation between LCOE and mortality rates, such that it is positive, the highest cost in the energy system, and the highest mortality rate. There was a low correlation as well between capacity production and mortality rate, which means that the lower the capacity factor, the lower the mortality rate. And there was a strong correlation between carbon intensity and the mortality rate.

The next three steps of the analysis will try to probe the causality relationships and determine if the three factors are produced by the same cause or not, in accordance with Kant's causality definition. Beyond the mathematical notion of causality, bringing back the etymology of cause as the reason for the response gets to the question of why. However, we know that cause and effect are not independent, and that there exist objective elements beyond the human experience [38]. Reducing carbon intensity will reduce the level of mortality, but it does not describe the human experience of that effect, nor the analysis of the energy system as a global model [43], [31,32]. Since the Industrial Era, we have positioned our energy systems as independent from lived experience, as disconnected from the universe. Dagget (2019) highlighted how the energy systems' applications are not easy to understand [39].

As described in Fig. 1, this study involved the application of DOE with a total of 32 runs, while taking into account three variables, each with two levels impacting the mortality rate. The factors are CO2 emissions, capacity factor and LCOE — annotated as A, B, and C, respectively — when performing statistical testing.

We have created a full factorial model with 3 factors and 4 replicate applied to the model randomized. The 4 replicates being mortality rates over 4 periods as available in the 2022 Tennessee Air Quality Epidemiology Report issued by TN Department of Health [24,25]. The study of three factors at two levels with a complete plan consists in running all the dots of the u cube, or 23 ∗4 = 32 runs. The replicates increase the power of the test as it improves the estimated error and the notice of the system. Unfortunately, we have chosen only 4 replicates as we do not have enough data due to the complexity and nature of the problem identified. The run tests were performed utilizing Minitab software [26].

The EIA expects the US energy production market to grow 21 % by 2050. As renewables will make up 48 % of this increased supply and their production will remain intermittent, energy providers as utilities face the dilemma of having to choose which energy sources to invest in among all the choices that exist in order to complement renewables [28]. Because of that, some utilities and energy providers request a new set of characteristics from energy generation: flexible, decentralized, reliable energy sources. From our study and the results above, we understand that this flexibility is not the main impactful criteria in protecting the environment and reducing mortality rates. But a portfolio strategy with a mix of affordable baseload production from advanced nuclear and geothermal together to balance out the intermittency of renewables is an optimized model. However, without including the human factor in the solver equation, we will not be able to successfully tackle the energy dilemma posed by the likes of London's 1950's fog crisis.

Traditional scientific definition of cause and effect, defined by Bernard's view [41], struggle to capture the complexity of modern energy system. This case study from Tennessee has demonstrates its own limitations. While traditional models offer a quantitative picture, they do not account for the specific socio-economic and geographic realities of the people living and experiencing the region [24,25]. This analysis aligns with the work of phenomenologists who argue that cause and effect are not objective concepts, but rather how we interpret our experiences [42,43]. Residents, policymakers, and leaders may feel powerless to address health issues without fundamentally changing the entire socio-economic model, even when reinforced with data.

As Alain Gras and Charles-Francois Mathis [44,45] suggest, a new way of thinking about energy systems is needed. We must move beyond purely technical and socio-technical perspectives. For example, the state of Tennessee, like many others, seeks a net-zero carbon future. However, transitioning from coal to cleaner energy is not simply technical. Coal represents an entire societal model. It's embedded into the local life, from job creation to energy production. While detrimental to health, it has undeniable benefits. Conversely, new, decentralized energy systems, reliant on data and AI, lack the same tangible connection for residents. This “immateriality" fuels the NIMBY (Not In My Backyard) phenomenon [45] when residents oppose advanced nuclear plants or wind farms despite their environmental and public health benefits.

Therefore, shifting to a sustainable future isn't just about replacing fossil fuels; it's about creating a new society driven by a virtuous energy socio-economic system. As Gaston Bachelard [29] suggests, our experience of a place shapes our perception and acceptance of new systems. For example, a specific street corner can evoke a wealth of memories and emotions, creating a deep connection to a place. Similarly, a coal-based community may feel a strong attachment to its coal plant, a value that mathematical models cannot capture.

Fortunately, architects and urban designers are leveraging new tools like AI and the metaverse to pre-conceptualize energy transformations. However, Edgar Morin [43] cautions that a truly successful transition requires a more humble, open approach. We must integrate diverse knowledge systems and acknowledge the inherent complexity of energy systems.

Both rigorous quantitative and qualitative analysis in this study provides valuable insight into the relationship between energy production variables and mortality rates with phenomenology. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the study reliance on specific datasets, such as the Department of Health in Tennessee [20], USA what may introduce biases due to data limitation in a single geography area. Thus, the use of advanced statistics has been privileged to reduce this limit in collaboration with phenomenology and stakeholders' engagement to ensure the rigor and relevance of the study.Secondly, the study integrates phenomenological perspectives to understand the human experience of energy systems that remains challenging. Phenomenological approaches emphasize subjective experiences. Therefore, this study has defined a novel phenomenological framework to allow replicability and reproducibility of the study. As the energy model has been optimized statistically and it is replicable according to Bachelard’ epistemology [34] it should be considered a virtuous model which supports citizen acceptability. A further step will be to use the same framework in any other smart territory to confirm it.

Conclusion

The quantitative analysis done in this study presents valuable insights into the relationship between energy production key variables and mortality rates due to air pollution produced by an energy system. This study is based on published data from the Department of Health Tennessee and Tennessee Air Quality Epidemiology Report. After statistically confirming the normality of the data, the study has built a DOE analysis to further understand causality relationship among variables in a complex energy system. The study demonstrated significant correlations between carbon intensity and mortality rates, reinforcing the potential positive impact of reducing carbon emissions and air pollution on the public health of a smart territory. However, study limitations have been identified as only local data in a single territory as Tennessee has been included in the study. The methodology is well described to reproduce a similar analysis in other territory.

Beyond the quantitative analysis, a new phenomenology framework has been presented in this study providing a new view to understanding the human experience and the socio-economic context of a given energy system. This study's second limitations are well acknowledged in the complexity of human perceptions and societal organizations. That's why the study highlighted the importance of phenomenology in envisioning and implementing science and technology driven solutions that aligned with dwellers experiences and societal values. To achieve so, the integration of disruptive technologies as AI and the metaverse with phenomena could pre-conceive and shape our future smart territories.

Ultimately, this study proposed an interdisciplinary approach and engaging with diverse stakeholders from the experience of the people to the territory which includes quantitative analysis, phenomenological insights, and socio-economic considerations. We can aspire to tackle the energy dilemma prioritizing public health, environmental sustainability, and societal well-being.
