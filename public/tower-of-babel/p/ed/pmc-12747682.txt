Assessing the Potential for Human Pathogen Contamination of Agricultural Fields by Dust From Animal Feeding Operations
Authors: Francisco Garces‐Vega, R Chris Owen, David Heist, Régis Pouillot, Hao Pang, Yuhuan Chen, Jane M Van Doren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFugitive dust from concentrated animal feeding operations (CAFOs) is a potential source of produce contamination with human pathogens. Our objective was to develop a general framework and methodology for predicting preharvest produce contamination with human pathogens arising from fugitive dust derived from a nearby CAFO. We applied this framework to a case study of lettuce grown in proximity to a bovine CAFO. We implemented the EPA's AERMOD dispersion model at two locations, assessing dust dispersion and deposition over a 30‐day period across 100 km2 surrounding a 10,000‐animal CAFO. E. coli O157:H7 contaminated lettuce servings grown on fields within the 100 km2 were predicted using a risk assessment approach, integrating data about dust deposition, pathogen contamination in cattle manure, and pathogen survival on crops. To contextualize the results, infectious servings were predicted based on the average number of E. coli O157:H7 per serving and the E. coli O157:H7 dose−response relationship. Dust from CAFOs has the potential to deposit across at least 100 km2. E. coli O157:H7 dispersion and deposition are impacted by wind direction and velocity, emission factor, and prevalence and concentration in dust. Mean E. coli O157:H7 concentrations on preharvest lettuce were predicted across the 100 km2 and declined considerably with distance from the CAFO. Surviving E. coli O157:H7 on preharvest lettuce arise primarily from dust deposited in the 2 weeks before harvest. Our modeling approach provides a flexible framework that can be adapted to any location, providing quantitative information to inform foodborne outbreak investigations and the development of prevention strategies.Keywords: CAFO, E. coli O157:H7, EPA‐AERMOD, fugitive dust, leafy greens

Introduction

Microorganisms can move through the environment via multiple mechanisms, including transport on dust by wind. Dust from animal feeding operations, including concentrated animal feeding operations (CAFOs), has been suggested as one of the potential sources of contamination of crops, including leafy greens associated with foodborne outbreaks (Duan et al. 2009; Gil et al. 2015; Nguyen et al. 2022; Waltenburg et al. 2021). Some leafy green outbreaks have been associated with Escherichia coli O157:H7 (Chen et al. 2023; Jay‐Russell et al. 2018), a pathogenic bacterium known for causing serious infections in humans (National Center for Emerging and Zoonotic Infectious Diseases 2024).

Previous field studies have examined dust dispersion around CAFOs (Berry et al. 2015; Dungan 2010; Wei, Aggrawal, Bond, and Atwill 2023; Yanamala, Miller, Loneragan, Gragg, and Brashears 2011). Berry et al. (2015) and Wei et al. (2023) examined the presence of human pathogens in dust via air sampling. Neither Berry et al. (2015) nor Wei et al. (2023) recovered E. coli O157:H7 in air samples proximate to CAFOs, but they recovered generic E. coli in those samples with decreasing numbers of bacteria with distance from the CAFO. Further, Berry et al. (2015) recovered E. coli 0157:H7 from lettuce grown 61, 122, and 183 m (200, 400, and 600 ft) from a CAFO, but no data linking the pathogens to the CAFO were available.

In 2019 and 2020, the FDA (in collaboration with the University of Arizona, the Wellton‐Mohawk Irrigation and Drainage District, local growers, industry groups, the California Department of Food and Agriculture, the University of California, Davis, Western Center for Food Safety, and agricultural stakeholders in the Central Coast of California) started two longitudinal studies to improve food safety through enhanced understanding of the ecology of human pathogens in the environment that may cause foodborne illness outbreaks in these produce growing regions (FDA 2019, 2020). Results of these studies are being analyzed, and reports will be forthcoming (FDA 2024).

In the present study, we developed a general framework and methodology for predicting preharvest produce contamination with human pathogens arising from fugitive dust derived from a nearby CAFO. Our predictive model systematically evaluates the impact of bovine CAFO facility characteristics and environmental conditions on the geographic distribution and magnitude of dust and pathogen dispersion and deposition. Our approach integrates data and information on human pathogen contamination, survival, and dose−response relationship with that for dust dispersion and deposition. To accomplish this, we integrate EPA's regulatory dispersion model AERMOD (Cimorelli et al. 2005) into our probabilistic risk assessment framework. We apply this approach to a case study on lettuce grown on fields near a bovine CAFO in two leafy green growing regions of the United States, estimating E. coli O157:H7 concentration per plant at the time of harvest. To place these estimates in context, we predict infectious servings from lettuce harvested from fields adjacent to or nearby the CAFO. Our study complements previous field studies and provides a foundation for understanding forthcoming results from the collaborative longitudinal studies (FDA 2024).

Materials and Methods

As a first step, we model the generation, transport, and deposition of dust from a CAFO. Within the CAFO's environment, dust is generated mainly by animal movement and the movement of vehicles that lift dust from the ground. Once dust is airborne, it can be transported by air currents and wind, both vertically and horizontally. Over time, dust particles will be deposited and accumulate on downwind surfaces. We estimate atmospheric concentrations and deposition rates on an hourly basis over 30 days and estimate the daily and cumulative deposition during that time.

As a second step, we overlay experimentally derived human pathogen prevalence and concentration estimates on CAFO‐derived dust, assuming the dust primarily arises from dried or partially dried bovine manure that may be present on the ground or in manure piles within the CAFO. Once that dust is deposited onto leafy green plants growing in nearby fields, we evaluate the survival of the human pathogens deposited, based on experimental data, from deposition day to harvest.

Finally, the leafy green plants in our model are virtually harvested, and divided into servings, based on intake data from What We Eat in America, the food consumption survey section of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. Predicted infectious leafy green servings from potentially contaminated lettuce are estimated from the doses in servings and an outbreak‐derived dose−response relationship for E. coli O157:H7.

Model Settings

The transport of dust is modeled with the AERMOD (Version 22112) dispersion model, which is a steady‐state Gaussian plume model (Cimorelli et al. 2005). Gravitational and wet deposition is also modeled in AERMOD and is primarily a function of the plume concentration, particle density and size, and precipitation rate. Dust transport and deposition are computed on an hourly basis, using meteorology obtained from each site, representing the unique variability in wind speeds and directions (the main driver for transport) for each site.

Emission Characterization

Emission Source

The baseline emission source for dispersion of dust and pathogens was defined as an area source of circular shape of 25 ha (∼61.8 acres, radius = 282 m); representative of a bovine animal feeding operation with 10,000 animals (hd). The dust emissions were modeled as a uniform distribution in the horizontal domain, with a release height at ground level (i.e., 0 m), representing dust generated from the movement of animals and vehicles in the operation. The emission source is characterized by the particle size distribution (PSD), the emission factor, and the particle density.

The emission factor we used is a weighted average emission factor of 81 g/(hd×day), estimated by Wanjura et al. (2004). It corresponds to the weighted average of 24 samples conducted over 4 days; under normal operation of the CAFO (as far as we know), considering vehicular traffic and dust abatement measures (if implemented) in the CAFO. The emission factor reported by Wanjura et al. (2004) in kg/(1000hd×day) was transformed to g/(m2×s) using an animal surface usage of 25 m2/hd. Guidelines for animal surface usage range from 14 to 46.5 m2/hd (American Dairy Science Association American Society of Animal Science, and Poultry Science Association 2020).

The particle density of composted manure was assumed to be 2.29 g/cm3, estimated fromWeindorf and Wittie (2003). This value corresponds to the average of two manure samples with 15 subsamples; density was estimated using a submersion method in hexane.

Animal Operation Size

To assess the impact of animal operation's size on dust dispersion and deposition, operations holding 1000, 10,000 (base‐line), and 100,000 animals were compared. All other variables, including animal surface usage, emissions factor, PSD, and particle density, remained constant.

Diurnal Emission Cycle

The emission factor described in the Emission source section is the weighted average of measurements taken during the day and night times (Wanjura et al. 2004). However, emissions from CAFOs have a day‐night pattern related to animal and operational activities (Wanjura et al. 2004), as well as environmental conditions. Using data from Wanjura et al. (2004), we can approximate this pattern beginning at midnight with 6 h night, 15 h day, and 3 h night, with emission factors of 0.46 kg/(1000hd×hour) and 5.17 kg/(1000hd×hour) for night and day times, respectably. To evaluate the impact of the day‐night pattern on long‐term deposition, we compared the predicted deposition under the same environmental conditions considering the day‐night variation in emission source with that predicted considering a weighted average of 81 kg/(1000hd×day) (i.e., 3.35 kg/[1000hd×hour]).

Domain

The modeling domain consists of a 10×10 km area (100 km2), including the emission sources (i.e., the emission source may or may not be centered). Concentrations and deposition values are computed at 625 receptors (a 25×25 grid with a 400 m resolution at ground level [0 m]). The model run consists of a 30‐day window of observation within the growing‐harvest season of crops (i.e., November to March in Arizona, March to November in California), with concentrations and deposition values calculated on an hourly basis across the domain and modeling time frame. While lettuce, and other crops, may grow on the ground for longer periods, we consider that the largest amount of dust depositing on the foliage will occur during the last 30 days before harvest, because of the foliage surface, and may be the most relevant from the food safety perspective.

Meteorology

To understand the range of potential dust transport, we considered two sites within the main produce‐growing regions in the United States, Arizona (Site 1) and California (Site 2); near locations previously associated with E. coli O157:H7 outbreaks and close to at least one bovine CAFO. For each site, environmental variables, including surface‐level meteorology (e.g., winds, rainfall, humidity), upper air meteorology, and land surface characteristics, are used as inputs to AERMOD, affecting both dispersion and transport as well as deposition rates. Data from each site were used for model inputs, which included surface (i.e., SFC files) and upper air (i.e., PFL files) data from Arizona (2014−2018) (https://azdeq.gov/node/2127 Yuma Airport [32.660N 114.608 W]) and California (2016−2020) (https://ww2.arb.ca.gov/resources/documents/harp‐aermod‐meteorological‐files Salinas Airport [36.664N 121.609 W]).

Impact of Precipitation

Precipitation events can play a significant role in the wet deposition of dust. Thus, during the window of observation at each of the sites considered, wet deposition was examined. Precipitation events can also impact the emission rate and uptake on leaf surfaces of potential pathogen loads. To illustrate the impact of precipitation, we quantified the cumulative wet deposition (g/m2) and the cumulative wet fraction deposition (i.e., wet deposition/total deposition × 100) as a percentage during the window of observation.

Pathogen Dispersion and Survival Assessment

To assess the dispersion of human pathogens from a CAFO, we assumed that the dust on the operation is primarily derived from (dry) manure. Therefore, we used data on E. coli O157:H7 prevalence and concentration from untreated cattle manure to define prevalence and concentration in the dust derived from the CAFOs (Jay‐Russell et al. 2018). The E. coli O157:H7 concentration per plant was estimated considering the daily deposited dust, the average E. coli O157:H7 concentration based on the Jay‐Russell et al. (2018) study, the plant coverage or footprint, and plant density (i.e., 9 plants/m2).

Plant coverage was estimated based on common agricultural practice for lettuce. We used the Lettuce Horticulture Information Leaflets from NC State Extension (Sanders 2021), which states “Regardless of the row width, the in‐row spacing between each head lettuce plant should be 12 inches” (∼30.5 cm); plant coverage was assumed to be 80% of the surface area, and the number of plants per square meter at the time of harvest was estimated to be nine. In the uncertainty analysis, we estimated E. coli O157:H7 deposition considering variable plant coverage. Variable plant coverage was estimated considering a linearly increasing plant footprint of radius from 0 to 16.66 cm from planting to harvest (i.e., 60 days), and we considered in the analysis the last 30 days of the growing cycle (from 8 to 16.66 cm radius).

Once human pathogens in the dust are deposited on plants in the field, the pathogen concentration will generally decrease (Jeamsripong et al. 2019). The final concentration of E. coli O157:H7 on plants at the end of the window of observation was estimated assuming microbial inactivation was log‐linear with a decay rate of 0.23 log10 CFU/g×day. This decay rate was derived from literature data; data sets were retrieved from various trials conducted under a wide range of environmental conditions (e.g., different geographic regions, different lettuce cultivar, and different experimental procedures) (Bezanson et al. 2012; Chase et al. 2019; Erickson et al. 2010; Islam et al. 2004; Jeamsripong et al. 2019; Moyne et al. 2011; Oliveira et al. 2012; Weller et al. 2017). Tables or digitized graphs were used to calculate daily die‐off rates from literature reports. Considerable differences in estimation of die‐off rates were observed among the studies; the reported daily die‐off rates range from 0.01 to 0.62 log10 CFU/g×day with a mean of 0.23 log10 CFU/g×day. The survival of E. coli O157:H7 was evaluated from the time between deposition and the end of the window of observation. For example, E. coli O157:H7 deposited on day 1 will start to decline on day 2 and will decline for 29 days, while pathogens deposited on day 30, the day of harvest, are assumed not to decline appreciably before harvest. The final concentration of E. coli O157:H7 was estimated as the sum of the surviving populations, under the assumption of net decline. This means that over time E. coli O157:H7 could grow and decline depending on multiple variables but, the net result is a decline (Jeamsripong et al. 2019; Mishra, Pang, Buchanan, Schaffner, and Pradhan 2017).

To investigate the impact of variability in E. coli O157:H7 prevalence and concentration in (dry) manure/CAFO dust, we implemented a MonteCarlo simulation with 1000 iterations for the 10,000‐animal operation. At each iteration, the prevalence and concentration were derived from the respective distributions reported by Jay‐Russell et al. (2018). The impact of this variability was described using relevant quantiles (e.g., 50%, 75%, 90%, 95%, and 90%) for the plant E. coli O157:H7 concentration. For any receptor, the concentration estimate indicates the upper‐bound concentration that is expected for a given percentile.

To illustrate the potential impact of uncertainty in the E. coli O157:H7 decline rate on our predictions, we evaluated and compared results using the estimated mean decline rate (i.e., 0.23 log10 CFU/g×day) with those assuming no decline and a faster decline (0.00, 0.43, and 0.62 log10 CFU/g×day).

Potential Public Health Impact

To translate the estimated E. coli O157:H7 concentrations on preharvest plants grown near a CAFO into estimated public health impact, we evaluated the number of E. coli O157:H7 infectious servings arising from consumption of lettuce grown on fields near the CAFO, following postharvest processing. Fields evaluated were 5 Ha (∼12.4 acres), each yielding approximately 450,000 plants, corresponding to 1.8 million servings (assuming 4 servings per plant). We evaluated one field from each of the 625 receptors (a 25×25 grid with a 400 m resolution). We considered the impact of postharvest washing (40% reduction) and washing plus sanitation with chlorinated water (94% reduction) on the E. coli O157:H7 dose per serving, where treatment efficacies are based on the results from Mokhtari et al. (2022). We assumed the whole field is processed in the same way.

We used the E. coli O157:H7 dose−response model used by Pang, Pouillot, and Van Doren (2022), which was adapted from the beta‐Poisson model by Strachan et al. (2005), to determine infectious servings. Briefly, the risk per serving is described by a Beta‐Poisson distribution with parameters α = 0.0571 and β = 2.2183, and the consumed dose was estimated from the plant E. coli O157:H7 concentration adjusted for postharvest processing described above. The expected number of infectious servings was estimated by Infectiousservings=Servings×mean(p), where p is the risk per serving. However, there is unavoidable uncertainty and variability on the dose−response E. coli O157:H7 model (Teunis et al. 2008). We considered the estimates from Strachan et al. (2005) to place our estimates of the contamination in context.

Data Processing

Model results from AERMOD were processed using R (R Core Team 2022) and RStudio (Rstudio Team 2022) with dplyr (Hardley Wickham et al. 2023), ggplot2 (Hadley Wickham 2016), viridis (Garnier et al. 2021), and openair (Carlslaw and Ropkins 2012) libraries. For surface plots, the response variable was binned using the automatic algorithm of the viridis and ggplot2 libraries.

When comparing our model results with measurements in other publications, estimating values at points different than the output grid was necessary. To estimate those values, linear interpolation using the interp function of the akima library (Akima and Gebhardt 2022) was used.

Impact of Location

Location, as defined by its physical topography and environmental conditions, has a major impact on the dispersion and deposition of dust generated from a CAFO. Wind rose, dust concentration in the air above the ground, and dust deposition for Site 2 are presented in Figure 4, considering a 10,000‐animal operation. In contrast with Site 1, the wind pattern was highly directional, with winds predominantly from the southeast (∼46%) and the northwest (∼23%) during the 30‐day window of observation, and with 1.3% of the time of calm conditions (Figure 4A). Dust concentration in the air ranged from 9.8×10−3 to 921 µg/m3 (Figure 4B) and cumulative deposition ranged from 1.21×10−3 to 32.9 g/m2 (Figure 4C) over the 100 km2 area of observation. Due to the differences in meteorology, the pattern of dispersion and deposition at Site 2 differs substantially from that for Site 1. At Site 2, dust is predicted to be deposited less widely across the area of observation. Instead, dust is concentrated in a narrow directional plume toward the northwest and to a lesser extent, the southeast. Also, at Site 2, we find the wind pattern changes during the growing season (October and November, see Supplementary Materials for examples), where wind coming from the southeast becomes important in the latter half of the season, and wind from the northwest decreases in frequency and speed.

Precipitation Events

Rain events are relatively rare for these two locations. Rain events lasted a few hours on 2 days of the 30 days examined for Site 1 and 5 days of the 30 days examined for Site 2. The intensity of all but one of these events was between 0.5 and 3 mm; one event (Site 1) was ∼9.9 mm. We note that the large volume rain event in 2018 was unusual when compared to the historical record and close to the total normal precipitation for March (NOAA 2021). Such precipitation events have a limited impact on deposition. Figure 6 illustrates the wet deposition fraction as a percentage of total deposition and cumulative wet deposition fraction at the end of the 30‐day window of observation for a 10,000 animals’ operation. At Site 1, the smaller rain event contributed approximately 2% to the deposition at five receptors impacted by the rain. The larger rain event contributed less than 0.5% to the total deposition for the few receptors impacted. Site 2 had multiple rain events that contributed up to ∼11.25% of total deposition for the specific receptors impacted.

Impact of Day‐Night Patterns

Human and animal activity are different during day and night hours, resulting in different emission patterns for different times of the day. Emissions may be larger when animals are being fed because of the movement of trucks and animals, for example. Considering a 15‐h day 9‐h night pattern (i.e., starting at midnight 6 h night, 15 h day, 3 h night); the day‐night emission patterns have a limited effect on the cumulative deposition over the 30‐day window (Figure 7) for a 10,000‐animal operation. As a result, we used the weighted average emission factor when evaluating human pathogen contamination, described below.

Human Pathogen Contamination of Plants

Average E. coli O157:H7 contamination estimates were derived considering dust deposition, E. coli O157:H7 prevalence and concentration in the dust, and E. coli O157:H7 decline. At the end of the 30‐day window of observation in the 100 km2 area surrounding a 10,000‐animal operation, plant E. coli O157:H7 contamination ranged between 1.42×10−8 and 2.30 pathogen/plant in Site 1 (Figure 8A), and 1.5×10−9 and 4.5 pathogen/plant in Site 2 (Figure 8B). Results indicate that all receptors in the 100 km2 evaluated have the potential to be contaminated with E. coli O157:H7, although predicted concentrations vary significantly. Very small, predicted concentrations (i.e., <6.9×10−7 log CFU/plant, or <1 E. coli O157:H7/receptor [160,000 m2]) indicate the probability of E. coli O157:H7 being present is less than one per crop cycle at those receptors. We also found that E. coli O157:H7 on the day of harvest primarily arose from deposition in the last week or two because of the E. coli O157:H7 decline with time.

For both sites, the vast majority of the 100 km2 area is predicted to have fewer than 1 E. coli O157:H7 per 100 plants, that is, ∼89% in Site 1 and ∼85% in Site 2, after 30 days of deposition.

The California Leafy Greens Handler Marketing Agreement (LGMA 2021) recommended the implementation of a buffer zone as a risk management tool, where no harvest of leafy greens for human consumption may occur within a certain distance of the CAFO. The LGMA‐recommended buffer zones from the edge of a CAFO are 366 m (1200 ft), for CAFOs between 1000 and 80,000 animals, and 1609 m (1 mile), for CAFOs larger than 80,000 animals. Our model predicts the concentrations of pathogens on lettuce; outside the LGMA‐recommended buffer zones in both of the 100 km2 study areas were estimated to be ≤1 E. coli O157:H7 per plant for a 10,000 animal operation (Figure 8) for most of the receptors.

Potential Impact on Public Health

To place the predicted plant E. coli O157:H7 concentrations in context, we estimated the number of infectious servings arising from lettuce harvested and processed from 5 Ha fields (∼12.4 acres; ∼450,000 plants, ∼1.8 million servings) within the 100 km2 study area around a 10,000‐animal operation CAFO. Postharvest processing of lettuce generally involves washing or washing plus sanitation steps, intended to remove debris from the surfaces (washing 40% pathogen reduction) and reduce microbial loads (sanitation 94% pathogen reduction). Our results for a 10,000‐animal operation are summarized in Figure 10, where the CAFO operation perimeter and LGMA‐recommended buffer zones are provided for context. We also included, for comparison, results for “no treatment,” which simply reflect the prevalence and concentration of E. coli O157:H7 at the point of harvest. Postharvest processing, when applied appropriately, is predicted to substantially reduce the number of E. coli O157:H7 infectious servings arising from E. coli O157:H7‐contaminated fugitive CAFO dust, especially when washing plus sanitation is performed. For Sites 1 and 2, the highest risk areas (>1000 infectious servings in 1.8×106 servings) are fully contained within the LGMA buffer zone when considering a 10,000 animal operation and postharvest processing that included washing plus sanitation appropriately applied. This is not the case for Site 2 when only washing is applied postharvest, where some of the highest risk areas extended outside the buffer zone along the primary wind direction. The estimates in Figure 10 were derived using the average prevalence and concentration of E. coli 0157:H7 in the CAFO dust. Results for different‐sized operations and considering variability in the prevalence and concentration of E. coli 0157:H7 in manure are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Fields predicted to have a large number of infectious servings will generally be closer to the CAFO, downstream of a CAFO with highly directional wind in the area, near a very large CAFO, and/or near a CAFO with a large prevalence and concentration of E. coli O157:H7 in the cattle manure.

Comparing Our Results With Field Studies

Berry et al. (2015) reported the fraction of positive samples for E. coli O157:H7 on leafy greens between the edge of a 6000‐animal CAFO and 180 m from the edge of the operation. The reported positivity at 180 m was 1.8% for E. coli O157:H7, from 10 g samples of leafy greens, although no data linking the detected pathogens to the CAFO was available. While the CAFO location of Berry et al. (2015) observations differed from the sites examined in this study, as well as the type of leafy greens, we can predict the concentration of E. coli O157:H7 per plant, and estimate the positivity of whole plants at 180 m from the edge of Sites 1 and 2 for comparison. We predicted the positivity of E. coli O157:H7 between ∼1% and ∼3% for Site 1, and between ∼0.05% and 4.8% for Site 2. The differing ranges reflect directional differences around the 180 m boundary of the operations. Our predictions are consistent with the observations, in terms of order of magnitude, with the field study, especially considering differences in location (impacting dust dispersion and deposition), size of operation (6000 vs. 10,000 animals), and limited information on pathogen prevalence and concentration in the specific CAFO manure/dust.

Wei, Aggrawal, Bond, and Atwill (2023) also sampled air at different distances from the operation and found no E. coli O157:H7 when sampling 6000 L. For Sites 1 and 2, we predict average pathogen concentrations in the air at 366 m (i.e., the LGMA‐recommended buffer zone) from the 10,000 animals operations to be between 5.17×10−3 and 1.09×10−2 CFU in 6000 L; which is consistent with their findings as those concentrations are too small, therefore, unlikely to be detected with a 6000 L sample.

Uncertainty Analysis

Animal Surface Usage

Animal surface usage can vary from CAFO to CAFO and within a CAFO over time. Recommended animal surface usage ranged from 14 to 46.5 m2/hd (American Dairy Science Association et al. 2020). Animal surface usage is unlikely to affect dust emission unless it dramatically modifies animal movement. Different surface usage affects the estimated size of the CAFO, which can affect the fraction of emitted dust that falls within the CAFO perimeter or the recommended buffer zone.

AERMOD Uncertainty

Apart from the emissions area (i.e., animal surface usage) and emission rate (i.e., the pathogen concentration in dust and dust density), there are several model inputs and settings that can contribute to variability and uncertainty in the resultant predicted pathogen loading. In addition to the total emissions, the meteorology, terrain/elevation information, and the deposition inputs are drivers of variability in the dispersion modeling. The two meteorological data sets used here show two distinct patterns. Site 1 includes low velocity winds from multiple directions over much (∼80%) of the 30‐day window of observation, where the most dominant wind direction is from the NW (21.5%). Site 2 has bidirectional winds, with nearly all winds (>75%) are from either the NW or SE. These two wind patterns result in a more uniform dispersion and deposition pattern, that is, almost omnidirectional for Site 1, and a highly directional dispersion and deposition pattern for Site 2. The wind data for both Sites 1 and 2 are collected at nearby airports, ranging from 60 to 110 km from the study sites, such that there may be differences in the winds at the CAFO locations evaluated and the airport measurement site. As described above, the rate of deposition is also a function of several meteorological variables and the size distribution of dust. Lower wind speeds or larger dust size distributions would result in more deposition close to the source, whereas higher wind speeds or smaller dust size distributions would result in deposition at greater distances. Finally, terrain features can also impact the meteorology. For example, strong diurnal wind patterns can result from mountain‐valley flows, and terrain can impede dust transport or divert plumes around terrain features.

Human Pathogen Concentration in Dust and Dust Density

In the absence of specific data on the fraction of CAFO‐generated dust that is comprised of (dry) manure and how that fraction may vary with particle size, we assumed that the concentration of E. coli O157:H7 in the dust of all particle sizes was the same as the concentration in (dry) untreated cattle manure and that the manure fraction determines the dust density. Our assumption provides an upper limit to both. Smaller fractions of manure in the dust would reduce dust density and ultimately human pathogen concentrations on plants and infectious servings predicted. As an example, if 50% of the dust were comprised of (dry) manure, the estimates of E. coli O157:H7 concentration per plant would be smaller, as shown in Figure 11.

Cumulative Dust on Plant

In our model, we assume that leafy green plants cover 80% of the surface area and that all dust deposited over the 30‐day growing period remains on the plant or ground with no possibility for exchange between plant and ground or among plants. Assuming a fixed size per plant of 80% of the surface area leads to an overestimation of dust deposited on the plants at harvest because the plants are smaller earlier in the season. However, this assumption has a much smaller impact on the human pathogen concentration per plant, Figure 12, because E. coli O157:H7 deposited early in the growing cycle, when the plant is smaller than 80% of the surface area, are not predicted to survive to the end of the growing cycle.

It is likely that dust movement at the individual plant level is dynamic, that is, there is some exchange among plant and ground and among plants in close proximity and these exchanges have the potential to alter the exact E. coli O157:H7 concentration on any given plant but it will not alter the mean concentration per plant across a 400 m × 400 m area, which is the resolution of our model. The potential wash‐off effect of rain or overhead irrigation was not considered in this study, given the limited information objectively quantifying those effects.

Human Pathogen Survival on Plant

During preharvest, the decline rate of the deposited E. coli O157:H7 is variable and uncertain; it depends on multiple factors, including the location of the pathogen on the leaf, weather conditions, bacterial strain, and so on. We estimated the mean decline rate (i.e., 0.23 log10 CFU/[g×day]) from available experimental data and assessed the impact of uncertainty based on the observed range of decline rates reported (i.e., between 0 [no decline] and 0.62 log10 CFU/[g×day]). Results (Figure 13) show that decline rate uncertainty has an impact on the estimated E. coli O157:H7 concentrations per plant at the time of harvest. More regionally and seasonally specific data and predictive models could reduce this uncertainty in the future.

Limitations

The methodology we developed requires specific data from the location and CAFO of interest; we have shown that results will generally depend on location and CAFO size. This study focused on bovine CAFOs, E. coli 0157:H7, and lettuce. Estimates of infectious servings used to provide context, considered mean reductions from postharvest processing, and did not include exceptional scenarios in which the lettuce was exposed to time‐temperature conditions that would support growth of E. coli O157:H7 or failures in postharvest processing, such as insufficient sanitation reagents or cross‐contamination. Similarly, rain or irrigation events hours before harvesting that may promote growth of E. coli O157:H7 were not considered in this framework.

Application to other types of CAFOs, pathogens, or produce would require modifications, for example, emission characteristics, pathogen decline kinetics, and production and consumption characteristics for the specific type of produce. We modeled direct deposition of fugitive dust from a nearby CAFO onto preharvest lettuce. Evaluation of the potential for CAFO dust to indirectly contaminate preharvest produce through, for example, dust deposition onto nearby surface water, which may be used for irrigation or involved in field flooding, requires additional considerations and is beyond the scope of the present study.

Conclusion

We developed a general framework and methodology to assess the potential for human pathogen contamination of preharvest produce grown in agricultural fields arising from direct deposition of fugitive CAFO dust. We applied our methodology to bovine CAFOs with E. coli O157:H7 potentially contaminated manure, surrounded by agricultural fields growing lettuce for human consumption. We explored the impacts of environmental factors on our results by evaluating results at two different locations during two different growing seasons within the United States. We found that fugitive dust from bovine CAFOs has the potential to be deposited across large areas (at least 100 km2). Dust dispersion and deposition are impacted strongly by emission factor, wind direction, wind velocity, and the size and activities of the CAFO. Mean E. coli O157:H7 concentration on preharvest lettuce plants arising from deposited CAFO dust is predicted to decline rapidly with distance from the CAFO and time since deposition. As a result, E. coli O157:H7 remaining on lettuce plants at harvest from deposited CAFO dust are predicted to have been deposited primarily in the last week or two before harvest. Our modeling approach provides a flexible framework that can be adapted to any location, pathogen, type of produce, or type/size of CAFO. Results of our analysis and use of our approach provide critical information to inform the development of risk management strategies and inform foodborne outbreak investigations.
