Spatial organization and trafficking dynamics of ANX/BUPS-RALF-LLG signaling complexes during pollen tube growth
Authors: Patricia L Seitz, Li-Jia Qu, Thomas Dresselhaus, Liang-Zi Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractKey message Recruitment of RALFs and LLGs for assembly at the apical plasma membrane of pollen tubes is orchestrated by ANXs/BUPSs through endomembrane trafficking. AbstractPollen tube growth requires precise regulation of cell wall integrity, which is maintained by ANX/BUPS-RALF-LLG signaling complexes. While structural and biochemical studies have revealed physical interactions between these components, their spatial organization and assembly dynamics in growing pollen tubes remain unclear. Here, we systematically investigated the subcellular localization and endomembrane trafficking of ANX/BUPS-RALF-LLG complex components through transient expression studies in tobacco pollen tubes. We demonstrate that each component exhibits distinct subcellular distribution patterns: RALF4/19 peptide ligands predominantly localize after secretion to the cell wall, ANXs/BUPSs receptors are enriched at the apical plasma membrane (PM), while GPI-anchored LLG2/3 co-receptors exclusively co-localize to cytosolic vesicles. Through co-expression studies, we show that ANXs/BUPSs recruit both RALFs and LLGs that localize to the same vesicles to the PM, indicating their role as master organizers and scaffolds of signaling complex assembly during pollen tube growth. RALFs stabilize ANX/BUPS-LLG interactions at the PM. Disruption of endocytosis by Brefeldin A treatment severely disrupts PM localization of ANXs/BUPSs and causes cytoplasmic aggregation of LLGs, while Wortmannin leads to partial trapping of proteins in pre-vacuolar compartments. Altogether, these findings indicate that ANX/BUPS-RALF-LLG complex assembly is highly dynamic and depends on proper endomembrane trafficking to maintain pollen tube integrity during growth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00497-025-00528-y.Keywords: Pollen tube growth, Signaling, Endomembrane trafficking, Transient transformation, Tobacco

Introduction

Successful sexual reproduction of angiosperms strongly depends on structural integrity and precise navigation of tip growing pollen tubes, highly specialized cells that contain two male gametes (sperm cells) that are delivered towards and inside the ovule that contains the female gametophyte (embryo sac). Recent studies have shown that pollen tube penetration in stigmatic tissues, growth and reception in embryo sacs rely on a complex and sophisticated signaling machinery involving secreted peptide ligands of the Rapid Alkalinization Factor (RALF) family, their Catharanthus roseus receptor-like kinase 1-likes (CrRLK1Ls) receptors, and associated proteins that are crucial for maintaining pollen tube integrity and modulating its growth kinetics (Ge et al. 2017, 2019b; Mecchia et al. 2017; Moussu et al. 2020; Zhong et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2024).

RALF peptides, initially characterized as extracellular signaling molecules involved in various developmental processes (Pearce et al. 2001; Murphy and De Smet 2014), have emerged as key regulators of pollen tube growth and integrity maintenance. These cysteine-rich peptides interact with specific transmembrane receptors and co-receptors at the pollen tube plasma membrane (PM), initiating signaling cascades that can lead to changes in pH, reactive oxygen species (ROS) and cytosolic calcium levels (Liu et al. 2024), ultimately modulating cellular processes which are essential for maintaining pollen tube integrity (Ge et al. 2017, 2019b; Mecchia et al. 2017; Zhou et al. 2024). Most knowledge has been obtained from studies in Arabidopsis thaliana (Arabidopsis) showing that ANXUR1/2 (ANX1/2) and BUDDHA’S PAPER SEAL 1/2 (BUPS1/2) from the CrRLK1Ls family play central roles in this signaling complex (Boisson-Dernier et al. 2009; Ge et al. 2017). As pollen-specific paralogs of the ubiquitously expressed FERONIA (FER) receptor (Cheung 2024), ANX1/2 and BUPS1/2 form heteromers and bind the peptide ligands RALF4/19 for maintaining pollen tube cell wall integrity during its rapid tip growth (Ge et al. 2017; Mecchia et al. 2017; Xiao et al. 2019). Further investigations have revealed additional complexity in this signaling network with the identification of LEUCINE-RICH REPEAT EXTENSIN cell wall proteins (LRXs) and LORELEI-LIKE GPI-ANCHORED PROTEIN 2 and 3 (LLG2/3) (Mecchia et al. 2017; Ge et al. 2019b), the latter likely forming a heterotypic receptor complex with RALFs and CrRLK1Ls (Xiao et al. 2019). LRXs appear to bind RALF4/19 and serve as cell wall monitor proteins to sense cell wall changes, while LLG2/3 bind to RALF4/19 and ANX/BUPS complexes as co-receptors (Ge et al. 2019a; Moussu et al. 2023). This intricate signaling module fine-tunes pollen tube growth, ensuring its integrity until it reaches the female gametophyte to release its sperm cell cargo (Zhong et al. 2024).

The subcellular localization of these proteins is critical for their spatial organization and efficacy of this signaling pathway. Previous immunolocalization and fluorescent protein fusion studies revealed that ANX/BUPS receptors predominantly localize to the PM of the pollen tube apex, enabling efficient perception of RALF ligands, while RALFs mainly accumulate in the cell wall at active growing sites of the pollen tube. LLGs were mainly detected in the cytosol and not restricted to the apex region with additional signal at the PM (Boisson-Dernier et al. 2009; Mecchia et al. 2017; Feng et al. 2019; Ge et al. 2019b; Li et al. 2023).

Altogether these findings indicate little co-localization of ANX/BUPS-RALF-LLG complex components. The precise dynamics of the subcellular compartmentalization of these components likely ensures the spatial organization of the signaling machinery, allowing efficient and precise signal transduction and rapid response to changes in the pollen tubes growing microenvironment. Such organization is critical for maintaining the delicate equilibrium between growth promotion and inhibition that is necessary for successful pollen tube growth, guidance and sperm cell delivery. However, it is still unclear whether subcellular localization of different components of this complex is influenced with one another during dynamic pollen tube growth, how the endomembrane system regulates exocytosis of complex components, for example, in different or the same vesicles or whether even fully formed complexes are delivered towards the apex and/or whether complexes are immediately endocytosed after formation and exist only transiently.

To answer these questions, we used the transient tobacco pollen transformation system to express the different complex components alone or in combination of two and three components, respectively, to observe their subcellular localization patterns. Furthermore, by applying pharmaceutical drugs that affect exo- or endocytosis, together with FM4-64 for counter staining, the trafficking machinery of each component was also investigated. This approach allowed us to study how subcellular localization of one component can influence that of another component, and enhances our understanding of the assembly and dynamics of endomembrane trafficking of ANX/BUPS-RALF-LLGs signaling complexes.

Material and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

Nicotiana tabacum cv. Petite Havana SR1 seeds were germinated in soil under controlled greenhouse conditions (16 h of light at 25 °C and 8 h of darkness at 21 °C). After 5–6 weeks, seedlings were transferred to larger pots and maintained under identical conditions until flowering. Mature anthers with pollen grains were collected from freshly opened flowers using tweezers and stored at -80 °C until usage.

Vector construction

All expression constructs were generated using the Gateway cloning systems under the control of the pollen-specific LAT52 promoter (Twell et al. 1989). Coding sequences were recombined into appropriate destination vectors using Gateway LR Clonase II Enzyme Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to generate LAT52:RALF4/19-GFP, LAT52:ANX1/2-GFP, and LAT52:BUPS2-GFP. Analogous, constructs with RFP, mTurquiose2 tags or no tag were generated using the same approach. LAT52:SP-mCitrine-LLG2/3 was generated as described before (Ge et al. 2019b), LAT52:SP-mScarlet-LLG2, LAT52:SP-mCitrine-LLG2/3 and LAT52:SP-mCherry-LLG3 were generated using a similar approach.

Transient tobacco pollen tube transformation

Tobacco pollen tube transient transformation was performed via particle bombardment as previously described (Ge et al. 2019b). 40 mg gold particles (1.6 µm diameter) were soaked in 70% ethanol for 15 min, washed three times with sterile water, and resuspended in 1 mL 50% glycerol. For each transformation, 5 µg of plasmid DNA was mixed with 25 µL gold particle suspension. The mixture was supplemented with 25 µL 2.5 M CaCl2 and 10 µL 1 mg/mL protamine, vortexed for 3 min, and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 30 s. The pellet was washed with 200 µL 100% ethanol, vortexed and centrifuged as above before the resulting pellet was resuspended in 16 µL 100% ethanol. Two aliquots of 8 µL each were loaded onto macro-carriers and air-dried. For bombardment, anthers from 5–10 flowers were gently resuspended in pollen germination medium (PGM, including 24% PEG3350, 2.5% Sucrose, 0.02 M MES-KOH pH6.0, 0.01% H3BO3, 0.02% MgSO4-7H2O, 0.01% KNO3, 0.003 M Ca(NO3)2) to release pollen grains. After 5 s centrifugation at room temperature, the resulting pellet of pollen grains was resuspended in 200 µL PGM and spread on a 1.5 cm filter paper placed on PGM-soaked filter paper in a 9 cm Petri dish. Transformation was performed using the GeneGun Model PDS-1000 He Biolistic® Particle Delivery System (Bio-Rad) with 1100 psi rupture discs at 6 cm distance. After bombardment, pollen grains were transferred to a fresh 6 cm Petri dish containing 1.8 mL PGM and cultured at room temperature on a horizontal shaker (120 rpm). Microscopic analysis was performed 4–8 h after germination. Each experiment was repeated independently three times with similar results (n ≥ 30 pollen tubes per construct).

Drug treatments

Chemical drug treatments were performed 4 h after pollen germination using the following compounds at specified concentrations: 5 µM Brefeldin A (BFA), 0.35 µM Wortmannin, 50 µM Tyrphostin A23 and 10 µM Endosidin2. 8 µM FM4-64 was used for counter staining. Images were captured from 2–4 h after treatment.

Fluorescence microscopy

Fluorescence images were collected using a ZEISS LSM 980 Airyscan2 Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (CLSM) with a 63 × (Plan-Apochromat 63 × /1.40 Oil DIC M27) objective. GFP and mCitrine were excited with a 488 nm or 514 nm laser and observed with emission from 505–550 nm and 520–560 nm, respectively. PI, FM4-64, RFP, mScarlet and mCherry were excited with a 561 nm laser and observed with emission from 590–680 nm. mTurquiose2 was excited with a 436 nm laser and observed with emission from 410–515 nm.

Plot profile analysis

Fluorescence intensity profiles were analysed using ImageJ software (Schneider et al. 2012). Linear regions of interest were drawn across pollen tubes, and fluorescence intensities were measured along these trajectories. Maximum fluorescence intensity for each fluorophore was normalized to 100, with all other values expressed as relative percentages. Plot profile analyses are displayed beneath each figure (yellow lines) or summarized in Suppl. Fig. S3A-D (white dashed lines), raw and normalized fluorescence intensity data corresponding to the yellow lines and white dashed lines are provided in Supplemental Dataset 1 and 2, respectively.

Pearson correlation coefficient analysis

Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) measurements were performed using ImageJ software (Schneider et al. 2012). Regions of interest were determined by the purpose of analysis for each transformation and measurements were conducted with Coloc2 plugin. Pearson correlation coefficient analyses are performed with bule/orange/green dashed line indicated regions of the pollen tubes from the corresponding images and measurement result is shown in Suppl. Fig. S2.

Results and Discussion

ANX/BUPS-RALF-LLG complex components exhibit distinct subcellular localization patterns in growing pollen tubes

To test the subcellular localization of each component from the receptor complex, we transiently expressed translational fusion proteins of RALFs, ANXs/BUPSs and LLGs using the LAT52 promoter in tobacco pollen tubes. Propidium iodide (PI) or FM4-64 were employed as specific markers for cell wall and PM/vesicles visualization, respectively. Bright field images are shown separately in Suppl. Fig. S1A-D. RALF4 and RALF19 mainly localize to the pollen tube cell wall, and additional punctate signals could also be detected in vesicles in the cytosol (Fig. 1A, 1B, 1I, and Suppl. Fig. S2). ANXs/BUPSs are enriched in the PM of the pollen tube as the signal peaks are clearly separated from PI. Signals could also be detected in vesicles in the cytoplasm (Fig. 1C-1E, 1J, and Suppl. Fig. S2). Notably, LLGs were exclusively detected in vesicles in the cytosol, while signals at either the PM or cell wall (outside of FM4-64 signal) could not be observed (Fig. 1F, 1G, 1K, and Suppl. Fig. S2). Co-expression of LLG2 and LLG3 revealed their co-localization within the same vesicles (Fig. 1H, 1L, and Suppl. Fig. S2), indicating potential functional redundancy, cooperative behaviour of both LLGs and/or detection in a specific type of vesicles. Similar localization pattern of each component have been observed before (Boisson-Dernier et al. 2009; Ge et al. 2017, 2019b; Mecchia et al. 2017; Li et al. 2023), but it remained unclear whether RALFs localize to cell wall, PM or both. Altogether these findings indicate that ANX/BUPS-RALF-LLG complexes may exist only transiently involving only a small fraction of the different components or homologous tobacco proteins are incapable of interacting with the respective Arabidopsis homologs. However, the latter hypothesis is unlikely because RALF4/19 as well as ANXs/BUPSs from Arabidopsis can interact with their corresponding maize homologs (Zhou et al. 2024).

ANXs/BUPSs function as PM anchors for RALFs and LLGs

Given the distinct subcellular distributions of individual components, which raises intriguing questions about molecular recruitment mechanisms and protein complex assembly, we next investigated whether their localization patterns can be influenced by co-expression of the other complex components. Co-transformation experiments in tobacco pollen tubes revealed that RALF4/19 were efficiently recruited to the PM when co-expressed with ANX1 (Fig. 2A, 2B, 2I and Suppl. Fig. S2). However, co-expression with LLG2/3 did not alter the cell wall distribution of RALF4/19 (Fig. 2C, 2D, 2J, Suppl. Fig. S2 and Suppl. Fig. S5A-C), indicating that LLGs alone are insufficient to recruit RALF4/19 to the PM, while in the cytosol of pollen tubes, co-localization of RALF4/19 and LLG2/3 in vesicles/endosomes can be detected (Fig. 2C, 2D). This spatial reorganization demonstrates ANXs/BUPSs’ capacity to actively recruit and retain RALF ligands at the PM interface. Similarly, co-expression of ANX1 with LLG2/3 resulted in PM localization of the co-receptors (Fig. 2E, 2F, 2K and Suppl. Fig. S2), further supporting ANXs/BUPSs’ role as membrane-anchoring proteins that may act, like FER during immunity (Stegmann et al. 2017), as RALF-regulated scaffolds that modulate receptor kinase complex assembly.

Bi-coloured vesicles indicate co-localization of components

To examine the potential formation of a complete signaling complex, we performed triple co-expression experiments. Co-expression of ANX1-GFP, SP-mCherry-LLG3, and RALF4 demonstrated PM co-localization of both, ANX1 and LLG3. Moreover, PM localization of the co-receptor is more intense after addition of the RALF peptide ligand (Fig. 2G, 2L and Suppl. Fig. S2). Further validation using RALF4-mTurquoise2, SP-mCitrine-LLG2, and ANX1-RFP confirmed simultaneous PM localization of all three components (Fig. 2H, 2L and Suppl. Fig. S2). This coordinated spatial reorganization suggests that ANXs/BUPSs serve as master organizers and scaffolds for complex assembly and stability at the PM, potentially facilitating signal integration and activation during pollen tube growth.

Endomembrane trafficking requirements for complex component distribution

Endomembrane trafficking is critical for proper localization of secreted proteins and receptor complexes. In order to study the role of endomembrane trafficking in ANX/BUPS-RALF-LLG component distribution, we applied a number of pharmacological drug treatments during pollen tube growth. Additionally, FM4-64 was used as a lipid-soluble fluorescent dye that can penetrate the PM and enter the endomembrane system (Parton et al. 2001). First, treatments were performed with Brefeldin A (BFA), which not only blocks the secretory system by disrupting vesicle formation at the Golgi apparatus (Ritzenthaler et al. 2002), but also enhances endocytosis of cell wall material (Wang et al. 2005). RALF4 largely maintained its cell wall presence. It showed only partial co-localization with BFA bodies in the cytosol (Fig. 3A, 3E and Suppl. Fig. S2), suggesting that RALFs are stably localized in the cell wall and do not undergo recycling in the manner of typical transmembrane proteins. In contrast, ANX1 and BUPS2 displayed complete loss of PM localization upon BFA treatment and were retained in cytosolic signals (Fig. 3B, 3C, 3F and Suppl. Fig. S2). This indicates that they are secreted via the Golgi apparatus where they are likely further modified by O-protein glycosylation as indicated indirectly already in a previous study suggesting the necessity of N-glycosylation for ANXs/BUPSs stability/activity (Lindner et al. 2015). Large cytosolic aggregates containing LLG2 were formed after BFA treatment, indicating transit of LLG2 through BFA sensitive organelles like the Golgi apparatus, TGN or endosomes and restricted protein recycling (Fig. 3D, 3G and Suppl. Fig. S2). The different responses to BFA treatment indicate that ANX/BUPS-RALF-LLG components are exocytosed/endocytosed via different types of vesicles.

Treatment with Wortmannin (Wort), an inhibitor of phosphoinositide-3-kinase (PI3K) that impairs intracellular membrane traffic and endocytosis including vacuolar trafficking (DaSilva et al. 2005), had minimal impact on the cell wall/PM/vesicle localization of RALFs, ANXs and LLGs, respectively. Notably, the PM localization of BUPSs appears more blurred after treatment, suggesting that BUPS may be regulated through a distinct mechanism within the secretion system (Fig. 3H-3N and Suppl. Fig. S2). However, cytosolic signals of all components showed partial sequestration in enlarged compartments, plausibly pre-vacuolar compartments (PVCs), implying a role of vacuolar trafficking in their turnover (Fig. 3H-3N and Suppl. Fig. S2). The selective impacts of Wort treatment highlight the nuanced regulatory mechanisms controlling ANX/BUPS-RALF-LLG component localization and trafficking. Notably, neither Tyrphostin A23 (TyrA23), which inhibits adaptor-cargo interactions and leads to defects of cargo protein internalization (Ortiz-Zapater et al. 2006), nor Endosidin2, an exocyst complex inhibitor (Zhang et al. 2016), significantly altered component distribution patterns. As shown in Suppl. Fig. S3A-N and Suppl. Fig. S2, under both TyrA23 and Endosidin2 treatments, the localizations of RALFs, ANXs and LLGs are similar to the control condition – enriched in the cell wall and vesicles, PM and vesicles, or exclusively in cytosol vesicles, respectively. Similar to the Wort treatment, BUPS again exhibited a more blurred PM localization compared to the control condition. This suggests that these trafficking pathways and endocytosis contribute differentially to the dynamic distribution of complex components in a case-dependent manner.

In summary, these studies demonstrate that the spatial organization of ANX/BUPS-RALF-LLG signaling complex assembly is highly dynamic and depends not only on the presence of the individual components, but also their secretion and endocytosis via different type of vesicles. It should be noted, however, that although tobacco pollen bombardment is a useful method for transient protein expression test, it has several limitations when used to assess the localization of Arabidopsis proteins. These include differences in cellular environments between tobacco and Arabidopsis, differences in membrane composition, post-translational modifications, and trafficking pathways, which may all result in mislocalization or altered protein stability. Additionally, high expression levels typically achieved through bombardment can saturate the trafficking machinery, leading to artificial localization patterns that do not reflect native conditions. Furthermore, it should be noted that Arabidopsis proteins often rely on tissue-specific cofactors or interacting partners that may be absent in tobacco pollen tubes and further complicate data interpretation. To overcome these limitations, we analysed hundreds of bombardments to understand the assembly and dynamics of the ANX/BUPS-RALF-LLG signaling complex.

Based on the results above, we proposed that polar localization of ANXs/BUPSs as scaffolds to the apical PM of pollen tubes appears to be the key for receptor complex assembly and a prerequisite to recruit RALFs and LLGs to the PM. Pre-assembly of complex components seems to play a minor role as LLG2/3, which are located to the same vesicles, hardly co-localize with RALF and ANXs/BUPS containing vesicles before exocytosis, respectively. Moreover, although vesicular co-localization between ANXs/BUPSs and RALFs appears to be stronger, the observation that BFA-treated pollen tubes lack PM-localization of ANXs/BUBPs while cell wall localization of RALFs is unchanged further indicates that the complexes are assembled at the PM. It will now be important to study the lifetime and activity of ANX/BUPS-RALF-LLG complexes as well as their sub-complexes at the apical pollen tube PM. This could be studied, for example, by sophisticated imaging and FRET-FLIM (Förster resonance energy transfer and fluorescence lifetime imaging) analyses. Such studies are challenging but have been successfully performed using growing pollen tubes (Lindner et al. 2015). Studies on how endomembrane trafficking of the components from the ANX/BUPS-RALF-LLG signaling complex influences pollen tube growth under native conditions will be an exciting, but challenging task for future studies.

Limitations of the study

It is difficult to study ANX/BUPS-RALF-LLG signaling complex assembly, activity and dynamics during pollen tube growth in Arabidopsis as the system is highly sensitive to modifications. Both, knock-out and overexpression mutants of RALFs and their receptors generate pollen tubes that immediately burst after germination. Therefore, the robust transient pollen tube transformation system of tobacco was used to study Arabidopsis complex components. It should be noted, however, that this heterologous system has limitations as, for example, Arabidopsis proteins could interfere with tobacco proteins, proteins are usually overexpressed and knock-outs of tobacco could not be generated for the same reasons as described above for Arabidopsis. Moreover, expression pattern and strength of signals during transient studies show variation from experiment to experiment. To overcome these limitations, hundreds of bombardments were perfomed to understand and quantify the spatial organization and dynamics of the ANX/BUPS-RALF-LLG signaling complex.
