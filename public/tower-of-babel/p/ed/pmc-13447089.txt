Pangenome-resolved structural variation drives adaptation and trait evolution in cucumber
Authors: Jiantao Guan, Xiangsheng Li, Han Miao, Xuemei Yan, Xiaoping Liu, Xingfang Gu, Richard G F Visser, Yuling Bai, Sanwen Huang, Zhonghua Zhang, Shaoyun Dong, Shengping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCucumber (Cucumis sativus L.) is a global vegetable crop and powerful model for sex determination, fruit development and vascular biology. We present high-quality genome assemblies for 125 cultivated and wild accessions, capturing worldwide genetic diversity. Syntenic gene family analysis characterized 37,897 gene families and revealed haplotype diversity shaped by geographic expansion. Comparative analyses uncovered copy-number variations linked to local adaptation, including a CsFT tandem duplication promoting early flowering at higher latitudes. This resource reduces reference bias, enabling the annotation of resistance loci and the discovery of CsCcu, a nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat-type R gene conferring scab resistance. We cataloged 135,597 structural variations and quantified their regulatory effects, with ~30% driving trait diversification among geographic groups. Integrating structural variations into genome-wide association studies identified 172 quantitative trait loci for 38 agronomic traits, including a rare long terminal repeat insertion regulating fruit length via CsSPL1. Our findings provide a genomic toolkit for cucumber evolution research and precision breeding.Subject terms: Plant genetics, Genetic association study, Genomics

Main

Cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.) stands among the world’s most widely cultivated vegetables, covering 2.29 million hectares and yielding 87.8 million tons in 2024, according to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO). Beyond its agricultural importance, cucumber is a premier model crop for investigating sex determination, fruit morphogenesis and vascular development. Cucumber was domesticated in India approximately 3,000 years ago from C. sativus var. hardwickii1–3, which has since diversified into many ecotypes and landraces adapted to a range of climates, cultivation systems, processing requirements and consumer preferences4. Genome-wide studies have mapped numerous quantitative trait loci (QTLs)5, identified selection signatures6 and pinpointed genes controlling flowering time7, disease resistance8,9 and fruit size10,11. However, the causal variants underlying many of these loci remain elusive, limiting progress in molecular breeding.

Recent pangenome initiatives in rice12, tomato13, potato14 and wheat15 have highlighted the profound impact of structural variations (SVs) and gene copy-number variations (gCNVs) on agronomic traits. In cucumber, four reference genomes1,16–18, including the near-complete CLv4.0 and SNP-based screens in a 115-accession core set6, have advanced our understanding of cucumber population structure, genetic diversity and trait genetics8,11. A long-read pangenome from three wild and nine cultivated accessions revealed extensive presence/absence variations and associations with domestication traits19. More recently, a graph-based pangenome of cucumber elucidated SV dynamics during breeding20. Nevertheless, the lack of high-quality, cultivar-representative genomes has hindered the resolution of complex variants and the rapid identification of causal genes for breeding.

To address these challenges, we generated chromosome-scale assemblies for 125 accessions, including 124 cultivated accessions and 1 wild accession6. We constructed a comprehensive pangenome and characterized variation in gene content, SVs and gCNVs shaped by selection for latitudinal adaptation, fruit-length diversification and disease resistance. Together, these resources establish a comprehensive foundation for cucumber genomics, facilitate rapid gene discovery and empower next-generation molecular breeding.

Results

De novo assemblies of 125 diverse accessions

To capture the full spectrum of cucumber genetic diversity, we analyzed whole-genome resequencing data from 422 globally distributed accessions (comprising 13 wild and 409 cultivated accessions), including 202 newly sequenced accessions in this study (Supplementary Fig. 1a). This expanded panel offers improved coverage of historically undersampled regions such as East Asia (n = 83), Europe (n = 39), North America (n = 19) and Africa (n = 12; Supplementary Table 1). Using the CLv4.0 reference genome16, we identified 5.90 million SNPs and 1.52 million insertions/deletions (InDels). Phylogenetic reconstruction based on 18,600 fourfold degenerate SNPs partitioned these accessions into four geographic clades: Indian (n = 34), Xishuangbanna (XIS; n = 18), Eurasian (n = 136) and East Asian (n = 234), in agreement with previous classifications6 (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1b,c).

From this diversity panel, we selected 125 representative accessions for high-quality genome assembly based on phylogenetic clustering. The selected panel comprised 16 Indian (including 1 wild), 6 XIS, 38 Eurasian and 65 East Asian accessions, spanning major cucumber-producing regions worldwide (Fig. 1a,b and Supplementary Table 1). These accessions encompass substantial phenotypic variation in plant height, fruit length, fruit weight and biotic stress resistance and include important parental lines in cucumber breeding (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3, Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Note 1).

We generated approximately 1.77 terabases of PacBio HiFi data (mean 38.59× per accession; Supplementary Table 3), yielding chromosome-scale assemblies ranging from 285.66 to 345.39 megabases (Mb; mean of ~300 Mb) with 94.18% of sequences anchored to chromosomes (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Table 4). Relative to previous assemblies19, continuity improved markedly; gaps per chromosome decreased from over 34 to fewer than 3, with 103 chromosomes (11.77%) being entirely gap free, and contig N50 increased from 3.96 Mb to 25.41 Mb (Fig. 1d,e). Multiple quality metrics, including benchmarking universal single-copy ortholog (BUSCO) completeness (98.57%), k-mer quality value (56.6) and Illumina read mapping rates (99.96%), consistently exceeded those of previous assemblies19 (Fig. 1f, Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Note 1).

All 125 genomes showed a similar transposable element content (~16%), with our assemblies capturing substantially more intact long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons than previous assemblies, reflecting improved contiguity in repeat-rich regions (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 4, Supplementary Tables 5,6 and Supplementary Note 1). Tandem repeats, including satellite DNA and 45S/5S ribosomal DNA in pericentromeric and subtelomeric regions, accounted for 14.13% of the genome (a 6.6-fold increase over previous pangenome assemblies), substantially contributing to the larger genome sizes observed (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Note 1). The improved assembly enabled the identification of 875 putative centromeres (806 contiguous and gap free) and 168 telomeres (Supplementary Table 7). Using an integrated approach combining ab initio, homology-based and transcriptome-derived evidence, we annotated a mean of 26,631 protein-coding genes per genome (mean BUSCO of 97.22%), with 95.51% supported by nonredundant (NR) database annotation (Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 8).

Syntenic gene family analysis of cucumber

To explore the diversity and evolutionary dynamics of the cucumber genome, we performed syntenic orthologous gene analysis using the 125 newly assembled genomes and the CLv4.0 reference genome. We identified 39,067 syntenic gene families, substantially more than the 26,822 reported in a previous analysis of 12 genomes19. The number of syntenic gene families reached saturation at approximately 100 accessions (Fig. 2a), surpassing that observed in crops such as rice21, tomato13 and cabbage22 and highlighting the exceptional gene diversity in cucumber. We further classified these syntenic gene families into 10,889 (27.9%) core, 5,810 (14.9%) softcore, 21,467 (54.9%) dispensable and 901 (2.3%) private gene families, with individual genomes containing a mean of 10,889 (53.8%), 5,691 (28.1%), 3,651 (18.0%) and 7 (0.034%) per genome, respectively (Fig. 2b, Extended Data Fig. 1a,b and Supplementary Table 9).

Core and softcore genes exhibited significantly higher expression levels and longer gene lengths than dispensable and private genes (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 6). Core genes showed significantly lower nonsynonymous substitution/synonymous substitution rate (Ka/Ks) ratios than softcore and dispensable genes (Fig. 2d), indicating stronger evolutionary conservation. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that core genes are primarily involved in fundamental processes such as RNA biosynthetic processes and regulation of the primary metabolic process, while dispensable genes are enriched in proteolysis and the stress response (Fig. 2e). We further identified 28,890 nonredundant genes absent from the previously reported cucumber pangenome19 (Supplementary Table 10), highlighting the substantial genetic diversity captured by our expanded assembly set.

Comparing syntenic gene families and haplotypes across four geographic groups, 58.03% (22,669) of syntenic gene families were shared across all groups (Fig. 2f), with the East Asian and Eurasian groups containing the most group-specific syntenic gene families. Although these groups harbored greater total haplotype numbers (Fig. 2g), they also possessed higher proportions of rare haplotypes (frequency < 5%; Extended Data Fig. 1c). Haplotype diversity, measured by Nei’s genetic diversity index, was highest in the Indian group and significantly reduced in the other three groups (Fig. 2h), suggesting that purifying selection diminished genetic diversity during the expansion of cucumber from its Indian origin.

We identified 6,822 syntenic gene families exhibiting significantly divergent haplotype content between at least two geographic groups, enriched in functions related to transcription regulation, cell cycle and metabolic processes (Extended Data Fig. 1d). Approximately 53% (3,587) of these genes coincided with genomic regions under selective sweeps identified in at least one corresponding pairwise comparison between geographic groups (Supplementary Table 11). This suggests that divergent selection driven by local adaptation contributes to their haplotype diversity. Among the 15 previously documented regulators identified in these genes, two characterized fruit-length regulators, CsSEP2 (ref. 11) and CsSF1 (ref. 23), possess fruit-length-associated haplotypes defined by nonsynonymous substitutions, and each harbors a previously unreported haplotype that is significantly associated with fruit length (Fig. 2i,j and Extended Data Fig. 1e–g), emphasizing their applicability in future breeding programs.

gCNVs drive adaptation during expansion

gCNVs are pivotal drivers of plant diversity and adaptation, yet their characterization has been constrained by short-read sequencing limitations in repeat-rich regions24. We identified 1,960 genes exhibiting gCNVs, gene presence/absence variations (gPAVs) or both. This set comprised 947 genes with only gCNVs, 378 with only gPAVs and 635 containing both forms of variation (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 12). These gCNVs were enriched in defense responses, flower development and carbohydrate metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 7), including the ACS1G duplication regulating gynoecy25 and the Tu deletion linked to the smooth-fruit phenotype26 (Supplementary Table 13 and Supplementary Note 2), validated by resequencing read depth and phenotype data (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9).

Our analysis revealed that gCNVs are not randomly distributed (Fig. 3a) but formed 212 clusters encompassing 1,614 gCNVs (87.6% of all CNV-related genes; Fig. 3b) that are significantly enriched at crossover hotspots27 (P = 4.15 × 10−3), suggesting that elevated local recombination rates may facilitate gCNV formation28. A striking example is the lipoxygenase (LOX) gene family, which is essential for defense signaling via oxylipin biosynthesis and the production of aromatic volatiles29–31. This family has undergone lineage-specific expansion in cucumber, forming two prominent clusters on chromosomes 2 and 4 (ref. 32). Most LOX genes in these regions display gCNVs (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 10), with LOX2 and LOX2.1 on chromosome 4 showing two to seven copies and notably higher copy numbers in Eurasian accessions (Fig. 3c), as confirmed by read-depth data (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Principal component analysis (PCA) based on gCNVs separated the 125 genomes into four geographic groups (Fig. 3d), closely paralleling the SNP-based population structure (Fig. 1b). This pattern suggests that gCNVs have been subject to selection during geographic expansion. To quantify this divergence, we calculated the fixation index (VST) score33, identifying 526 highly differentiated gCNVs among the groups (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 14). These differentiated gCNVs include several defense-related genes, which likely contribute to adaptation under distinct microbial environments (Supplementary Note 2).

Our gCNV analysis uncovered a previously unreported tandem duplication at the CsFT locus7, with high VST between Eurasian and East Asian groups (Fig. 3e). Accessions with this duplication possess multiple near-identical (99.9%) copies of a 20-kilobase (kb) segment (Fig. 3f, Extended Data Fig. 2a–c and Supplementary Note 2), indicating a recent origin. The presence of this duplication was validated by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR; Extended Data Fig. 2d and Supplementary Table 15). Integrating this duplication with two reported deletions7 classified accessions into five alleles; only alleles carrying the tandem duplication (CNV-Long and CNV-Short-2) were significantly associated with elevated CsFT expression and earlier flowering (Fig. 3g and Extended Data Fig. 2e,f) and were more prevalent in accessions from higher latitudes (Fig. 3h). These findings highlight the role of recent CsFT duplication in latitudinal adaptation.

PanNLRome diversity and disease resistance

Nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat receptors (NLRs) are a major class of plant immune receptors that detect pathogens and trigger defense responses34. We comprehensively characterized the cucumber NLR repertoire (termed the PanNLRome) and identified 8,835 NLR genes across the 125 de novo genomes and the CLv4.0 reference genome. The number of NLRs per accession ranged from 46 to 77 (Supplementary Table 16), with coiled-coil NLRs (CNLs) being the most prevalent type (33.78–43.66%), followed by Toll/interleukin-1 receptor NLRs (TNLs), non-canonical NLRs (NLs) and resistance to powdery mildew 8 (RPW8)-NLRs (RNLs; Fig. 4a and Supplementary Table 17).

Sequence similarity clustering grouped NLRs into 73 orthogroups, which were classified as core (28 orthogroups, 46.24% of all NLRs), softcore (29 orthogroups, 44.96%), dispensable (14 orthogroups, 6.73%) and private (2 orthogroups, 0.023%), with 53.73–65.28% of NLRs per accession physically clustered (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Table 18). We identified nine NLR clusters (three or more NLRs within 200 kb) with four larger clusters (five or more NLRs) on chromosomes 2, 4, 5 and 7. Several clusters exhibited CNV among geographic groups, suggesting that local adaptation has shaped NLR diversity (Supplementary Fig. 12 and Supplementary Note 3).

A SNP-based genome-wide association study (GWAS) across 125 accessions for resistance to five major pathogens (Sphaerotheca fuliginea (powdery mildew), Cladosporium cucumerinum (scab disease), Pseudomonas amygdali pv. lachrymans (bacterial angular leaf spot), Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cucumerinum (fusarium wilt) and Pseudoperonospora cubensis (downy mildew)) identified 13 QTLs (Supplementary Fig. 13 and Supplementary Table 19), with two QTL regions containing NLR genes. Notably, a major QTL (Ccu) for resistance against scab caused by C. cucumerinum was mapped to the proximal end of chromosome 2 (3.34–3.96 Mb) harboring four PanNLRome NLR genes (Fig. 4c,d), consistent with a previous study35. To rapidly identify the causal gene within this large candidate region that contains 80 protein-coding genes, we performed a gene-level haplotype-based GWAS36 based on syntenic gene families and PanNLRome across 125 genomes. Notably, this analysis pinpointed a TNL gene within the four NLR genes on chromosome 2 as the most significant candidate for scab resistance (P = 1.67 × 10−40; Fig. 4e).

Haplotype analysis of this TNL gene revealed five distinct major haplotypes, of which only haplotype1 (Hap1) conferred scab resistance with significantly lower disease indices (Fig. 4f,g and Supplementary Table 20). Susceptible haplotypes (Hap2–Hap5) harbored either premature termination codons or nonsynonymous mutations within the NB-ARC (nucleotide-binding domain shared by APAF-1, R proteins, and CED-4) and leucine-rich repeat domains. Furthermore, Hap1 exhibited pathogen-induced expression in resistant accession ‘CG52’ (Fig. 4h). We further validated this candidate genetically by fine-mapping the Ccu locus using 3,176 F2 plants derived from a cross between the susceptible accession ‘9930’ and resistant ‘CG52’, which independently confirmed a 30.13-kb interval containing the candidate gene (Extended Data Fig. 3a). These findings indicate the TNL gene as the causal CsCcu gene.

We validated its function by generating CRISPR–Cas9 knockout mutants in the resistant line ‘NCG121’, which carries the Hap1 allele (Extended Data Fig. 3b). Both mutants exhibited markedly increased scab susceptibility and disease index in emerging leaves compared to the wild-type controls (Fig. 4i,j, Extended Data Fig. 3c and Supplementary Table 21), with transcriptome profiling supporting the role of CsCcu in defense activation (Extended Data Fig. 3d,e, Supplementary Tables 22 and 23 and Supplementary Note 3). Geographic distribution analysis showed that Hap1 was predominantly found in Eurasian accessions (Fig. 4k), with three East Asian accessions also carrying Hap1, likely due to introgression from Eurasian germplasm, consistent with the Eurasian-specific distribution of scab resistance reported previously6. These results demonstrate how pangenome-enabled NLR cataloging and haplotype-based analysis can rapidly identify causal resistance genes.

SV landscape and its impact on gene expression in cucumber

We identified 135,597 high-confidence, nonredundant SVs (>20 basepairs (bp)) across 133 genomes (125 from this study and 8 published previously19) comprising 71,344 deletions, 55,891 insertions, 550 inversions, 3,411 duplications and 4,401 translocations, with a total span of 786 Mb and PCR-based validation confirming 96.05% accuracy (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 14 and Supplementary Tables 24 and 25). Individual accessions contained 4,850–34,646 SVs, with wild accessions W8 and Cuc64 harboring the highest numbers, and 41,681 SVs were unique to a single genome, with the Indian clade showing the most private SVs (Supplementary Fig. 15).

SVs were more prevalent in repetitive than in nonrepetitive regions (Fig. 5a and Extended Data Fig. 4a). Approximately 18.70% of SVs overlapped with genic regions, impacting 45.38% of all protein-coding genes (Supplementary Table 26). SVs were predominantly localized to UTRs and regions within 2 kb of genes, with larger SVs being less common (Extended Data Fig. 4b,c and Supplementary Note 4). SVs impacting functional elements exhibited lower minor allele frequencies (MAFs; Fig. 5b), consistent with stronger negative selection against functionally relevant variants37,38. Among the large-scale SVs, our pangenome analysis corroborated three previously described wild cucumber karyotypes defined by large inversions (>1 Mb)19 and identified a karyotype in ‘CG88’ characterized by only two such inversions on chromosomes 4 and 7, confirmed by high-throughput chromosome conformation capture data (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Fig. 16 and Supplementary Table 27). Beyond large-scale rearrangements, we also detected SVs directly disrupting the coding sequences of genes underlying known phenotypic variations. This includes three previously reported causal SVs (Supplementary Table 28) and several additional potential causal SVs (Supplementary Fig. 17 and Supplementary Note 4). To facilitate population-scale application of these SVs, we constructed a graph-based pangenome incorporating PAVs and inversions.

To assess the genome-wide regulatory impact of SVs in a controlled genetic background, we performed SV expression QTL (SV-eQTL) mapping in an F9 recombinant inbred line (RIL) population derived from a cross between the East Asian accession ‘CG104’ and the Eurasian accession ‘CG37’. This population captures a broad spectrum of genetic and phenotypic diversity (Supplementary Fig. 18). We resequenced 186 RILs and conducted strand-specific RNA sequencing (ssRNA-seq) on fruit tissue at anthesis (Supplementary Table 29). Using 33,476 SVs (MAF > 0.05), eQTL mapping identified 143,552 SV-eQTLs (significance threshold P = 1.49 × 10⁻6) associated with 4,182 expressed genes, including 3,709 lead SVs regulating 3,207 eGenes (genes with a significant eQTL) (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table 30). Comparison with SNP-based eQTL mapping revealed that 361 genes (11.26%) were uniquely regulated by SVs but not by SNPs under equivalent significance thresholds (Supplementary Table 31). These likely represent causal SVs in incomplete linkage disequilibrium (R2<0.7) with neighboring SNPs (Fig. 5e).

SV-eQTL effects could either increase or decrease gene expression (that is, had bidirectional effects on expression), except for those overlapping exons (Extended Data Fig. 4d). Longer SVs exerted larger regulatory effects (Fig. 5f). Most SV-eQTLs (85.7%) were located within 10 kb of transcription start sites (Extended Data Fig. 4e), predominantly upstream (<2 kb) or in UTRs (Fig. 5g), suggesting their involvement in transcriptional and/or post-transcriptional regulation, supported by significant overlap with open chromatin regions determined by assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing39 (Fig. 5h).

We classified SV-eQTLs as cis (≤2 Mb from the eGene) or trans (>2 Mb or on different chromosomes) and identified 6,124 cis-eQTLs (62.02%) and 3,750 trans-eQTLs (37.98%; Extended Data Fig. 4f,g). Cis-eQTLs explained significantly more expression variance (19.3%) than trans-eQTLs (12.3%), and nearly half of all eQTLs regulated multiple genes (Extended Data Fig. 4h,i and Supplementary Note 4). We identified 218 cis-eQTL and 55 trans-eQTL hot spots affecting 1,575 and 1,246 eGenes, respectively (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table 32), with cis hot spots strongly correlating with gene-dense regions (Pearson’s r = 0.90, P < 2.2 × 10−16; Extended Data Fig. 4j,k). GO enrichment of hotspot eGenes revealed strong functional coherence in biological processes and molecular functions (Supplementary Table 33). Collectively, these findings highlight the extensive and complex regulatory roles of SVs in shaping gene expression landscapes.

SVs associated with trait diversification

We identified 43,225 putative diversification-related SVs (Div-SVs; 35.86% of all SVs) by comparing the frequencies of 120,554 SVs among cultivated accessions across geographic groups, with the greatest divergence between Indian and East Asian groups and the least between Indian and XIS groups (Fig. 5i and Extended Data Fig. 5a–e). Functional annotation revealed 15,819 protein-coding genes associated with diversification, of which 1,425 had coding sequence disrupted by Div-SVs (Supplementary Tables 34 and 35 and Supplementary Note 5). For example, a 1.18-kb deletion in CsHXK2 (an ortholog of a known regulator of fruit and seed size in tomato40) prevalent in the Eurasian group introduced a stop-loss mutation and significantly reduced CsHXK2 expression in fruit at anthesis (Fig. 5j). Remarkably, 904 diversification-related genes were shared across all geographic comparisons (Extended Data Fig. 5f), including stress-associated genes (Supplementary Table 36), underscoring their potential roles in environmental adaptation during cucumber expansion.

SVs are established regulators of gene expression, as demonstrated in plants including tomato13 and rice41 and supported by our SV-eQTL analysis (Fig. 5d). Among the Div-SVs, we identified several SVs associated with genes involved in fruit quality and stress response. For example, a 72-bp promoter deletion in CsMYC6 (a positive regulator of trichome development42) was enriched in the Eurasian group, which displayed smaller and fewer fruit trichomes, alongside significantly lower CsMYC6 expression than East Asian accessions (Fig. 5k). Similarly, a 180-bp promoter insertion in CsWRKY49 (a cold resistance ortholog43,44) was absent in most East Asian accessions but present in most accessions from the other three groups. Expression of CsWRKY49 was significantly higher in cold-tolerant accessions than in cold-sensitive accessions after treatment at 13 °C (Fig. 5l). These results suggest that SVs substantially contribute to cucumber trait diversification.

A rare LTR insertion regulates fruit length

We performed SV-based GWAS using 42,552 SVs (MAF > 0.05) for 38 agronomic traits related to fruit size, fruit quality, flower, vegetative organ, stress tolerance and disease resistance across 11 seasons in 422 accessions (Fig. 6a). This analysis identified a total of 172 associated signals, of which 111 (64.53%) were exclusive to SV-based GWAS and absent from SNP-based GWAS (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Table 37), indicating that SV-based GWAS complements conventional SNP-based approaches13,45,46. For example, the previously reported 802-bp insertion at CsGL1 (ref. 47) was detected only by SV-based GWAS (Supplementary Table 37 and Supplementary Note 6). We further identified a 218-bp insertion associated with fruit bitterness (P < 1.13 × 10⁻5) located within the 3′ UTR of CsaV4_4G002969, which encodes β-glucosidase. Accessions carrying this insertion were bitter and showed increased expression in fruit (Supplementary Fig. 19).

Notably, no significant SV signal was identified for fruit length (Supplementary Table 37), a complex quantitative trait with profound implications for both yield and market quality in cucumber48. Heritability estimation revealed that rare SVs account for ~40% of total SV heritability (Extended Data Fig. 6a), potentially limiting the power of population-wide GWAS for this trait. Given that 65.59% of rare SVs are private to a single geographic group (Extended Data Fig. 6b), we performed an SV-based GWAS for fruit length within each group separately, thereby leveraging their higher relative frequency to improve detection power. Within the Eurasian group, an ~5.3-kb insertion (INS22773) containing a 2,726-bp LTR/Gypsy retrotransposon was among the most significant SVs (Fig. 6c). This rare SV is present in only 6 of 422 accessions, 5 of which belong to the Eurasian group (Extended Data Fig. 6c), and accessions carrying it showed significantly reduced fruit length (Supplementary Fig. 21). The insertion resides within the first exon of CsSPL1, a SQUAMOSA promoter binding protein-like transcription factor (Fig. 6c). Phylogenetic analysis placed CsSPL1 within a well-supported clade containing SlSPL15, AtSPL9, OsSPL14 and TaSPL17, established regulators of the vegetative-to-reproductive transition and yield-related traits49–51 (Supplementary Fig. 22), and CsSPL1 was highly expressed in fruit tissue with expression peaking at anthesis (Extended Data Fig. 6d).

To validate the association of the LTR insertion with fruit length, we performed an SV-based GWAS, eQTL mapping and transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) in an RIL population derived from a cross between the Eurasian short-fruited accession ‘CG37’ (carrying INS22773) and the East Asian long-fruited accession ‘CG104’ (lacking it; Supplementary Fig. 23). SV-based GWAS identified INS22773 as the most significant SV, eQTL analysis detected it as the strongest cis-eQTL for CsSPL1, and TWAS independently identified CsSPL1 as one of the most significantly associated genes with fruit length (Fig. 6d,e and Extended Data Fig. 6e). Bayesian colocalization52 yielded a posterior probability exceeding 0.90, supporting INS22773 as the causal variant underlying both CsSPL1 expression and fruit length (Supplementary Table 39).

To further confirm CsSPL1 function, we generated three CRISPR–Cas9 mutants with small deletions, an inversion or a 306-bp deletion between two protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sites (Extended Data Fig. 6f), all resulting in premature stop codons and truncated proteins (Supplementary Fig. 24). At 0 and 10 days after anthesis (DAA), fruit lengths in these mutants were reduced to 74.5–77.0% and 65.1–77.7% of wild type, respectively (Fig. 6f,g and Extended Data Fig. 6g). These results establish CsSPL1 as a positive regulator of fruit length.

Analysis of CsSPL1 coding sequences across 125 accessions identified five distinct alleles (Fig. 6h, Extended Data Fig. 6h, Supplementary Fig. 25 and Supplementary Table 40). Accessions carrying CsSPL1-L produced longer fruits, whereas those with CsSPL1-S1 to CsSPL1-S4, marked by nonsynonymous SNPs or frameshift InDels, or SVs including INS22773 (CsSPL1-S3) had significantly shorter fruits (Fig. 6i and Extended Data Fig. 6i). Among wild cucumbers, only CsSPL1-L and CsSPL1-S1 were found, differing by a nonsynonymous substitution (the CsSPL1-L protein contains glycine, while CsSPL1-S1 contains serine). Phylogenetic analysis across 12 plant species showed conservation of this glycine residue (Supplementary Fig. 26), indicating that CsSPL1-L is ancestral to CsSPL1-S1.

Among 20 Indian cultivars, 17 carried CsSPL1-S1, one possessed CsSPL1-L, and two harbored CsSPL1-S2 or CsSPL1-S3, suggesting that they may be new alleles that emerged during domestication. Notably, as cucumbers dispersed from their Indian origin, significant differences in allelic composition emerged between Eurasian and East Asian cultivars (P < 3.1 × 10⁻36, two-sided χ2 test; Fig. 6j). Eurasian cultivars predominantly carried short-fruit alleles (CsSPL1-S1 to CsSPL1-S4), while East Asian cultivars mostly harbored the long-fruit allele (CsSPL1-L), likely reflecting divergent consumer-driven selection pressures4. These findings illustrate the power of pangenome-enabled SV discovery in resolving the missing heritability of complex agronomic traits and identifying causal variants invisible to reference-based approaches.

Discussion

Understanding the full spectrum of genomic variation, particularly large-scale structural differences often missed by single-reference frameworks, is important for both fundamental biology and modern breeding. Here, we constructed a cucumber chromosome-scale graph pangenome from 125 high-quality assemblies, capturing a comprehensive inventory of genes, SVs and gCNVs that establishes a foundational framework for dissecting cucumber domestication and adaptation.

CNV is increasingly recognized as an important driver of plant adaptation12,24, yet systematic studies in non‑model crops remain limited. Previous work has demonstrated the contribution of CNVs to domestication and diversification traits in cereals, including flowering time53 and grain size54. Leveraging 125 chromosome‑scale genome assemblies, we identified 1,960 high‑confidence gCNVs, including a recent tandem duplication of CsFT that drives early flowering in Eurasian cucumbers independently of previously described upstream deletions7. CNV of FT homologs has been independently associated with flowering adaptation in wheat55, barley56 and tomato57, supporting copy‑number alteration as a recurrent mechanism for fine‑tuning flowering time in plants.

We constructed the cucumber PanNLRome and identified 8,835 NLR genes whose population-level diversity primarily reflects allelic variation across haplotypes rather than increased gene number per genome32,58. Compared to many other plants where NLR repertoires often comprise hundreds of genes14,59,60, NLR complements in cucumber and across Cucurbitaceae are notably reduced58, likely due to frequent lineage-specific gene loss and limited tandem duplication58. Critically, CsCcu has undergone pseudogenization in multiple accessions, directly abolishing scab resistance, highlighting how pangenome resources recover functional alleles invisible to single-reference approaches and providing a foundation for mining hidden resistance genes.

SVs are known to explain a greater proportion of phenotypic variation than SNPs for numerous traits45, yet their low frequency and restriction to genetically related populations make it difficult to estimate their effect size and reduce statistical power for association analyses. Our findings indicate that rare SVs contribute substantially to fruit length heritability, likely explaining the absence of significant associations in population-wide SV-based GWAS. Given that most rare SVs are private to a single geographic group, stratified analyses within each group improved detection power and identified 34 fruit-length-associated QTLs, including the causal LTR insertion in CsSPL1, a functional target for marker-assisted selection in cucumber breeding.

Several limitations define clear avenues for future work. The inclusion of only one wild accession limits coverage of the broader gene pool; expanding with diverse wild relatives is imperative, as they harbor valuable resistance and stress-tolerant alleles lost during domestication. Although 806 contiguous centromeres were assembled, their functional cores and evolutionary dynamics remain undefined and would benefit from centromeric histone H3 mapping and comparative epigenomic analyses. Finally, integrating Oxford Nanopore Technologies ultralong reads and full-length isoform sequencing would resolve persistent challenges such as LTRs and complex splicing patterns, enhancing annotation accuracy. Addressing these limitations will deepen our understanding of cucumber genome biology and accelerate its application in molecular breeding.

Methods

Samples and genome sequencing

A total of 202 accessions were subjected to short-read sequencing, while 125 accessions underwent long-read sequencing. Young leaves were collected from individual plants 2 weeks after germination and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for DNA extraction. Genomic DNA was isolated using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide method, followed by purification with the Qiagen Genomic DNA kit to prepare whole-genome sequencing and PacBio HiFi sequencing libraries. Sequencing was performed on the DNBSEQ T7 platform (BGI Genomics) and the PacBio Sequel II platform (Novogene Technologies), respectively (Supplementary Table 3).

For genetic mapping, a RIL population of 186 F9 lines was developed from a cross between the East Asian ecotype line ‘CG104’ (long fruited) and the Eurasian ecotype line ‘CG37’ (short fruited). The RIL population and parental lines were grown at the Experimental Station of the Institute of Vegetables and Flowers, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences during the spring of 2019. Young leaves were collected for genomic DNA extraction using the Plant Genomic DNA kit (Tiangen Biotech), followed by whole-genome sequencing on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Biomarker Technologies). Additionally, whole fruits were collected at 0 days after pollination (DAP) for total mRNA extraction using TRIzol (Invitrogen) and sequenced on the DNBSEQ T7 platform. Fruit-length measurements were taken at two developmental stages, immature (10 DAP) and mature (35 DAP), for all 186 RIL lines and parental lines during the spring and autumn of 2019, as well as the spring of 2020.

Long-read sequencing, assembly and quality assessment

Chromosome-level assemblies were generated from HiFi long reads using Hifiasm (v0.19.5)61 with default parameters. Given the large contig sizes (N50 = 15.94–38.25 Mb), contigs from each accession were aligned to the near-complete cucumber CLv4.0 reference genome and anchored into chromosomes using RagTag (v2.1.0)62. To comprehensively evaluate assembly quality, we used a multi-faceted approach. Gene completeness was assessed using BUSCO (v.5.2.2)63 with the embryophyta_odb10 database, which includes 1,440 embryophyte genes. High-quality short reads were mapped to the assemblies using Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (v0.7.17-r1198)64 to assess genome accuracy and completeness. High-quality long reads were aligned to the assemblies using Minimap2 (v2.17-r941)65 to further validate assembly integrity. Finally, k-mer completeness, base pair quality and false duplication rates were evaluated using Merqury (v1.1)66 to ensure high-quality genome assemblies.

Repeat and protein-coding gene annotations

To annotate repetitive elements in the assembled genomes, we used a combination of ab initio and homology-based approaches. An ab initio repeat library was constructed using RepeatModeler (v2.0.3)67 with default parameters, followed by homology-based annotation using Repbase (v20180823) in conjunction with RepeatMasker (v4.1.2-p1)68. For gene annotation, ssRNA-seq data from 29 tissues across various developmental stages from the reference cultivar ‘9930’ (for example, leaf, root, stem, tendril, flower, fruit and seed)16 were aligned to each of the 125 genome assemblies using HISAT2 (v2.2.1)69. Gene models were predicted using EVidenceModeler (v1.1.1)70, which integrated evidence from ab initio predictions (BRAKER2 (ref. 71)), homology-based searches (GenomeThreader72 v1.7.1) and ssRNA-seq alignments (BAM files). The ssRNA-seq alignments provided intron boundary hints for BRAKER2 (ref. 71), and transcripts were assembled using StringTie (v2.2.1)73. The longest transcript for each gene was predicted using EVidenceModeler70 (--segmentSize 100000 --overlapSize 10000), and transcripts overlapping repetitive elements by more than 20% were filtered out. Functional annotation was performed using DIAMOND (v2.0.15)74 against the NR database (E < 1 × 10−5), with GO terms derived from InterPro entries and pathway assignments obtained via BLASTP75 against the KEGG database (E < 1 × 10−5).

Syntenic gene family analyses

We performed a syntenic gene family analysis for 126 cucumber accessions. Syntenic gene families were clustered using mSynOrths76 (v0.1; https://gitee.com/zhanglingkui/msynorths; https://github.com/xiagjt/Cucumber.Pangenome) with parameters ‘-n 20 -m 0.6’, following an all-versus-all protein comparison performed by DIAMOND (v2.0.15)74 under an E value cutoff of 1 × 10−5. Genes without syntenic pairs or tandem duplicates were classified as orphan genes and excluded. The resulting gene families were classified as core (present in all 126 genomes), softcore (114–125 genomes), dispensable (2–113 genomes) or private (single genome). The ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous substitution rate (Ka/Ks) for each gene within the syntenic gene family was calculated with the CODEML program in the PAML package (v4.10.10)77 based on multiple sequence alignments between homologous pairs performed with ClustalW2 (ref. 78;v2.1) and PAL2NAL79 (v14.1). Based on protein sequences per syntenic gene family, we counted haplotypes that were group specific or shared among the four geographic groups. Haplotypes with significantly different frequencies between any two groups were identified using a χ2 test (false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.001).

Gene content comparison

To identify genes unique to the pangenome, we extracted the coding sequences of all predicted genes from the 125 cucumber genomes and aligned them against the coding sequences of the previously reported cucumber pangenome using GMAP (v2021-08-25)80 with default parameters. Following criteria adapted from a tomato pangenome study81, we retained genes with less than 90% sequence identity or less than 75% alignment coverage. Redundancy was further reduced using CD-HIT82 (v4.8.1, default parameters), resulting in a nonredundant set of 28,890 genes.

Centromere and telomere identification

Putative centromere regions were identified based on the enrichment of the cucumber centromere-specific 177-bp type III satellite repeats16 using BLASTN and BEDTools (v2.30.0)83. Telomeric regions were detected using TelomereSearch (https://github.com/jamiemcg/TelomereSearch).

Chromatin interaction analysis

High-throughput chromosome conformation capture data from CG88, generated by Benagen Technology Company, were processed using HiC-Pro84 to identify valid interaction pairs, filtering out invalid pairs. Chromatin interaction heatmaps were generated using HiCExplorer85 with 150-kb resolution windows.

SNP and InDel calling of 422 accessions

Using the near-complete CLv4.0 genome16 as a reference, SNPs and small InDels were identified by analyzing the resequencing data from 422 diverse accessions and 186 RILs. The raw sequencing data underwent quality assessment with fastp (v0.25.0) using default settings. High-quality short reads were subsequently aligned to the reference genome using Burrows–Wheeler Aligner-maximal exact matches (v0.7.17-r1198)64. Duplicate reads were removed with Picard (v1.118; http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/), and SNPs and InDels were detected using the HaplotypeCaller algorithm within the Genome Analysis Toolkit (v4.1.5.0135)86. Filtering criteria included ‘QD < 2.0||FS > 60.0||MQ < 40.0||MQRankSum<−12.5||ReadPosRankSum<−8.0’ for SNPs and ‘QD < 2.0||FS > 200.0||ReadPosRankSum<−20.0’ for InDels. Non-biallelic SNPs and InDels with a read depth below 5 were excluded from further analyses.

To identify genomic regions and genes under artificial selection, we used two complementary methods: FST and XP-CLR. Calculations were performed in 20-kb sliding windows with a 2-kb step size using VCFtools (v0.1.17)87 for FST and a Python script (https://github.com/hardingnj/xpclr) for XP-CLR. Windows containing fewer than ten SNPs were excluded. Candidate selective regions were defined as those windows falling within the top 5% of the empirical distributions of FST or XP-CLR scores.

SV-based graph pangenome construction and SV genotyping

A total of four variant callers, as recommended in previous research13 (Sniffles (v.2.3.2)88, SVIM (v.2.0.0)89, CuteSV (v.2.1.1)90 and PBSV (v.2.9.0; https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/pbsv)), were used for variant calling across each accession using long-read data. Before SV calling, HiFi reads from 125 accessions and PacBio continuous long reads from eight accessions in a previous study19 were mapped to the CLv4.0 genome. The pbmm2 (v.1.13.1; https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/pbmm2) tool served as input for the PBSV software, while minimap2 (v.2.28-r1209)65 was used for the other three callers. Variants were retained if they displayed a ‘pass’ flag, a read depth of at least 4 and a length of less than 20 kb. To identify SVs across the 133 genome assemblies, the Syri91 tool (v1.6.3) was applied to genome alignments produced by MUMmer (v.4.0)92 with the parameters (--mum -c 100 -l 50 -g 500). For the 133 accessions with SVs identified by the five callers, all SVs shorter than 20 kb were merged using SURVIVOR (v.1.0.6)93, with a maximum allowed distance of 1 kb, and only calls supported by at least two callers that agreed on the variant type were reported. SVs longer than 20 kb detected by MUMmer92 were preserved.

We developed a graph-based pangenome using PAVs and inversions with the vg (v1.67.0) toolkit94, subsequently genotyping these SVs across 422 diverse accessions and 186 RILs using short-read sequencing. Initially, short reads were aligned to the pangenome graph via the giraffe function, followed by calculating read support through the pack function. SV genotyping for each accession was performed using the call function. Duplication-type SVs were genotyped across 422 diverse accessions and 186 RILs using short-read sequencing with the Paragraph95 software (v2.4a).

Phylogeny and population structure

To elucidate the phylogenetic relationships among the 422 cucumber accessions, we first extracted 18,600 fourfold degenerate synonymous third-codon transversion (4DTv) sites from the annotated SNP VCF file using the degenotate tool (v1.3; https://github.com/harvardinformatics/degenotate). These 4DTv sites were subsequently used to construct a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree in IQ-TREE (v.2.3.6)96 using the ‘GTR + R10’ model. Additionally, PCA was performed on the 4DTv sites for the 422 cucumber accessions using GCTA (v.1.94.1)97. Population structure was also assessed using ADMIXTURE (v.1.3.0)98, with the number of clusters (K) varying from 2 to 12, based on the same 4DTv sites.

Gene-level haplotype-based GWAS

We performed haplotype-based GWAS to identify genes associated with scab disease index using a linear mixed model framework36. For each gene, haplotypes were defined based on syntenic gene families and the PanNLRome across 125 genomes. associations between haplotype and phenotypes were tested using likelihood ratio tests. Population structure was controlled using the first two principal components and a genome-wide kinship matrix estimated from 1,215,886 high-quality SNPs. Genome-wide significance was determined using Bonferroni correction (threshold P < 0.01 / n, where n is the number of genes tested).

SNP- and SV-based GWAS

GWAS was performed on best linear unbiased prediction values for 38 traits (165 phenotype sets) across 11 environments in 422 accessions using GEMMA (v0.98.5)99, with the first three principal components as covariates. SNPs and SVs with a MAF of ≥0.05 were used; missing genotypes for SNPs were imputed using Beagle (v.21Jan17.6cc)100, and SVs with a missing rate of >0.5 were excluded. The genome-wide significance threshold was set at 1/n. Independent loci were defined by a distance of ≤50 kb and a linkage disequilibrium r2 of ≥0.2 to the lead variant. Subpopulation-stratified SV GWAS for fruit length was conducted in three geographic groups (East Asian, Eurasian and Indian); the XIS group was excluded due to insufficient sample size (n = 7).

Heritability estimation

Heritability was estimated using the MAF-stratified genomic restricted maximum likelihood method implemented in GCTA v1.94 (ref. 97). Following established criteria, rare variants were defined as those with a MAF<12n (where n is the number of accessions), a sample size-dependent threshold101. SVs were partitioned into two MAF bins: rare (0 < MAF < 0.034) and common (MAF > 0.034). A genomic relatedness matrix was constructed for each bin, and stratified restricted maximum likelihood was performed using the two genomic relatedness matrices simultaneously to partition heritability across MAF categories.

RNA-seq, eQTL and TWAS analysis

Raw RNA-seq reads were processed using fastp (v0.25.0) software102 and aligned to the genome with STAR (v.2.7.10a_alpha_220601)103, retaining uniquely mapped reads. Gene expression was quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads and TPM. Differentially expressed genes were identified using the DESeq2 R package v1.30.1 (ref. 104), with differentially expressed genes defined as those exhibiting an adjusted P value of <0.05 and an absolute log2 (fold change) of ≥1. GO enrichment analysis was performed using the R clusterProfiler package (v3.18.1)105.

For eQTL mapping, genes with TPM of >1.0 in at least ten RILs were retained. Expression levels were quantile-normalized before association testing. SVs with a MAF of >0.05 were tested for SV–gene associations using EMMAX (v2012-02-10)106 with a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold (P = 0.05 / n). Lead eSVs (SVs identified as eQTLs) were identified by linkage disequilibrium-based clustering in PLINK (v1.90b6.10 (ref. 107); --ld-window-r2 0.2 --ld-window-kb 100). eQTLs were classified as cis if the lead eSV fell within 2 Mb of the transcription start site or transcription end site of the target gene and trans otherwise; this 2-Mb cutoff was determined empirically (Extended Data Fig. 4f). eQTL hot spots were identified by permutation testing (1,000 iterations) in 100-kb windows (P < 0.01, minimum of three SVs). Colocalization between eQTL and GWAS signals was assessed using COLOC (v5.2.3)108, with a posterior probability of H4 > 0.50 used to define shared causal variants. TWAS was performed using gene expression (TPM) and fruit-length data from 182 RILs with EMMAX106, applying Bonferroni correction (P = 0.05/n).

Reannotation and classification of nucleotide-binding resistance genes

We initially identified NLR genes from EVidenceModeler annotations using InterProScan software (v5.59_91.0)109. To annotate the NLR genes that were not predicted by EVidenceModeler, we used NLR-annotator (v2.1)110 to identify genomic segments containing NLR genes for each accession. The segments that did not overlap with EVidenceModeler-predicted NLR genes and their 20-kb flanking regions were used to predict additional NLR genes using the BRAKER2 software71, implementing RefPlantNLR111 as homologous protein evidence and RNA-seq alignment data from 29 distinct tissues. The reannotated NLR gene models were subsequently integrated with the EVidenceModeler annotation results.

According to previous research34, we classified genes as NLR genes if they contained a nucleotide-binding, Toll/interleukin-1 receptor-like or coiled-coil with RPW8 domain. Specifically, genes with only an LRR or a coiled-coil motif were not classified as NLR genes. We further categorized genes into different subtypes based on their domain compositions: TNLs (containing at least a Toll/interleukin-1 receptor-like domain), CNLs (coiled-coil and nucleotide-binding domains), RNLs (containing at least an RPW8 domain) and NLs (containing at least a nucleotide-binding domain).

Gene-level PAV and CNV analysis

To detect gene-level PAVs and CNVs, we aligned the 27,360 high-confidence protein-coding genes from the CLv4.0 annotation to each genome using miniprot (v0.18)112 and processed the results with the pangene113 tool (v1.1-r231). A gene was defined as a gPAV if it was absent in any genome and as a gCNV if it was present in multiple copies in any genome. Population differentiation in gPAV/gCNV frequencies was quantified using the VST statistic33, an analog of the FST fixation index, calculated as VST = (VT – VS) / VT, where VT represents the total variance across populations, and VS denotes the average variance within populations.

Estimation of gCNV for the CsFT gene via ddPCR

CNV of CsFT was quantified by ddPCR on a QX200 platform (Bio-Rad) following the manufacturer’s guidelines, using CsUBQ (CsaV4_5G002584) as a single-copy reference gene for normalization. Copy number per haploid genome was calculated from positive and negative droplet counts using the Poisson distribution. Primers are listed in Supplementary Table 41. Detailed reaction conditions and data analysis procedures are provided in Supplementary Note 7.

Real-time quantitative PCR with reverse transcription analysis of the CsFT gene

Total RNA was isolated in accordance with the protocols outlined by the TransZol Up Plus RNA kit (TransGen Biotech). First-strand cDNA was subsequently generated using the TransScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix kit (TransGen). For real-time quantitative PCR with reverse transcription (RT–qPCR), SYBR Green Supermix was used alongside the CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Primer design for RT–qPCR was performed using Primer3.04 (Supplementary Table 41). CsActin (CsaV4_2G001628) was used as an internal control for normalization. A total of three biological replicates were analyzed, and relative expression levels were determined using the 2-ΔΔCt method114.

Fine mapping of the Ccu locus

To refine the interval around the Ccu locus, an F1 population was created by crossing the resistant accession ‘9110Gt’ (also known as ‘CG52’) with the susceptible accession ‘9930’, followed by selfing the F1 progeny to generate an F2 population. A total of 3,176 F2 individuals were genotyped using the flanking markers InDel-1 and InDel-10, and these individuals were subsequently selfed to establish an F2:3 population. The F2:3 lines were evaluated for resistance after scab inoculation and a systematic phenotypic assessment.

Disease resistance evaluation and inoculation

Disease resistance against five pathogens (S. fuliginea (powdery mildew), C. cucumerinum (cucumber scab), P. amygdali pv. lachrymans (bacterial angular leaf spot), F. oxysporum f. sp. cucumerinum (Fusarium wilt) and P. cubensis (downy mildew)), was evaluated at the Institute of Vegetables and Flowers in May and September 2021. All experiments were arranged in a randomized complete block design with three replications of ten plants each. For all diseases, the disease index (DI) was calculated using the formula: DI = ∑[(s × n)/(S × N)] × 100, where n is the number of plants with each disease rating, s is the disease rating scale, N is the total number of plants under investigation, and S is the highest disease rating scale. Detailed inoculation procedures, postinoculation conditions and disease rating scales for each pathogen are provided in Supplementary Note 7.

Phenotypic evaluation of agronomic traits

We evaluated 38 traits across 422 accessions in 11 environments over 6 years (2017–2024) in Shouguang, China, following the Descriptors and Data Standards for Cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.) with minor adjustments. Traits encompassed 21 fruit-related, 3 flower-related, 8 vegetative, 4 disease resistance and 2 chilling-tolerance phenotypes. Detailed measurement protocols for each trait category are provided in Supplementary Note 7.

Functional verification of Ccu and CsSPL1 genes

To create CRISPR–Cas9 mutations in CsSPL1 and CsCcu, highly specific guide RNA target sites were designed using the CRISPR-GE toolkit115, comprising two sites for CsSPL1 and one site for CsCcu. The guide RNA was inserted into the pKSE402 vector using Bsa-I and T4 ligase116. The resulting construct was introduced into the Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105. Transformation was performed in the long-fruit accession ‘9930’ for CsSPL1 and the scab-resistant accession ‘NCG121’ for CsCcu, following previously established protocols116,117. Verification of mutated plants was performed through sequencing of the target regions. All mutant lines generated in this study are available upon request from the corresponding author.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.0.4) unless otherwise noted. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using a two-sided Student’s t-test or a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test. χ2 tests were used to evaluate differences in haplotype or allele frequencies between geographic groups. Multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure where applicable. Genome-wide significance thresholds for GWAS and eQTL analyses were set at 1/n (Bonferroni correction), where n is the total number of variants tested. Sample sizes, numbers of biological replicates and exact P values are reported in the figures, figure legends and Supplementary tables. All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates.
