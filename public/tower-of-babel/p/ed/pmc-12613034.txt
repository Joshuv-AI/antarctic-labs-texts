Effect of cinnamaldehyde pickering emulsion on Pseudomonas fragi biofilms: Characterization, antibiofilm effects in vitro and in vivo, and a transcriptomic analysis
Authors: Yanwei Zhou, Xiaosu Hu, Huaimao Tie, Qiujin Zhu, Minping Wei, Yongguang Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPseudomonas fragi (P. fragi), a predominant biofilm-forming spoilage bacterium in fresh chilled meat, causes persistent contamination and poses a significant food safety concern. However, research on its biofilms remains exploratory, and effective control measures are currently lacking. This study developed an innovative biofilm-targeted preservation system by encapsulating cinnamaldehyde (CA), the most potent essential oil component against P. fragi (MIC = 0.3125 mg/mL), within a Pickering emulsion to overcome its inherent volatility and poor aqueous solubility. Molecular docking simulations revealed stable interactions among whey protein isolate (WPI), sunflower oil, and CA, guiding rational emulsion design. The optimized emulsion (oil-water ratio 6:4) exhibited excellent physical stability (particle size: 11.30 ± 1.88 μm; zeta potential: −31.6 ± 0.82 mV), oxidation stability, and sustained-release properties, along with biosafety validated by murine acute toxicity and hemolysis assays. Crucially, while maintaining identical MIC values to free CA, the CA Pickering emulsion demonstrated significantly enhanced biofilm inhibition and eradication capabilities against P. fragi. Transcriptomic analysis revealed, for the first time, that the CA emulsion induced dysregulation of 191 bacterial genes (114 downregulated, 77 upregulated), which simultaneously suppressed signaling and metabolic pathways while inducing stress adaptation responses, thereby disrupting biofilm formation and maintenance. This work establishes a novel stabilization paradigm for essential oils and provides a theoretical foundation for developing green biofilm-targeting technologies against meat spoilage bacteria, facilitating the practical applications of CA Pickering emulsions in meat preservation.Keywords: Cinnamaldehyde, Pickering emulsion, Pseudomonas fragi, Biofilm, Transcriptomic analysis

Introduction

Fresh chilled meat has become a crucial solution for meeting urban dietary needs amid accelerated urbanization and evolving lifestyles. According to data from China's National Bureau of Statistics, the production of fresh chilled meat in China alone exceeded 20 million tons in the first half of 2025, representing a cumulative growth rate of 11.8 % compared to the same period in the previous year (https://www.stats.gov.cn/). Notably, microbial contamination accounts for more than 25 % of meat quality deterioration incidents, with Pseudomonas fragi (P. fragi) identified as the predominant spoilage bacterium in chilled meat storage systems (Hu, 2022; Zhang et al., 2025). This bacterium exhibits exceptional biofilm-forming capacity, a key determinant of persistent meat contamination. Although studies indicate that biofilms formed by this bacterium pose substantial risks to meat safety and quality through mechanisms such as enhanced antimicrobial resistance and horizontal gene transfer, research on its biofilm is still at an exploratory stage (Zhang et al., 2025). Globally, annual meat spoilage exceeds 10 million metric tons, resulting in significant economic losses throughout the food supply chain and elevating food safety concerns. Current intervention strategies, primarily based on physical/chemical approaches, show limited efficacy against established biofilms and may potentially compromise product safety (Rahman et al., 2022). Therefore, the development of novel preservatives targeting biofilms formed by the primary spoilage bacterium in fresh chilled meat holds considerable practical significance.

Essential oils (EOs) exhibit significant antimicrobial efficacy and biosafety (Khwaza & Aderibigbe, 2025). Cinnamaldehyde (CA), the principal bioactive component of cinnamon essential oil, is under regulatory approval from governing bodies, including the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), for food applications (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/Cinnamaldehyde). Extensive studies have validated the biofilm inhibitory efficacy of CA against some pathogens (Hu et al., 2023). However, CA also has inherent limitations of essential oils, such as high volatility, limited aqueous solubility, and a required antibacterial concentration that often exceeds the organoleptically acceptable threshold (Leite de Souza, 2016). In recent years, researchers have effectively solved these problems by formulating CA as Pickering emulsions (Which have enhanced physical stability and good loading/release characteristics compared to surfactant-stabilized emulsions), thereby maximizing its biological activity (Wang, Xu, et al., 2023). Whey protein isolate (WPI) can be used as a stabilizer for Pickering emulsions. The free amino groups on the protein molecules of WPI can react with the free carboxyl groups in CA through a Schiff base reaction, thereby exerting excellent antibacterial effects (Chen et al., 2017; Sepúlveda et al., 2024). To facilitate the better application of CA Pickering emulsions against biofilms on meat products, elucidating their inhibitory mechanism is essential. Nevertheless, studies specifically investigating the mechanism of biofilm inhibition of CA are limited (Li, Liao, et al., 2024), and the inhibitory mechanism of CA Pickering emulsions against microbial biofilms remains unreported.

Transcriptomics has the advantages of high-throughput detection and the ability to process large amounts of data, making it a valuable tool for the holistic investigation of biofilm formation (Liu et al., 2023). Recent studies have employed this approach to elucidate the inhibitory mechanisms of essential oils against biofilms. For example, transcriptomic analysis showed that sub-minimum inhibitory concentrations (Sub-MIC) of clove and oregano essential oil could significantly reduce the expression of genes related to energy metabolism in the biofilm of Salmonella Derby (Liu et al., 2023). Additionally, eugenol reduced the expression of genes associated with extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), transcription factors, and quorum sensing (QS) in the biofilm of Staphylococcus aureus (Li, Li, et al., 2025). Therefore, transcriptomics can be used to preliminarily investigate the inhibitory effect of CA Pickering emulsion on P. fragi biofilm.

In this study, CA was selected as the active ingredient due to its MIC among ten essential oil constituents. First, molecular docking simulations and instrumental characterization were performed on the prepared emulsion to systematically investigate the stability, safety, and sustained-release behavior of CA-loaded Pickering emulsions. Subsequently, the anti-biofilm efficacy against target strains was quantitatively assessed through crystal violet staining and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). Additionally, in vivo (On fresh meat surfaces) and in vitro experiments confirmed that the CA emulsion has potent biofilm eradication capacity. Finally, transcriptomic analysis elucidated the inhibitory mechanisms of the emulsion, including suppression of the two-component signal (TCS) transduction system, disruption of central metabolic pathways, and impairment of bacterial stress adaptation responses. The results of this study can provide new insight into the stabilization strategies of essential oil constituents and offer a theoretical basis for the application of CA Pickering emulsion in the prevention and control of biofilms of foodborne spoilage microorganisms.

Materials and methods

Materials

P. fragi ATCC 4173 was purchased from the BeNa Culture Collection (Henan, China). Bacterial stocks were preserved in cryovials containing 20 % glycerol and stored at −80 °C. CA (98 % purity) was sourced from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All chemicals used in the study were of analytical grade.

Preparation of CA Pickering emulsion

The preparation of the CA Pickering emulsion was based on the studies of other scholars with appropriate modifications (Chen et al., 2017). First, the aqueous phase containing 2 % (w/v) WPI emulsifier was prepared by dissolving WPI in deionized water under continuous stirring at room temperature for 2 h. Subsequently, the solution was stored at 4 °C for 12 h to ensure complete protein hydration. The oil phase was prepared by mixing sunflower seed oil (SSO) and CA in a 1:1 (v/v) ratio. To prepare the emulsions, the aqueous phase and oil phase in different proportions were homogenized in an ice bath using a high-speed disperser (Scientz, Ningbo, China) operated at 12,000 rpm for 3 min.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking simulations were performed using Discovery Studio 2019 software with the CHARMm force field to analyze the interactions among CA, linoleic acid (LA, the primary component of SSO), and WPI. The three-dimensional structure of WPI (PDB ID: 5IO5) was retrieved from the PDB, and the structures of CA and LA were obtained from PubChem. The protein was prepared by removing water molecules and adding hydrogen atoms. Docking parameters included pre- and post-docking minimization, ring flexibility, and soft grid potential. The docking process was validated by calculating binding energies, and the pose with the lowest binding energy was selected for further analysis.

Fluorescence spectroscopy

The fluorescence spectra of the samples were recorded using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-2700, Hitachi, Japan) (Kazemi-Taskooh & Varidi, 2025). WPI solutions from different treatment groups were diluted to a concentration of 0.02 mg/mL. The final concentrations of CA and SSO in the measurement system were 0.001 mg/mL and 0.1 mg/mL, respectively. The spectral parameters were set as follows: an excitation wavelength of 280 nm, an emission wavelength range of 300–500 nm, slit widths of 5 nm for both excitation and emission monochromators, and each sample was scanned three times and averaged.

CD spectroscopy

The far-ultraviolet CD spectra of the samples were acquired in the wavelength range of 180–260 nm using a circular dichroism spectrometer (Chirascan, Applied Photophysics Ltd., UK) (Kazemi-Taskooh & Varidi, 2025). The sample preparation protocol was identical to that used for the fluorescence spectroscopy measurements, except that the concentration of CA was 0.01 mg/mL. The spectra were recorded at a scanning speed of 100 nm/min, with a bandwidth of 1 nm and a data pitch of 0.1 nm.

Characterization of CA Pickering emulsion

Wettability test

The wettability of WPI particles was measured using an optical contact angle meter (Harke, Beijing, China) (Li, Wu, et al., 2025). WPI particles were compressed into a tablet (Diameter 13.0 mm and thickness 2.0 mm). Subsequently, 2 μL aliquots of deionized water were deposited onto the tablet surfaces using a microliter syringe. Images were taken by a high-speed camera. The contact angle was calculated with the software attached to the instrument.

Determination of the optimal oil-to-water ratio

The emulsification properties of proteins were evaluated according to previous methods (Yin, Cao, et al., 2025). Pickering emulsions were prepared using a 2 % (w/v) whey protein isolate (WPI) solution as the aqueous phase at oil-to-water volume ratios of 3:7, 4:6, 5:5, 6:4, and 7:3 to investigate the effect of the oil-to-water ratio on the emulsification characteristics. After homogenization, 10 μL of the emulsion from the bottom phase was collected and diluted to 1 mL with 0.1 % (w/v) sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) solution. The absorbance at 500 nm (A₀) was measured immediately after vortex mixing. After allowing the emulsion to stand for 30 min, the sampling and dilution procedure was repeated, and the absorbance (A₁) was measured. The emulsion was assessed for macroscopic stability through storage and centrifugal tests. Storage stability was evaluated at room temperature, while centrifugal stability was determined by observing phase separation after centrifugation (8000 rpm, 5 min).

The microscopic morphology of the emulsion was characterized using optical microscopy (OM) and CLSM, following established methods (Yin, Chen, et al., 2025). Emulsion characterization

Zeta potential and particle size

According to a previous study (Chen et al., 2017), the particle size of the emulsions was measured during a 10-day storage period at 4 °C and 28 °C using a laser particle size analyzer (Beckman, California, USA). Zeta potential was analyzed under the same storage conditions with a nanoparticle size analyzer (Beckman, California, USA). Before each zeta potential measurement, samples were diluted 100-fold with deionized water (Refractive index: 1.33).

Rheological characterization of CA emulsions

The freshly prepared emulsion was analyzed using a rheometer (HR10, TA, USA) with a 40 mm diameter plate at 25 °C and a 1.0 mm gap distance. Shear viscosity test (Range: 0.1–100 s−1) and frequency scans (Range: 0.1–10 Hz) were performed (Li, Zhang, et al., 2024).

Oxidation stability of CA emulsion

The extent of lipid oxidation in the emulsions was evaluated according to the method of Song et al. (2026) with modifications. The peroxide value (POV) and thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) were measured as indicators of primary and secondary oxidation products, respectively.

POV: Briefly, 0.3 g of emulsion was mixed with 9.8 mL of a chloroform-methanol mixture (7:3, v/v) by vortexing. Subsequently, 50 μL of ammonium thiocyanate solution (4 M) and 50 μL of iron (II) solution were added sequentially. After reacting at room temperature for 5 min, the absorbance was measured at 500 nm. The POV was expressed as milligram equivalents of peroxide per kilogram of fat.

TBARS: One gram of the emulsion was mixed with 5 mL of TBARS reagent [containing 0.375 % (w/v) thiobarbituric acid, 15 % (w/v) trichloroacetic acid, and 0.25 N hydrochloric acid]. The mixture was heated in a boiling water bath for 10 min, cooled to room temperature, and then centrifuged at 5500 ×g for 25 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 532 nm. The TBARS value was calculated using Eq. (3) and expressed as milligrams of malondialdehyde (MDA) per 1000 g of emulsion.

Safety evaluation of CA emulsion

Acute oral toxicity testing was conducted in compliance with institutional animal care guidelines and prior methodology (Wang, Yuan, et al., 2023), with approval from the local ethics committee. Fasting C57BL/6 mice (Approximately 30 g) received a limit test dose of 100 mg/kg BW CA equivalent. The emulsion was diluted with saline to 10 mg/mL CA. Experimental mice (n = 5) were administered 10 mL/kg BW emulsion via oral gavage, while control mice (n = 5) received equivalent volumes of saline. Evaluation of the anti-biofilm activity of CA Pickering emulsion

Determination of MIC

MIC was determined using a 96-well plate two-fold dilution method (Li, Wu, et al., 2024). The experimental procedure comprised serial two-fold dilution of TSB medium (100 μL/well) with CA emulsion (Prepared in water)/ EO solution (Prepared in 20 % ethanol), followed by inoculation with 100 μL bacterial suspension in the logarithmic growth phase (1.5 × 106 CFU/mL). Negative control (20 % ethanol) and blank control (Sterile TSB medium) were established concurrently. Following 24 h incubation at 28 °C, the MIC was defined as the lowest concentration at which no visible colonies appeared visually.

Biofilm inhibition and removal at sub-MIC

Biofilm inhibition assay

The amount of biofilm formation was determined using crystal violet staining (Wei et al., 2022). P. fragi was inoculated in TSB medium and incubated overnight until reaching the stationary phase. The diluted bacterial suspensions (6 × 108 CFU/mL) were combined with TSB-containing gradient concentrations of CA solutions/emulsions (Final concentration of 1/10, 1/5, and 1/2 MIC, respectively) at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio. The mixtures were transferred to 96-well plates and set up three control groups: blank (Sterile TSB), solvent (20 % ethanol), and vehicle (CA-free emulsion), each in triplicate. The initial biofilm was formed by aerobic incubation at 4 °C and 28 °C for 2 days. After incubation, the microplates were gently rinsed with 0.85 % NaCl solution to remove planktonic cells and subsequently stained with 0.2 % crystal violet solution. Excess stain was removed by washing with sterile saline solution, and the bound dye was finally dissolved in 95 % ethanol for quantification. Biofilm was determined by using a microplate reader (Thermo, Massachusetts, USA) at 595 nm. In addition, the CA-supplemented medium was replenished to continue incubation for 2 days, and the amount of mature biofilm was determined by the same method. Biofilm removal assay

In the in vitro model, pre-cultured biofilms on surfaces were gently washed with sterile saline to remove planktonic cells and then co-incubated with CA emulsion/solution (Diluted in saline) for 24 h. The removal efficacy on biofilms of different maturation stages was evaluated by plate counting, with control groups treated with saline alone.

In the in vivo model (Cho et al., 2024), fresh pork (Loin part) samples were obtained from commercial sources, sterilized by immersion in 75 % ethanol (10 min), rinsed in distilled water, and subjected to UV irradiation. The samples (5 g, 2 × 2 × 0.5 cm) were inoculated with 50 μL bacterial suspension (25 μL each on the top and bottom) and incubated at 4 °C and 28 °C for 2 days and 4 days. Following biofilm formation, the samples of Pseudomonas-inoculated fresh meat were rinsed twice with sterile saline to remove loosely adhered bacteria. For biofilm clearance assessment, samples were immersed in 10 mL of CA emulsion/solution for 24 h, washed to remove residual compounds, homogenized in 45 mL saline, and plated for removal after serial dilution.

Observation of biofilm structure with CLSM

The bacterial suspension (1.5 mL, 6 × 108 CFU/mL) was combined with an equal volume of TSB containing graded CA concentrations (Final concentrations: 1/10, 1/5, 1/2 MIC for emulsion; 1/2 MIC for solution), then inoculated into 12-well plates preloaded with 18 mm cell culture slides. Following 48 h incubation at 28 °C, planktonic cells were removed through sterile saline rinses. Biofilms were stained with SYTO9 (20 μM) under dark conditions for 25 min, and images were acquired via CLSM at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm.

Transcriptomic analysis

Following bacterial strain revival, cell suspensions (6 × 108 CFU/mL) were inoculated into 6-well plates containing TSB (With or without 1/2 MIC emulsion), followed by incubation at 28 °C for 24 h. After medium removal, biofilms were washed with physiological saline, and then a pipette gun was used to vortex until the biofilm was completely detached from the plate. The resulting suspensions were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min (4 °C). The supernatant was discarded, the sample frozen in liquid nitrogen (≥ 0.5 h), and stored at −80 °C, with three biological replicates per experimental group.

RNA quality was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. High-quality reads were aligned to the Pseudomonas fragi reference genome (GenBank: GCF_015476275.1) using Bowtie2. Then the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed according to the selection criteria |log2(FoldChange)| ≥ 0.585, P < 0.05 (Zhang et al., 2019). The DEGs were further subjected to function annotation and pathway enrichment analysis by Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclo­pedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG).

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was repeated at least three times. All data were expressed as the mean ± SD. IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0 was used for ANOVA, one-way ANOVA was used, and the Duncan test was used. All data met the homogeneity of variance, and the significance of the difference between treatments was determined (P < 0.05).

Results and discussion

MIC of essential oil components

In this study, ten plant-derived antimicrobial compounds with documented efficacy (Hexanal, salicylic acid, thymol, linalool, eugenol, carvacrol, perillaldehyde, CA, cinnamic acid, and citral) were initially screened to evaluate their inhibitory effect against P. fragi. As shown in Table 1, CA demonstrated the strongest antimicrobial activity with the lowest MIC (0.3125 mg/mL) against the target strain. These results were consistent with the previous reports that CA exhibits excellent antibacterial activity (Liu et al., 2020). Based on this evidence, CA was selected as the active ingredient for the next study. Furthermore, to facilitate the development of biofilm inhibitors, the inhibitory mechanism of CA on biofilms will be studied. Therefore, subsequent experiments employed sub-MIC (1/10, 1/5, and 1/2 MIC) of the essential oil components that did not inhibit bacterial growth.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking is a useful tool for visualizing interactions between receptors and ligands. In this study, docking simulations elucidated the interaction mechanisms among WPI, CA, and LA. As shown in Fig. 1A, the calculated binding energy between WPI and the two ligands (CA and LA) was −8.424 kcal/mol, indicating a strong affinity. This value falls within the typical range of protein-ligand interactions, consistent with previously reported binding energies between whey proteins and fatty acids and aromatic compounds (Zhang et al., 2023). Stability analysis revealed that the complex was primarily stabilized by the following non-covalent interactions: First, the long alkyl chain of LA formed hydrophobic interactions with nonpolar residues of WPI (Such as leucine, valine), a common force of lipid-protein complexes (Zhang et al., 2023). The double-bond structure of LA may enhance binding flexibility, thereby optimizing interfacial droplet alignment in emulsion. Second, the aldehyde and hydroxyl groups of CA formed hydrogen bonds with polar residues of WPI (Such as asparagine, glutamine), while water molecules served as bridging agents to further stabilize the complex. Lastly, the aromatic ring of CA formed parallel or perpendicular π-stacking interactions with aromatic residues of WPI (Such as tyrosine, phenylalanine), thereby significantly reinforcing the rigidity of the interfacial layer.

These results provided a theoretical foundation for developing novel food-grade emulsifiers.

Spectroscopic characterization of conformational changes

The molecular interactions between WPI, CA, and SSO and their impact on protein conformation were investigated by CD and fluorescence spectroscopy.

Fluorescence spectroscopy revealed quenching of the intrinsic fluorescence and a blue shift in the maximum emission wavelength for WPI-CA and WPI-CA-SSO complexes (Fig. 1B). This indicates that CA binding compacted the tertiary structure, relocating tryptophan residues to a more hydrophobic environment (Kazemi-Taskooh & Varidi, 2025).

The CD spectra (Fig. 1C) revealed characteristic negative peaks at 208 and 222 nm for WPI, indicative of a well-defined secondary structure (Li, Zhang, et al., 2024). Upon complexation with SSO and CA, the molar ellipticity at these wavelengths was markedly altered, suggesting substantial conformational changes. This change was further supported by the results of quantitative secondary structure analysis (Fig. 1D). Native WPI was primarily composed of β-turn (42.7 %), with substantial random coil (27.9 %), and lower contributions from α-helix (25.5 %) and β-sheet (3.9 %). However, the formation of complexes led to a significant structural remodeling. Specifically, the addition of SSO (WPI-SSO) or CA (WPI-CA) dramatically increased the contents of both α-helix and β-sheet, while the random coil content dropped to 0 % and 6.6 %, respectively. The ternary complex (WPI-CA-SSO) exhibited the highest β-sheet content (38.4 %) and a high α-helix content (29.6 %), indicating that CA and SSO synergistically promoted a more ordered and stable secondary structure.

These spectroscopic findings align with molecular docking results, which predicted stable binding via hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and π-stacking. The conformational changes, increased structural order, and compaction directly explain the enhanced stability of the emulsion. The structurally reinforced WPI forms a cohesive and mechanically robust interfacial layer, effectively encapsulating the oil phase and CA, thereby contributing to the superior physical stability and sustained-release properties.

Determination of optimal oil-to-water ratio for CA Pickering emulsions

The EAI and ESI serve as critical parameters for evaluating the performance of emulsifiers, which directly reflect the efficiency and stability of emulsifiers in forming emulsions (Hou et al., 2022). As demonstrated in Fig. 1E, WPI-stabilized emulsions showed a tendency to increase and then decrease in both EAI and ESI values with increasing oil fraction. Maximum values were achieved at an optimal 6:4 (v/v) oil-to-water ratio. This phenomenon was mechanistically explained by the dual effects of WPI particle surface modification: Moderate increases in oil phase proportion enhanced particle hydrophilicity, thereby improving interfacial adsorption capacity. However, excessive hydrophilicity beyond the optimal ratio (> 6:4) induced colloidal instability through reduced adsorption energy at the oil-to-water interface, ultimately compromising emulsification efficiency (Yin, Cao, et al., 2025).

Storage stability and centrifugal stability tests further validated these findings (Fig. 1F and G). While all tested oil-to-water ratios formed Pickering emulsions initially, systems with 3:7 to 5:5 ratios exhibited progressive phase separation during storage. Remarkably, emulsions containing 60 %–70 % oil phase maintained structural integrity post-centrifugation, demonstrating the capacity of WPI to stabilize high oil-load systems (Ji et al., 2025). Long-term stability assessments revealed that the 6:4 formulation preserved macroscopic homogeneity throughout a 6-month storage period at ambient temperature, showing no visible phase separation.

To elucidate the microstructural characteristics of the prepared emulsions, OM and CLSM were used. CLSM analysis revealed that all the emulsions formed a typical oil-in-water (O/W) structure, with the proteins forming continuous interfacial layers around the oil droplets (Red) (Fig. 2B). Combined with OM (Fig. 2A), the emulsions with a 6:4 oil-to-water ratio had the smallest mean droplet diameter and narrowest size distribution, significantly reducing Ostwald ripening and droplet coalescence risks (Di et al., 2024). These structural characteristics confirm the exceptional storage stability and centrifugal resistance of the emulsion system.

Characterization of CA Pickering emulsion

Determination of emulsion type

Surface wettability serves as a critical indicator in both the formation and stability of Pickering emulsions. Contact angle measurement is the most direct way to assess the surface wettability of particles, with the emulsion type being predictable from these values. Particles exhibiting contact angles <90° preferentially stabilize water-in-oil (W/O) emulsions, whereas those with angles >90° favor O/W systems (Song et al., 2022). The WPI proteins demonstrated a contact angle of 72.1° (Fig. 1H), approaching the optimal 90° threshold where maximal desorption energy ensures enhanced interfacial particle stability (Binks, 2002). This wettability profile confirms that the protein can form stabilizing emulsions, and the prepared Pickering emulsions are of O/W type, which is consistent with both CLSM observations and prior findings using WPI-stabilized CA emulsions (Bellalta et al., 2012).

Zeta potential and particle size

To further evaluate the stability of the produced emulsions, zeta potential and particle size analyses were conducted. Under optimal oil-to-water ratio conditions, the emulsion exhibited a normal unimodal distribution with an average particle diameter of 11.30 ± 1.88 μm (Fig. 3A). The zeta potential reached 31.6 ± 0.82 mV (Fig. 3B), demonstrating strong electrostatic stabilization, a critical determinant of emulsion stability. This particle size parameter aligns with reported values for Pickering emulsions containing common essential oils such as cinnamon and thyme (Cahyana et al., 2022).

The stability of Pickering emulsions at 28 °C (Ambient temperature) and 4 °C (Simulated application temperature) showed a significant temperature dependence (Fig. 3C and D). A reversible “destabilization-recovery” pattern emerged at 4 °C, potentially attributable to temperature-modulated surfactant redistribution causing interfacial charge reconstruction. The results suggest that low temperatures should be used for short-term storage to take advantage of the late-stability self-repairing property, while higher ambient temperatures should be avoided for long-term storage to prevent irreversible aggregation.

Rheological characterization

Macroscopic rheology was employed to characterize the flow and viscoelastic behavior of the emulsions, providing insight into their physical stability. The viscosity of all emulsions decreased with increasing shear rate, exhibiting shear-thinning behavior (Fig. 3E). The CA Pickering emulsion exhibited significantly higher viscosity than the emulsion formulated without SSO across the entire shear rate range. This elevated viscosity can impede droplet migration and coalescence by reducing Brownian motion and collision frequency, thereby enhancing the kinetic stability of the emulsion system (Wei et al., 2020).

The viscoelastic properties were further investigated by oscillatory frequency sweeps (Fig. 3F). For the CA Pickering emulsion, the storage modulus (G') was consistently greater than the loss modulus (G") over the measured frequency range, indicating a dominant elastic response and a stable, solid-like gel network. The high G' value suggests the formation of a robust interfacial layer and a three-dimensional network within the emulsion, which can effectively prevent droplet aggregation. In contrast, the emulsion without SSO showed much lower moduli and a transition from liquid-like (G" > G') to weak gel-like (G' > G”) behavior with increasing frequency, reflecting its relatively fragile structure. The superior viscoelasticity of the CA emulsion is primarily attributed to the co-stabilization by WPI and SSO, which forms a cohesive and elastic interface around the droplets. This dense network is crucial for conferring resistance against deformation and phase separation during storage, ensuring long-term stability. Similar reinforcement effects of oil phases on the rheological properties of protein-stabilized Pickering emulsions have been previously reported (Li, Zhang, et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2020).

Oxidation stability

The oxidative stability of the emulsion system was assessed by monitoring the levels of primary and secondary oxidation products throughout storage (Fig. 3G). The POV remained consistently low without a significant increase, indicating effective suppression of primary lipid oxidation. Similarly, the TBARS value remained stable within a low range, suggesting no substantial accumulation of secondary oxidation products. These findings demonstrate the excellent oxidative stability of the emulsion, which is comparable to that of cinnamaldehyde emulsions stabilized by cellulose nanofibers (Jiang et al., 2025). This robust stability can be attributed to several factors: First, cinnamaldehyde, as a natural bioactive compound, possesses inherent antioxidant activity that retards lipid oxidation (Yan et al., 2023). Second, the dense interfacial layer formed by WPI acts as a physical barrier, hindering the penetration of pro-oxidative factors such as oxygen and metal ions, thereby curbing the initiation and propagation of oxidation chains.

Releasing behavior

Controlled release kinetics are essential for the sustained antibacterial action of emulsions. Using 95 % ethanol as an oil-phase simulant, we characterized CA release profiles (Fig. 3H) (de Kruijf & Rijk, 1997). All emulsions exhibited a biphasic release pattern characterized by an initial rapid release followed by a slow, sustained release of CA. Notably, consistent with previous studies, Pickering emulsions with external oil-phase stabilization demonstrated significantly higher cumulative CA release than the emulsion without SSO (Cui et al., 2025), attributable to the capacity of SSO to suppress Ostwald ripening through interfacial stabilization (Chen et al., 2017). The results showed that Pickering emulsions stabilized by WPI and SSO have some sustained-release ability, which is mainly based on the mechanism of dissolution and diffusion to release CA (Mao et al., 2019). In addition, the observed release profile, rapid initial delivery (82 % within 3 h) transitioning to prolonged release (18 % over 48 h), is the preferred release profile for inhibition of the biofilm. The burst phase facilitates immediate microbial load reduction, while sustained-release maintains sub-inhibitory concentrations, thereby mitigating drug resistance development (Forier et al., 2014).

Safety evaluation

To assess the safety of the Pickering emulsion for chilled meat preservation, we systematically evaluated acute oral toxicity and its hemocompatibility (Fig. 4). Acute oral toxicity testing via the limit-dose method revealed no mortality or abnormal behaviors in treated mice at practical application concentrations. Relative organ weight, a sensitive toxicity biomarker (Mazhar et al., 2024), showed no significant deviations from normal ranges (Fig. 4B). Body weights remained stable across groups (Fig. 4C), indicating no systemic toxicity. Gross examination and histopathological analysis further confirmed no emulsion-related lesions or damage in major organs (Fig. 4D and E).

The different lowercase letters in Fig. 4B, C, and F indicate significant differences in organ index, body weight change, and hemolysis rate, respectively (P < 0.05).

The hemolysis rate, a critical hemocompatibility indicator for food-contact materials, increased with the concentration of CA (Fig. 4F and G). At 0.156 mg/mL, hemolysis was negligible, and even at 0.625 mg/mL, it remained low (∼2 %). Since hemolysis rates <5 % are considered non-hemolytic and safe (Wang et al., 2021), these results indicate that the emulsion exhibited excellent blood compatibility under the application conditions. Collectively, these findings confirm the biocompatibility and safety of the emulsion.

Thus, the developed emulsion system demonstrates superior stability, controlled-release performance, and safety characteristics, which can effectively overcome the inherent limitations of CA, such as its high volatility and strong odor, while enhancing its antimicrobial efficacy. This formulation strategy shows promising potential for practical applications. Future studies could explore co-encapsulation with synergistic antimicrobial agents through multi-target mechanisms (Li, Zhao, et al., 2024).

Effect of CA of sub-MIC on biofilm formation

The MIC of the CA Pickering emulsion was determined to be 0.3125 mg/mL, identical to that of the CA solution. Fig. 5 shows the inhibition and removal effects of CA emulsion/solution on P. fragi biofilm at different temperatures (4 °C and 28 °C), CA concentrations (1/10, 1/5, and 1/2 MIC), and incubation times (2 days and 4 days). In addition, control experiments confirmed the absence of antimicrobial activity in the blank emulsion (Data not shown), therefore excluding it from subsequent analyses.

The different lowercase letters in Fig. 5A and B indicate significant differences in biofilm inhibition and removal at 2 days and 4 days for different concentrations of CA, respectively (P < 0.05).

CA exhibited significant concentration-dependent inhibition of P. fragi biofilm formation (Fig. 5A). Taking the early-stage biofilm at 4 °C as an example, the 1/2 MIC emulsion achieved an inhibition rate of 48.91 %, markedly higher than the 18.98 % observed for the solution (P < 0.05), demonstrating a 2.6-fold efficacy enhancement. This disparity likely arises from the interfacial stabilization properties of the Pickering emulsion system, which enhances antimicrobial efficacy by enabling controlled release of CA, improving its dispersion, and reducing its volatility and degradation (Jiang et al., 2025). These results were consistent with previous findings where Pickering emulsions exhibited 2–4 times greater biofilm inhibition compared to individual dextro-borneol or citral treatments (Wang et al., 2022). Notably, while solution efficacy increased at 28 °C (31.81 % inhibition at 1/2 MIC), presumably due to temperature-enhanced volatile permeability, the emulsion maintained consistent performance across temperature regimes (4 °C: 48.91 %; 28 °C: 47.25 %), confirming its formulation stability. In addition, both formulations showed reduced efficacy against mature biofilms, suggesting adaptive bacterial resistance development during prolonged incubation (Kregiel, 2014). Notably, the emulsion maintained a high inhibition rate at a low concentration (1/10 MIC), which was significantly higher than that of the solution, suggesting that the emulsion carrier could enhance the bioavailability of low-dose drugs (Wilson et al., 2022).

Significant in vitro and in vivo efficacy disparities were observed in biofilm removal experiments using 1/2 MIC concentrations (Fig. 5B). For example, for 2-day biofilms at 4 °C, in vitro removal rates of 80.47 % (Emulsion) and 69.93 % (Solution) decreased to 49.86 % and 51.57 % in vivo, respectively (Fig. 5B1), likely attributable to biofilm matrix complexity in biological environments (Such as the presence of meat-derived proteins and lipids, which can impede antimicrobial penetration and sequester active compounds) (Wang, Zhao, et al., 2023). Temporal analysis revealed progressive reductions in CA efficacy for both formulations, suggesting biofilm maturation enhances resistance mechanisms (Liu et al., 2024).

The CA emulsion prepared in this study demonstrated superior efficacy against target bacterial strains compared to pure oil solutions. This advantage was also observed in emulsions made from other essential oils (Such as cedarwood oil, olive oil, and isoeugenol), which exhibited significant inhibitory effects on spoilage bacteria, including Pseudomonas fluorescens and Escherichia coli (Huang et al., 2021; Krogsgård Nielsen et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2022). This superiority, particularly evident at higher concentrations (1/2 MIC) and lower temperatures, underscores the practical advantage of the emulsion formulation. Its sustained-release properties contribute to an extended shelf life, enhancing both its inhibitory effect and commercial viability. Furthermore, the emulsion state may impart positive effects on the sensory qualities of chilled meat and mangoes (Jiang et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025). Interestingly, the observation that the inhibition rate was lower than the removal rate suggests that the CA emulsion may be more effective at disrupting established biofilm structures (e.g., by degrading EPS) than at preventing initial bacterial attachment.

Microscopic observation of biofilm

The inhibitory effect of sub-MIC CA treatment on P. fragi biofilm formation was visually confirmed via CLSM, as shown in Fig. 5C. Under high magnification, the biofilm exhibited different aggregation states. Compared with the control, the biofilm density was significantly reduced by CA treatment, with the extent of reduction varying with CA concentration and formulation (Emulsion vs. solution). These CLSM observations were consistent with the crystal violet staining results, reaffirming that CA is an effective inhibitor of P. fragi. The CA Pickering emulsions at the same concentration (1/2 MIC) resulted in less biofilm formation than the free CA solution and showed a dose-dependent inhibitory effect. This enhancement may stem from the sustained-release properties and interfacial stabilization of Pickering emulsions, which prolong antimicrobial exposure and promote small-molecule diffusion. Analogous studies report that essential oil emulsions (Such as citral and tea tree oil) significantly inhibit biofilms of Gram-negative bacteria, including Shewanella putrefaciens and Pseudomonas putida (Hu et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2025). For instance, citral emulsions impede biofilm development by disrupting structural integrity and downregulating QS-related gene expression (Hu et al., 2024). These findings suggest that essential oil emulsions may share common antibacterial mechanisms, though the targeting specificity may vary depending on both the phytochemical composition and bacterial species. Therefore, to better analyze the mechanism, a concentration of 1/2 MIC was selected as the concentration for subsequent experiments to investigate the inhibition mechanism of CA Pickering emulsion on P. fragi biofilm.

Transcriptomic analysis

Global analysis of transcriptomic profile

To comprehensively elucidate the inhibition mechanism of CA Pickering emulsion on P. fragi biofilm, we performed comparative transcriptomic analysis of cells treated with sterile physiological saline (Control group) and 1/2 MIC of CA emulsion (Treatment group). The RNA-Seq results demonstrated that approximately 15.06–17.18 million high-quality clean reads were obtained for each sample, with Q20 ≥ 98 %, and Q30 ≥ 95 % (Supplementary Table S1). Subsequently, these clean reads were aligned to a reference genome, yielding 99.41 %–99.76 % of the reads mapped to the genome (Supplementary Table S2). The quality control results indicated that the sequencing data from all samples were of high quality and suitable for subsequent bioinformatics analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct clustering between treatment and control groups (Fig. 6A), indicating significant changes induced by the CA emulsion. A total of 191 genes were classified as DEGs after CA emulsion treatment. The DEGs included 114 down-regulated genes (59.69 %) and 77 up-regulated genes (40.31 %) (Fig. 6C). Hierarchical clustering was conducted for the 191 DEGs, in which cluster 1 (Red) and cluster 2 (Blue) denoted the down-regulated and up-regulated genes, respectively (Fig. 6B).

GO functional enrichment analysis

The GO annotation analysis of DEGs includes three functional categories, which are biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF). A total of 191 biofilm-associated DEGs in P. fragi were analyzed. The top 20 entries with the highest down-regulated GO enrichment levels (Fig. 6D) and the top 20 entries with the highest up-regulated levels (Fig. 6E) are listed separately. The down-regulated DEGs were mainly enriched in the BP of iron ion transport (GO:0006826), transition metal ion transport (GO:0000041), and siderophore transmembrane transporter activity of MF (GO:0015343). This indicates a significant inhibition of iron-related pathways. Given iron's critical role as a cofactor for bacterial QS and EPS biosynthesis, the observed suppression of iron transport genes (efeU, efeB) likely impairs bacterial iron acquisition, thereby disrupting biofilm matrix development. Leitão et al. (2025) found through quantitative analysis that iron chelators can reduce biofilm formation, which is consistent with our research results. In addition, the down-regulation of transmembrane transport genes (Such as oprD) may hinder nutrient absorption and signal transmission, further weakening the stability of the biofilm. These findings suggest that the CA emulsion exerts anti-biofilm effects through multiple targets.

Upregulated DEGs demonstrated predominant enrichment in two GO categories: BP and MF. The entries related to redox enzyme activity in MF were the most prominent, including cytochrome-c oxidase activity (GO:0004129), oxidoreduction-driven active transmembrane transporter activity (GO:0016675), and redox-driven active transmembrane transport activity (GO:0015453). These findings suggest that CA emulsion may enhance cellular energy metabolism through the activation of electron transport chain complexes and oxidative phosphorylation pathways (Fasnacht & Polacek, 2021). In terms of BP, the main enriched pathways are response to stress (GO:0006950), lipid catabolic process (GO:0016042), and cytochrome complex assembly (GO:0017004). These results suggested that CA emulsion severely disrupted the metabolism of P. fragi.

KEGG pathway analysis

KEGG pathway analysis was further conducted to map the DEGs to functional pathways (Fig. 6F and G). In slight contrast to most essential oils that greatly impair the expression of genes related to toxicity, QS, motility, adhesion, and virulence genes (Maggio et al., 2025), CA emulsion treatment of P. fragi resulted in down-regulated DEGs that were mainly enriched in folate biosynthesis, TCS and amino acid metabolism (Such as Phenylalanine metabolism, Phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan biosynthesis). The up-regulated DEGs were mainly enriched in three types of pathways: metabolism, damage repair, and drug resistance.

Metabolic disruption and energy imbalance

Fig. 6F demonstrated that key metabolic pathways, including folate biosynthesis (KEGG: ko00790), amino acid metabolism, and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (KEGG: ko00020), were significantly downregulated in P. fragi following CA emulsion treatment. Folate metabolism, central to one‑carbon (1C) metabolic transformations (Zarou et al., 2021), was impaired through the inhibition of pathway-associated genes (queE, phhA), potentially limiting nucleotide synthesis and cellular proliferation. Concurrent suppression of the TCA cycle directly reduced ATP production, leading to insufficient energy supply (Martínez-Reyes & Chandel, 2020). This process is typically accompanied by dysfunction of the electron transport chain, thereby increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and further exacerbating oxidative stress and metabolic dysfunction (Martínez-Reyes & Chandel, 2020). Furthermore, diminished glycolysis and amino acid metabolism (Such as phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan) restricted cofactor and protein synthesis, which aligns with the observed downregulation of “sigma factor activity” in GO analysis.

Meanwhile, upregulated pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation (KEGG: ko00190) and fatty acid degradation (KEGG: ko00071) suggested bacterial adaptation via lipid catabolism for energy acquisition. However, concomitant ROS overproduction likely exacerbated metabolic stress, impairing the synthesis and secretion of the biofilm matrix through inducing oxidative damage and inhibiting energy supply, such as down-regulation of the TCA cycle. This metabolic dysregulation is similar to that of antimicrobials that cause microbial dysfunction and death by stimulating cellular metabolism, such as carbohydrate metabolism and amino acid metabolism (Chen et al., 2022). This is corroborated by the abnormal energy metabolism of Listeria monocytogenes caused by CA (Yang et al., 2025). Similarly, Fan et al. (2023) reported that ZnO nanoparticle-mediated downregulation of branched-chain amino acid (Valine, leucine, isoleucine) pathways inhibited P. fragi motility and biofilm formation.

Signal transduction and QS inhibition

The TCS (KEGG: ko02020) is one of the bacterial signaling mechanisms, enabling environmental perception and coordinated cellular responses to regulate stress adaptation, motility, QS, and biofilm formation (Sultan et al., 2021). QS (KEGG: ko02024) is a bacterial density-dependent mechanism through which bacteria communicate with each other. After CA emulsion treatment, the expression of genes responsible for TCS (Such as ccoN, oprD, fecR, phoQ) and QS (Such as phoQ, fecR, qseB) in P. fragi was significantly downregulated. This further validates the inhibition of iron ion transport in the GO analysis, indicating that the CA emulsion may interfere with the bacterial cooperative behavior by blocking the perception of environmental signals (Such as iron signals) and related QS signal transduction. This impairment of environmental signal perception likely ultimately inhibits the TCS-driven expression of extracellular polysaccharides, flagella, and other virulence factors (Yang et al., 2025). In particular, the histidine kinase (phoQ) in TCS is a key factor regulating EPS synthesis and cell movement. Its inhibition may directly reduce the secretion of extracellular polysaccharides and motility (Zhang et al., 2020). These findings align with reported phoP/phoQ-mediated EPS modulation in Cronobacter sakazakii biofilms (Ma et al., 2022), suggesting that the emulsion may inhibit biofilm maturation through a similar pathway. The observed higher biofilm removal rate compared to the inhibition rate in crystal violet assays further supports EPS disruption as one of the primary inhibitory mechanisms.

Stress response and resistance regulation

Transcriptomic analysis revealed that sub-MIC CA emulsion triggered a complex adaptive response in P. fragi, characterized by the upregulation of genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation (KEGG: ko00190; such as ccoN, ccoP) and fatty acid degradation. Although these changes may promote transient survival under stress, they coincided with a more critical impairment of core defense mechanisms. Specifically, we observed the downregulation of key resistance genes (Such as acrA, involved in cationic antimicrobial peptide and β-lactam resistance pathways) and virulence factors (Such as fimA, which is essential for fimbria-mediated biofilm formation), alongside the suppression of DNA repair pathways such as base excision repair (Wang et al., 2018). This paradoxical state, where partial metabolic adaptation occurs alongside compromised resistance, virulence, and repair capabilities, suggests that the multi-target action of CA emulsion disrupts bacterial homeostasis without strongly promoting stable resistance. This paradoxical response aligns with prior findings on CA-mediated inhibition of Listeria monocytogenes biofilms, where EOs downregulated TCS genes to exert inhibitory effects while bacteria attempted, yet ultimately failed to counteract such suppression through partial repair mechanisms, resulting in net biofilm inhibition (Yang et al., 2025). Consequently, targeting biofilm-specific pathways at sub-inhibitory concentrations, as opposed to exerting broad bactericidal pressure, represents a promising strategy for mitigating the risk of resistance development (Benny et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2025).

In summary, transcriptomic analysis indicated that CA emulsion inhibits the biofilm of P. fragi through multiple targets. The main inhibitory action pathways were the metabolism of the TCA cycle, folate biosynthesis, and amino acid metabolism. Secondly, the environmental adaptation-related pathways, such as TCS and QS systems, were impaired by CA emulsion. Lastly, CA emulsion manifests as a Stress response and resistance regulation, thus effectively inhibiting the formation of P. fragi biofilm. Conclusions

In the present study, a CA-loaded Pickering emulsion was developed with a mean particle diameter of 11.30 ± 1.88 μm and zeta potential of 31.6 ± 0.82 mV. The Pickering emulsion exhibited good physical stability, controlled-release characteristics, and biological safety, and exhibited significant inhibition/removal effects for the biofilm formation of P. fragi. Transcriptomic profiling revealed multi-target interference mechanisms involving metabolic, signaling, and stress responses by transcriptomic analysis. This study demonstrates the potential of CA Pickering emulsion for cold fresh meat preservation and provides the mechanistic foundation for the development of next-generation biofilm control strategies. Future research will focus on elucidating the specific molecular pathways underlying its multi-target effects. Integrated proteomic and metabolomic analyses will be employed to dynamically monitor temporal expression changes of key regulatory nodes, complemented by gene knockout techniques to validate the functional contributions of core targets.
