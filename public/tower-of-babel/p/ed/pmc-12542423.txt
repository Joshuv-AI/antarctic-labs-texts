ITGB5-mediated biomechanical regulation in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma stroma impacts tumor progression and prognosis
Authors: Aoran Yang, Chengli Gu, Yining Liu, Baihe Luo, Yu Zeng, Minghui Zhou, Chunli Li, Chen Pan, Linxuan Wu, Zhiwen Hu, Xin Zhang, Feng Gao, Yu Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is characterized by stromal remodelling andstiffening, a process involving extracellular matrix (ECM) depositionand cross-linking, which forms a dense physical barrier that limits the penetration of chemotherapeutic agents and restricts immune cell-mediated cytotoxicity. In addition, the tumor stroma modulates nutrients availability and fosters cancer cell invasion and metastasis. Therefore, therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating the tumor stroma hold promise for overcoming some of the major challenges in pancreatic cancer treatment. However, most clinical trials of stromal-targeted therapies have not demonstrated meaningful improvements in overall survival, underscoring an urgent need to identify novel stromal targets and approaches that modulate the tumor microenviroment in ways that enhance therapeutic response and improve patient outcomes.MethodsTranscriptomic datasets of PDAC sampleswere obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). By integrating immune infiltration algorithms with weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), this study aims to identify hub genes associated with cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) infiltration in PDAC, explore the prognostic relevance of CAF infiltration, and construct a gene‑expression-based prognostic mode. Furthermore, we will employ a multi-modal approach incorporating magnetic resonance elastography, atomic force microscopy, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), histological assays, cell-based functional experiments, and animal models to identify candidate core genes involved in CAF infiltration and stromal stiffening in pancreatic cancer, and to assess the potential therapeutic relevance of targeting these genes.ResultsIn PDAC, increased stromal stiffness and poor prognosis are both strongly linked to activation of CAFs. WGCNA identified six hub genes—ANTXR2, ANXA1, BCAT1, PLAU, TGFβ1, and ITGB5—which were used to construct a predictive model for estimating CAF abundance and forecasting PDAC prognosis. Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis showed that ITGB5 is highly expressed in CAFs. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that targeting ITGB5 in CAFs reduces tumor stiffness, thereby inhibiting the malignant progression of PDAC. These findings suggest a potential therapeutic benefit, although impact on clinical prognosis remains to be established.ConclusionThe integration of magnetic resonance elastography, atomic force microscopy, and single-cell sequencing allows detailed research characterization of the biomechanical properties of PDAC. ITGB5 has been identified as an important regulator of the biomechanical features of PDAC. Targeting ITGB5 in CAFs may reduce the excessive stiffness of PDAC tissue and mitigate gemcitabine-associated stromal fibrosis, suggesting a potential strategy to improve treatment response. The effect on patient prognosis, however, requires confirmation in clinical studies.Trial registrationThis study is a prospective single-center clinical trial, which has been registered in the U.S. Clinical Trials Database (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06526442) with the trial registration number NCT06526442. The registration date was October 9th, 2020.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07119-5.

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is the most common exocrine malignancy of the pancreas. Because symptoms are often subtle and the pancreas is anatomically concealed, PDAC is usually diagnosed at an advanced stage. The overall 5‑year survival rate remains approximately 13% according to recent epidemiological data [1]. The aggressive clinical course of PDAC is partly attributable its complex tumor microenvironment [2], which involves the activation of a large number of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) that secrete abundant collagen fibers [3]. On one hand, these collagen fibers provide essential nutrients for tumor growth, such as branched-chain amino acids [4], that support tumor growth. On the other hand, the dense stromal barrier impairs drug delivery and efficacy [5], suppresses immune responses [6], and facilitates tumor immune evasion. Additionally, the accumulation of collagen fibers contributes to the “stiff” biomechanical characteristics of PDAC [7], a phenomenon that is also observed, though to varying degrees, in other tumors such as breast cancer [8]. This biomechanical phenotype can be described by four aspects [9]: elevated tissue stiffness, increased solid pressure, heightened interstitial fluid pressure, and altered extracellular matrix architecture in tumor microstructure.

For PDAC, these four factors contribute to intratumoral hypoxia, stabilization and activation of HIF-1α activation of HIF-1α, and the enhancement of malignant traits such as invasion, metastasis, and resistance to therapy in PDAC [10]. Additionally, the intense pressure compresses blood vessels, reducing vessel patency and perfusion, which hinders the effective delivery of chemotherapeutic agents to the tumor, leading to poor prognosis. Therefore, the biomechanical characteristics of PDAC are increasingly recognized as potential diagnostic or prognostic indicators, and may also represent novel therapeutic targets [11].

Currently, there are several methods to assess the biomechanical characteristics of PDAC, including endoscopic ultrasound elastography [12], atomic force microscopy [13], Shore durometer measurements [14], and histopathological assessment of extracellular matrix remodeling. However, many of these approaches are invasive or minimally invasive, and both atomic force microscopy and histopathological assessment rely on tissue sampling, which are limited by the pronounced spatial heterogeneity of PDAC, often leading to sampling bias [15]. 3D magnetic resonance elastography (3D-MRE) has been extensively applied in the clinical diagnosis and treatment staging of liver fibrosis [16]. It is non-invasive, clinically feasible and provides macroscopic-scale measurements that reduce the impact of tumor heterogeneity.

Integrin Beta-5 (ITGB5) is a gene encoding the integrin β5 subunit, which forms heterodimeric receptors with specific α subunits and participates in cellular adhesion and signaling. For example, in gastric cancer, ITGB5 has been reported as a prognostic biomarker and promotes tumor progression via TGFBR2 [17]. In oral cancer, ITGB 5contributes to tumorigenesis and disease progression [18]. Notably, previous studies have demonstrated that ITGB5 plays a significant role in PDAC. For instance, acetylation-mediated modification of ITGB5 enhances perineural invasion in PDAC, leading to poor prognosis [19]. Additionally, ITGB5 confers radiotherapy resistance by promoting DNA damage repair and activating the MEK/ERK signaling pathway [20]. These findings support ITGB5 as a potential therapeutic target in PDAC. However, the impact of ITGB5 on the biomechanical properties of PDAC and its potential as a stromal-targeting therapeutic strategy remain insufficiently explored. Therefore, this study investigates how ITGB5 regulates biomechanical characteristics of PDAC and contributes to malignant progression.

Materials and methods

Patient selection and PDAC sample collection

The inclusion criteria for PDAC patients were consistent with those applied in our previous study [21]. Briefly, Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Histologically confirmed PDAC; (2) Main pancreatic duct (MPD) diameter ≥ 3 mm; (3) Age ≥ 18 years and ≤ 80 years; (4) No history of extra pancreatic malignant tumors; (5) No prior neoadjuvant chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or biliary drainage before surgery. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Inability to maintain adequate breath-holding or intolerance to the mechanical driver stimulus required for MRE; (2) Tumor diameter < 10 mm; (3) Contraindications to magnetic resonance imaging (e.g., claustrophobia). Ultimately, 80 PDAC patients with a median age of 60 years (range, 35–79 years) and a median body mass index of 21.66 kg/m2 (range, 16.60–28.70 kg/m2), were included in this study. Detailed information is provided in Table S1. Fresh tumor tissues were obtained during surgery and either snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen or stored in preservation solution for subsequent analyses.

Isolation of primary CAFs and conditioned medium collection

For freshly collected PDAC tissues, the tissues were first rinsed 3–5 times with PBS to remove blood, and then minced into approximately 1 mm3 pieces with sterile scissors. The samples were dissociated using a SeekMate A pro (Beijing, China) and filtered through a 70-μm sterile cell strainer. Subsequently, Anti-LUM IgG primary antibody was added according to the protocol, incubated at 4 °C for 30 min, and unbound antibodies were removed by elution. After cell counting, 20 μl of Anti-mouse IgG-conjugated magnetic beads were added per 110⁷ cells and incubated at 4 °C for 15 min to allow the magnetic beads to bind to the antibodies attached to cell surface antigens. MACS separation columns were then used to sort and collect LUM-positive cells. The collected cells were transferred to a 6-well plate and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin to grow CAFs.

Approximately 5 × 10⁶ CAFs were seeded in a 10-cm dish until ~ 70–80% confluence. Cells were then washed and incubated in serum-free DMEM for 48 h. Then the conditioned medium (CM) was collected and centrifuged to remove cell pellets. CFPAC-1 and Mia Paca-2 cells were then cultured with the CM (refreshed every 2–3 days) for 2 weeks before being used in experiments.

Cell culture

The human PDAC cell lines (CFPAC-1, Mia Paca-2) were purchased from Procell biotechnology company (Wuhan, China). CFPAC-1 cells were cultured in Iscove's Modified Dulbecco's Medium (IMDM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Mia Paca-2 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. CAFs were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 15% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cells were maintained in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Single-cell RNA sequencing preparation and analysis

Freshly harvested tissues were washed in ice-cold RPMI 1640 and enzymatically dissociated using the Miltenyi Biotec’s Multi-Tissue Dissociation Kit 2 per manufacturer specifications, with DNase treatment applied selectively based on homogenate viscosity. Following erythrocyte depletion (Miltenyi 130-094-183) and optional removal of cellular debris/dead cells (Miltenyi 130-109-398/130-090-101), cell counts and viability were quantified via fluorescence-based analysis (Countstar® Rigel S2) using acridine orange/propidium iodide dual staining. Viable cells underwent two washes in RPMI 1640 before resuspension at 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL in PBS containing 0.04% bovine serum albumin. Single-cell RNA sequencing libraries were constructed with the SeekOne® Digital Droplet Single Cell 3’ Library Kit, where preprocessed cells were mixed with reverse transcription master mix and loaded into a SeekOne® DD Chip S3 alongside hydrogel beads and partitioning oil. Reverse transcription in generated emulsion droplets proceeded at 42 °C for 90 min, followed by enzymatic inactivation at 80 °C for 15 min. cDNA was subsequently recovered, PCR-amplified, then fragmented, end-repaired, A-tailed, and adapter-ligated. Indexed PCR enriched DNA templates containing 3’ poly-A regions, cell barcodes, and unique molecular identifiers. Final libraries were purified (SPRI beads), quantified via qPCR (KAPA Biosystems KK4824), and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (PE150) or DNBSEQ-T7 (PE100) platforms. All bioinformatic processing and analysis were performed using SeekSoul Tools software.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol kit (Sevenbiotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a Nano-2000 microspectrophotometer. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the extracted RNA using a reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green qPCR Supermix (Vazyme) on an LC96 PCR system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) with GAPDH as the reference control gene. Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The reactions were run in triplicate, and detailed qPCR primer sequences are listed in Table S2. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. Melting curve analysis was carried out from 60 to 95 °C to verify amplification specificity.

Western blot analysis

Protein extracts were resolved via 4–20% SDS-PAGE, followed by transfer onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were then probed with primary antibodies. A peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse or anti-rabbit antibody served as the secondary antibody, and the ensuing antigen–antibody interaction was visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence assay. Detailed information on antibodies can be found in Table S3.

Immunohistochemistry

Tissues were fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, andsectioned at a thickness of 3 μm thickness. For paraffin sections, antigen retrieval was performed either by heat-induced epitope retrieval or by proteinase K digestion, while this step is omitted for frozen sections. To minimize background staining, slides were blocked with 1–5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies, diluted at a ratio of 1:100–1:500, are incubated either overnight at 4 °C or for 2 h at room temperature. After that, HRP-conjugated anti-mouse or anti-rabbit igG secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature, according to the host species of the primary antibody. Subsequently, DAB chromogenic detection (lasting 5 min) and hematoxylin counterstaining are conducted, followed by dehydration and mounting of the sections. Brown staining signals were evaluated under a light microscope for their distribution and intensity. Detailed antibodies information can be found in Table S3.

Atomic force microscopy measurements

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements were conducted using an MFP3D AFM (Asylum Research, London, UK), followed previously established protocols [22]. Briefly, for PDAC tissues, frozen tissue sections of PDAC, 20 µm thick, were prepared. Prior to measurement, tissue sections or cells were immersed in PBS. During AFM examination, the samples were maintained in PBS containing a protease inhibitor and placed in an acoustic isolation enclosure for scanning. A borosilicate glass spherical probe with a diameter of 5 µm mounted on a silicon nitride cantilever with a spring constant of 0.06 N/m was used for measurement. Before each experiment, the cantilever was calibrated using the thermal oscillation method. For morphological imaging of tissues/cells, a scan matrix of 256 × 256 was applied. For AFM mechanical mapping, a scan area of 16 µm × 16 µm was used. The Hertz model was employed to calculate the elastic modulus (E1) of tissues and cells. The samples were assumed to be incompressible, and then a Poisson’s ratio of 0.5 was used for the Young’s modulus calculation. For each tissue sample, measurements were taken at three different positions, and the results were averaged.

Scanning electron microscopy

Cells were seeded on sterile coverslips in culture dishes and fixed prior to SEM processing. After cell collection, the culture dishes were stored at 4 °C. Tissue blocks were washed three times with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB, pH 7.4) for 15 min each, then transferred to 0.1 M PB (pH 7.4) containing 1% osmium tetroxide and incubated at room temperature for 1–2 h; afterwards, they were washed again 3 times with 0.1 M PB (pH 7.4), 15 min each time, followed by photographing.

Animal experiments

Six-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were used in this study. To establish the xenograft model, PDAC cells co-cultured with CAFs (shITGB5 or shNC) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of mice. Tumor volume was calculated as short diameter2 × long diameter/2. Mice were divided into two experimental groups. In group A, tumor growth rate and weight were measured, and IHC was performed on harvested tumor tissues. In Group B, mice underwent MRI or MRE scanning when tumor volume reached ~ 1000 mm3 (to minimize stiffness measurement bias), followed by euthanasia.

LSL-K-ras G12D (KI/+), LSL-Trp53 R172H (KI/+), and Pdx1-Cre (TG/+) (KPC) mice were purchased from Cyagen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). The KrasLSL-G12D/+ and Pdx1-Cre mouse strains were initially bred with Trp53flox/flox mice. After two successive generations of breeding, F1 progeny carrying the KrasLSL-G12D/+ ; Trp53flox/flox genotype and those with the Pdx1-Cre; Trp53flox/flox genotype were intercrossed to generate triple-genotype KPC mice.

At 10–12 weeks of age, KPC mice were examined for palpable tumors—according to the experimental protocol, the tumors typically measured 4–5 mm in diameter, corresponding to the late pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia/early tumor stage. After two independent researchers confirmed the presence of tumors for two consecutive days, the mice were randomly divided into two cohorts: the first cohort (15 KPC mice per treatment group) was desighated for survival analysis. The second cohort (5 KPC per treatment group) was used for histopathological analysis.The first group (control group) received intraperitoneal injections of an equivalent volume of 0.9% saline (vehicle) twice a week. The second group (gemcitabine group) was treated with intraperitoneal injections of gemcitabine at a dose of 50 mg/kg twice a week. The third group (cilengitide group) received cilengitide via tail vein injection at a dose of 30 mg/kg once every three days. The fourth group (gemcitabine plus cilengitide group) was administered gemcitabine (25 mg/kg twice a week) in combination with cilengitide via tail vein injection (15 mg/kg once every three days). For the histopathology cohort, treatment was discontinued 4 weeks after the initiation, and animals were euthanized. For the survival cohort: mice were monitored until they reached the experimental endpoint. Humane endpoints were defined as signs of advanced pancreatic cancer, including abdominal distension, cachexia-like deterioration, reduced mobility, ascites, overnight weight loss > 10% or total weight loss ≥ 20% of maximum body weight, hunched posture, signs of distress, or tumor burden interfering with mobility or food/water access. Maximum tumor burden permitted by ethical approval was not exceeded. Mice reaching endpoints prematurely due to non-tumor-related causes were censored.

MRE imaging and data processing

MRE acquisition and data processing were performed as previously described [21, 23]. Before imaging, water was withheld from the for 6 h. Anesthesia was induced and maintained with 1.5% isoflurane during scanning. Mice were positioned laterally within a custom-made, eight-channel small-animal magnetic resonance coil (5-cm inner diameter; Suzhou Medcoil Healthcare Co., Ltd.). A hemispherical aerodynamic mechanical wave transducer was placed in close contact with the tumor to deliver 200 Hz vibrations during MRE.

CCK-8 assay

Cell viability was assessed using a CCK-8 assay (Dojindo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were seeded at 2 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates. At designated time points, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and plated were incubated per the manufacture’s protocol. Absorbance at 490 nm was measured using a Varioskan Flash microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Colony formation assay

PDAC cells co-cultured with CAFs from the indicated experimental groups were seeded into 6-well plates. After 2 weeks of incubation, colonies were fixed in 4% formaldehyde and stained with 0.5% crystal violet. Colonies were imaged using a digital camera (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Transwell assay

For each Transwell insert, 200 µL of PDAC cell suspension a containing 3 × 104 cells was added to the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with 600 µL of culture medium supplemented with. After 24 h, cells that had migrated to the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5% crystal violet. Invaded cells were counted under aa Nikon microscope.

EdU

The EdU assay was used to assess cell proliferation in PDAC cells. Cells were incubated with 20 μM EdU for 2 h. Cells were then fixed with paraformaldehyde and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. The percentage of EdU-positive cells was quantified.

Bioinformatics analysis

Transcriptomic data and tumor mutation burden (TMB) data for PDAC patients were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), andtranscriptomic data for 63 pancreatic cancer patients were retrieved from GEO (accession GSE57495). Allanalyses were performed in R (version 4.0.5). Cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) infiltration in the both TCGA and GEO cohorts was estimated using four algorithms (EPIC, TIDE, MCP-counter, and xCell) and a stromal score was calculated to quantify stromal infiltration. Survival was assessed by the Kaplan–Meier method and group differences tested with the log-rank test. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was used to identify co-expressed gene module the module showing the strongest positive correlation with CAF infiltration and stromal score was selected. Overlapping genes between the TCGA- and GEO-derived modules were identified (Venn diagram) and subjected to KEGG and GO enrichment analyses. Prognosis-related genes in PAAD were screened by univariate Cox regression and refined by LASSO to define core genes. A risk score based on core-genes expression was used to dichotomize patients at the median into high- and low-risk groups; receiver operating characteristic ROC curves evaluated predictive performance. Spearman correlation analysis assessed relationships between core genes and pro-stromal genes. Immunotherapy response in the risk groups was predicted using TIDE. Finally, mutational landscapes for the risk groups were computed and visualized using the R package maftools.

Statistical analysis

Cntinuous variables with an approximately normal distributionare reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as medians. Categorical variables are summarized as countss and percentages unless otherwise specified. Fortwo groups comparisons,Student’s t-tes was used for normally distributed continuous variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normal data. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons among three or more groups were performed with one-way ANOVA (Tukey’s post hoc test) for parametric data or the Kruskal-Walli’s test (Dunn’s post hoc test) for non-parametric data. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test (χ2 test) or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were < 5. Survival curves are generated by the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. All statistical analyses and figures were produced using R (version 4.0.5) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.1). All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Infiltration of CAFs mediates stromal stiffening and poor prognosis in PDAC

In this prospective clinical trial, 80 patients with PDAC underwent conventional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) (Table S1). T2FS images did not distinguish biomechanical differences between tumors, whereas MRE detected marked biomechanical heterogeneity (Fig. 1A, B, the first row). Using the median MRE shear stiffness at 40 Hz (median = 2.3), patients were dichotomized PDAC patients into high-stiffness (HS) and low-stiffness (LS) groups (Fig. 1E, HS vs. LS, p < 0.0001). The HS group showed increased collagen deposition (Fig. 1A–B, second row; Fig. 1F, p < 0.0001) and elevated expression of the activated CAF marker α-SMA (Fig. 1A–B, third row; Fig. 1G, p < 0.0001). Ki-67 immunostaining was also higher in the HS group, indicating greater tumor cell proliferation (Fig. 1A, B, the fourth row, and Fig. 1H, HS vs. LS, p < 0.0001). Furthermore, Collagen deposition correlated strongly with stromal shear stiffness (Fig. 1J, r = 0.795, p < 0.0001). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) on the same tissue sections corroborated the MRE findings: the HS group exhibited a significantly higher Young’s modulus and pronounced surface irregularity on 2D/3D deflection-retrace topographies (Fig. 1C–D; HS vs. LS, p < 0.0001). In independent transcriptomic cohorts (TCGA-PAAD and GSE57495), higher estimated CAF infiltration was associated with worse survival in multiple algorithms In TCGA-PAAD by EPIC (HR = 4.363, 95% CI 1.589–11.977, p = 0.02) and MCP-counter (HR = 2.905, 95% CI 1.260–6.699, p = 0.009) (Fig. 1K–L), and in GSE57495 by MCP-counter (HR 2.37, 95% CI 1.074–5.229, p = 0.028) and TIDE (HR = 2.169, 95% CI 1.147–4.102, p = 0.015) (Fig. 1M–N). In conclusion, these data indicate CAF activation and collagen accumulation contribute to stromal stiffening in PDAC and that increased CAF infiltration is associated with poorer prognosis. Identifying genes that regulate CAF infiltration may therefore reveal candidate therapeutic targets for this aggressive malignancy.

Identification of core genes mediating CAFs infiltration by weighted gene co-expression network analysis

Using WGCNA, we identified co-expressed gene modules in both TCGA-PAAD and GSE57495 cohorts. In TCGA, the blue module was strongly correlated with estimated CAF infiltration (Fig. 2A, r = 0.76, p = 2e−34; r = 0.8, p = 3e−40). In GSE57495, the green module correlated with CAF infiltration and stromal score (Fig. 2B; r = 0.81, p = 2e−50; r = 0.64, p = 2e−26). Intersection of these modules yielded 120 overlapping genes (Fig. 2C) which were enriched for pathways implicated in stromal remodeling and fibrosis, including the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 2D, E). Univariate COX regression identified 34 prognosis-associated, high-risk genes, including ITGB5 (all p < 0.01, Fig. 2F). LASSO regression further prioritized six core genes (ANTXR2, ITGB5, ANXA1, BCAT1, PLAU andTGFB1) most predictive of outcome (Fig. 2G). Patients were dichotomized at the median expression of the core-gene signature into high- and low-risk groups; the resulting model demonstrated good discriminative performance (Fig. 2H, AUC: 0.756, 95% CI 0.678–0.828). High-risk patients had significantly worse survival in TCGA (Fig. 2I, p = 0.025; HR 1.596, 95% CI 1.055–2.412) and GSE57495 (Fig. 2J, p < 0.001; HR 3.321, 95% CI 1.729–6.379). These findings nominate ANTXR2, ITGB5, ANXA1, BCAT1, PLAU, and TGFB1 as candidate regulators of CAF infiltration and potential targets for stroma-directed therapies in PDAC.

Hub genes mediate CAFs infiltration and attenuate immune response in the PDAC

To validate the robustness of the CAF signature as a predictor of CAF infiltration, we computed Spearman’s rank correlation between the CAF risk score and stromal scores, as well as CAF abundance estimated by EPIC, xCell, MCP-counter and TIDE. In the TCGA-PAAD cohort (Fig. 3A), the CAF risk score correlated strongly and positively with CAF abundance across multiple estimation methods and with stromal scores. Moreover, within the TCGA-PAAD (Fig. 3B–C), both the CAF risk score and the expression levels of the six core genes were positively associated correlations with a panel of established CAF markers. Using the TIDE algorithm to evaluate predicted response to immune checkpoint blockade, only 22% of patients in the high-risk group were predicted to respond, compared with 57% in the low-risk group (Fig. 3D–E, all p < 0.001). This suggests that low-risk patients are less likely to undergo immune escape and more likely to benefit from immunotherapy. In addition, high-risk patients exhibited higher frequencies of mutations in commonly altered PDAC genes than low-risk patients (KRAS: 72% vs. 51%; TP53: 69% vs.43%; SMAD4: 25% vs.19%; CDKN2A: 21% vs. 14%), indicating an association between the hub-gene signature, the PDAC mutational landscape, and the tumor microenvironment. Collectively, these findings suggest that hub-gene signature predicts CAF abundance, correlates with the immunotherapy response predicted by TIDE, and reflects differences in PDAC mutational profiles. Analysis of the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) showed that ANTXR2, ITGB5, BCAT1, and TGFB1 were enriched in fibroblast lines but not in PDAC cell lines (Fig. 3H–I; all p < 0.05). ANXA1 did not show significant enrichment, whereas PLAU was predominantly expressed in PDAC cells.

To identify candidate therapeutic targets, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing on tumor specimens from eight PDAC patients, four classified as high-stiffness (HS) group and four as low-stiffness (LS) group. Using canonical marker genes (Fig. 4D), we annotated 12 distinct cell types, including a fibroblast cluster (Fig. 4B–C). We next projected expression of ITGB5, TGFB1, ANTXR2, and BCAT1 onto the UMAP embedding to determine their cellular localization. We performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses for these four genes. For overall survival (OS), univariate analysis associated high ITGB5, ANTXR2, and BCAT1 expression with shorter OS (Table S5, Univariate analysis, all p < 0.05), whereas TGFB1 exprssion remained an independent predictor of worse OS (Table S5, Multivariate analysis, p = 0.033), whereas ANTXR2 and BCAT1 did not. By contrast, LS samples were enriched for signaling pathways such as PSAP, TENASCIN, TNF, TRAIL, CLDN, and IL-6, which are more closely associated with tumor cell malignancy and inflammatory responses (Fig. 5E).

Differences in stromal stiffness were also associated with distinct cell-state trajectories. Pseudotime trajectory analysis mapped developmental landscapes in HS and LS groups, and revealed three major branches (Fig. 5F–G). Notably, cells from the HS samples were preferentially localized to branch 1 (pre-branch) and branch 2. Of the core genes TGFB1, ANTXR2, BCAT1, and ITGB5, only ITGB5 was preferentially enriched in branch 1 (Fig. S1A). Pseudotime analysis of CAF subpopulations revealed that branch 1 overlapped with trajectories of multiple CAF subtypes, including myofibroblastic CAFs (mCAFs) and inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs) (F5g. 5I), supporting a specific association of ITGB5 with CAFs and their subtypes.

Although subcutaneous xenografts provide initial evidence that CAF ITGB5 promotes tumor fibrosis and growth, they do not fully recapitulate the native PDAC microenvironment. Therefore, we employed a genetically engineered KPC mouse model in this study, assigning animals to the Vehicle, Gemcitabine, Cilengitide, and combination Gemcitabine + Cilengitide treatment groups. Immunohistochemistry in KPC tumours showed highes ITGB5 expression in vehicle mice, reduced levels in single-agent groups, and the greatest suppression in the combination treatment (Fig. 7K–O, the first row, all p < 0.01). α-SMA staining was reduced across treatment groups, most prominently in the combination treatment (Fig. 7K–N, the second row, and Fig. 7P, all p < 0.01).

Notably, Gemcitabine treatment increased collagen fiber deposition relative to Vehicle, (consistent with prior reports [24]). Despite increased collagen, α-SMA levels in Gemcitabine-treated tumors did not differ significantly from Vehicle (Fig. 7L, P, second row, p > 0.05). AFM measurements indicated that Gemcitabine-treated tumors had the highest tissue Young’s modulus, whereas Cilengitide-treated tumors had the lowest; combination therapy attenuated Gemcitabine-induced stiffening (Fig. 7K–N, R, fourth row, all p < 0.001).

Alough Gemcitabine increased matrix stiffening, it improved survival; importantly, Gemcitabine + Cilengitide combination produced the greatest survival benefit (Fig. 7S, p < 0.0001). Collectively, these data indicate that targeting ITGB5 reshapes PDAC tumor biomechanics in vivo, suppresses tumor progression, and may enhance therapeutic efficacy. Further studies are warranted to define the underlying mechanisms.

Discussion

PDAC is characterized by pronounced tissue stiffness, which largely arises from the complexity of its TME [25]. CAFs play a central role in shaping this microenvironment [26]. CAFs suppress immune cell function through secretion of cytokines and metabolic mediators, thereby promoting tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis [27]. They also remodel the extracellular matrix (ECM) to form physical barriers that impede drug delivery and immune-cell infiltration [28]. In addition, CAFs are major determinants of the adverse biomechanical properties of PDAC. Therefore, modulating CAF phenotypes or overcoming their barrier function to inhibit tumor progression and normalize PDAC biomechanics represents a promising therapeutic strategy.

However, broad stromal-targeting approaches in clinical and preclinical studies have largely failed, underscoring the need for more precise, cell-type–specific stromal targets. In this study, using single-cell transcriptomic and bioinformatic analyses, we identified ITGB5 as a CAF-enriched candidate therapeutic target in PDAC. Through complementary in vitro and in vivo experiments, we show that ITGB5 promotes PDAC malignancy and that elevated ITGB5 expression is associated with poorer patient prognosis. Biomechanical assays demonstrated the ITGB5 silencing significantly decreased key stiffens parameters, including shear stiffness measured by 3D-MRE and Young’s modulus measured by AFM. These results indicate that ITGB5 is a critical regulator of the biomechanical properties of PDAC.

Cilengitide is a cyclic RGD peptide antagonist of αvβ3/αvβ5 integrins. Previous studies have shown that it can suppress ITGB5(αvβ5) expression in various tumors [29–31] and can inhibit CAF proliferation and remodel the tumor immune microenvironment to enhance antitumor cytotoxicity [32]. In PDAC models, improved delivery and release efficiency of Cilengitide has been shown to improve intratumoral perfusion, increase drug penetration, and thereby enhance cytotoxic effects on pancreatic cancer cells [33].

Gemcitabine, a first-line chemotherapeutic agent for PDAC [34], can prolong patient survival but is associated with significant toxicities. Recent study reported that gemcitabine exacerbates stromal fibrosis in PDAC [24], consistent with our own findings; increased fibrosis can impair drug penetration and compromise therapeutic efficacy.

In the KPC mouse model, we evaluated whether combining cilengitide with gemcitabine could target ITGB5/αvβ5-dependent CAF activity, remodel PDAC biomechanics, mitigate gemcitabine-induced fibrosis, and improve therapeutic efficacy and survival. We found that cilengitide treatment reduced stromal fibrosis in vivo. Cilengitide monotherapy provided limited survival benefit relative to gemcitabine, whereas the combination of cilengitide plus gemcitabine both attenuated fibrosis and significantly extended survival in KPC mice These results suggest that targeting ITGB5/αvβ5 with agents such as cilengitide may represent a viable stromal-targeted approach to enhance chemotherapy efficacy in PDAC. Further preclinical and mechanistic studies are warranted to define optimal dosing, delivery strategies, and safety.

In this study, we adopted tumor shear stiffness measured by 3D-MRE as a primary indicator of PDAC biomechanical characteristics. Previous biomechanical assessments of PDAC have typically relied on EUS, Shore durometer, or AFM. By contrast, 3D-MRE is a non-invasive clinically translatable modality for biomechanical assessment that has been infrequently applied to PDAC. To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the relationship between shear stiffness measured by 3D-MRE and PDAC biomechanical properties regulated by ITGB5. Our results demonstrate that 3D-MRE can accurately detect biomechanical differences in PDAC, suggesting its broader utility beyond established applicationsin liver [35] and brain diseases [36, 37] to include PDAC biomechanical evaluation.

This study inevitably faces several challenges and limitations. First, our clinical cohort included only resectable PDAC patients who underwent primary surgery, which may limit generalizability to advanced or unresectable disease because tumor biomechanics and CAF subpopulation dynamics can differ by stage. Second, the clinical cohort comprised only 80 PDAC patients from a single center. Future multicenter prospective studies with larger patient cohorts are needed, as are phase I/II clinical trials of ITGB5 inhibitors to further elucidate links between ITGB5 signaling and ECM stiffness in PDAC.

Future research should stratify CAFs by subtype—e.g., inflammatory CAFs (infCAFs), developmental CAFs (dCAFs), and reactive CAFs (rCAFs)—to identify which fibroblast subsets are directly modulated by ITGB5. Moreover, in addition to CAF-derived collagen, other ECM components such as hyaluronic acid also contribute to PDAC biomechanics. Our study focused on ITGB5 effects on collagen deposition and did not evaluate its role in hyaluronic acid synthesis or binding [38]. The PDAC microenvironment is highly heterogeneous, and immune cells and other stromal components also participate in regulating tumor stiffness [39]. Integrated application of emerging technologies—single-cell RNA sequencing [40] and spatial transcriptomics [41], as well as organoid systems [42], patient-derived xenografts (PDX) [43], and integrated multi-omics [43] approaches—can provide spatially resolved single-cell insight, enable mechanistic validation in vitro and in vivo, and facilitate identification of key cell subsets, intercellular signaling pathways, and translational targets. Here, we examined only the effects of ITGB5-knockdown in CAFs on PDAC cells but did not explore how ITGB5 influences interactions between CAFs and other cell types such as immune or endocrine cells; Futher experiments are required to delineate how ITGB5 shapes intercellular communicationin the PDAC microenvironment.

Finally, our current MRE setup does not support in vivo biomechanical measurements in KPC mice. Future work will explore higher-field MRI systems (e.g.,7T) and develop corresponding high-frequency mechanical wave drivers (e.g., ~ 1000 Hz) to enable in vivo detection of small tumors in KPC models.

In summary, this study highlights a central role for ITGB5 in the PDAC stroma and demonstrates, for the first time, that targeting ITGB5 in CAFs can modulate the PDAC matrix. In the KPC model, inhibition mitigated gemcitabine-exacerbated stromal fibrosis, decreased matrix stiffness, and improved survival. Methodologically, we propose 3D-MRE as a noninvasive tool for diagnosis and treatment monitoring to better characterize tumor mechanics and guide individualized stromal-targeted strategies. Collectively, these findings support further preclinical and translational evaluation of ITGB5-directed stromal therapies in PDAC.
