High Densities of Large Herbivores Rapidly Disrupt Ecosystem Integrity

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman‐induced high densities of large wild herbivores may pose a threat to natural ecosystems, modifying biodiversity and ecosystem functioning. Although the negative impacts on individual taxa and processes are well documented, a comprehensive understanding of how ecosystem integrity responds to high herbivore densities remains limited. This study addresses this gap through an extensive herbivore‐density manipulation experiment conducted in two Mediterranean woodlands. We established two scenarios of high red deer ( Cervus elaphus ) density, representative of some protected areas (~30 individuals·km2) and intensively managed hunting estates (~90 individuals·km2). We monitored 38 metrics encompassing a broad spectrum of biodiversity and functional responses to synthesize the short‐term impacts of high herbivore densities. We revealed an overall 13% decline in ecosystem integrity after one year of exposure to high herbivore densities, with particularly widespread impacts on functional responses and detrimental effects on biodiversity components. We detected rapid declines in plant diversity and a positive response of epigeic invertebrate orders. Conversely, ants (analyzed separately), birds, and small mammals remained mostly unaffected. Functional responses were consistently impaired, including declines in plant regeneration and physiological performance, simplified pollination networks, degraded soil structure, and increased disease vectors. These findings show that human‐induced high herbivore densities rapidly create conditions that disrupt ecosystem integrity, reveal early signs of ecological degradation, and underscore the urgent need for population regulation.

Introduction

Large wild herbivores comprise nearly half of the total biomass of terrestrial mammals worldwide (Greenspoon et al. 2023). They are key drivers of ecosystem structure and function, influencing vegetation, nutrient cycling, and habitat heterogeneity (Côté et al. 2004; Pringle et al. 2023). Present‐day large herbivores are typically confined to isolated and often disturbed areas due to habitat loss and fragmentation (Haddad et al. 2015; Ripple et al. 2015). This, together with the expansion of big‐game management and the loss of top predators, has led to very high population densities in some areas, especially among wild ungulates such as deer (Carpio et al. 2021). Thus, across Europe, America, and Oceania, many herbivore populations have reached unusually high local densities (Forsyth et al. 2007; Williams et al. 2013; Carpio et al. 2021).

According to Caughley (1981), overabundance occurs when a species' population reaches densities that negatively affect other species, including humans, or ecosystem functioning. This concept aligns directly with ecosystem integrity, commonly defined as the degree to which an ecosystem retains its species composition, structure, and processes (Andreasen et al. 2001; but see Svenning 2026). Thus, under this framework, herbivore densities become “overabundant” only when they cause negative impacts on ecosystem integrity. Negative biodiversity responses to such high densities have been extensively documented (Côté et al. 2004; Carpio et al. 2021), including plants (Perea et al. 2014; Borowski et al. 2021; Cuerdo et al. 2025), vertebrates (Allombert et al. 2005; Crystal‐Ornelas et al. 2021), invertebrates (Allombert et al. 2005), and soil microorganisms (Baraza et al. 2025). A growing body of evidence also documents negative functional responses (Côté et al. 2004; Forbes et al. 2019), including effects on plant regeneration (Perea et al. 2014; Staver and Bond 2014), pollination (Guy et al. 2021; Hernández‐Castellano et al. 2025), food web dynamics (Bressette et al. 2012), plant physiology (Capó et al. 2024; Rello et al. 2025), nutrient cycling (Forbes et al. 2019), and soil structure (Ibañez‐Alvarez et al. 2022; Baraza et al. 2025). Exceptionally high herbivore densities can also exacerbate human‐health ecosystem disservices, for instance by increasing the risk of vector‐borne diseases (Gortázar et al. 2006; Carpio et al. 2021). The threats to biodiversity and ecosystem functions posed by wild herbivore overabundance have spurred ecological research, yet comprehensive and integrative studies are still lacking.

The temporal scale is fundamental to understanding the ecosystem impacts of herbivores (Bender et al. 1984). A rapid increase in wild herbivore populations provides a clear example of a pulse disturbance. Such pulse disturbances are often linked to human activities that favor particular species of economic or recreational interest. Big‐game ungulates are frequently subject to management actions (e.g., translocations and supplementary feeding), which can trigger rapid local increases that reach high densities (Carpio et al. 2021; Carvalho et al. 2025). Adopting a short‐term perspective is therefore critical for two main reasons. Firstly, it captures the immediate ecological consequences of sudden population surges and allows the direct effects of high herbivore densities on both biodiversity and functional responses to be disentangled. These relationships become difficult to isolate in long‐term studies because indirect and successional effects accumulate over time. Secondly, the accelerating pace of global change is making pulse disturbances increasingly frequent and more challenging to monitor (Senf et al. 2025). Thus, understanding the vulnerability of ecosystem integrity to local increases in herbivore density requires an integrative framework. Such a framework should simultaneously consider biodiversity and functional responses under comparable disturbance impacts, both before and during the disturbance. This integrated methodological approach, to our knowledge, has not yet been implemented. Analyses conducted under such standardized conditions would advance our understanding of herbivore pulse disturbances and provide critical understanding that guides management decisions.

Density‐manipulation experiments represent one of the most powerful and challenging approaches for assessing the impacts of large wild herbivores (Bernes et al. 2018; Pringle et al. 2023). Nevertheless, most current evidence derives from exclusion experiments, which present two key limitations. Firstly, they do not control herbivore density or identity. Secondly, exclusion plots are typically far smaller than the spatial scale at which ecosystem processes operate. For instance, the mean plot size identified in a recent meta‐analysis on herbivore effects on ecosystems (Trepel et al. 2024) is only 1 m2, whereas Forbes et al. (2019) adopted a more conservative threshold and considered only plots > 25 m2. Such scales may still fall short of representing spatial heterogeneity and capturing landscape‐scale effects. To overcome these limitations, Pringle et al. (2023) advocate for experimental designs involving large plots (hectares), combined with the manipulation of specific herbivore species at known densities. When integrated within a BACI (Before‐After‐Control‐Impact) experimental framework, which involves measuring ecological responses before and after the disturbance, in both control and manipulated plots, such designs enable isolation of the pure effects of herbivore density from confounding environmental factors. This combination thus provides a robust framework for assessing short‐term biodiversity and functional responses to high‐density scenarios involving large wild herbivores.

We present the first hectare‐scale experiment manipulating large herbivore density to assess ecosystem vulnerability. This study was conducted in two contrasting Mediterranean woodlands in the Iberian Peninsula, a region where, despite the general challenge of establishing natural baselines for large herbivore densities (Fløjgaard et al. 2022), historical evidence indicates that large herbivore densities over the last millennia were several orders of magnitude lower than present‐day levels (Morales‐Molino et al. 2019). We assessed the effect of red deer ( Cervus elaphus ) at two densities (high and hyper‐high) that potentially generate overabundance conditions. We analyzed the independent short‐term sensitivity of multiple biodiversity and functional responses using effect size contrasts. Next, we integrated them via a multilevel meta‐analytic approach to provide a holistic view of how high herbivore densities affect ecosystem integrity. Finally, we discuss the ecological mechanisms driving these impacts and outline practical management strategies to prevent or reduce overabundance conditions. Thus, this study provides new insights into the exceedance of ecological thresholds under extreme herbivore pressure.

Material and Methods

Study Sites

This study was conducted in two Mediterranean woodlands in the Iberian Peninsula: Quintos de Mora (QM) and Muela de Cortes (MC). These sites represent natural Mediterranean ecosystems where the current vegetation structure and landscape mosaic are shaped by ongoing natural dynamics (e.g., wildfires, herbivory, and plant competition). QM is located in Central Spain and characterized by a continental Mediterranean climate with mean annual rainfall of ~500–600 mm and mean annual temperature of ~14°C. Vegetation is a mosaic of small oak woodlands interspersed with a sclerophyllous shrub layer. MC is located in Eastern Spain, with a climate similar to QM. However, lithology and soils differ (calcareous in MC vs. siliceous in QM), therefore supporting different plant composition and vegetation structure (scrublands with pines and oaks), although some species are common to both areas. See Appendix S1 for a detailed description of the study sites.

Experimental Setting

A hectare‐scale red deer density manipulation experiment was established at both study sites in 2021. Three contrasting deer population scenarios were recreated during one year: high density, hyper‐high density, and control (no deer). These densities were selected because they represent deer densities found in many natural Mediterranean areas of the Iberian Peninsula (values commonly exceed 30 ind·km2; Perea et al. 2014) and the extreme densities often observed in intensively managed game estates in central and southern Iberia (frequently surpassing 90 ind·km2; Azorit et al. 2012). For the exact number of female deer maintained in each enclosure, as well as their equivalences to standard population densities (assuming a 1:1 sex ratio and all age classes), see Table S2.1 in Appendix S2. Hectare‐scale enclosures (2‐m‐high wire fences; mean area = 7.4 ha, range = 2.6–10.8 ha) were established in areas with similar habitat composition among enclosures. It is important to note that the experimental design was not entirely identical in both sites. In QM, three enclosures were established: control, high density, and hyper density. In contrast, in MC, two large enclosures were established, and each was divided into two sub‐enclosures, including a treatment (high or hyper‐high density) and a paired control (Figure S1.1). To facilitate wild red deer management, only subadult (average 21 months old) female individuals were released into the enclosures. Cervid densities before the experiment were low at both study sites: ~8–10 ind·km2 at QM and ~1–2 ind·km2 at MC (natural densities that are considered sustainable; Morales‐Molino et al. 2019).

Replication was limited to two independent study sites due to logistical constraints. These included the large size of the experimental enclosures (mean = 7.4 ha) and the intensive effort required to capture, health‐test, and translocate wild red deer. To address this limitation, we focused on contrasts of mean responses based on a large number of independent sampling units (e.g., quadrats, pitfall traps, transects) distributed within each enclosure. This high‐resolution sampling design allowed us to capture within‐enclosure heterogeneity and to obtain robust estimates of means and variances for effect size estimation.

Sampling Biodiversity and Functional Responses

To assess biodiversity and short‐term functional responses to high herbivore densities, two sampling campaigns were conducted: one shortly before the deer introduction (baseline data), and another one conducted one year after the onset of the experiment. Sampling dates varied slightly among surveys due to logistical constraints or phenological adjustments (Appendix S3). Biodiversity responses were measured across several taxonomic groups, including herbaceous plants, woody plants, ants, epigeic invertebrates, rodents, and birds (Table 1). We quantified species richness, abundance, and diversity using the Hill number with q = 2 (i.e., the inverse Simpson diversity index, which represents the effective number of species while giving greater weight to dominant taxa; Chao et al. 2014). Functional responses included soil bulk density, decomposition and stabilization of organic matter, habitat composition, tick abundance (as a proxy for disease risk), invertebrate trophic efficiency and biomass, and plant‐related variables such as browsing degree, chlorophyll and anthocyanin concentrations, recruitment, fruit set, and pollination network structure (Table 2). A complete list of variables measured (and the metrics within variables), associated sample sizes, and sampling procedures is provided in Tables 1 and 2. See Appendix S3 for a detailed description of sampling procedures and sample sizes, and Appendix S4 for each metric's hypothesized change after a pulse of high herbivore densities.

Data Analysis

Sampling completeness was assessed using incidence‐based rarefaction and extrapolation in the iNEXT R package (Hsieh et al. 2015). Richness estimates were computed via interpolation and extrapolation to evaluate asymptotic behavior, and 95% confidence intervals were derived from 100 bootstrap replicates. Ratio of Means effect size measurements (lnRR; Hedges et al. 1999) were used to quantify biodiversity and functional responses to high herbivore densities. For lnRR estimation, the relation between mean values in deer‐treatment (X¯t) and control enclosures (X¯c) was calculated separately for each metric, in the two sites, both before and after deer introduction. This effect size measurement is deemed appropriate given the heterogeneity of variables, their variances, and differences in sample sizes. Mean values and variances were calculated from the total pool of independent measurements (n) taken within each enclosure (e.g., quadrats, pitfalls, transects, etc.). Next, a delta (ΔlnRR) between response ratios before and after deer introduction was calculated (Equation 1) to produce a standardized measure of change attributable to high herbivore density effects, thereby fully exploiting the robustness of the Before‐After Control‐Impact (BACI) design. (1) ΔlnRR=lnX¯tAfterX¯cAfter−lnX¯tBeforeX¯cBefore Effect sizes (lnRR) were computed by the escalc function in the metafor R package (Viechtbauer 2010). Variance estimates and sample size were incorporated to compute 95% confidence intervals for each lnRR, allowing the determination of whether observed effect sizes were significantly positive, negative, or non‐significant. For intuitive interpretation of ΔlnRR: values around 0 indicate no change, values above indicate an increase, and values below indicate a decrease. Changes are considered statistically significant if the 95% confidence interval does not include 0. Relative (multiplicative) changes are obtained by exponentiating the ΔlnRR values. For example, a ΔlnRR value of +0.23 indicates a relative change of 26% (exp [0.23] = 1.26).

To evaluate the robustness of our analyses to the choice of effect size metric, we calculated effect sizes using both the Ratio of Means (lnRR) and the Standardized Mean Difference. We found a strong correlation between lnRR and SMD effect sizes (lnRR Before vs. SMDBefore Kendall's τ = 0.82, p < 0.01; lnRR After vs. SMDAfter Kendall's τ = 0.78, p < 0.01), indicating that our results are robust and do not vary depending on the effect size metric (Appendix S5).

To assess the effects of high herbivore densities on ecosystem integrity, we used multilevel meta‐analytic models. Rather than relying on effect sizes from the literature, as in traditional meta‐analyses, we used our own previously calculated effect sizes (ΔlnRR) for each metric, treatment, and site. Several levels of non‐independence were considered in all the models: multiple effect sizes derived from (i) the same variable, (ii) the same study site, and (iii) observation‐level random effect. Firstly, a multilevel intercept‐only model was fitted to test the overall effect of high herbivore densities on ecosystem integrity, along with its uncertainty and heterogeneity. Secondly, we fitted our meta‐regression models considering the following moderators: the type of variable (biodiversity or functional responses), the density scenario (high vs. hyper‐high) and the response variable. All meta‐regressions corresponded to uni‐moderator meta‐regressions and were computed to assess whether the effect magnitude varied across different levels. Additionally, we incorporated the standard error of each metric as a moderator to account for potential artifacts related to sampling precision, analogous to the small‐study effects described by Nakagawa et al. (2022). For all meta‐regressions, we estimated R 2 marginal, representing the proportion of heterogeneity explained by the moderators (Nakagawa and Schielzeth 2013). Following the pluralistic approach proposed by Yang et al. (2025), we estimated absolute heterogeneity (σ 2, representing the total variation not attributable to sampling error), its sources (relative heterogeneity I 2), as well as mean‐standardized heterogeneity (CVH 2) and variance–mean‐standardized heterogeneity (M 2). Asymmetry in effect sizes was evaluated using a funnel plot exploration (Figure S6.1). We implemented the analyses using the rma.mv function for the metafor R package, applying the REML method and the t‐test for moderator significance and confidence interval estimation. Some effect sizes were reversed in sign to facilitate the interpretation of the meta‐analysis in terms of the detrimental (negative) versus beneficial (positive) effects on the ecosystem. This adjustment was only necessary for metrics for which an increase is known to represent a negative impact on the ecosystem (e.g., soil bulk density or the degree of browsing at plant community level). We excluded from the analysis the variables with the least taxonomic resolution (the order diversity and richness for epigeic invertebrates) and the least robust effect size estimates (vector encounter risk and fruit set of the palatable plant Cistus populifolius; see Figure S6.2). The data and R code used in this study are in Isla et al. (2026).

Results

Our ecosystem‐level sampling yielded 44,196 independent biodiversity records (individuals detected). Plants accounted for 14.2% of the total (201 herbaceous and 55 woody species), ants contributed 20.8% (49 species), epigeic invertebrates comprised 58.3% (34 orders), birds represented 0.7% (30 species) and small mammals 0.6% (two species). Functional response sampling recorded 2962 regenerating plants (25 species) and 13,783 pollination interactions (530 unique interactions) between 25 flowering species and 228 pollinator species. Detailed species richness data before and after deer introduction across sites and treatments are provided in Table S6.1 in Appendix S6. Sample coverage was high across all taxonomic groups (range: 85–100; mean = 96.58%), as estimated from the observed incidence frequencies (Chao and Jost 2012). Moreover, the rarefaction and extrapolation curves of each group approached an asymptote, confirming that sampling effort was sufficient (Figure S6.3).

Biodiversity Responses to High Herbivore Densities

Plant Responses

Woody and herbaceous plant communities were significantly sensitive to high herbivore densities (Figure 1). The herbaceous community was negatively affected across the three measured dimensions (richness, diversity, and abundance), with abundance being the most affected component. Herbaceous abundance (cover) consistently dropped by about half under hyper‐high‐density conditions (−45% in QM and −43% in MC). In the case of the woody community, statistically significant effects were only detected in MC, where species richness decreased under both high‐ and hyper‐high‐density scenarios (by −32% and −23%, respectively), while diversity decreased by −35% only under the high‐density scenario.

Animal Responses

Epigeic invertebrates showed positive responses to the pulse of high herbivore densities in QM (Figure 1). Order richness increased by ~57% and similar responses were detected for order diversity. The strongest responses were recorded for invertebrate abundance, which increased by ~88% and 100% under hyper‐high and high‐density scenarios, respectively. The positive response was generalized across the main epigeic invertebrate orders, although some (e.g., Collembola) showed particularly strong increases in abundance. Ants, birds, and small mammals show no significant responses.

Ecosystem Functioning Responses to High Herbivore Densities

Soil Structure and Function Responses

Below ground, soil bulk density increased by 26% due to the hyper‐high‐density treatment in MC (Figure 2). Soil organic matter stabilization and decomposition parameters remained unaffected.

Plant‐Related Processes Responses

Above ground, the average value of herbivory supported by the plant community (CWM B ) increased by 118% in hyper‐high‐density treatment enclosures. This result represents a more than twofold increase in the mean browsing score for the entire community (Figure 2). This intense pressure on the plant community induced physiological stress responses (anthocyanins and chlorophyll) in the new leaves of holm oak ( Quercus ilex ). In the short term, anthocyanin concentrations (a herbivory‐defense compound) more than doubled, while chlorophyll concentration decreased, reducing photosynthetic capacity by half.

Woody regeneration was significantly reduced in abundance, richness, and diversity of recruits by −85%, −58% and −52%, respectively, in the high‐density enclosures of MC. Additionally, for the two focal species selected to assess plant fitness, the fruit set (transition from flowers to fruits) was severely reduced in the palatable species (Cistus populifolius), leaving almost no flowers available under both high herbivore density scenarios.

Plant–pollinator networks showed broad short‐term sensitivity to high herbivore densities. Both compositional attributes (richness of flowering plant and unique interactions) and topological metrics (interaction evenness, connectance, and modularity) responded strongly (Figure 2). Selective loss of flowering species (up to a 60% reduction under hyper‐high density) led to a parallel 50% decline in interaction richness, resulting in an erosion of network topological metrics.

Ecosystem Disservices

Regarding vector encounter risk, we detected 68 ticks in MC after one year of deer presence, whereas we found only one in the control enclosures. These highly contrasting results yield very large effect size estimates (asterisks in Figure 2) and clearly point to an increase in vector encounter risk due to high herbivore densities. We did not find any ticks in QM.

Neither habitat structure nor invertebrate trophic structure showed significant responses. However, the proportion of bare ground increased under high densities at MC (Figure 2). Estimated invertebrate biomass also increased, consistent across both sites and both high‐density treatments (Figure 2).

Sensitivity of Biodiversity and Functional Responses to High Herbivore Densities

The intercept‐only multilevel meta‐analysis revealed that high herbivore densities caused a statistically significant 13% decrease in overall ecosystem integrity (Figure 3a; ΔlnRR = −0.14, 95% CI: [−24.72, −0.39]; p = 0.044). The heterogeneity among effect sizes was statistically significant (Q = 209.6, p < 0.01; Figure 3a). Most variability was found among groups (I 2 = 38.05%; Figure 4), followed by site differences (I 2 = 10.31%; Figure S6.4).

Our multilevel meta‐regressions provided no evidence that the overall impact of high herbivore densities on ecosystem integrity differed between biodiversity and functional response metrics (p = 0.20; Figure 3b), and both types of metrics exhibited a general negative response. However, this moderator explains an important percentage of heterogeneity (R 2 marginal = 7.1%), and this meta‐analytic mean statistically decreased only for functional responses (ΔlnRR Functional metrics = −0.17; Figure 3b), but not for diversity metrics (ΔlnRR Biodiversity metrics = −0.07; Figure 3b). No significant differences were detected between the two density scenarios (p = 0.84; Figure 3c). This indicates that both high‐ and hyper‐high‐density conditions exerted similar detrimental effects on ecosystem integrity in the short term (despite substantial variability in the sensitivity of individual metrics). Finally, we found no evidence of sampling precision in the overall effect of large wild herbivores (p = 0.35; Figure 3d).

Discussion

The impacts of high densities of large herbivores are well studied (Côté et al. 2004; Barbero‐Palacios et al. 2024; Carvalho et al. 2025), but short‐term biodiversity and functional responses remain unclear and are poorly integrated. Our study fills this gap in knowledge by presenting the largest hectare‐scale wild herbivore density manipulation experiment conducted to date. This experiment allowed us to assess the short‐term impacts of high densities of large herbivores on ecosystem integrity. Ten of the 16 variables were significantly impacted in at least one site or treatment, and meta‐analysis showed negative effects on ecosystem integrity (a decline of 13%) that did not differ in magnitude between high and hyper‐high densities. Together, these findings demonstrate that herbivore high density rapidly disrupts not only natural communities, but also the abiotic components and ecological processes that support ecosystem integrity. Given the widespread negative impacts documented here, the high deer densities tested clearly constitute overabundance conditions. Below, we discuss the potential underlying mechanisms, broader implications, and the relevance of these results to evidence‐based management strategies addressing large herbivore overabundance and ecosystem integrity maintenance.

Biodiversity Responses

Our results clearly indicate that scenarios of herbivore overabundance generate immediate and widespread impacts on biodiversity across multiple taxa. This finding is particularly novel, as most studies have traditionally assessed the effects of herbivore overabundance on biodiversity over long temporal scales rather than in the short‐term (Perea et al. 2014; Pringle et al. 2023; Ramirez et al. 2021). In vegetation, both the herbaceous and woody layers showed reductions in cover, richness and diversity. While large herbivores can sometimes enhance herbaceous cover and diversity (Côté et al. 2004; Trepel et al. 2025), our results show that under overabundance scenarios, plant communities shift toward an overgrazing situation where species decline according to their palatability (Mysterud 2006). This pattern is consistent with previous observations in similar studies (Cuerdo et al. 2025; Perea et al. 2014) and aligns with the fact that the red deer are mixed‐feeders that consume both woody and herbaceous plants. Red deer can browse very heavily when herbaceous plants are missing or dry and, particularly during the long Mediterranean summer drought (3–4 months) when only browse is available (Perea et al. 2014; Perea and Gil 2014). In contrast to vegetation, epigeic invertebrates showed a positive response generalized across the main orders, resulting in positive responses across multiple guilds (detritivores, predators, coprophages, etc.). These results contrast with those found in other ecosystems such as grasslands (Wang et al. 2022) and likely mask order‐specific responses (Zhu et al. 2022). This pattern may be explained by habitat amelioration and resource (food) enhancement by the transfer of nutrients and energy from plants to soil mediated by deer activity. Other taxonomic groups, such as ants, birds, and small mammals, showed no significant responses. Previous studies, however, suggest that these taxa can become vulnerable to herbivore high densities (Chollet and Martin 2013; Teichman et al. 2013; Crystal‐Ornelas et al. 2021; Afonso et al. 2024). This may reflect greater resilience, weaker and more indirect interactions with herbivores, or the need for broader spatial scales. Taken together, these results highlight that herbivore overabundance exerts rapid impacts on key taxa, raising concerns about potential cascading effects on other groups and on ecosystem functioning (Bressette et al. 2012; Ramirez et al. 2021), which we address in the following section.

Ecosystem Functioning Responses

Alongside biodiversity responses, the impact of large wild herbivore overabundance on process‐related variables was more prevalent than on biodiversity responses. Although previous research has shown that these processes could be altered by large herbivores, such evidence principally emerges from small‐scale enclosure experiments, under uncontrolled herbivore densities, and over medium‐ to long‐term timescales (Côté et al. 2004; Forbes et al. 2019; Pringle et al. 2023; Trepel et al. 2024). As expected, we found that herbaceous and woody vegetation were particularly sensitive to herbivory, as browsers can constrain plant growth and persistence (Bradshaw and Waller 2016). They can further limit regeneration by damaging flowers, fruits, and seedlings (Perea and Gil 2014; Staver and Bond 2014). Here, the community‐level browsing degree (CWM B ) increased sharply under hyper‐dense conditions, revealing a clear signal of overbrowsing that affected the entire plant community and prevented regeneration in many species. As expected, these impacts were not uniformly distributed among species as they were strongly modulated by herbivore dietary preferences. For instance, the reproductive success of highly palatable Cistus populifolius was severely compromised due to intense florivory and fruit predation, whereas the avoidance of the unpalatable Cistus ladanifer enabled this species to fruit successfully, thereby favoring population recruitment (Malo and Suárez 1996). However, whether long‐term high densities of ungulates undermine these potential benefits depends strongly on the abundance of alternative palatable food and the probability that those alternative taxa go locally extinct (Perea et al. 2014). Importantly, low‐palatable plants (mainly C. ladanifer ) persist in pollination networks, helping to sustain structural integrity and prevent further fragmentation. Nevertheless, these pollination networks were substantially altered under herbivore overabundance, as has been reported in other ecosystems such as savannas (Guy et al. 2021). This primarily occurred through the extirpation of flowering species (Beckett et al. 2022), leading to a loss of interactions and network architectural shifts towards less connected and more vulnerable topologies. Over longer timescales, such shifts are likely to promote further destabilization, compromising pollination services to plants and the maintenance of pollinator communities. Additionally, plants exhibited short‐term, stress‐induced physiological and spectral responses, including reduced chlorophyll and increased anthocyanin concentrations, reflecting an early redistribution of resources to activate defensive pathways (Capó et al. 2024; Rello et al. 2025). Along with other rapid negative responses, this reallocation of resources likely contributes to decreased plant fitness, particularly in species with limited historic exposure to herbivory and thus reduced tolerance capacity (Capó et al. 2021; Huot et al. 2014). Overall, these findings underscore that overabundance pressures can trigger rapid physiological and community‐level responses in vegetation‐related processes, with potential cascading consequences across trophic levels, above and below ground.

Beyond vegetation‐mediated effects, soil physical properties responded quickly to herbivore overabundance, as evidenced by a clear increase in bulk density under hyper‐high density conditions. Although organic matter decomposition is often expected to decline when soils become compacted (Vogel et al. 2018), we did not detect changes in standardized organic matter decomposition or stabilization. This result is consistent with studies reporting limited short‐term effects of herbivores on organic matter decomposition (Ibañez‐Alvarez et al. 2022). In contrast to ecosystems where herbivores consistently reduce decomposition by altering microbial nutrient demand or creating less favorable microclimates (Ramirez et al. 2021), Mediterranean soils are characterized by strong small‐scale spatial heterogeneity that can mask short‐term functional responses to trampling (Ibañez‐Alvarez et al. 2022). Therefore, short‐term increases in bulk density may not translate into detectable shifts in decomposition or stabilization of organic matter, even though longer‐term reductions in grazing pressure have been associated with lower decomposition rates in similar systems (Oggioni et al. 2020). Finally, broader ecosystem responses were also strong; for example, the probability of tick encounters, and thus the risk of vector‐borne disease, increased markedly under herbivore overabundance, highlighting an important ecosystem disservice. This finding highlights how herbivore overabundance can rapidly generate socio‐ecological impacts that extend beyond biodiversity loss and biophysical degradation, influencing human well‐being and disease risk. Altogether, these results illustrate the multidimensional nature of ecosystem functional responses under extreme herbivore pressure, in which biotic, abiotic, and even human‐health‐related processes are negatively impacted.

Limitations and Future Perspectives

Through our integrative approach, robust evidence that herbivore overabundance disrupting key processes that underpin ecosystem functioning and biodiversity. Our results also reveal strong context‐dependent effects of herbivores on ecosystems in line with previous studies (Forbes et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2022). While some of the most sensitive metrics (e.g., herbaceous vegetation) show consistent responses across both study sites and treatments, others (e.g., epigeic invertebrates) exhibit more variable responses, but never opposite trends. Following the experimental framework proposed by Pringle et al. (2023), our study prioritized ecological realism by using large hectare‐scale enclosures, which inevitably entails a trade‐off in spatial replication. Consequently, we acknowledge that further large‐scale experiments across diverse climatic and geographical contexts are needed to support generalizations about the impacts of high herbivore densities on ecosystems. Recent research assessing the ecosystem effects of large herbivores indicates that their influence remains consistent across spatial scales (Trepel et al. 2025), thereby supporting the extrapolation of our findings to broader scales. Nevertheless, our findings should be interpreted in light of the restricted ungulate mobility imposed by fencing (Boone and Hobbs 2004; Carpio et al. 2015). Such spatial confinement, though typical of intensively managed areas, can modify natural behavior and potentially amplify the ecological impacts of ungulates (Boone and Hobbs 2004). Future work could address these limitations and generate more specific transferable insights by establishing intermediate ungulate densities. These future approaches would improve our capacity to identify ecological thresholds and carrying capacities, parameters that have thus far been inferred only indirectly (Morellet et al. 2007; Perea et al. 2015).

Implications for Conservation and Management

For the first time, our study simultaneously captures ecosystem responses by combining direct manipulation experiments, multiple sampling techniques, and an integrative analytical framework. By applying multilevel meta‐analysis, our work provides an approach to assessing ecosystem integrity sensitivity that can be extended to other complex experimental designs and ecologically relevant contexts. For instance, similar approaches could be applied in heterogeneous datasets to evaluate ecosystem recovery following the cessation of herbivore disturbance, or to assess the success of restoration strategies (such as those following wildfire). Our results clearly show that the large herbivore densities currently observed in many natural areas and hunting estates are ecologically unsustainable. The short‐term ecosystem responses documented here further indicate that such elevated densities are incompatible with ecosystem stability, raising concerns about potentially irreversible consequences. The degradation observed affects multiple components and functions: severe impacts on plant communities and their processes (regeneration, pollination, photosynthesis), along with soil structure and disease vectors, reveal a critical impact of a high‐density large herbivore pulse on key elements of ecosystem integrity. Taken together, these findings underscore an urgent need for management interventions to reduce overabundant large herbivore populations below key sustainability thresholds. We therefore recommend measures such as limiting supplementary feeding, strengthening population control programs, avoiding excessive fencing and unnecessary translocations, and facilitating the recovery of natural predators. Such efforts should be guided by the most sensitive ecological metrics identified here, which provide an early and reliable indication of when population pressures begin to compromise ecosystem integrity.
