Cost‐effectiveness of percutaneous nephrostomy vs JJ stent in obstructive urolithiasis: economic evaluation alongside the STent Or NEphrostomy (STONE) randomised controlled trial
Authors: Hugo W Schuil, Nora Hendriks, Mohamed El Alili, Judith E Bosmans, Ruben G Duijnhoven, Bart CH Oskam, Tom JH Arends, Diederick Duijvesz, Alexander D Horsch, Bart van der Heij, Irene Tjiam, Hans van Overhage, Armand BGN Lamers, Otto M van Delden, Wout Scheepens, Jamie MA Drossaerts, Frank CH d'Ancona, Steven Boering, Janneke IM van Uhm, Rutger W van der Meer, Herman JH van Roijen, Martin de Kiefte, Ad JM Hendrix, Saskia Weltings, Rob CM Pelger, Harrie P Beerlage, Guido M Kamphuis, Barbara MA Schout
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate whether percutaneous nephrostomy (PCN) is cost‐effective compared to JJ ureteric stent in the acute treatment of obstructive urolithiasis.Patients and MethodsA randomised controlled non‐inferiority trial with 204 patients with obstructive urolithiasis requiring decompression. Participants were randomly assigned to either PCN or JJ stent, with follow‐up until the stone was surgically removed or passed spontaneously. Effectiveness outcomes included time to recovery and quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs). Costs were assessed from a societal perspective, incorporating healthcare, patient‐related, and lost productivity costs.ResultsWeighted mean procedure costs were €685 for PCN and €792 for JJ. Mean total societal costs were €8468 for PCN and €7122 for JJ. Largest cost differences were in productivity loss and home care. Time to recovery and QALYs were similar between groups. The probability that JJ was cost‐effective compared to PCN was 0.74 for quicker recovery time and 0.74 for QALYs at a willingness‐to‐pay threshold of €0. Some cost and quality‐of‐life data were self‐reported, and the analysis reflects the Dutch healthcare system, which may limit generalisability.ConclusionThe JJ ureteric stent was found to be the likely cost‐effective option compared to PCN, despite higher procedural costs. Procedural costs can be lowered by performing procedures outside of an operating theatre. Most societal costs were made during the waiting period for definitive stone removal; future research should investigate whether total cost can be lowered by reducing waiting times.Keywords: Cost‐effectiveness, JJ ureteric stent, percutaneous nephrostomy, randomised controlled trial, urolithiasis

Introduction

Urolithiasis is a common urological disease, and its incidence is growing globally [1]. Affected patients often need medical attention for analgesia, acute renal decompression, stone removal and metaphylaxis. Recurrences are frequent during life, which further increases the burden [2]. As the incidence grows, so does the economic impact of diagnostics, treatment and lost productivity. In the United States alone, costs for stone disease are projected to exceed $4 billion American dollars (~€3.7 billion euros) by 2030 [3]. Since most patients are of working age, temporary work disability and other societal costs form an important part of total expenditure [4]. There is a need for cost‐effective management strategies to mitigate its future economic impact.

Acute renal decompression in obstructive urolithiasis is indicated in case of infection, decline in renal function or pain refractory to analgesics. Decompression can be achieved by percutaneous nephrostomy (PCN) or a ureteric stent (JJ). Both relieve obstruction effectively, but they differ in quality of life, urinary symptoms, costs and implications for subsequent treatment [5, 6]. High quality evidence on cost effectiveness of these two drainage methods is lacking. We therefore conducted the STent Or NEphrostomy (STONE) trial, a multicentre randomised controlled trial (RCT) in which patients with obstructive urolithiasis were allocated to PCN or JJ [7]. The present economic evaluation, performed alongside the trial, assesses whether PCN is cost effective compared to JJ from a societal perspective, including healthcare, patient and productivity costs.

Patients and Methods

Trial Design

This non‐inferiority RCT was performed in 11 Dutch hospitals between June 2020 and November 2023, registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04594161). Adults aged ≥18 years with obstructive urolithiasis on ultrasonography or CT‐scan in whom both PCN and JJ were considered feasible and safe by the treating physician were eligible. Patients who could not complete Dutch questionnaires or who were pregnant were excluded. Randomisation was 1:1, web based, stratified by centre to account for differences in anaesthesia and setting, operating theatre, or radiology suite. Follow‐up continued until definitive stone removal or spontaneous passage, with a maximum of 3 months. The protocol was approved by a Medical Ethics Committee (METC‐LDD decision NL70822.058.19, 22‐01‐2022) and the trial was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients gave written informed consent. Consistent with standard practice for trial based economic evaluations, the non‐inferiority design of the STONE study applied to the primary clinical outcome of time to ercovery, whereas the economic evaluation focused on estimating incremental costs and effects and their associated uncertainty.

Effectiveness

Outcomes were time to recovery and quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs). Recovery was defined according to the indication for drainage and results for clinical recovery have been published previously [7]. For infection, recovery required a decrease in white blood cell count on two consecutive measurements, a value <15 × 109/L and a body temperature between 36 °C and 38.5 °C. For pain, the numeric rating scale had to improve and be below 3 on a 1–10 scale. For impaired renal function, creatinine had to improve on two consecutive measurements. Health‐related quality of life was measured using the EuroQoL five Dimensions five Levels (EQ‐5D‐5L) [8, 9]. Utilities were calculated using the Dutch tariff which ranges from −0.446 to 1, where 1 indicates full health [10]. Because maximum follow up was 3 months, the maximum QALYs were 0.25.

Costs

Costs were assessed from a societal perspective and included healthcare costs (comprised of costs in primary care, secondary care visits, medication, home care, procedure, and repeat procedures), patient and family costs (travel costs) and lost productivity costs (absenteeism, presenteeism, and unpaid work).

Use of care and productivity losses was measured with modified versions of the Institute for Medical Technology Assessment (iMTA) Medical Consumption Questionnaire (iMCQ) and iMTA Productivity Cost Questionnaire (iPCQ) [11]. All costs were expressed in euros (€) for the Year 2022.

Healthcare costs were valued using standard costs from the Dutch costing guideline [12]. Medication costs were valued using national medication prices (www.medicijnkosten.nl). The costs of the PCN and JJ procedures were estimated using a bottom‐up micro‐costing approach. Procedure costs for PCN and JJ were estimated with a bottom‐up micro‐costing approach, including staff time, materials, and anaesthesia. Costs/min for staff were based on Dutch salary tables, which is in line with the costing guideline [12]. Costs related to anaesthesia were based on Dutch tariffs negotiated by Dutch health insurers. Procedures could be performed in a radiology suite or an operating theatre, the cost/min in a radiology suite was assumed to be half of that in an operating theatre, based on Dutch hospital and private operating room rental rates. This assumption was subsequently tested in scenario analyses assuming all procedures were performed in a single setting. If the allocated treatment failed and a second intervention was needed, both procedures were included in the total costs.

Travel costs were calculated using the average hospital distance from the costing guideline multiplied by €0.26/km [12]. Productivity losses due to absenteeism were valued according to the human capital approach using sex‐specific wage rates. Although the Dutch guideline recommends the friction cost method, follow‐up was shorter than the friction period of 108 days, so both approaches give the same outcome [12]. Presenteeism was calculated by multiplying the self‐reported efficiency score with the hours worked and mean wage. Unpaid work, such as household activities, was valued using the Dutch shadow price for domestic work [12].

Statistical Analysis

Both a cost‐effectiveness analysis and a cost‐utility analysis were performed according to the intention‐to‐treat principle. Missing data on costs and effects were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), assuming data were missing at random [13, 14]. The imputation model included all variables included in the analysis models and predictor variables that either differed at baseline, were related to missing data, or were associated with the outcome. Predictive mean matching was used for cost variables to account for skewed distributions [15]. In all, 10 imputed datasets were created, which resulted in <5% loss of efficiency, and results were pooled using Rubin's rules [15, 16].

Multilevel models were used to estimate incremental costs and effects between the JJ group and PCN group; clustering in the data was corrected for by allowing the intercepts to vary between clusters (random intercepts model). For all outcomes, a two‐level structure was used where hospitals and patients represented the first and second level, respectively [17, 18]. Incremental cost‐effectiveness ratios (ICERs) were calculated by dividing the incremental costs by incremental effects. Bias‐corrected bootstrapping was used to estimate statistical uncertainty (5000 replications). Statistical uncertainty surrounding ICERs was illustrated by plotting the bootstrapped cost‐effect pairs on a cost‐effectiveness plane (CE‐plane). Cost‐effectiveness acceptability curves (CEACs) were estimated, which show the probability that the intervention is cost‐effective compared to usual care for a range of different ceiling ratios (willingness‐to‐pay threshold for 1 point effect extra) [19]. The CEACs were estimated using the normal‐based approach for incremental net‐monetary benefits [20]. In the Netherlands, the reference values for the willingness‐to‐pay threshold range between €20 000 and €80 000 per QALY gained depending on the burden of disease [21]. Based on Kilonzo et al. [22], we estimate the disease burden of obstructive urolithiasis at 0.27, meaning that the relevant willingness‐to‐pay threshold is €20 000 per QALY gained. For outcome measure time to recovery, no formal willingness‐to‐pay threshold has been determined.

Four sensitivity analyses (SA) were performed to test robustness, a healthcare perspective analysis (SA1), a per protocol analysis including only patients who received the allocated treatment (SA2), and two scenario analyses assuming all procedures were performed in a radiology suite (SA3) or in an operating theatre (SA4).

All analyses were performed in Stata 16 (Stata Corp. LP, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

In total, 204 patients were enrolled and randomised to PCN (n = 101) or JJ (n = 103). Baseline characteristics were comparable and have been reported previously [7]. The mean age was 60 years, 38% were female, median stone size was 8 mm, and 61% of stones were located in the proximal ureter. Indications for drainage were infection in 35%, pain in 73% and impaired renal function in 62%. The primary clinical outcome of the trial (time until recovery) was completely observed, while for the iMCQ and the iPCQ surveys the rate of complete follow‐up was 60%, and for the EQ‐5D‐5L 59%.

Figure 1 illustrates treatment allocation and interventions received. Six patients in the PCN group and two in the JJ group did not undergo drainage because symptoms had resolved. Technical failure occurred in 12 PCN procedures (12%), 11 of these were converted to JJ and one patient passed the stone spontaneously. Two JJ procedures (2%) failed and were converted to PCN. At discharge, 111 patients had a JJ and 85 had a PCN in situ. JJ was mainly placed in the operating theatre under general anaesthesia (80%), whereas PCN was more often placed in the radiology suite under local anaesthesia (61%). The mean procedure time for PCN placement was 23.8 min in the radiology suite and 14.7 min in the operating theatre. For JJ stent placement, the mean times were 18.0 min and 16.6 min, respectively.

During follow‐up, a repeat procedure was required for eight patients randomised to PCN and six patients in the JJ group due to dysfunction of the primary drainage method.

Costs

Table 1 presents an overview of the average costs per patient stratified by randomisation group. Weighted mean procedure costs per patient were €685 for PCN and €792 for JJ. In radiology suites, mean procedure costs were €483 for PCN and €388 for JJ, in operating theatres €824 and €880, respectively (Table S1). The mean total societal costs were €8468 for PCN and €7122 for JJ (difference of –€1347, 95% CI −4125 to 1273). The largest cost differences were in productivity loss and home care, both lower in the JJ group. Lost productivity costs were €6372 for PCN and €5264 for JJ, (difference –€1108, 95% CI −3765 to 1315), and home care costs were €474 lower for JJ (95% CI −727 to −317). The higher costs of productivity loss in the PCN group were mainly driven by increased absenteeism and presenteeism, which may reflect greater functional limitations associated with the external nephrostomy catheter during follow up.

Effectiveness and Cost‐Effectiveness

Time to recovery between the PCN and JJ groups did not differ significantly (difference of −0.29 days, 95% CI −0.83 to 0.25 days; Table 2). Most bootstrapped cost‐effect pairs are situated in the south‐east quadrant of the CE‐plane (Fig. 2a) showing that JJ treatment is dominant over PCN treatment, with shorter time to recovery and lower costs in the JJ group as compared to the PCN group. The CEAC (Fig. 2b) shows that the probability that the JJ is cost‐effective in comparison with PCN is 0.74, 0.78 and 0.89 at willingness‐to‐pay values of €0, €1000 and €10 000 per day of quicker time to recovery, respectively.

Sensitivity Analyses

Performing the cost‐effectiveness analysis from the healthcare perspective (SA1) gives relatively similar results (Table 2). However, because of the small difference in QALYs the impact of the change in the cost difference from −€1347 to −€241 on the ICER was relatively large (€49 435 per QALY instead of €275 716 per QALY). Performing the analysis according to a per protocol principle (SA2), assuming that the procedures take place within an radiology suite only (SA3) or within an operating theatre only (SA4) resulted in results similar to these of the main analysis.

Discussion

In this RCT comparing PCN and JJ for decompression of obstructive urolithiasis, clinical outcomes and QALYs were similar, but JJ was associated with lower total societal costs. Procedural costs were higher for JJ because of the procedural setting and use of general anaesthesia; however, this was outweighed by lower home care use and lower productivity loss. At a willingness‐to‐pay of €0, JJ had a 74% probability of being the cost effective option.

These findings add high level evidence to an area where earlier work was limited. A notable RCT by Pearle et al. [23] examined 42 patients with obstructive urolithiasis and concurrent infection. Consistent with our findings, they reported lower technical success for PCN compared to internal stents (95% vs 100%). Unlike our study, all JJ procedures were conducted under general anaesthesia in the operating theatre, whereas PCN was performed in angiography suites under local anaesthesia. Their cost analysis, using a limited set of factors (excluding pre‐ and post‐procedural costs), revealed procedural costs of €2686 for PCN and €5669 for JJ (corrected for purchasing power parity [PPP] and converted to euros), which is considerably higher than our estimates (€685 and €792), most likely because of local tariffs and because our study included more procedures outside the operating theatre.

Sammon et al. [24] analysed a large administrative dataset of >390 000 admissions for obstructive urolithiasis with concurrent infection and found longer length of stay and higher costs in PCN patients, although this was probably influenced by selection of sicker patients for PCN. Average cost for PCN was €58 824 per patient, compared to €50 310 for JJ (corrected for PPP and converted to euros), mainly due to higher re‐intervention rates and prolonged hospital stays. They included patients with infections, who typically have longer recovery times and higher complication rates, impacting costs.

A small retrospective study in pregnant women with obstructive urolithiasis by Song et al. [25] likewise found higher procedural costs for PCN than for JJ, with mean costs of €1911 versus €1273 (corrected for PPP and converted to euros), mainly because the PCN catheters were imported in the Chinese setting while the JJ stents were not.

Considering the scarcity of healthcare resources, not only decision makers but also clinicians should take the cost‐effectiveness of provided treatments into account when making treatment decisions. A European survey among urologists and radiologists demonstrated substantial variation in clinical preference for PCN vs JJ stent placement [26]. Several scoring systems and decision tools have been developed to support structured decision making in (emergency) endourology [27]. Evidence from the STONE study and this accompanying economic evaluation provides additional guidance by clinical outcomes and cost effectiveness, thereby supporting more informed treatment selection [7]. Our study was the first to report on a rigorously conducted economic evaluation alongside a RCT with a large study population from a societal perspective, meaning that we also included lost productivity costs. It was conducted in multiple hospitals with different workflows, which decreases homogeneity of practice within randomisation groups, but increases generalisability. It used a societal perspective, which is appropriate for a condition that affects working‐age adults. Missing data on costs and QALYs were handled with multiple imputation according to current best practice, which reduces bias [28]. Some cost data were self‐reported, which may introduce recall bias, although recall periods were short. We recorded procedure time but not total room occupation time, which probably underestimated theatre costs, especially for the JJ group. Precise published estimates for room and overhead costs of operating theatres vs radiology suites are limited, and the assumed difference in unit costs was therefore based on estimated hospital tariff information, which may affect absolute procedure cost estimates; for this reason, scenario analyses assuming all procedures were performed exclusively in either setting were conducted and showed similar cost‐effectiveness conclusions. The study was conducted in the Dutch healthcare system, so absolute costs may differ in other countries, but the relative differences and the main cost drivers, home care and productivity loss, are likely comparable in other settings.

Procedural costs can possibly be lowered by changing workflows. For instance, by changing the setting, method of anaesthesia and limiting operating time and personnel when possible. Additionally, as most (societal) costs are made during the waiting time for definitive stone treatment, costs could possibly be further reduced by prioritising definitive stone treatment for patients with obstructive urolithiasis.

Conclusions

Time to recovery and QALYs were similar between groups. At a willingness‐to‐pay threshold of €0 per additional unit of effect, JJ had a 74% probability of being cost‐effective compared to PCN. These findings suggest that JJ may offer better value for money than PCN in the acute management of obstructive urolithiasis. Since most (societal) costs are made during the waiting period for definitive stone treatment, future research should explore whether prioritising earlier stone removal could reduce overall costs.
