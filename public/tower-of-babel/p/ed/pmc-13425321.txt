Analysis of Gelling Characteristics and Mesoscopic Factors of Water-in-Oil Waxy Crude Oil Emulsions
Authors: Hang Dong, Shunke Zhang, Jian Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, the simultaneous rheology and in situ microscopy measurements, as well as the microfluidic technique, were employed to investigate the dynamic formation law of the gelled system in waxy crude oil emulsions, the mesoscopic influencing factors of gel strength, and their corresponding mechanisms under different water cuts, stirring rates, and temperature conditions. The results show that the mesoscopic structural evolution of the emulsion follows a distinct three-stage pathway: wax crystal/water droplet monomers → homogeneous/heterogeneous aggregates → wax crystal-water droplet floc structure, and each evolutionary stage corresponds to the variation of the macroscopic storage modulus. During the structural evolution, emulsified water droplets act as filling units that strengthen the interfacial connection, and their specific surface area is the primary mesoscopic parameter affecting the structural strength of the gelled system, which directly determines the scale of the oil–water interface and the interfacial interaction between wax crystals and water droplets. Wax crystals constitute the continuous network skeleton of the gelled system, and their quantity governs the compactness and continuity of the three-dimensional floc structure, playing a more dominant role in the macroscopic gel strength. Individual regulation of either the specific surface area of water droplets or the quantity of wax crystals can significantly alter the gel strength of the system. The simultaneous optimization of the two parameters produces an obvious synergistic strengthening effect, leading to an exponential rise in gel strength; conversely, the rheological properties of the emulsion can be remarkably improved. The dual-phase synergistic enhancement and weakening model proposed from a topological perspective clarifies that the “small and abundant” mesoscopic morphology featured by high-specific-surface-area water droplets and a large number of wax crystals is the core cause for the strengthened gel structure and deteriorated rheological properties of the emulsion. This model can systematically explain the influence mechanisms of various working conditions on the gel strength of water-in-oil (W/O) waxy crude oil emulsions. The conclusions of this research are well applicable to waxy crude oil emulsions characterized by high saturates, low aromatics, and low heavy polar components, and can provide theoretical references for the rheological regulation and pipeline transportation optimization of waxy crude oil emulsions.

Introduction

During the gathering and transportation of waxy crude oil, stable water-in-oil (W/O) emulsions are often formed from oil–water multiphase systems under shear action. When the ambient temperature drops below the wax appearance temperature (WAT), wax crystals precipitate and interact with dispersed water droplets, gradually forming a three-dimensional networked gel structure. This significantly increases the flow resistance in pipelines and threatens operational safety. , Therefore, revealing the gelation behavior and structure formation mechanism of waxy crude oil emulsions and identifying their influencing factors are of great significance for the flow assurance of waxy crude oil during gathering and transportation.

The rheological properties of waxy crude oil emulsions are jointly affected by their formation conditions and actual operational process conditions. Formation conditions include water cut, crude oil properties, as well as the quantity, morphology and chemical composition of dispersed droplets; − process conditions involve cooling rate, shear rate and other parameters. − Previous studies have demonstrated that dispersed water droplets, wax crystals and surface-active components are the key factors governing the rheological behavior of emulsions. − For instance, Haj-Shafiei et al. and Sun et al. found that water droplets and wax crystals collectively affect the rheological properties. Sun et al. and Guo et al. , pointed out that the viscoelasticity and thixotropy of emulsions are stronger than those of neat waxy crude oil, and an increase in water cut enhances the elasticity of the system. More recently, Guimaraes et al. developed fractal-based rheological models that integrate elastoplastic and viscosity descriptions for waxy oil suspensions, providing a unified theoretical framework. Moraseli et al. employed a flow loop approach to demonstrate that water droplets act as nucleation sites for asphaltene deposition, thereby increasing deposit mass in live oil emulsions. Jain et al. showed that applying an electric field significantly reduces viscosity by breaking down the wax network, with the reduction following an exponential decay dependent on shear rate and wax crystal volume fraction.

Researchers have investigated the evolution of emulsion rheological properties by varying the quantity and morphology of water droplets. Sun et al. and Huang et al. found that a reduction in droplet size and uniform size distribution can improve emulsion stability. Liu et al. revealed the two-stage characteristics of droplet size reduction. Huang et al. and Li et al. verified that increasing water cut and stirring intensity raises the number of water droplets and strengthens thixotropy. Wei et al. and Chen et al. observed that a higher number of water droplets intensifies flocculation, leading to increased viscosity and enhanced shear-thinning behavior. Wang et al. and Le et al. determined the gelation nucleation rate of emulsions with different water cuts using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). In general, an increase in either the number or size of water droplets tends to deteriorate the rheological properties. Wax crystals and surface-active substances also exert a significant influence on rheological behavior. Le et al. and Otsubo & Prud’homme found that an increasing cooling rate elevates the characteristic gelation temperatures and degrades the rheological properties. Li et al. and Jiang et al. reported that a higher paraffin content or resin/asphaltene interactions reduce the oil–water interfacial tension, strengthen the interfacial film, and improve emulsion stability. Marinho et al. and Chen et al. discovered that wax crystal morphology exerts a dual effect on emulsions, with needle-like wax crystals being more conducive to the development of gel structures. In summary, the rheological properties of waxy crude oil emulsions are influenced by multiple factors including water droplets, wax crystals and surface-active components, which interact with each other through complex mechanisms.

Extensive studies have clarified that the gel structure of waxy crude oil–water-in-oil (W/O) emulsions presents a typical “skeleton–filler” structure, in which wax crystals form the fundamental skeleton while water droplets act as the filling phase. ,,,, This structure derives from a well-established theory for interpreting the mechanical behaviors of composite materials in civil engineering and materials science, and has been widely applied to asphalt mixtures, concrete and particle-reinforced composite materials. Its core mechanism is that “the continuous phase constructs the load-bearing skeleton, and the dispersed phase fills the internal voids to achieve synergistic strengthening”. − The low-temperature gelled system of waxy crude oil W/O emulsions is essentially highly consistent with this structural model: wax crystals interlock with each other to form a three-dimensional load-bearing skeleton, and emulsified water droplets, as the filling phase, are embedded in the voids of the skeleton, which jointly determine the gel strength and rheological properties of the system.

Regarding experimental techniques and analytical methods, conventional rheological tests combined with ex situ microscopic observation have yielded numerous research findings and important insights. However, such approaches face certain limitations in directly capturing the dynamic evolution of mesostructures during the gelation process. In recent years, the application of simultaneous rheo-optical in situ microscopic measurement has enabled real-time correlation between the macroscopic mechanical response of emulsions and their mesoscopic structural evolution, providing direct evidence for elucidating the gelation mechanism. In addition, microfluidic technology offers a novel approach for investigating the gelation characteristics of emulsions under simulated pipeline flow conditions, showing broad application prospects.

In summary, although numerous scholars have conducted extensive research on the rheological properties of waxy crude oil emulsions, there is still insufficient understanding regarding the quantitative characterization of key mesoscopic parameters (e.g., water droplets and wax crystals) and their multifactor coupled interaction mechanisms. Most relevant studies remain at the level of qualitative description, without establishing a clear quantitative correlation between mesoscopic factors and gel strength, nor forming a systematic theory of structural evolution. To address these gaps, this study adopts the simultaneous rheo-in situ microscopy measurement with an optimized light source, combined with microfluidic experiments. By adjusting the water cut of emulsions, the stirring rate for preparation, and the initial cooling temperature of crude oil, experimental investigations are performed on the gelation characteristics and mesostructural formation laws of waxy crude oil emulsions under various conditions. From the perspective of macro-mesoscopic multiscale synergy, the action mechanisms of mesoscopic factors such as water droplets and wax crystals on the gel structure strength are revealed. Furthermore, a dual-phase synergistic structural model is proposed based on the experimental findings, providing a theoretical basis for flow assurance and technological innovation in energy conservation and consumption reduction for waxy crude oil gathering pipelines.

Basic Physical Properties of Crude Oil

The density of this crude oil at 20 °C is 855.4 kg·m–3, with a wax appearance temperature (WAT) of 47.46 °C and a pour point of 30 °C. Its wax content reaches up to 20.34 wt %, classifying it as a typical high-wax crude oil. It exhibits a strong tendency for wax precipitation and gelation during cooling, making it an ideal experimental subject for studying the gelation behavior of waxy crude oil emulsions.

In terms of group composition, the crude oil presents a typical distribution feature of high saturates, low aromatics and low heavy polar components: the saturate content is as high as 73.11 wt %, acting as the absolutely dominant component; the aromatic content is only 8.32 wt %; the contents of resins and asphaltenes are 8.8 and 3.2 wt %, respectively, and their total amount (heavy polar components) accounts for merely 12 wt % of the crude oil.

For the carbon number distribution of n-alkanes, it covers a wide range from C7 to C38, with the main peak carbon concentrated in the medium- and long-chain alkane region of C19–C21. The proportion of medium- and long-chain n-alkanes is significantly higher than that of short-chain and extra-long-chain alkanes. These medium- and long-chain n-alkanes serve as the core constituents of paraffin wax in the crude oil, and directly determine the wax precipitation characteristics and gelation behavior of the crude oil.

Based on the functional group characterization by Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, the structural features of the four SARA fractions and their core effects on the emulsion system can be summarized as follows:Saturates are mainly composed of long-chain n-alkanes and isoalkanes without polar functional groups or unsaturated structures, acting as the only source for wax crystal precipitation during cooling, which directly determines the compactness of the gelation network skeleton and the macroscopic gel strength.Aromatics are dominated by aromatic hydrocarbons with alkyl side chains, which can inhibit excessive aggregation of wax crystals and synergistically improve the oil–water interfacial stability.Resins, as the key natural surface-active substances in crude oil, are rich in polar heteroatom functional groups. They can adsorb directionally at the oil–water interface to form a high-strength interfacial film to ensure the stability of W/O emulsions, and also act as heterogeneous nucleation sites for wax crystals to regulate their precipitation behavior.Asphaltenes are the components with the highest aromaticity and polarity in crude oil. They can synergistically strengthen the interfacial film performance with resins, and significantly regulate the aggregation of wax crystals and the structural characteristics of the gelation network.

Preparation Method of Emulsion

The emulsions were prepared using a mechanical stirring apparatus. The stirrer employed was an IKA RW 20 digital mixer manufactured by IKA, Germany, equipped with a four-bladed pitched paddle impeller. It has a rotational speed accuracy of 0.01 rpm and a speed range of 250–2000 rpm.

The detailed preparation procedure is as follows:First, the pretreated crude oil and water were placed in a thermostat and kept stationary for 2 h, with the thermostat set to the initial cooling temperature. After the temperature inside the samples became uniform and stable, crude oil and water were measured according to the predetermined volume ratio and placed in separate beakers. The beakers were sealed and immersed in a constant-temperature water bath at 50 °C to prevent wax precipitation from interfering with emulsion preparation. Once both the oil and water reached 50 °C, the water was poured into the crude oil beaker in one batch.The oil–water mixture was stirred mechanically, and emulsions were prepared at different preset stirring rates, with a fixed stirring duration of 20 min for all cases.

Experimental Method

Sample Preparation

With droplets and wax crystals as the key factors governing the rheological properties of waxy crude oil W/O emulsions, various experimental samples were designed. For the mesoscopic characteristics of droplets, emulsions with water cuts of 10%, 20%, 30% and 40% were first prepared at a fixed stirring rate of 1000 rpm. Then, at a constant water cut of 10%, the droplet size and distribution were modified by adjusting the stirring rate in the range of 750 to 2000 rpm. Considering that the precipitation and growth of wax crystals are highly temperature-sensitive, the initial cooling temperature and final cooling temperature were regulated to change the precipitation quantity, morphology and aggregation state of wax crystals. In this way, the influence of mesoscopic morphological parameters of wax crystals on the rheological behavior of emulsions was systematically investigated.

Simultaneous Rheo-In Situ Microscopic Experiments

The viscoelasticity tests of emulsions under static cooling conditions were carried out using an MCR702 rheometer. The detailed procedure is as follows:An appropriate amount of sample was placed in the instrument and kept isothermally at 46 °C for 5 min to ensure the sample reached the initial experimental temperature. The sample was cooled to the final cooling temperature at a rate of 0.5 °C/min, during which the storage modulus and loss modulus (G′ and G″) of the sample as functions of temperature were recorded. The sample was then held isothermally at the final cooling temperature for 40 min, and the time-dependent variations of G′ and G″ were measured simultaneously.

In synchronization with the above rheological measurements, an in situ microscopic system was used to observe the internal structure of the emulsion, and a CCD camera was employed to record the evolution of the mesoscale morphology of the emulsion.

Microscopic Observation Experiments Based on the Microfluidic System

To observe the formation process of the gel structure of emulsions in microchannels in situ, the microfluidic experimental protocol was designed as follows: During emulsion preparation, the microfluidic chip was placed on the microscope hot stage and preheated at 46 °C, consistent with the initial experimental temperature of the rheometer. Once the emulsion was prepared, it was immediately drawn into a syringe and injected into the microfluidic chip. The system was then held isothermally at 46 °C for 10 min to stabilize the emulsion in the microchannels. Subsequently, the emulsion was cooled at a rate of 0.5 °C/min, and microscopic images of the emulsion morphology within the microchannels were captured during the cooling process.

Gray Relational Calculation Method

To quantify the relative significance of mesoscopic structural parameters of water droplets (number, particle size, specific surface area) on the macroscopic mechanical parameter (storage modulus), the gray relational analysis (GRA) was adopted for evaluation.Gray relational analysis is a core quantitative method derived from gray system theory, and is widely used to analyze the correlations in multifactor, nonlinear and small-sample systems. Compared with traditional linear correlation analysis, GRA does not require the raw data to follow a specific probability distribution, and can accurately characterize the synchronicity and correlation degree of parameter variation trends in complex systems. It has been widely applied to the quantitative assessment of rheological influencing factors of waxy crude oils, making it suitable for the correlation analysis between mesoscopic structural parameters and macroscopic rheological properties in this study. − To address the sensitivity of this method to normalization methods and data set size, a unified interval-based dimensionless normalization was adopted throughout this study. The analysis covered all independent experimental conditions, and the correlation ranking results were cross-validated combined with macro-mesoscopic mechanism analysis, so as to minimize the deviation caused by method sensitivity and ensure the robustness of the results.

The detailed calculation procedure is as follows:First, the raw data were subjected to mean-based dimensionless processing. Then, the gray relational coefficients between each parameter sequence and the target performance sequence were calculated at each sampling point. Finally, the comprehensive gray relational degree reflecting the synchronicity of variation trends was obtained by averaging the relational coefficients across all sampling points (see eqs –). A higher gray relational degree indicates a greater influence of the variation trend of the corresponding parameter on the target performance.

Results and Discussion

Formation Process of Gel Structure in Waxy Crude Oil Emulsions

From the microscopic videos of simultaneous rheo-in situ measurements and microfluidic experiments, it is found that the development process of the gel structure in such crude oil emulsions shows remarkable consistency under different preparation and experimental conditions, and the detailed process is presented in Figure .The evolution sequence of the gel structure obtained by both experimental methods follows the same law:wax crystal/water droplet monomer structure → homo- (hetero-) aggregate structure → wax crystal-water droplet flocculated structure. A synchronous analysis is performed combined with the modulus values measured during the cooling process:As shown in Figure (a), at the initial cooling stage, the system exhibits good fluidity. Most emulsified water droplets and wax crystals in the field of view exist as monomers, and undergo slow movement and collision under thermophoretic force, but usually separate again after collision.The storage modulus of the emulsion at this stage is extremely small with a gentle increasing trend, not exceeding 0.2% of that at the end of cooling. As the temperature decreases further, the amount of precipitated wax crystals increases, the system viscosity rises, and the mobility of droplets and wax crystals declines.At this point, after contacting each other, they can gradually form stable aggregates under van der Waals forces, including wax crystal aggregates and emulsified water droplet aggregates.Meanwhile, partial wax crystals attach to and grow on the droplet surfaces, forming heteroaggregates, namely wax crystal-droplet copolymers. Due to the emergence of these aggregates, the system structure is gradually strengthened. Generally, the system has approached or reached the gel point (where the storage modulus equals the loss modulus) at this stage, and the storage modulus rises significantly, ranging from 0.2% to 10% of that at the cooling end point. As the temperature continues to drop, wax crystals on the droplet surfaces keep growing and may embed into the droplets. Meanwhile, newly formed wax crystals continuously precipitate from the oil phase and fill the gaps between copolymers. Eventually, wax crystals, droplets and aggregates interlock with each other to form a dense floc structure with certain mechanical strength, marking the formation of a stable emulsion gel structure.In this stage, the storage modulus increases sharply, with a growth rate more than 20 times that of the previous stage (dominated by aggregates).

Evolution process of gel structure in waxy crude oil emulsions under different testing methods.

The gel structure evolution shown in Figure (b) is highly consistent with that in Figure (a), still following the evolution law of wax crystal/water droplet monomer structure → homo- (hetero-) aggregate structure → wax crystal-water droplet flocculated structure with decreasing temperature.

To further verify the universality of this gelation evolution pathway and eliminate the influence of differences in crude oil basic physical properties on the conclusions, another typical waxy crude oil in China (Crude Oil H, wax appearance temperature (WAT) 42.8 °C, wax content 17.62%) was additionally selected to prepare W/O emulsions for parallel verification experiments. The emulsion preparation procedure, simultaneous rheo-in situ measurement conditions, and cooling program in the verification experiments were completely consistent with those of the aforementioned crude oil system, with single variables strictly controlled to ensure the comparability of experimental results. The results are shown in Figure . The parallel experiments indicate that during the cooling gelation process of this waxy crude oil emulsion, its mesoscopic structural evolution and macroscopic mechanical response still strictly follow the three-stage variation law: At the initial cooling stage, the system is dominated by independent wax crystal monomers and water droplet monomers, with an extremely low storage modulus, corresponding to the monomer structure stage; As the temperature decreases, homogeneous/heterogeneous aggregates are gradually formed by the interconnection between wax crystals, between water droplets, and between wax crystals and water droplets, and the storage modulus increases significantly, corresponding to the aggregate structure stage; With continuous cooling below the wax appearance temperature, wax crystals and aggregates interweave and connect to form a continuous three-dimensional flocculated structure, and the storage modulus rises by orders of magnitude, corresponding to the floc structure stage. The stage division of this evolution process and the corresponding relationship between mesoscopic structure and macroscopic rheology are completely consistent with the experimental results of the aforementioned crude oil system, which further proves the universality of the gelation evolution pathway.

Comprehensive observations of the cooling gelation process and viscoelastic data analyses were performed on emulsions with different water cuts (I–IV) and stirring rates (V–VIII). The results show that although all samples follow the basic structural evolution from monomers and aggregates to flocs, the detailed behaviors, final morphologies, and gel strengths during gelation are regulated by droplet mesoscopic characteristics and correlate with the variation trend of the storage modulus. The storage modulus increases with rising water cut and stirring rate (as shown in Figure ). This phenomenon can be explained by the differences in mesoscopic structures. An increase in water cut (from I to IV) directly raises the volume fraction and number of droplets. Although the interfacial film strength weakens due to the relative reduction in wax, resins and asphaltenes, which promotes droplet coalescence into larger droplets (Figure ), these increased droplets (including coalesced large ones) act as “fillers” and bind tightly with precipitated wax crystals during gelation, forming more wax crystal–droplet copolymers and enhancing the overall strength of the gel network. On the other hand, a higher stirring rate (from V to VIII) generates smaller and more uniformly distributed droplets under strong shearing action (Figure ), yielding a larger specific surface area. This promotes the adsorption of resins and asphaltenes at the interface and strengthens the interfacial film, which suppresses droplet coalescence and migration during cooling, allowing a large number of uniformly sized and interface-stabilized droplets to participate more evenly in network construction. Meanwhile, the larger specific surface area significantly increases the total contact interface between wax crystals and droplets, strengthening the connections between copolymer units. Ultimately, a more homogeneous gel network with higher strength is formed, reflected in the remarkable increase in the storage modulus.

The mesoscopic characteristics of droplets not only determine the final strength of the gel structure but also lead to differences in the formation rate of the emulsion gel structure, which is specifically reflected in the formation time of wax crystal–droplet copolymers and the macroscopic gelation temperature. Microscopic observations (Figure ) show that with increasing water cut (rising droplet number and particle size), the initial formation temperature of wax crystal–droplet copolymers is advanced. For instance, copolymers appear in Emulsion IV at 42 °C, whereas they are not observed in Emulsion I until 36 °C. This is attributed to the fact that a large number of large-sized droplets provide abundant and easily accessible attachment sites for wax crystals, greatly promoting the rapid nucleation and development of copolymer structures. The acceleration of this evolution process directly leads to the advancement of macroscopic gelation behavior. Rheological tests (Figure ) confirm that emulsions with a higher storage modulus (G′) exhibit a significantly higher gelation temperature (the intersection of G′ and G″). The intrinsic mechanism is as follows: more copolymer units formed at an earlier stage, acting as key cross-linking points in the network, can bridge and solidify the entire system more rapidly, accelerating the increase in the solid elastic characteristics of the emulsion and thus causing the storage modulus curve to intersect with the loss modulus curve earlier. The effect of stirring rate is inherently consistent with the above law. Although high-speed stirring (from V to VIII) produces more, smaller and more uniformly distributed droplets, it also greatly increases the effective contact interface between wax crystals and droplets, thereby promoting the uniform and rapid formation of copolymers. Therefore, emulsions prepared at a high stirring rate also exhibit a higher gelation temperature. Under the above effects, the structural strength of the emulsion after isothermal stabilization also increases with the rise of water cut and stirring rate. Effects of Wax Crystal Mesoscopic Parameters on the Gel Structure of Emulsions

During the cooling process of waxy crude oil emulsions, the continuous precipitation of wax crystals gradually densifies the three-dimensional network structure of the system, leading to an increase in its structural strength. , To further investigate the influence of wax crystals on the emulsion gel system, rheological-microscopic simultaneous experiments at different final cooling temperatures were conducted on the same emulsion sample. Meanwhile, quantitative statistical analysis was performed on the mesoscopic parameters of wax crystals at various final cooling temperatures, and the results are displayed in Figure . −

According to the isothermal test results shown in Figure , the storage modulus of emulsions at final cooling temperatures of 24 °C, 28 and 32 °C consistently follows the order: 24 °C > 28 °C > 32 °C. This phenomenon is closely associated with the precipitation of wax crystals and the differences in their mesoscopic structures under different final cooling temperatures.

At a fixed cooling rate, a lower final cooling temperature provides sufficient thermodynamic driving force for the precipitation and growth of wax crystals. From the quantitative statistical results (Figure ), as the final cooling temperature drops from 32 to 24 °C, the number of wax crystals in the system increases from 1479 to 2589, and the average area of wax crystals rises from 2.43 μm2 to 5.88 μm2. These data demonstrate that lowering the final cooling temperature significantly promotes both the total precipitation and individual size growth of wax crystals.

Nevertheless, the specific surface area of wax crystals decreases with falling temperature. This is because the surface area growth rate of wax crystals lags behind that of their volume or quantity during crystal growth, leading to a relative reduction in the effective contact interface provided by per unit mass or volume of wax crystals. Although the reduced specific surface area might theoretically weaken interfacial connections between wax crystals and between wax crystals and water droplets, the substantial increase in the number and size of wax crystals collectively produces a stronger structural reinforcement effect. More wax crystals act as network nodes and void fillers, forming abundant “wax crystal–droplet copolymers” with droplets that effectively strengthen the support and connectivity of the three-dimensional network. For this reason, the sample quenched to 24 °C contains more densely distributed wax crystals, forming a much denser network with the dispersed droplets.

Further analysis shows that the growth mechanism of wax crystals varies across temperature intervals:In the range of 32–28 °C, the average area of wax crystals increases markedly, indicating that precipitated wax molecules tend to deposit and grow on existing crystals.In the range of 28–24 °C, the number of wax crystals rises sharply, implying that elevated supersaturation triggers the formation of numerous new crystal nuclei. These two mechanisms jointly raise the total content and structural compactness of wax crystals. Therefore, despite the decrease in specific surface area, the structural strengthening effect dominated by the increased number and size of wax crystals leads to a distinct improvement in gel structure strength as the final cooling temperature decreases.

The initial cooling temperature also exerts a significant influence on the precipitation characteristics of wax crystals in waxy crude oil, thereby affecting the interaction between wax crystals and water droplets within the emulsion. This further alters the formation process of the emulsion gel structure and results in differences in the final gel strength. To investigate this factor, crude oil was treated at two initial cooling temperatures (70 and 80 °C), and emulsions were prepared accordingly for experimental testing.

Figure presents the viscoelastic variation of different emulsions during the cooling process. The results show that the storage modulus of the emulsion system prepared from crude oil treated at an initial cooling temperature of 80 °C is consistently lower than that treated at 70 °C, and its gelation temperature is also lower (34.25 and 41.51 °C, respectively), indicating better fluidity of the emulsion at low temperatures.

To more intuitively display the spatial distribution and mesomorphic characteristics of wax crystals at different initial cooling temperatures, threshold processing was performed on the microscopic images of wax crystals in emulsions at the end of cooling, as presented in Figure . The emulsion under 70 °C initial cooling shows significantly more and smaller wax crystals: quantitative statistics show an average number of 2072 wax crystals with a mean particle size of 4.11 μm. In contrast, the emulsion under 80 °C initial cooling has only 1891 wax crystals on average, with a mean particle size of 5.2 μm, showing remarkable differences in morphology and quantity. Combined with the conclusions in ref , at the relatively high initial cooling temperature of 80 °C, wax molecules dissolve more fully and distribute more uniformly; resins and asphaltenes are fully liberated and activated, promoting sufficient growth of wax crystals during cooling, eventually forming Type-I wax crystals with fewer numbers, larger sizes and lower specific surface areas. In contrast, 70 °C initial cooling cannot fully activate the activity of resins and asphaltenes, thus deteriorating the subsequent crystallization behavior of wax molecules, generating Type-II wax crystals with more numbers, smaller sizes and higher specific surface areas. Such differences in wax crystal mesoscopic characteristics induced by initial cooling temperature directly determine the compactness and structural continuity of the emulsion gel network skeleton. The abundant small-sized wax crystals at 70 °C provide more network nodes and interfacial interaction sites between wax crystals and water droplets for the gelling system, greatly improving the formation efficiency of aggregates and forming a denser and more continuous three-dimensional wax crystal skeleton, which provides core support for gel strength. Whereas the fewer large-sized wax crystals at 80 °C lead to insufficient network nodes and limited effective interfacial contact area, resulting in a weaker gel skeleton structure. This ultimately accounts for the lower gelation temperature and smaller storage modulus of the emulsion. Gray Relational Analysis between Mesoscopic Structural Parameters and Gel Structure Strength

To quantify the relative importance of droplet mesoscopic structural parameters (number, particle size, specific surface area) influencing the macroscopic mechanical parameter (storage modulus), the gray relational analysis method was employed. To assess the effect of experimental measurement errors on the analytical results, mesoscopic parameter data from three independent repeated experiments were used to calculate the correlation degree corresponding to each data set separately. The arithmetic mean of the three correlation degrees was taken as the final result, and error bars were used in the figure to represent the fluctuation range of the correlation degree of each parameter. The results are shown in Figure .

Calculation results of correlation degrees between droplet mesoscopic parameters and storage modulus.

According to the results, the correlation degrees between each parameter and the storage modulus are ranked as follows: specific surface area (r = 0.74) > droplet number (r = 0.73) > average particle size (r = 0.64). This indicates that the storage modulus of the waxy crude oil emulsion gelling system is most sensitive to the variation of droplet specific surface area, which is the most critical mesoscopic characteristic parameter affecting gel strength. This result is consistent with the previous mechanism analysis: the increase in stirring rate directly strengthens network connectivity by enlarging the specific surface area; although the rise in water content reduces the specific surface area due to droplet coalescence, the filling effect derived from the increased droplet number (with the second-highest correlation degree) still promotes the enhancement of the macroscopic structure. The filling effect referred to in this paper is defined as the physical mechanism whereby emulsified water droplets, as dispersed phase particles, fill the voids in the three-dimensional network skeleton formed by interlocked wax crystals. This increases the compactness of the system and the number of structural junctions, making the overall gel denser and more stable, and ultimately improving the strength of the gelling structure. Gray relational analysis quantitatively verifies that maximizing interfacial contact (high specific surface area) and providing sufficient structural units (high droplet number) are the two key factors contributing to gel structure strengthening during the formation of the wax crystal–droplet gelling system.

To further verify the robustness of the gray relational analysis results, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used for cross-validation of the correlations between mesoscopic parameters and the storage modulus. As a nonparametric statistical method based on data ranks, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis characterizes the synchronizm and correlation degree of variable trends by calculating the rank correlation between two variable sets. It does not require the original data to follow a normal distribution or a strict linear mapping relationship between variables, making it a standard analytical tool for quantifying multifactorial nonlinear correlations and determining the priority of parameter influences. − This method is highly compatible with the calculation requirements of this study for three reasons. First, this study adopts controlled variable experiments with a limited small-sample data set of effective working conditions. The method shows good adaptability to small samples and can effectively avoid statistical biases caused by sample size limitations. Second, the relationship between mesoscopic parameters of waxy crude oil emulsions and macroscopic gel strength is a multifactorial coupled nonlinear correlation without a clear linear mapping law. The method can accurately capture the trend correlation between variables, fully aligning with the core analytical objective of this study: clarifying the ranking of parameter influence weights. Third, it shares a consistent core logic with gray relational analysis, both taking the synchronizm of variable change trends as the key judgment criterion, enabling unbiased cross-validation of the parameter importance ranking obtained by gray relational analysis and thoroughly examining the robustness and reliability of the gray relational analysis results. The results show that the variation trend of wax crystal number is the closest to that of storage modulus, indicating that the number of wax crystals exerts the most significant influence on the storage modulus, followed by the average area, while the effect of specific surface area is relatively weak. This is different from the case of water droplets, where the specific surface area shows the strongest correlation and the number takes the second place. Such difference mainly stems from their distinct roles in the gel network. During the gelation of waxy crude oil emulsions, wax crystals act as the continuous phase and form the main skeleton of the gelling structure. Their number directly determines the density and continuity of the three-dimensional network skeleton. A larger number of wax crystals leads to denser network nodes and stronger spatial supporting effects, thus contributing most critically to the final modulus and presenting the highest correlation degree. In contrast, water droplets serve as the dispersed phase embedded in the wax crystal network, and their function focuses more on “interfacial connection strengthening” rather than constructing a continuous skeleton. The specific surface area of droplets directly determines the scale of the oil–water interface, which acts as the adsorption site for natural emulsifiers such as resins and asphaltenes and also the key region for interactions with precipitated wax crystals. A higher specific surface area enables droplets to provide more effective connection sites for the wax crystal network, thereby significantly enhancing the integrity of the network, which explains its highest correlation with the modulus.

Synergistic Effect Analysis of Influencing Factors on Gel Structure Strength

From the above analysis, it can be concluded that the number of wax crystals dominates the density of the network skeleton, while the specific surface area of droplets governs the efficiency of interfacial connection. The two factors act synergistically to ultimately regulate the macroscopic rheological properties of the emulsion gel. Although the dominant parameters affecting the modulus differ between wax crystals and droplets, both follow the mechanism for strengthening gel structure strength: “maximizing interfacial contact and network connectivity through unit refinement and quantity increase”. Based on this principle, the fundamental pathways by which water content, stirring rate, final cooling temperature and initial cooling temperature affect gel strength can be interpreted from a topological perspective: conditions that promote “biphasic refinement and multi-quantification” give rise to enhanced structural strength and deteriorated rheological behavior, and vice versa. Accordingly, the strong gel structure and weak gel structure of waxy crude oil emulsions can be described by the following models.

Strong Gel SystemSmall-Sized and Abundant Biphasic Reinforced System

It is composed of numerous fine spherical droplets (with high specific surface area) uniformly filled in a continuous three-dimensional network constructed by a large number of fine wax crystals (high quantity, small size). The two phases form a system with high coordination numbers and bond closely through a large interfacial area, forming a compact composite structure with uniform force chain distribution, as shown in Figure .

Weak Gel SystemLarge-Sized and Scarce Biphasic Weakened System

A small number of large-sized droplets (low specific surface area) are randomly dispersed in the loosely structured wax crystal skeleton. The gelling system formed by the two phases exhibits low coordination numbers and weak bonding strength, as shown in Figure .

Conclusions

(1)Under different preparation and experimental conditions, the gel structure of crude oil emulsions featured by “high saturated hydrocarbons, low aromatics and low heavy polar components” follows a universal evolutionary path for this type of crude oil: wax crystal/water droplet monomers (macroscopic storage modulus <0.2% of that at flocculation structure formation) → homo/hetero-aggregates (macroscopic storage modulus = 0.2%–10% of that at flocculation structure formation) → wax crystal–water droplet flocculation structure.(2)As the dispersed phase, water droplets regulate the gel strength of the system mainly by filling the voids of the wax crystal skeleton and forming interfacial contacts with wax crystals. Among the relevant parameters, specific surface area is the most core factor governing gel structure strength, with a higher influence weight than droplet number and average particle size. An increase in specific surface area promotes the formation of wax crystal–droplet copolymers to strengthen the gelling structure, while an increase in droplet number enhances gel strength primarily by raising the packing density of the network.(3)As the continuous-phase skeleton of the gel network, wax crystals determine the compactness, continuity and ultimate structural strength of the three-dimensional network directly through their number and average size. Among these, wax crystal number is the dominant factor affecting gel strength, with a higher influence weight than average area and specific surface area. Lowering the initial cooling temperature can increase the number of wax crystals and significantly strengthen the gelling behavior of the system.(4)Wax crystals and water droplets follow the gel-strengthening mechanism of refining structural units and increasing their quantity to maximize interfacial contact and network connectivity. Biphasic refinement and multiquantification deteriorates the rheological properties of emulsions, whereas the reverse trend improves them. The contributions of the two phases to gel strength are not simply additive; instead, they produce an exponentially enhanced synergistic effect through mutual reinforcement.
