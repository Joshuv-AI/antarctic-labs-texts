Unveiling FERONIA receptor kinase–mediated cellular mechanisms with a small-molecule inhibitor
Authors: Mengze Sun, Baiyan Lu, Ying Yang, Junping Fan, Weiwei Ren, Xiaonan Chu, Yihui Gao, Jun Wu, Jue Wang, Han Ke, Zhiwen Liu, Shaojun Dai, Xiaoguang Lei, Chao Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceFERONIA (FER) receptor kinase has been implicated in diverse biological processes, yet the precise molecular mechanisms governing its function remain elusive. We characterize Ferovicin (FRV), a highly selective FER kinase inhibitor. Through integration of structural biology with phosphoproteomics, we elucidate how RALF1 activates FER via Ser695 phosphorylation to inhibit AHA2 activity, thereby controlling apoplastic alkalinization and root meristem cell expansion. These findings establish FRV as a powerful molecular tool for the study of FER regulatory mechanisms.

FERONIA receptor kinase (FER) has been extensively studied and established as a master regulator in pleiotropic aspects of plant growth and development, as well as in responses to abiotic stresses and biotic infections (1–3). Extensive studies on FER have revealed its diverse functionalities, many of which are dependent on its kinase activity, though some are independent of it. The groundbreaking discovery of FER as the receptor for RAPID ALKALINIZATION FACTOR 1 (RALF1) sheds light on its involvement in cell expansion regulation. Upon RALF1 treatment, FER is activated, complexing with its coreceptor LLG1, leading to the phosphorylation of FER and subsequent phosphorylation of the plasma membrane H+-ATPase2 (AHA2). This cascade of events induces the apoplastic alkalization and ultimately represses cell expansion (4). During pollination, pollen-derived peptide PCP-B and stigma-derived RALF23/33 compete for the interaction with FER, which either maintain or open the stigma lock (5). FER functions upstream of ROPGEFs and ROP GTPases, which serve as key regulators of root hair development and pollination (5–7). Moreover, RALF1 promotes FER-mediated phosphorylation of the translation initiation factor eIF4E1, enhancing mRNA affinity and modulating mRNA translation (8). Additionally, RALF1 induces FER-dependent phosphorylation of glycine-rich RNA-binding protein 7, which enhances its association with the spliceosome component U1-70 K to modulate dynamic alternative splicing (9). Upon bacterial pathogen attack, FER phosphorylates and destabilizes the MYC2 transcription factor, inhibiting jasmonic acid and phytotoxin COR signaling pathways (10). A comprehensive phosphoproteomics analysis has further revealed that mutations in the FER gene result in global alterations in the phosphorylation levels of over a thousand proteins (11).

The above studies collectively highlight the significance of FER kinase activity. However, certain studies engage in debates regarding the transduction of signaling by FER through its kinase activity and the mechanisms involved. During pollen tube reception, it has been demonstrated that the kinase domain, rather than the kinase activity, is essential for the function of FER. This is evidenced by the phenotype observed in the STS-AAA changes of the S695, T696, and S701 in the activation loop, which successfully complement the fer-1 mutant (12). Moreover, the kinase-dead K565R mutation in FER resulted in a reduced ability to complement fer-4 knockout phenotypes in the root, although not in the ovule, compared to wild-type FER (13). Transgenic lines carrying the K565R mutation reveal that the inhibition by RALF23 is mediated by active FER signaling, while FER’s positive role in immune signaling appears to be kinase activity independent (14). The sophisticated regulatory mechanisms demonstrated in these studies underscore the fundamental importance of deciphering FER kinase’s role in orchestrating specialized signaling pathways at the molecular level.

Small-molecule inhibitors play a pivotal role in advancing plant science, offering invaluable insights and applications across diverse research domains. Through selective modulation of specific pathways, these inhibitors enable researchers to unravel the intricate mechanisms governing plant physiology. A striking illustration of their significance lies in the discovery of the abscisic acid receptor. The identification of ABA small-molecule inhibitors, pyrabactin, and its derivatives proved instrumental in characterizing the ABA receptor PYR1 (Pyrabactin Resistance 1), ushering in a new era of ABA research (15, 16). Similarly, in the realm of auxin research, several small-molecule inhibitors have assumed pivotal roles (17, 18). For instance, the auxinole antagonist was designed to competitively block auxin-TIR1 (Transport Inhibitor Response 1) receptor interactions, thereby enabling more precise investigation of complex auxin signaling pathways (19). Chemically engineered convex (cvx) IAA analogue could specifically interact with the engineered concave (ccv) TIR1 receptor rather than the natural IAA, allowing for fine manipulation of the TIR1/AFB signaling (20).

To date, the lack of highly selective chemical probes that enable precise manipulation of FER kinase activity has posed a significant obstacle in unraveling the FER signaling pathways. In this work, we aimed to address this limitation by employing plant growth assays and structural analyses to characterize a small-molecule inhibitor OTSSP167, named Ferovicin (FRV), that specifically targets the ATP-binding pocket of the FER kinase domain, thereby effectively suppressing its kinase activity. Using FRV in phosphoproteomics and functional studies, we demonstrate that RALF1 activates FER through the phosphorylation of Ser695, which inhibits H+-ATPase via Ser899 to regulate root meristem cell expansion. These findings highlight FRV as a valuable tool for advancing the intricate functional study of FER regulatory mechanisms.

Results

Structure-Based Screening of Small-molecule Inhibitors Targeting the FER Kinase.

To search for inhibitors targeting FER, we performed a high-throughput virtual screening of 4,378 compounds using Gnina molecular docking software (21) and identified small molecules that bind to the kinase domain of FER (Fig. 1A), the ranking of CNN scoring function (22) represents the binding affinities between small molecules and FER kinase. Besides, we also conducted homologous sequence alignment for FER kinase, and based on the high degree of kinase domain similarity (approximately 60%) between FER and human Interleukin-1 Receptor Associated Kinase 4 (IRAK4) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A), we further investigated the above screening compounds with pronounced IRAK4 inhibitory activity. Five compounds were selected with the consideration of their core scaffolds, membrane permeability, and commercial availability (23). Among the screened inhibitors, five small molecules demonstrated remarkable inhibitory activity (SI Appendix, Table S1).

Two fer mutants exhibit pronounced arrest in root hair growth and are not responsive to RALF1 in root growth (4, 6). The fer-4 allele, characterized by a T-DNA insertion in the extracellular domain-coding region, represents a null mutant. On the other hand, the fer-5 allele carries truncated transcripts, in line with its T-DNA insertion proximity to the end of its kinase domain-coding region. We then performed root hair growth assays using the five selected molecules. Notably, four out of the five candidate inhibitors effectively suppressed root hair growth in wild-type seedlings, reminiscent of the fer-4 and fer-5 mutants, with the most pronounced effect observed for OTSSP167 (Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C). Moreover, among all tested small molecules and the broad-spectrum kinase inhibitor K-252a (24, 25), OTSSP167 emerged as the most effective one in suppressing RALF1 effect (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). Therefore, we named OTSSP167 as FER kinase inhibitor, FRV, given its potency and selectivity for FER.

To investigate the mechanism by which FRV inhibits FER, we expressed the FER kinase domain (FER-KD, residues 518-820) using an insect expression system. We obtained the cocrystal structure of FER-KD in complex with FRV at a resolution of 1.90 Å (SI Appendix, Table S2). The structure revealed a typical kinase sandwich architecture of FER, with a smaller N-lobe (residues 518-611) rich in β-sheets and a larger C-lobe (residues 618-816) primarily composed of α-helices. The two lobes were connected by a six-residue hinge (residues 612-617), forming an ATP-binding pocket at their junction. Notably, the activation segment (residues 678-708) extended outward from the core structure, suggesting an activated conformation of the FER kinase domain (Fig. 1D). The cocrystal structure showed that the activation segment, spanning residues 686-799, was not fully resolved, indicating its inherent disorder and high flexibility. Furthermore, the cocrystal structure demonstrated that FRV occupied the ATP-binding pocket, and its interaction with the key residues in the FER kinase, including Lys565, Tyr610, Tyr612, Met613, and Asp679, plays crucial roles in stabilizing the FER–FRV complex through hydrogen bonds and π–π stacking interactions (Fig. 1D). Importantly, Lys565, Asp679, and Tyr610 have been reported as critical residues involved in substrate phosphorylation catalyzed by FER kinase (26). We further assessed the binding affinity of the FRV inhibitor to FER-KD using the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay. Our results revealed that the equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) of FRV binding to FER was determined to be 0.4 μM (Fig. 1E). Moreover, the binding affinity of FRV to FER-KD surpassed that of ATP (with a KD of 18.37 μM), suggesting that FRV could function as an ATP competitive inhibitor by targeting the ATP-binding pocket to inhibit FER kinase activity.

Inhibition and Binding Affinity of FRV Against FER Kinase Variants.

To evaluate the inhibitory effect of FRV on FER kinase, we developed a robust kinase activity assay using the ADP-GloTM system. Recombinant FER kinase was incubated with increasing FRV concentrations, revealing a dose-dependent luminescence reduction that enabled dose–response curve construction. The IC50 value of FRV against FER kinase was determined to be 70 nM, indicating its potent inhibitory activity (Fig. 2A). To further elucidate the inhibitory mechanism, we investigated the binding affinity and inhibition effect of FRV on FER kinase mutants. Based on the cocrystal structure of FER kinase in complex with FRV and an analysis of key amino acid residues in the binding pocket, we selected five FER-KD point mutations (K565A, Y610A, Y612A, M613A, and D679A) for study. These mutants were cloned into the pFastBac vector and expressed in SF9 insect cells using the Bac-to-Bac baculovirus expression system (SI Appendix, Table S3). Dose–response curves for FRV against these mutants revealed significantly reduced inhibition for K565A, Y610A, and D679A mutants, with IC50 values exceeding 100 μM, while Y612A and M613A mutations impaired FER kinase catalytic activity (Fig. 2A).

To assess FRV’s binding affinity with FER-KD mutants, we purified five mutants for quantitative surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis. The results revealed differential impacts of these mutations (K565A, Y610A, Y612A, M613A, and D679A) on FRV–FER interaction (Fig. 2 B–F). K565 and Y610 were identified as critical ATP-binding residues that allosterically modulate FRV engagement. Furthermore, the Y612A mutation abolished FRV binding affinity (>1,000-fold reduction) in the SPR assay, confirming its essential role in the inhibitor recognition. Systematic comparison of pharmacological parameters (IC50/KD values) across mutants (Fig. 2G) suggests that spatial combinations of mutations within the binding pocket could synergistically disrupt FRV–FER interactions.

FRV Specifically Inhibits FER Kinase.

To evaluate the selectivity of FRV, we employed SPR experiments and ADP-Glo™ kinase assays to evaluate its binding affinity and inhibitory effects on key plant Receptor-Like Kinases (RLKs), including TRANS-MEMBRANE KINASE 4 (TMK4) (27), ANJEA (ANJ)(28), ANXUR1 (ANX1) (29), Buddha’s Paper Seal 1 (BUPS1) (30), and BRASSINOSTEROID INSENSITIVE 1 (BRI1) (31, 32). SPR results revealed dissociation constants (KD) of 15.3 μM and 19.4 μM for FRV binding to TMK4 and ANJ, respectively (Fig. 3 A and B). Notably, FRV exhibited significantly weaker binding (>100-fold reduction) to ANX1, BUPS1, and BRI1 compared to FER (Fig. 3 C–E). These findings demonstrate that FRV possesses remarkable specificity for FER, showing at least several dozen-fold greater selectivity over other receptor kinases. The observed differential binding affinity of FRV between FER and other kinases can be rationalized through structural analysis. Previous studies on kinase inhibitors have shown that even minor differences in hydrophobic interactions or hydrogen bonding networks can dramatically alter inhibitor specificity (33). Comparative structural analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A) indicates that subtle variations in key residues (e.g., K565, Y610, Y612, M613, D679) likely underlie FRV’s preferential binding to FER. Further supporting this observation, molecular docking simulations indicate that FRV forms more stable interactions with FER’s ATP-binding pocket compared to these CrRLK1L members. These findings validate FRV as a potent FER-specific inhibitor and provide structural insights for designing selective inhibitors that target other RLKs.

FER is evolutionarily conserved in plant species ranging from algae to angiosperms and has largely propagated in land plants adapting to complex developmental and environmental changes (34, 35). Extensive research on FER has been conducted in Arabidopsis and other land plant species, including rice, pear, and brassica, among others (36–38). To further investigate the kinase domain of representative plant species, including Arabidopsis thaliana, Brassica napus, Brassica rapa, Oryza sativa, Zea mays, Solanum lycopersicum, Pyrus bretschneideri, Selaginella moellendorffii, Physcomitrium patens, and Marchantia polymorpha, we found that the critical amino acids responsible for FRV interactions were highly conserved (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). These results suggest that FRV may have broad applicability across land plant species.

FRV Facilitates the Dissection of FER Signalings in Phosphoproteomics.

Given that FER is involved in numerous biological processes and the lack of FER results in over one thousand differentially phosphorylated proteins (11), we expect to gain a comprehensive understanding of the FER kinase–dependent signaling pathways with the help of FRV as a powerful chemical tool. In previous phosphoproteomics of the seedlings treated with 1 μM RALF1 for 5 min, four abundant proteins were identified, including FER, AHA2, calcium-dependent protein kinase 9 (CAP9), and PEN3/ABCG36 transporter, and one decreased protein: FER homolog ERULUS (4). However, later on, studies have largely expanded our understanding of RALF1–FER pair-regulated signaling pathways, and therefore, deep phosphoproteomics analysis targeting FER kinase–dependent pathways is urgently needed. Hence, we performed a phosphoproteomics analysis focusing on FRV’s inhibition of RALF1 in 7-day-old Arabidopsis seedlings. After 1 μM RALF1 treatment for 30 min, a total of 769 up-regulated and 456 down-regulated phosphopeptides were identified, with 106 new phosphopeptides and 456 new dephosphopeptides appearing (Fig. 4A). In contrast, in samples with 1 μM FRV pretreatment, 460 up-regulated and 451 down-regulated phosphopeptides were identified, including 345 phosphopeptides and 313 dephosphorylated peptides that appeared after FRV pretreatment (Fig. 4A). Notably, 153 up-regulated and 169 down-regulated phosphoproteins overlap between FRV+RALF1/Control (CK) and the fer-4/wild type, as reported in the previous phosphoproteomics study (11), indicating the inhibitory effect of FRV on FER (Fig. 4B).

Based on the RALF1–FRV result, we confirm that RALF1 exerts its function through activation of FER kinase activity. Therefore, we focus on the phosphorylation of FER kinase. From the spectrometry data analysis, we detected two activation sites of FER, Ser695, and Ser866, that are phosphorylated by the RALF1 treatment (increased by 1.79- and 4.71-fold, respectively) and suppressed by the FRV treatment (Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B). Ser695 is located in the activation segment of the FER kinase domain, which has been identified in in vitro expressed FER-KD as one of the primary autophosphorylation sites and the S to A mutation of which does not influence the FER’s function in pollen tube reception (12, 26). Ser866 is located in close proximity to the previously reported 5-min RALF1-induced Ser871 and Ser874 in the C-terminal of FER (4). Furthermore, between RALF1/CK and RALF1/FRV+RALF1, we identified 345 up-regulated and 364 down-regulated overlapped phosphorylated proteins, which are supposed to be FER kinase–dependent downstream signaling components (Fig. 4D). Further Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of these differentially expressed phosphoproteins revealed the enrichment of multiple biological processes, such as establishment of localization in cell (GO: 0051649), cell growth (GO: 0016049), cell division (GO: 0051301), plant–pathogen interaction (GO: ath04626), regulation of defense response to fungus (GO: 1900150), acceptance of pollen (GO: 0060321), and responses to mechanical stimulus (GO: 0009612) (Fig. 4E). Moreover, through protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks analysis of the differential abundance phosphoproteins between RALF1/CK and RALF1/FRV+RALF1, we identified several proteins direct interacting with RALF–FER pair, including ROPGEF4, AHA2, RIPK, BIG5, ATL6, and BRG5, and the protein network involved in its secondary interactions, which provide valuable information for the future directions (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). Our further investigation on the phosphoproteomics found that the GO terms “cell growth” and “regulation of signal transduction” are enriched in the identified phosphopeptides and dephosphopeptides in FRV pretreatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D).

RALF1-FER Regulates Root Meristem Cell Expansion via AHA1/2S899-Mediated Alkalinization.

It has been revealed that most of the RALF family peptides function as negative regulators regarding cell expansion (39). As to the mutant phenotypes, the ralf1 mutant showed consistent longer root epidermal cells and longer primary root over the seedling stage (4). However, the phenotype of the fer-4 mutant is rather complicated. The fer-4 mutant exhibited longer primary roots than those of the wild type before day 6 but became shorter from day 7 onward (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). Although FER functions as the receptor for RALF1, it also participates in multiple phytohormone signaling pathways, including auxin, ethylene, brassinosteroid, abscisic acid, and jasmonic acid (6, 10, 40, 41). Regarding the root meristem cells, it has been revealed that auxin induces a transient alkalinization of the extracellular matrix, resulting in the inhibition of cellular elongation of the root meristem cells (42). Due to these discrepancies, we next focused on the cell expansion process of the root distal meristematic cells. Hereafter, the term root meristem cells is used to represent the epidermal cells in the distal meristem region (the last four cells of the meristem). We found that 1 μM RALF1 treatment significantly suppressed root meristem cell growth in wild-type seedlings, but not in the fer-4 mutant (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). Remarkably, the inhibitory effect of RALF1 was alleviated by the addition of 200 nM FRV, suggesting that FER kinase activity is required for this process (Fig. 5A).

Since the PPI analysis identified the central role of AHA1/2 in the RALF1–FER signaling network, we further examined the phosphoproteomics data and found that the phosphorylation levels of S899 in both AHA1 and AHA2 are up-regulated by RALF1 induction and suppressed by FRV treatment (Fig. 5B and SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). The Ser899 residue of AHA2 exhibits complex and multifunctional regulation of the proton pump (43), acting as an inhibitory site in many biological processes, including cell elongation, primary root growth, and immune responses (4, 44, 45), while also displaying potential activating effects under certain conditions (e.g., as observed in yeast studies) (46). This functional divergence across signaling pathways or species suggests its dynamic role in modulating H+ flux, warranting further investigation to elucidate its precise molecular mechanisms and biological significance. Consistently, the phosphorylation of the AHA2 S899 has been reported previously with 1 μM RALF1 treatment for 5 min (4). It has been established that the apoplastic pH acidification is highly associated with cell wall expansion and subsequent cellular growth in elongation cells (42, 47). Therefore, we further investigated the cell length of root meristem cells and found that the pAHA2:AHA2-eGFP aha2-4 transgenic plants exhibit no apparent distinctions compared to the wild type (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Then, we examined the primary root length of AHA2S899D transgenic lines which showed significantly reduced sensitivity to RALF1 treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). Moreover, we examined the length of the epidermal cells in the root meristem cells and observed similar insensitivity to the RALF1 treatment in the AHA2S899D transgenic plants and the fer-4 mutant (Fig. 5C). Consistently, the cell length of the elongation zone in AHA2S899D transgenic lines exhibited significantly reduced sensitivity to RALF1 treatment, which was dramatically repressed in the wild type and insensitive in the fer-4 mutant (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). Furthermore, we investigated the impact of RALF1 and FRV on the pH of the root rhizosphere. Using the bromocresol purple staining assay (48), we revealed that 1 μM RALF1 treatment led to the alkalinization of the seedling rhizosphere, which was notably inhibited by FRV at concentrations of 50 to 200 nM (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). These results manifest that RALF1 activates the kinase activity of FER and in turn leads to the phosphorylation of S899 in AHA1/2, which regulates the apoplastic pH change for the expansion of meristem cells. Furthermore, we examined the apoplastic pH change in the root meristem cells using a membrane-impermeable ratiometric pH indicator: 8-hydroxypyrene-1,3,6-trisulfonic acid (HPTS), which has been confirmed a reliable system for the study of apoplastic pH change-related root cell expansion (42). By analyzing the signal intensity ratio between the 458 nm and 405 nm channels using high-resolution laser confocal microscopy, we found that the AHA2S899D transgenic plants are insensitive to RALF1 in the apoplastic alkalization (Fig. 5D). We observed that the treatment with RALF1 significantly increased the apoplastic pH in the meristem cells, which is similar to that of the fer-4 mutant which was effectively suppressed by 200 nM FRV pretreatment (Fig. 5D). These findings demonstrate that the FRV small molecule effectively suppresses cell expansion regulated by RALF1-FER-AHA1/2 signaling.

To further investigate the functional importance of FER-KD residues (K565R, Y610A, Y612A, M613A, and D679A), we generated a series of FER mutants and introduced them into the fer-4 background. In this study, we performed the RALF1-induced primary root growth assay and found that Y612A/M613A/D679A triple mutant exhibited attenuated RALF1 responses, while the Y610A/D679A and K565R/D679A double mutants, Y610A/K565R/D679A triple mutant and K565R/Y610A/Y612A/M613A/D679A quintuple mutant showed insensitivity to RALF1 treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 E and S6 F). We further examined the meristematic and elongation zones of epidermal cells in Y610A, K565R, Y610A/D679A, and K565R/D679A mutants. Compared to the wild-type FER that could completely complement the fer-4 mutant, these transgenic plants of the FER-KD mutant conferred insensitivity to the RALF1 treatment (Fig. 5E and SI Appendix, Fig. S6G). To assess the functional contributions of residues FER Y612 and M613, we expressed and tested the response of the FER mutants to RALF1 in N. benthamiana leaves to determine pH. The results showed that Y610A, Y610A/Y612A, Y610A/M613A, and Y610A/Y612A/M613A mutants did not respond to RALF1, while Y612A and M613A mutants exhibited a response to RALF1 similar to the wild type (SI Appendix, Fig. S6H). Further pH assays on various FER mutant transgenic plants revealed that single mutations at Y610 and K565, as well as their corresponding double and higher-order mutants, remained insensitive to RALF1 (Fig. 5F). These results suggest that among the five key residues, Y610 and K565 appear most critical for RALF1 signaling, whereas Y612, M613, and D679 show lesser effects.

RALF1-FER Regulates Cell Expansion Via the Phosphorylation of FER-S695.

Phosphoproteomics data revealed that FER-S695 is a potential phosphorylation site induced by RALF1. To further support this finding, we treated pFER:FER-Flag seedlings with RALF1 and analyzed phosphorylation using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). To further investigate the in vivo function of S695 phosphorylation, we generated the pFER:FERS695A-GFP fer-4 transgenic plants and found that the phosphorylation abundance of FER-S695 was induced by RALF1 treatment (Fig. 6A). Further analysis using parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) revealed that the phosphorylation level of FER-S695 in the RALF1-treated group was 2.4 times higher than that in the control (Fig. 6B). We then pretreated the pFER:FER-GFP transgenic seedlings with 1 μM FRV for 3 h before subject to 1 μM RALF1 treatment for 30 min and performed FER-GFP immunoprecipitation. The FRV treatment effectively suppressed the RALF1-induced phosphorylation of FER (Fig. 6C). Moreover, using homemade pho-antibody of FER at Ser695 and Thr696, we found that RALF1 treatment significantly stimulated the phosphorylation level of FER in WT but not in FERS695A/T696A seedlings (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, we found that RALF1 treatment could not induce the meristem cell and elongation cell expansion in pFER:FERS695-GFP fer-4 transgenic plants as in pFER:FER-GFP fer-4 plants (Fig. 6 E and F). Similarly, no significant pH alteration was detected in the FERS695 mutant line (Fig. 6G). These findings identify S695 as an essential phosphorylation site in the FER-RALF1 signaling pathway that controls meristem cell expansion.

Data are presented as means ± SD. The significance was determined by one-way ANOVA with P < 0.01.

Discussion

The versatile receptor kinase FER regulates many biological processes through its kinase activity. In this study, we characterized a small-molecule tool, FRV, which effectively and selectively inhibited the FER kinase activity. We successfully identified the ATP-binding pocket through high-resolution cocrystal structure analysis of the FER kinase domain with FRV. Furthermore, we confirmed the involvement of key amino acids, including Lys565, Tyr610, Tyr612, Met613, and Asp679, in stabilizing the FER–FRV interaction using SPR assays. Our phenotypic analysis of transgenic mutants (assessed by primary root growth, cell expansion, and apoplastic pH) revealed that these residues are critical for FER activity, where positions 565 and 610 represent key functional amino acid sites in FER.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis was performed to evaluate the binding affinities of FRV across various important receptor kinases, including CrRLK1L family members. The results demonstrated that FRV’s binding affinity for FER was 48.5-fold higher than that for kinase ANJ and 38.3-fold higher than for kinase TMK4. FRV also showed significantly weaker and nearly undetectable binding to ANX1, BUPS1, and BRI1. This enhanced binding may arise from optimized steric complementarity or additional electrostatic contributions unique to FER’s active site. For example, molecular docking studies and structural comparisons reveal that although FRV occupies the ATP-binding pocket in both kinases, the FER–FRV cocrystal structure demonstrates a crucial molecular distinction: BRI1 lacks the essential hydrogen-bonding interaction between FRV and the conserved tyrosine residue at position Y612, which is prominently present in FER (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). This specific molecular interaction deficiency likely accounts for the significantly reduced binding affinity of FRV for BRI1 relative to FER. While FRV does exhibit some degree of binding to other receptor kinases, differences in binding affinity may suggest subtle structural variations among these proteins. In a recent study, a small-molecule library screening identified 4 compounds out of 1,494 molecules that demonstrated an inhibitory effect on the kinase activity of FER and its homologs, among which reversine and lavendustin A exhibited relative specificity for FER-CD while cenisertib and staurosporine also inhibited the kinase activities of the CrRLK1Ls (49). It is demonstrated that the treatment of roots with reversine, staurosporine, and lavendustin A enhanced innate immunity and alleviated soil-borne diseases but did not impact the growth of Arabidopsis, rice, and tobacco plants, even at a concentration of 5 μM. We then further conducted a more in-depth evaluation of the enzyme activity inhibition of reversine, which exhibits greater specificity toward FER compared to the other three inhibitors in the literature. The kinase activity assay revealed that FRV seems more effective than reversine in repressing the kinase activity of FER (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Compared to these inhibitors, FRV exhibited significantly higher binding affinity with the FER kinase domain, surpassing them by more than ten-folds. This could be the reason why FRV could exert an impact on the growth and development of plants at lower concentrations. In this study, we applied broad-spectrum inhibitors along with several candidate inhibitors to seedlings and evaluated their effects by measuring primary root length. Notably, FRV demonstrated superior efficacy in suppressing RALF1-induced root shortening compared to the broad-spectrum inhibitor K-252a and other candidate inhibitors (Brigatinib, AZD-7762, Tesevatinib, and IRAK-1-4 Inhibitor I), achieving significant inhibition at 100 nM (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). These results demonstrate a potent FER kinase inhibitor in planta, with nanomolar-range efficacy sufficient to suppress FER-mediated biological processes.

Notably, we revealed that RALF1 repressed the cell expansion of root meristem cells in a FER kinase activity–dependent manner and through the sites of Ser695 and Ser866, adding insights into the complex mechanism of FER kinase activation. We further investigated the function of FER Ser695 in transgenic plants and found that the FER S695A mutation resulted in a diminished response to RALF1, including effects on phosphorylation level and cell length. Therefore, Ser695 may represent a previously unrecognized phosphorylation site in the RALF1–FER signaling pathway. Kong et al. purified FER protein and LC-MS/MS analysis revealed that S695 is one of autophosphorylation sites in vitro. However, western blotting revealed that the autophosphorylation level of the S695A mutant was similar to the wild type, suggesting that S695 may not be a key site regulating FER autophosphorylation in vitro (26). Phosphorylation of residues within the activation loop serves as a critical regulatory mechanism for kinase activity. The S695 residue, located within this phospho-regulatory loop of FER, was found to complement the pollen tube reception defects of fer-1 mutant when mutated to alanine (S695A) (12). This observation suggests that phosphorylation of the activation loop may not be essential for FER function during pollen tube reception, implying that S695 likely exerts distinct functional roles in specific biological contexts. The phosphorylation of FER exhibits considerable complexity, and its S695 residues may represent critical phosphorylation sites involved in the downstream regulation of RALF1–FER signaling. Phosphorylation of FER-S866 increased by 4.71-fold under RALF1 treatment and was not detected in the FRV treatment for unknown reasons. Therefore, the function of FER-S866 has not been further investigated. It has been found that RALF1 induced the phosphorylation of Ser871, Ser874, and Ser858 in the C-terminal of FER (4). Although the mechanism of RALF1-induced phosphorylation of FER on downstream signaling is not fully understood, it is possible that S866, as well as the reported S871, S874, and S858 residues, is involved in the signaling by modulating C-terminal-related functions. Although the mechanisms by which FER phosphorylation regulates downstream signaling remain unclear, these findings together contribute to the understanding of the relationship between FER kinase activity and its function.

Small-molecule inhibitors have been successfully used in plant biology (19, 20, 50, 51). A crucial aspect is the involvement of FER kinase activity in pleiotropic biological processes, which has been a persistent challenge in research. One of the fundamental aims behind developing small-molecule inhibitors is to precisely apply these inhibitors locally to specific tissues and cells within plants at a certain time period, thereby accurately influencing the biological processes. Given that auxin critically regulates root meristem cell expansion, the fer-4 mutant exhibits auxin insensitivity (42), and RALF1 induces auxin synthesis within one hour to inhibit root cell elongation (44), the mechanism through which FER regulates apoplastic pH and meristem cell expansion remains intriguing. In this study, we engineered transgenic plants with a point mutation at the AHA2 Ser899 and conducted a thorough phenotypic analysis of RALF1 and FRV treatment root and root meristem cells. This approach allowed us to verify the specific mechanism by which AHA1/2 responds to the RALF1 signal and FER activation. AHA2 Ser899 plays a complex regulatory role in the proton pump activity, which has stimulated extensive mechanistic studies (43). Studies revealed that RALF1–FER-induced phosphorylation of AHA Ser899 down-regulates H+-ATPase function and plant growth in A. thaliana (4). As in auxin-mediated growth, this rapid apoplast alkalinization RALF1–FER induced H+ influx and reversibly inhibits primary root growth (44). These studies suggest that the AHA2 S899 site may be an inhibitory site, potentially playing a role in inhibiting H+ efflux or promoting H+ influx in different signaling pathways. However, since this site is not highly conserved within the plant AHA family, and the function of heterologous proteins varies in different biological processes—such as early studies have found that Ser899 in yeast may exhibit partial activation ability (46). In the present study, AHA2 S899 as a potential inhibitory phosphorylation site involved in regulating RALF–FER-mediated cell elongation. Therefore, the regulation of the proton pump by AHA2 S899 may have differential functions in different biological processes, and it is worthwhile for researchers to explore its underlying mechanisms. Given the broad roles of FER in plant development, FRV offers a potential tool to locally suppress specific FER-mediated processes. In reproductive organs, FRV application at the stigma may reduce ROS levels and facilitate interspecific pollen hydration, germination, and tube penetration in Brassicaceae, thereby overcoming reproductive barriers among species within this family and promoting distant hybridization (5, 36, 52). In vegetative tissues, FER exhibits distinct functional roles across different cell types (2, 6, 7). The localized application of FRV offers a selective approach to inhibit FER activity in specific organs, thereby enabling precise dissection of its contributions to various cellular processes. For instance, root-directed FRV treatment may modulate primary root elongation and root hair development, while leaf application could alter pavement cell morphogenesis. Moreover, FER modulates immune responses in a tissue-specific manner, with distinct regulatory patterns emerging for different pathogen classes (10, 53, 54). Targeted use of FRV could therefore help elucidate tissue-specific FER-mediated immune mechanisms.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions.

A. thaliana plants were maintained on a 22 °C, 16 h/20 °C, 8 h light/dark cycle at a relative humidity of 60% in the growth room. The wild-type Col-0 line is used as the control for other experiments. The fer-4 and fer-5 homozygous mutants were described previously (5, 6). Using the pCAMBIA1300 vector, FER mutations (K565R, Y610, Y613, M613, and D679) were introduced and fused with GFP, and used to construct various mutants. The Ser899 residue of AHA2 was mutated to aspartic acid (S899D) to generate the pAHA2:AHA2S899D-eGFP construct in the pCAMBIA1300 vector, which was subsequently transformed into the aha2-4 mutant.

Protein Expression and Purification.

The kinase domains of FER (residues 518 to 820; AT3G51550), TMK4 (residues 578 to 928; AT3G23750), BUPS1 (residues 462 to 878; AT4G39110), ANX1 (residues 451 to 850; AT3G04690), and BRI1 (residues 883-1158; AT4G39400) were cloned as N-terminally His-tagged recombinant proteins into the pFastBac vector for baculovirus expression. To express FER-KD, its mutants, and FER homologs, the Bac-to-Bac baculovirus expression system was used with SF9 cells at 27 °C following a standard protocol to express FER-KD and its mutants. After being infected with baculovirus for 48 h, cells were harvested via centrifugation and frozen at −80 °C until further use. The cell pellet was resuspended in the lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, and 10 mM imidazole and was sonicated on ice for 20 min for 5 s each at a power of 200 W, with a 5-s incubation between each sonication. Subsequently, the lysate was then clarified through centrifugation at 15,000×g for 30 min at 4 °C. Following the sonication and centrifugation steps as described, the cleared cell lysate was then loaded onto a Ni-NTA column (5 mL, GE Healthcare). The Ni-NTA column was washed with two column volumes of washing buffer (50 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, 3 mM TCEP, and 30 mM imidazole) and subsequently eluted with elution buffer (50 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, 3 mM TCEP, and 300 mM imidazole). The eluted protein was then concentrated using centrifugal concentrators (10 kDa, Millipore, UFC901096) and further purified through size-exclusion chromatography (Superdex 75 prep grade, GE Healthcare) using a buffer containing 50 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, and 3 mM TCEP. The FER (518-820) protein was concentrated to approximately 8 mg/mL in preparation for crystallization. To form the FER-KD-FRV complex, 8 mg/mL purified FER-KD was incubated with 10 mM FRV at 277 K for 1 h. The process of expressing FER mutants was the same as described above.

Data Collection and Structure Analysis.

An initial crystallization condition of the FER–FRV complex was obtained by screening commercial crystallization kits (Hampton Research, Aliso Viejo, CA) using the hanging-drop vapor-diffusion method. Optimized crystals were grown at 16 °C for 8 d by mixing 1 μL of a protein complex with 1 μL of reservoir solution composed of 0.3 M (NH4)2SO4, 0.1 M Bis-Tris (pH 7.0), 20% PEG 3350. The crystals with the best quality were directly immersed in a cryoprotectant solution (reservoir solution supplemented with 30% [v/v] glycerol) and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen at a temperature of 100 K. X-ray diffraction data were collected at beamline BL41XU using a 39 × 23 µm beam of the Super Photon Ring-8 (SPring-8) synchrotron facility (Japan). A single crystal was diffracted for 360° with a 0.2° oscillation angle with 1.0 Å wavelength X-rays. Data were indexed, integrated, and scaled using the program package HKL2000 (55). The statistics of the data collection are summarized in SI Appendix, Table S2.

ADP-Glo Kinase Assay.

All kinase activity assay mentioned in the main text was performed as follows. A luminescence assay was used to monitor the phosphotransferase activity of FER-KD, TMK4-KD, and BRI1-CD kinases. When activated, FER phosphorylated a peptide substrate (RRRVTSPARRS) by converting ATP to ADP. The kinase reaction was stopped, and unreacted ATP was removed using the ADP-Glo reagent. Kinase detection reagent was added to convert ADP back to ATP, which was then converted to a luminescent signal by the luciferase reaction (Promega, catalog No. V9102). The assay mixture contained 20 μL final volume of 50 mmol/L Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 1 mmol/L CaCl2, 5 mmol/L MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 0.05 mmol/L ATP (Promega, Cat#: V9102), 1 μM RALF1, 0.1 g/mL bovine serum albumin, and 6 ng/μL targeting proteins (FER-KD, BAK1-CD, TMK4-KD, and BRI1-CD). The kinase reaction was conducted at room temperature (25 to 27 °C) for 60 min. DMSO was used as a negative control for small-molecule inhibitors, and a system without ATP was used to measure background signals. Luminescence was measured using an EnVision microplate reader (PerkinElmer). The data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 software, fitting an inhibition curve to determine the IC50 value.

Root Cell Growth Assay.

To observe the length of root meristem and elongation cells, the seedlings were grown on 1/2 MS plates for four days (Fig. 5B) and 4.5 d (Fig. 4F). The seedlings were transferred to 1/2 MS liquid medium for 30 min pretreatment and then transferred to the liquid medium containing 200 nM FRV or mock solution for 3 h. The seedlings were then transferred to the liquid medium supplemented with 1 μM RALF1 and gently shaken for 16 h (Fig. 5 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A), 18 h (Figs. 5 F and G and 6E), or 12 h (Figs. 5D and 6F and SI Appendix, Fig. S6F). The seedlings were treated with 40 μg/ml Propidium Iodide staining buffer for 10 min and washed with ddH2O three times. The roots were then put on the slides and observed under the STELLARIS 8 confocal microscope (Leica). The equipment of the WLL laser and HyD2 detector were used to capture the images (Ex: 535 nm, Em: peak 617 nm) with a 20× objective. The distal meristematic root epidermal cells (meristematic cells that are about to elongate) were defined as the four cells below the first cell that is twice as long as it is wide and were chosen for measurement as described in the literature (42). ImageJ software was used to measure the length of the chosen cells in the vertical direction, and the average length of the four cells was recorded as the cell length for each root.

HPTS Staining of Root Cells.

For the RALF1 and FRV treatment, 4-d-old seedlings were placed in liquid 1/2 MS growth medium supplemented with 1 μM RALF1 treatment for 6 h with or without 200 nM FRV pretreatment for 2 h. In the N. benthamiana leaf system, the FER mutant proteins were introduced into N. benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium strain GV3101 for transient expression for 48 h, followed by treatment with or without 1 µM RALF1 for 30 min. The HPTS staining was performed by incubating the seedlings in 1/2 MS liquid growth medium containing 1 mM HPTS (from 100 mM water stock) for 15 min. The samples were subsequently rinsed with ddH2O three times, then mounted on a microscopy slide in the same growth medium supplemented with HPTS, and covered with a coverslip. Seedling images were captured using an Ultra High-Resolution Laser Leica STELLARIS 8 confocal microscope equipped with a WLL laser and HyD2 detector in line scanning mode (Ex 458 nm and 405 nm, Em 505 to 550 nm). A 40× objective lens was used for image acquisition. It is worth noting that no fluorescence signals were detected without staining to avoid interference from other fluorescence sources. TauGating and counting techniques were employed as capture modes to ensure accurate and specific detection. The acquired images were processed using ImageJ software, employing various tools such as Segmentation, Gaussian Blur (with a value of 1.5), and Background Subtraction (with a value of 20). To quantify the relative intensity of the fluorescence signals, the pixel values from the 458 nm channel were divided by the pixel values from the 405 nm channel. This ratio calculation was based on a previous study (42).
