Comparing the Perfusion and Functional Assessment Capabilities of Electrocardiography- and Photoplethysmography-monitored Phase-resolved Functional Lung and Dynamic Contrast-enhanced Perfusion MR Imaging
Authors: Yoshiyuki Ozawa, Alicia Palomar-García, Masanori Ozaki, Masao Yui, Maiko Shinohara, Natsuka Yazawa, Kaori Yamamoto, Yuichiro Sano, Masato Ikedo, Takahiro Ueda, Masahiko Nomura, Takeshi Yoshikawa, Daisuke Takenaka, Yoshiharu Ohno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe present study directly compared the quantitative capabilities of regional perfusion and pulmonary functional change assessments among electrocardiography (ECG)- and photoplethysmography (PPG)-monitored phase-resolved functional lung (PREFUL) MRI and dynamic contrast-enhanced (CE) perfusion MRI in thoracic oncologic patients.MethodsSeventeen thoracic oncologic patients prospectively underwent ECG- and PPG-monitored PREFUL MRI, dynamic CE-perfusion MRI, and pulmonary function tests. ECG- and PPG-monitored perfusion-weighted PREFUL MRI (PW-MRI) and quantitative perfusion maps from dynamic CE-perfusion MRI were generated. Regional perfusions were determined using ROI measurements. For each patient, the overall perfusion from each method was determined as the average ROI measurement value. To determine the relationship between regional perfusion among all methods, Pearson’s correlations were performed. Tukey’s honest significant difference test was performed to compare regional perfusion among ventral, middle, and dorsal slice positions on ECG- and PPG-monitored PW-MRI and quantitative perfusion maps. To assess the pulmonary functional loss evaluation capability of each MRI method, each overall perfusion was correlated with %VC and %FEV1 using Pearson’s correlation.ResultsThe correlation of regional perfusion between ECG- and PPG-monitored PW-MRI was significant and good (r = 0.79, P < 0.0001). However, the correlations between ECG- or PPG-monitored PW-MRI and quantitative perfusion maps were assessed as significant and fair (ECG: r = 0.4, P < 0.0001; PPG: r = 0.36, P < 0.0001). ECG- and PPG-monitored PW-MRI demonstrated significantly higher perfusion than the quantitative perfusion map (P < 0.0001). Furthermore, ECG-and PPG-monitored PW-MRI and quantitative perfusion maps had significant and moderate correlations (%VC: 0.60 ≤ r ≤ 0.63, P < 0.05; %FEV1: 0.52 ≤ r ≤ 0.69, P < 0.05).ConclusionECG- and PPG-monitored PREFUL MRI had similar potential to dynamic CE-perfusion MRI for quantitatively assessing regional perfusion and pulmonary functional changes in thoracic oncologic patients. Furthermore, PPG-monitored PREFUL MRI showed little difference in regional perfusion evaluation compared with ECG-monitored PREFUL MRI and the potential to play a complementary role in this setting.

Introduction

One of the major roles of the lungs is to facilitate gas exchange between the circulatory system and external environment. Gas exchange occurs in the lungs between the alveolar air and blood of the pulmonary capillaries. For effective gas exchange, alveoli must be ventilated and perfused. Ventilation (V) refers to the flow of air into and out of the alveoli, whereas perfusion (Q) refers to the flow of blood to the alveolar capillaries. Collective changes in ventilation and perfusion in the lungs were measured clinically using the ratio of ventilation to perfusion (V/Q). Changes in the V/Q ratio can affect gas exchange and contribute to hypoxemia. Therefore, V, Q, and V/Q ratio assessments are important for the diagnosis, monitoring, and therapy response in various lung diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cystic fibrosis (CF), asthma, interstitial lung disease (ILD), pulmonary thromboembolic disease, and pulmonary hypertension. Non–contrast-enhanced (non-CE-) and CE- perfusion MRI have been quantitatively and qualitatively tested, and their clinical potential in various pulmonary diseases has been determined in the last few decades, and both techniques have been suggested as useful for lung diseases based on experts’ opinions.1–7

Since 2018, phase-resolved functional lung (PREFUL) MRI has been a proton-based, contrast agent–free technique derived from the Fourier decomposition (FD) approach to quantitatively or qualitatively measure regional V and Q dynamics during free breathing in different pulmonary diseases.8–21 Furthermore, different PREFUL pulse sequences and image registration methods have been compared and validated by different investigators, field strengths, institutions, and vendors.14,16,17,22–27 Currently, the major published FD or PREFUL MRI techniques apply the non-electrocardiography (non-ECG) method for sorting the cardiac phase for all images by signal fluctuation of the main pulmonary artery by proprietary software.8–27 However, no study has applied ECG or photoplethysmography (PPG) and reported the influence of different methods on regional perfusion or pulmonary functional change evaluations between these 2 methods on perfusion-weighted PREFUL MRI (PW-MRI) in patients with and without lung diseases. We hypothesized that ECG- and PPG-monitored PREFUL MRIs have similar potential for quantitatively assessing regional perfusion and pulmonary functional changes and play a complementary role in dynamic CE-perfusion MRI.

The purpose of this study was to directly compare the quantitative capabilities of regional perfusion and pulmonary functional change assessments among ECG- and PPG-monitored PREFUL MRI and dynamic CE perfusion MRI in thoracic oncologic patients.

Materials and Methods

Protocol, support, and funding

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Fujita Health University Hospital and was compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. Written informed consent was waived. This study was technically or financially supported by Canon Medical Systems Corporation and JSPS KAKENHI Grant Number JP 25K10970.

Eight of the authors (A. PG., M.O., M.Y., M.S., N.Y., K.Y., Y.S., and M.I.) are employees of Canon Medical Systems Spain and Portugal or Canon Medical Systems Corporation but do not have control over any of the data and information submitted for publication or over which data and information were to be included in this study.

All data were controlled and statistically analyzed by Y.O., T.U., M.N., T.Y., D.T., and Y.Oh.

Subjects

From January 2024 to May 2024, a total of 23 pathologically diagnosed thoracic oncologic patients who were potential candidates for surgical treatment and underwent ECG- and PPG-monitored PREFUL MRI, dynamic CE-perfusion MRI, and pulmonary function tests before surgical treatment were included in this study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) an incomplete MRI examination; (b) contraindications to MRI (e.g. claustrophobia, pacemaker, or gadolinium [Gd] contrast media); (c) a history of lung surgery based on other lung malignancies; and (d) any other severe comorbidities that could limit imaging. Six out of 23 patients were excluded based on the above-mentioned criteria as follows: criteria (a) (n = 1) due to feeling unwell, criteria (b) (n = 2) due to claustrophobia (n = 1) and pacemaker (n = 1), criteria (c) (n = 2) because of surgical treatment of lung metastases due to colon cancer (n = 1), breast cancer (n = 1), and criteria (d) due to cardiac dysfunction (n = 1). Ultimately, 17 patients were enrolled in the study. The patient selection flowchart for this study is shown in Fig. 1.

MRI examinations

All MRI examinations were performed using a 1.5 Tesla (T) MRI system (Vantage Orian; Canon Medical Systems, Tochigi, Japan) with 16-channel phased-array coils with parallel imaging capability (SPEEDER; Canon Medical Systems).

For the PREFUL MRI acquisitions, 2D fast field-echo (FFE) sequences with a 500 × 500 mm2 FOV, 15 mm slice thickness, slice gap 15 mm, 128 × 128 matrix size interpolated to 256 × 256, reconstruction voxel size 2.0 × 2.0 × 15 mm3, 0.82 msec TE, 3 msec TR, flip angle 5°, number of excitations (NEX) 1, reduction factor 2, and number of segments 1 were used. In addition, ECG/PPG trigger information was acquired simultaneously. Three slices were obtained for each PREFUL MRI scan.

For dynamic CE-perfusion MRI with first-pass perfusion technique, a 3D FFE sequence with a 500 × 350 mm2 FOV, 8.5 mm slice thickness, 64 × 256 matrix size interpolated to 512 × 512, reconstruction voxel size 0.7 × 0.7 × 4.25 mm3, 1.1-msec TE, 3.1-msec TR, flip angle 40°, NEX 1, reduction factor 2, and number of segments 2 was used. The dynamic series number of PREFUL MRI was 256, and that of dynamic CE-perfusion MRI was 20. Gd contrast medium (0.1 mL/kg) (Magnescope, gadoterate meglumine [Gd-DOTA]; Guerbet Japan, Tokyo, Japan) was administered to all patients via the cubital vein with an automatic infusion system (Sonic shot; Nemoto Kyorindo, Tokyo, Japan) at a rate of 5 mL/s, followed by 20 mL of saline solution at the same rate. The dynamic first-pass CE-MRI sequence was initiated at the time of the contrast medium injection. Before the MRI studies, patients were carefully instructed in the breath-holding technique and practiced reproducing exactly the same degree of inspiration for each scan series. The mean acquisition times for PREFUL MRI and dynamic CE-perfusion MRI were 150s and 42s, respectively. PREFUL MRI was performed under free-breathing, and dynamic CE-perfusion MRI was requested for cases requiring more than 28s of breath-holding and acquired by breath-holding for as long as possible with 100% oxygen administration in each patient.

Clinical information

The following clinical information was obtained by searching electronic medical records: the age, sex, clinical diagnosis of thoracic tumor or underlying lung disease, and pulmonary function test results obtained within less than 2 weeks (mean ± standard deviation [SD]: 5 ± 3 days) of the MRI examination. For each patient, the percentage of vital capacity (%VC) and percentage of predicted forced expiratory volume in 1s (%FEV1) were assessed according to the American Thoracic Society guidelines.28 The FVC was expressed as the volume (L) and percentage (%) of the predicted normal values.

Quantitative regional pulmonary perfusion assessments

PW-MRI from PREFUL MRI data

All ECG- and PPG-monitored PREFUL post-processing was performed on a workstation with 754 GB of RAM and an Intel Xeon Gold 6254 CPU using an in-house software program developed in Python 3.11.8. A board-certified chest radiologist with 32 years’ experience (Y.Oh) then quantitatively assessed regional perfusion maps from ECG- and PPG-PREFUL-MRI data as ECG- and PPG-monitored PW-MRI based on pixel-by-pixel analyses at 3 slices per patient. Figure 2 shows a flow chart of PREFUL post-processing. First, registration for all images was performed using a group-oriented registration approach29 and Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs). Second, to sort the images by cardiac phase, we applied a different approach from the original one. The original method sorts the cardiac phase for all images by signal fluctuation of the main pulmonary artery; however, we applied cardiac trigger information from ECG or PPG to sort the images by cardiac delay. Therefore, accurate sorts of ECG- and PPG-monitored PREFUL MRI examination are enabled to calculate pulmonary perfusion assessment from entire cardiac phase evaluation, although the trigger delays of ECG and PPG are different in each patient or between patients. Third, regarding the quantitative perfusion map calculation, Qnorm was calculated as a percentage by normalizing the signal intensity of each voxel to that of a fully blood-filled voxel obtained from an ROI11: [1]Qnorm=Q/SBlood

where Q corresponds to the signal amplitude of the lung parenchymal signal within the cardiac frequency range, and SBlood is the corresponding signal amplitude of a fully blood-filled voxel. Quantitative perfusion (unit: mL/min/100 mL) was calculated using the formula below [2]. [2]QuantitativePerfusion=Qnorm* (1/tperf)

where tperf is the time between 2 heartbeats calculated by cardiac trigger information.

Dynamic CE-perfusion MRI

All dynamic CE-perfusion MRI data were transferred to a personal computer (Precision; DELL, Kanagawa, Japan), and automated 3D motion corrections were performed using in-house software programs. The details have been published in a number of technical papers.30–32 The same board-certified chest radiologist also generated a 3D quantitatively assessed pulmonary perfusion map by pixel-by-pixel analyses from dynamic CE-perfusion MRI data based on the dual-input maximum slope method, selected based on past study results.30–32

Image analyses

After generating quantitatively assessed ECG-monitored PW-MRI and pulmonary perfusion maps from dynamic CE-perfusion MRI examinations in each patient, these data were transferred to a personal computer (Precision; DELL) for the evaluation of regional perfusion with automated spatial matching using the same in-house software program.30–32 By reviewing thin-section CT data as well as PW-MRI and quantitative perfusion map, circular ROIs with areas of 1000 mm2 were placed by the same board-certified chest radiologist at the central zone, excluding large vessels and peripheral lung zone 10 mm away from the chest wall in the upper, middle, and lower lung fields in both lungs at 3 matched slices (total 36 ROIs =2 ROIs × 3 lung fields × 2 lungs × 3 slices) on a quantitative perfusion map from dynamic CE-perfusion MRI in each patient. All ROIs were automatically and computationally copied on all slices of ECG- and PPG-monitored PW-MRI. On a quantitative perfusion assessment using 3 MRI methods, all evaluated perfusion values were averaged, and the overall perfusion in each patient was determined.

Statistical analyses

To determine the relationship between regional perfusion among all methods, Pearson’s correlations were performed for the entire lung, central lung zone, and peripheral lung zone.

To compare regional perfusion among ventral, middle, and dorsal slice positions on ECG- and PPG-monitored PW-MRI and quantitative perfusion maps, Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) test was performed for the entire lung, central lung zone, and peripheral lung zone. Furthermore, regional perfusion in the entire lung, central lung zone, and peripheral lung zone was also compared among all methods within the entire lung and at the ventral, middle, and dorsal slice positions using Tukey’s HSD test.

For regional perfusion difference evaluations among all MRI methods, the mean difference and the limits of agreement of regional perfusion between each of the 2 MRI methods were assessed using Bland–Altman analyses of the entire lung, central lung zone, and peripheral lung zone.33

To assess the pulmonary functional loss evaluation capability of each MRI method, each overall perfusion was correlated with %VC and %FEV1 using Pearson’s correlation.

Statistical significance was set at < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using commercially available statistical software programs (JMP version 14: SAS Institute Japan, Tokyo, Japan; StatMate V: Nihon 3 B Scientific, Niigata, Japan) or a free statistical software program (EZR version 1.54: https://www.jichi.ac.jp/saitama-sct/SaitamaHP files/statmedEN.html).34

Results

A representative case is shown in Fig. 3. All 17 patients performed ECG- and PPG-monitored PREFUL MRI and dynamic CE-perfusion MRI examinations in this study, and none of them had arrythmias.

Table 1 shows the patient characteristics of this study cohort, which comprised 17 consecutive patients with thoracic tumors: 11 males (age range, 45–80 years old; mean age, 63 years old) and 6 females (age range, 39–86 years old; mean age, 66 years old). Pathologic examination results led to a diagnosis of invasive adenocarcinoma in 7 subjects, adenocarcinoma with other subtypes in 4, thymoma in 4, squamous cell carcinoma in 1, and large cell carcinoma in 1. The study cohort consisted of 1 stage IA1, 6 stage IA2, 4 stage IA3, 1 stage IIA, and 1 stage IIB non–small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) case as well as 4 thymoma cases, including 3 thymoma stage I and 1 thymoma stage II. Six out of 17 patients had underlying lung diseases, such as interstitial lung abnormalities (ILA, n = 3), pulmonary emphysema (n = 1), and interstitial lung disease (ILD, n = 2), based on collagen vascular disease-related interstitial lung diseases (CVD-ILDs) due to dermatomyositis (DM, n = 1) and systemic sclerosis (SSc, n = 1).

Correlations of regional perfusion between the 2 MRI methods in the entire lung, central lung zone, and peripheral lung zone are shown in Table 2. When ROIs were assessed within the entire lung or central and peripheral lung zones, the correlation of regional perfusion between ECG- and PPG-monitored PW-MRI was significant and good (entire lung: r = 0.79, P < 0.0001; central lung zone: r = 0.8, P < 0.0001; peripheral lung zone: r = 0.79, P < 0.0001). However, correlations of regional perfusion between ECG- or PPG-monitored PW-MRI and quantitative perfusion maps were assessed as significant and fair (ECG: entire lung, r = 0.4, P < 0.0001; central lung zone, r = 0.41, P < 0.0001; peripheral lung zone, r = 0.39, P < 0.0001; PPG: entire lung, r = 0.36, P < 0.0001; central lung zone, r = 0.33, P < 0.0001; and peripheral lung zone, r = 0.33, P < 0.0001).

Table 3 shows the comparison of regional perfusion among ventral, middle, and dorsal slice positions for each method and among the 3 methods at all slice positions in the entire lung, central lung zone, and peripheral lung zone. When comparing the regional perfusion of each method among the 3 slice positions, the quantitative perfusion map showed significant differences among the ventral, middle, and dorsal slice positions in the entire lung, central lung zone, and peripheral lung zone (P < 0.0001). A comparison of regional perfusion among the 3 methods at each slice position in the entire lung, central lung zone, and peripheral lung zone for ECG- and PPG-monitored PW-MRI demonstrated significantly higher perfusion than the quantitative perfusion map at each slice in the entire lung and peripheral lung zone and at the ventral position in the central lung zone (P < 0.0001).

The mean differences and limits of agreement of regional perfusion between 2 of 3 MRI methods for the entire lung, central lung zone, and peripheral lung zone are shown in Table 4. In the entire lung, central lung zone and peripheral lung zone, mean differences between ECG- and PPG-monitored PW-MRI (entire lung: −7.7 ± 2.1 mL/100 mL/min, central lung zone: −7.8 ± 3.0 mL/100 mL/min, peripheral lung zone: −7.5 ± 3.0 mL/100 mL/min) were smaller than those between each PW-MRI and quantitative perfusion map (ECG-monitored PW-MRI: entire lung, 47.9 ± 3.6 mL/100 mL/min, central lung zone, 25.9 ± 5.4 mL/100 mL/min, peripheral lung zone, 69.9 ± 4.3 mL/100 mL/min; PPG-monitored PW-MRI: entire lung, 47.9 ± 3.6 mL/100 mL/min, central lung zone, 33.7 ± 5.6 mL/100 mL/min, peripheral lung zone, 77.5 ± 3.9 mL/100 mL/min). The limits of agreement between ECG- and PPG-monitored PW-MRI were determined from −109.2 mL/100 mL/min to 93.8 mL/100 mL/min in the entire lung, from −109.8 mL/100 mL/min to 94.2 mL/100 mL/min in the central lung zone, and from −108.6 mL/100 mL/min to 93.6 mL/100 mL/min in the peripheral lung zone. Conversely, the limits of agreement between ECG-monitored PW-MRI and quantitative were assessed from −126.3 mL/100 mL/min to 222.1 mL/100 mL/min in the entire lung, from −148.3 mL/100 mL/min to 200.1 mL/100 mL/min in the central lung zone, and from −79.1 mL/100 mL/min to 218.9 mL/100 mL/min in the peripheral lung zone. Furthermore, the limits of agreement between PPG-monitored PW-MRI and quantitative were assessed from −126.3 mL/100 mL/min to 222.1 mL/100 mL/min in the entire lung, from −159.5 mL/100 mL/min to 226.9 mL/100 mL/min in the central lung zone, and from −55.1 mL/100 mL/min to 210.1 mL/100 mL/min in the peripheral lung zone.

The correlations between overall perfusion for each perfusion method and %VC or %FEV1 are shown in Fig. 4. When the overall perfusion correlated with %VC and %FEV1 in all patients, ECG- and PPG-monitored PW-MRI and the quantitative perfusion map showed significant and moderate correlations with them (ECG: %VC, r = 0.60, P = 0.01, %FEV1, r = 0.69, P = 0.002; PPG: %VC, r = 0.62, P = 0.007, %FEV1, r = 0.58, P = 0.02; quantitative perfusion map: %VC, r = 0.63, P = 0.006, %FEV1, r = 0.52, P = 0.03).

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that ECG- and PPG-monitored PREFUL MRI had similar potential to dynamic CE-perfusion MRI for quantitatively assessing regional perfusion and pulmonary functional change assessments and may play a complementary role in thoracic oncologic patients. Furthermore, when comparing quantitatively assessed regional perfusion among all 3 methods, each PREFUL MRI showed little difference and significantly overestimated regional perfusion compared to dynamic CE-perfusion MRI in each gravitational position in the entire lung, central lung zone, or peripheral lung zone. To our knowledge, this study is the first to directly compare among ECG- and PPG-monitored PREFUL MRI and dynamic CE-perfusion MRI, which has been widely applied for academic as well as clinical purposes in the last few decades.1–32

When correlated or compared quantitatively, regional perfusion in the entire lung, central lung zone, and peripheral lung zones, as well as these areas in the gravitational direction among ECG- and PPG-monitored PW-MRI from PREFUL MRI and quantitative perfusion map from dynamic CE-perfusion MRI, ECG- and PPG-monitored PW-MRI showed significant and good correlations. However, each PW-MRI method had a significant but fair correlation with the quantitative perfusion map. Furthermore, ECG- and PPG-monitored PW-MRI demonstrated little regional perfusion differences and significantly higher regional perfusion than quantitative perfusion maps in each area or position; however, regional variations in quantitatively assessed 3D dynamic CE-perfusion MRI have been well known and suggested by the influence of Gd contrast media since the first publication of this technique.35 In addition, when the mean differences and limits of agreement of regional perfusion between each of the 2 MRI methods were assessed, both indices between ECG- and PPG-monitored PW-MRI were smaller than those between each PW-MRI method and quantitative perfusion map from dynamic CE-perfusion MRI in the entire lung, central lung zone, and peripheral lung zone. Considering the underlying methodological differences between quantitatively assessed PREFUL MRI and dynamic CE-perfusion MRI, these results are not surprising. When comparing these 2 methods, they used different traces to obtain MRI data and applied different mathematical models to quantitatively assess regional perfusion within the entire lung. As tracers, PREFUL MRI uses endogenous protons, whose molecular size, weight, and physical properties are markedly different from those of Gd contrast media with different pharmacological structures. Moreover, the relationship between Gd contrast agent dose and signal intensity change on dynamic CE-perfusion MRI is not linear at high concentrations. Therefore, difference in tracer, their kinetic difference, the influence of gravity, susceptibility, MRI sequence, and image analysis methods, including mathematical models for quantification, affected our study results. Further studies are warranted to standardize these techniques and reduce their influence.

In contrast to the above-mentioned study results, all overall perfusions had significant and moderate correlations with them and were considered to have little difference in pulmonary functional change assessment among ECG- and PPG-monitored PW-MRI and quantitative perfusion map from dynamic CE-perfusion MRI. Therefore, ECG- and PPG-monitored PW-MRI may have similar potential and play a complementary role in dynamic CE-perfusion MRI to assess pulmonary functional changes for different purposes, although further investigation is warranted.

Several limitations associated with the present study warrant mention. First, the study population was small, and the number of underlying lung diseases was small. Moreover, pulmonary functional loss in this study did not vary. Therefore, these factors may have affected our study results. In addition, both lung cancer and mediastinal tumor patients whose surgical strategies differed substantially between these entities, particularly regarding the extent of lung tissue loss were included in this study. Therefore, clinical significance of regional perfusion assessment was not uniform, and this heterogeneity was also considered as one of the limitations in this study. Second, both PREFUL MRI approaches involved 2D sequences and obtained only 3 sections with different section thicknesses and gaps as well as acquisition and reconstruction matrices, compared with 3D dynamic CE-perfusion MRI. Therefore, regional perfusion measurements were not fully matched. We also placed each ROI while trying to exclude the large vessels. However, regional perfusion from vessels larger than the pulmonary capillary bed could not be excluded, as seen in previously published pulmonary perfusion studies involving not only MRI but also other modalities. Moreover, lobe-specific perfusion analyses for postoperative lung function prediction had not been performed in this study. Therefore, the results of the present study were affected by these factors, and further study for lobe-specific perfusion analysis is warranted. Third, only %VC and %FEV1 were compared with each overall perfusion, although other pulmonary function test parameters were not correlated with overall perfusion in all methods. In addition, the %VC and %FEV1 changes in the study cohort were relatively small. Therefore, the clinical potential of each method for pulmonary functional changes was limited. Fourth, this study compared the potential of ECG- and PPG-monitored PREFUL MRI with dynamic CE-perfusion MRI and did not directly compare with original PREFUL MRI as well as other perfusion imaging approaches, including MRI, CT, and nuclear medicine studies with different traces and methods. This study also only used a 1.5T scanner from a single vendor and did not evaluate other 1.5- and 3T scanners from the same vendor or other vendors. Moreover, lung perfusion SPECT remained a standard modality for regional pulmonary perfusion assessment, and regional perfusion was not directly compared among ECG- and PPG-monitored PREFUL MRI, dynamic CE-perfusion MRI and perfusion single-photon emission tomography (SPECT). Therefore, this fact was also considered as one of the limitations, and further comparative study among these methods is warranted in future study. Furthermore, ventilation-weighted imaging from ECG- and PPG-monitored PREFUL MRI has not been compared with other ventilation imaging techniques, including other MRI techniques such as hyperpolarized noble gas MRI, oxygen-enhanced MRI, etc., or with CT or nuclear medicine studies. Fifth, this study applied previously published mathematical models for the quantitative assessment of regional perfusion from PREFUL MRI and dynamic CE-perfusion MRI; other models for PREFUL MRI or dynamic CE-perfusion MRI were not tested.11,13,14,17,25,32,35

Therefore, further investigations with larger study cohorts with different pulmonary diseases and pulmonary functional changes, different MRI sequences, and MRI scanners with different field strengths, vendors, and institutions are warranted to demonstrate the clinical relevance of PPG-monitored PREFUL MRI in the near future.

In conclusion, ECG- and PPG-monitored PREFUL MRI had similar potential to dynamic CE-perfusion MRI for quantitatively assessing regional perfusion and pulmonary functional changes in thoracic oncologic patients. Furthermore, PPG-monitored PREFUL MRI showed little difference in regional perfusion evaluation compared with ECG-monitored PREFUL MRI and the potential to play a complementary role in this setting.
