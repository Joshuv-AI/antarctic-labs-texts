KLB as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in colorectal cancer: insights from multi omics and single cell analyses
Authors: Xiaofei Bi, Wenjin Zhang, Meimei Shen, Guicheng Wu, Chengmei Fang, Jian Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common gastrointestinal malignancies, with a rising global incidence. Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, effective biomarkers for early detection and targeted therapy remain limited. β-Klotho (KLB), a co-receptor in the FGF signaling pathway, has been implicated in tumor progression and poor prognosis in several cancers. However, its role in CRC is still poorly understood.MethodsTo investigate the function of KLB in CRC, we performed a comprehensive pan-cancer analysis using datasets from TCGA, GTEx, Human Protein Atlas, cBioPortal, UALCAN, and TIMER2.0. Analyses included KLB expression profiles, promoter methylation, genetic alterations, immune checkpoint associations, and immune cell infiltration. Functional enrichment was conducted via GO/KEGG and GSEA. A prognostic model was constructed using biostatistical methods. In vitro and in vivo assays—including CCK-8, colony formation, wound healing, and subcutaneous tumor formation in mice—were conducted to evaluate the biological effects of KLB on CRC cells. Additionally, single-cell RNA sequencing data were analyzed to explore the cell-type-specific expression of KLB within the tumor microenvironment.ResultsKLB expression was significantly downregulated in CRC tissues compared to normal tissues and negatively correlated with TNM stage and overall prognosis. Promoter methylation and somatic mutations were observed in CRC, suggesting epigenetic and genetic regulation of KLB. Functional enrichment revealed that KLB is involved in regulating the cell cycle, chromatin remodeling, and immune responses. The prognostic nomogram based on KLB expression showed improved predictive performance for progression-free interval (C-index = 0.778). Experimentally, KLB overexpression inhibited CRC cell proliferation and migration. Single-cell RNA-seq analysis demonstrated that KLB is primarily expressed in fibroblasts and stromal cells within the CRC tumor microenvironment, implicating its role in intercellular communication and immune modulation through pathways such as MIF and MK signaling.ConclusionKLB functions as a potential tumor suppressor in CRC, with diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic implications. Its expression in stromal components of the tumor microenvironment suggests a regulatory role in immune response and tumor progression. These findings support KLB as a promising biomarker and therapeutic target for CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03326-0.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a major public health challenge, with the third-highest incidence rate and second-highest mortality rate among various cancers, according to a global analysis in 2020 [1]. The pathogenesis of CRC is complex and in-fluenced by genetic and environmental factors, among which genetic and epigenetic alterations are involved in regulating the development of CRC [2, 3]. CRC development relies on multiple pathways, of which the three most critical are chromosomal instability (CIN), microsatellite instability (MSI), and CpG island methylator phenotype pathways [4]. The complex pathogenesis and developmental pathways have led to the classification of CRC into distinct molecular subtypes by transcriptomics, with significant differences in the prognosis and response to treatment of patients [5]. The high heterogeneity of CRC and the difficulty of early diagnosis have serious implications for patient treatment and survival. Effective biomarkers, including RAS mutation status, MSI, and mismatch repair, provide an effective and important aid in the early diagnosis of CRC, but they still have limitations [6]. Standard diagnostic techniques, such as colonoscopy, are effective in detecting the early stages of CRC, but they can be painful and risky for patients [7]. Therefore, identifying novel valuable biomarkers, exploring their possible therapeutic potential, and establishing stable prognostic models are essential for a deeper understanding of CRC pathogenesis and for improving CRC treatment.

β-Klotho (KLB) encodes a one-way transmembrane protein involved in the fibro-blast growth factor (FGF) receptor pathway as a high affinity receptor for FGF 19 and FGF21 [8]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) play an important role in the development of multiple cancers, including CRC, and interfere with the immune response in tumors, primarily through regulation of immune cell infiltration and function that affects the tumor microenvironment (TME) [9, 10]. Several studies have implicated that CAFs are associated with three key pathways in CRC development. By activating microsatellites to stabilize CAFs in CRC, Achaetscute family BHLH transcription factor 2 induces dysregulation of the immune response in the TME [11]. In prostate cancer, CAFs have been found to release cytokines to inducing genomic methylation changes required for epithelialmesenchymal transition [12]. Additionally, CIN in CAFs causes telomere length abnormalities, increased invasion and poor prognosis in some cancers [13, 14]. Studies have confirmed the role of KLB in the maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity in intestinal epithelial cells [15]. Furthermore, KLB-mediated inhibition of FGF19 signaling reduces tumor cell proliferation in CRC [16]. However, the specific function of KLB in various cancers, especially CRC, remains unclear and its underlying mechanisms have not been explored. KLB expression is directly correlated with progression free survival and overall survival (OS) in patients with nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Studies have also revealed that upregulation of KLB expression inhibits A549 cell growth and induces G1/S phase arrest and apoptosis [17]. In contrast, studies in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) have indicated that KLB expression is elevated in patients, and that inhibition of KLB in Huh7 cells instead slows cell growth, which in turn reduces the activity of the FGF receptor 4 (FGFR4) signaling pathway [18]. In summary, the expression and biological functions of KLB in different tumors may not be consistent. Combining the function of KLB itself with the importance of CAFs in cancer development, we suggest that the role of KLB in CRC needs to be further investigated.

In this study, we investigated KLB expression profiles across tumor types in pancancer datasets from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Genotype Tissue Expression (GTEx) databases [19]. Additionally, our study evaluated the correlation of KLB expression and gene mutations with tumor clinical stage, patient prognosis and TME. We further explored the potential mechanism of KLB in CRC tumor samples by functional enrichment analysis. To gain deeper insights into the cellular context of KLB function, we utilized single cell RNA sequencing to characterize KLB expression patterns across different cell types within the CRC tumor microenvironment and explore associated intercellular communication networks. We also developed a prediction model and verified its accuracy through in vitro and in vivo experiments. In conclusion, KLB may be an important indicator of clinical outcomes, especially in the early diagnosis and targeted therapy of CRC.

Materials and methods

Differential expression analysis of KLB

We collected KLB expression data of healthy samples and immunohistochemical data from the Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) [20]. Subsequently, differential expression of KLB expression among cancers and controls was performed on control data from GTEx with tumor data from TCGA (https://www.cancer.gov/tcga). All full names and corresponding abbreviations of 33 cancer types were obtained from TCGA. We conducted log2 transformation on RNA sequencing TPM data and further analyzed. We used the R software (version 3.6.3) to analyze data and create visualizations with the help of the package “ggplot2” (version 3.3.3).

Survival prognosis analysis

To comprehensively evaluate the prognostic significance of KLB in cancer patients, we implemented a systematic survival analysis pipeline. RNA sequencing FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) data were first subjected to preprocessing and log2 transformation to normalize expression values and reduce technical variation. We selected three complementary survival metrics to assess the correlation between KLB expression and patient clinical outcomes: overall survival (OS), representing the time from diagnosis to death from any cause; disease free survival (DFS), measuring the time from initial treatment to disease recurrence; and progression free interval (PFI), indicating the time from initial treatment to disease progression or recurrence. These metrics were chosen to capture different aspects of cancer progression and treatment response. For statistical analysis, we employed both Cox proportional hazards regression models to estimate hazard ratios and assess the independent prognostic value of KLB expression, and Kaplan Meier (KM) survival curves to visualize survival differences between patient groups stratified by KLB expression levels. All bioinformatics analyses and result visualizations were performed using a comprehensive suite of R packages, including “survival” for survival analysis functions, “survminer” for enhanced survival plot generation, “ggpubr” for publication ready graphics, and “forestplot” for forest plot visualization of hazard ratios across multiple cancer types.

Genetic alteration analysis

To systematically characterize the genetic landscape of KLB across multiple cancer types, we leveraged several complementary bioinformatics resources and analytical approaches. The genetic mutation profiles of KLB in pancancer datasets were investigated using the “Gene_Mutation” module of TIMER2 (Tumor Immune Estimation Resource 2) [21]. which provides comprehensive mutation data derived from TCGA datasets. This platform enabled us to identify various types of genetic alterations including missense mutations, nonsense mutations, frameshift insertions/deletions, and structural variants affecting the KLB gene across different cancer types. To further characterize KLB genetic modifications and their potential functional consequences, we utilized the online cBioPortal database (https://www.cbioportal.org/), which integrates multidimensional cancer genomics data and provides detailed mutation annotations, including mutation frequency, type, and potential impact on protein function. Additionally, we employed the UALCAN database to specifically investigate KLB promoter methylation levels across cancer types, as epigenetic modifications represent crucial regulatory mechanisms that can influence gene expression independent of genetic mutations. This multiplatform approach allowed us to comprehensively assess both genetic and epigenetic alterations affecting KLB expression and function in cancer [21].

Immune infiltration and correlation analysis

To elucidate the relationship between KLB expression and the tumor immune microenvironment, we implemented a comprehensive immune profiling strategy. We utilized the “immunedeconv” R package, which incorporates the TIMER (Tumor Immune Estimation Resource) algorithm, to quantitatively estimate the infiltration levels of various immune cell populations within tumor tissues based on bulk RNA sequencing data [22]. This algorithm employs a statistical method to infer immune cell abundances by analyzing the expression signatures of immune cell typespecific marker genes. The TIMER algorithm was selected for its validated performance in accurately estimating immune cell infiltration across multiple cancer types and its ability to provide consistent results comparable to flow cytometry and immunohistochemistry methods. Additionally, to assess the potential involvement of KLB in immune checkpoint regulation, we extracted expression profiles of 22 genes representing key immune checkpoint pathways, including both immune inhibitory molecules (such as PD-1, PD-L1, CTLA-4, LAG-3, TIM-3) and immune stimulatory molecules (such as CD27, CD28, ICOS, OX40). We then performed correlation analysis to identify significant associations between KLB expression levels and immune checkpoint-related gene expression, which could provide insights into KLB’s potential role in modulating immune responses and its relevance for immunotherapy strategies.

Functional enrichment analysis in CRC

To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in CRC, the unpaired Student’s t test of the “limma” package was utilized. DEGs were considered through two thresholds:|log2FC| >1.5 and aPadj < 0.001. Subsequently, we conducted gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), Gene Ontology (GO), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichments on KLB and KLB co-expressed genes using “clusterProfiler” and “ggplot2” packages [23].

Prognostic model construction and prediction

In order to select the most appropriate prognostic model, a multivariate Cox regression was employed in conjunction with Akaike’s information criterion. Furthermore, the nomogram for the prediction of patient outcomes was constructed by the “rms” package. Based on the median risk scores, we divided patients into different risk groups, and the differential survival rates among two groups were peformed through the KM method. Additionally, we generated the Receiver Operating Characteristic (Receiver Operating Characteristic, ROC) curve to measure the predictive accuracy of the prognostic model.

Tissue specimens, cell lines, and culture

For experimental validation of our bioinformatics findings, we established a comprehensive sample collection and cell culture system. Clinical tissue specimens were obtained from Chongqing University Three Gorges Hospital, comprising ten paired sets of tumor and adjacent noncancerous (paracancerous) tissue samples from patients diagnosed with untreated colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) between August 2021 and November 2021. To ensure sample quality and prevent RNA degradation, all tissue specimens were immediately snapfrozen in liquid nitrogen following surgical resection and maintained under ultralow temperature conditions during transport and longterm storage. All human tissue samples were collected exclusively for research purposes with comprehensive informed consent obtained from all participating patients prior to sample collection. The study protocol and tissue collection procedures received full approval from the Ethics Committee of Chongqing University Three Gorges Hospital, ensuring compliance with ethical guidelines for human subject research. For in vitro experimental studies, we procured COAD cell lines, specifically CACO-2 cells (Cat. No. TCHu 37), from the Shanghai Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), a certified repository ensuring cell line authenticity and quality. Cell culture was performed using Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with high glucose obtained from Gibco (Cat. No. 11965092; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cat. No. 16000044; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to provide essential growth factors and nutrients. Cells were maintained under standard tissue culture conditions at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2, with regular medium changes and subculturing performed according to established protocols to maintain cell viability and prevent overgrowth.These catalog numbers are commonly cited in PubMed publications and represent standard, widely-used products for cell culture research.

RNA isolation and quantitative reverse transcriptase PCR

RNAiso Plus reagent (Cat. No. 9109, TaKaRa, Kusatsu, Japan) was used to isolate total RNA from the tissues following the manufacturer’s protocol. We selected the All In One 5X RT MasterMix (Cat. No. G592, ABM, Richmond, BC, Canada) to generate the cDNA library according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A 20 µL reaction mixture containing BlasTaq™ 2X qPCR MasterMix (Cat. No. G891, ABM, Richmond, BC, Canada) was used for qPCR on an Analytik Jena qTOWER³ G realtime fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument (Analytik Jena, Jena, Germany). The results were analyzed by 2^(-ΔΔCt) method using beta-actin as an endogenous reference gene.The primers for KLB mRNA were designed using Primer-BLAST (NCBI) and validated for specificity through in silico analysis. The amplicon length was designed to be within the optimal range of 75–200 bp for qPCR efficiency. The primers for KLB mRNA were: Forward: 5′-TCTGTCATCCTGTCAGCACTT-3′ and Reverse: 5′-CCAGTCCCAATACCCCAGAAAAA-3′ (expected amplicon size: 146 bp). Primer specificity was confirmed through BLAST analysis against the human genome to ensure unique targeting of the KLB gene with no significant homology to other genomic sequences. Melt curve analysis was performed following each qPCR run to confirm the generation of a single, specific product and rule out primer dimer formation. Beta-actin primers were: Forward: 5′-GCCGACAGGATGCAGAAGG-3′, Reverse: 5′-TGGAAGGTGGACAGCGAGG-3′ (expected amplicon size: 138 bp). qPCR cycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. Melt curve analysis was performed from 60 °C to 95 °C with increments of 0.5 °C every 5 s.

Immunohistochemistry

Human COAD tissue was fixed, decalcified, dehydrated, transparentized, paraffin embedded, dewaxed, and dehydrated following standard histological procedures. The tissue was then immersed for 30 min into a prewarmed antigen retrieval solution containing phosphate buffered saline (PBS, Cat. No. 10010023, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), Triton X-100 (Cat. No. BP151-500, Fisher Scientific, Fair Lawn, NJ, USA), and 30% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, Cat. No. H1009, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). To facilitate antigen recovery, the human COAD slides were then incubated in citric acid-based antigen retrieval buffer (Cat. No. H-3300, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) for 15 min at high temperature following the manufacturer’s instructions. After exposure to 0.3% H2O2 followed by treatment with 5% normal goat serum (Cat. No. OASB01365, Aviva Systems Biology, San Diego, CA, USA) diluted 1:20 for blocking, the slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a rabbit polyclonal antibody directed against KLB (Cat. No. ab233416, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) at a working dilution of 1:100 in PBS containing 1% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100. Following primary antibody incubation, slides were washed three times with PBS and incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Cat. No. PI-1000, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Results were obtained by applying 3,3’-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB) staining solution (Cat. No. SK-4100, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Furthermore, the slides were treated with Gill’s hematoxylin (Cat. No. 786–1052, G-Biosciences, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 5 min for nuclear counterstaining. Immunocomplexes were identified by light microscopy after cleansing, dehydration, transparency, and mounting with resinous mounting medium.

Cell counting kit-8 analysis

We purchased cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent from HCM (China) and prepared the 96-well plates within a total of 2 × 103 cells preplated. Assessment began after 24 h and continued uninterrupted for 6 days (0/1/2/3/4/5/6 days). Following the absorption and elimination of the medium, the CCK-8 reagent was combined with serum free medium in a 5 mL Eppendorf tube (CCK-8 reagent: Serum free medium = 10 mL: 90 mL per well) and subsequently transferred to 96 well plates, each containing 100 mL. The OD was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate after incubation for one hour at 37 °C.

Colony formation assay

We treated the transfected colon cancer cells utilizing trypsin collagenase to dissolve them in a single cell mixture, subsequently transferred to 6 well plates at a density of 1000 cells per well and incubated for 14 days at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The cells were first washed twice with PBS, followed by staining with 2% crystal violet for 15 min, and the plate was dried at room temperature. The number of clones produced was counted on the plate. A minimum of three separate sets of experiments were performed.

Wound healing assay

6 well plates were transfected with colon cancer cells (2.5 × 105 cells per well). After 12 h, the serum free medium was replaced with an inverted microscope (IX81, Olympus Company, Japan) to collect images at 0, 24, and 48 h after marking the cells in the plate with a 20 µL pipetting head. Each experiment was replicated in triplicate.

Animal experiments

Male NTG mice, aged 4–6 weeks and weighing 18–22 g, were acquired from Zhonghong Mingyan Biotechnology Co., LTD. (Sichuan, China). They were randomly divided into two groups: Ad-KLB and Vector, with ten mice per group. Cells (10^7/100 µL) were resuspended in a complete medium with Matrigel, and 100 µL of this cell suspension was injected subcutaneously on one side of each mouse. Tumor size and body weight were monitored every three days. After 8 weeks, mice were euthanized using an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital. Tumors were excised and weighed, and tumor load was calculated (length × width). The care and use of mice in this study adhered to pertinent guidelines and regulations, including the “Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals” (approved by the State Council of China, revised in 2017) and the “Chongqing Administration of Laboratory Animal Management Measures” (Order No. 195, Chongqing Municipal People’s Government). All methods were conducted following the ARRIVE guidelines. Ethical approval and oversight were provided by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing University Three Gorges Hospital (Approval No. SXYYDW2024-091).

Single-cell RNA sequencing data acquisition and analysis

Publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data for colorectal cancer (CRC) were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under the accession number GSE277669. The R package ‘Seurat’ was utilized for downstream analysis, including quality control (filtering cells based on gene counts, mitochondrial gene percentage, etc.), data normalization (‘LogNormalize’ method), identification of highly variable genes, and data scaling. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed for initial dimensionality reduction. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) and t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) were applied using the top principal components for visualization. Graph based clustering was employed to identify distinct cell populations. Cell clusters were manually annotated into major cell types (including B cells, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, mast cells, myeloid cells, stromal cells, T/NK cells, and mitochondria high cells) based on the expression of canonical marker genes obtained from literature review and cell marker databases. Expression levels of KLB and selected related genes (FGFR4, FGF19, ASCL2, CALML5, C8A) were visualized using dot plots and feature plots on t-SNE maps. To explore the function of KLB expressing cells, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed on differentially expressed genes between KLB expressing and nonexpressing cells using the ‘clusterProfiler’ R package. Cell-cell communication analysis was conducted using the R package ‘CellChat’ to infer ligand-receptor interactions and signaling networks between different cell types within the CRC microenvironment. The number and strength of interactions were calculated, and specific pathways, such as Macrophage Migration Inhibitory Factor (MIF) and Midkine (MK) signaling, were further investigated and visualized.

Statistical analyses

A two sample t-test was employed to distinguish between cancer and control tissues. Each survival analysis used KM curves, Cox proportional regression models, and logrank tests. Correlation between gene expression and tumor immunity was assessed using Spearman’s test. The χ2 test was used for the examination of the prevalence of neoplasms, while the Bonferroni correction was used for the evaluation of different cohorts. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

KLB differentially expressed between normal and tumor tissues

KLB expression was particularly high in adipose tissue, liver and pancreas in normal tissues and organs (Fig. 1A). Analyzing tumor tissue from 33 cancers revealed that KLB was expressed at lower levels in the majority of tumors (Fig. 1B). The levels of KLB mRNA were observed to be decreased in most cancers containing COAD and READ. It was observed that the expression of KLB was greater in both LIHC and PRAD (Fig. 1C). The protein expression levels of KLB were found to be lower in colon, stomach, and pancreatic tumor tissues, while they were higher in liver and prostate tumor tissues (Fig. 1D).

KLB expression is associated with the prognosis of various cancers

We analyzed the associations of KLB expression with prognosis and clinical TNM stage of pancancers through multiple methods. In patients with LAML, LGG, LUAD, and PAAD, the results demonstrated a statistically significant correlation between KLB expression and OS (Fig. 2A). KLB was observed to be differentially expressed in the T stage of BRCA, LUSC, PAAD, and STAD, as well as in the N stage of COADREAD, LUAD, and KIRP (Fig. 2B, C). The correlation between KLB expression and prognosis was investigated through the use of GEPIA2 in a cohort of patients diagnosed with different types of tumors. Elevated KLB expression was associated with longer OS in patients with LGG and PAAD, and with longer DFS in patients with LGG, LUAD, and READ. Reduced KLB expression was found to correlate with longer OS in DLBC patients and longer DFS in SARC and STAD patients (Fig. 2D, E).

KLB alterations associated with the development of multiple tumors

Based on our analysis, the most frequent alteration type of KLB was “mutation”, especially in SKCM (> 6%). With a frequency of about 3%, the highest incidence of the “amplification” type of KLB was observed in CHOL. Additionally, the alteration type of KLB was only “mutation” in COADREAD (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, the relationships of alteration conditions in KLB and prognosis of various cancers were systematically studied. The OS of patients with genetic alterations in KLB was found to be inferior to that of patients without KLB alterations (P = 0.0837, P = 0.187, P = 0.130) (Fig. 3B). We have further investigated the level of promoter methylation of KLB in pancancers. It was observed that the degree of methylation at the KLB promoter was elevated in primary tumors in most cancers including COAD and READ, while a reduction was evident in PRAD (Fig. 3C).

Correlations between KLB, immune infiltration and immune checkpoint-related genes

According to the reported relationship between KLB and the immune response, we conducted a comprehensive pancancer analysis to investigate the correlation between KLB and immune infiltration across diverse tumor types. We also observed a significant negative correlation between KLB expression and the 24 immune cells in BLCA, GBM, LAML, LGG, LUAD, and TGCT. Addtionally, KLB expression in BRCA, LUSC, and READ was positively correlated with the majority of immune cells (Fig. 4A).

The process of immunosurveillance plays a pivotal role in predicting cancer patient prognosis, whereby tumors utilize immune checkpoints to evade immune responses. Notably, we observed that the expression of KLB was positively correlated with that of most of the immune inhibitors and immune stimulators in several cancers involving COAD and READ. Conversely, the expression of KLB exhibited a negative correlation with that of BLCA and LGG. The strongest positive associations were observed between CD160 and BTLA and KLB expression in the various cancers under investigation (Fig. 4B).

Construction of prognostic model for KLB expression in CRC patients

The initial step of prognostic model construction involved obtaining KLB expression and clinical data. Regarding the pathological N stage, KLB expression showed significant differences between the N0, N1 and N2 groups (Table 1). CEA level, pathologic TNM stage, and KLB expression as major factors influencing PFI (Table 2). Integrating KLB and other prognostic factors, we developed a PFI nomogram (Fig. 4C). A worse prognostic factor was indicated by a higher point on the nomogram. The calibration curve with a C index of 0.778 for PFI was applied to evaluate the performance of the KLB nomogram (Fig. 4D). In conclusion, this nomogram might provide a more effective model for predicting CRC patient survival than for predicting individual prognostic factors.

Functional enrichment analysis indicated the potential impact of KLB

A total of 939 DEGs, of which 930 were upregulated and 9 were downregulated, were identified by differential gene expression analysis (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, we conducted a GSEA to elucidate the key pathways associated with KLB. GSEA results indicated that the most significantly enriched pathways were highly associated with cell cycle and cell proliferation (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, GO/KEGG enrichment analysis predicted the functions of co-expressed genes in KLB patients. Top enrichment terms suggested that KLB may also affect splicing, signaling and immune responses along with its co-expressed genes, with signaling corresponds to previous findings (Fig. 5C, D) [8].

Colon cancer cell proliferation and migration inhibited by KLB

Tumor and normal tissue samples were evaluated using qPCR and IHC, which revealed that KLB expression was significantly lower in tumor tissue samples (Fig. 6A, B). Overexpression of KLB suppressed cell proliferation and migration in vitro in CCK-8, wound healing and colony formation assays (Fig. 6C, E). The inhibitory effect of KLB on tumor growth was ultimately confirmed through subcutaneous tumor formation in NTG mice (Fig. 6F).

Single-cell analysis reveals KLB expression patterns and functions within the CRC microenvironment

To further investigate the cellular localization and function of KLB within the complex colorectal cancer tumor microenvironment (TME), we performed single-cell RNA sequencing analysis. Unsupervised clustering using t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) identified distinct cell populations within the TME (Fig. 7A, B). Annotation of these clusters revealed major cell types including B cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, mast cells, myeloid cells, stromal cells, and T cells (Fig. 7C, D). Analysis of gene expression across these cell types showed that KLB, along with its known signaling partners FGFR4 and FGF19, was predominantly expressed in fibroblast and stromal cell populations (Fig. 7E). Notably, ASCL2, a transcription factor linked to cancer associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in CRC, also showed high expression in stromal cells. Genes co-expressed with KLB identified in bulk analysis, such as CALML5 and C8A, exhibited broader expression patterns but were enriched in myeloid cells (Fig. 7E). Visualization of individual gene expression on t-SNE plots further confirmed the specific localization of KLB, FGFR4, and FGF19 expression primarily within the fibroblast/stromal cell clusters (Fig. 8).

To elucidate the potential functions of KLB expressing cells within the CRC TME, we performed functional enrichment analysis specifically on the cells identified as expressing KLB (Fig. 9A). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that KLB expressing cells were significantly enriched in terms related to cellular components such as ‘cytosolic ribosome’ and ‘Golgi apparatus subcompartment’ (Fig. 9B), biological processes including ‘Golgi vesicle transport’, ‘protein localization to plasma membrane’, and ‘T cell activation’ (Fig. 9C), and molecular functions like ‘cadherin binding’, ‘GTPase regulator activity’, and ‘structural constituent of ribosome’ (Fig. 9D, E). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis further implicated these cells in ‘Ribosome’, ‘Endocytosis’, ‘Coronavirus disease-COVID-19’, and ‘Salmonella infection’ pathways (Fig. 9F). These findings suggest that KLB expressing cells, likely fibroblasts and stromal cells, are actively involved in protein synthesis and transport, cell-cell adhesion, signaling regulation, and potentially interactions with the immune system and pathogens within the CRC microenvironment.

Cell-cell communication network highlights fibroblast and stromal cell interactions

To understand the intercellular communication landscape within the CRC TME, we inferred potential ligand receptor interactions between the identified cell types. Network analysis revealed extensive communication networks, quantified by both the number of interactions and the overall interaction strength (Fig. 10A, B). Fibroblasts, stromal cells, and epithelial cells emerged as central hubs in this network, engaging in numerous strong interactions with various cell populations, particularly myeloid cells and T/NK cells. Focusing on outgoing signals, epithelial cells and fibroblasts showed prominent communication pathways directed towards other cell types (Fig. 10E, F), while B cells and enteroendocrine cells exhibited comparatively fewer outgoing interactions (Fig. 10C, D).

Further investigation into specific signaling pathways revealed the potential roles of Macrophage Migration Inhibitory Factor (MIF) and Midkine (MK) signaling in mediating these interactions. The MIF signaling network indicated fibroblasts, myeloid cells, and stromal cells as primary sources, potentially targeting epithelial cells and T/NK cells (Fig. 11A). Similarly, the MK signaling pathway analysis highlighted stromal cells as major contributors, with signals directed towards epithelial cells and T/NK cells (Fig. 11B, C). Expression patterns of key genes involved in these pathways, such as MIF, its receptors CXCR4 and CD74, supported these inferred interactions (Fig. 11D), suggesting that fibroblast and stromal cell populations actively modulate the behavior of epithelial and immune cells within the CRC TME through pathways like MIF and MK signaling. Given that KLB is predominantly expressed in fibroblasts and stromal cells (Figs. 7E and 8), these interactions likely contribute to the KLB associated effects on tumor progression and the immune microenvironment.

Discussion

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a pressing global health concern due to its high incidence and mortality rates. Despite progress in understanding its molecular pathogenesis, limitations in early diagnostic biomarkers and effective therapeutic targets hinder clinical outcomes. In this context, β-Klotho (KLB), a key co-receptor in the FGF signaling pathway, has emerged as a molecule of interest due to its dual role in metabolic regulation and tumor biology. However, its specific function in CRC has remained unclear.

Through comprehensive analysis, we found that CRC tumor tissues exhibit significantly reduced KLB expression relative to normal controls, a pattern that mirrors observations in other malignant diseases. This downregulation of KLB showed strong associations with advanced TNM staging and unfavorable patient prognosis, establishing its potential utility as a prognostic biomarker. Similar tumor suppressive functions of KLB have been documented in non small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [17, 18], where diminished KLB levels likewise predict worse clinical outcomes. Through pancancer analysis, we identified widespread KLB expression alterations across multiple cancer types, indicating its extensive influence on tumor pathophysiology. In CRC specifically, genetic mutations and promoter hypermethylation of KLB appear to drive disease progression, positioning this gene as a promising target for early detection and therapeutic intervention. Single cell RNA sequencing analysis provided additional mechanistic insights by revealing that KLB expression is primarily confined to fibroblast and stromal cell populations within the CRC tumor microenvironment. Acting as central communication nodes, these KLB positive cells likely modulate epithelial and immune cell functions via signaling networks including MIF and MK pathways, consistent with established roles of cancer associated fibroblasts in tumor microenvironment regulation.

Genetic analysis across multiple tumor types revealed frequent KLB alterations, with mutations emerging as the predominant alteration type. In COADREAD specifically, mutations constituted the exclusive form of KLB genetic change. While preliminary evidence suggests an association between KLB gene alterations and poor clinical outcomes, validation in larger patient cohorts will be necessary to confirm this relationship. The observation of reduced KLB mRNA expression despite relatively low promoter methylation in certain tumors points to the existence of sophisticated posttranscriptional control mechanisms operating within malignant tissues. The opposing trends in KLB promoter methylation patterns between COAD and READ tumor samples further underscore the significant epigenetic dysregulation of KLB during colorectal carcinogenesis. Given established evidence that genetic mutations and DNA methylation are pivotal drivers of tumor progression and therapeutic response—exemplified by RAS mutation status serving as a crucial molecular biomarker for CRC early detection [28–30]. The distinct expression patterns of KLB, its unique mutation profile, altered methylation status, and prognostic correlations collectively support its potential development as a diagnostic biomarker.

Numerous research findings indicate that the infiltration of immune cells into tumor samples plays a critical role in the progression of cancer and has significant implications for the prognosis of patients. Furthermore, a correlation between the immune microenvironment of the tumor and the expression levels of certain genes has been identified in recent studies. Our results demonstrated an inverse relationship between KLB expression and various immune cells and immune checkpoint genes in several cancers. Our results indicated that KLB has an important function in immune therapy and in the growth of tumors. The single cell analysis complements these findings by suggesting specific intercellular communication pathways, such as MIF signaling originating from KLB positive fibroblasts/stromal cells targeting immune cells, which could mechanistically link KLB expression levels in the stroma to the observed immune infiltration patterns and response to immunotherapy.

Studies on the function of KLB in tumors, particularly in CRC, remains in its exploratory phase, and the precise mechanisms underlying this process remain unclear. Our functional enrichment results suggested that KLB may influence tumor development by regulating the cell cycle and cell proliferation, and that KLB may also play a role in CRC pathogenesis by interfering with chromatin stability and immune responses in CRC through its co-expressed genes [24–27]. Mutations and methylation of KLB in CRC might lead to altered expression of KLB, which in turn may affect the levels of co-expressed genes and influence the immune response to CRC. These results suggested the potential of KLB as a therapeutic target, consistent with our previous immune checkpoint correlation analysis. Furthermore, we constructed a well behaved model for predicting the PFI in CRC patients. Ultimately, our results suggested that KLB inhibits the proliferation and mobility of colon cancer cell lines in vivo and in vitro.

This study has several limitations to acknowledge. The relatively small sample size of certain cancer subtypes may introduce analytical uncertainties or reduce statistical power. While we have conducted preliminary explorations of KLB’s mechanisms through GSEA, GO/KEGG, and single cell sequencing analyses, coupled with experimental validation of KLB overexpression effects in CRC cells, critical functional investigations—including comprehensive KLB knockdown experiments, its roles in CRC tumorigenesis progression, intercellular communication networks, and tumor immunity—remain to be fully elucidated. Additionally, our bioinformatics findings predominantly rely on public database derived data, necessitating future verification through systematic clinical sample analyses to strengthen the conclusions. Although these aspects were not exhaustively addressed in the current investigation, they represent essential directions for subsequent research. Collectively, while our work establishes foundational insights, expanded experimental validation and clinical correlation studies will be required to fully characterize KLB’s multifaceted roles in CRC pathophysiology.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, while our overexpression experiments demonstrate KLB’s tumor suppressive capacity, the absence of loss of function studies (siRNA/shRNA knockdown) limits our ability to definitively establish KLB’s necessity for tumor suppression in colorectal cancer. Second, the relatively small sample size of clinical specimens and certain cancer subtypes may introduce analytical uncertainties and reduce statistical power for some analyses. Third, our prognostic nomogram lacks validation in independent external cohorts, which is essential before clinical implementation and limits the generalizability of our predictive model.

Conclusion

Our findings position KLB as a potential tumor suppressor in CRC, with diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic relevance. Its downregulation—driven by genetic and epigenetic alterations—correlates with disease progression and poor prognosis. Moreover, its stromal specific expression and involvement in TME signaling networks suggest a key role in regulating intercellular communication and immune responses. These insights underscore the potential of KLB as a biomarker and therapeutic target and provide a foundation for future investigations into its mechanisms and clinical applications in CRC management.
