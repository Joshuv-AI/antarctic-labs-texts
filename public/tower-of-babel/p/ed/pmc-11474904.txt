Reassessing the economic burden of geriatric falls: a call for preventive action
Authors: Abdul Tawab Saljuqi, Tanya Anand, Bellal Joseph
Published: 2024

Zangbar and colleagues1 present a timely and essential study comparing medical and combined (combination of medical costs and value of a statistical life) costs associated with fatal geriatric falls to those from fatal firearm injuries. Their findings reveal an increasing incidence and rise in medical costs for geriatric falls over the years, with a marked increase in combined costs for fatal falls among adults aged 45–85 since 2019. The study highlights geriatric falls as a greater economic burden and is a call for immediate prioritization of preventive measures to minimize the incidence of falls.

The reasons behind a fall in a geriatric patient are often complex and multifaceted. Thus, strategies addressing fall prevention should consider the various contributors that place the older adult at risk. The American Geriatrics Society recommends annual screening for a history of falls or balance impairment for adults older than 65 years.2 The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends regular exercise and physical therapy to prevent falls.3 Balance, strength and gait training exercises improve muscle strength and are reported effective in preventing falls with no difference between types of exercises.4 Frailty has been linked to an increased likelihood of fractures in geriatric falls.5 Being frail increases the odds of mortality by 75% and readmissions by 49% in geriatric patients after ground-level falls.6 Hence, early assessment of frailty and incorporation into discharge planning is important and may lead to improved outcomes, reduced risk of fractures, and decrease in the overall financial burden. In addition, certain medications such as antihypertensives, antidepressants, antipsychotics, sedatives, and hypnotics are associated with an increased risk of falls. Carefully reviewing medications outweighing risks and benefits and discontinuing unnecessary medications can significantly aid in reducing fall risk after discharge. The burden of falls is significantly influenced by socioeconomic status and racial and ethnic differences among older adults. Fall prevention programs should be tailored to different populations, particularly minority groups, to address these disparities effectively.

Other contributors to an increased fall risk are also important to examine; management of postural hypotension, home hazard assessments, such as proper handrails on both sides of stairs and well-illuminated rooms, regular evaluation of vision and hearing, and use of assistive devices are some effective strategies to prevent geriatric falls. Fall prevention clinics involving an interprofessional team,7 implementing broader management guidelines, and establishing a multidisciplinary care pathway are crucial and can help counteract the anticipated rise in geriatric falls and reduce the associated healthcare costs.

Incorporating comprehensive, multifaceted fall prevention strategies and addressing disparities among diverse populations are critical in reducing the significant economic and health burden posed by the increasing incidence of fatal geriatric falls.
