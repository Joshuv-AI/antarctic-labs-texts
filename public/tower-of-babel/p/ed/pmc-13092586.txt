Widely targeted metabolomics reveals conserved and differential metabolite patterns related to nutritional and bioactive compounds in stems and leaves of three Portulaca oleracea cultivars
Authors: Mingzhao Zhu, Yanpeng Ding, Hanying Wu, Ran Zhao, Wei Liu, Long Xie, Xiangyang Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study employed a widely targeted metabolomics approach to compare the stems and leaves of three Portulaca oleracea L. cultivars. A total of 1315 metabolites were identified, for which we pioneered a novel analytical framework classifying them into a ‘conserved’ and ‘specific’ dual-layered architecture. Overall, the leaves exhibited higher metabolic activity. The different cultivars displayed distinct metabolic specializations: Cultivar 1's stems were rich in amino acids, while its leaves accumulated high levels of phenolic acids and various functional lipids. Cultivar 2's leaves acted as a “super bio-factory” for the production of phenolic acids, flavonoids, and oleraceins. Cultivar 3's stems accumulated phenolic acids, while its leaves maintained robust primary metabolism and simultaneously developed balanced and diverse secondary metabolic pathways. This study reveals the organ-specific metabolic partitioning patterns in P. oleracea, providing a scientific basis for its targeted development as a functional food and for the selection of high-value germplasm resources.

Introduction

Portulaca oleracea L., reputed as the “longevity vegetable,” is an annual succulent herbaceous plant belonging to the family Portulacaceae. Known for its remarkable adaptability and resilience, it is widely distributed across the globe. Traditionally, this species has long been regarded as a low-value feed resource and a field-associated weed, often overlooked due to its unique texture and slightly astringent taste, making it less popular in mainstream culinary settings. In reality, however, P. oleracea is a quintessential dual-purpose crop for both medicinal and dietary applications. In traditional Chinese medicine, it is renowned for its diverse functions, including clearing heat and detoxifying, cooling the blood, stopping bleeding, and alleviating dysentery (Li, 2021; Shennong, 2021). Nutritional studies indicate that the plant is rich in essential minerals such as potassium, calcium, and magnesium, as well as diverse vitamin complexes (Mattera et al., 2024; Saffaryazdi et al., 2020). Modern pharmacological research has revealed that the flavonoids, phenylpropanoid derivatives, and polyunsaturated fatty acids contained in P. oleracea possess synergistic antioxidant, broad-spectrum antibacterial, and anti-inflammatory activities, while also demonstrating potential for bidirectional regulation of metabolic syndrome by modulating blood lipid and glucose levels (Ebrahimian et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022). Its unique ω-3/ω-6 fatty acid ratio, along with its high content of soluble dietary fibers, has shown synergistic effects in maintaining cardiovascular homeostasis and improving gut microbiota balance (Fu et al., 2021; Melilli et al., 2020). In addition, P. oleracea is rich in alkaloids, anthocyanins, betalains, and terpenoids, all of which exhibit distinct pharmacological properties (Table 1). P. oleracea is a highly adaptable and nutritionally rich edible plant, with its stems and leaves exhibiting notable differences in texture, characteristics, and culinary applications. The leaves of P. oleracea are thick, tender, and refreshing, making them ideal for cold or raw preparations such as salads and appetizers, whereas the stems are crisp and juicy; the younger, less-lignified sections are particularly well suited to soups and stir-fries. These differences between the stems and leaves not only influence their modes of consumption but also play a significant role in determining their suitability for various cooking methods, serving as a key consideration in ingredient selection and practical application. There are also significant differences in stem and leaf traits—such as morphology and texture—among different varieties of P. oleracea. For instance, wild P. oleracea typically has smaller, thicker leaves and reddish-purple stems, with a growth pattern characterized by multiple branches, whereby the lower branches grow close to the ground while the upper branches extend diagonally. In comparison, Dutch P. oleracea primarily exhibits upright growth, with larger and fuller leaves, making it better suited for commercial cultivation. These morphological variations not only define the edible characteristics of the plant but also result in significant differentiation in the active components across different organs (Zhou et al., 2015).

Previous studies have further confirmed that organ-specific metabolic differentiation carries significant ecological and functional implications. For example, differences in primary and secondary metabolites between flowers and leaves of Pimpinella brachycarpa Nakai are closely associated with its antioxidant activity (Sathasivam et al., 2025); phenolic substances from different parts of Angelica keiskei can regulate the inhibitory effects on xanthine oxidase (Kim et al., 2014); and the unique phytochemical composition of various organs, including the peel and bark of pomegranate, reveals complex and diverse metabolic characteristics (Yisimayili & Chao, 2022). In the case of P. oleracea, previous studies have long indicated that different organs possess distinct medicinal values. For example, the seeds have been used to treat diarrhea and throat infections (Younos et al., 1987), and the leaves have been used to relieve gastric pain (Hussein & Dhabe, 2018). In addition, the stems are used against liver, gastric, kidney, and bladder diseases and as a hypoglycemic agent (Nanagulyan et al., 2020), while the roots have been reported to be used for the treatment of diabetes mellitus (Mosaddegh et al., 2012). Surprisingly, this understanding has long been reflected in the practices of traditional Chinese medicine, showcasing clear functional differentiation across plant organs. For example, goji berries primarily “nourish and replenish the liver and kidneys,” emphasizing their tonic and harmonizing effects, whereas goji leaves are renowned for “clearing heat and detoxifying,” often used to reduce excessive internal heat. Similarly, lotus seeds focus on “calming the mind and nurturing the heart,” aiding in soothing the spirit, while lotus root specializes in “strengthening the spleen and aiding digestion,” favoring gastrointestinal regulation. This functional differentiation, rooted in ancient practice, resonates across time with the metabolic distribution patterns uncovered by modern biology, not only solidifying the theoretical foundation of traditional Chinese medicine but also providing a comprehensive framework for the development of organ-specific functional foods. The significance spans from molecular mechanisms to application-oriented transformations, offering profound implications for both research and commercialization.

It is well recognized that China possesses abundant germplasm resources of wild vegetables, many of which feature the “food-medicine homology” property. From the legend of Shennong tasting hundreds of herbs to Compendium of Materia Medica (Bencao Gangmu), traditional Chinese medicine has made important contributions to the understanding and utilization of wild vegetable resources. In times of scarcity, wild vegetables were often essential foods to relieve hunger; today, with improving living standards, the pursuit of health benefits has renewed and intensified interest in wild vegetables. P. oleracea, ranked as the eighth most widely distributed species worldwide, is a representative wild vegetable, and how to develop and utilize it efficiently and rationally is one of our key concerns. Chaachouay et al. (2019) reported that cooked P. oleracea leaves are edible and can be used to ameliorate hypercholesterolemia, suggesting that everyday cooking and consumption practices may help achieve an integration of diet and therapy and highlighting the considerable potential of P. oleracea for functional food and drug development. Nevertheless, studies on the composition of functional metabolites and their bioactivities in P. oleracea remain relatively limited. Although bioactive constituents in different cultivars and organs have been identified (Nemzer et al., 2020; Siriamornpun & Suttajit, 2010), existing evidence is still insufficient in terms of systematic coverage and comprehensiveness.

Three P. oleracea accessions were used in this study, designated Cultivar1, Cultivar2, and Cultivar3. Cultivar3 was introduced from Iran and has shown stable traits after years of continuous selection and evaluation, and it has been developed into a commercial cultivar with established market sales. Cultivar1 and Cultivar2 were collected from Henan Province, China; although their yields are lower than that of Cultivar3, they exhibit distinct phenotypic characteristics such as stem and leaf coloration (see “Materials and Methods”). In addition, our previous work showed that the overall anthocyanin contents of Cultivar1 and Cultivar2 are higher than that of Cultivar3 (Zhu et al., 2025), indicating greater potential for further development and utilization. Based on our prior observations of phenotypic variation among the three cultivars, we propose the following hypothesis: Cultivar1 and Cultivar2 possess cultivar-specific metabolic features that distinguish them from Cultivar3; these differences may be associated with quality-related traits and can be exploited to screen potential metabolic biomarkers. Meanwhile, P. oleracea cultivars from different origins and their different tissues are expected to share a set of conserved metabolites that remain relatively stable across cultivars and tissues. Accordingly, we performed a metabolomics-based systematic identification and comparison of metabolite profiles among Cultivar1, Cultivar2, and Cultivar3 to elucidate metabolomic differentiation driven by cultivar origin and tissue type, to screen key differential metabolites and potential biomarkers associated with quality traits, and to further summarize the shared and relatively stable conserved metabolites across the three accessions. The specific objectives were to: (i) prioritize resource development and utilization by evaluating the development potential and application value of Cultivar1 and Cultivar2 based on a systematic identification and comparison of metabolite compositions, thereby providing a scientific basis for resource exploration and quality improvement; (ii) characterize the shared and relatively stable conserved metabolites among the three P. oleracea accessions, providing scientific support for public-oriented science communication, nutrition and health awareness, and product marketing; and (iii) identify key differential metabolites to mine potential metabolic markers for cultivar discrimination and quality evaluation, thereby providing a theoretical basis for precise germplasm identification and targeted breeding in P. oleracea. Previous metabolomics studies on P. oleracea, as well as many comparative food-omics investigations, have mainly focused on differential metabolites. In contrast, conserved metabolite components that are shared and relatively stable across cultivars and organs have rarely been explicitly defined or quantitatively summarized (Table 2). Notably, this study introduces two key innovations. (i) We employed a widely targeted metabolomics approach to systematically identify and compare bioactive compounds across multiple tissues from different P. oleracea cultivars, substantially improving our overall understanding of metabolite composition and variation among tissues and among cultivars. Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

The chemicals used in this study included methanol (Cat. No. CAEQ-4-003302-4000, CNW, Shanghai, China), acetonitrile (Cat. No. CAEQ-4-000308-4000, CNW, Shanghai, China), and formic acid (Cat. No. F112034–500 mL, Shanghai, China).

Plant materials and preliminary treatment

Three P. oleracea materials, Cultivar1, Cultivar2 and Cultivar3, were cultivated at the Yanqing experimental base of the Vegetable Research Center, Beijing Academy of Agricultural and Forestry Sciences. Cultivars 1 and 2 were collected from Henan Province, China, and have undergone multi-year domestication and directional selection in our breeding program; they are currently maintained as pre-breeding lines (pre-commercial candidates). Cultivar 1 has small leaves and slender stems, with red stems and leaves, and shows a semi-erect growth habit. Cultivar 2 has medium-sized leaves and stems that are thinner than those of Cultivar 3 but thicker than those of Cultivar 1; its leaves are green and stems are red, and it also shows a semi-erect growth habit. Cultivar 3 was introduced from Iran and has been subjected to multi-year selection in our breeding program and is currently commercialized. It has large, fleshy leaves and thick, robust stems, is entirely green, and shows an erect growth habit (Zhu et al., 2025). The samples were from the same batch as in our previous study (Zhu et al., 2025).

The biological samples were placed in a freeze-dryer for vacuum freeze-drying processing. The dried samples were ground into powder using a grinder at a frequency of 30 Hz for 1.5 min. Approximately 50 mg of the powdered sample was weighed, and 1200 μL of pre-cooled (−20 °C) 70% methanol aqueous solution was added. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s every 30 min, for a total of 6 times. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 3 min. The supernatant was carefully collected, filtered through a microporous membrane with a pore size of 0.22 μm, and transferred to an injection vial for UPLC-MS/MS analysis.

Metabolite analysis via UPLC-MS/MS

The sample extracts were analyzed using a UPLC–ESI–MS/MS system. Chromatographic separation was performed on an ExionLC™ AD UPLC platform (SCIEX, Marsiling Industrial Estate, Singapore). Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) data for widely targeted metabolomics were acquired on a QTRAP 6500 triple quadrupole–linear ion trap mass spectrometer (SCIEX, Marsiling Industrial Estate, Singapore). In addition, high-resolution MS/MS spectra were collected on a Q-TOF 6600 high-resolution mass spectrometer (SCIEX, Marsiling Industrial Estate, Singapore) to support metabolite identification and confirmation. The analysis was performed under the following conditions: an Agilent SB-C18 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) was used, and the mobile phase consisted of solvent A (ultrapure water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). Elution gradient (B%): 5% B at 0.00 min; B was linearly increased to 95% over 0.00–9.00 min; held at 95% from 9.00 to 10.00 min; decreased to 5% over 10.00–11.10 min; and equilibrated at 5% from 11.10 to 14.00 min. The flow rate was set at 0.35 mL/min, the column oven temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the injection volume was 2 μL. The effluent was alternately introduced into a triple quadrupole-linear ion trap (QTRAP) mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source for analysis. Each sample was analyzed with three biological replicates, and each replicate was injected once for instrumental analysis. Representative MS chromatograms are shown in Fig. S1, Fig. S2.

The operational parameters of the ESI source were as follows: the source temperature was maintained at 500 °C, and the ion spray voltage (IS) was set to 5500 V in positive ion mode and − 4500 V in negative ion mode. The ion source gases, including gas I (GSI), gas II (GSII), and curtain gas (CUR), were adjusted to 50 psi, 60 psi, and 25 psi, respectively. Collision-activated dissociation (CAD) was configured to a high level. QQQ scans were conducted through MRM experiments, with nitrogen used as the collision gas at a medium setting. The declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE) for each MRM transition underwent additional optimization. Specific MRM transitions were monitored during designated periods, corresponding to the elution times of the metabolites.

The targeted MRM transitions and ion-pair panels were derived from an in-house MRM transition and spectral library provided by Wuhan Maiwei Metabolic Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for widely targeted metabolomics. The Q1/Q3 m/z values and fragment-ion pairs were further refined using accurate precursor/product ions obtained from high-resolution MS/MS data collected on a Q-TOF 6600, together with Q-TOF-derived retention time (RT) windows. High-resolution MS/MS spectra were acquired on a Q-TOF 6600 using pooled quality control samples and representative extracts from each group. The resulting RT windows and confirmed Q1/Q3 fragment-ion pairs were then used to construct scheduled MRM methods on the QTRAP 6500 for all study samples. In the qualitative stage, high-resolution MS/MS spectra and corresponding RTs of target compounds acquired on the Q-TOF 6600 were matched one-by-one against the reference in-house spectral library established by Wuhan Maiwei Metabolic Biotechnology Co., Ltd., determining the final candidate ion pairs. These Q-TOF-confirmed Q1/Q3 values, fragment-ion pairs, and RT windows were directly used to define the scheduled MRM transitions on the QTRAP 6500. During subsequent QTRAP acquisition, scheduled MRM monitoring was performed only within the predefined RT windows. Optimization of DP and CE was carried out using a “candidate combination screening followed by project-stage confirmation” workflow. Specifically, during method development, representative compounds and their candidate ion pairs were selected, and multiple DP and CE combinations were systematically tested on the QTRAP 6500 in MRM mode. The optimal DP and CE for each ion pair were selected based on MRM peak response and peak-shape performance, including response intensity, peak area, and the stability of the qualitative ion response. During project sample analysis, ion pairs with excessively high intensities (saturation and nonlinearity risk) or abnormal response behavior were re-optimized for DP and CE using the candidate DP/CE combinations established during method development. The DP/CE settings yielding the best response and the most stable peak shapes were then used for final acquisition, ensuring reproducibility across different analytical batches.

Metabolite identification and data analysis

Metabolite identification was performed by Wuhan Maiwei Metabolic Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China. Identification was based on accurate mass, MS/MS fragmentation information, isotopic distribution, and RT. The MS/MS spectra and RT obtained from the samples were matched, on an entry-by-entry basis, against the corresponding MS/MS spectra and RT in the in-house spectral library. The MS and MS/MS mass tolerances were both set to 20 ppm, and the RT tolerance was set to 0.2 min. Metabolite identification levels (Level) were used to indicate annotation reliability, with Level 1 representing higher confidence than Level 2 and Level 3 and confidence decreasing from Level 1 to Level 3; in this study, metabolites were identified at Level 1, which represents the highest confidence. Specifically, Level 1 was assigned when the MS/MS spectrum (including all fragment ions) and RT of the target compound matched the corresponding database entry with a match score ≥ 0.7; Level 2 was assigned when the MS/MS spectrum (including all fragment ions) and RT matched with a score of 0.5–0.7; and Level 3 was assigned when the first quadrupole (Q1), third quadrupole (Q3), RT, DP, and CE were consistent with the corresponding database information.

Quantitative analysis was performed using MRM for data acquisition, and relative quantification was based on integrated peak areas. After peak area extraction, peak matching and RT correction were applied across samples to reduce systematic errors introduced by instrument drift and other factors, yielding a quantitative matrix for subsequent statistical and differential analyses. For two-group analysis, differential metabolites were identified using Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) > 1, |Log2FC| ≥ 1.0 and p-value <0.05. VIP values were extracted from the OPLS-DA results (including score plots and permutation plots) generated with the R package MetaboAnalystR. Data were log-transformed (log2) and mean-centered before OPLS-DA, and a 200-permutation test was conducted to prevent overfitting. Metabolites were annotated using the KEGG Compound database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/) and mapped to KEGG Pathway (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html). Significantly enriched pathways were identified via metabolite set enrichment analysis (MSEA) and evaluated with hypergeometric test p-values. Heatmaps were constructed from the expression profiles of the selected differential metabolites after Z-score normalization.

Identification of conserved and marker metabolites

In all comparative analyses, the results were expressed in the form of “treatment group vs control group.” The analysis process for conserved metabolites was as follows: Metabolites showing no significant differences among the three stem groups (1-J, 2-J, 3-J) were defined as the total conserved metabolites in stems (Set 1), while those showing no significant differences among the three leaf groups (1-Y, 2-Y, 3-Y) were defined as the total conserved metabolites in leaves (Set 2). Subsequently, the metabolites showing no significant differences in the three pairwise comparisons between stems and leaves (1-J vs 1-Y, 2-J vs 2-Y, 3-J vs 3-Y) were identified, and their intersection was designated as the common conserved metabolites (Set 3). The stem-specific conserved metabolite candidates (Set 4) and leaf-specific conserved metabolite candidates (Set 5) were obtained by removing Set 3 metabolites from Set 1 and Set 2, respectively. After excluding the overlapping metabolites between Sets 4 and 5, the remaining metabolites were defined as stem-specific and leaf-specific conserved metabolites, respectively. The analysis process for marker metabolites was as follows: In the three paired comparisons between stems and leaves (1-Y vs 1-J, 2-Y vs 2-J, 3-Y vs 3-J), metabolites significantly upregulated in leaves were intersected and defined as leaf marker metabolites, whereas those upregulated in stems were defined as stem marker metabolites. Moreover, to determine fingerprint markers for each sample group, comparisons were carried out across and within tissues. For example, for 1-Y, metabolites upregulated relative to 2-Y, 3-Y, and 1-J were identified, and their intersection was considered as the fingerprint markers of 1-Y. The same approach was applied to other groups (2-Y, 3-Y, 1-J, 2-J, 3-J). The simplified workflow for the visual screening of conserved and marker metabolites was shown in Fig. S3.

Results

Comprehensive metabolomic analysis of stems and leaves from different P. oleracea cultivars

Metabolite classification and statistical analysis were performed on stem and leaf samples from three P. oleracea cultivars. In total, 11 major classes of metabolites were detected (Fig. 1a; Table S1). Among them, amino acids and their derivatives accounted for the highest relative abundance (17.87%), followed by phenolic acids (14.60%), other metabolites (14.37%), alkaloids (12.85%), and lipids (10.34%). In addition, flavonoids (7.98%), organic acids (7.22%), nucleotides and their derivatives (5.48%), lignans and coumarins (4.64%), terpenoids (3.57%), and a small proportion of quinones (1.06%) were also identified. To explore the shared and specific metabolite compositions between stems and leaves of different P. oleracea cultivars, metabolites detected in six sample groups (1-J, 2-J, 3-J, 1-Y, 2-Y, and 3-Y) were subjected to intersection analysis using an UpSet plot (Fig. 1b). The results showed that most metabolites were distributed across multiple sample groups, indicating a high degree of metabolic overlap. The largest intersection contained 16 metabolites shared by all samples except for 3-J. Several intersections were absent in a single sample, showing distinct distribution patterns: the intersections missing only 1-Y, 1-J, 2-J, 2-Y, and 3-Y contained 15, 13, 8, 6, and 3 metabolites, respectively. Moreover, a unique intersection containing 1 metabolite was observed exclusively in leaf samples (1-Y, 2-Y, and 3-Y). Another unique intersection was found between stem and leaf samples of cultivar 1 (1-J and 1-Y), containing a single metabolite. Overall, the UpSet analysis revealed a strong consistency in metabolite composition among the three P. oleracea cultivars, while some degree of tissue- and cultivar-specific variation was also present, providing valuable information for subsequent screening of characteristic metabolites.

To assess metabolic differences between stems and leaves of different cultivars, differential metabolite analyses were performed for Cultivars 1, 2, and 3 (Fig. 1c). A total of 83, 122, and 103 cultivar-specific differential metabolites were identified, respectively. There were 112 differential metabolites shared between Cultivars 1 and 2, 57 between Cultivars 1 and 3, and 159 between Cultivars 2 and 3. In addition, 291 differential metabolites were commonly detected across all three comparisons. In summary, a considerable overlap in stem–leaf differential metabolites was observed among the three P. oleracea cultivars, while each cultivar also exhibited a certain number of unique differential metabolites. These findings suggest both common and distinct metabolic characteristics among P. oleracea cultivars.

To evaluate the global metabolic separation between stems and leaves across different P. oleracea cultivars, OPLS-DA models were constructed for multivariate statistical analysis. The OPLS-DA scores plots for each pairwise comparison showed clear separation among groups and distinct clustering patterns within each group, indicating that the metabolic profiles differed among cultivars and between tissues (Fig. S4). Moreover, the permutation tests demonstrated that the model performance was not significantly better than that obtained from randomly permuted models, suggesting that the models were robust, predictive, and less likely to suffer from overfitting (Fig. S5). Collectively, these results support the reliability of the observed metabolomic variations and provide a basis for the subsequent screening of characteristic differential metabolites.

In addition, we compared the metabolic profiles among different groups of P. oleracea, including 1-J vs 1-Y, 2-J vs 2-Y, 3-J vs 3-Y, 1-J vs 2-J, 1-J vs 3-J, 2-J vs 3-J, 1-Y vs 2-Y, 1-Y vs 3-Y, and 2-Y vs 3-Y (Table S2). All subsequent analyses were conducted based on the results of these comparisons.

Conserved metabolite analysis

Previous metabolomic studies have predominantly focused on differential metabolites; however, we propose that metabolites consistently present across different conditions are also of great importance. When viewed from a cross-species perspective, these persistently stable metabolites in P. oleracea may serve as metabolic signatures distinguishing it from other species. Furthermore, the identification of conserved metabolites indicates their crucial roles in maintaining fundamental metabolic processes and physiological homeostasis in P. oleracea. Therefore, in this study, we innovatively conducted a systematic analysis of the compositional characteristics of these conserved metabolites. The results showed that both the stems and leaves of P. oleracea were rich in multiple classes of conserved metabolites, mainly including amino acids and their derivatives, alkaloids, phenolic acids, lipids, organic acids, flavonoids, terpenoids, lignans and coumarins, and quinones. Given that the “Others” category primarily consisted of metabolites with low relative abundance or unclear classification, and lacked explicit physiological relevance, this group was not considered in subsequent analyses.

A total of 542 conserved metabolites were detected in the stems, among which amino acids and their derivatives accounted for the highest proportion (24.17%), followed by organic acids (10.70%) and phenolic acids (10.33%). The most abundant representative metabolites were 2-amino-4-hydroxy-3-methylpentanoic acid, succinic acid, and 6-O-caffeoyl-d-glucose, respectively (Fig. 2A, Table S3). In the leaves, 685 conserved metabolites were identified, with amino acids and their derivatives (24.96%), alkaloids (12.70%), and phenolic acids (11.39%) as the dominant categories. The major representative compounds were 2-amino-4-hydroxy-3-methylpentanoic acid, 4-methylazetidine-2-carboxylic acid, and 4-nitrophenol, respectively (Fig. 2B, Table S4). A total of 381 conserved metabolites were shared between stems and leaves, mainly comprising amino acids and their derivatives (19.42%), lipids (15.49%), alkaloids (11.81%), and phenolic acids (10.50%). The most abundant representative metabolites were DL-leucine, palmitaldehyde, vanillylamine, and 4-nitrophenol, respectively (Fig. 2C, Table S5), indicating that these compounds form a stable chemical basis across different tissues of P. oleracea. The stems contained 134 stem-specific conserved metabolites, predominantly flavonoids (17.91%) and amino acids and their derivatives (13.43%), along with considerable proportions of phenolic acids (12.69%), organic acids (11.19%), and lipids (10.45%). Representative metabolites were 3-O-acetylpinobanksin, L-glycyl-L-phenylalanine, 6-O-caffeoyl-d-glucose, citric acid, and γ-linolenic acid, respectively (Fig. 2D, Table S6). The leaves contained 251 leaf-specific conserved metabolites, mainly composed of amino acids and their derivatives (19.92%), alkaloids (16.73%), and phenolic acids (14.34%). In contrast, flavonoids were the most abundant class among the stem-specific metabolites, highlighting the tissue specificity of secondary metabolite accumulation in the stem. Further comparative analysis showed that stem-specific conserved metabolites exhibited greater structural diversity. In addition to amino acids and their derivatives, the stems were enriched in flavonoids, phenolic acids, and organic acids, suggesting more active defense-related and antioxidant metabolic pathways. Conversely, the leaf-specific metabolites were mainly composed of amino acids and their derivatives, alkaloids, and phenolic acids, which together accounted for more than 50% of the total, indicating that leaf metabolism is more active in nitrogen utilization, signaling regulation, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis. These results reveal a clear chemical and metabolic functional differentiation between the stem and leaf tissues of P. oleracea, providing a biochemical basis for further elucidation of its tissue-specific metabolic pathways and physiological functions.

Tissue-specific accumulation and biosynthetic pathways of P. oleracea-unique metabolites

To further elucidate the chemical composition and secondary metabolic characteristics of P. oleracea, a systematic analysis of its representative metabolites was conducted. Although many of these compounds have been reported in other plants, they generally occur at higher abundance or display unique structural modifications in P. oleracea. Some even bear the plant's name (e.g., portulacanones and oleraceins), highlighting its species-specific metabolic traits. Comparative analysis of metabolite distribution, chemical features, and potential functions among different tissues (stems and leaves) and cultivars provides insights into its metabolic regulation related to antioxidant defense, lipid metabolism, and tissue specialization, thereby offering a metabolic basis for the development of its bioactive compounds and quality differentiation.

Based on structural characteristics and chemical classification, the distinctive metabolites of P. oleracea can be divided into six major categories: flavonoids, isoflavones, alkaloids, plumerane-type phenolic compounds, free fatty acids, and glycerol esters (Table S16). Discussion

Research objective

The pursuit of nutritional value in food by consumers is driving the development and utilization of wild edible plant resources. In this study, we take P. oleracea as a representative to systematically develop this wild vegetable and to extend the exploration and promotion of development and utilization pathways applicable to other wild vegetables. Given that P. oleracea is often traditionally regarded as a field weed in public perception, our research also aims to integrate science communication to help the public change attitudes and understanding, thereby enhancing awareness and application potential of wild vegetable resources. Consequently, a comprehensive and systematic grasp of the metabolic profile of P. oleracea is required. Reviewing existing research, several prior efforts have laid important foundations: Farag and Shakour (2019) conducted a metabolomic study on three different P. oleracea, identifying 85 metabolites and providing chemical evidence for its nutritional and health benefits; Siriamornpun and Suttajit (2010) measured total phenolics and total flavonoids in the leaves, stems, and flowers of Thai wild P. oleracea, identifying 8 phenolic acids and 5 flavonoids (as well as total flavonoids), and highlighting P. oleracea as a high-quality vegetable for human diets; Nemzer et al. (2020) compared the nutritional and phytochemical composition of cultivated versus wild P. oleracea, identifying 184 compounds and demonstrating P. oleracea's potential as a raw material for nutraceuticals. Previous studies have been limited by technical constraints, resulting in relatively few identified metabolites and a lack of a systematic, comprehensive understanding of P. oleracea's metabolome. To gain a more complete understanding of P. oleracea, we selected three distinct materials, each with unique characteristics, and performed broad-target metabolomics for comparative analysis. In total, 1315 metabolites were identified, spanning categories such as amino acids, phenolic acids, flavonoids, fatty acids, alkaloids, and others. The results reveal discernible metabolic differences among crop varieties as well as among organs, underscoring both variety-specific and organ-specific metabolic features. These findings not only expand the coverage of P. oleracea metabolite identification but also provide a data foundation for subsequent investigations into mechanisms underlying organ-specific and varietal variation. Moreover, they offer scientific evidence for the high-value utilization of P. oleracea as a wild vegetable resource and for the selection of germplasm in future exploitation efforts.

Conserved metabolites and potential nutritional value

A considerable number of studies have been conducted on metabolomics and food nutrition. Zhang et al. (2025) analyzed the aroma characteristics of teas prepared from 25 goji varieties, including goji leaf white tea and green tea, and identified seven high-quality varieties suitable for tea processing. Lee et al. (2025) conducted a dry-matter-based quantitative analysis of total saponin content across 15 spinach varieties, selecting two high-potential cultivars with substantial saponin levels. Zheng et al. (2025) investigated six apple varieties for their molecular constituents, revealing the molecular basis of excellent edible quality in apples and providing guidance for the selection and breeding of high-quality cultivars. It is evident that prior research has largely focused on metabolic variation between different varieties and its relationship to varietal traits, while attention to potential commonalities or stable components within the metabolic network has been limited. This study is the first to propose and systematically define the concept of “conserved metabolites” in P. oleracea, aiming to reveal the intrinsic stability of its metabolic network from a novel perspective and to clarify the potential significance of these conserved components in food chemistry and biological function. As the most stable and representative components within the metabolic system, conserved metabolites can be regarded as biochemical markers that define the intrinsic nutritional characteristics and functional stability of P. oleracea, providing scientific evidence for elucidating the species' core nutritional attributes. Furthermore, they offer theoretical support and practical guidance for developing P. oleracea as a health food resource with consistent quality and well-defined functionality. Additionally, the term “conserved metabolites” provides a clearer interpretive framework for public science communication by highlighting stable and representative key components, thereby promoting evidence-based understanding of the fundamental nutritional properties and potential functions of P. oleracea and supporting informed dietary choices and effective outreach.

Conserved metabolites in P. oleracea can be regarded as taxon-specific markers for this plant. Notable examples include Allium fistulosum, where isoalliin represent a clearly characteristic metabolite feature (Liao et al., 2022); Capsicum spp., where capsaicinoids, exemplified by capsaicin, have diagnostic significance (Sun et al., 2022); Camellia sinensis, where catechins, theaflavins, and caffeine serve as markers associated with processing methods and cultivar differences (Muthulingam et al., 2025); Solanum lycopersicum, with lycopene as the core enriched metabolite and other carotenoids also providing discriminative value (Zhang et al., 2018); and Citrus spp., where major markers such as limonene and naringin are commonly used to distinguish cultivar, maturity, and origin (Bacanlı et al., 2015). In this study, we identified 381 conserved metabolites in the stems and leaves of three different cultivars of P. oleracea, including various amino acids and derivatives (19.42%), Lipids (15.49%), Alkaloids (11.81%), phenolic acids (10.50%), and other bioactive compounds. This finding is broadly consistent with the results reported by Nemzer et al. (2020). The richness of these metabolites not only reflects the chemical diversity of P. oleracea but also highlights its significant value in traditional medicine and modern nutrition, indicating that both the leaves and the stems may confer similar health benefits. Studies indicate that amino acids such as glycine-phenylalanine (Glu-Phe) and arginine-glycine (Arg-Gly) can bolster immune function, promote cellular repair, and exhibit anti-inflammatory effects (Chaisri et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2017). Xiu et al. (2019) notes that P. oleracea contains antioxidant alkaloids; Silva and Carvalho (2014) found that its phenolic extracts provide significant protection against DNA damage induced by hydroxyl radicals. Regarding lipids, early research shows that purslane is one of the most abundant sources of ω-3 and ω-6 fatty acids among terrestrial plants (Melilli et al., 2020; Simopoulos et al., 1992), and these fatty acids have potential benefits for anti-aging and metabolic health (Liu et al., 2020). However, the metabolites listed above represent only a small subset of the 381 conserved metabolites identified in P. oleracea, suggesting that its benefits extend beyond these compounds. The functions of the remaining metabolites warrant further investigation.

The analysis of specific conserved metabolites in the stems and leaves of P. oleracea shows that both parts contain various bioactive compounds, demonstrating significant potential for promoting human health. Notably, flavonoids and organic acids are significantly more abundant in the stems than in the leaves, while alkaloids, lignans, and coumarins are found in higher concentrations in the leaves compared to the stems. Flavonoid metabolites such as sakuranetin show strong anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer activities in various plant extracts, with positive effects on cardiovascular health (Prathap et al., 2021; Rauf et al., 2024), which provides new possibilities for its use as a functional food ingredient (Velderrain-Rodriguez et al., 2021). Organic acid metabolites, such as citric acid, serve as effective food additives with antioxidant and preservative properties (Lacorn & Hektor, 2025). They can also reduce stone formation by increasing citrate concentration in urine (Phillips et al., 2015). Alkaloid metabolites, including 5-hydroxytryptophol, are directly related to mental health and may serve as potential therapeutic targets for depression and anxiety (Javelle et al., 2020). Lignans and coumarins, such as esculetin, exhibit a wide range of biological activities and have been shown to possess significant antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antitumor properties, and show potential for treating Alzheimer's disease (Duan et al., 2020; Pruccoli et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2020). Additionally, in traditional Chinese medicine, P. oleracea is believed to possess heat-clearing, detoxifying, and diuretic properties, which may be attributed to compounds such as isoscopoletin-β-D-glucoside (Xiang et al., 2011). The stable presence of conserved metabolites in the stems and leaves means that consumers can obtain corresponding nutrients and health benefits whether they consume the leaves or the stems of P. oleracea.

Characteristic metabolites and potential nutritional functions and utilization of different cultivars of P. oleracea

The analysis of differential metabolites provides key insights into the nutritional components and functional characteristics of plants. In this study, we identified a series of metabolites that were significantly upregulated among different cultivars of P. oleracea and between its leaf and stem tissues. These differential metabolites not only reflect the physiological and biochemical diversity among the cultivars but also directly relate to their flavor and nutritional value. Siriamornpun and Suttajit (2010) reported that the total phenolics and total flavonoids in P. oleracea leaves are significantly higher than in the stem, and that leaves are rich in α-linolenic acid. Nemzer et al. (2020) arrived at a similar conclusion, showing that the metabolic activity and nutritional enrichment of stems are lower than those of leaves, with leaves exhibiting greater nutritional potential in ω-3 fatty acid enrichment, antioxidant constituents (phenolics and carotenoids), and vitamins and minerals. Building on these findings, our study further expands the existing knowledge. The number of metabolites enriched in leaves is 259, significantly higher than the 17 found in stems, indicating markedly higher metabolic activity in leaves; this aligns with Nemzer et al. (2020). The metabolites enriched in stems include seven classes—Phenolic acids, Flavonoids, Lipids, among others—these seven classes are also enriched in leaves, though the specific metabolites and their quantities differ. Moreover, we found that leaves are enriched with organic acids, terpenoids, and quinones, which were not enriched in stems. This advances our understanding of the leaf–stem metabolic differences. Regarding fatty acids, leaves not only enriched α-linolenic acid but also contained γ-linolenic acid, a finding not reported by previous studies. The metabolic activity differences between stems and leaves reveal P. oleracea's adaptive capacity to external environmental pressures, as well as the complementary metabolic strategies employed by different plant organs. This insight is also of considerable significance in the food-development field. The diversity and abundance of compounds in the leaves highlight their higher value, suggesting potential use as fresh vegetables; the stems may have potential applications as sources of plant extracts based on their biological characteristics. These potential advantages require further empirical research to delineate their scope and limitations and to evaluate their feasibility in actual product development.

To elucidate the metabolic characteristics of different P. oleracea cultivars, six sample groups (1-J, 2-J, 3-J, 1-Y, 2-Y, 3-Y) were subjected to independent metabolomic analyses. Differentially accumulated metabolites were identified in each group and defined as characteristic metabolic fingerprint markers for the respective cultivars and tissue types. The results showed that Cultivar 1 possessed the highest total number of fingerprint markers in both stems and leaves, indicating the richest metabolite diversity and relatively high metabolic activity. This suggests that Cultivar 1 may exhibit broader nutritional and physiological functional potential. In contrast, Cultivar 2 displayed the greatest difference in the number of marker metabolites between stems and leaves, reflecting pronounced metabolic differentiation among tissues. This pattern implies strong tissue specificity in the synthesis and accumulation of metabolites and highlights distinct functional roles and regulatory mechanisms between stems and leaves in this cultivar. Specifically, Oleracein D was highly abundant in 1-J and is reported to possess antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities. In 1-Y, multiple glycerol ester compounds, including 1-α-linolenoyl-glycerol-3-O-glucoside, 1-α-linolenoyl-glycerol-2,3-di-O-glucoside, 2-α-linolenoyl-glycerol-1,3-di-O-glucoside, and 2-α-linolenoyl-glycerol-1-O-glucoside, were significantly enriched. These metabolites are involved in the regulation of lipid metabolism, improvement of cell membrane integrity, and energy supply, thereby contributing positively to metabolic homeostasis (Xue et al., 2017). In 2-Y, Oleracein H, Oleracein A, Oleracein P, and Oleracein C were markedly enriched; these compounds exhibit strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, enabling the scavenging of reactive oxygen species and delaying cellular senescence (Zhou et al., 2015). In 2-J, pyridoxine (Vitamin B6) was significantly accumulated and is known to promote protein and amino acid metabolism, maintain normal nervous system function, enhance immune responses, and support energy metabolism (Wassie et al., 2022). Furthermore, 3-J was enriched in 17-hydroxylinolenic acid and 1-α-linolenoyl-glycerol, whereas 3-Y contained high levels of γ-linolenic acid and α-linolenic acid. These unsaturated fatty acids are closely associated with brain development, improvement of blood lipid profiles, and a reduced risk of cardiovascular diseases (Forouhi et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2021). In summary, the three cultivars exhibited pronounced differences in characteristic metabolite composition between stems and leaves. For example, Cultivar 1 showed a high abundance of glycerol esters in leaves, such as 1-α-linolenoyl-glycerol-3-O-glucoside; Cultivar 2 was enriched in Oleracein series compounds in leaves and accumulated pyridoxine in stems; and Cultivar 3 contained higher levels of γ-linolenic acid, α-linolenic acid, and other unsaturated fatty acids. These patterns suggest cultivar-specific emphases in nutritional composition and potential bioactivities, providing candidate evidence to inform subsequent germplasm selection and functional product development. Importantly, the proposed applications are hypothesis-generating and based on relative metabolite abundances; the actual functional effects and product feasibility require further evaluation, including quantitative determination of key bioactive compounds, stability and processing suitability assessments, sensory analysis, and in vivo bioavailability and efficacy validation.

Research significance and outlook

This study systematically revealed the conserved and specific patterns of secondary metabolism among different tissues and cultivars of P. oleracea, providing a new perspective for understanding metabolic homeostasis in plants. By constructing a dual-layer analytical framework of “conservation–divergence,” the research enables more precise identification of functional metabolites and provides a theoretical basis for the resource utilization and genetic improvement of P. oleracea. The analysis of conserved metabolites tags P. oleracea as a distinctive crop, representing an important hallmark that differentiates it from other crops, while the comparison between stems and leaves not only provides insights into tissue-specific metabolic regulatory mechanisms but also lays the foundation for evaluating the nutritional characteristics and functional value of P. oleracea and its derived food products, thereby promoting high-value food innovation and formulation optimization. The analysis of differential metabolites underpins the development of metabolic fingerprints to distinguish the three materials studied, resulting in high-resolution characteristic patterns capable of differentiating the samples. Building on this, a systematic analysis integrating metabolite profiling with functional assessment—and validating the potential impact of key metabolites on product functionality, alongside consumer-oriented sensory and market studies—can be conducted to enhance the translation from data to application. Ultimately, a comprehensive model linking “metabolic features–functional attributes–application value” can be established, providing scientific support for the high-value development of P. oleracea. Notably, this study identified a large number of metabolites, including Quinolinic Acid, Hesperetin-7-O-glucoside, and Crepenynic acid, all of which were reported for the first time in P. oleracea. This highlights the comprehensiveness and systematic nature of our research. However, this study focuses solely on metabolites, and our understanding of the biosynthetic pathways of P. oleracea metabolites remains somewhat limited. Future work may further integrate transcriptomics, proteomics, and single-cell metabolomics to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying tissue-specific metabolic regulation.

For a long time, P. oleracea has been regarded as an inconspicuous wild herb in the fields and has not received due attention. Scientific understanding of this plant remains limited, and its nutritional and health values are often overlooked in modern dietary systems. However, in traditional Chinese culture, P. oleracea has long been studied from an alternative perspective by ancient scholars, forming a cognate framework that complements modern science. Traditional Chinese medicine has long emphasized the concept of “homology of medicine and food” and the theory of “five colors nourishing the five organs.” P. oleracea has been referred to as the “five-element vegetable” (wuxing cai) because its roots, stems, leaves, flowers, and seeds can exhibit diverse colors such as white, red, green, yellow, and black. Despite variations in coloration among cultivars and tissues, this terminology reflects an experiential understanding of its morphological diversity and functional attributes, resonating with the traditional idea of “recognizing properties by color and achieving health through food.” As an integral part of nature, humans have, throughout the long course of evolution and continuous food development, constantly derived nutrients and energy from diverse plant resources, thereby forming a relationship of mutual dependence and dynamic adaptation with nature—a manifestation of the harmonious principle of coexistence and mutual reinforcement among all living things. From a broader perspective, such harmony and balance within life systems also echo the traditional Chinese philosophy of “the unity of man and nature” and “the coexistence of all beings,” embodying the intrinsic logic of self-regulation and co-evolution within natural systems.

The present study highlights its abundant metabolic features and remarkable bioactive potential, indicating its dual significance in scientific research and practical application. With the continuous advancement of research on functional foods and natural nutritional resources, the unique nutrient composition and health benefits of P. oleracea are being rediscovered. Looking back at dietary history, many of today's staple vegetables have likewise undergone a transition from wild resources to domesticated cultivation and, ultimately, large-scale supply; driven by domestication and breeding, standardized production, and industrial promotion, wild edible plants such as P. oleracea are also expected to gradually enter broader consumer and retail markets. It is anticipated that, in the near future, P. oleracea may no longer be seen merely as a neglected wild vegetable, but rather as a valuable dietary resource that rightfully earns its place on people's tables as part of a sustainable and health-promoting diet.

Conclusion

Study design and scope

This study employed a widely targeted metabolomics approach to comprehensively profile and jointly analyze the metabolic signatures of stems and leaves across Portulaca oleracea L. cultivars.

Metabolic differentiation and tissue-specificity

(I) Significant metabolic differentiation observed across cultivars and organs, with leaves showing higher metabolic activity and serving as the primary site for enrichment of bioactive compounds.

(II) A total of 1315 metabolites identified, with pronounced cultivar-tissue differentiation.

(III) Conserved metabolites: 381 common to both stems and leaves; 134 stem-specific conserved metabolites; 251 leaf-specific conserved metabolites.

These consistently present metabolites constitute the plant's core nutritional foundation as a food source, providing reliable chemical indicators for assessing basic quality and nutritional value.

Cultivar-specific enrichment patterns

(I) Cultivar 1: 32 stem-specific and 48 leaf-specific enriched metabolites.

(II) Cultivar 2: 10 stem-specific and 55 leaf-specific enriched metabolites.

(III) Cultivar 3: 13 stem-specific and 28 leaf-specific enriched metabolites.

The cultivar-specific enrichment differences offer a precise scientific basis for targeted development of P. oleracea as a functional food and for the selection of high-value germplasm resources.

Implications and applications

(I) Conserved metabolites provide a robust framework for quality and nutritional assessment.

(II) Leaf tissues, due to higher activity and enrichment of phenolic acids, flavonoids, oleraceins, and functional lipids, are especially relevant for functional food design.

(III) The demonstrated cultivar-specific metabolite profiles guide breeding, germplasm improvement, and targeted product development in P. oleracea.
