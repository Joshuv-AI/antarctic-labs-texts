Abnormal circadian pattern of urinary sodium excretion and chronic kidney disease progression
Authors: Lingling Liu, Sirui Liu, Menglei Jv, Shengnan Ge, Kehang Xie, Qing Xie, Hui Peng, Cheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAbnormal circadian pattern of urinary sodium excretion was associated with high blood pressure and target-organ injury. However, whether urinary circadian pattern of sodium excretion is associated with chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression has not been elucidated.MethodsWe evaluated 1604 participants with CKD in this retrospective cohort study. We studied the association between clinical outcomes and day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion, using urine collected separately during daytime and nighttime. The primary outcome was defined as a decrease of ≥ 30% in eGFR from baseline or initiation of renal-replacement therapy. The secondary outcome was a decrease of ≥ 50% in eGFR from baseline or initiation of renal-replacement therapy.ResultsThe primary and secondary outcome occurred in 319 and 247 patients during 5710.2 person-years of follow-up, respectively. The Kaplan-Meier (KM) analysis showed an association between lower day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion and primary and secondary outcome (both P < 0.05). In multivariate analysis, fully adjusted hazard ratios (95% confidence intervals) for patients with day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion in the lowest quartile were 2.29 (1.55–3.39) for the primary outcome and 2.42 (1.53–3.85) for the secondary outcome, compared to the highest quartile.ConclusionAbnormal circadian pattern of urinary sodium excretion, characterized by a lower day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion, is associated with CKD progression in CKD patients. These findings suggest day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion may serve as a marker to recognize CKD progression.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-04825-0.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, Circadian rhythm, Renal progression, Sodium, Urine

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a common noncommunicable disease, with a prevalence of 8% to 16% worldwide [1–3]. Hypertension has a vital role in the occurrence and development of CKD, and it is also a controllable factor for CKD [4]. Mechanisms underlying hypertension in CKD patients include sodium retention, volume overload, and activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system [4]. Sodium excretion is intimately associated with these mechanisms. Kidney is the most important organ for sodium excretion [5], which becomes abnormal when the kidney is combined with impairment such as CKD [6, 7]. There are two important aspects of sodium excretion from kidneys: the total amount and the circadian pattern [8]. A normal kidney excretes much more sodium during the day than during night, which is recognized as normal circadian pattern of urinary sodium excretion [9]. It’s been established that 24-hour urinary sodium excretion is related to hypertension in patients with CKD [10–12]. Moreover, abnormal circadian pattern of urinary sodium excretion was found to be closely associated with increased nocturnal blood pressure (BP) in the general population and correlated with elevated office BP in CKD patients [13]. Our recent study of patients with CKD also showed that abnormal circadian pattern of urinary sodium excretion was correlated with higher nocturnal BP and blunted nocturnal dipping [14], which are well known as risk factors for CKD progression [15, 16].

In light of the association between urinary excretion of sodium and hypertension, one might expect that sodium excretion can be predictive of prognosis in CKD participants. A series of studies have evaluated the association between 24-hour sodium excretion and clinical outcomes in patients with CKD. There is evidence making paradoxical conclusions on the interaction between 24-hour urinary sodium excretion and CKD [17–25]. Some studies showed that higher sodium excretion was associated with a worse prognosis [17–20]. While other studies found a negative [21] or no association [22–25] between them. It suggests that the relationship between urinary sodium excretion and clinical outcome in CKD may be more complicated than we imagine. Indeed, many factors may contribute to the contradictory outcomes, including characteristics of the population (race, sex, age, et al.), sensitivity to salt [26–29], transport capacity along distal nephron [30], hormone/peptide [31], declined renal function, and drugs that regulate the renin-angiotensin system [32]. However, less is known about the relationship between circadian rhythm of urinary sodium excretion and clinical outcome. A previous study found that CKD participants with abnormal circadian pattern of urinary sodium excretion exhibited severer target-organ injury [33]. However, whether the urinary circadian pattern of sodium excretion is associated with CKD progression has not been elucidated.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between circadian pattern of urinary sodium excretion and CKD progression using day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion tests.

Methods

This is a retrospective, dual-center, observational cohort study conducted in the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University (Zhuhai, China) and the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University (Guangzhou, China). We enrolled 2096 individuals with CKD from March 2011 to December 2019. In the participating centers, patients were routinely followed every 6 months through outpatient or inpatient re-examination or telephone visits. The participants were monitored until death or an outcome occurs or December 2022. The CKD patients were included in this study if they were ≥ 18 years old, with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) less than 60 ml/min/1.73 m2 for at least 3 months [34], or with evidence of kidney injury (albuminuria, or pathologic or structural abnormalities confirmed by kidney biopsy or imageology examination) [35]. Exclusion criteria included: a previous dialysis or a kidney transplant; a single kidney (with the other kidney surgically removed or congenitally absent); advanced heart failure; liver failure or liver cirrhosis; history of malignancy; and current pregnancy. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the participating centers (No. [2023] K55-1). Written informed consent was signed by all participants.

Measurements

Patient data were obtained from patient interviews, medical records and physical examinations, and recorded by trained clinical research physicians at enrollment. Main baseline laboratory variables included serum concentrations of hemoglobin, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), uric acid, glucose, albumin, triglyceride, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), phosphorus and intact parathyroid hormone (iPTH). We used the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation to calculate eGFR based on serum creatinine levels [34].

During the period of monitoring, all patients were asked to continue with their usual eating habits and activities while not providing unified meals and avoiding severe physical activity. In the participating centers, urine was routinely collected separately during daytime and nighttime on the second day of admission. According to the patients’ reported timings for getting up and going to bed, daytime and nighttime periods were determined [36, 37]. The first urine in the morning was treated as nighttime urine. All the patients were asked to try not to hold in their urine. The full-time research nurses checked if the urine was truly collected, and patients would be asked to provide a new specimen if a full collection was not feasible. Levels of urinary sodium were measured using a Cobas c702 automatic biochemical analyzer (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). 24-hour sodium excretion and urinary protein were calculated using daytime parameters plus nighttime parameters.

Measurement of office BP and ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) follows a standard protocol, which is described elsewhere [14]. Patients who had BP data missing for 2 h continuously, and those who woke up during the night and had difficulty falling back asleep were excluded. High nocturnal SBP (≥ 120mmHg) and riser (or reverse dipper) (a nocturnal BP decline of > 0% of daytime values) status were defined according to international guidelines [38].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was defined as a decrease of ≥ 30% in eGFR from baseline or initiation of renal-replacement therapy [23], whichever occurred first. The secondary outcome was a decrease of ≥ 50% in eGFR from baseline or initiation of renal-replacement therapy [17]. Follow-up was censored at the time of death or end of the follow-up period, whichever occurred first. Survival time was defined as the period of time from enrollment to the date of death, outcome, or December 2022.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median [interquartile range (IQR)]. We used Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to examine the distribution of the data. Numbers (n) and proportions (%) of participants are used to report categorical variables. Baseline parameters of the patients are expressed by quartile of day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion.

Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate cumulative hazard rates using the log rank test. The cumulative incidence function for a competing risk was used to calculate cumulative outcomes and the Gray test was used to compare the difference between the curves [39]. Cox proportional hazard models were used to evaluate the unadjusted (Model 1) and adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) of CKD progression by quartile of day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion. In Model 2, we adjusted for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities (diabetes and cardio-cerebrovascular disease), smoking history, dyslipidemia and alcohol drinking habit. In model 3, eGFR and 24-hour urinary sodium were further included. In model 4, we further added 24-hour proteinuria, office SBP, office DBP, and medications, including renin-angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitors, other antihypertensive drugs and corticosteroids.

Analyses of subgroups were used to examine the predictive value of the day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion regarding the group of sex, age, diabetes, use of RAS inhibitors, eGFR, 24-hour urinary sodium excretion, high nocturnal SBP, SBP riser, cardio-cerebrovascular disease and tubulointerstitial nephropathies, adjusting for important covariables for CKD progression. Sensitivity analysis was performed in individuals without a history of cardio-cerebrovascular disease, and taking antihypertensive drugs, and in multivariate models including more confounders (including 24-hour urinary protein, nocturnal BP, and additional clinical parameters). Analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2) and SPSS (version 26.0, SPSS Inc.), with a P value less than 0.05 considered significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 1604 patients were eventually included for analysis (Fig. 1). Participants in the study had a mean day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion of 1.62 (1.04–2.75). Patients were divided into quartiles according to the day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion. Compared to patients in the higher quartile of day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion, those who had a lower day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion were older; more likely to have diabetes, cardio-cerebrovascular disease and SBP riser; had lower hemoglobin and eGFR; and had higher nocturnal SBP and office SBP (Table 1).

Day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion and CKD progression

During 5710.2 person-years of follow-up, 319 primary outcome events (55.9/1000 person years) and 247 secondary events (42.8/1000 person-years) were reported. Lower day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion was associated with significantly higher cumulative incidence of primary and secondary outcome (both P < 0.05, Fig. 2). Cumulative incidence of a competing risk revealed a pattern that was similar (Supplemental Fig. 1).

Association of day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion with cumulative hazard rates of CKD progression. Kaplan-Meier plots of cumulative hazard rates of primary outcome(A), and secondary outcome (B) according to the quartile of day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion. P value was evaluated by Log Rank test

The risk for primary and secondary outcome increased from the highest to the lowest quartile of day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion (Fig. 3, Supplemental Table 1). In Cox regression models, the highest unadjusted hazard ratios of CKD progression were observed in the lowest quartile of day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion (≤ 1.04) (model 1 in Table 2, both P < 0.05). The hazard ratios of the lowest quartile were also higher than those in the higher quartiles even after adjusting for sociodemographic parameters, comorbid diseases, and laboratory variables (models 2 and 3 in Table 2, all P < 0.05). Hazard ratios [95% confidence intervals (CIs)] for the lowest quartile of day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion were 2.29 (1.55–3.39) for primary outcome, and 2.42 (1.53–3.85) for the secondary outcome when compared to quartile four, after further adjusting for use of medications (including renin-angiotensin inhibitors, other antihypertensive agents, and corticosteroids) (model 4 in Table 2, both P < 0.05).

Subgroup analyses

We compared the risk of the lowest quartile of day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion with the highest quartile in different subgroups stratified by sex (men and women), age (< 60 or ≥ 60 years), diabetes status (with or without), use of RAS inhibitors (no or yes), eGFR (< 60 or ≥ 60 ml/min/1.73 m2), 24-hour urinary sodium excretion (≤ median or > median), high nocturnal SBP (no or yes), SBP riser (no or yes), cardio-cerebrovascular disease (no or yes) and tubulointerstitial nephropathies (no or yes). Interactions between day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion and subgroups were not significant (all P > 0.01), suggesting the association between day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion persisted regardless of these factors (Fig. 4). We found similar results in subgroup analyses for the secondary outcome (Supplemental Fig. 2).

Sensitivity analyses

In sensitivity analyses, overall similar findings were observed in individuals without a history of cardio-cerebrovascular disease (Supplemental Table 2), and individuals taking antihypertensive drugs (Supplemental Table 3). For the primary outcome, the risk of the lowest quartile was 1.85 and 2.60 times that of the highest quartile (all P < 0.05). When further adjusting for more confounders including nocturnal BP, and riser status (model 2), and additional important risk factors for prognosis in CKD patients (model 3) (Supplemental Table 4), the corresponding hazard ratios (95% CIs) were 2.08 (1.40–3.08) and 2.37 (1.60–3.51) for primary outcome, respectively (all P < 0.05). And the hazard ratios (95% CIs) were 2.11 (1.33–3.37) and 2.62 (1.63–4.20) for the secondary outcome, respectively (all P < 0.05).

Discussion

Our study revealed that a low day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion is associated with primary and secondary outcome. This association is still significant after adjustments for confounders including baseline eGFR, 24-hour urinary sodium, comorbid diseases, office BP, and use of antihypertensive medications. The findings were consistent across major study subgroups. Our findings help to better understand the clinical value of the circadian pattern of urinary sodium excretion in patients with CKD.

Evidence on the association between circadian pattern of sodium excretion and prognosis among CKD patients is scarce. However, the association between circadian pattern of sodium excretion and target-organ damage in CKD participants has been investigated in a few studies [33]. Zhang et al. observed that CKD patients with abnormal circadian pattern of urinary sodium excretion were more likely to have left ventricular hypertrophy, decreased renal function, and severer carotid intima-media thickness, supporting a lower day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion may help to recognize CKD progression. However, this study did not include follow-up or analysis of prognosis among CKD patients. Our results clearly showed that CKD participants with abnormal circadian pattern, characterized by lower day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion, tended to have a more rapid CKD progression.

There are several explanations for poor renal outcome in CKD patients with a low day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion. First, we found that patients with decreased day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion had elevated nocturnal blood pressure and higher proportion of riser. Our findings are consistent with previous studies suggesting that decreased sodium excretion during daytime is linked to elevated nocturnal BP and non-dipping [13, 14, 40], which is considered a risk factor for the advancement of CKD. Our findings are consistent with the hypothesis that decreased renal excretion of sodium during daytime increases nocturnal BP, compensating for impaired daytime natriuresis through enhanced pressure natriuresis. In addition, previous studies have found that urinary sodium excretion during the nighttime increases (day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion decreases) as renal function declines [6, 41]. In this study, patients in low quartile of day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion had a reduced eGFR, which may strengthen the relationship between day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion and CKD progression. However, abnormal circadian rhythm-related increase in BP and decrease in renal function might not be the only explanation for CKD progression in participants with CKD. Since significant relationship between day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion and CKD progression still remained even after adjusting eGFR, office BP, nocturnal BP and riser status. Similarly, a randomized controlled trial involving 661 CKD patients found that receiving at least one antihypertensive drug at night reduced the incidence of cardiovascular events to one-third compared to receiving all medications in the morning [42]. The reduction in cardiovascular events appears to be disproportionate to the modest decrease in blood pressure, suggesting that the effect of circadian rhythms on clinical outcome could be independent of blood pressure.

Previous studies and our findings suggest that circadian rhythm per se may affect renal health. It is well known that kidney is one of the most important organs for maintaining sodium homeostasis. Most of the sodium is excreted through the kidneys, whose function determines the ability to excrete sodium [43]. This is why accumulating studies have used 24-hour urinary sodium excretion as an indicator of sodium intake. Indeed, the ability to excrete sodium involves not only the total amount but also the circadian rhythm. As early as the 1950s, studies have established that renal function is rhythmic [44–46]. It’s found that electrolytes, including urinary sodium, were excreted in a 24-hour rhythm, as well as urine output and pH. To be noted, these experiments were carried out under relatively stable conditions, with variables such as fluids, food intake, posture, and sleep all controlled. It was possible to conclude that rhythmic changes were endogenous rather than caused by behavior or posture. Circadian rhythm disturbances such as sleep, blood pressure, and proteinuria are often present in CKD. Decreased renal function in humans is associated with reduced sleep duration and increased sleep fragmentation [47]. In addition, circadian disturbance is prior to the onset of microalbuminuria or other clinical evidence of renal impairment in patients with type 1 diabetes [48]. Furthermore, animal experiments have shown that mice harboring a deletion of the circadian CLOCK protein are susceptible to CKD after adenine induction when compared with wild-type mice [49], suggesting the underlying molecular mechanism. From a microscopic point of view, abnormal renal excretion of electrolyte rhythm may be the result of local renal circadian clocks of different cells in the kidney [50, 51], closely related to the function of the kidney itself. These findings imply that circadian disruption may increase the risk of kidney disease. Moreover, there may be a bidirectional relationship between CKD and circadian rhythm disturbances, whereby renal disease causes irregularities in circadian rhythms and circadian disruption deteriorates renal dysfunction.

It should be noted that circadian pattern of urinary sodium excretion in this study mainly focuses on the diurnal pattern. Given that circadian disorganization of sodium excretion was significantly associated with CKD progression, the day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion may be taken into account in clinical practice. We should admit that our findings cannot determine the causal relationship between low day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion and CKD progression. The lower eGFR, higher nocturnal blood pressure, and increased prevalence of cardiovascular diseases observed in patients with a low day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion cannot fully exclude that abnormal circadian pattern was accompanied by more advanced CKD, even though these covariates were adjusted.

Our study contains a few limitations. First, the nature of this study is observational, thus, we cannot make causal conclusions. Second, sodium intake has not been assessed in this study. Thus, we cannot exclude the influence of diet on the day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion. Still, it should be noted that various factors may cause bias even if diet is included, such as lack of knowledge on the salt contents of many foods, patients’ imprecise reporting, and recall bias. Third, day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion was not evaluated repeatedly in this study. Fourth, only inpatient Chinese were included in our study, and glomerulonephritis was the most common cause of CKD [15]. Thus, we should be cautious about extrapolating the conclusions to the Western population. Finally, night-shift workers were not excluded, which may cause bias.

In conclusion, abnormal circadian pattern of urinary sodium excretion, characterized by a low day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion, was associated with kidney disease progression in CKD patients. These findings suggest day: night ratio of urinary sodium excretion may serve as a marker to recognize CKD progression.
