Giant right atrial myxoma emerging from the suprahepatic inferior vena cava, extending to the right atrium; a case report and literature review
Authors: Ahmad K. Darwazah, Maroun Helou, Younis Yasin, Raed Masalma, Anas Tuqan, Anas Barabrah, Basel Zaben, Bashar Yaghi, Moath Nairat, Hasan Al-Zammar, Abdelhalim Abu Haltam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Atrial myxomas are the most common primary cardiac tumors. In 20% of cases, they arise from the right atrium. Only a few such tumors are reported to have arisen from the inferior vena cava. We present the case of a 20-year-old female with recurrent vomiting and supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), found to have a massive myxoma emerging from the suprahepatic Inferior vena cava (IVC) involving the eustachian valve, and extending to the right atrium. Surgical excision was successful, leading to an uneventful recovery. This case underscores the importance of prompt diagnosis and surgical intervention in managing right atrial myxomas to prevent potential complications such as embolism and sudden death.

Introduction

Atrial myxomas are the most common primary cardiac tumors. Approximately 75% of primary cardiac tumors are benign, and myxoma accounts for almost half of all benign tumors in the heart. About 75% originate in the left atrium, and only 15–20% originate from the right atrium [1, 2] Very rarely, they originate from the IVC or Eustachian valve. The clinical symptoms of myxomas are determined by their location, size, and mobility. For most patients, one or more of a triad of embolism, intracardiac obstruction, and constitutional symptoms are present. However, a symptom-free presentation can be seen, especially with smaller tumors. Treatment is surgical excision of the mass, which should be carried out promptly after a diagnosis is established, because of the increased possibility of embolism and sudden death [1].

We present a case of a 20-year-old female who presented with recurrent vomiting and supraventricular tachycardia (SVT). Cardiac imaging revealed the presence of a large right atrial myxoma. She was successfully treated with surgical excision of the myxoma.

Case presentation

A 20-year-old female patient who was unknown to have any chronic diseases presented with a chief complaint of new-onset palpitations associated with repeated vomiting that has been persistent for the past seven months. There have been no other significant symptoms associated with the presenting complaint.

A physical exam was conducted which revealed regular tachycardia without any added murmurs. Initial investigations included an electrocardiogram (ECG), which revealed a supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), her blood pressure was stable at 130/70. Vagal maneuvers, including carotid massage and the Valsalva maneuver, were attempted but were unsuccessful. Subsequently, 6 mg of adenosine was administered intravenously, restoring normal sinus rhythm. Subsequent monitoring with a second ECG showed normal sinus rhythm, with no evidence of arrhythmias or acute ischemic changes. An echocardiogram was conducted, it revealed a mass in the right atrium, with an approximate size of 7 X 6 cm (Fig. 1). Due to the huge size of the mass, the IVC could not be evaluated using echocardiography. Thus, to delve deeper into the cardiac condition and to better evaluate the IVC, a cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMRI) scan was performed and it confirmed the presence of a 7 X 6 cm heterogenous filling defect of variable components near-totally occupying the right atrium. The occupying mass was causing right atrial expansion, displacing the left atrium and compressing on the esophagus. In light of the above findings, a decision was made to proceed with surgery. An excision of a large mass obstructing the right atrium was deemed the treatment priority. Exposure of the heart was performed through median sternotomy. Due to the presence of a huge mass, the cannulation technique for cardiopulmonary bypass was modified. Both cannulation and tapping of IVC were not performed. Instead, cannulation of the right femoral vein was done beside the aorta and SVC. The mass was exposed through oblique right atriotomy extending as low as possible to the anterior wall of IVC. A substantial mass was found occupying the entire right atrium. Gentle dissection of the mass from the right atrium was performed. The lower portion of the mass was found attached to the Eustachian valve extending to the suprahepatic portion of the IVC. The decision was made to reduce systemic temperature to 28 degrees Celsius. Total circulatory arrest was performed, and during dissection, the attachment site of the mass was found originating by a short stalk from the suprahepatic portion of IVC, total removal of the mass was performed (Fig. 3). Discussion

Cardiac tumors are uncommon [3]. The estimated incidence of primary cardiac tumors (PCTs) is approximately 0.02 percent [4]. Of these, myxomas account for 30% to 50% of cases, with 75% of them developing in the left atrium and only 10% to 20% in the right atrium [3, 5, 6]. Very rarely, they originate from the IVC or Eustachian valve. Myxomas are generally characterized by their female predominance and a typical onset between the ages of three and six decades. The clinical presentation of myxomas arising from the IVC encompasses a spectrum of constitutional, obstructive, and embolic symptoms, influenced by factors such as tumor size, fragility, and mobility, [3, 6, 20]. Constitutional indicators, such as fatigue, fever, and dyspnea, are more common in patients with larger or recurrent tumors and may mimic rheumatic illnesses [1, 21]. Obstructive symptoms, including hepatomegaly-related abdominal distension, ascites, and peripheral edema, often manifest due to compression of surrounding structures by the tumor [22]. Additionally, embolic events can lead to symptoms such as pulmonary dyspnea and coronary angina [1, 22]. Interestingly, at least one-third of cases were found to have a murmur, while only one case was complicated by an arrhythmia (Table 1). In our presented case, the patient experienced frequent vomiting for several months, attributed to esophageal compression by the presence of myxoma in the right atrium and a displaced left atrium. Furthermore, an electrocardiogram (ECG) uniquely revealed supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), successfully managed with intravenous adenosine.

The accurate diagnosis of cardiac myxoma is significantly challenging due to its resemblance to other benign and malignant cardiac tumors, as well as various cardiovascular and infectious conditions. Furthermore, distinguishing between right atrial myxomas and right atrial thrombus can be particularly difficult. To differentiate myxomas from other masses, additional imaging modalities such as cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMRI) or computed tomography (CT) are often utilized to provide a more detailed characterization of the tumor [23]. CMRI allows for precise evaluation of the location and functional effects of cardiac masses across any imaging plane without exposing patients to ionizing radiation. Notably, CMRI outperforms echocardiography in characterizing cardiac lesions, helping to distinguish between myxomas and thrombi [24]. Nevertheless, echocardiography remains the primary imaging modality due to its accessibility and ability to identify specific echocardiographic features that aid in differentiation [25]. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is the primary imaging tool, while transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) provides detailed localization and size assessment. Histopathological examination remains the gold standard for confirmation [9]. In this case, echocardiography identified a right atrial mass, which was confirmed as a myxoma through cardiac MRI and histopathology.

Surgical removal is considered the preferred treatment approach for myxomas. It has shown highly favorable outcomes, with operative mortality rates below 5%. The procedure is usually performed using a median sternotomy incision with the aid of CPB. Classically, venous cannulation in cardiac surgeries involving the CPB is performed using the bi-caval approach. Although most right atrial myxoma procedures were performed using this method, only one reported case used the femoral vein and SVC during cannulation, which was also the largest in terms of tumor size (Table 1). When the tumor arises from the inferior vena cava (IVC), femoral vein cannulation may be preferred to minimize the risk of embolism. This approach reduces direct manipulation of the tumor within the IVC, thereby lowering the likelihood of fragmentation and systemic embolization. The procedure mostly requires mild to moderate hypothermic arrest, however, there is only one case reported using total circulatory arrest. In our case, the right femoral vein and the SVC were cannulated and total circulatory arrest was needed to perform the resection. Excision of the main bulk of the right atrial mass was performed under 33 °C (mild hypothermia) and blood cardioplegia to protect the myocardium. Removal of the rest of the mass extending to the inferior vena cava was done using total circulatory arrest after dropping the systemic temperature to 28 °C (moderate hypothermia. The tumor measured 6 × 7 cm, making it one of the largest inferior vena cava (IVC) myxomas reported in the literature.

In most cases, surgical resection is curative, although there is a possibility of tumor recurrence, particularly in familial cases of myxomas. Therefore, follow-up is necessary with regular echocardiograms to assess cardiac function, and valvular integrity, ensure the absence of residual masses, and detect any signs of recurrence to prevent complications [26].

Conclusion

Right atrial myxomas arising from the inferior vena cava (IVC), although uncommon, require timely diagnosis and management through a comprehensive approach. Surgical excision is the main line of treatment, offering favorable outcomes and long-term prognosis, while regular follow-up is essential to monitor for recurrence and prevent complications. However, myxomas originating from the IVC present a rare challenge due to their variable presentations, and during surgery, various challenges may arise that necessitate adjustments to standard cannulation techniques and the use of total circulatory arrest to ensure complete excision of the tumor.
