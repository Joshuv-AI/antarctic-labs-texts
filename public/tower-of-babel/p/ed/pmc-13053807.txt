Adult adherence to multi-dose vaccine schedules: A population-based assessment of guideline concordance
Authors: Matthew A Miller, Lindsay K Nicholson, Gerard R Barber, Bryan C Knepper, Catherine Mastromauro, Edward N Janoff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPneumococcal vaccination provides an accessible intervention to prevent pneumococcal infections. Approval of the 13-valent conjugate (PCV-13) with the existing 23-valent polysaccharide vaccine for adults resulted in cumbersome recommendations for 2-3 doses with complex spacing. Given these challenging logistics, we evaluated pneumococcal vaccination frequencies and guideline concordance. The objective was to determine concordance with pneumococcal vaccine guidelines among distinct at-risk groups and factors associated with and reasons for discordance.Study designRetrospective, population-based cohort study.MethodsRecords were reviewed for PCV-13 and/or PPSV-23 vaccination frequency and timing among vaccine-eligible adults aged ≥65 years (153,525 [57.1%]) and 19-64 years (115, 216 [42.9%]) with at least 2 clinic visits over 4 years. Underlying conditions, vaccine type and dates were systematically extracted from electronic health records (EHR) and statewide vaccine registry for a large academic health system in Colorado, covering the period 2014-2018. Descriptive statistics were used for vaccine frequency and guideline concordance. We determined the odds ratios for concordance among subgroups and multivariable logistic regression to identify factors associated with discordance.ResultsVaccine uptake was 69% among those ≥65 years and 27% for those aged 19-64 years. Chronic conditions were identified in 61% and immunocompromising conditions in 39% of persons 19-64 years of age. Vaccine recommendations were followed by 30.7% and 17.2% for those aged ≥65 years and 19-64 years respectively (p < 0.001). Guideline concordance was highest among persons living with HIV-1 infection (72.3%) (OR 8.03; 95% CI, 7.33-8.79 vs. overall cohort). Inclusion of statewide vaccine registry data increased ascertainment 22% (29,774 doses) over EHR alone. Limitations were potential missed vaccinations due to administrations outside the health-system which may not be reported to state vaccine registry.ConclusionConcordance with complex national guidelines for pneumococcal vaccination was low in state-wide practices. Recent guidelines with simplified timing with one or two doses should improve these outcomes.Keywords: Multi-dose vaccines, Pneumococcal vaccination, Vaccine guidance concordance, Barriers to vaccine concordance

Introduction

Infections caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae, including pneumonia and invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD), are a significant cause of morbidity and mortality in the United States [1]. In 2022, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Active Bacterial Core (ABC) Surveillance estimated 27,770 cases (8.3/100,000) and 3230 deaths (0.97/100,000) from IPD, with the frequency of pneumococcal pneumonia estimated to be 10-fold higher [2,3]. Specific demographic groups identified by age and underlying comorbidities are at increased risk of pneumococcal infection and are targeted for vaccination. Risk factors associated with IPD include age extremes (<5 years and >65 years), those with immunocompromising disease states or receiving immunosuppression, functional or anatomic asplenia, heavy alcohol use and smoking, and those with certain underlying medical conditions [1]. Immunization is recommended for prevention of pneumococcal disease in these risk groups.

Prior to 2021, two vaccines were available in the United States; 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV-23) licensed in 1983 and, building on the success of PCV-7 in preventing invasive disease in children, 13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV-13) licensed for adults in 2011 [[4], [5], [6]]. Updated recommendations for vaccinating immunocompromised adults with PCV-13 were added in 2012 by the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) to complement the use of PPSV-23 in certain high risk populations [7]. In 2014, use of PCV-13 in addition to the PPSV-23 in non-immunocompromised patients 65 years and older was recommended [8]. The rate of IPD in adults began to decline in response to childhood immunization, prior to expanded PCV-13 recommendations for this age group [9]. IPD has continued to decline with expanded vaccination recommendations of high-risk groups. Among adults ≥65 years of age, IPD declined from 59 to 26 cases per 100,000 from 1998 to 2017, and among those 19-64 years, from 16 to 7 cases per 100,000 from 1998 to 2015 [3].

Given the current schedule complexity, cost, and frequency of updates, adherence to the ACIP pneumococcal vaccination recommendations was of concern in each designated risk group [10]. The 2015 National Health Interview Survey reported that pneumococcal vaccination rates with either PCV-13 or PPSV-23 were 23% among adults aged 19-64 years at higher risk and 63.6% in those 65 years and older [11]. Among Medicare beneficiaries, only 18.3% of adults aged ≥65 years had received at least one dose each PCV-13 and PPSV-23, but rates were higher among those with immunocompromising and/or chronic medical conditions. However, both PCV-13 and PPSV-23 were recommended for adults under the age of 65 years at increased risk of IPD. Additionally, the timing and sequence of administration were important considerations in determining adherence rates.

The primary objective of this study was to determine the overall rate of adherence to ACIP guidelines for pneumococcal vaccination among adults 19-64 years of age with immunocompromising and/or chronic conditions and those 65 years and older within our state-side health system. Secondary objectives included (1) guideline concordant vaccination by subgroup, (2) specific vaccine and number doses administered by subgroup, and (3) reasons for guideline discordance. Subgroups evaluated include age ≥65 years, age 19-64 years with one or more chronic conditions and those 19-64 years of age with immunocompromising conditions, including HIV infection.

Methods

The University of Colorado Health system is a large network of acute care facilities and ambulatory primary care and specialty practices, comprised of 11 community hospitals and a quaternary referral care academic medical center spanning a large multi-state territory. Annually, affiliated clinics log approximately 1.1 million visits, and the medical center supports approximately 180,000 patient days.

Study design and data sources

This retrospective study utilized the electronic health record (EHR) database to ascertain the prevalence of pneumococcal vaccination and concordance with Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) guideline recommendations [7,8,12]. Patients eligible for vaccination were identified in the EHR from Primary Care and Infectious Diseases clinics beginning January 1st, 2014 through December 31st, 2017, and followed through December 31st, 2018. Patients were included if they were over the age of 19 years and had two or more clinic visits within our health system in the previous 2 years. Patient ascertainment of the vaccine eligible population was validated against the known number of persons living with HIV infection seen by the Infectious Diseases Group Practice (IDGP) clinic, as well as immunizations documented within the state health department database. We excluded those with fewer visits who may have been less likely to obtain consistent vaccine ascertainment and routine follow-up within our health-system. Candidacy for vaccination was determined according to institutional algorithm based on the then current ACIP guidelines for pneumococcal vaccination (Fig. 1) ([7,8,12]), including age, underlying comorbidities, and previous vaccination history. The institutional algorithm was initially implemented and distributed in 2014 following updated ACIP recommendations on pneumococcal vaccination for adults 65 years of age and older, with an update in 2015 incorporating ACIP modification for intervals between conjugate and polysaccharide vaccine administration. The study timeframe excluded later updates in 2019 onward. Given the back-to-back updates for pneumococcal vaccination, the 2015 guidelines were applied with regard to intervals for immunocompetent adults 65 years and over, with a 6-month grace period applied from the time a repeat or subsequent vaccine administration was indicated to account for change implementation and variability in follow-up scheduling. Indicated vaccine doses administered beyond the 6-month grace period within the study timeframe (2014-2018) were considered discordant as outside time window per ACIP recommendations. Pneumococcal vaccine history prior to 2014 was included in adherence assessment for repeat PPSV23 dosing and timing of PCV13 administration if indicated in addition to assessing PCV13 adherence for those with at risk features according to 2012 ACIP guidance. Immunocompromising and chronic conditions matching ACIP recommendations for pneumococcal vaccination were identified from International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) and ICD-10 codes (Supplemental appendix). Vaccine concordance to ACIP guidelines, as determined by the EMR report, was validated based on review of a representative sample of patients (L.K.N) receiving care through the IDGP clinic due to ease of immunization record accessibility. Additionally, all patient vaccinations were cross-referenced by matching patient first name, last name and date of birth against the Colorado Immunization Information System (CIIS) database, introduced in 2001, for pneumococcal vaccine administration to ensure accuracy of vaccination rates.

Data analysis

We used descriptive statistics to compare the demographic characteristics of our population. Vaccination rates were calculated for any pneumococcal vaccine given, individual vaccine formulations (PCV-13 and PPSV-23), and both vaccine formulations given to the candidate population as defined by ACIP guidelines. Concordance with ACIP recommendations was calculated using the proportion of patients' adherent to the algorithm in Fig. 1. Univariate analysis was conducted to assess guideline concordance among subgroups compared with the overall population. In addition, a multivariable logistic regression was constructed to identify factors associated with concordance or discordance. Factors were added to the logistic regression model in stepwise fashion based on each factor's addition to model fit as measured by model area under the curve (AUC). Factors were selected based on model fit, though additional factors were considered. The “vaccination group” variable was derived from demographic and comorbidity data, reflecting groups recommended for pneumococcal vaccination: those over 65, with chronic conditions, immunocompromised status, or HIV. Individuals under 65 with both chronic and immunocompromising conditions were assigned to the immunocompromised group. All patients over 65 were placed in the >65 group except those with HIV, who were always categorized in the HIV group regardless of age. Goodness of fit for the model was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. All analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide Version 7.1. (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

We identified 268,741 patients eligible for pneumococcal vaccination from our health system during the 4-year study period: 115,216 aged 19-64 years and 153,525 aged ≥65 years. From those aged 19-64 years, 61% had underlying chronic conditions (particularly diabetes and lung disease) and 39% had immunocompromising conditions (particularly malignancy and organ transplant) thereby qualifying them for one or more pneumococcal vaccines. The majority of patients were white (81%) and male (66%). The predominant payor sources were commercial insurers for those aged 19-64 years (66%) and Medicare for those ≥65 years (85%). Overall, 51% of all eligible patients received one or more pneumococcal vaccine: doses, 69% in those aged ≥65 years and 27% aged 19-64 years (Fig. 2). Only 7% and 41% of patients in the 19-64 years and ≥65 years groups, respectively, had documentation of receiving both the recommended PPSV-23 and PCV-13 (Fig. 2). Concordance with guideline vaccination for PPSV-23 and PCV-13 among all eligible patients was 24.9% (Table 2). Among subgroups, those living with HIV infection (72.3%, OR 8.03; 95% CI, 7.33-8.79) and age ≥65 years (30.7%, OR 2.13; 95% CI, 2.09-2.17) were significantly more likely to be guideline concordant than other eligible groups. Reasons for guideline discordance were: vaccine not administered (62.3%), administered outside of recommended window (27.3%), administration too soon (8.8%), and incorrect sequence of PCV-13 and PPSV-23 (1.6%) (Fig. 3). In logistic regression analysis, age (as a continuous variable), sex, race, payer type, and vaccination group were included in the model. Factors associated with a significantly lower risk of guideline discordance were HIV infection, non-white ethnicity, and age ≥65 years (Supplementary Table 1).

Discussion

Complex recommendations for multi-dose vaccine schedules can limit adherence, particularly when significant changes occur frequently as with pneumococcal vaccine (Supplemental Table 2). Among patients with documented increased risk for pneumococcal disease, only 27% for those 19-64 years of age and 69% for those 65 years and older had received any pneumococcal vaccine. These results are somewhat higher than previously reported by Yang et al., among Medicare Advantage population 65 years and older (51%) and Williams et al., for adults 19-64 years old at increased risk (23%) [1,7,8,11,13]. However, actual concordance with approved guidelines was lower yet, with only 24.9% overall receiving the full set of recommended pneumococcal immunizations, including 31% of those eligible based on age ≥65 years and 17% of those19-64 years at increased risk. Of all recommended groups, those living with HIV infection had the highest vaccine coverage (85% PCV-13 and 74% ≥ 1 dose PPSV-23) and ACIP guideline concordance (72%) compared with other sub-populations (P < 0.001). Among those 65 years and older, the primary reasons for low ACIP vaccine concordance (31%) were vaccine administration outside recommended time frame or sequence. Missed opportunities for pneumococcal vaccination were identified for those <65 years at increased risk but not living with HIV.

Our findings are consistent with other published data focused on United States (US) Medicare beneficiaries 65 years of age and older and select individuals 50-64 years of age at increased risk for pneumococcal disease [10,14]. Utilizing the 2018 Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS) US national dataset, Whaley et al., reported pneumococcal vaccine prevalence among adults 50 years over to be 59% (95% CI, 57-62) [10]. The prevalence was lowest among younger patients and increased with age, 24% (95% CI, 66-72) among those 50-54 years and 80% (95% CI, 78-83) 75 years and older. Among 26.6 million US Medicare beneficiaries 65 years and older for PCV-13 vaccination status with or without PPSV-23 receipt, Kobyashi et al., reported a prevalence of PCV-13 vaccination of 41.6% in 2017 [14]. In both studies, vaccination with influenza vaccine, older age, the presence of comorbidities and a higher frequency of outpatient visits were associated with a greater likelihood of pneumococcal vaccination. Unlike in the present study, these reports did not assess the overall concordance to ACIP recommended schedule among those at increased risk indicated for pneumococcal vaccine receipt.

Other studies of adherence to multi-dose vaccine series in adults and adolescents confirm a significant drop-off with each subsequent dose and overall limited series completion rates. Preto et al., demonstrated this effect among 302,603 individuals initiating a 3 dose Dengue vaccine in Brazil (40.6% received all 3 doses) [15]. Similarly, Nelson et al., found low completion rates with hepatitis A (HAV; 2 dose; 40-50%), hepatitis B (HBV; 3 dose; 60%) and Varicella-Zoster (VZV; 2 dose; 36-56%) vaccine series amongst adults and adolescents [16]. This decrement was most pronounced in adolescents and Medicaid recipients. Gallagher et al., performed a systematic review of 61 multi-dose vaccine adherence studies, including Human papilloma virus (HPV), HAV, HBV, and VZV, with a wide range of series completion (27-90%) [17]. Adherence was associated positively with factors that do not apply in our study population, such as parental healthcare seeking behavior and vaccine delivery in schools. Johnson et al., found very limited vaccine series completion rates for HAV and HBV vaccines (10-22%) among adults in the UK, as did LaMori et al., among adults in the US (14-32%) [18,19].

Vaccines for prevention of COVID-19 infection are a recent example of complex, multiple dose vaccine series that have undergone frequent updates. With their introduction, the primary series consisted of 2 or more closely-spaced doses depending on age and immunocompromised status, with additional boosting recommendations at later time points. Current clinical guidance recommends 1-3 doses of the updated 2023-2024 formulation depending on multiple factors such as age, prior immunization status, immunocompromised status, and product formulation [20]. According to the CDC, for the originally authorized vaccine formulations, vaccine prevalence was high among all age groups receiving at least 1 vaccine dose, 81.4%, and increased with age (85.8% for age ≥50 years vs. 95% for age ≥65 years). However, series completion with a second or subsequent dose declined to 69.5% overall and just 17% and 22% receiving the updated bivalent booster and 2023-2024 COVID-19 vaccine formulations, respectively [21,22]. Additional factors such as social, political, and safety/efficacy concerns confounded vaccine uptake with COVID-19 immunizations in addition to the multiple dose series and frequently changing recommendations. The use of infographics to provide education about these vaccines improves the incidence of second dose completions [23].

Since these publications and the timeframe of our study, three new pneumococcal conjugate vaccines have received FDA approval for use in adults and pediatrics. These include the 15-, 20-, and 21-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccines PCV-15, PCV-20 and PCV-21), whose benefit includes broader coverage of additional serotypes associated with pneumococcal disease in association with simplified vaccine schedule. ACIP currently recommends either one dose (PCV-20 or PCV-21) or two doses (PCV-15 followed by PPSV-23 one year except for those with immunocompromised status, cochlear implants, or CSF leak where the minimum interval between PCV-15 and PPSV-23 is 8 weeks) in all adults at increased risk for pneumococcal disease [24]. PCV-21(Capvaxive®), is uniquely formulated for adults to include several additional serotypes not contained in PCV-15, PCV-20, or PPSV-23 [25]. Coverage against pneumococcal serotypes associated with IPD in adults is improved by 31% for those ≥65 years and 23% for those aged 19-64 years of age compared to PCV-20. [26], but this was dependent on local serotype prevalence. In addition to other factors, a simplified schedule utilizing a single-dose of PCV-20 or PCV-21 could improve current pneumococcal vaccine coverage in adults.

This study adds to the current literature by highlighting not only the complexity of ACIP pneumococcal vaccine recommendations but quantifying concordance with these recommendations within specific at-risk groups. ACIP guideline concordance with respect to the sequence of vaccine administration, time elapsed between vaccine types, and repeat dosing, are each highlighted in the current analysis. In addition, our data include enhanced ascertainment of vaccination, a potential limitation in other studies utilizing a single source for vaccine administrations, by utilizing two complementary sources, the healthcare systems electronic medical record and the Colorado Immunization Information System, a statewide database for vaccine administration records. Nevertheless, limitations to our study include the possibility of incomplete ascertainment of vaccine administrations in the setting of delayed/non-reporting to the statewide immunization database. Additionally, our exclusion of patients without follow-up in the health-system most likely lead to higher vaccine prevalence and adherence given that those without routine follow-up may be less likely to receive routine preventative care. However, among those with routine follow-up, adherence across at-risk adult populations remained relatively low in specific groups, highlighting a promising opportunity to improve immunization practices with respect to pneumococcal disease prevention. Our study highlights the discernible basis for nonadherence to ACIP guidelines (Fig. 3). However, complex, multidose schedules and other factors including psychosocial, access, time constraints, care priorities during visits, and clinician awareness play a significant role behind non-adherence [27,28]. Ongoing political polarization in the current landscape may further fuel public distrust and contributes to negative attitudes towards vaccinations, crippling public health policies that have eliminated or significantly prevented many serious conditions.

In conclusion, pneumococcal vaccine prevalence and adherence to prior ACIP guidelines varied substantially across different age-groups and at-risk adult populations in a large health-system. Persons living with HIV were significantly more likely to receive pneumococcal vaccination in concordance with ACIP guidelines. Efforts to simplify and consolidate recommendations for pneumococcal and other multi-dose vaccine recommendations should be considered to reduce complexity and improve appropriate preventive vaccination of at-risk adults. Vaccines are important, safe, and effective preventative tools. Public health policies must incorporate evidence-based and practical considerations (such as limiting the number of doses when feasible) in their recommendations to provide clear and consistent messaging around vaccine efficacy and safety.
