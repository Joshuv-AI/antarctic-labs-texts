Genetic improvement of FHB and DON resistance by combining the Fhb1 gene with additional resistance QTL in winter wheat population
Authors: Anjan Neupane, Ljiljana Tamburic‐llincic, Anita Brûlé‐Babel, Curt A. McCartney
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFusarium head blight (FHB) of wheat (Triticum aestivum L.), caused by Fusarium graminearum, is a major wheat disease that causes significant yield and quality loss. The use of resistant cultivars is an effective tool for managing FHB; however, FHB resistance is a complex trait. The objectives of this study were to identify quantitative trait locus (QTL) associated with FHB resistance in the Triumph/25R51 doubled haploid (DH) population. The DH population was evaluated for FHB resistance across six field environments and an additional greenhouse study. The population was genotyped using single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers, and a linkage map was constructed with a total length of 3018 cM across 26 linkage groups. QTL analysis identified multiple FHB resistance loci, including on chromosomes 2B, 2D, 3B, 5A, and 7D. The QTL on chromosome 3BS was the most important QTL for all FHB‐related traits and explained 27%, 25%, 14%, and 13% of phenotypic variation for FHB severity, FHB index, Fusarium‐damaged kernels (FDK) level, and deoxynivalenol (DON) content, respectively. Marker validation and haplotype analysis confirmed this QTL was due to Fhb1, with the resistance allele from 25R51 parent. FHB resistance QTL on 2D was collocated with both plant height and days to anthesis QTL at the Ppd‐D1 locus. The population segregated for Rht‐B1 locus, coincided with plant height QTL on 4B, but was not linked with FHB traits. This study showed that combining Fhb1 with additional resistance alleles enhances resistance to FHB, and the significant QTLs identified will be further useful for introgression in winter wheat breeding.

INTRODUCTION

The growing global population relies on wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) as a major staple food crop, and Canada is a major wheat exporter. Fusarium head blight (FHB), commonly called scab and caused by Fusarium graminearum Schwabe (teleomorph Gibberella zeae (Schwein.) Petch), is a serious concern among wheat producers in Canada and worldwide. In addition to yield losses and quality reduction, FHB leads to the accumulation of mycotoxins such as deoxynivalenol (DON). Estimates of the high economic impact of FHB reported in several epidemic periods include $2.5 billion loss in the United States during 1993–2001 (Nganje et al., 2004), $220 million in Ontario, Canada, during 1996–2004, and $300 million in Manitoba, Canada, during 1993–1998 (Clear & Nowicki, 2009). The maximum level of DON permitted is 2 ppm for adult food and 1 ppm for baby food in Canada (Health Canada, 2024). Maximum tolerance levels of DON allowed in foods and grain are strictly regulated around the world, including 1 ppm for food in the United States (FDA, 2018) and 0.75 ppm across Europe (EU, 2017), making wheat trade difficult. FHB management requires an integrated approach involving the use of resistant cultivars, crop rotations, and fungicide applications at the time of anthesis (McMullen et al., 1997). The main tool for FHB control and research is focused on resistance breeding efforts due to its effectiveness, low cost, and environmentally friendly natuwre. However, FHB resistance breeding is often challenging due to its quantitative nature of resistance, low heritability, and high genotype by environmental interaction. Further, multiple types of FHB resistance exist and have been categorized into five groups (Mesterházy, 1995): type I (resistance to initial infection in the spike), type II (resistance to pathogen spread in the spike after infection), type III (resistance to kernel infection), type IV (tolerance to yield reduction), and type V (tolerance to DON accumulation). FHB is also correlated with several morphological factors, including plant height (PHT; shorter plants are more susceptible to FHB), anther characteristics (retained anthers increase FHB disease), awned characteristics (awns increase FHB), and spikelet density (dense spikelets cause more infection) (M. Buerstmayr et al., 2020).

Multiple studies of FHB resistance have been conducted and more than 100 FHB resistance quantitative trait loci (QTLs) have been detected across all 21 wheat chromosomes (H. Buerstmayr et al., 2009; Liu et al., 2009). Most of the resistance relies on Asian sources, including the Chinese cultivar Sumai‐3 and its derivatives. Sumai‐3 contains major FHB resistance on chromosomes 3B (Fhb1), 5A, and 6B (Fhb2) (Anderson et al., 2001; Cuthbert et al., 2007). The major FHB resistance genes, including Fhb1 (Cuthbert et al., 2006), Fhb2 (Cuthbert et al., 2007), Fhb4 (Xue et al., 2010), Fhb5 (Xue et al., 2011), and Qfhs.ifa‐5A (Steiner et al., 2019), have been subject to fine mapping to identify tightly linked markers for marker‐assisted selection (MAS). The use of kompetitive allele specific PCR (KASP)‐based single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) marker umn10, wMAS0008, and simple sequence repeat (SSR)‐based marker Xgwm533 have been successfully applied in MAS for Fhb1 (Liu et al., 2008; Prat et al., 2017). There are several studies of FHB resistance in North American germplasm (Tamburic‐Ilincic & Rosa, 2017; Neupane et al., 2024); however, the search for effective genetic resistance is still ongoing. The use of native FHB resistance would reduce linkage drag and associated yield reduction. Tamburic‐Ilincic (2012) reported a FHB resistance QTL on chromosome 3B that did not reduce grain yield and protein content.

A Triumph/25R51 doubled haploid (DH) winter wheat population was used in this study. Triumph is a North American hard red winter wheat cultivar with good agronomic characteristics (high yield and protein content). 25R51 is a Pioneer Hi‐Bred International, Inc. soft red cultivar with FHB resistance from Asian sources (Tamburic‐Ilincic et al., 2011; Zhu et al., 2019). The objectives of this study were to create a genetic map of Triumph/25R51 population, map QTL associated with morphological traits (PHT and days to anthesis [DA]), FHB resistance (both type I and type II), Fusarium damaged kernels (FDK), and DON resistance, and examine the effect of QTL pyramiding among DH lines.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials

A winter wheat DH population (n = 102) from the cross Triumph/25R51 was used in this experiment. The DH population was produced at Tamburic‐Ilincic Lab, University of Guelph using the wheat–maize pollination method and embryo rescue method (Tamburic‐Ilincic et al., 2007). Triumph is hard red winter wheat, originated from Oklahoma in 1940 maintained by the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA), National Small Grains Collection (CItr 12132), and has the pedigree of Kanred/Blackhull/2/Florence/3/Kanred/Blackhull (https://www.ars‐grin.gov). 25R51 is a Pioneer Hi‐Bred International, Inc. (currently under Corteva Agriscience) soft red winter wheat and has the pedigree Kavkaz/Hart//2550/3/2555 sib./2548/4/25R18/sib./5/2571 (Serajazari, 2013).

Field disease evaluation

The parents, DH lines, and checks were evaluated for FHB across six field environments using a randomized complete block design with three replicates for each experiment (Table S1). The field environment represented locations across Eastern and Western Canada in multiple years: Winnipeg (Manitoba) 2016, Carman (Manitoba) 2016, Winnipeg 2017, Carman 2017, Ridgetown (Ontario) 2017, and Winnipeg 2018. Plots were planted in mid‐September in Manitoba and mid‐October in Ontario following standard agronomic practices in each region. The Manitoba plots were single rows 1 m long with 20 cm row spacing, while Ontario plots were single rows, 2 m long and spaced 20 cm apart. The checks FHB 148, 32C*17, and Emerson were resistant checks, and Hanover and Caledonia were susceptible checks used in Manitoba trials. Ava and AC Morely were used as moderately resistant checks, and Emmit was used as susceptible check in the Ontario trials.

Plants were inoculated at the 50% anthesis stage and again 2–3 days later. The inoculation procedure followed the methodology described by Neupane et al. (2024). The inoculum for Manitoba plots consisted of a mixture of four Fusarium graminearum isolates: M7‐07‐1, M9‐07‐1, M1‐07‐2, and M3‐07‐2. These isolates represented two chemotypes of F. graminearum: 15‐acetayldeoxynivalenol (15‐ADON) and 3‐acetayldeoxynivalenol (3‐ADON) with first two as the 15‐ADON type and the last two as the 3‐ADON type. Isolates were originally obtained from Dr. Jeannie Gilbert of the Cereal Research Centre of Agriculture and Agri‐Food Canada. The inoculum for each isolate was prepared with slight modification to von der Ohe et al. (2010). Briefly, the isolates were grown separately in Spezieller Nahrstoffarmer agar media plates and transferred to carboxymethyl cellulose flasks. The final spore suspension mix was set to a concentration of 50,000 macroconidia/mL with each isolate in equal concentration in the mix. Four milliliters of Tween 20 (Uniqema Americas LLC) was added into each 2‐L mixture to increase conidia adhesion. The inoculum was spray inoculated onto wheat spikes in each plot using a CO2 backpack sprayer set at 30 psi with an 80.02 spray nozzle. Plots were mist irrigated for 10 min/h for 10 h following inoculation for optimal disease environment.

The disease inoculation in Ontario plots followed procedures outlined by Tamburic‐Ilincic and Rosa (2019). The isolates were a mixture of four F. graminearum isolates: Q‐06‐23 (3‐ADON), ON‐06‐39 (3‐ADON), ON‐06‐17 (15‐ADON), and DF‐FG‐144 (15‐ADON), which were maintained in Tamburic‐Ilincic's wheat lab, Ridgetown Campus, University of Guelph. The inoculum was prepared using a suspension of macroconidia produced in modified Bilay's liquid culture medium. The spore suspension was inoculated on each plot using a backpack sprayer at the 50% anthesis stage, with a total of three applications at 2‐day intervals, followed by mist irrigation.

Phenotypic data collection in field trials

The following phenotypic data were collected for each experiment. PHT was recorded for each genotype at maturity by measuring PHT from the ground to the tip of the spike (excluding awns) of the main stem. Three measurements were recorded per plot using a meter stick and mean PHT of each plot was calculated. The Julian calendar date in which 50% of spikes in each plot were at anthesis (i.e., DA) was recorded by visually assessing the flowering spikes in each plot. The FHB data were recorded 18–21 days after inoculation using two parameters: disease incidence (DI; percent of spikes infected) and disease severity (DS; percent of spikelets infected within infected spikes). FHB index (incidence × severity/100) was calculated for all plots. FDK was measured from 50 g composite samples of three replicates for each genotype in each field environment. The FDK measurements were based on proportion of FDK by weight from 50 g sample (Canadian Grain Commission, 2020). Ten grams of the 50 g composite sample for each genotype was ground fine enough to pass through a 20‐mesh sieve, for measurement of DON. DON level was quantified for each genotype using Enzyme Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)‐based Veratox DON 5/5 quantitative test kit (Neogen Corporation) at Biovision Seed Lab (currently under SGS Canada). The DON quantification limit was 5 ppm, and the samples over 5 ppm were kept within limit using dilutions of filtrate.

FHB evaluation in greenhouse

The Triumph/25R51 population was also phenotyped for DS (type II reaction) in the greenhouse (located at the University of Manitoba, Winnipeg) using dual floret point inoculation (inoculation of two adjacent florets on the opposite side and present in second/third portion from base). This technique has been mainly used to detect type II resistance (resistance to spread of the pathogen within the spike) (Cuthbert et al., 2007). All the DH lines, along with parents and checks, were used for the greenhouse experiment (16 h light with 22 ± 6°C and 8 h dark with 18 ± 2°C). The checks used were Emerson, and FHB148 (resistant checks), Ava (moderately resistant check), and Caledonia and Hanover (susceptible checks). The experiment was arranged in a randomized complete blocks with five replicates for parents, checks, and DH lines. Each replicate consisted of five spikes from each genotype. Spikes were taken from single plants for each replicate. The isolate and inoculum preparation was identical to Manitoba field inoculum preparation. The DA of each spike was marked when 50% of the spike had reached anthesis. Each spike used for the experiment was inoculated using a dual floret inoculation method (Cuthbert et al., 2007) with 5 µL of spore suspension (50,000 macroconidia/mL). It was followed by a gentle water spray over spikes and covered with a glycine bag for 48 h to provide humidity for disease development. After 18–21 days, a visual rating was conducted for each inoculated spike as a percentage of infected spikelets per spike.

Statistical data analysis

The measured phenotypic data were subjected to a statistical analysis using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., 2023). The “proc mixed” function was used for the analysis of variance (ANOVA) where treatments were considered fixed effects and environment and block as random effects. The least‐squares means were calculated for FHB and related traits using all replicate data for each environment as well as for the combined dataset. Normality of the dataset was tested using “proc univariate test” and homogeneity of variance for the combined dataset was checked using the “Levene test” in SAS. The correlation between all phenotypic variables was calculated using the “proc corr” function in SAS. Variance components were estimated using “proc var” procedure in SAS, and board‐sense heritability was calculated for each trait using the following equation: h2=σ2G/(σ2G+σ2GE/E+σ2e/ER); h 2 is the trait heritability, 𝜎 2 is the variance component, G is the genotypes, E is the environments, GE is the genotype × environment interaction, e is the error variance, and R is the replicates/blocks.

Genotyping and linkage map development

The genomic DNA was extracted from all DH lines in the population and the parents for the single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) assay. For DNA extraction, the DH lines and parents were grown on cotton balls using four to five seeds for each line placed on Deep Inserts trays. These trays contained 48 cells with cell dimension of 2″ × 2.25″ × 3.25″ and were placed over flat trays (https://www.greenhousemegastore.com/containers‐trays/trays‐flats/deep‐inserts). Cotton balls were moistened regularly for optimal growth. Trays were placed at room temperature and 12 h light conditions for 2 weeks. Seedlings were grown until the two‐leaf stage. Leaves were harvested and immediately frozen using liquid nitrogen and placed in a −78°C freezer until DNA extraction could be done. Extraction of DNA from freeze‐dried leaf tissues was done using the Qiagen DNeasy kit assay (Qiagen). The Picofluor Handheld Fluorometer (Molecular Probes, Inc.) was used to determine DNA concentration with PicoGreen stain reagent and the working DNA concentration was adjusted to 50 ng/µL for each genotype for further work. A DNA concentration of 4 ng/µL was used for SNP analysis. The population was genotyped using the wheat 90K Infinium iSelect SNP array (Illumina; Wang et al., 2014). The genotyping assays were conducted based upon the manufacturer's protocols and genotypic clusters were analyzed using Genome Studio V2011.1 software (Illumina, 2011). Markers with more than 10% missing data and highly skewed markers (X 2 value at p < 0.001) were excluded from the dataset and high‐quality markers were used for linkage map development and QTL analysis.

The linkage map of the population was created using MapDisto version 1.7.7 (Lorieux, 2012) with a similar method as described in McCartney et al. (2016) and Neupane et al. (2024). The Automap function in MapDisto was used with the “Kosambi” mapping function to create linkage groups (LG) and estimate cM genetic distance between markers. The criteria used included initial stringent criteria with a logarithm of the odds (LOD) score of 4 and recombination fraction (RF) of 0.20 cM to identify the number of LGs and was then later relaxed to an LOD score of 3 and RF of 0.3 cM to finalize the LGs. The available reference map was used to assign and orient (long arm/short arm) each LG to specific chromosomes (Lukaszewski et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2014). The linkage map was further visualized using QTL IciMapping V4.0.6.0 (H. Li et al., 2008).

Marker testing

The Triumph/25R51 DH population was genotyped with two Fhb1 KASP markers (wMAS00008 and wMAS00009) to check the presence of major FHB gene (Bernardo et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2008). The population was also genotyped for few major dwarfing and photoperiod genes. The dwarfing genes, Rht‐B1 and Rht‐D1, were analyzed using KASP markers wMAS00001 and wMAS00002, respectively (Ellis et al., 2002). The photoperiod sensitivity gene, Ppd‐D1, was tested using KASP markers (wMAS00024 and wMAS00025) (Beales et al., 2007). The population was further tested with the Xgwm261 SSR marker, which serves as a reference marker for the Rht8 dwarfing gene, as Rht8 maps 1.95 cM proximal to Xgwm261 (Gasperini et al., 2012). All the markers were also added in linkage map of population using the marker genotype data.

QTL analysis

The most informative (i.e., least missing data) SNP was selected per linkage bin for QTL analysis. QTL IciMapping version 4.0.6.0 (H. Li et al., 2008) was used for QTL analysis for both interval mapping (IM) and inclusive composite interval mapping (ICIM) function approaches. The QTL analysis was conducted with 0.1 cM scanning steps. The LOD threshold was declared using a 5% experiment‐wise LOD significance threshold with 10,000 experiment‐wide permutation analyses, resulting in an LOD of 3.15 when using ICIM. The QTL confidence interval was estimated based on a one‐LOD drop from the peak QTL position. The significant QTLs, that fit the minimum LOD thresholds, were reported across individual environment(s) and combined dataset. The QTLs detected across multiple environments were considered major QTLs. Those significant QTLs detected across multiple environments or linked across traits were QTLs of interest in this research and were further discussed. The physical position of the detected QTL region was estimated using the Chinese Spring International Wheat Genome Consortium (IWGSC) RefSeq v1.0 (Appels et al., 2018; IWGSC, 2021; URGI, 2024). The potential candidate genes present in detected QTL intervals were searched using Wheat URGI and Triticeae Toolbox/T3 wheat database (Alaux et al., 2024).

Haplotype analysis

Haplotype analysis was performed using a collection of 19 winter and spring wheat lines with different levels of FHB resistance (Table S2). The analysis included lines with, or without, the Fhb1 region from Sumai‐3 or related Asian sources, which were previously validated. Wheat lines known to carry Fhb1 were Sumai‐3 and its derivatives (DH181 and FL62R1), Wangshuibai, and Nyubai, while lines not carrying the Sumai‐3 Fhb1 gene were AC Barrie, Cutler, and Reeder (Thambugala et al., 2020). Additional FHB resistance lines (FHB 148, Emerson, and Frontana), and the susceptible line, Caledonia, were also included. The haplotypes of both parental lines were compared with the Fhb1 carrying lines, including Asian wheat Sumai‐3, Nyubai, and Wangshuibai, as well as Fhb1 lacking lines, including AC Barrie, Cutler, and Reeder. A total of 97 SNP markers from 3B QTL region were initially selected and after removing monomorphic SNP between Sumai‐3 and the diverse wheat lines, 57 SNPs were further used in final haplotype analysis (Table S2).

RESULTS

Phenotypic analysis of population

The field FHB experiments had good FHB disease development and provided good differentiation among the test lines. The checks used in the study were in the expected range (Table S1), which was optimal for phenotypic data collection. The phenotypic distributions of the population, using data combined across multiple environments, are illustrated in Figure 1. The population segregated in a continuous distribution pattern for all measured morphological traits (PHT, DA), and FHB‐related traits (DI, DS, FHB Index, FDK, and DON). The mean PHT of the population was 70.9 cm and the mean DA was 162 days based on the Julian calendar. Among the parents, 25R51 was 9.7 cm shorter and flowered 3 days earlier than Triumph. In terms of FHB traits, the 25R51 parent showed more FHB resistance than the Triumph parent (Figure 1). The Triumph parent showed mean DI, DS, FHB index, FDK, and DON content of 31.6%, 24.6%, 7.5%, 5.3%, and 4.9 ppm, respectively, while 25R51 showed mean DI, DS, FHB index, FDK, and DON content of 15.1%, 8.8%, 1.8%, 1.9%, and 3.1 ppm, respectively. The population was slightly skewed toward resistance where the mean FHB index, FDK, and DON content of the population were 5.6%, 5.1%, and 5.4 ppm, respectively. However, there was transgressive segregation for all FHB and morphological traits in the population.

Genetic map of population

The genetic linkage map was developed with SNP data generated on the Triumph/25R51 DH population. In total, 11,516 SNP markers were used for marker grouping into LGs, and after further refining, 2174 high quality, informative SNPs were used to generate the final linkage map (Tables S3 and S4). The map consisted of 26 LGs belonging to 21 wheat chromosomes with a total map length of 3018.11 cM (Tables S4 and S5; Figure S1). The average marker spacing was 1.39 cM and 6.20 Mb based on the genetic map and physical map, respectively (Table S5). The B genome contained the highest number of polymorphic markers (45% of total polymorphic marker) and the lowest average marker spacing (1.15 cM). The D genome contained the lowest number of polymorphic markers (14% of total polymorphic markers) and highest average marker spacing (2.58 cM). The average map length and marker density were consistent with typical wheat maps (da Silva et al., 2019; Dhariwal et al., 2020; Neupane et al., 2024). The genetic and physical map alignment was also consistent with the available reference map (Lukaszewski et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2014).

QTL detection across traits

PHT and DA QTL

QTL for FHB‐related traits

QTL associated with FHB parameters; FHB incidence, FHB severity, FHB Index, FDK, and DON content are illustrated in Table 3. QTLs for FHB incidence, severity, and FHB index were designated as QFhb, while QTLs for FDK and DON were designated as QFdk and QDon, respectively.

FHB incidence, FHB severity, and FHB index

Four major QTLs for FHB resistance were detected across a combination of field FHB parameters (FHB incidence, severity, and FHB index) on chromosomes 2B, 2D, 3B, and 7D (LG:7D.1), which were named as QFhb.umb‐2B, QFhb.umb‐2D, QFhb.umb‐3B, and QFhb.umb‐7D, respectively (Table 3). Two additional major QTLs for FHB resistance, specific to FHB incidence, were detected on chromosomes 2A (LG:2A.2) and 3A, named as QFhb.umb‐2A and QFhb.umb‐3A, respectively (Table 3). The Triumph allele contributed resistance on QFhb.umb‐2A and QFhb.umb‐2D, while the 25R51 allele contributed resistance on QFhb.umb‐2B, QFhb.umb‐3A, QFhb.umb‐3B, and QFhb.umb‐7D. While both parents contributed to FHB resistance, the 25R51 parent was primary source of FHB resistance with a strong QTL on 3B. Phenotypically, 25R51 was more resistant to FHB than Triumph (Figure 1). QFhb.umb‐3B was consistently detected across environments and explained a higher proportion of phenotypic variation (R 2) than other QTL. This QTL contributed 16.6%, 30.5%, and 25.5% of the R 2 for FHB incidence, severity, and index, respectively, using the combined dataset. QFhb.umb‐3B explained R 2 of 1.7%–16.7%, 14.8%–31.1%, and 14.4‐23.5% for DI, DS, and FHB index, respectively across individual environments. QFhb.umb‐3B was mapped at the 0–3 cM on 3B where the Fhb1 wMAS0008/wMAS0009 KASP markers were also mapped (Figure 2).

QFhb.umb‐2A.1, QFhb.umb‐2A.2, and QFhb.umb‐3A explained R 2 of 11.9%–25.4%, and 12.2%–15.6%, respectively, for FHB incidence, across environment. QFhb.umb‐2B explained R 2 of 16.5% of each for FHB incidence and index across environment. QFhb.umb‐2D explained R 2 of 12.6%–26.3%, 19.5%, and 26.5% for FHB incidence, severity, and index, respectively, across individual environment(s). QFhb.umb‐7D explained R 2 of 12.2%, 15.0%–16.7%, and 17.5% for FHB incidence, severity, and index, respectively, across individual environment(s). QTLs were also reported for FHB incidence on chromosomes 1A, and 1D and for FHB severity on 5A in single environment.

FDK and DON content

One major QTL each for FDK and DON content was detected on chromosomes 3B, which were named as QFdk.umb‐3B and QDon.umb‐3B, respectively (Table 3). QFdk.umb‐3B and QDon.umb‐3B explained R 2 of 13.5%–22.7%, and 15.4%–20.1%, respectively, across individuals. QFdk.umb‐3B and QDon.umb‐3B collocated with QFhb.umb‐3B QTL for FHB incidence, severity, and index. The 25R51 allele reduced disease levels, FDK, and DON content. Two additional QTLs for FDK content were detected on chromosomes 3D (LG:3D.1) and 5A, which were named as QFdk.umb‐3D and QFdk.umb‐5A, respectively. However, those two QTLs were detected in single environment. QFdk.umb‐3D (Triumph carried the resistant allele) and QFdk.umb‐5A (25R51 carried the resistant allele) explained R 2 of 10.9% and 9.6%, respectively. Likewise, two additional DON QTLs were detected on chromosomes 4B and 5A in single environments. QDon.umb‐4B (Triumph carried the resistant allele) and QDon.umb‐5A (25R51 carried the resistant allele) explained R 2 of 13.5% and 8.9%, respectively. QFdk.umb‐5A and QDon.umb‐5A were in a 3 cM vicinity on 5A.

Type II greenhouse DS

Analysis of the greenhouse FHB experiment identified a DS QTL on chromosome 3B, which was present in the same location as the field DS QTL (QFhb.umb‐3B) (Table 3). Interestingly, no other QTLs passed the LOD threshold level. QFhb.umb‐3B accounted for 27.1% of the phenotypic variation and the 25R51 allele reduced DS.

Effect of QTL pyramiding

To observe the effect of pyramiding QTL, the major QTLs contributed for each trait were visualized using a boxplot procedure. QTL pyramiding effect for FHB index was analyzed using QTL on chromosomes 2D, 3B, and 7D (Figure 3). The Triumph allele at 2D QTL, and 25R51 allele at 3B and 7D QTL reduced FHB index. The mean FHB index of DH lines with the susceptible alleles at the QTL on chromosomes 2D, 3B, and 7D was 10.2%. The presence of a single major resistance allele at the chromosome 3B QTL significantly reduced mean FHB index to 5.6%.The presence of two cumulative resistance alleles, 2D/3B, 2D/7D, and 3B/7D, reduced FHB index to 4.1%, 3.7%, and 3.4%, respectively. The cumulative pyramiding effect of the presence of all three alleles for FHB resistance based on the 2D/3B/7D QTL further reduced FHB index to 3.2%. The individual effect of the major FHB QTL on chromosome 3B for FHB index, FDK, and DON showed that the presence of a single resistant allele at the chromosome 3B QTL significantly decreased FHB index, FDK, and DON content (Figure 4). The mean FHB index for lines without the 25R51 allele at the 3B QTL was 7.2%, which was reduced to 4.1% for lines with the 25R51 allele. The mean FDK for lines without the 25R51 allele at 3B QTL was 6.3%, which was reduced to 4.1% for lines with the 25R51 allele. Similarly, the mean DON content for lines without the 25R51 allele at 3B QTL was 6.5%, which was reduced to 4.4% for lines with the 25R51 allele.

Marker validation of major PHT and DA QTL

The major PHT and DA QTL were analyzed and mapped using reference markers (Figure 2a,b). The Rht‐B1 KASP marker (wMAS00001) segregated for Triumph/25R51 population and mapped perfectly on 4B within the region of the markers flanking the QPht.umb‐4B reported in this study. The PHT QTL on chromosome 4B, QPht.umb‐4B, corresponded with Rht‐B1 and was flanked by SNP markers IACX557 and RAC875_c27536_611. The QPht.umb‐4B was located within 28,954,114–32,250,820 bp on the physical map based on IWGSC RefSeq v1.0, which is the expected position of Rht‐B1 (IWGSC, 2021). The Rht‐D1 dwarfing gene was monomorphic for the population. The Xgwm261 SSR marker was polymorphic for the population, and it mapped in the expected location of the PHT QTL on chromosome 2D in this study. The Xgwm261 SSR marker is considered as a reference for the Rht8 gene since it lies within 10 cM of Rht8 gene. The photoperiod gene Ppd‐D1 was used for validation of the DA QTL on chromosome 2D using two Ppd‐D1 KASP markers (wMAS00024 and wMAS00025). The population segregated for Ppd‐D1 and perfectly mapped in the expected position of the QDa.umb‐2D. Interestingly, both QPht.umb‐2D and QDa.umb‐2D collocated with Ppd‐D1, which was mapped at ∼44 cM in the Triumph/25R51 population. Further, two tightly linked flanking markers for QDa.umb‐2D (RAC875_rep_c105135_247 and BobWhite_c2116_131) were found between the 32,799,784 and 43,994,195 bp location of the physical map based on the IWGSC RefSeq v1.0 (IWGSC, 2021). This is the expected position of Rht8 and Ppd‐D1 based on BLAST location (Gasperini et al., 2012).

Marker validation of major FHB QTL

Since the FHB QTL on the short arm of chromosome 3B was most prominent for FHB resistance based on all measured FHB related traits (DI, DS, FHB index, FDK, and DON), the population was further analyzed for Fhb1 as a candidate gene. The Fhb1 KASP markers (wMAS00008 and wMAS00009) were polymorphic for Triumph/25R51 population and mapped perfectly within the region of the markers flanking the FHB QTL (QFhb.umb‐3B, QFdk.umb‐3B, and QDon.umb‐3B) approximately at 3 cM of chromosome 3B (Figure 2c). This validates that the QTL on chromosome 3B associated with FHB resistance in this population is due to Fhb1. Further, the two tightly flanking markers (Excalibur_c20277_483 and Kukri_c49752_254) were used to narrow down the QTL associated with Fhb1 resistance. The physical position was located between 5,585,770 and 8,811,838 bp in IWGSC RefSeq v1.0. This is the expected position of the major Fhb1 gene from Sumai‐3 and derivatives based on BLAST location and previous studies (Bai et al., 2018; Cuthbert et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2008; Rawat et al., 2016; Thambugala et al., 2020). The candidate gene for Fhb1 codes for a histidine‐rich calcium‐binding protein (TaHRC) and is located at 8,528,450–8,528,472 bp of IWGSC RefSeq v1.0 based on BLAST analysis (Alaux et al., 2024; Su et al., 2019). This position overlapped with FHB QTL in this study.

Haplotype analysis of Fhb1 region

The haplotype analysis of a diverse set of wheat lines across chromosome 3B showed that Sumai‐3, DH181, and FL62R1 contained the same set of haplotypes (Table S2). The 25R51 parent used in this study shared the same haplotype with Nyubai. This suggests that the 25R51 parent might have inherited the Fhb1 region from Nyubai or related lines. The Asian line Nyubai had the same alleles as Sumai‐3 at 53 out of 57 SNPs loci in the 3B region used in the haplotype analysis (i.e., Fhb1 region). This suggested that the FHB resistance in Nyubai is similar to the Fhb1 locus in Sumai‐3 Fhb1. Thambugala et al. (2020) also reported identical polymerase chain reaction amplification among Sumai‐3, Nyubai, and Wanghiuibai using histidine‐rich calcium‐binding protein (TaHRC) as a potential Fhb1 gene analyzed with primer pairs TaHRC‐GSM‐F/TaHRC‐GSM‐R. This suggests that the Sumai‐3 Fhb1 locus and Nyubai Fhb1 locus are equivalent in terms of FHB resistance. Thus, the FHB resistance in the chromosome 3B region from 25R51 parent was likely due to Nyubai or related lines background. The marker testing with TaHRC and pore‐forming toxin‐like protein (TaPFT) candidate gene from Fhb1 would be useful to confirm source and candidate gene for Fhb1‐based resistance in Triumph/25R51 population. The following 15 SNP markers differentiate the 25R51 haplotype from Triumph and diverse wheat lines: Ra_c17608_960, BobWhite_c17021_269, BS00098868_51, wsnp_Ex_rep_c67625_66279491, Kukri_c49752_254, wsnp_JD_c222_352320, CAP7_c1576_371, Tdurum_contig80344_144, GENE‐1851_76, CAP7_c455_467, Tdurum_contig79629_538, RAC875_c8459_1291, BS00010849_51, Tdurum_contig17943_547, and RCC875_c30782_188. All these SNP markers could be used in combination for MAS for FHB resistance. The peak SNP marker Kukri_c49752_254 and all markers from same linkage bin detected for the chromosome 3B FHB QTL in this study will be useful. The SNP marker based on TaHRC or TaPFT from the 25R51 QTL allele would be the most prominent marker for MAS for FHB resistance.

The spring wheat lines AC Barrie, Reeder, and Cutler haplotypes were completely different than Sumai‐3 and Nyubai haplotypes in this study. It was first reported in Thambugala et al. (2020) that AC Barrie, Reeder, and Cutler do not contain the Sumai‐3 Fhb1 gene. This study showed that the haplotypes of common FHB resistance lines including, Frontana, Emerson, and FHB148, were different from Sumai‐3. The haplotypes of additional winter wheat lines, including Triumph, D8006W, Superior, Caledonia, Freedom, and RCALT33, were also different from Sumai‐3 or Nyubai haplotypes. This indicates the Sumai‐3 or Nyubai Fhb1 allele is lacking in Triumph, D80006W, and Superior; however, it is present in 25R51.

DISCUSSION

There is an ongoing effort to find better resistance to FHB in North America and worldwide. The sources of resistance present in Canadian wheat are not well understood, especially in winter wheat. Many studies report that current FHB resistance is primarily Sumai‐3‐based resistance (Anderson et al., 2001; Buerstmayr et al., 2020; Cuthbert et al., 2007; Prat et al., 2017). This study used a Triumph/25R51 DH population where 25R51 has FHB resistance likely transferred from an Asian source of FHB resistance (Tamburic‐Ilincic et al., 2011; Zhu et al., 2019). This study aimed to find the QTL associated with FHB resistance in the Triumph/25R51 DH population.

Significance of traits segregation

The population segregated as expected for all measured traits. Similar segregation of DH or recombinant inbreed line populations was observed in multiple studies (McCartney et al., 2016; Neupane et al., 2024; Tamburic‐Ilincic & Rosa, 2019; Thambugala et al., 2020). The field FHB evaluation was conducted using multiple replicates, environments, and a large number of entries. Genotype and genotype × environment interactions were significant. FHB is often highly influenced by the environment, which has been reported in multiple studies. The heritability associated with DI (0.56) and FHB index (0.61) was intermediate, while FHB severity (0.73), FDK (0.74), and DON (0.75) were high in this study. The heritability of FHB traits in similar range was reported in other studies (Miedaner et al., 2011; Thambugala et al., 2020) and can be explained by the large genotypic variation in the population. The negative correlation between PHT and FHB index was consistent with several FHB evaluation studies (Draeger et al., 2007; Srinivasachary et al., 2009). There are multiple assumptions behind this relationship. One is that the microclimatic effect of FHB inoculum where taller plants are assumed to be in less contact with inoculum (Jenkinson & Parry, 1994). However, in this study spray inoculation was conducted evenly across the wheat spikes. The next assumption is FHB susceptibility associated with shorter plants due to the pleiotropic nature of the dwarfing gene, which is likely the case in this study. This is mainly associated with the 2D QTL where the 25R51 allele contributed to shorter plants and increased FHB index (Table 3). The QTL for DON and PHT on chromosome 4B explains the negative association of DON to PHT.

Sources of morphological traits QTL

This study detected several QTLs for morphological traits QTL with allelic source from both parents. The 25R51 allele reduced PHT on QPht.umb‐2D, QPht.umb‐4B, and QPht.umb‐6A, while the Triumph allele reduced height on QPht.umb‐7D. Likewise, 25R51 allele reduced DA on QDa.umb‐6A, while the Triumph reduced anthesis days on QDa.umb‐2B and QDa.umb‐2D. The combination of shorter stature and early allele will be useful. Marker analysis confirmed few morphological QTLs corresponded to known major genes. The two dwarfing genes Rht‐B1 (on 4B) and Rht‐D1 (on 4D) were widely used in the green revolution, and Rht8 (on 2D) has been used to a lesser extent. In terms of time to anthesis, Ppd‐D1 (on 2D) is one of the major and frequently used gene (Guo et al., 2009). QPht.umb‐4B in this study was due to Rht‐B1 as indicated with marker test, while Rht‐D1 was lacking in population. PHT QTLs near Rht‐B1 have been previously illustrated (H. Buerstmayr et al., 2003; He et al., 2016; Neupane et al., 2024; Tamburic‐Ilincic & Rosa, 2017). Another height QTL, QPht.umb‐2D, was likely due to the cumulative effect of PHT (Rht8) and photoperiod genes (Ppd‐D1) as illustrated in the results section. The association of Rht8/Ppd‐D1a reduces time to anthesis and PHT (Worland & Law, 1986). This position of PHT QTL near Rht8 and Ppd‐D1 has been reported (Korzun et al., 1998; McCartney et al., 2016; Tamburic‐Ilincic & Rosa, 2019). The QDa.umb‐2D in this study was due to Ppd‐D1. DA QTL near Ppd‐D1 have also been previously reported (Gasperini et al., 2012; McCartney et al., 2016; Neupane et al., 2024). This study further suggested that pyramiding favorable alleles could improve morphological traits. For instance, genotypes with cumulative alleles from QPht.umb‐2D, QPht.umb‐4B, QPht.umb‐6A, and QPht.umb‐7D, or even from two major QTLs on 2D and 4B, result in shorter stature. Likewise, genotypes with allelic combinations of QDa.umb‐2B, QDa.umb‐2D, and QDa.umb‐7D result in the earliest anthesis. Tamburic‐Ilincic and Rosa (2019) also reported the benefit of pyramiding QTL for morphological traits.

FHB resistance linkage to morphological traits QTL

Resistance to FHB is quantitative and correlates, or overlaps, with morphological traits (M. Buerstmayr et al., 2012; Miedaner et al., 2011; Neupane et al., 2024). In this study, QTLs for visual FHB traits (DI, DS, and FHB index) were also collocated with PHTand DA QTL on 2D and 7D. The QFhb.umb‐2D, QPht.umb‐2D, and QDa.umb‐2D were located approximately at 44 cM of 2D (25R51 was a favorable allele for QPht and QDa, while the Triumph was a favorable allele for QFhb). Likewise, QFhb.umb‐7D, QPht.umb‐7D, and QDa.umb‐7D were located in vicinity at 24 cM of 7D (Triumph was a favorable allele for QPht and QDa and 25R51 was a favorable allele for QFhb). The FHB resistance QTLs on 2D have been previously identified across wheat genotypes, including Ernie (Liu et al., 2013), Kenyon (McCartney et al., 2016), Frontana (Agnes et al., 2014), and D8006W (Neupane et al., 2024), and were reported to overlap with Rht8 and Ppd‐D1. However, a few reports showed that PHT and DA QTL on 2D were independent of FHB traits (Basnet et al., 2012; He et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2013). The association of FHB and PHT QTL on 7D has been illustrated using the CIMMYT line Catbird (Cativelli et al., 2013), the Canadian spring wheat Kenyon (McCartney et al., 2016), and the Asian winter wheat line Qing Shoumai (Ren et al., 2019). Previous studies showed that dwarfing alleles (Rht‐B1b and Rht‐D1b) usually associate with FHB susceptibility (He et al., 2016; Srinivasachary et al., 2009; Tamburic‐Ilincic et al., 2017). In this study, the Rht‐B1 (on 4B) region was not associated with FHB traits. Another dwarfing gene, Rht‐D1, did not segregate in the population. A minor DA QTL, QDa.umb‐5A, also mapped on the 5AL region along with FHB QTL QFhb.umb‐5A. Based on Appels et al. (2018), the Vrn‐A1 locus is located in the interval of 136.8–152.7 cM, suggesting that QDa.umb‐5A may be due to Vrn‐A1, which requires further confirmation. FHB QTL on 5A was previously identified in cultivars Soru (He et al., 2016), Arina (M. Buerstmayr & Buerstmayr, 2015), Everest (da Silva et al., 2019), D8006W (Neupane et al., 2024). Unlike the major linkage between FHB and morphological traits on chromosomes 2D and 7D, as well as a few minor associations, the major FHB QTL on 3B appeared to be independent of these traits.

Characterization of FHB QTL and pyramiding effects for resistance

In terms of FHB and related traits, this study suggested that both parental alleles contributed to resistance and may exhibit linkage across traits. The FHB QTLs on 3B (QFhb.umb‐3B/QFdk.umb‐3B/QDon.umb‐3B) and 7D (QFhb.umb‐7D) were contributed by resistance from 25R51 allele, while Triumph provided resistance to QFhb.umb‐2D. Interestingly, the major QTLs reported for FHB visual symptoms on 3B were also reported for FDK and DON; however, QTLs on 2D and 7D were not detected for FDK and DON content. Likewise, some QTLs detected in position where no other FHB traits QTLs were in vicinity, such as QFdk.umb‐3D and QDon.umb‐4B, while some QTLs were detected in position with traits in vicinity, such as QFdk.umb‐5A and QFDON.umb‐5A. This further illustrates that FHB‐related QTL comprised both unique QTLs for individual FHB trait as well as shared QTL among FHB‐related traits. Similar nature of FHB resistance with unique or shared QTL among traits has been previously discussed (M. Buerstmayr et al., 2020; Neupane et al., 2024).

The QTL pyramiding effect for FHB traits was an important consideration for FHB resistance in this population. For instance, genotypes with a cumulative favorable alleles from QFhb.umb‐2D, QFhb.umb‐3B, and QFhb.umb‐7D reduced the FHB index to the greatest extent compared to the presence of two alleles or a single allele. Likewise, the presence of the 25R51 allele on 3B reduced the FHB index, FDK, and DON content compared to absence of this allele. This suggested pyramiding multiple sources of resistance QTL, along with 3B QTL in this adapted background, provides best resistance. QTL pyramiding for FHB resistance was also illustrated in a few previous studies (H. Buerstmayr et al., 2009; Burt et al., 2015; Cai et al., 2016; Tamburic‐Ilincic & Rosa, 2019).

Insight into FHB resistance QTL on 3B due to Fhb1

FHB resistance QTLs have been reported across all 21 wheat chromosomes; however, the majority of FHB QTLs were sourced from Sumai‐3‐based Asian germplasm, including QTL on 3B (Fhb1) and 6B (Fhb2) (H. Buerstmayr et al., 2009; M. Buerstmayr et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2009). In this study, the major resistance QTL was in the 3BS region of the Triumph/25R51 population, which was detected for all FHB traits (DI, DS, FHB index, FDK, and DON). This illustrates that the correlation among FHB‐related traits can be explained by this single major QTL. The phenotypic variation explained by 3B FHB QTL was high, up to 17%, 31%, 23%, 22%, and 15% for DI, DS, FHB index, FDK, and DON, respectively. Similarly, the QFhb.umb‐3B explained R 2 up to 27% for DS in greenhouse experiment. This showed that the 3B FHB QTL in greenhouse study contributed to type II resistance, while the field FHB study indicated that 3B FHB QTL likely contained both type I and type II resistance. QFdk.umb‐3B and QDon.umb‐3B were likely due to the effect of QFhb.umb‐3B that reduces disease development, leading to lower FDK and DON accumulation. Some studies also indicated that Fhb1 can detoxify DON (Lemmens et al., 2005). The source of resistance to all FHB traits contributed from 25R51 allele was likely due to its Sumai‐3 or related Asian lines background (Zhu et al., 2019). The association of QFhb.umb‐3B to Fhb1 region was verified using multiple approaches: positioning in genetic and physical maps in the Fhb1 region, Fhb1 KASP marker validation, and haplotype analysis using a diverse set of lines. The QFhb.umb‐3B was mapped at 3BS region of Triumph/25R51 map together with Fhb1 KASP marker (Bernardo et al., 2012) and overlapped with physical location of Fhb1 candidate gene (G. Li et al., 2019). The 25R51 parent shared the same haplotype with Asian line Nyubai, suggesting 25R51 may have inherited Fhb1 loci from Nyubai or related Asian lines including Sumai‐3. Zhu et al. (2019) reported that 25R51 contained FHB resistance source inherited from Chinese sources including Sumai‐3. It is also possible that 25R51 contains resistance sources in its background, which was similar to those Asian lines. Detailed pedigree analysis will be required for confirmation of resistance source. The Fhb1 locus, in 25R51, is likely equivalent to the Fhb1 locus in Sumai‐3 or Nyubai for FHB resistance (Cuthbert et al., 2006) as it reduces the FHB to the same extent. The SNPs flanking the QFhb.umb‐3B in this study can be used in MAS.

FHB resistance QTLs on 3B have been previously illustrated (Anderson et al., 2001; Bai et al., 2018; Cuthbert et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2008; Prat et al., 2017; Thambugala et al., 2020). Fine mapping efforts of Fhb1 have also been conducted (Anderson et al., 2001; Brar et al., 2019). The potential candidate genes, including the pore‐forming toxin (PFT) gene and the putative histidine‐rich calcium‐binding protein (TaHRC) gene, were reported (G. Li et al., 2019; Rawat et al., 2016). The analysis of Triumph/25R51 population with TaHRC as a potential Fhb1 candidate gene could be useful as has been shown in studies by Thambugala et al. (2020). The 3B FHB QTL results in this study were strong and promising; however, further research is required to dissect the potential Fhb1 gene associated with QFhb.umb‐3B in this current study.

CONCLUSION

In summary, this study identified multiple QTLs across traits: PHT (2D, 4B, 6A, 7D), DA (2B, 2D, 6A, 7D), FHB incidence (2A, 2D, 3A, 3B, 7D), FHB severity (2D, 2B, 7D), FHB index (2B, 2D, 3B, 7D), FDK (3B, 3D, 5A), DON (3B, 5A), and greenhouse DS (3B). The QTL associated with all FHB‐related traits on chromosome 3B showed major effect with higher phenotypic variation and detection across environments. The resistance allele (3B FHB QTL) on 25R51 parent originated from its Fhb1 background, which was verified using KASP marker and haplotype analysis. QTL specific for FDK and DON content resistance was detected on chromosome 5A, with 25R51 resistance allele. Further, the FHB resistance allele on 2D was from Triumph, and on 7D was from 25R51. FHB QTLs on chromosomes 2D were correlated with PHT and DA QTL (Ppd‐D1 region). The favorable alleles for shorter PHTand early anthesis were from 25R51, and the FHB resistance was from Triumph and vice versa based on QTL on chromosomes 2D and 7D, respectively. QTL for shorter PHT was also on chromosome 4B (Rht‐B1 region) contributed by 25R51. This study resulted in unique QTL where the major dwarfing gene, Rht‐B1, was very weakly associated with FHB traits and another major dwarfing gene, Rht‐D1, was not detected at all. This is considered a significant result as breeders struggle to dissociate FHB and PHT traits. The dissociation of the 25R51 Rht‐B1b allele with FHB susceptibility can be further verified using new crosses. The major FHB resistance QTL from this study can be incorporated in the breeding program using MAS. The evaluation of FHB traits with larger population size and across additional genetic materials would further validate and strengthen the finding from this study. The major QTLs from this population, including QFhb.umb‐3B, seem promising and confirmation of potential genes could be conducted by further narrowing down QTL resolution using fine mapping, genome‐wide association studies, or nested association mapping approach.
