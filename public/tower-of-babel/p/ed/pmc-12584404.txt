Identification and functional characterization of the histone acetyltransferase gene family in Bursaphelenchus xylophilus
Authors: Lichao Wang, Yajing Song, Ruocheng Sheng, Muhammad Faizan Latif, Ming Xu, Yang Wang, Min Li, Fengmao Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcetylation is a widely occurring post-translational protein modification in animals, plants, and microorganisms. Histone acetyltransferase (HATs) are positive regulators of acetylation and are responsible for the growth, development, and virulence of pathogens. The pine wood nematode (PWN) Bursaphelenchus xylophilus has caused extensive pine tree mortality, leading to significant loss in Asia; however, there is no HATs have been characterized in PWN.ResultsIn the present study, 7 HATs in PWN were identified and grouped into 3 subfamilies with conserved protein structures and motif compositions. The RT-PCR analysis revealed that HAT expression levels varied across developmental, temperature, and infection stages in pines. After the interference of BxPCAF and BxElp3, two acetyltransferase genes belonging to the GANT subfamily, the reproductive number and the pathogenicity of PWN decreased.ConclusionsWe identified 7 HATs belonging to 3 subfamilies in PWN, and these proteins may play specific roles in undergrowth, environmental stress, and host invasion and are associated with the growth and pathogenicity of PWN.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12175-8.Keywords: Histone acetyltransferases, Bursaphelenchus xylophilus, Pines, Expression analysis, Gene function

Introduction

The pine wood nematode (PWN), Bursaphelenchus xylophilus, is the most devastating forest disease affecting pines, and causes great damage to pine forests worldwide, particularly in China [1]. PWN is believed to have originated in North America and subsequently introduced to China at least 42 years ago, leading to the massive death of many native pine species including Pinus massoniana, P. yunnanensis, P. taiwanensis, P. armandii and P. koraiensis [2, 3]. Despite significant annual investments by the Chinese government and efforts by many professional technicians to prevent and control the PWN [4], it remains widely distributed across 18 provinces and 663 county-level administrative regions in China (No. 4 announcement of the National Forestry and Grassland Administration in 2024). To better understand the pathogenic mechanism of the PWN and reduce economic loss, several experiments have been conducted, with promising results [4–6]. However, further exploration of its pathogenic mechanism is urgently needed to understand and manage strategies against PWN.

Protein acetylation is a post translational modification widely observed in both eukaryotes and prokaryotes. It is involved in multiple biological processes such as gene transcription, metabolic regulation and signal transduction [7, 8]. Numerous studies have shown that acetylation plays an essential regulatory role in plant diseases, influencing the growth and development of pathogens, host plant defence responses, and their interactions [9–11]. Acetylation is jointly regulated by histone acetyltransferases and deacetylases, with acetyltransferases transferring acetyl groups from acetyl-CoA to target proteins [12]. Acetyltransferases can be categorized into five subfamilies, including GNATs, CBP/P300, MYSTs, and basal transcription factor (TFIID) [13, 14].

Previous research on acetyltransferases highlights their essential roles in the functions of pathogens and nematodes. In pathogenic fungi, knocking out homologous genes of Gcn5, SAS3 or Hat1 has been shown to cause significant changes in fungal growth, reproduction, tolerance, and virulence [9, 15]. In nematodes, interfering with acetyltransferase activity can hinder or cause defects in development or nematode death [16, 17]. Plant parasitic nematodes (PPNs), are very common and notorious plant pathogens that inhibit plant health and cause significant economic losses [18] but there are no reports yet on their acetyltransferase genes. This sparked great interest in exploring acetyltransferase in PWN.

In this study, the HAT gene family in PWN was investigated through the genome-wide identification, expression pattern analysis and functional characterization. The results of this study provide valuable insight into the role of HATs in PWN in response to different growth phases and environmental stress conditions.

Materials and methods

Identification of HATs in the PWN and other PPNs

BxHATs in PWN were identified using the following methods: (1) HATs previously identified in Caenorhabditis elegans and Toxoplasma gondii (Supplementary Table S1) were used as query sequences to perform BLAST search against PWN, and (2) the keyword acetyltransferase was used to search for genome files for potential candidates (GCA_904066235.2). The predicted protein sequences obtained from these approaches were further verified by analysing their conserved domains. The physicochemical characteristics of the predicted HATs and proteins were determined using ExPASy (http://cn.expasy.org/tools).

To obtain the HAT sequences in other PPNs, the identified HAT protein sequences from PWN were used as queries for BLAST searches on the NCBI or UniProt websites. The resulting sequences were collected and downloaded, and their accession numbers in NCBI or UniProt are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Phylogenetic, domain and motif analysis

To understand the evolutionary relationships between the PWN and other nematodes, the HAT sequences from PPNs, animal parasitic nematodes, and model nematodes were selected. The HAT protein sequences of other nematodes were obtained through sequence alignment in NCBI databases. Multiple sequence alignment was subsequently performed using ClustalW with default parameters. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining method with 1000 bootstrap replicates in MEGA7. Multiple protein sequences were analysed in NCBI for CDD analysis and in MEME for motif analysis. The annotated domains and motifs were visualized using TBtools [19].

Nematodes cultivation and collection

To characterize the expression patterns of HATs in PWN under different temperatures and stages of invasion, experiments were conducted using the highly virulent AmA3 strain of B. xylophilus. This strain was preserved at the Key Laboratory of Pest Invasion Prevention and Control in Jiangsu Province and cultured on Botrytis cinerea in a dark incubator at 25 °C. Nematodes that had just finished feeding on B. cinerea mycelium were isolated using the Bellman funnel method. After three washes, the nematodes were evenly divided into 4 groups, each containing no less than 5000 nematodes. The groups were then exposed to temperatures of 10, 20, 30 and 40 ℃ for 24 h. The nematodes exposed to 10 ℃ were used as the control group. To understand the potential role of HATs in pine infection, newly cultivated PWNs were divided into 6 groups, each with at least 5000 nematodes. These groups were inoculated into 5-year-old P. thunbergii and isolated at 0, 4, 8, 12, 24 and 48 h after inoculation. The nematodes not inoculated into pine trees were used as controls (0 h). After collection, the nematodes were washed 3 times with sterile water and stored at −80 ℃.

RNA isolation and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and the main steps were as follows: Nematodes stored at −80 ℃ were frozen in liquid nitrogen and quickly ground. Afterwards, 1 mL of TRIzol reagent was added, and the tubes were centrifuged. The supernatants were transferred to a new centrifuge tube, and 200 µL of chloroform was added. The tubes were shaken vigorously for 15 s and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 ℃. The supernatant and an equal volume of isopropanol were transferred to a new tube and centrifuged for 10 min. RNA was precipitated at the bottom of the centrifuge tubes. The RNA pellet was subsequently washed twice with 75% ethanol and dissolved in sterile water at 85 ℃.

To obtain cDNA, first-strand cDNA for RT-qPCR was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the EasyScrip One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SupperMix kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Each reaction system had a total volume of 20 µL, including nematodes total RNA, Anchored Oligo(dT)20, 2xES Reaction Mix, EasyScript RT/RI Enzyme Mix, gDNA Remover and RNase-free water. The reaction conditions involved incubation at 42 ℃ for 30 min, followed by heating at 85 ℃ for 5 s to inactivate the EasyScript RT/RI Enzyme Mix and gDNA Remover.

Expression pattern of HATs in the PWN

To analyse the expression patterns of HATs, transcriptome data for nematodes of different lifespans were downloaded from NCBI (PRJDB3458) and analysed, and egg-stage nematodes were used as the control group [20]. The average values of different stages based on FPKM values were determined, after which the ratios of other stages to the egg stage were calculated to obtain the relative expression levels. To determine the relative expression of HATs at different temperatures and inoculation times, RT-qPCR assays were performed using TransStart® Top Green qPCR SuperMix (+ Dye II) (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The actin gene of PWN (GenBank EU100952) was used as a constitutively expressed endogenous control gene. All the assays were performed three times. The sequences of the primers used for the experiments are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

RNA interference (RNAi) design and treatment

To explore the function of HATs in PWN, we designed specific primers with T7 promoter to silence BxPCAF and BxElp3, and the specific primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table S4. In vitro Transcription T7 Kit product manual was used, and GFP gene primers were used as a negative control in the experiments. An in vitro RNAi silencing assay of BxPCAF and BxElp3 was conducted according to previously described methods [21]. The small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) corresponding to BxPCAF, BxElp3 and the negative control GFP were synthesized using an in vitro Transcription T7 Kit (for siRNA synthesis) (TaKaRa). The nematodes were soaked in a solution containing 1000 ng/µL BxPCAF siRNA, BxElp3 siRNA, GFP siRNA and non-siRNA solution (CK). The nematodes were incubated at 25 ℃ in a shaking incubator at a rotation rate of 200 rpm for 48 h. After incubation, the nematodes from each treatment were thoroughly washed 3 times with ddH2O. Approximately 2000 nematodes from three different treatments were collected to evaluate the silencing efficiency of BxPCAF and BxElp3 by RT-qPCR.

Inoculation assays

To analyse whether HATs play a role in reproduction in the PWN, each PDA plate containing B. cinerea was inoculated with 500 mixed-staged nematodes that had been treated with BxPCAF siRNA, BxElp3 siRNA, GFP siRNA and non-siRNA solution (CK). The PDA plates were then incubated in the dark at 25 ℃. Once the fungal hyphae in the CK treatment were completely consumed, the nematodes were isolated and their numbers were counted to assess production levels.

To further evaluate the effects of HATs on PWN pathogenicity, nematodes treated with BxPCAF siRNA, BxElp3 siRNA, GFP siRNA and non-siRNA solution (CK) were inoculated onto 5-year-old black pine trees (P. thunbergii), with 2000 nematodes per tree. Five pine trees were used for each treatment. The experiment was repeated 3 times. The degree of morbidity was categorized, and formulas were used to calculate the morbidity and disease severity index of the P. thunbergia seedlings [22].

Statistical analysis

The data were analysed using Microsoft Excel. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted using IBM SPSS statistics 25. The figures were created using GraphPad Prism 8.

Conserved domains and gene structure of HATs in PWN

Analysis of domain architectures revealed that all HAT protein sequences in PWN contained conserved domains. For instance, BXYJ5.030112800 contained the ZnF-TAZ, KIX, BROMO and HAT KAT11 domains; BXYJ5.040124000 contained the Acetyltransf_1 domain, PCAF and BROMO domains; and BXYJ5.060218500 had the Acetyltransf_1 and Elp3 domains, and the remaining HATs all possessed MOZ_SAS domains. On the basis of this analysis, the 7 HATs were classified into 3 subfamilies: CBP, GNAT and MYST subfamilies. The HATs in the PWN were renamed following the nomenclature guidelines established for C. elegans (Table 1).

The gene structure of the PWN HATs is diverse (Fig. 1B). The longest fragment is BxMYST4, which contains 16 exons, whereas BxSYST1 is the smallest fragment with 6 exons in the MYST subfamily. BxPCAF and BxElp3 have different sizes and exon numbers, with 11 and 6 exons, respectively. In addition, the BxCBP contains 11 exons.

Phylogenetic analysis of HATs from the PWN and other nematodes

To reveal the evolutionary relationships among HATs in PWN and other nematodes, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA 7.0 based on the amino acid sequences from plant parasitic nematodes, animal parasitic nematodes, and model organisms. As shown in Fig. 2, the HAT proteins from the 12 species clustered into 3 major branches. As expected, the BxHATs proteins shared high homology with those of Bursaphelenchus spp or Aphelenchoides spp, but showed a relatively distant evolutionary relationships with BxHATs and C. elegans, as well as the malaria parasites L. loa and B. malayi. Among these groups, MYST was the largest, with 4 ABeHATs, 3 ABiHATs, 3 AfHATs, 4 BokHATs, 4CeHATs, 4 LoHATs, 4 BmHATs, 2 AaHATs and 1 MgHAT, and they were further divided into 4 branches.

To further explore the characteristics of HATs in PPNs, HATs were searched and analysed for their conserved domains in 14 other PPNs. This resulted in the identification of 77 HATs from Aphelenchoides, Aphelenchus, Bursaphelenchus, Ditylenchus, Globodera and Meloidogyne. The Elp3 protein of the GANT subfamily was not identified in Ditylenchus or Meloidogyne. The number of MYST subfamily proteins varied among nematodes; for example, 4 protein sequences were observed in B. xylophilus, M. enterolobii, and C. elegans, while only 2 were found in A. avenae, A. bicaudatus and M. javanica. All the identified HAT sequences were included in the phylogenetic analysis of PPNs (Fig. 2). The BxHATs proteins showed high homology with those of Bursaphelenchus spp or Aphelenchoides spp, but exhibited greater evolutionary distance from BxHATs and Ditlenchus or Meliodogyne. Nevertheless, the proteins from all genera displayed similar conserved domains (Fig. 3), corroborating the phylogenetic relationships shown in Fig. 2.

Analysis of conserved motif of HATs in the PWN

To characterize the putative motifs in the PWN HAT family, the predicted amino acid sequences were analysed using the MEME website. Twenty conserved motifs were predicted within these proteins (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig S1). Proteins in the CBP subfamily of plant parasitic nematodes exhibited the greatest number of motifs, ranging from 15 to 21. Common motifs in this subfamily included motif 1, motif 2, motif 4, motif 5, motif 6, motif 8, motif 12, motif 15 and motif 20. Compared with those from the CBP subfamily, fewer motifs in GNAT subfamily from the PPNs. While PCAF and Elp3 belong to the GANT subfamily, only shared motif 2 (the other motifs are different). Among them, the number of PCAFs included other 5–7 motifs, such as motif 5, motif 8, motif 13, motif 14, motif 16 and motif 18, while in Elp3 the number of motifs is approximately11-12, including motif 1, motif 4, motif 6, motif 7, motif 9, motif 10, motif 11, motif 12, motif 15, motif 17, motif 19 and motif 20, indicating that there may be protein functional specificity between the two proteins. The four MYST subfamily proteins in PWN had five identical motifs (motif 1, motif 2, motif 4, motif 5 and motif 8), but each protein also had a unique motif, for instance, BxMYST2 has motifs 11 and 16, BxMYST3 has motifs 13 and 19, and BxMYST4 has motifs 10, 14, and 15.

Investigation of the expression profiles of BxHATs

To improve the understanding of acetyltransferase genes in PWN, their expression levels were analysed under different conditions, including different developmental stages, temperatures and time points after inoculation in pine trees. The experiment was conducted by analysing transcriptome data and RT-qPCR.

The transcriptome data across different developmental stages showed that the expression level of the acetyltransferase gene was highest in the egg stage and that the expression level generally decreased with age, except for that of BxElp3 (Fig. 5A), suggesting that the acetyltransferase gene plays an important role in the embryonic development of PWN.

The expression pattern of the acetyltransferase gene in response to temperature was consistent. The expression level of the acetyltransferase gene was highest at 20℃, followed by 30℃, and lowest at 40℃. Compared with the other four genes, BxMYST4, BxCBP and BxElp3 were more sensitive to temperature (Fig. 5B). These findings indicate that acetyltransferase genes play a key role in the adaptation of the PWN to environmental fluctuations, whether the temperature is excessively high or low.

The expression patterns of PWN HATs tended to initially increase, but then decrease at different time points after inoculation. In the MYST subfamily, the BxMYST2 gene exhibited the most variation. In the GNAT subfamily, the expression levels of two genes peaked 24 h after inoculation. The expression of the BxCBP gene peaked 4 h after inoculation, followed by a decrease and subsequent upregulation at 24 h (Fig. 5C), suggesting that HATs may be involved in the parasitic process for PWN within pine trees.

PWN HATs are responsible for feeding

The acetyltransferase gene function was validated, and RNA interference was performed on BxPCAF and BxElp3. Compared with those in the CK group and the GFP siRNA treatment group, the expression levels of these two HAT genes in PWN were significantly lower. The expression levels of BxPCAF and BxElp3 decreased by 87.3% and 89.1% respectively (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6A, B).

RNA interference to HATs reduces reproduction in PWN. A RNA interference efficiency detection for BxPCAF, (B) RNA interference efficiency detection for BxElp3, (C) Effect of RNA interference for BxPCAF and BxElp3 on feeding, (D) Effect of RNA interference for BxPCAF and BxElp3 on population size

The RNAi-treated nematodes and control groups were subsequently transferred to B. cinerea for cultivation. After 4 days, the fungal hyphae in the CK and GFP siRNA treatment groups were completely consumed by nematodes. However, approximately 50% of the fungal hyphae remained on the culture medium in the BxPCAF siRNA and BxElp3 siRNA treatment groups (Fig. 6C).

The effects of the acetyltransferase gene on the nematodes were investigated and the number of nematodes was determined after the different treatments. The results revealed that the average reproduction in the CK and GFP siRNA treatment groups were 15833.3 and 15,000, respectively. The average reproduction in the BxPCAF siRNA and BxElp3 siRNA treatment groups was 6073.33 and 7750, respectively. There was a significant difference (p < 0.05) between the CK and GFP siRNA treatment groups and the gene interference treatment group (Fig. 6D). The results indicated that BxPCAF and BxElp3 gene interference can significantly reduce nematodes feeding and reproductive behaviour.

HATs contribute to the PWN pathogenicity

The RNAi-treated nematodes were inoculated into P. thunbergia. The health status of the pine trees was observed and photographed regularly (Fig. 7; Table 2). After 13 days, the morbidity of the pine trees in the CK and GFP siRNA treatment groups was 100% and 80% respectively. The morbidity of the pine trees in the BxPCAF siRNA and BxElp3 siRNA treatment groups was 40%. After 32 days, the morbidity in all treatment groups reached 100%. Nevertheless, the susceptibility index of the pine trees in the CK and GFP siRNA treatment groups were 90% and 80%, respectively. The morbidity of the pine trees in the BxPCAF siRNA and BxElp3 siRNA treatment groups were 73% and 75%. At this stage, the needles of three pine trees in the CK and GFP siRNA treatment groups significantly decreased. This findings suggest that interfering with the acetyltransferase gene can effectively reduce the pathogenicity of nematodes.

Discussion

Acetyltransferases are responsible for the positive regulation of acetylation and their corresponding genes have been widely identified in animals, plants and microorganisms [23–25]. An increasing number of studies highlight that acetyltransferase genes are essential for growth, development and environmental adaptation in various organisms [26, 27]. However, no studies on identification and function of HATs in PPNs have been reported.

In this study, we first identified acetyltransferases in PPNs. Seven acetyltransferase genes were identified in the PWN genome and 77 acetyltransferase genes were identified from 14 other PPN species (Aphelenchoides, Aphelenchus, Bursaphelenchus, Ditylenchus, Globodera and Meloidogyne). These genes belong to 3 subfamilies, CBP, MYST, and GNAT subfamilies, based on their domains. The presence of acetyltransferase genes appears to be closely related to the nematode species. The acetyltransferase Elp3 was not found in B. mucronatus, Meloidogyne spp, or Heterodera spp on NCBI and UniProt. There are two possible explanations for these findings. First, the identification of acetyltransferase in PPNs has not been extensively conducted. Second, the acetyltransferase genes are absent in specific nematodes, for example, the CBP protein is absent in Toxoplasma gondii [28]and Plasmodium falciparum [29].

In the present study, we found that the PWN is closely related to Bursaphelenchus spp and Aphelenchoides spp, because of their similar survival habits and parasitic environments [30, 31]. Like those in plants [24], the numbers of proteins, domains and motifs in PPNs are inconsistent. However, despite these differences, PPNs belong to the same subfamily with similar conserved domains and motifs. These findings suggest that HATs within the same subfamily likely perform similar functions, even when PPNs are classified into different species.

Numerous studies have shown that HATs play a crucial role in abiotic stress responses, development and pathogensis in various pathogens [32–34]. Like in previous reports, the gene expression level of PWN HATs were lower under extreme temperatures (40 °C–10 °C) than under optimal temperatures (20 °C and 30 °C). The HAT gene expression level was higher during the early developmental stages than in the adult stage. HATs exhibited varying degrees of upregulation at different stages of infection. These results indicate that PWN HATs are equally important for responding to environmental changes as they are in response to other pathogens. Researchers suggest that HATs regulate the reproduction and pathogenicity of pathogens [35–37]. In this study, RNAi silencing of BxPCAF and BxElp3 in vitro resulted in a slower feeding rate, reduced reproduction and decreased pathogenicity. Even if the efficiency of RNAi cannot be sustained by the mating and reproduction of nematodes [38, 39], leading to the feeding rate and pathogenicity being temporarily inhibited, our work confirms the important role of HATs in the PWN.

Although HATs are considered essential for pathogenesis, reports on their role in PPNs are limited. The regulatory mechanism of HATs in nematode reproduction and parasitism still needs to be explored, including (1) the functions of most HATs in the PWN and other PPNs; (2) The expression levels of HATs that are consistently low under both low- and high- temperature conditions. However, the underlying mechanisms may differ. At low temperatures, the reduced metabolic activity of the PWN may be a key factor, while environmental stress may exceed the tolerance range and disrupt regulatory mechanisms under high-temperature conditions; and (3) HATs can acetylate both histones and nonhistone proteins. It is unclear whether the decrease in feeding ability and pathogenicity is due to changes in gene transcription levels controlled by histone acetylation levels or whether the function of nonhistone proteins is related to feeding and pathogenicity after HAT interference.
