Engineering a serum response factor superactivator of smooth muscle gene expression with a condensate-forming domain
Authors: Peiheng Gan, Xinyi Zhou, Akansha M. Shah, Svetlana Bezprozvannaya, Kenian Chen, Qianqian Ding, Hui Li, Lin Xu, Ning Liu, Eric N. Olson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceSRF is a relatively weak transcription factor that regulates cardiac and smooth muscle genes. Its interaction with myocardin (MYOCD), a strong coactivator, establishes a molecular switch that drives cardiovascular differentiation. MYOCD activates smooth muscle genes by forming nuclear condensates that recruit transcriptional machinery. To harness this mechanism, we engineered a “superactivator” by fusing the DNA-binding domain of SRF with the condensate-forming activation domain of MYOCD. This chimeric protein formed nuclear condensates and robustly activated smooth muscle genes. Our findings demonstrate how a ubiquitous transcription factor can be converted into a lineage-specific activator and suggest a broader strategy for cell fate reprogramming by engineering synthetic transcription factors that combine DNA-binding specificity with the ability to form transcriptionally active condensates.

Cell lineage specification requires robust activation of specialized de novo gene programs. Serum response factor (SRF) is a widely expressed transcription factor that controls cardiac and smooth muscle differentiation through interactions with tissue-specific cofactors (1–4). On its own, SRF displays relatively weak transcriptional activity, which can be greatly enhanced through association with cofactors, such as GATA4, Nkx2-5 and myocardin (MYOCD) (5, 6).

SRF contains a highly conserved MADS-box domain that mediates dimerization and binding to the consensus CC(A/T)6GG DNA sequence, referred to as a CArG box (7). MYOCD, a lineage-specifying coactivator expressed in cardiovascular tissues, lacks intrinsic DNA-binding capacity and is recruited to target genes through the association of its N-terminal domain with the MADS-box of SRF, allowing the potent transcription activation domain (TAD) of MYOCD (MYOCDTAD) to localize to SRF binding sites. At sufficient concentrations, MYOCD undergoes liquid–liquid phase separation to form nuclear condensates that concentrate transcriptional components and activate cardiovascular gene expression (8). This property is conferred by its TAD, which is intrinsically disordered and enriched in hydrophobic residues. Similar condensate-forming behavior has been observed in other transcription factors, where the TAD drives phase separation and gene activation (9). While MYOCD forms nuclear condensates, SRF does not. Thus, their reversible association enables SRF to gain access to condensate-mediated transcriptional machinery, thereby driving cardiac and smooth muscle differentiation (6). We hypothesized that engineering SRF to form nuclear condensates by fusing it with the condensate-forming MYOCDTAD could yield a consolidated and uniquely potent activator of cardiovascular gene expression.

In this study, we tested this hypothesis by replacing SRF’s weak TAD (SRFTAD) with the strong, condensate-forming MYOCDTAD or with unrelated condensate-forming domains to create a unique transcription factor with enhanced tissue-specific transcriptional activity. Our findings provide insights into transcription factor–coactivator interactions and highlight a generalizable strategy for engineering potent transcriptional activators of cell fate by linking a DNA binding domain with a heterologous condensate-forming module.

Results

An SRF-MYOCDTAD Chimera Forms Nuclear Condensates and Strongly Activates SMC Genes.

SRF contains a relatively weak TAD at its C-terminus (residues 406 to 508) (10), with only 4 aromatic residues (3.9%). In contrast, the MYOCDTAD has a higher aromatic content (14 residues; 6.3%) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). MYOCD forms nuclear condensates dependent on these aromatic residues within the TAD and mutating them to alanines (in a construct referred to as MYOCDTAD-FWY/A) abolishes both condensate formation and transcriptional activity. While SRF does not form condensates, it can be incorporated into MYOCD condensates through its interaction with MYOCD (8). We hypothesized that swapping the weak SRFTAD with the strong MYOCDTAD would confer condensate-forming ability to SRF and bypass the requirement for full-length MYOCD for SMC gene activation (Fig. 1A). As a control, we also created the SRF-MYOCDTAD-FWY/A chimera, which is incapable of forming condensates (Fig. 1A).

To test the transcriptional activity of these chimeric proteins, we transduced 10T1/2 fibroblasts with retroviruses expressing mEGFP-tagged MYOCD, SRF, SRF-MYOCDTAD, or SRF-MYOCDTAD-FWY/A. Western blot analysis confirmed comparable expression of all constructs (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Reprogramming efficiency was assessed by immunofluorescence (IF) for ACTA2, a smooth muscle marker expressed at basal levels in 10T1/2 cells. SRF-MYOCDTAD showed high reprogramming efficiency similar to MYOCD, while SRF and SRF-MYOCDTAD-FWY/A showed no reprogramming activity (Fig. 1 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 C and D).

Superresolution confocal microscopy revealed that both MYOCD and SRF-MYOCDTAD formed discrete nuclear condensates, whereas SRF and SRF-MYOCDTAD-FWY/A did not (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S1E). Quantitative analysis using Imaris software showed similar condensate densities and signal intensities for MYOCD and SRF-MYOCDTAD nuclear condensates, although SRF-MYOCDTAD condensates were slightly larger (Fig. 1 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S1F). These results demonstrate that the MYOCDTAD endows SRF with condensate-forming capacity. In prior work, we showed that intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) rich in aromatic amino acids can substitute for MYOCDTAD function (8). To examine potential IDR specificity in SMC gene activation, we created additional SRF chimeras with IDRs from the RNA binding protein FUSN (aromatic-rich, 12.7%) and the DNA replication factor CDT1 (aromatic-poor, 2.7%) (Fig. 1A). Both SRF-FUSN and SRF-CDT1 formed nuclear condensates in transduced 10T1/2 cells when expressed at equivalent levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and G). However, only SRF-FUSN reprogrammed 10T1/2 cells into SMCs with similar efficiency to MYOCD and SRF-MYOCDTAD, while SRF-CDT1 was inactive (Fig. 1 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 C and D). These results demonstrate that aromatic-rich IDRs are necessary for SRF-mediated condensate formation and SMC gene activation.

An SRF-MYOCDTAD Chimera Acquires Genomic Occupancy and Strong Transcriptional Activity on SMC Genes.

To evaluate transcriptional changes induced by the SRF chimeras, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on reprogrammed 10T1/2 cells. The transcriptome of SRF-MYOCDTAD-reprogrammed cells closely resembled that of MYOCD-reprogrammed cells, differing significantly from SRF-expressing cells. A total of 261 genes were up-regulated in MYOCD and SRF-MYOCDTAD-reprogrammed cells relative to SRF alone (Fig. 2A). Gene ontology (GO) analyses linked these genes to muscle development and SMC function (Fig. 2B), while 411 down-regulated genes were associated with fibroblast identity and cell proliferation (Fig. 2C). Heatmaps of canonical SMC genes (e.g., Tagln, Tpm1 and Actg2) showed robust activation by SRF-MYOCDTAD, often surpassing expression levels achieved by MYOCD (Fig. 2D). Neither SRF nor MYOCDTAD-FWY/A activated SMC genes (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 C and D). SRF-FUSN activated a subset of SMC genes, underscoring the specificity of aromatic-rich IDRs in driving transcription (Fig. 2D).

To determine if SRF-MYOCDTAD can also activate cardiac or nonmuscle genes, we analyzed the expression of known SRF target genes with CArG boxes (11, 12). SRF-MYOCDTAD or MYOCD did not activate nonmuscle SRF target genes, such as Fos, but showed mild activation of cardiac target genes, such as Myh6 and Myh7 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Although SRF-MYOCDTAD acquired transcriptional activity on cardiac CArG-box-containing genes, the actual RNA-seq read number of these cardiac genes was hundreds of fold less than SMC genes, such as Acta2, Actg2 and Tagln (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Therefore, we conclude that SRF-MYOCDTAD acquired specific and strong transcriptional activity on SMC genes.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) analysis using anti-GFP and H3K27ac antibodies revealed that SRF-MYOCDTAD and SRF-FUSN occupied genomic binding regions also bound by MYOCD, while SRF did not (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). Motif analysis confirmed that SRF-MYOCDTAD gained access to CArG box motifs, consistent with MYOCD binding profiles (Fig. 2F). GO term analysis of SRF-MYOCDTAD binding regions showed enrichment for genes involved in vasculature and smooth muscle development (Fig. 2G and Dataset S1). SRF-FUSN also preferentially bound SMC-associated regions (SI Appendix, Fig. S2D and Dataset S1), suggesting that aromatic-rich IDRs enable SRF to associate with these genes.

By integrating RNA-seq and ChIP-seq data, we identified 579 genes up-regulated by MYOCD (RNA FPKM: MYOCD/GFP > 2) with nearby MYOCD binding peaks, which we defined as direct MYOCD target genes (Dataset S1). GO term analysis confirmed these targets are central to SMC differentiation (SI Appendix, Fig. S2E). Given MYOCD’s lack of DNA binding activity, these peaks likely represent recruitment by SRF or other factors. To assess chromatin accessibility, we examined H3K27ac enrichment around MYOCD target genes. SRF-MYOCDTAD and SRF-FUSN induced modest increases in accessibility compared to SRF (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 F and G). Some genes (e.g., Actg2) showed increased binding near known functional CArG boxes (13) without changes in H3K27ac levels (Fig. 2H), while others (e.g., Tagln) showed both increased binding and acetylation (Fig. 2I), suggesting dual mechanisms of activation. Overall, these data demonstrate that the SRF-MYOCDTAD acts as a potent activator of SMC genes—more so than MYOCD at comparable expression levels (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Therefore, we refer to SRF-MYOCDTAD as a superactivator of the SMC gene program.

MYOCDTAD Condensates Recruit Transcriptional Machinery and mRNA Processing Factors.

Previously, we showed that MYOCDTAD condensates aggregate key transcriptional components, such as RNA polymerase II and p300 (8). To profile proteins contained within condensates of reprogrammed 10T1/2 cells, we performed proximity labeling with APEX2 (14, 15) fused to the C-terminus of MYOCD, SRF and SRF-MYOCDTAD (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Upon exposure to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and biotin-phenol, APEX2 generates biotin-phenoxyl radicals that covalently label nearby proteins (15). The APEX2 fusion did not impair nuclear condensate formation or reprogramming efficacy (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 B–D). Efficient biotin labeling was confirmed by streptavidin-HRP blots with nuclear extracts from reprogrammed 10T1/2 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 E–G). Mass spectrometry of GFP-APEX2-labeled samples was used as a negative control, since all other constructs contained a GFP epitope (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A).

We first compared the profiles of labeled proteins between GFP-APEX2 and SRF-APEX2 samples (SI Appendix, Fig. S3H and Dataset S2) and identified 749 proteins significantly enriched in SRF-APEX2 samples (fold change>1.5, P < 0.05). Given that SRF alone cannot activate the SMC gene program or reprogram 10T1/2 cells, it is likely that many proteins in close proximity with SRF are not directly relevant to these processes (SI Appendix, Fig. S3I). Next, we compared MYOCD-APEX2 samples and SRF-APEX2 samples (SI Appendix, Fig. S3J and Dataset S2) and identified 796 proteins significantly enriched in MYOCD samples (fold change>1.5, P < 0.05). These proteins were largely involved with mRNA processing, cell cycle regulation, and chromosome organization (SI Appendix, Fig. S3K). Comparison of SRF-MYOCDTAD-APEX2 samples with SRF-APEX2 samples (SI Appendix, Fig. S3L) revealed 345 proteins significantly enriched in SRF-MYOCDTAD samples (fold change>1.5, P < 0.05) with many involved in chromosome organization, cell cycle regulation, and mRNA processing (SI Appendix, Fig. S3M).

To pinpoint shared components, we compared the MYOCD- and SRF-MYOCDTAD-enriched protein profiles and found substantial overlap (Fig. 3A). GO analysis of the overlapping proteins revealed enrichment for mRNA processing, ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis and chromosome organization (Fig. 3B), suggesting that MYOCDTAD aggregates transcriptional regulators into condensates for gene activation. In contrast, proteins uniquely enriched in MYOCD-APEX2 samples were primarily related to RNA processing (SI Appendix, Fig. S3N). Notably, 286 overlapping proteins fell into three functional categories: chromatin remodelers, RNA polymerase II subunits and mRNA processing factors (Fig. 3 C–E), consistent with our hypothesis that MYOCDTAD selectively aggregates active transcriptional components. Many of these proteins are intrinsically disordered and known to promote condensate formation (16).

Validation of Colocalization with MYOCDTAD Condensates.

We next validated the colocalization of MYOCDTAD condensates with selected enriched proteins by superresolution confocal microscopy and IF staining, using antibodies against endogenous proteins. We selected one representative from each category of the enriched proteins, including an mRNA processing factor (RBM39), an RNA polymerase II subunit (RPB1), and a chromatin remodeler (SMARCA1). RNA-binding motif protein 39 (RBM39) is an RNA-binding protein involved in RNA alternative splicing and transcription coactivation or corepression in cancer cells (17). We observed that 18.0% of MYOCD condensates colocalized with RBM39, which also formed condensates. A similar percentage of SRF-MYOCDTAD condensates (16.7%) colocalized with RBM39 (Fig. 4 A and B). RPB1, the largest subunit of RNA polymerase II, also displayed condensate-like structures in the nucleus. Approximately 18.8% of MYOCD condensates and a similar percentage of SRF-MYOCDTAD condensates (17.2%) colocalized with RPB1 (Fig. 4 C and D). SMARCA1 is one of the two homologs of the ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling protein ISW1 and has been reported to modulate transcriptional activation (18, 19). We observed that SMARCA1 aggregated into several large condensates in nuclei with MYOCDTAD condensates, while in nuclei without MYOCDTAD condensates SMARCA1 was expressed as more diffuse tiny puncta (Fig. 4E and SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). We found that 57.6% of SMARCA1 condensates colocalized with MYOCD condensates, and 52.4% of SMARCA1 condensates colocalized with SRF-MYOCDTAD condensates (Fig. 4F). As a control, we examined HP1α, a marker of transcriptionally repressed chromatin. MYOCD condensates showed minimal colocalization with HP1α (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 B and C), consistent with our prior study (10). For all three regulators, only partial colocalization was observed (<20%), suggesting that MYOCDTAD condensates incorporate subsets of each component. Further co-IF staining for combinations of proteins (e.g., RBM39 + RPB1) revealed that fewer than 2% of MYOCD condensates overlapped with two factors simultaneously (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 D–F), consistent with the dynamic and selective nature of condensates (20).

Functional Importance of MYOCDTAD Condensate-Associated Proteins in SMC Gene Activation.

To assess the functional relevance of condensate-associated proteins, we knocked down Rbm39 using shRNAs. Rbm39 depletion reduced reprogramming efficiency for both MYOCD and SRF-MYOCDTAD (Fig. 5 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and D) and decreased expression of SMC markers Acta2 and Actg2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 B and C). SRF-FUSN-mediated reprogramming was similarly impaired (Fig. 5 C and D), suggesting that RBM39 is required for activation of SMC genes by aromatic IDR-containing constructs. We also used indisulam, a small molecule that degrades RBM39 via the CUL4-DCAF15 E3 ubiquitin ligase (21, 22). Indisulam treatment eliminated RBM39 expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S5E), impaired reprogramming (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 F–H), and disrupted RBM39 condensates without affecting condensate size or density (SI Appendix, Fig. S5I), further supporting its essential role in reprogramming.

We then knocked down Smarca1 using shRNAs. Similar to Rbm39, Smarca1 depletion reduced reprogramming efficiency and Acta2 and Actg2 expression in the presence of MYOCD, SRF-MYOCDTAD and SRF-FUSN (Fig. 5 E–H and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A–D). SMARCA1 condensates were markedly reduced in shRNA-treated cells, whereas MYOCD and SRF-MYOCDTAD condensates remained unaffected (SI Appendix, Fig. S6E). We did not attempt to knockdown RPB1, as disrupting RNA polymerase II would have widespread, nonspecific effects. Collectively, our results demonstrate that MYOCDTAD condensates and their associated transcriptional components are essential for SMC gene activation.

Discussion

Our findings reveal a mechanism by which a broadly expressed, weak transcription factor (SRF) can acquire strong tissue-specific transcriptional activity through recruitment of the potent TAD from MYOCD in a condensate-dependent manner. The MYOCDTAD not only redirects SRF onto regulatory elements associated with SMC genes but also aggregates chromatin remodelers, RNA polymerase II subunits and mRNA processing factors, thereby activating the SMC gene program and inducing a cell fate switch. Although we observed broad activation of the SMC gene program in 10T1/2 cells by the SRF-MYOCDTAD chimera, it remains unclear if the reprogrammed cells acquired tissue SMC phenotypes. In summary, by covalently coupling SRF to the MYOCDTAD, we transformed it into a superactivator capable of reprogramming fibroblasts into SMC-like cells. These findings highlight a strategy for creating a superactivator of cell lineage specification by appending a sequence-specific DNA binding domain to a heterologous condensate-forming IDR.

In smooth muscle cells (SMCs), competitive and mutually exclusive association of MYOCD-related transcription factors (MRTFs) and ternary complex factors (TCFs) with SRF directs opposing transcriptional programs, contractile versus proliferative, highlighting SRF’s role as a context-dependent regulatory hub (23, 24). By directly linking the strong TAD of MYOCD to the DNA-binding domain of SRF, the chimeric SRF-MYOCDTAD can bypass the inhibitory influence of TCFs, thereby enabling activation of SRF-dependent genes even in pathological settings in which they would otherwise be repressed. Thus, whereas the two-component system of SRF and MYOCD expressed independently provides regulatory potential, the conversion of the two-component to the all-in-one system of SRF-MYOCDTAD provides for an especially powerful and signal-resistant mechanism for maintenance of SMC gene expression (Fig. 6).

Schematic showing roles of SRF and MYOCD in gene regulation. MYOCD associates reversibly with SRF and is responsive to signaling, whereas fusion of the MYOCDTAD to SRF creates a constitutive superactivator. The MYOCDTAD creates condensates on SRF-dependent genes and recruits RNA Pol II, chromatin remodeling proteins and RNA processing factors.

Unlike structured DNA-binding domains, TADs are frequently composed of low-complexity, intrinsically disordered sequences (9). These domains promote weak, multivalent interactions, which are difficult to capture by traditional pull-down assays (20, 25, 26). Recent studies, including those from the Young lab, have shown that such TADs can form condensates that drive gene activation (9). Our results extend this concept, showing that a noncondensate-forming transcription factor like SRF can be functionally augmented by appending a condensate-forming TAD like that of MYOCD. The condensate-based transcriptional mechanism offers two major advantages: 1) Tissue-specific versatility: SRF, a general transcription factor involved in diverse biological processes (27), can adopt tissue-specific functions by engaging with different cofactors. 2) Efficient consolidation of transcriptional components: The MYOCDTAD not only enhances SRF genomic binding near SMC genes but also facilitates condensate formation for efficient transcriptional activation. Transcriptional condensates concentrate essential, broadly used components, such as Mediator, Pol II, and mRNA processing factors more effectively than conventional complexes (28). This is especially advantageous during processes requiring highly robust and coordinated gene expression, such as maintenance of cell identity (29) and cell lineage specification (8).

Through protein proximity labeling and quantitative mass spectrometry, we identified hundreds of MYOCD-interacting proteins, including many not previously known to interact with MYOCD, such as RBPMS, a critical RNA splicing regulator in the cardiovascular system (30, 31). MYOCD can alter the expression of RBPMS to regulate gene splicing during SMC differentiation (32). Reciprocally, RBPMS regulates the alternative splicing of MYOCD to modulate vascular SMC phenotypes in atherosclerosis and vascular injury (33). RBPMS was not detected in prior co-IP experiments but was identified as a MYOCD-interacting protein in our proximity assay (Fig. 3C), suggesting condensate-driven association via weak, multivalent interactions. Thus, understanding the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms from the perspective of condensate biology provides a framework to identify new regulatory interactions and mechanisms. The dynamic assembly and disassembly of condensates provides versatility to the transcriptional machinery. Decoding the mechanisms that control the entry and exit of transcriptional components within condensates will deepen our understanding of this intricate machinery.

We focused on the fibroblast-to-SMC reprogramming function of the engineered SRF superactivator in this study, while SRF-MRTF signaling is also important for other cell fate transitions or maturation processes in the cardiovascular system, including cardiac fibroblast transdifferentiation into myofibroblasts (34), cardiomyocyte sarcomere maturation (35), epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition in the developing heart (36), and vascular SMC phenotypic switching (37, 38). It will be interesting to investigate how the engineered SRF superactivator might impact a broader scope of cellular events and its potential therapeutic impact in cardiovascular diseases.

Finally, our finding that substituting the native SRFTAD with the MYOCDTAD or the aromatic-rich IDR of FUSN confers upon SRF strong transcriptional activity for SMC genes, highlights new possibilities for engineering superactivators of gene expression and cell fate specification by fusing modular DNA-binding domains with orthogonal condensate-forming IDRs.

Materials and Methods

A full description of experimental materials and methods is provided in SI Appendix, Supplementary Materials and Methods.

10T1/2 Cell Culture and Reprogramming.

All cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified, sterile incubator. As detailed in SI Appendix, retroviruses expressing different chimeric mutants were produced in Platinum E cells and concentrated with a Retro-X concentrator for 10T1/2 cell infection. 2 d after infection, cells were reprogrammed with differentiation medium supplemented with 2% horse serum for 5 d before further analyses.

Superresolution Confocal Microscopy.

Superresolution live-cell and immunofluorescence images were acquired with a CSU-W1 Yokogawa Spinning Disk Field Scanning Confocal System using a Nikon 100× Plan Apo Lambda Oil Immersion objective from the Quantitative Light Microscopy Core Facility. Condensate colocalization analysis was achieved through overlaying two layers of detected spots and gating the “Shortest Distance to Spots” in the Imaris software. Condensate colocalization ratio in each nucleus acquired from Imaris was confirmed by manually quantifying colocalization events in three randomly selected regions within the nucleus by ImageJ-Fiji software. See details in SI Appendix.

ShRNA-Mediated Gene Knockdown in 10T1/2 Cells.

ShRNAs targeting Smarca1 and Rbm39 were predesigned and purchased from Sigma-Aldrich as individual plasmids (pLKO.1-puro, 4 shRNAs for each gene). Lentivirus was produced by lentiX 293 T cells and concentrated by the LentiX concentrator. Gene knockdown efficiency of each shRNA was tested and the most efficient shRNA targeting each gene was used for gene knockdown assays on reprogrammed 10T1/2 cells. See details in SI Appendix.

Proximity Protein Labeling and Identification.

10T1/2 cells were infected by retrovirus expressing APEX2 fusion constructs for 2 d. 5 d after differentiation, 10T1/2 cells were supplemented with 1 mM biotin-tyramide prepared in DMSO. H2O2 was added for a final concentration of 1 mM in the culture medium, and dishes were incubated in the incubator for 5 min. Cells were lysed and nuclei were isolated and washed for nuclear lysate extraction. Biotinylated proteins were enriched by Avidin Resin and concentrations of purified proteins were determined by the BCA assay. Equal amounts of samples (triplicate of each group) were submitted for tandem mass tag-mass spectrometry (TMT-MS) analysis. See details in SI Appendix.

ChIP-seq and RNA-seq.

ChIP-seq and RNA-seq were performed and analyzed as previously described (8). See details in SI Appendix.

Quantification and Statistical Analysis.

GraphPad Prism 10 software was used for statistical analysis. Error bars, n values, number of replicates, P values, and statistical tests used for analysis are indicated in figure legends. Parametric or nonparametric tests were determined after performing normality tests on samples. GraphPad denotes P values in our data as follows: ns P > 0.05, **P ≤ 0.05, **P ≤ 0.01, ***P ≤ 0.001, ****P ≤ 0.0001. Multiple comparison tests were Dunnett’s test or Dunn’s test for nonparametric samples against a control group. No statistical method was used for predetermining sample size. All data are displayed as mean ± SEM unless otherwise indicated.
