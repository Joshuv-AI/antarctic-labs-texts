Evaluation of Radiolucent Lesions in Cortices of Long Bones in Osteogenesis Imperfecta Patients
Authors: Carolyn Cook, Lydia Turk, Alicia January, Teresa Cappello, Peter Smith
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteogenesis imperfecta (OI), a rare genetic disorder of collagen synthesis and metabolism, is characterized by cortical bone thinning and decreased trabecular bone. We have noted individuals with OI develop radiolucent lesions in the cortices of the long bones and these have not been previously described. The prevalence of these lesions in the long bones of children and the significance of their appearance in relation to subsequent fractures is unknown.MethodsIn a retrospective study, upper and lower extremity radiographs of 328 pediatric patients with OI were examined to determine the occurrence and clinical course of these lesions in patients with various subtypes of OI.ResultsLesions were present in 55 (17%) of the 328 patients. A total of 89 lesions were identified in these 55 patients, with 138 positive radiographs across the OI subtypes. Of the 138 positive radiographs, lesions were present in 90/138 (65%) tibiae, 10/138 (7%) fibulae, 24/138 (17%) femora, 4/138 (3%) ulnae, 1/138 (1%) radius, and 9/138 (7%) humeri. In affected patients, there was an average of 1.62 lesions per patient. In 99% (136/138) of the radiographs, the lesion was present on the diaphysis of the long bone, and in 86% (119/138) of the x-rays, the lesion was located on the tension side. 64% (57/89) of the lesions developed after a previous fracture, and in 24% (21/89) of lesions, a fracture subsequently occurred through the area of the lesion.ConclusionsWe present that radiolucent lesions of long bone diaphyses are a characteristic finding in OI and often presage fractures.Key Concepts(1)Radiolucent lesions are a characteristic finding of OI and have not been yet described in literature.(2)These lesions may represent a probable failure of ossification and lack of remodeling in the area of the lesion.(3)There is no clear etiology of this finding, although many arise in areas of prior fracture or osteotomy.Keywords: Osteogenesis imperfecta, Brittle bone disease

Introduction

The genetic disorder osteogenesis imperfecta is associated with increased bone fragility and bone deformities attributed to defects in collagen production [1]. The features of OI are variable depending on the specific mutation, with five general categories of severity recognized by the modified Sillence classification. Diagnosis is clinical based on the patient's medical history, family history, physical exam, and imaging [2]; modern genetic testing can confirm the diagnosis and guide treatment planning. The common radiographic findings of patients with OI include extremity fractures, limb bowing, and spinal vertebral compression fractures. In evaluation of radiographs, we have noticed radiolucent bone lesions of the long bones. This lesion is a well-circumscribed, circumferential-to-ovoid defect with a central lucency in the cortices of a long bone. The occurrence of this lesion in the long bones of children has not been previously documented. The goal of this study was to determine the occurrence of these lesions in patients with OI and evaluate the relevant clinical course.

Materials and methods

In a retrospective study, the radiographs of 328 pediatric patients with OI followed at [hospital] from October 1926 through February 2022 were reviewed. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board. 23,245 routine upper and lower extremity films were examined retrospectively to evaluate for the presence of the radiolucent lesion, its location, and pattern of progression. The imaging was available in the form of radiographs from our electronic medical record (EMR), images scanned into our database, and hard copy films.

We defined a characteristic lesion as a round or oval radiolucent area within the cortex of a long bone, completely surrounded by thickened lamellar bone, which distinguishes it from a stress fracture, healing fracture, corticotomy, or nonunion. The features evaluated included the size and location of the lesion. All available imaging was reviewed and examined for any clinical deformities, along with previous, current, or subsequent fractures of the affected long bone. We documented deformities of the limbs, including varus or valgus deformity, bowing, or fracture. The lesion progression was followed by measuring the lesion size over the course of routine radiographs, and the date of lesion resolution was recorded for those with available follow-up imaging. Fractures through the lesion during its course of progression were also documented. Any intraoperative images were excluded from the study, along with images with casting materials obscuring proper visualization of the bone cortex.

Patient information was collected on those with positive radiographs, including age, sex, and OI subtype.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. The data on the radiographic features between OI subtypes was analyzed using Chi-square analysis. Data on lesion progression by OI type were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Additionally, since data for lesion size and progression were not normally distributed; it was analyzed using nonparametric tests. When examining lesion size, if a patient had multiple images of the same extremity taken on the same day; lesion size measurements were averaged to obtain a mean lesion size. Values on the average lesion size between patients with and without fractures were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. This nonparametric approach was used because lesion size was a continuous variable that did not meet parametric assumptions. Finally, a receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve was also generated to examine the sensitivity and specificity of lesion height for predicting fracture through lesions. The optimal cut point was determined using the Youden’s index, which maximizes sensitivity and specificity. The collected data were analyzed using SPSS, and p values ​< ​.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The radiographs of 328 patients were reviewed in three separate modalities, including in the EMR, scanned into the [hospital] database and hard copy images. One hundred fifty-nine (48%) of the patients had radiographs within the EMR, 36% (117/328) of the patients had scanned radiographs, and 16% (52/328) had hard copy radiographs. Of the 328 patients reviewed, 52% (172/328) were male and 48% (156/328) were female. The age at the onset of the lesion ranged from 1 month to 22 years, with the average age of 7 ​± ​5 years. There were multiple OI subtypes represented in this cohort, including 27% (90/328) patients with Type I OI, 15% (50/328) with Type III OI, 18% (59/328) with Type IV OI, 1% (3/328) with Type V OI, <1% (1/328) with Type VIII OI, <1% (2/328) classified as “other” subtypes, and 36% (117/328) patients not classified by the Sillence criteria [3]. Six patients of the 328 had the diagnosis of Bruck syndrome. Of note, the 117 patients who were not classified were found to have OI prior to the advent of the Sillence criteria and genetic testing so could not be accurately classified. They were not included in the subsequent statistical analysis as we only sought to identify trends in patients with known subtypes.

A total of 23,245 radiographs of the 328 patients were reviewed. There was an average number of 71 images per patient. There were 4130 (18%) radiographs of the spine, 4203 (18%) femur, 755 (3%) pelvis, 4180 (18%) tibia-fibula, 920 (4%) foot-ankle, 1038 (4%) humerus, 1196 (5%) forearm, 564 (2%) hand-wrist, 373 (2%) facial, 3050 (13%) lower extremity annual screen, 1357 (6%) upper extremity annual screen, and 1479 (6%) joints, including the shoulder, elbow, and knee.

After review of the radiographs, 55 (17%) of the 328 patients had lesions that were identified on 138 radiographs. Eleven (20%) of the patients had type I OI, 20 (36%) of the patients had type III OI, 21 (38%) of the patients had type IV OI, and 3 (5%) of the patients had Bruck syndrome. In affected patients, there was a range of 1–7 lesions per patient and an average of 1.62 ​± ​1.11 lesions per patient. Numerous patients had multiple lesions on either one affected long bone or multiple long bones and had many radiographs taken of the involved extremity over the length of their follow-up. Thirty-six patients had one lesion, 10 patients had 2 lesions, 6 patients had 3 lesions, 2 patients had 4 lesions, and 1 patient had 7 lesions. Due to the variable positioning of the extremities in the radiographs, the lesion heights could only be measured on 94 of the affected radiographs, and the lesion widths could only be measured in 92 of the affected radiographs. The lesion heights ranged from 1.6 to 11.4 ​mm, with an average height of 3.9 ​± ​1.7 ​mm. The lesion widths ranged from 1.0 to 9.0 ​mm, with an average width of 3.5 ​± ​1.4 ​mm. A total of 138 of the positive radiographs from the 55 affected patients were reviewed (Table 1). Lesions were present on 90/138 (65%) tibiae, 10/138 (7%) fibulae, 24/138 (17%) femora, 4/138 (3%) ulna, 1/138 (1%) radius, and 9/138 (7%) humeri (Fig. 1). There were no lesions identified on the skull, facial, or spinal images. In 99% (136/138) of the radiographs, the lesion was present on the diaphysis of the long bone and in 86% (119/138) of the radiographs, the lesion was located on the tension side of the bone. Eighty-four (61%) of the 138 of the positive radiographs demonstrated anterior bowing of the affected limb, and 21/138 (15%) had current fractures through the area of the lesion.

There were a total of 89 lesions identified across the OI subtypes (Table 2). 64% (57/89) of the lesions developed after a previous fracture, and in 24% (21/89) of lesions, a fracture occurred through the area of the lesion. 43% (38/89) of the lesions developed after the placement of an intramedullary rod. Of these 89 lesions, 14 from the Type III patients and 3 from the Type IV patients, it was unknown if they formed with or without prior fractures due to insufficient radiographs in the area of the lesion. The average lesion sizes were compared in patients with and without fractures involving the lesions (Table 3). In patients without fractures through the lesion, the average height was 3.85 ​± ​1.72 ​mm and the average width was 3.62 ​± ​1.42 ​mm. In patients with fractures through the lesion, the average height was 4.54 ​± ​1.34 ​mm and the average width was 3.21 ​± ​1.41 ​mm. Results of ROC analysis suggested that height was significantly better than chance at predicating the “positive state” of fractures through the lesion, (AUC ​= ​0.681, 95% confidence interval: 0.53–0.83, P ​= ​.027). The cutoff that maximized the sensitivity and specificity of predicting fractures involving lesions was 3.94 ​mm. At this cutoff, sensitivity was 80% and specificity was 61%.

Discussion

Osteogenesis imperfecta patients demonstrate a long history of unique radiographic findings in pediatric patients. In 1975, Bauze et al. reported the “cystic appearance” of long bones in a subset of patients [4]. In 1980, Goldman et al. identified “popcorn calcifications” identified in the metaphyseal area of the long bones of children that disappeared after puberty [5]. In 1987, Raut et al. described “metaphyseal flaring” and “cystic changes” at the ends of long bones in patients with Type III OI [6]. The lesion described in this current study has not been previously documented, and to our knowledge, this lesion has not been described.

Regardless of the underlying genetic type of OI, all forms are associated with poor bone formation by osteoblasts and increased osteoclastic activity [7]. Bone specimens in OI have been shown to demonstrate decreased cortical thickness and diminished volume of trabecular bone, in addition to increased cortical porosity. Mineral content is higher in OI bone [8]. Although this study is limited to examination of radiographs, the cortical lesion appearance suggests poor bone formation surrounded by thickened cortical lamellar bone, a macroscopic manifestation of what is seen on mesoscale imaging [9,10].

We classified children according to the modified Sillence criteria. Type I OI is typically the mildest form of the disease and is nondeforming [11,12]. Type III OI is the most severe form of the disease and may present with fractures at birth with progressive limb deformity in adolescence. It has been traditionally described that OI Type III is associated with significant clinical deformities [3,11,12,13]. Type IV OI is considered to be a moderate disease form with milder deformity compared to Type III but has a variable presentation with bowing of the long bones and moderately shortened stature. Type V has characteristic ossification of the interosseous membrane of the forearm and tendency to form hypertrophic callus. Bruck syndrome is clinically variable but usually severe and is associated with limb contractures and spine deformity. Fractures of the vertebral bodies and ribs may be seen in this OI subtype [14,15]. We found bone lesions across I, III, IV, and Bruck subtypes. Within our study population, lesions were the most common in OI subtypes III and IV.

Long bone deformities are common in patients with OI due to the poor bone quality and overall decreased osseous strength associated with this condition [16]. These deformities typically affect the lower limbs, although the upper limbs, skull, and spine may be affected. Common long bone deformities include the anterolateral bowing of the femur and the anterior bowing of the tibia. In review of the upper and lower extremity radiographs in this study, it was found that 84 radiographs had anterior bowing of the affected long bone, and this was the most prominent clinical deformity in this patient population. In 15 of the lesions identified, it appeared these lesions developed after a fracture healed, but this was not the cause in the majority of cases. It is unclear if the lesion development is associated with lower extremity bowing alone, as this deformity is common in OI patients. Overall, it is unclear of the influence of clinical deformity on lesion development.

Rodding has been the standard of treatment for fractures and long bone deformities in patients with OI since the introduction of the technique by Sofield and Millar [17]. Surgical rods are helpful for patients with OI because they support weak, long bones, reduce the fracture rates, maintain straight bone growth, and prevent bone displacement after fractures [18]. In our study, 28/89 (31%) of the affected patients developed a lesion after osteotomy and intramedullary rod placement (p ​= ​.410). This suggests the possibility that defects in the cortex from surgical osteotomies can be a focus for lesion formation, despite union.

Bone is a complex material with an intrinsic remodeling and repair process, and fracture modeling is complex and poorly understood in OI [19]. In this study, we sought to examine if the lesion predicted the long bone's future potential to fracture. Of the 89 identified lesions, 21 (24%) had recurrent fractures through the area of the lesion (p ​= ​.588). This finding was not found to be statistically significant, and there was no correlation between fracture rate and lesion development.

It is unknown if there is a correlation between the lesion dimensions and its clinical course. Lesions that fractured did have significantly greater height than lesions, which did not. When exploring deeper how lesion height could be used to predict fractures, 3.94 ​mm was identified as a potentially adequate cutoff for predicting risk of fracture. At this cutoff point, 80% of positive fractures were correctly identified but with a false positive rate of roughly 39%. Large lesions are at risk of fracture and may be an indication for prophylactic rodding, with or without osteotomy.

A clear limitation of this study was the variability in the number of routine radiographs per patient. Prior to the implementation of the EMR in 2004, not all patients had annual lower extremity imaging. Therefore, our study was not able to include many patients who did not have those routine radiographs performed. It is now standard that the OI patients obtain annual screening lower extremity radiographs. The purpose of this study was to identify this as a pertinent lesion in this patient population. Further studies will determine more accurately the prevalence or incidence of this lesion in OI patients’ long bones.

Another limitation is that the effect of bisphosphonate use was not examined in this study. Although it may have an important effect on remodeling of bone in OI, we observed this bone lesion in children who were both treated and untreated with bisphosphonates. The influence of bisphosphonate use on the lesion development is currently unknown. Next, the lesions were measured by two of the authors (CC and LT), and there may be subjective differences in the measurements. Lesions identified on the hard copy radiographs were measured manually, while images within the EMR database were measured with a digital ruler, which may account for discrepancies within the data set. Lastly, approximately 17 of the patients with lesions were lost to follow-up or had insufficient imaging; therefore, details regarding their lesion progression and whether they formed with or without prior fractures was unknown.

Conclusion

We describe radiolucent lesions in the long bones of children with OI patients that have not been previously reported. We found that it is fairly common and present in 17% of the patients whose x-rays we reviewed, and we believe it is relatively specific to OI, representing a probable failure of ossification and lack of remodeling. We found no clear etiology of this lesion, although many arise in areas of prior fracture or osteotomy, which has united but has a persistent area of poor ossification surrounded by thickened lamellar bone. Behavior is variable: some go on to fracture, and others remain stable or regress with time. These findings warrant further evaluation of the progression of this lesion by conducting a prospective study with a routine schedule of radiographs.
