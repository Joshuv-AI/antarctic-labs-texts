Using immunization registry data to explore initiation of HPV vaccination at ages 9 and 10
Authors: Grace W. Ryan, Amanda Kahl, Emily Janio, Bethany Kintigh, Don Callaghan, Natoshia M. Askelson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDespite evidence highlighting benefits of age 9/10 initiation of human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination, little is known about who is initiating the series earlier. We describe the population of children who initiate earlier and assess the relationship of earlier initiation with series completion. We used individual-level immunization registry data from Iowa which contained data on male (49.9%) and female (50.1%) children born between 2004–2013 ages 9 and 17. We compared sociodemographic characteristics using chi-square statistics and used logistic regression to assess odds of initiation. Of children with a record of HPV vaccination, only 3.2% initiated early. In comparison to children who initiated at later, the early initiator group had a higher percentage of females (p < .0001), greater representation of nonwhite children (p < .0001), higher percentage living in urban areas (p < .0001) and higher private insurance (p < .0001). In logistic regression models, earlier initiation was significantly associated with higher rates of on-time completion (OR: 10.8 (8.6, 13.5)). These results can support prioritization of efforts to promote earlier initiation. For example, rural areas may need targeted outreach. Future work could integrate geospatial analyses so that local organizations could be able to target specific counties or even zip codes with the lowest rates of initiation.

Introduction

For optimal protection against cancers associated with human papillomavirus (HPV), the HPV vaccine series should be completed by age 13 and is routinely recommended to 11- and 12-year-olds. Yet, the most recent data from the National Immunization Survey shows that across the United States, only 49% of 13-year-olds are considered up-to-date on this series.1 Since the vaccine was first approved and recommended by the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices, the recommendation language has stated that vaccination can begin as early as age 9,2 however the focus for vaccination and measuring outcomes has centered on 11/12-year-olds. New evidence suggests that initiating at ages 9 or 10, compared to 11 or 12, leads to higher rates of completion by age 13.3,4 This is due to multiple factors including more time and opportunities to complete the series,5,6 having fewer competing vaccines,7,8 and higher acceptance of vaccination at this earlier age.9 This data is so compelling that both the American Academy of Pediatrics and the American Cancer Society have updated their HPV vaccine recommendation language to emphasize earlier initiation. To date, several large studies have explored age 9 initiation, using either National Immunization Survey-Teen data5,10 or medical claims data,6 but there have been fewer analyses using state immunization registry data to assess progress on age 9/10 initiation.11 Moreover, none of these studies compared sociodemographic differences among age 9/10 initiators. Here, we analyzed individual-level immunization registry data to explore the relationship between sociodemographic characteristics of children who initiate the series at age 9/10 and complete the series.

Methods

Through an agreement with Iowa Health and Human Services, we received data from the Iowa Immunization Registry Information System (IRIS), which functions bi-directionally with electronic health record systems and currently has records for 5,022,611 individuals.12 We analyzed individual-level IRIS records for children born between 2004 and 2013 that contained data on vaccines administered including date of administration, child sex (male, female, unknown), type of insurance that paid for a vaccine (public, private, or none), race/ethnicity, and zip code of child. From zip code, we determined rurality using the RUCA ZCTA-codes (urban, large rural, small rural).13 Following, the RUCA ZCTA definitions, urban was defined as RUCA codes 1, 1.1, 2, 2.1, 3, 4.1, 5.1, 7.1, 8.1, 10.1; large rural as 4.0, 4.2, 5.0, 5.2, 6.0, 6.1; and small rural as 7, 7.2, 7.3, 7.4, 8, 8.2, 8.3, 8.4, 9, 9.1, 9.2, 10, 10.2, 10.3, 10.4, 10.5, 10.6. IRIS’ demographic data (i.e., sex and race/ethnicity) for children is taken from data in electronic medical records. We also calculated completion of the series based on age at initiation and year of initiation to account for changes in Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices guideline updates. For children who initiated after 2016, we defined completion as two doses for children who initiated the series between ages 9–14 and as three doses for those who initiated the series at age 15 or later. For children who initiated before 2016, we defined completion as three doses.

Analysis

We split children into three groups: (1) HPV vaccine series initiated at age 9/10, (2) initiated at 11/12 and (3) initiated between ages 13 and 17. We used chi-square statistics to compare sociodemographic characteristics between these three groups. Then, we performed logistic regressions to assess the odds of initiating the vaccine at ages 9/10 compared to a later age (11–17) and the odds of completing the vaccine by age 13 compared to completed after age 13 for those who initiated at ages 9–12, controlling for sociodemographic characteristics in both models. Since the race/ethnicity variables had a higher percentage of unknown data (3.4%), we also performed a sensitivity analysis in which we excluded children with either unknown race or ethnicity data. The directionality and magnitude of the results were similar, thus we only present the full analysis here. Analyses were performed in SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Statistical significance was determined if p-value <.05. We received a project determination of non-human subjects research from the University of Iowa Institutional Review Board.

Results

We had records for a total of 460,320 children and of those, 55.3% (n = 254,602) had at least one HPV vaccine administered between the ages 9–17 in their IRIS record. Of those who initiated the series, 74.7% completed the vaccine series. The majority of children initiated the series (n = 173,831) at ages 11/12, and very few initiated at ages 9/10 (n = 8,355; Table 1). We observed significant differences across all demographic characteristics between age at initiation. Specifically, we saw a greater proportion of females initiate at age 9 (55.8%) compared to on-time (50.8%) or later (46.9%). In looking at race/ethnicity, greater proportions of non-White children initiated early compared to on-time or later. Discussion

To date, this is among the first studies to use immunization registry data to carry out individual-level analysis comparing sociodemographic factors associated with age 9/10 initiation and completion of the HPV vaccine series. We identified individual (i.e., gender and race/ethnicity) and area-level (i.e., rurality) variables associated with both age 9/10 initiation and series completion. Like other studies,6,10 across all children, we observed very low rates of initiating the vaccine series at age 9/10. Recent analyses have found that increasing the percentage of children who initiate the series at ages 9/10 by just 1–3% could have a significant impact on raising completion rates.14 Here, we have gained insights into what populations may be early adopters of age 9/10 HPV vaccination and now we can focus efforts on populations that are currently experiencing the lowest rates of initiation, like males and those living in rural areas, may help to move the needle even further.

The finding that non-White race/ethnicity was associated positively with initiation at age 9/10 and negatively with completion of the series by age 13 has important implications to consider. There is mixed evidence related to racial/ethnic differences in HPV vaccination15; however, clearer patterns emerge when exploring HPV-associated cancer rates by race/ethnicity. For example, in a study using national cancer registry data, Hispanic, Black, and American Indian/Alaskan Native women had higher lifetime cumulative risk of developing and dying from HPV-associated cancer than White women.16 One possible explanation is that Hispanic, Black, and American Indian/Alaskan Native women have more direct experience with an HPV-associated cancer given the higher rates in their communities and in turn are more receptive to messaging about initiating the series at age 9/10. The lower odds of on-time completion observed among Black/African American children suggests possible barriers to follow-up vaccination that merit further exploration. The disparity between White and non-White children in adhering to yearly well child visits is well documented17,18 and this may be contributing to lower on-time completion rates of HPV vaccination, despite higher rates of early initiation. Moreover, a large meta-analysis found that documented visits to a healthcare provider in the previous year were strongly associated with both initiation and completion of the HPV vaccination series.19 Emphasizing early initiation, particularly for these historically underrepresented populations, may be an especially critical way to address HPV vaccine and cancer disparities.20

Existing interventions like EHR alerts, reminder calls, and parent-focused education may be appropriate for early initiation, but it will be necessary to make adaptations to account for both rural context and this earlier age group.7 Recent work has identified that multilevel interventions that address providers’ and families’ needs can be successful in promoting age 9 initiation in clinical settings,21,22 a finding that has been previously documented in studies focused on rural HPV vaccination as well.23,24 Beyond the clinics setting, another promising approach is pharmacist-led interventions,25 which has previously been explored in rural contexts for HPV vaccination with findings that pharmacists are willing to be engaged in HPV vaccination26 and that these projects have high feasibility.27

Limitations to consider include the fact that Iowa’s registry does not interface with other states and, therefore, would not capture vaccines that were given outside the state. Iowa shares a border with six other states and thus it is possible that children who live in border cities may receive healthcare out of state. There is also the possibility that vaccines that occurred within the state are not reflected in IRIS. These two factors could be contributing to bias in our results, either leading to over or under-estimation of early initiation. However, IRIS is a robust immunization registry that captures over 98% vaccinations that occur within the state. There is the potential for misclassification related to human error; for example, if a provider checks the wrong box for race/ethnicity or sex in the EHR, that data would be incorrect in IRIS as well. Finally, it is important to acknowledge that these findings are limited to a single state and not generalizable beyond Iowa.

These results can help partner organizations focused on HPV vaccine promotion to better target communication efforts to promote age 9/10 vaccination. As indicated by our findings, rural areas may be in particular need of increased efforts. In addition to adapting existing interventions, future work could include integrating geospatial analyses so that local organizations could be able to target specific counties or even zip codes with the lowest rates of initiation.
