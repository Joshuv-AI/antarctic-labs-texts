Abundant A-to-I RNA editing in spermatocytes may suppress transposons to compensate for piRNA downregulation in male germlines
Authors: Yuange Duan, Qiuhua Xie, Ling Ma, Qi Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe germline genome serves as a crucial battleground for transposon expansion, as transposons can increase their copy numbers in offspring when activated within germ cells. Unexpectedly, during Drosophila spermatogenesis, the piRNA pathway, typically responsible for transposon silencing in female germ cells, is significantly downregulated, coinciding with a burst of transposon expression in spermatocytes. This suggests that germ cells might rely on alternative mechanisms for transposon suppression. By leveraging single-cell Smart-seq transcriptomic data, we found that transposon expression, Adar expression, and A-to-I RNA editing efficiency are markedly elevated in Drosophila spermatocytes. Adar mutant flies exhibit higher testicular TE expression, likely resulting from the loss of editing-mediated suppression. In the absence of a fully functional piRNA pathway in male germline, Adar-mediated RNA editing may act as an alternative mechanism for transposon silencing, highlighting a potential role for Adar in maintaining genome integrity.KEYWORDS: RNA editing, spermatocytes, transposon, suppression, piRNA

RNA editing and transposon silencing in Drosophila

ADAR (adenosine deaminase acting on RNA)-mediated A-to-I RNA editing is commonly recognized for introducing transcriptomic variability and enhancing proteomic diversity [1–3]. Although insects possess only a single Adar gene homologous to mammalian ADAR2 [4], studies have suggested that the loss of dAdar RNA editing activity in Drosophila leads to innate immune activation [5], indicating a dual function of insect Adar homolog and raises a more fundamental question of whether dAdar can exhibit crucial functions in other biological contexts.

Transposable elements (TEs), also known as transposons, pose a significant threat to genomic stability [6]. In germ cells, uncontrolled transposon activity can lead to heritable mutations. A large amount of transposons can undergo bidirectional transcription [7], forming double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) structures that are substrates for ADARs [8]. In Drosophila, transposons constitute approximately 12% of the genome [9] and about 16% of the recorded RNA editing sites occur within repetitive regions [10], indicating potential interactions between RNA editing and transposons as documented in human and C. elegans [11,12].

In Drosophila somatic tissues, the siRNA (small interfering RNA) pathway provides a primary line of defence against TEs [13,14]. In female germ cells, the piRNA pathway has been firmly established as a key mechanism for transposon silencing [15]. However, during spermatogenesis, as germ cells differentiate into spermatocytes, there is a marked reduction in TE-derived piRNA populations [16]. This phenomenon has been observed in both D. melanogaster and D. simulans [17]. Such attenuation of piRNA-mediated TE control in spermatocytes raises the critical question of whether alternative mechanisms, such as RNA editing, exist to complement the piRNA pathway in maintaining genome stability during male germline development.

Single-cell Adar expression, RNA editing, and TE abundance in Drosophila testis

To understand whether transposons in Drosophila testis can be silenced by RNA editing, previous bulk RNA-Seq analyses (constrained by limited resolution) have reported low overall expression levels of Adar in the Drosophila testis, leading to a long-standing notion that Adar and RNA editing might be non-essential in male germ cells [18]. However, in Adar mutant flies, although spermatogonia retains the capacity for mitotic proliferation, subsequent differentiation is impaired, indicating that Adar plays an essential role in later stages of sperm development [19].

To overcome the resolution limitation of bulk RNA-Seq, we retrieved single-cell Smart-seq2 data of 13,025 well-annotated cells from an adult D. melanogaster provided by a previous literature [20]. Three hundred and twenty-two cells are annotated as male testis and have library sizes >1 × 105, which were used in the downstream analysis (Materials and Methods). These 322 cells include 62 spermatocytes and 260 non-spermatocyte cells (denoted as other testicular cells). We found that Adar has higher expression in spermatocytes than other cells (Figure 1A).

To identify RNA editing signals from single-cell transcriptomes, we collected all reported RNA editing sites in D. melanogaster and obtained a union of 8070 unique sites (Supplementary Table S1). Six thousand four hundred and eighty-nine (80.4%) of these editing sites are located in optimal dsRNA structures, suggesting a high tendency for editing. We further performed two-step strategies to exclude the false positive editing sites (which might be single nucleotide polymorphisms, SNPs) in w1118 (see Materials and Methods for detail). Five thousand two hundred and fifteen sites were finally regarded as the bona fide editing sites in our dataset (Supplementary Table S2). Notably, RNA editing sites are more abundant in spermatocytes compared to other testicular cells (Figure 1B, T-test, p value = 1.0E-11).

Then, a differential expression analysis of repetitive elements between 62 spermatocytes and other 260 testicular cells revealed 279 repetitive sequences significantly upregulated and only 11 downregulated in spermatocytes (|log2FC| > 2, adjusted p < 0.05) (Figure 1C, left). After removing simple repeats, a pronounced expressional burst of transposons was observed in spermatocytes, where 109 transposon families were significantly upregulated, while no transposon was downregulated (Figure 1C, right). This suggests that transposons are dramatically up-regulated in spermatocytes. The next question is how prevalent are transposons edited in spermatocytes and other testicular cells?

Among the 322 testicular cells, we identified 592 RNA editing sites detected in at least two cells. Intersecting these sites with annotated Drosophila transposable elements revealed that 19.8% (117/592) sites are located within repetitive sequences, the fraction of which is higher than genome-wide baseline of TE content [9]. If simple repeats were removed, then the most frequently edited site across spermatocytes also lies within the roo_LTR_Bel-Pao element (Figure 2). Moreover, 17 of 40 spermatocytes-edited sites are located in LTR retrotransposons spanning multiple families, including Bel-Pao and Gypsy. These results demonstrate the prevalence of transposon RNA editing in Drosophila spermatocytes.

We further compared the expression of transposons harbouring RNA editing sites in spermatocytes. Totally, five differentially expressed transposons are edited in spermatocytes (roo_LTR_Bel-Pao, FB_DNA_Tc1-Mariner, INE-1_DNA_Helitron, Quasimodo_LTR_Gypsy, and springer_LTR_Gypsy). Not surprisingly, four out of five edited transposons (except FB_DNA_Tc1-Mariner) have expression levels significantly up-regulated in spermatocytes than other testicular cells, suggesting a correlation between RNA editing and up-regulation of TE expression.

Validation of transposon expression in Adar mutant flies

To directly examine whether TE expression is altered in those Adar-deficiency strains, we downloaded three transcriptome datasets of male Adar mutant flies and the corresponding wild-type (Materials and Methods). Testes of Adar5G1, heads of Adar5G1, and AdarE374A were obtained. Adar5G1 mutant has Adar deleted and AdarE374A mutant has a point mutation that abolishes the deaminase activity [21]. The removal of RNA editing signals in mutant flies has been explicitly demonstrated in the original study [5] and also examined by ourselves, and therefore we will only focus on the expressional changes of TEs.

We found that in all Adar mutant flies, TEs are overall up-regulated compared to wild-type, since we constantly identified more up-regulated TEs compared to down-regulated ones (Figure 3A for testes and Supplementary Figure S1 for heads). For example, in testes of Adar5G1, 87 TEs were significantly up-regulated compared to wild-type, and only 45 TEs were down-regulated. This up-regulation tendency holds true if we only focus on edited TEs in single-cell data (Figure 3B). Notably, although Adar has both editing-dependent (deaminase) and editing-independent (dsRNA-binding) functions [5], the fact that differentially expressed TEs in AdarE374A mutant are all up-regulated (Supplementary Figure S1) indicates an editing-dependent regulatory role on TEs.

One potential concern is how to explain those up-regulated TEs in mutants that do not exhibit editing in the wild-type? We address this by emphasizing that our editing sites came from previously reported RNA editing candidates in D. melanogaster (Materials and Methods), and this list is far from a complete repertoire. Take human RNA editing for instance, it has been estimated that the human genome contains more than a hundred millions of editable adenosines [22], but in the RNA editing databases [10,23], the documented numbers of editing sites are lower for orders of magnitude. A similar situation may apply to D. melanogaster, where many TEs may undergo RNA editing but were regarded as unedited ones in our analysis.

Moreover, the number of up-regulated TEs in Adar5G1 mutant is much higher for testes compared to heads (Figure 3A and Supplementary Figure S1). This supports our notion that the TE-suppression role of RNA editing might be prevalent in male germline, where the piRNA pathway is down-regulated. In heads, other strategies might be involved in TE silencing [24], which might reduce the total TE transcript abundance and also weakening the statistical power in identifying differentially expressed TEs. In summary, our finding underscores a previously underappreciated role of Adar in safeguarding genomic stability during spermatogenesis in Drosophila.

Materials and Methods

Data acquisition

The reference genome of D. melanogaster (version number dmel_r6.06) was downloaded from FlyBase (https://flybase.org/). The scRNA-Seq libraries of D. melanogaster (w1118 strain) were retrieved from a previous literature [20]. Over 13,000 cells are available. According to the annotation provided by the original literature, 322 cells annotated as male testis were used, including 62 spermatocytes and other 260 testicular cells.

For male Adar mutants of D. melanogaster, we have three pairs of datasets downloaded from NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Two different mutants were used, the Adar5G1 mutant has Adar deleted (under w1118 background), and the AdarE374A mutant has a point mutation (under Canton-S background) that abolishes the deaminase activity only. The testis transcriptomes of Adar5G1 mutant and the corresponding wild-type were downloaded from NCBI under accession IDs SRR27212497-SRR27212502 (three biological replicates for each genotype).

DNA re-sequencing data of male (SRR14470609) and female (SRR14470610) of w1118 strain were downloaded from NCBI. These data were used for calling SNPs which should be excluded from the single-cell RNA editing profile of w1118 strain.

Annotation of transposable elements (TEs)

In this study, the terms transposable elements, transposons, or TEs, refer to the broad-sense TEs which include simple repeats, unless otherwise specified. To assess the expression level of transposable elements, we downloaded the Drosophila TE library D_mel_transposon_sequence_set_v10.2.fa provided by the Bergman Lab (https://github.com/bergmanlab/drosophila-transposons/tree/master). Genomic TE annotations were generated using RepeatMasker v4.1.5 (https://www.repeatmasker.org/) with default parameter. The resulting gff file was converted to gtf format (file provided as Supplementary Data S1) for compatibility to the downstream analyses.

RNA-Seq mapping and gene expression

For next generation sequencing data, adapter trimming and quality filtering were performed using fastp v0.20.1 with default parameters [25]. We mapped the RNA-Seq reads to the reference genome using hisat2 v2.2.1 with default parameter following a previous literature that quantified TE expression [26]. PCR duplicates were removed using Picard version 2.27.5 (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). Software featureCounts version 2.0.3 [27] was used to quantify the reads count (featureCounts -F GTF -t exon -g gene_id -s 0 -M -p -T 30 -a TE.gtf -o output.txt input.bam). TE expression levels were measured by FPKM (fragments per kilobase per million mapped fragments).

Library size is defined as the number of uniquely mapped reads to the genome. A previous study analysing Smart-Seq in human brain cells required a minimum cut-off of 1 × 106 uniquely mapped reads per cell [28]. Considering that the human genome is over 18 times larger than the Drosophila genome, we required 1 × 105 uniquely mapped reads as cut-off.

Differential analysis between spermatocytes and other cell types was performed using DESeq 1.34.0 [29]. Default parameters were used. The output file provides the log2 fold-change and adjusted p value (FDR) of each gene.

SNPs in w1118 strain

DNA re-sequencing reads of male and female w1118 strain were aligned to the reference genome using BWA-mem v0.7.17 with its default settings [30]. PCR duplicates were removed with Picard version 2.27.5. Variant calling was performed using the GATK HaplotypeCaller version 4.3.0.0 [31]. 650,042 SNPs were identified in female sample, 660,482 SNPs were identified in male sample, and a union of 677,560 SNPs were obtained (Supplementary Data S2).

RNA editing analysis

A list of all known A-to-I RNA editing sites reported in D. melanogaster were retrieved from different studies and a union was created [10,32–36]. A union of 8070 unique editing sites was obtained (Supplementary Table S1). Then, 677,560 SNPs in w1118 strain were excluded from the list of RNA editing candidates, resulting in 7749 unique editing sites. REDItools version 2.0 was used to directly extract the reads counts on candidate RNA editing sites [37]. 5 bps of read ends were trimmed. Base quality Q > 30 was required. SnpEff version 4.5 with default parameter was used to annotate the RNA editing sites [38].

For each editing site in each sample, a binomial test based on depth and alternative reads count was performed to judge the probability (P) of a variation site coming from sequencing error. Multiple testing correction was used to obtain an FDR [39]. This methodology has been sufficiently described in our previous works [40–42]. Here, we provide the conclusion that a site with FDR < 0.05 is likely a true variation in RNA instead of sequencing error.

Another criterion to exclude SNPs from RNA editing is to require cell specificity. For example, if we observe editing at sites in the germline cells where there is no editing in the somatic cells, then this editing is likely true. Thus, we applied this strategy to filter the 7749 editing sites resulted from the previous step. Among the 13,025 total cells, we required an editing site to have an editing signal (level > 0 and binomial test FDR < 0.05) in at least one cell, while having no editing signal in at least one other cell (depth ≥10 and no edited reads detected). This cell specificity suggests that the observed variations in RNAs are unlikely SNPs. Five thousand two hundred and fifteen sites were finally regarded as the bona fide editing sites in our dataset (Supplementary Table S2).

dsRNA structure was predicted using RNALfold with default parameter [43]. Each RNA editing site was extended 150 bp to upstream and downstream and then subjected to RNALfold. We counted the number of focal RNA editing site, which is located in the predicted local hairpin structure.

Statistics and graphical works

Statistics and graphical works were accomplished in R language (version 3.6.3).
