Ozone therapy by rectal insufflation in long COVID syndrome: a preliminary study with thermographic evaluation and quality of life measurement
Authors: Raquel Sacristan Perez, Maria José Martínez Vivas, Silvia Menéndez-Cepero, Alessio Cabizosu, Francisco-Javier Hidalgo Tallón
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPost-acute coronavirus disease 2019 syndrome (PACS) is a multisystemic clinical condition that clinically starting weeks or even months after acute SARS-CoV-2 (COVID 19) infections. The objective of this study was to explore change in quality of life, mechanical force and metabolic activity after ozone therapy treatment.MethodsA preliminary clinical open label study was carried out. Twenty-three patients diagnosed with PACS more than 5 months ago participated in the study. Thermography, Handgrip test and Quality of life scale SF12 were used to analyze the effects of ozone therapy on these patients.ResultsRelated to the Handgrip test, a significant improvement was recorded in T0-T1 time interval (p = 0.0003; d 0.88), but in T1-T2 time variation was not statistically significant (p = 0.957). About the SF-12 test, a significant difference was also found in both the PCS (p = 0.0038; d = 0.67) and the MCS (p = 0.0088, d = 0.60) in T0-T1 time, but not in T1-T2 for either the PCS (p = 0.5933) or the MCS (p = 0.3917). Finally, regarding temperature, statistically significant differences were observed in the T0-T1 time interval (p = 0.0465, d = 0.44) but not between T1 and T2 (p = 0.1038). No significant differences between the sexes were observed for any of the four parameters.ConclusionOur results confirmed that clinical ozone could be a potentially useful drug in improving the strength, quality of life and basal metabolism in patients with long COVID. However, we believe that further studies and larger sample sizes are needed to corroborate and support the results obtained in this preliminary trial.Keywords: Thermography, Long covid, Ozone, Handgrip, Quality of life

Introduction

Post-acute coronavirus disease 2019 syndrome (PACS) or long COVID is a multisystemic clinical condition, currently affecting more than 400 million people and clinically starting weeks or even months after acute SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) infections [1]. PACS is characterized by symptoms such as chronic fatigue, dyspnea, cognitive alteration, joint pain, headache and even skin alterations, drastically affecting the quality of life of affected patients [2]. Despite the growing body of literature on this subject, there is currently no cure for this clinical condition. As therapeutic options are limited and lack strong evidence, unconventional approaches such as ozone therapy have been tried by various authors [3–5].

Medical ozone is a mixture of oxygen and ozone that can be delivered to patients both locally and systemic. The systemic way is mainly administered by rectal insufflations (RI) [3,6], or by indirect endovenous ozone therapy [7]. In the first case, a gaseous enema is applied, and in the second case, a certain volume of blood is made to react ex-vivo with an equivalent volume of gas. Within minutes, the blood will be re-infused after having reacted with the medical ozone. These techniques have been shown to improve the quality of life in fibromyalgia patients [8,9], and as mentioned above, some authors have tested the application in Long COVID.

Ozone therapy has demonstrated significant benefits in various diseases, both acute and chronic, due to its anti-inflammatory, immunostimulant and antioxidant capacity, improving oxygenation and tissue perfusion as well as mitochondrial function [10].

Some authors suggest that ozone therapy may activate cellular defense pathways, increasing endogenous antioxidant capacity and attenuating oxidative stress in humans [9,11]and animals [12,13]. A clinical study involving patients with multiple sclerosis showed that systemic ozone therapy increased Nrf phosphorylation and reduced glutathione levels, decreasing pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNFα and IL-1β) [11], while in animals, Menga et al. [12] and Oliveira et al. [13] observed that oxidative ozone preconditioning induces Nrf2 activation, protects the heart against ischemia-reperfusion injury by attenuating mitochondrial damage and stimulates superoxide dismutase and glutathione peroxidase activity, thus having a positive effect on mitochondria.

Based on these findings, in recent years, numerous studies with very promising results have been published on ozone therapy in COVID-19, as both clinical and metabolic improvements have been demonstrated in this type of patient [14,15], however, in PACS, there are currently no in-depth studies that objectively and reliably quantify the metabolic response associated with treatment and clinical improvement in the short, medium, and long term. In this context, infrared thermography (IT) could be of great help in analyzing the metabolic response to ozone treatment in PACS, as it is a noninvasive, inexpensive, objective, easy-to-use, and fast-response technique [7,16–18].

The IT is a diagnostic tool that, through the analysis of infrared radiation, provides a view of the skin temperature (TSK) of the analyzed body region. Changes in TSK depend on variations in infrared emission, which in turn is the result of both superficial and deep tissue response associated with vascular, inflammatory, or thermoregulatory processes [19–21]. Numerous studies have already demonstrated its efficiency in analyzing tissue metabolic response in different clinical settings, including ozone treatment, but its use in PACS is not well known [16,22].

The objective of this preliminary study was to explore the response of a group of patients affected by PACS related to their quality of life, mechanical force and metabolic activity. To that purpose the Short-Form Health Survey (SF-12), the Handgrip Strength Test (HST) and an infrared thermographic study (IRT) were respectively applied. The working hypothesis was that ozone therapy has the capacity of improving the quality of life, the muscular response, and the metabolic function of these patients.

Methods

Study design

The study was conducted over several sessions. During the first session, the orientation session, the study procedures were explained to the volunteers and main patients characteristics (age, sex, BMI) were collected. All participants received an information sheet about the study procedures and signed an informed consent form in this session. In the subsequent sessions T0 (Baseline), T1 (6 weeks), and T2 (12 weeks), the investigators obtained thermographic data from the abdominal region Handgrip test (HTS) force value, administered rectal insufflations for 12 weeks, and collected data using the HST [23] and the SF-12 health survey [24] (Figure 1).

Sequence of the study protocol. The assessments were performed at baseline (T0), after 6 weeks (T1), and after 12 weeks (T2).

All patients participated voluntarily and did not receive any financial or other incentives for taking part in the study. In order to minimize bias in the results, patients were not allowed to make any changes to their diet or medication regimen throughout the 12-week study period without informing the researchers. The study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki [25] and was approved by the ethics committee of the Catholic University of San Antonio of Murcia (CE092407) approved in September of 2024. This study was registered in the ISRCTN registry with ID: 48227, secondary identification numbers: TC/1924.

Participants

Twenty-three patients of Long-Covid Association of Murcia were recruited and finished the study. Patient characteristics are presented in Table 1. The inclusion criteria were patients over 18 years of age, diagnosed with PACS more than 5 months ago. Only those patients who were able to provide medical certificates showing positive antigen test results for the virus SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) were admitted. No limits were set regarding sex, race, or BMI (Body Mass Index). Patients with gastrointestinal disorders and patients taking supplements or medication that could alter thermoregulatory response prior to the test were excluded. Patients with high blood pressure, heart failure, arrhythmias, or a history of cardiovascular events, were excluded because these are comorbidities of long COVID that can affect the thermographic response. Finally, patients with strong cognitive sequelae, strong brain fog, that prevented the normal conduct of the study were also excluded.

Thermographic protocol

As part of the thermographic protocol, three imaging sessions were conducted -T°0 (baseline), T°1(midpoint), and T°2 (end of the protocol) targeting the abdominal region, in strict adherence to all Thermographic Imaging Standardization in Experimental Medicine (TISEM) guidelines [26,27]. The thermograph used was the Hikmicro M60 model (Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China). This thermograph has thermal sensitivity <0,04 °C and registers from −20 °C to +150 °C. The emissivity was established at 0.98 as described by other author [28]. For the thermographic protocols, the acclimatization of the subjects was carried out in a room (12 m2), T° 23 °C - 24 °C, humidity of 46 (± 0.8%), 1 ATM for 15 minutes. The volunteers were wearing underwear and shorts. The temperature and humidity in the room were monitored and recorded by digital hygrometer. There were no electronic devices or artificial lights near the measurement point that could influence the volunteers’ body temperatures. The thermographic device was turned on 30 minutes before the first recording and was located 1 m from the patient. The camera was positioned in front of the volunteers’ abdominal region at an angle of 10◦ (Figure 2).

ROI of thermographic images (ROI) of the abdominal area. R1 indicates the ROI analyzed in the abdominal region.

Handgrip test

The handgrip test (HTS) used was the model Camry EH101 (Zhongshan Camry Electronic Co. Ltd., Zhongshan, China). Three recordings with the HST (0 basal, week 6 at the middle, and week 12 at the end of the protocol) were made on the dominant hand, using the mean of these three recordings as the reference value. This diagnostic tool has already been studied by other researchers in relation to this condition [29].

Quality of life test

To measure the quality of life, data from the SF-12 test were collected, also at 3 different times. The SF-12 questionnaire generates two types of data, Physical Component (PCS) and Mental Component (MCS). These scores can vary between 0 and 100, and higher scores correspond to a better state of health [30].

Ozone protocols

Regarding the ozone protocol and following the methodology described by Hidalgo et al. [31], 8 mg of medical ozone (200 mL of gas at a concentration of 40 µg/mL) was administered per session. The complete protocol consisted of 24 rectal ozone therapy sessions over a 12-week period. The ozone machine used for rectal intestinal insufflation was Hyper Medozon comfort (Hab Herrmann Apparatebau GmbH, Elsenfeld, Germany). The insufflation power of the device was calibrated at 100–240 V / 50–60 Hz, with an insufflation rate of 1.0 l/min. Five sessions per week were administered during the first two weeks, two sessions per week from weeks 3 to 6, and one session per week from weeks 7 to 12.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM Social Sciences (SPSS, v. 21.0, Chicago, IL, USA). The homogeneity and normality of the data were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). To assess whether there was a statistically significant relationship among the three tests throughout the protocol (T0: baseline, T1: 6 weeks, T2: 12 weeks), a repeated-measures ANOVA was performed for each dependent variable with time as the factor: T0: baseline, T1: 6 weeks, T2: 12 weeks. Cohen’s d effect sizes (95% confidence interval) were calculated for comparisons of temperature, strength, and quality of life in cases where significance was found in the ANOVA models. Tukey’s post hoc analysis was also performed on all ANOVA models. The threshold values for ES statistics were those suggested by other authors; the significance level was set at p ≤ 0.05 [32,33].

Discussion

The objective of this preliminary study was to evaluate the response of a group of patients affected by PACS to a three-month rectal insufflation (RI) protocol, assessing quality of life, muscle strength, and metabolic activity. Given the exploratory, open-label design of this study and the absence of a control group, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Although improvements were observed in quality of life, muscle strength, and metabolic activity during the intervention period, these results should be considered preliminary and cannot establish therapeutic efficacy or a causal relationship between ozone therapy and the observed changes. Rather, they suggest that rectal ozone therapy may warrant further investigation in adequately powered randomized controlled trials.

Firstly, both the PCS and MCS of the SF-12 questionnaire showed statistically significant increases across the treatment timeline, with the most pronounced increase occurring in the first phase T0–T1 PCS (p = 0.0038), MCS (p: 0.0230). These improvements may indicate not only a perceived enhancement of general health and emotional well-being, but also a possible impact on the psychosocial dimensions of PACS, which often include fatigue, cognitive dysfunction, and emotional distress. However, because of the study design, alternative explanations such as placebo effects, spontaneous recovery, or regression to the mean cannot be excluded.

A similar therapeutic protocol was employed in the study by Hidalgo-Tallón et al. [31], which investigated the effects of rectal ozone therapy in patients with fibromyalgia. In that trial, 24 sessions of rectal ozone insufflation were administered over a 12-week period (200 mL of gas at a concentration of 40 µg/mL per session). In terms of quality of life, the authors observed that the impact of the disease according to the Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQ) improved in the first four weeks, with a tendency to remain stable from week 4 to week 12. Similarly, in our study, quality of life assessed by the SF 12 (PCS and MCS) improved most at the start of treatment. Although both variables tended to stabilize from week 6 onwards. While these observations are consistent with those reported in fibromyalgia, they should be interpreted as exploratory and require confirmation in controlled studies before any conclusions regarding treatment effectiveness can be drawn.

The authors [31] reported significant improvements in physical symptoms, as measured by the Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQ), along with a significant increase in the PCS. These findings are consistent with ours in terms of improvements in physical functioning. However, in terms of mental health, these authors did not report significant changes in the SF-12 or the depression item of the FIQ, but they did observe significant improvements in mood according to the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), while our study showed a statistically significant improvement in the MCS (p = 0.012). Taken together, these findings may suggest a potential benefit on emotional well-being; however, this interpretation remains preliminary because our study was not designed to demonstrate efficacy. On the other hand, the study by Clavo et al. [34] specifically assessed the psychological effects of ozone therapy, focusing on anxiety and depression in patients with refractory symptoms of severe chronic illnesses. Despite the heterogeneity of the study population and the pilot nature of the design, the authors reported significant reductions in both anxiety and depression, as measured by validated instruments such as the Beck Depression Inventory and the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory. These findings are in line with our observations and may support the hypothesis that systemic ozone therapy could influence emotional and psychological dimensions in chronic disease, although confirmation in larger controlled trials is still required.

The convergence of evidence across these studies provides a biologically plausible framework for interpreting our findings. Previous experimental and clinical studies have suggested that ozone therapy may modulate oxidative stress, enhance mitochondrial efficiency, and regulate neuroinflammatory processes. However, these mechanisms were not directly evaluated in the present study and should therefore be considered as hypothetical explanations rather than demonstrated mechanisms underlying the observed clinical changes. In parallel, the handgrip strength test, a well-established functional biomarker of global muscular capacity and a predictor of morbidity in chronic disease, showed a statistically significant improvement as early as week six, which was sustained through week twelve. Likewise, potential mechanisms such as enhanced peripheral muscle oxygenation or improved energy metabolism remain speculative in the context of the present study, as no physiological or biochemical markers were assessed [35].

Given its role as an objective and reproducible functional measure, it is particularly noteworthy that, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to employ the handgrip strength test to evaluate the effects of systemic ozone therapy in a cohort of patients with post-COVID syndrome (PACS). This methodological inclusion represents a novel and distinctive contribution, as it allows for a simple, standardized, and quantifiable assessment of muscular performance over the course of treatment.

To date, we have identified only one prior study that used the handgrip strength test in the context of ozone therapy (albeit via topical rather than systemic administration). In that randomized controlled trial, El-Sabbahi SA et al [36], investigated the use of ozonated gel applied via phonophoresis in the treatment of chronic lateral epicondylitis and reported significant improvements in grip strength as a functional outcome. While the mechanisms of action and route of administration differ, the inclusion of the handgrip strength test in both studies supports its sensitivity as a clinical marker of response to ozone therapy. In our study, the progressive improvement observed in handgrip strength should be considered a preliminary finding that requires confirmation in larger randomized controlled studies.

Regarding this variable, it is worth noting that, in line with other authors, higher mean values have been observed in men compared to women; however, the increase between T0 and T1 is very similar regardless of gender, and the lack of increase from T1 to T2 is also similar in both sexes. This could suggest a certain reliability of the technique for evaluating the results of ozone therapy, a point that needs to be investigated in greater depth and with greater specificity in future studies.

Thermographic analysis, although not statistically significant between T0 and T2 (p = 0.4139), revealed a pattern characterized by an initial statistically significant decrease in skin surface temperature during the first six weeks (p = 0.0465), followed by return to baseline values by the twelfth week. This finding, as observed by other authors, may depend on vascular changes secondary to ozone administration. In this regard, preclinical studies in rabbits demonstrated that, following the insufflation of 150 ml of rectal ozone, PaO2 increased by 230% in the mesocolic vein and by 121% in the portal vein [37]. The transient reduction observed in our records could hypothetically be to processes of deep vasodilation versus superficial vasoconstriction, although these vascular responses were not directly investigated in the present study and therefore remain speculative.

However, it should be noted that other authors have reported superficial increases in local temperature following the administration of ozone via infiltration, suggesting nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation. Hidalgo et al. recorded significant thermographic changes in the treatment of refractory tendinopathy in a professional athlete [38].

Nowicka et al. also reported a significant increase in skin temperature in patients with systemic sclerosis treated with topical ozone therapy, in this case by using ozonated water baths. The changes could be correlated with clinical improvement [39,40].

In comparison, rectal applications appear to generate less specific thermographic responses, which could be attributed to the lower regional specificity of rectal ozone administration, with less defined diffusion patterns and vasomodulatory effects conditioned by anatomical particularities. These findings may suggest that, depending on the route of ozone administration, different thermographic responses may occur, or alternatively that a physiological adaptation to treatment develops after the first six weeks. However, these interpretations represent hypotheses derived from previous literature and should not be considered mechanisms demonstrated by the present study.

The improvements observed in both the SF-12 domains and handgrip strength, as well as in the thermographic analysis during the first phase of treatment, may support the role of ozone therapy in restoring function and subjective well-being. Furthermore, the thermographic protocol, despite its limited statistical significance in the final phase, offers a reproducible and noninvasive method for monitoring physiological responses and may warrant further refinement in future research.

Therefore, the present preliminary findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and support the rationale for future randomized controlled studies rather than providing definitive evidence of therapeutic efficacy [31,32]. Similarly, the biological mechanisms discussed throughout this section are based on previous experimental and clinical evidence and are proposed only as plausible explanatory hypotheses. Future randomized controlled studies incorporating biochemical, inflammatory, oxidative stress, and metabolic biomarkers will be required to determine whether these mechanisms are involved in the clinical response observed in patients with PACS.

Limitation and strength

Nevertheless, this study presents significant limitations, particularly regarding its design. Although the prevalence of PACS may be considered high, considerable variability has been reported across populations, likely due to diagnostic challenges. In our setting, the limited number of patients influenced the selection process and the sample size, which restricted the scope of our conclusions. It should be kept in mind that this is a preliminary, exploratory clinical study and may be enough to draw conclusions that would encourage a trial with different characteristics.

It′s known that in PACS tends to be more severe in women than in men, and is also more prevalent, with women being 1.5% more likely to develop the condition. In our trial, there was a difference in recruitment with regard to gender (18 women and 5 men), but at the end of the study no significant differences were recorded between the sexes regarding the variables studied, probably due to the small sample size.

The open-label design lacks a control group, and placebo effects cannot be ruled out. Moreover, while the SF-12 and handgrip tests are validated measures, the complexity of PACS suggests the utility of broader neurocognitive and cardiopulmonary assessment tools. The absence of longitudinal follow-up also limits understanding of the durability of observed improvements.

Finally, according to these preliminary results and the excellent tolerability of the treatment, with no adverse events of interest recorded, we consider that future research should include randomized controlled trials with larger cohorts, standardized ozone protocols, and extended follow-up periods. Incorporating biochemical markers of oxidative stress, endothelial function, and autonomic regulation could provide valuable pathophysiological information. Furthermore, expanding thermographic evaluation to other anatomical regions or incorporating dynamic imaging protocols might capture subtle thermal changes associated with ozone-induced metabolic modulation.

These findings are consistent with previous reports suggesting ozone therapy’s ability to modulate oxidative stress, improve oxygen metabolism, and reduce inflammatory mediators implicated in PACS pathophysiology.

Conclusions

Our results indicate that medical ozone administered via rectal insufflation was well tolerated in this preliminary exploratory study. Although improvements in quality of life, muscle strength, and thermographic parameters were observed during the intervention period, these results should be considered preliminary and need to be confirmed through more specific study designs with larger sample sizes. This is primarily because, although the observed changes are consistent with the biological mechanisms previously proposed for ozone therapy, these mechanisms were not directly investigated in the present study. Therefore, larger randomized controlled trials incorporating metabolic, inflammatory, redox, and other mechanical biomarkers are needed to confirm both the clinical efficacy of ozone therapy and the biological pathways that may be involved.
