Tunable PEGylation platform for efficient siRNA delivery using mesoporous silica nanoparticles
Authors: Taeho Kim, Dongseong Seo, Jonghyun Park, Dongmin Yu, Chanju Choi, Sohyeon Yu, Jeongmin Shin, Simseok A. Yuk, Daekyung Sung, Hyungjun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) coatings are widely used to improve nanoparticle stability and biocompatibility, yet conventional PEGylation methods rely on multi-step conjugation that limits reproducibility and restricts control over charge–shielding balance critical for siRNA delivery. Here, we present a composition-tunable PEGylation strategy based on poly(2-(dimethylamino)ethyl methacrylate-co-poly(ethylene glycol) methacrylate) (poly(DMAEMA-co-PEGMA), hereafter referred to as AP) copolymers for the efficient delivery of PD-L1 siRNA using mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs). Copolymers with varying DMAEMA: PEGMA ratios (2:1, 1:1, 0.5:1) and PEG chain lengths (Mn 450 or 950 Da) were synthesized via one-step free-radical polymerization, enabling precise tuning of surface charge and steric stabilization. The optimized MSN/copolymer complexes exhibited enhanced siRNA loading, colloidal stability, and serum resistance while maintaining favorable cytocompatibility. Cellular studies demonstrated improved uptake and significant PD-L1 gene silencing. This work establishes a composition-tunable and scalable PEGylation platform that overcomes the limitations of conventional surface modification and provides a potential strategy for siRNA-based applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13036-026-00701-5.

Introduction

Poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) is one of the most widely utilized polymers in nanomedicine due to its hydrophilicity, biocompatibility, and capacity to minimize nonspecific interactions at the bio–nano interface [1–3]. A hydrated PEG corona introduces a steric barrier that suppresses protein adsorption, aggregation, and opsonization by the mononuclear phagocyte system, thereby prolonging systemic circulation and improving pharmacokinetics [4–6]. PEGylation also enhances colloidal stability under physiological conditions where ionic strength and serum proteins can otherwise trigger aggregation or premature release [7, 8]. Owing to its safety and versatility, PEG has become a standard component in nanoparticle drug delivery, functioning as both a stealth coating and a tunable molecular spacer. However, PEGylation for gene delivery poses distinct challenges compared with small-molecule systems. For nucleic acid therapeutics such as small interfering RNA (siRNA), carriers must condense negatively charged siRNA through electrostatic interactions while maintaining sufficient PEG shielding to prevent serum adsorption and cytotoxicity [9–11]. This dual requirement—strong complexation versus colloidal stability—defines the charge–stealth trade-off. Insufficient charge leads to poor siRNA encapsulation and low transfection, whereas excessive cationic density causes aggregation and membrane damage [12]. Thus, the degree, architecture, and method of PEG incorporation critically determine delivery performance and safety.

PEGylation has been extensively used to modify cationic polymers such as polyethyleneimine (PEI), poly(L-lysine), poly(amidoamine) dendrimers, or poly(2-dimethylaminoethyl methacrylate) (PDMAEMA), reducing their cytotoxicity and improving serum stability [13–15]. Conventional methods involve covalent coupling of PEG chains to these backbones—typically via carbodiimide (EDC/NHS) amidation, maleimide–thiol linkages, or silane grafting on inorganic supports [16, 17]. While these reactions generate stable PEG layers, they demand rigorous control of pH and stoichiometry, as well as post-reaction purification to remove residual reagents, which complicates scalability. Furthermore, every conjugation step consumes amine groups responsible for siRNA binding, thereby reducing charge density and impairing complexation [18]. Balancing PEG coverage between sufficient steric protection and retained cationic charge remains an enduring limitation. To refine PEG control, surface-initiated polymerizations such as atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP) and reversible addition–fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT) have been used to form high-density PEGMA or OEGMA brushes [19–21]. These enable precise chain-length regulation but require initiator immobilization, oxygen-free conditions, and metal catalysts—factors incompatible with labile biological cargoes. In mesoporous nanocarriers, in-pore polymer growth may also reduce available pore volume. Additionally, dense PEG layers often produce a PEG-shielding effect that hinders siRNA binding and cellular uptake [22, 23]. Although pH-cleavable PEG linkers can mitigate this, they add further chemical complexity [24]. Consequently, a simplified and composition-tunable PEGylation strategy is needed—one that simultaneously controls electrostatic binding and steric stabilization while preserving reproducibility and scalability.

Amphiphilic copolymers incorporating both cationic and PEG segments offer an elegant solution. Copolymers of 2-(dimethylamino)ethyl methacrylate (DMAEMA) and poly(ethylene glycol) methacrylate (PEGMA) provide a single macromolecule capable of both siRNA complexation and steric stabilization [25, 26]. DMAEMA units yield tertiary amine groups that protonate under physiological conditions, imparting positive charge for siRNA binding, while PEGMA chains generate a hydrophilic corona that minimizes nonspecific adsorption. Varying the DMAEMA: PEGMA molar ratio tunes the surface charge, and altering PEG chain length modulates steric repulsion and hydration. This dual control allows optimization of the charge–shielding equilibrium governing siRNA loading and release. Unlike multi-step coupling or grafting approaches, poly(DMAEMA-co-PEGMA) can be synthesized by a single free-radical polymerization, eliminating catalysts and complicated post-functionalization. The resulting copolymer can adsorb directly onto nanoparticles via electrostatic interaction, simplifying PEGylation and enabling reproducible, scalable fabrication.

Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) are attractive for gene delivery because of their large surface area, tunable pore architecture, and biocompatibility [27–29]. Their silanol-rich surfaces enable facile modification, yet the intrinsic negative charge of silica hinders direct siRNA loading. Cationic polymer coatings—PEI, chitosan, or PDMAEMA—have been applied to improve electrostatic binding [30–32], but excessive positive charge often induces cytotoxicity and aggregation. Adding PEG can mitigate these effects, though conventional PEGylation again suffers from complex synthesis and poor reproducibility. A poly(DMAEMA-co-PEGMA) coating resolves these conflicts by combining both functionalities in a single layer: DMAEMA provides electrostatic affinity for siRNA, and PEGMA ensures hydration-layer stabilization. The process requires no silanization or ligand exchange, maintaining mesopore accessibility and consistent particle morphology while allowing facile control of polymer composition. The programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)/PD-1 axis is a major pathway of tumor immune evasion [33, 34]. Although monoclonal antibodies targeting this checkpoint have revolutionized oncology, issues of cost, immunogenicity, and limited tissue penetration persist [35]. Small-interfering RNA directed against PD-L1 mRNA (PD-L1 siRNA) offers a gene-silencing approach to restore T-cell activation and reinstate antitumor immunity [36, 37]. Yet siRNA’s clinical utility remains restricted by rapid enzymatic degradation and inefficient cell entry. A nanocarrier that stably loads, protects, and delivers PD-L1 siRNA is therefore essential for realizing RNA-based immune modulation. Given these challenges, an efficient nanocarrier capable of protecting and delivering PD-L1 siRNA is required. Among various nanocarrier systems, MSNs are particularly attractive carriers for siRNA delivery due to their high surface area, tunable pore size, and versatile surface functionalization. These properties enable efficient loading and protection of nucleic acids while allowing further modification for targeted delivery. In addition, the mesoporous structure provides opportunities for co-delivery of additional therapeutic agents or immunomodulatory molecules, which could be advantageous for combination cancer immunotherapy strategies targeting immune checkpoint pathways such as PD-L1. This work presents a simplified PEGylation platform for PD-L1 siRNA delivery using mesoporous silica nanoparticles coated with poly(DMAEMA-co-PEGMA) copolymers (hereafter referred to as Amine Polymers, APs). The APs were synthesized by one-step free-radical polymerization with varying DMAEMA: PEGMA ratios (2:1, 1:1, 0.5:1) and PEG molecular weights (Mn = 450 and 950 Da). These parameters govern the interplay between cationic charge and steric shielding. We hypothesize that higher DMAEMA content enhances siRNA binding via electrostatic attraction, whereas longer PEG chains improve colloidal stability and reduce nonspecific interactions. The optimized formulation is expected to achieve a balance between gene-binding efficiency and biological compatibility. To validate this concept, we synthesized and characterized the copolymers (NMR, GPC), coated siRNA-loaded MSNs, and examined their physicochemical, structural, and biological properties. We further evaluated siRNA protection, cellular uptake, PD-L1 knockdown, and cytotoxicity in vitro. This study demonstrates that rational composition tuning within a single copolymer enables simple, controllable, and scalable PEGylation of MSN carriers— offering a practical route for efficient and biocompatible siRNA delivery. Materials and methods

Materials

Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, 98%), tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, 98%), and triethanolamine (TEA, 99%) were purchased from Merck (Sigma-Aldrich). PD-L1 siRNA and RNase A were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. 2-(Dimethylamino)ethyl methacrylate (DMAEMA, 99%), polyethylene glycol methacrylate (PEGMA, 99%, Mn ≈ 950 g mol⁻¹), polyethylene glycol methacrylate (PEGMA, 99%, Mn ≈ 450 g mol⁻¹), tetrahydrofuran (THF, 99.9%), and other analytical-grade solvents were purchased from Merck. 2,2′-Azobis(2-methylpropionitrile) (AIBN, 99%) was obtained from Junsei Chemical. CT26 murine colon carcinoma cells, Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), penicillin–streptomycin, and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Welgene (South Korea). 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl-2 H-tetrazolium bromide (MTT) was obtained from Amresco (USA), and molecular biology–grade agarose was purchased from Bio-Rad. All materials were used as received unless otherwise stated. PD-L1 siRNA (sense, 5′-CCCACAUAAAAAACAGUUGTT-3′; antisense, 5′-CAACUGUUUUUUAUGUGGGTT-3′) were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT, Coralville, Iowa, USA). PD-L1 ELISA kit was purchased from Biomatik (Wilmington, DE, USA).

Synthesis of mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs)

Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) were synthesized using a modified sol–gel method. Briefly, cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, 200 mg) was dissolved in 96 mL of deionized (DI) water under magnetic stirring, followed by the addition of 0.7 mL of 2 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH). The solution was heated to 80 °C and equilibrated for 30 min. Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, 1 mL) was then added dropwise under vigorous stirring, and the reaction was maintained at 80 °C for an additional 2 h. After completion, the reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature, and the resulting nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 15 min. To remove the CTAB template The precipitate was then refluxed in ethanol containing 1% (v/v) HCl (prepared by adding 5 mL of 37% HCl to 500 mL ethanol) at 70 °C for 48 h with continuous stirring. The particles were subsequently washed with ethanol and DI water, centrifuged, and redispersed in DI water for storage.

Amine functionalization of MSNs (MSNa)

To introduce amine groups onto the MSN surface, the as-synthesized MSNs were functionalized with (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (APTES). Briefly, MSNs (50 mg mL⁻¹) were dispersed in ethanol and mixed with APTES (25 µL) under stirring at room temperature for 24 h. The resulting amine-functionalized MSNs (MSNa) were collected by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 10 min, washed three times with ethanol to remove unreacted APTES, and redispersed in DI water for further use.

Synthesis of APs

Synthesis of Amine Polymers (APs): APs were synthesized via free-radical copolymerization of DMAEMA and PEGMA. Before starting the polymerization, DMAEMA and PEGMA were run through a column with an inhibitor removal system for 1 and 3 h, respectively. To synthesize the copolymers, 2-(Dimethylamino)ethyl methacrylate (DMAEMA, 99%), and poly(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate (PEGMA, 99%; Mn ≈ 450–950 g mol⁻¹) were dissolved in 5.5 mL of tetrahydrofuran (THF, anhydrous, 99.9%). Here, PEG side chains with number-average molecular weights (Mn) of approximately 450 and 950 g mol⁻¹ were used in the PEGMA monomers for copolymer synthesis. The overall molecular weight of the resulting copolymers differs from these values and is reported in Table 1. The inhibitor-removal columns, DMAEMA, PEGMA, and THF, were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The molarity of PEGMA was set to 0.5 mmol, and the DMAEMA ratios were changed from 0.5 to 2 mmol, resulting in a library with varying proportions of three species. Next, 0.12 mmol of 2,2-Azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN, 99%) (Daejung, Seoul, Korea) was added as a radical initiator. The combination of polymers and initiator was degassed with Ar gas for 4 min before being sealed with Teflon tape. Polymerization was carried out at 70 °C for 24 h with vigorous stirring. After polymerization, a 10% concentration of product polymers was achieved, cooled to 25 °C, and kept at 4 °C until use. The amine polymers (APs) were termed AP1, AP2, and AP3 according to their molar ratios (DMAEMA: PEGMA = 2:1, 1:1, and 0.5:1, respectively). To distinguish PEG chain lengths, the polymers were further denoted as APx-450 or APx-950 depending on the molecular weight (Mn) of PEGMA used in the synthesis.

Characterization of APs

The chemical composition and purity of the polymers were determined by proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy (400 MHz, 25 °C, Dimethyl sulfoxide-d6 (DMSO d6, 99.8%) (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) as the deuterated solvent, JEOL JNM-ECZ400S/L1, Tokyo, Japan). Gel-permeation chromatography (GPC, Agilent 1200 S/miniDAWNTREOS, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used to measure average molecular weight (Mn; g mol-1) and polydispersity index (PDI, ÐM = Mw/Mn) using relative calibration with polystyrene (PS) standards and THF as the eluent at a flow rate of 1.0 mL min-1 at 35 °C.

Preparation of MSNa/PD-L1 siRNA complexes

AP1, AP2, and AP3 were prepared from stock solutions in THF (100 mg mL⁻¹). A defined volume of each polymer solution was transferred and dried overnight under a fume hood to completely remove THF. The dried polymers were then redispersed in HEPES buffer (pH 7.2) to obtain a final concentration of 1 mg mL⁻¹. Amine-functionalized MSNs (MSNa) were also dispersed in HEPES buffer (pH 7.2) at 1 mg mL⁻¹. MSNa and PD-L1 siRNA were first mixed and vortexed for 20 min to allow electrostatic complex formation. Subsequently, AP solution was added, and the mixture was further vortexed for 1 h to ensure stable coating. For a representative formulation (200 nM siRNA), 90 µL of siRNA, 270 µL of MSNa, and 108 µL of AP2-950 were combined and diluted to a total volume of 4.5 mL using DMEM, resulting in final concentrations of 200 nM siRNA, 60 µg mL⁻¹ MSNa, and 24 µg mL⁻¹ AP. For higher concentrations (400 and 600 nM), the amounts of siRNA, MSNa, and AP were proportionally increased while maintaining constant relative ratios and total volume. The N/P ratio was defined as the ratio of protonatable amine groups (N) contributed by both MSNa and APs to the phosphate groups (P) of siRNA. Due to the heterogeneous nature of the polymeric amines in APs, an exact molar N/P ratio could not be precisely determined. Therefore, an effective N/P ratio was adopted, and the formulation ratios were optimized experimentally to ensure efficient siRNA complexation and colloidal stability.

Physicochemical characterization

The hydrodynamic size and zeta potential of nanoparticles were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments, UK; model ZEN3690). For dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements, samples were prepared by dispersing nanoparticles (30 µL, 1 mg mL⁻¹ in HEPES buffer) in deionized (DI) water to a final volume of 1.5 mL, resulting in a final nanoparticle concentration of 20 µg mL⁻¹. Measurements were performed in triplicate. For zeta potential analysis, samples were prepared under the same dilution conditions as DLS measurements. The measurements were conducted in aqueous media consisting of HEPES buffer and DI water, with a pH of approximately 7.0 (± 0.2), and were performed in triplicate. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were obtained using a JEOL JEM-2100 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Japan). Samples were prepared by drop-casting 10 µL of nanoparticle dispersion onto Formvar/carbon-coated copper grids (FCF300-Cu-50, 300 mesh; Sigma-Aldrich), followed by drying overnight under ambient conditions. No staining was applied prior to imaging.

Colloidal stability analysis

Colloidal stability of MSNa, MSNa/siRNA, and MSNa/siRNA/AP2-950 was evaluated under physiologically relevant conditions. Each formulation was dispersed at a concentration of 0.5 mg mL⁻¹ in PBS (pH 7.4), PBS (pH 6.5), and PBS supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and incubated at 37 °C for up to 24 h. At predetermined time points, the hydrodynamic diameter of the nanoparticles was measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS). All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the average particle size and standard deviation were recorded to assess stability over time. Changes in particle size and size distribution were used as indicators of colloidal stability and aggregation behavior.

Agarose gel electrophoresis

The binding efficiency and enzymatic stability of PD-L1 siRNA were evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis. For complex formation, MSNa (30 µL, 1 mg mL⁻¹), PD-L1 siRNA (10 µL, 10 µM), and AP polymers (12 µL, 1 mg mL⁻¹) were mixed and incubated as described above. To assess resistance to enzymatic degradation, RNase A (Thermo Scientific) was diluted to 1 mg mL⁻¹ and 2 µL was added to each sample prior to electrophoresis. No additional quenching or siRNA release step was performed before gel analysis. All samples, including control siRNA, were prepared with the same siRNA amount (10 µL, 10 µM) prior to loading. Samples were loaded onto a 2% agarose gel containing a nucleic acid stain and electrophoresed in TAE buffer at 80 V for 40 min. siRNA migration patterns were analyzed to evaluate relative complex formation and nuclease stability.

MTT assay

CT26 murine colon carcinoma cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ for 24 h. Cells were then treated with MSNa/siRNA/AP2-950 complexes prepared as described above at final siRNA concentrations (200, 400, and 600 nM). Untreated cells were used as controls, and all treatments were performed in quintuplicate (n = 5). After 24 h of treatment, cell viability was evaluated using the MTT assay. MTT solution (0.5 mg mL⁻¹) was added to each well and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to untreated control cells.

Gene silencing assay

CT26 murine colon carcinoma cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h. To induce PD-L1 expression, the culture medium was replaced with interferon-γ (IFN-γ) containing medium, and the cells were further incubated for 12 h. MSNa/PD-L1 siRNA/AP2-950 complexes were prepared as described above and applied to cells at final siRNA concentrations of 200 or 600 nM. Experimental groups included untreated control cells, cells treated with IFN-γ alone, and cells treated with IFN-γ followed by complex treatment. Cells were incubated for 48 h prior to analysis. PD-L1 expression levels were quantified using a Mouse PD-L1 ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Fluorescence microscopy

For intracellular trafficking studies, MSNa/siRNA complexes were prepared using Cy5-labeled siRNA in place of PD-L1 siRNA, as described above. CT26 cells were incubated with MSNa/siRNA-Cy5 complexes for 4 h and stained with LysoTracker-FITC according to the manufacturer’s instructions to label lysosomes via accumulation in acidic compartments. The cells were then washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 4% formaldehyde. Fluorescence images were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope (CKX53, Olympus, Japan) with FITC (green, lysosomes) and Cy5 (red, siRNA) channels. All samples were stained using the same LysoTracker concentration and imaged under identical acquisition settings. Merged images were used to assess intracellular distribution and lysosomal colocalization of siRNA. It should be noted that LysoTracker staining was performed prior to fixation, and the resulting images are intended to provide qualitative insight into intracellular distribution rather than definitive or quantitative assessment of lysosomal localization.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of Amine Polymers (APs)

Amine Polymers (APs) were successfully synthesized via a one-step free-radical polymerization using AIBN as the initiator. DMAEMA units provide tertiary amine groups that can be protonated under physiological conditions, whereas PEGMA units contribute hydrophilic PEG side chains for steric stabilization. By varying the DMAEMA: PEGMA feed ratios (2:1, 1:1, and 0.5:1), three copolymers (AP1, AP2, and AP3) were obtained to enable systematic modulation of cationic charge density and PEG content. The chemical structures of AP1–AP3 were confirmed by ^1H NMR spectroscopy in DMSO-d₆ (Fig. 2). Characteristic resonances corresponding to PEGMA (δ ≈ 3.78–3.59 ppm, –OCH₂CH₂–; δ ≈ 3.38 ppm, –O–CH₃) and DMAEMA (δ ≈ 3.9–4.1 ppm, –CO₂–CH₂–; δ ≈ 2.0–2.23 ppm, N–CH₃) were clearly observed, with broad signal envelopes consistent with polymer formation. Notably, the vinyl proton peaks of the methacrylate monomers (5.3–6.2 ppm) disappeared completely in all three spectra, indicating near-quantitative consumption of both monomers. Quantitative NMR analysis revealed conversion efficiencies > 96% for every AP (Table 1), confirming highly efficient copolymerization under these simple radical conditions.

AP1–AP3 exhibited weight-average molecular weights (Mw) of approximately 6.7–9.1 kDa and number-average molecular weights (Mn) of 5.7–7.2 kDa (Table 1). Although absolute values may be slightly biased by the polystyrene calibration, the consistently low dispersity across AP1–AP3 demonstrates reproducible synthesis and comparable chain growth for all compositions. Overall, these data confirm that the free-radical route yields well-defined poly(DMAEMA-co-PEGMA) amine polymers with high conversion, tunable monomer composition, and uniform molecular weight distributions suitable for subsequent MSN coating and siRNA delivery studies.

Physicochemical characterization of AP Coated MSN/siRNA complexes

The hydrodynamic size and surface charge of the MSN-based nanocarriers at each modification step were evaluated by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential measurements (Fig. 3). Pristine MSNs exhibited an average hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 110 nm and a positive surface potential, consistent with the presence of surface silanol groups under the measurement conditions. After amine functionalization with APTES, the particle size remained within a comparable range, while the zeta potential shifted toward a more positive value, confirming successful introduction of surface amine groups. Upon siRNA loading onto MSNa (amine-functionalized MSNs prepared via APTES modification), a pronounced change in surface charge was observed. The zeta potential of MSNa/siRNA became strongly negative (approximately − 13 mV), reflecting effective electrostatic complexation between the cationic amine groups and the anionic phosphate backbone of siRNA. This charge reversal provides indirect evidence of siRNA adsorption on the particle surface. Concomitantly, the hydrodynamic diameter increased modestly, suggesting the formation of a siRNA layer without inducing significant aggregation. Subsequent coating with APs resulted in a further increase in particle size to the range of ~ 180–220 nm, depending on AP composition and PEG side-chain length. This size enlargement is attributed to the formation of a polymer corona surrounding the MSNa/siRNA core, consistent with successful AP deposition. Notably, all AP-coated formulations maintained hydrodynamic diameters below 250 nm, a size regime generally considered favorable for cellular uptake and endocytic internalization. The surface charge of MSNa/siRNA/AP complexes was strongly influenced by both the DMAEMA: PEGMA ratio and the PEG molecular weight of the Aps. For APx-450 formulations, increasing DMAEMA content led to a gradual shift of the zeta potential from negative to near-neutral or slightly positive values. In particular, AP1 (DMAEMA: PEGMA = 2:1) restored a moderately positive surface charge, indicating dominant cationic character and effective charge compensation of siRNA. In contrast, AP2 and AP3 produced near-neutral or mildly negative zeta potentials, reflecting increased PEG shielding and reduced surface charge density. A similar but more pronounced shielding effect was observed for APx-950 formulations. Regardless of DMAEMA content, APx-950 coatings yielded zeta potentials closer to neutrality compared with their APx-450 counterparts. This trend suggests that longer PEG chains generate a thicker hydrated layer, which partially masks the underlying cationic domains and attenuates the measured surface potential. Importantly, despite reduced apparent surface charge, these formulations remained colloidally stable, underscoring the dominant role of PEG in steric stabilization. Collectively, these results demonstrate that AP coating enables precise and independent tuning of particle size and surface charge through simple compositional control. The systematic modulation of zeta potential without inducing excessive particle growth highlights a key advantage of the poly(DMAEMA-co-PEGMA) strategy over conventional PEGylation approaches, where PEG conjugation often irreversibly suppresses surface charge. Here, the balance between electrostatic interaction (DMAEMA content) and steric shielding (PEGMA content and PEG length) can be rationally optimized to achieve stable, siRNA-loaded MSNs with physicochemical properties favorable for gene delivery.

The colloidal stability of MSNa, MSNa/siRNA, and MSNa/siRNA/AP2-950 was evaluated under physiologically relevant conditions. The nanoparticles were incubated in PBS, PBS (pH 6.5), and PBS containing 10% FBS for up to 24 h, and changes in hydrodynamic size were monitored by dynamic light scattering (DLS). As shown in Figure S2, all formulations maintained relatively stable particle sizes over the incubation period without significant aggregation, indicating good colloidal stability across different media. Notably, MSNa/siRNA/AP2-950 exhibited reduced size variation and narrower error bars compared to MSNa/siRNA, suggesting improved dispersion stability. This enhanced stability is attributed to the PEGMA segments in AP2-950, which provide steric stabilization and reduce interparticle interactions, particularly in protein-containing environments. These results support the role of PEGylation in improving colloidal stability and maintaining nanoparticle integrity under biologically relevant conditions.

Evaluation of siRNA complexation in AP-coated MSN systems

The loading of siRNA onto amine-functionalized MSNs and its subsequent stabilization by APs coating were evaluated using agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig. 4). Free siRNA (Control) migrated toward the anode and appeared as a distinct band at the bottom of the gel, consistent with its small size and strong negative charge. This result confirms the integrity and electrophoretic mobility of naked siRNA under the applied conditions. In contrast, MSNa/siRNA complexes exhibited partial retardation. A fraction of siRNA signal remained near the loading well, indicating successful electrostatic association with the positively charged MSNa surface. Simultaneously, a residual siRNA band was observed migrating toward the bottom of the gel, suggesting that a portion of the bound siRNA was weakly associated and could be dissociated under the applied electric field. This behavior is consistent with surface adsorption–dominated loading, where electrostatic interactions are sufficient for siRNA attachment but not strong enough to fully prevent electrophoretic migration. Upon coating MSNa/siRNA with APs, the electrophoretic behavior of siRNA changed markedly depending on the DMAEMA: PEGMA composition and PEG chain length. For AP1 (DMAEMA: PEGMA = 2:1) with PEG450, siRNA bands were predominantly retained near the loading wells, with minimal migration toward the bottom of the gel. This pronounced retardation indicates strong electrostatic interactions between siRNA and the DMAEMA-rich AP coating, effectively immobilizing siRNA within the MSN/AP complex. The strong cationic character of AP1 likely overcompensates the negative charge of siRNA, resulting in net positive or near-neutral complexes that are resistant to electrophoretic transport. However, such excessive electrostatic interaction may not be advantageous for efficient siRNA delivery, as overly strong binding can hinder intracellular release and increase nonspecific interactions with negatively charged cell membranes, potentially leading to aggregation and reduced biocompatibility. In contrast, AP3 (DMAEMA: PEGMA = 0.5:1) exhibited incomplete retardation, as evidenced by the presence of distinct siRNA bands migrating toward the bottom of the gel. This behavior indicates insufficient electrostatic stabilization due to the lower DMAEMA content and higher PEG fraction, resulting in weaker siRNA binding and partial release under the applied electric field. Interestingly, AP2 (DMAEMA: PEGMA = 1:1) displayed the most balanced retardation profile. For APx-950 formulations, siRNA was largely retained near the wells with minimal free siRNA migration, while avoiding the overly strong immobilization observed for AP1. This intermediate behavior suggests an optimal balance between electrostatic interaction and PEG-mediated shielding, allowing effective siRNA complexation without excessive charge domination. A similar trend was observed for the APx-950 formulations, although the overall retardation was slightly reduced compared to their APx-450 counterparts. The longer PEG chains in APx-950 likely introduce a thicker hydrated layer that partially screens electrostatic interactions, attenuating siRNA binding strength. Nevertheless, AP2-950 maintained effective siRNA retention, reinforcing the notion that both DMAEMA fraction and PEG chain length cooperatively govern siRNA stabilization. Collectively, these electrophoretic results demonstrate that siRNA loading and retention on MSNa can be finely tuned by the composition of the AP coating. While high DMAEMA content ensures strong siRNA immobilization, excessive cationic character may limit release and downstream bioavailability, whereas insufficient cationic content (e.g., AP3) leads to incomplete siRNA complexation. Among the tested formulations, AP2-950 exhibited the most favorable balance, achieving effective siRNA retention with controlled electrostatic interaction, thereby identifying it as an optimal candidate for subsequent cellular delivery and gene-silencing studies. Although an exact N/P ratio could not be precisely determined, the complete or near-complete retardation of siRNA migration observed in the gel analysis confirms effective electrostatic complexation under the selected conditions, highlighting the importance of balancing electrostatic interaction and PEG-mediated shielding. While further optimization of component ratios could potentially improve complexation, the present study focuses on demonstrating a composition-tunable PEGylation strategy rather than exhaustive formulation optimization. These findings are in line with previous reports highlighting the critical role of electrostatic interactions and polymer composition in MSN-based siRNA delivery systems. To further contextualize the enhanced siRNA loading capacity observed in this system, it is important to consider both the formulation conditions and the structural features of the carrier. In the present study, siRNA was first electrostatically adsorbed onto amine-functionalized MSNs (MSNᵃ), followed by secondary stabilization with poly(DMAEMA-co-PEGMA) (AP) copolymers. This two-step assembly provides a cooperative interaction mechanism, where MSNᵃ contributes initial electrostatic binding, while the cationic DMAEMA segments in AP further reinforce siRNA condensation and surface retention. Compared with previously reported MSN-based siRNA delivery systems, where siRNA loading is often limited by the negatively charged silica surface or insufficient cationic density, the incorporation of amine groups and additional cationic polymer layers significantly improves loading efficiency [38]. In particular, studies using PEI- or PDMAEMA-modified MSNs have demonstrated that increased cationic charge enhances siRNA adsorption through strong electrostatic interactions, although often at the expense of cytotoxicity [39]. In contrast, the present system achieves efficient siRNA complexation while maintaining cytocompatibility by balancing cationic DMAEMA content with PEG-mediated steric stabilization. Moreover, the tunable composition of the AP copolymers enables precise control over the charge–shielding balance, which is a key factor governing siRNA loading and retention. The observed complete or near-complete retardation of siRNA migration in agarose gel electrophoresis further supports the effective complexation under the selected formulation conditions. These results are consistent with previous reports emphasizing that both electrostatic interaction strength and polymer architecture play critical roles in determining siRNA loading efficiency in MSN-based delivery systems [40].

Protection of siRNA from RNase degradation by AP-coated MSNs

The stability of siRNA against enzymatic degradation was evaluated by RNase treatment of AP-coated MSN/siRNA complexes, followed by agarose gel electrophoresis and semi-quantitative band analysis (Fig. 5). It should be noted that this assay primarily reflects the relative protection of siRNA associated with the nanocarrier rather than absolute quantification of intact siRNA, as no release step was performed prior to electrophoresis. Free siRNA (Control + RNase) was almost completely degraded, as evidenced by the disappearance of the siRNA band, confirming the strong nuclease activity under the applied conditions. In contrast, MSNa/siRNA complexes retained a detectable siRNA signal after RNase exposure, indicating that adsorption onto the amine-functionalized MSN surface provides partial protection against enzymatic degradation. Upon coating with APs, the resistance of siRNA to RNase digestion was further enhanced, with clear dependence on both copolymer composition and PEG chain length. For AP2-coated complexes (DMAEMA: PEGMA = 1:1), substantial siRNA retention was observed after RNase treatment for both AP2-450 and AP2-950 formulations. Semi-quantitative analysis revealed that MSNa/siRNA/AP2-950 preserved the highest relative siRNA amount, approaching ~ 90–95% of the untreated control, whereas AP2-450 exhibited moderately lower protection (~ 75–80%). These results suggest that the intermediate cationic content of AP2 provides sufficient electrostatic stabilization of siRNA, while the longer PEG chains in AP2-950 contribute additional steric shielding that limits RNase access. Although formulations with higher cationic charge density, such as AP1, may provide stronger physical sequestration of siRNA against enzymatic degradation, excessively stable complexation, while beneficial for protection, may compromise intracellular release efficiency and limit the effective bioavailability of siRNA for gene silencing. Therefore, this study focused on formulations that achieve an optimal balance between protection and release efficiency. In contrast, AP3-coated complexes (DMAEMA: PEGMA = 0.5:1) displayed reduced resistance to RNase degradation. Although AP3 provided some level of protection compared to free siRNA, the relative siRNA amounts remained consistently lower (~ 60–70%) for both AP3-450 and AP3-950 formulations. This diminished protection is attributed to the lower DMAEMA content of AP3, resulting in weaker electrostatic interactions with siRNA and less effective immobilization within the MSN/AP matrix. The increased PEG fraction in AP3, while beneficial for hydration and colloidal stability, appears insufficient to compensate for the reduced cationic binding strength required to prevent enzymatic attack. Comparison between PEG chain lengths further highlights the cooperative role of electrostatic and steric effects. Across both AP2 and AP3 series, APx-950 formulations generally outperformed their APx-450 counterparts in RNase resistance, indicating that longer PEG chains form a thicker hydrated corona that sterically hinders RNase diffusion toward the siRNA. However, this protective effect was most pronounced only when adequate cationic stabilization was present, as exemplified by AP2-950. In PEG-rich but charge-deficient AP3 systems, PEG length alone could not fully prevent siRNA degradation. Collectively, these results demonstrate that effective protection of siRNA in RNase-rich environments requires a balanced interplay between electrostatic binding and PEG-mediated steric shielding. The association of siRNA with the MSN-based carrier is likely governed primarily by electrostatic interactions between the negatively charged phosphate backbone of siRNA and the positively charged amine-functionalized MSN surface. In this configuration, siRNA is first adsorbed onto the MSN surface, after which the AP copolymer coating forms an additional stabilizing layer that helps retain the associated siRNA and protects it from enzymatic degradation. Rather than extracting siRNA from the MSN surface, the AP coating is expected to interact with both the nanoparticle surface and the adsorbed siRNA, resulting in a stabilized ternary assembly. Following cellular internalization, the release of siRNA may occur through gradual weakening of these electrostatic interactions within the intracellular environment. Endosomal acidification, competitive binding with intracellular polyanions, and polymer rearrangement could collectively facilitate the dissociation of siRNA from the carrier, enabling its availability for gene silencing in the cytoplasm. Among the tested formulations, MSNa/siRNA/AP2-950 achieved the most favorable protection profile, underscoring the importance of optimizing both copolymer composition and PEG chain length. This enhanced nuclease stability suggests that AP2-950-coated MSNs are well suited for subsequent cellular delivery and gene-silencing applications, where resistance to enzymatic degradation is critical. Although direct measurement of siRNA release kinetics was not conducted in this study, multiple experimental observations collectively support effective intracellular release of siRNA from the AP-coated MSN system. Agarose gel electrophoresis results (Fig. 4) confirm stable complex formation, while RNase protection assays (Fig. 5) demonstrate that siRNA remains protected yet without complete immobilization within the complexes. Importantly, fluorescence imaging (Fig. 7) reveals partial escape of siRNA from lysosomal compartments into the cytoplasm, indicating successful intracellular trafficking beyond endosomal confinement. Furthermore, significant PD-L1 gene silencing (Fig. 8) provides direct functional evidence that siRNA is released in an active form capable of engaging the RNA interference machinery. These findings indicate that the electrostatic interactions between siRNA and the AP coating are sufficiently stable for protection, yet dynamic enough to enable intracellular release, likely facilitated by endosomal acidification and competitive interactions with intracellular biomolecules.

Morphological characterization of AP-coated MSN/siRNA complexes

The morphological characteristics of amine-functionalized mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNa) and AP-coated MSNa/siRNA complexes were examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 6). Pristine MSNa exhibited a well-defined spherical morphology with smooth and uniform surfaces, and the particles appeared highly monodisperse. The observed particle sizes were consistent with those obtained from DLS measurements, indicating that amine functionalization did not compromise the structural integrity of the mesoporous silica framework. In contrast, MSNa/siRNA/AP2-950 complexes retained their overall spherical morphology but exhibited noticeable changes in surface texture. Compared to MSNa, the AP2-950-coated particles showed a slightly roughened and less sharply defined outer boundary, suggesting the presence of a polymer layer on the particle surface. This morphological change is consistent with successful coating of the MSNs with AP2. The difference between the particle size observed by TEM and the larger hydrodynamic diameter measured by DLS is likely attributable to the distinct measurement environments, reflecting the hydrated polymer conformation under solution conditions. To further validate the morphological changes induced by polymer coating, additional TEM images of pristine MSNs, MSNᵃ/siRNA, and MSNᵃ/siRNA/AP complexes with different AP compositions (AP1 and AP3) were obtained and are provided in the Supplementary Information (Figure S3). The pristine MSNs and MSNᵃ/siRNA samples exhibited smooth surfaces with well-defined morphology, whereas AP-coated nanoparticles displayed relatively roughened surfaces, indicating successful polymer adsorption. Although low-magnification TEM images suggested partial aggregation, likely due to sample preparation limitations (e.g., absence of plasma treatment), the overall particle morphology and size distribution remained consistent across different formulations, supporting the structural integrity and reasonable colloidal stability of the system.

Intracellular distribution of AP-coated MSN/siRNA complexes

Intracellular distribution of siRNA was evaluated using Cy5-labeled siRNA loaded into MSNa/siRNA/AP2-950 complexes (Fig. 7). Cells were incubated with the complexes for 4 h, followed by staining with LysoTracker to visualize acidic endo/lysosomal compartments. Fluorescence microscopy images revealed strong intracellular red fluorescence corresponding to siRNA-Cy5, indicating efficient cellular internalization of the AP2-950-coated complexes. LysoTracker staining showed discrete green fluorescent puncta associated with lysosomal compartments. In the merged images, partial overlap between siRNA-Cy5 and LysoTracker signals was observed; however, a substantial fraction of the red fluorescence appeared spatially separated from the lysosomal signal. Notably, diffuse red fluorescence was detected throughout the cytoplasmic region, suggesting that a significant portion of siRNA was no longer confined to lysosomal compartments after 4 h of incubation. The observed intracellular distribution indicates that MSNa/siRNA/AP2-950 complexes are capable of escaping, at least in part, from endo/lysosomal compartments and releasing siRNA into the cytosol. Although only a single time point (4 h) was evaluated in this study, previous reports have shown that intracellular siRNA trafficking typically involves initial endosomal localization within the first few hours (∼2–3 h), followed by progressive cytosolic release at later time points. Therefore, the 4 h time point represents a transitional stage at which partial endosomal escape can be observed. In this context, the reduced colocalization with lysosomal signals and the presence of diffuse cytoplasmic fluorescence support the occurrence of endosomal escape in our system [41]. This behavior is consistent with the physicochemical properties of AP2-950, which combines sufficient cationic character for membrane interaction with PEG-mediated stabilization. The balanced DMAEMA: PEGMA composition may facilitate disruption of endosomal membranes or promote vesicular destabilization, thereby enhancing cytosolic availability of siRNA. Although quantitative colocalization analysis (e.g., Mander’s or Pearson’s coefficients) was not performed in this study, the clear reduction in lysosomal colocalization and the presence of cytoplasmic siRNA signal at 4 h post-treatment qualitatively support effective intracellular trafficking beyond simple endosomal sequestration. These results correlate well with the enhanced siRNA stability and optimal surface charge characteristics observed for AP2-950 in earlier physicochemical and RNase protection studies. Overall, the cellular uptake results suggest that AP2-950-coated MSNs not only promote efficient internalization but also enable favorable intracellular distribution of siRNA, providing a mechanistic basis for the subsequent gene-silencing efficacy observed in PD-L1 knockdown experiments.

Cytocompatibility and PD-L1 gene silencing of MSNa/siRNA/AP2-950 complexes

The cytocompatibility of MSNa/siRNA/AP2-950 complexes was evaluated using an MTT assay by increasing the siRNA dose (and correspondingly the MSN amount) to 200, 400, and 600 nM (Fig. 8A). Across all tested concentrations, cell viability remained above 90% and showed no statistically significant difference compared to untreated control cells. These results indicate that AP2-950-coated MSNs exhibit minimal cytotoxicity over the tested concentration range, even when the particle dose was increased. The favorable cytocompatibility is consistent with the balanced DMAEMA: PEGMA composition of AP2-950, in which PEG segments mitigate the potential membrane-disruptive effects associated with cationic polymers. The gene-silencing efficacy of PD-L1 siRNA delivered by MSNa/siRNA/AP2-950 was assessed in CT26 cells using a PD-L1 siRNA assay kit (Fig. 8B). To induce PD-L1 expression, cells were stimulated with interferon-γ (IFN-γ), resulting in a marked increase in PD-L1-associated absorbance compared to untreated controls. Treatment with MSNa/siRNA/AP2-950 at 200 nM significantly reduced PD-L1 expression relative to the IFN-γ-treated control, demonstrating effective functional delivery of PD-L1 siRNA. Interestingly, increasing the siRNA concentration to 600 nM did not lead to a further reduction in PD-L1 expression compared to the 200 nM treatment. This apparent plateau in gene-silencing efficacy suggests that PD-L1 knockdown was not strictly dose-dependent under the applied experimental conditions. This behavior is consistent with previous reports that nanoparticle-based and PEGylated delivery systems often require relatively higher extracellular siRNA concentrations to compensate for limited cellular uptake efficiency, while maintaining low cytotoxicity. One possible explanation is that the PEG-containing AP2-950 coating, while beneficial for stability and cytocompatibility, may partially limit cellular uptake at higher particle concentrations within the fixed incubation period (48 h). As a result, increasing the extracellular dose may not proportionally increase the intracellular siRNA level available for RNA interference. This plateau behavior indicates that intracellular delivery efficiency, rather than extracellular dose alone, is the primary limiting factor under the present conditions. The AP2-950 system may exhibit improved performance in more complex biological environments, where PEGylation can enhance circulation stability and tumor accumulation. These properties may contribute to sustained siRNA availability and effective gene silencing beyond simple cellular uptake conditions. Although a scrambled (non-targeting) siRNA control was not included in this study, the observed PD-L1 knockdown is therefore attributed to the sequence-specific activity of PD-L1-targeting siRNA, as the carrier system itself does not possess intrinsic gene regulatory effects. Inclusion of a scrambled siRNA control would further strengthen the study and will be considered in future work. While ELISA-based quantification provides direct measurement of protein-level changes, additional validation at the mRNA level (e.g., RT-qPCR) or by Western blot analysis could further strengthen the findings and will be considered in future studies.

Conclusion

We report a composition-tunable siRNA delivery platform based on mesoporous silica nanoparticles coated with APs. The APs were synthesized via one-step free-radical copolymerization, enabling precise control over cationic charge density and PEG content without complex conjugation or post-functionalization. Systematic variation of the DMAEMA: PEGMA ratio and PEG molecular weight allowed fine tuning of surface properties critical for siRNA delivery. Among the tested formulations, AP2-950 exhibited the most balanced performance, providing efficient siRNA binding, enhanced resistance to RNase degradation, favorable surface charge modulation, and excellent cytocompatibility. AP2-950-coated MSNs demonstrated effective cellular uptake and intracellular redistribution of siRNA, leading to significant PD-L1 gene silencing in IFN-γ-stimulated CT26 cells at relatively low siRNA concentrations. The observed plateau in gene-silencing efficacy at higher doses highlights that intracellular trafficking efficiency, rather than administered dose alone, governs in vitro performance. Importantly, the PEG-rich architecture of AP2-950 may offer potential advantages in more complex biological systems, which could be beneficial for future in vivo evaluation.
