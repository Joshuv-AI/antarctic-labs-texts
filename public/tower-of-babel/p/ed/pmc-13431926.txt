Five‐Year Outcomes and Disease Trajectories in Moderate‐to‐Severe Ulcerative Colitis: A Korean Multicenter Inception Cohort
Authors: Byong Duk Ye, Hyo Jong Kim, Dong Il Park, Young Sook Park, Kang‐Moon Lee, Jong Pil Im, Sung‐Ae Jung, Yoon Tae Jeen, Jae Myung Cha, Jae Hee Cheon, Sung Noh Hong, Youngdoe Kim, Jong Min Choi, YoungJa Lee, Chang Kyun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimThe incidence of ulcerative colitis (UC) has been increasing in Asia. Comprehensive data on the long‐term course of moderate‐to‐severe UC remain scarce. This study investigated the clinical outcomes and factors associated with a disabling disease course in Korean patients with moderate‐to‐severe UC.MethodsThe moderate‐to‐severe ulcerative colitis in Korea study was a prospective, multicenter, hospital‐based inception cohort study that enrolled patients with newly diagnosed moderate‐to‐severe UC between August 2014 and February 2017. Clinical outcomes and factors associated with a disabling disease course were evaluated over 5 years.ResultsAmong 353 patients included, the median follow‐up duration was 4.9 (interquartile range, 2.1–5.0) years, and 214 patients (60.6%) completed the 5‐year follow‐up. At 5 years, the cumulative rates of outcomes were clinical relapses, 62.9%; proximal disease extension, 29.5%; and UC‐related hospitalization, 22.4%. Colectomy was required in two patients (0.6%), without mortality. A disabling disease course occurred in 171 patients (48.4%), with cumulative probabilities of 41.0%, 51.1%, and 53.9% at 12, 36, and 60 months, respectively. Elevated baseline white blood cell count was independently associated with a disabling disease course (adjusted hazard ratio: 1.06; 95% confidence interval: 1.00–1.12; p = 0.035). Five distinct partial Mayo score–based disease trajectory clusters were identified, demonstrating clear prognostic separation.ConclusionsWhile Korean patients with moderate‐to‐severe UC showed a benign structural disease course with minimal colectomy rates, approximately half experienced a disabling disease course. The identified disease trajectories provide a novel framework for risk stratification and personalized management.Keywords: Asian people, cluster analysis, cohort studies, prognosis, ulcerative colitis

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) characterized by alternating periods of relapse and remission, often leading to cumulative bowel damage and impaired quality of life [1]. In recent decades, the incidence and prevalence of UC have risen markedly in Korea—the age‐ and sex‐adjusted annual incidence of UC has increased from 0.33 per 100 000 in 1986 to 6.58 per 100 000 in 2015 [2], while prevalence more than doubled between 2010 (31.6/100000) and 2018 (65.5/100000) [3]. UC is therefore a condition of increasing clinical relevance in Korea with a need to clarify its contemporary disease behavior and long‐term prognosis.

Accumulating evidence suggests that UC in Asian patients may follow distinct clinical patterns compared with Western patients, reflecting underlying genetic and environmental heterogeneity [4]. However, data describing the natural history of UC in Asian patients remain limited. Previous studies, including the Asia‐Pacific Crohn's and Colitis Epidemiology Study (ACCESS) and a population‐based study from the Songpa‐Kangdong district of Korea, have reported outcomes in newly diagnosed UC patients. However, these cohorts encompassed a wide range of disease severities, limiting insights into the natural history of moderate‐to‐severe UC [5, 6]. Moreover, the disease trajectory of Asian patients with UC in the era of biologics and small molecules remains insufficiently defined.

To address this knowledge gap, the moderate‐to‐severe ulcerative colitis in Korea (MOSAIK) study, a prospective, multicenter, inception cohort study enrolling patients with newly‐diagnosed moderate‐to‐severe UC in Korea, was launched [7]. Early outcomes from 354 patients were previously reported, showing that at 1 year, 58.2% of patients achieved remission or mild endoscopic activity, with no colectomy or mortality observed [8]. Despite favorable early outcomes, patients with moderate‐to‐severe UC remain at risk for disabling disease over time, underscoring the need for long‐term outcome studies [9].

The present study provides a comprehensive evaluation of long‐term clinical outcomes and risk factors for a disabling disease course over the 5‐year follow‐up in MOSAIK, with the aim of delineating the disease trajectory and reporting the final outcomes of this inception cohort.

Methods

Study Design and Population

Patients newly diagnosed with moderate‐to‐severe UC (within 4 weeks of diagnosis) were recruited from 30 academic teaching hospitals in Korea between August 2014 and February 2017. The inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as the case identification and ascertainment process, were described previously [7, 10]. Briefly, diagnosis of UC was based on a combination of clinical, endoscopic, radiological, and histological criteria; symptoms consistent with UC were required to last for more than 4 weeks [11, 12]. The date of diagnosis was defined as the date of the first endoscopy (colonoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy) showing findings consistent with UC. Moderate‐to‐severe UC was defined by a full Mayo score (FMS) of 6–12 points (6–10 points indicating moderate disease and ≥ 11 indicating severe disease) and a Mayo endoscopic subscore of ≥ 2 points on colonoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy [11, 13]. Disease extent was classified as proctitis (E1), left‐sided colitis (E2), and extensive colitis (E3), according to the Montreal classification [14]. Proximal disease extension was defined as endoscopic and histological involvement beyond the baseline disease extent (E1 or E2) [6].

Baseline demographic and clinical data, including patient characteristics, lifestyle factors, comorbidities, family history, extraintestinal manifestations, and disease‐related assessments, were systematically collected, as described in our previous report [8]. The study was registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02229344).

Clinical Management and Treatment Protocol

Patients were treated according to the standard guidelines for moderate‐to‐severe UC in Korea, which are largely aligned with Western guidelines and have been described previously [6, 7, 8, 15]. Systemic corticosteroids were administered to patients with moderate‐to‐severe UC, and their doses were tapered off over 2–3 months. Corticosteroids included prednisolone and methylprednisolone, but excluded beclomethasone dipropionate or budesonide (not available in Korea). Thiopurines or advanced therapies (including biologics and small molecules) were administered to patients with corticosteroid‐dependent or corticosteroid‐refractory UC [15]. Intravenous systemic corticosteroids were used as first‐line treatment for hospitalized patients with acute severe UC. Advanced therapies or colectomy were considered as rescue therapies in patients unresponsive to optimal corticosteroid therapy. During the study period, advanced therapies were reimbursed by the national health insurance program only for patients with moderate‐to‐severe UC unresponsive to conventional treatment (5‐aminosalicylic acid [5‐ASA], corticosteroids, and thiopurines). Follow‐up intervals were determined by the treating physician based on patient condition.

Definition of Clinical Outcomes

Patients were followed at prespecified timepoints up to Week 260 (5 years). The following clinical outcomes were assessed annually: clinical response, clinical remission, clinical relapse, proximal disease extension, treatment escalation, UC‐related hospitalization, disabling disease course, colectomy, and mortality.

Clinical response was defined as a decrease in partial Mayo score (PMS) of ≥ 2 points and ≥ 30% from baseline PMS, together with either a decrease in the rectal bleeding subscore of ≥ 1 point or an absolute rectal bleeding subscore of 0 or 1 [16]. Clinical remission was defined as clinical improvement in PMS of ≤ 2 points and no individual subscore of > 1 point [17]. Clinical relapse was defined as an increase in PMS of ≥ 3 points from clinical response or remission [8]. Treatment escalation or colectomy could be considered relapse at the investigator's discretion [8]. Unscheduled visits were permitted when patients had symptom aggravation, and relapse was assessed during these visits [8].

Disabling disease course was defined as meeting at least one of the following conditions after baseline evaluation: (1) requirement of a new cycle of systemic corticosteroids within ≥ 7 days and ≤ 365 days from the previous corticosteroid use; (2) step‐up to thiopurines: no use of thiopurines at baseline but starting thiopurines at any subsequent visits; (3) step‐up to advanced therapies (biologics or small molecules), no use of advanced therapies at baseline but starting advanced therapies at any subsequent visits; (4) switching or swapping of advanced therapies; (5) hospitalization from UC flare for > 3 days, excluding admissions for disease evaluation or drug administration as well as those unrelated to UC; (6) colectomy from UC; and (7) death. Switching was defined as a change from one advanced therapy to another within the same therapeutic class, whereas swapping was defined as a change to an agent with a different mechanism of action. Acute severe UC was not defined as a separate outcome component; however, its clinical consequences, including hospitalization, treatment escalation, and colectomy, were captured within the predefined components of the disabling disease course.

For sensitivity analyses, alternative definitions were applied by excluding treatment‐related components: Definition A excluded Components (1)–(4) (new systemic corticosteroid cycle, step‐up to thiopurines, step‐up to advanced therapies, and switching/swapping of advanced therapies), and Definition B excluded Components (2)–(4) (step‐up to thiopurines, step‐up to advanced therapies, and switching/swapping of advanced therapies).

Statistical Analysis

Study outcomes were described using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables are summarized as means with standard deviations (SDs) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), as appropriate, and categorical variables are presented as frequencies with percentages in parentheses. The cumulative probabilities of clinical relapse, proximal disease extension, UC‐related hospitalizations, and a disabling disease course were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. A Cox proportional hazards model was used to evaluate associations between baseline factors and a disabling disease course.

Unsupervised cluster analysis using the K‐means algorithm was performed to identify disease trajectory patterns based on PMS measured at baseline and annually from Years 1 to 5. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the elbow method. To address missing values in post‐baseline PMS, nonparametric imputation was performed using a random forest algorithm, and the resulting imputed data were subsequently used for cluster analysis. All time‐to‐event analyses for clinical outcomes were conducted using observed data without imputation. The detailed distribution and missingness of PMS by timepoint are presented in Table S1. Disease trajectories for each cluster were visualized using box plots (from baseline to Year 5). All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, United States) and R (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 355 patients diagnosed with moderate‐to‐severe UC between August 2014 and February 2017 at 30 hospitals were enrolled; after excluding two patients whose diagnosis was revised from UC to Crohn's disease, 353 patients were included in the analysis (Figure S1). The median duration of follow‐up was 4.9 (IQR: 2.1–5.0) years, with 214 patients (60.6%) completing the 5‐year (260‐week) follow‐up. The availability of PMS data decreased over time, with 100% completeness at baseline and 57.8% at 5 years, reflecting expected attrition during long‐term follow‐up (Table S1). The mean age at UC diagnosis was 37.6 years (SD: 15.2), and 208 patients (58.9%) were men (Table 1). The median baseline FMS and PMS were 8 (IQR: 7–9) and 6 (IQR: 5–7), respectively. Disease extent at diagnosis showed E1 (10.5%, 37/353), E2 (45.9%, 162/353), and E3 (41.9%, 148/353). Extraintestinal manifestations were observed in 5.1% (18/353) of the patients. Initial treatments most frequently used were oral 5‐ASAs (92.4%), topical 5‐ASAs (53.0%), and systemic corticosteroids (49.3%).

Medical Therapies for Moderate‐to‐Severe UC During the 5‐Year Follow‐Up

The 5‐year cumulative exposure to medical therapies was as follows: oral 5‐ASAs (96.3%, 340/353), topical 5‐ASAs (70.8%, 250/353), systemic corticosteroids (65.7%, 232/353), immunomodulators (thiopurines or methotrexate) (38.5%, 136/353), and advanced therapies (biologics [20.1%, 71/353] and small molecules [1.7%, 6/353]). Biologics included infliximab (11.0%, 39/353), adalimumab (6.5%, 23/353), infliximab biosimilars (5.7%, 20/353), vedolizumab (5.4%, 19/353), golimumab (3.1%, 11/353), and ustekinumab (0.3%, 1/353). Thirty patients (8.5%) were treated with two or more advanced therapies. The cumulative rates of medication use over time, including oral and topical 5‐ASAs, systemic corticosteroids, immunomodulators (thiopurines or methotrexate), biologics, and tofacitinib, are shown in Figure 1. The medication usage Sankey diagram over time is presented in Figure S2. Given the complexity of longitudinal treatment patterns, treatment transitions—including continuation, initiation, discontinuation, and switching/swapping of therapies—were summarized descriptively using the Sankey diagram rather than as isolated discontinuation or treatment‐escalation rates. The low exposure to tofacitinib likely reflects the timing of cohort enrollment and follow‐up, which largely preceded the widespread clinical use and reimbursement of small‐molecule therapies in Korea. Methotrexate use was rare in this cohort and primarily reflects historical real‐world clinical practice rather than an evidence‐based treatment strategy for UC.

Main Outcomes of Moderate‐to‐Severe UC During the 5‐Year Follow‐Up

During study follow‐up, 341 patients (96.6%) achieved clinical response (median time: 1.0 month; IQR: 1.0–1.4) and 331 (93.8%) achieved clinical remission (median time: 1.2 months; IQR: 1.0–2.6). Of these, 189 (53.5%) patients experienced a first relapse (median 8.2 months; IQR: 4.0–17.2), with a 5‐year cumulative relapse rate of 62.9%.

Among 120 patients with E1 or E2 disease extent at diagnosis and a follow‐up evaluation, 16 (13.3%) showed at least one proximal disease extension (median time: 15.9 months; IQR, 11.5–40.3); the 5‐year cumulative extension rate was 29.5%. Overall, 68 patients (19.3%) were hospitalized (5‐year cumulative rate: 22.4%), with a median duration of 9 days (IQR: 5–14). Hospitalization frequency was once in 48 (13.6%), twice in 16 (4.5%), and ≥ 3 times in four (1.1%) patients. Colectomy was required for two (0.6%) patients due to uncontrolled disease activity (at 30.3 and 58.6 months). No malignancies or deaths were observed.

A total of 171 patients (48.4%) developed a disabling disease course. The cumulative probability rose steeply within the first year, reaching 41.0%, 51.1%, and 53.9% at 12, 36, and 60 months, respectively. The cumulative probabilities of each clinical outcome are presented in Table 2. Sensitivity analyses using stricter definitions excluding treatment‐related components showed that the 5‐year cumulative incidence of disabling disease course remained clinically meaningful (18.8%) even under the most stringent definition (Table S3).

Five‐Year Disease Trajectory Clusters

Unsupervised cluster analysis based on PMS at baseline and from Years 1 to 5 identified five distinct disease trajectory patterns (Figure 2). The baseline characteristics of the five clusters are shown in Table S2. Clusters 1 and 2, representing 67.1% of the patients, showed rapid initial improvement followed by long‐term maintenance. Cluster 3 (13.6%) maintained persistent moderate disease activity, whereas Cluster 4 (10.8%) showed delayed improvement and intermittent exacerbations. Cluster 5 (8.5%) demonstrated persistently elevated disease activity throughout follow‐up. Clusters 4 and 5 had a higher risk for major clinical outcomes, including clinical relapse (p < 0.001), proximal disease extension (p = 0.021), UC‐related hospitalization (p < 0.001), and disabling disease course (p < 0.001), compared with the other clusters (Figure 3A–D). The distinct disease trajectories were already distinguishable at the 1‐year timepoint and were highly predictive of long‐term clinical outcomes over 5 years, including the cumulative risk of developing a disabling disease course (p < 0.001). Notably, clusters associated with unfavorable outcomes, particularly Clusters 4 and 5, already showed higher PMS distributions at the 1‐year timepoint compared with other clusters. The distribution of PMS at the 1‐year timepoint across clusters is provided in Table S4.

Factors Associated With a Disabling Disease Course

In an analysis comparing baseline characteristics in patients with a disabling disease course (n = 171) and those without (n = 182), disease activity as measured by FMS and PMS, initial disease extent, baseline laboratory tests (white blood cell [WBC] count, serum albumin, and C‐reactive protein), and initial medications (systemic corticosteroids, immunomodulators, and biologics) showed differences between the two groups (Table S5). Factors with a p‐value < 0.1 in the univariate Cox proportional hazards model were included in the multivariable analysis (Table 3). Elevated baseline WBC count was independently associated with a disabling disease course (adjusted hazard ratio: 1.06; 95% confidence interval: 1.00–1.12; p = 0.035).

Discussion

This is the first prospective multicenter inception cohort study in Asia characterizing the comprehensive 5‐year disease course of newly‐diagnosed moderate‐to‐severe UC. Our findings reveal a striking dichotomy in the natural history of UC in Asian patients in the biologic era. Although the structural disease course appeared benign, characterized by an extremely low colectomy rate (0.6%) and no mortality, the clinical burden remained substantial, with approximately half of patients experiencing a disabling disease course. Furthermore, using longitudinal PMS data, we identified five distinct disease trajectories that offer a novel, noninvasive framework for early risk stratification.

Despite high disease activity at baseline, most patients showed a rapid and favorable response to initial induction therapy. The 1‐year cumulative rates of first clinical response and remission were 98.6% and 92.3%, respectively, with a median time to response of approximately 1 month. However, the long‐term burden was significant. The 5‐year cumulative exposure rates to systemic corticosteroids (65.7%), immunomodulators (38.5%), and advanced therapies (21.8%) in our cohort were notably higher than those reported in population‐based studies, such as the Epi‐IBD cohort (13% for advanced therapies) or other Asian cohorts [18]. This higher utilization likely reflects our specific inclusion of patients with moderate‐to‐severe disease, as well as the evolving treatment paradigm shifting toward earlier intervention with effective therapies to prevent disease progression. Differences in availability and access to advanced therapies in respective countries and regions should also be taken into account. Early initiation of biologics within the first year of diagnosis was infrequent across all trajectory clusters, reflecting the stringent national reimbursement restrictions during the study period. Consequently, our capacity to formally evaluate and compare the potential disease‐modifying impact of early biologic use on subsequent clinical trajectory patterns was limited. The 5‐year cumulative rate of UC‐related hospitalization was 22.4%. This was similar to the reported rate of 23% in the Epi‐IBD study, which enrolled all incident cases of UC [19]. However, as hospitalization rates could be influenced by multiple factors other than UC activity and severity (such as healthcare cost and accessibility), direct comparison of hospitalization rates between studies from different medical care systems requires caution [20].

Interestingly, although the initial disease extent was E2 or E3 in nearly 90% of cases, the 5‐year cumulative colectomy rate was notably low at 0.6%. This figure is lower than the 5‐year cumulative colectomy rates reported in the Songpa‐Kangdong population–based Korean cohort (1.9%) and a recent Chinese cohort (3.4%). While the Epi‐IBD cohort reported 10‐year crude colectomy rates of 4%–6%, comparing these rates across cohorts requires careful attention due to discrepancies in follow‐up durations and healthcare systems [6, 19, 21]. Although our findings might suggest a favorable genetic or environmental phenotype in Korean patients, this interpretation requires caution [6, 22]. The relatively brief 5‐year follow‐up period in our study may partly account for the low colectomy rate. Furthermore, cultural reluctance toward colectomy, including concerns about major organ resection and postoperative functional changes even after restorative proctocolectomy, may have further contributed to this finding [23]. Nevertheless, nearly half of the patients experienced a disabling disease course, underscoring that “colectomy‐free survival” or “survival with an intact colon” does not inherently equate to a favorable clinical outcome.

Although colectomy was rare, approximately half the study patients experienced a disabling disease course over 5 years. In the present study, a disabling disease course was defined by medication changes, hospitalization from UC flare, colectomy, or death to encompass the wide range of poor outcomes that significantly affect patients' quality of life. In Korea, access to advanced therapies during the study period was regulated by the national reimbursement system and generally required inadequate response or intolerance to conventional therapies. Therefore, treatment escalation may reflect both disease severity and healthcare system‐specific reimbursement criteria rather than physician preference alone. In addition, sensitivity analyses using alternative definitions excluding treatment‐related components demonstrated that the 5‐year cumulative incidence of disabling disease course remained clinically meaningful (18.8%), supporting the robustness of our findings independent of treatment escalation.

Our findings suggest that attention should be paid to patients diagnosed with moderate‐to‐severe UC to reduce the risk of unfavorable outcomes. Moreover, the association between elevated baseline WBC count and a disabling disease course, although a moderate effect size, indicates a need for intensive management of patients with high disease activity at baseline.

This divergence between structural preservation (very low colectomy rate) and high clinical burden (frequent disabling course) underscores the need for better risk stratification. Our identification of five disease trajectories using unsupervised clustering of PMS provides a useful framework for clinical risk stratification. PMS, comprising bowel frequency, rectal bleeding, and physician's global assessment, is a widely used measure of UC disease activity because of its noninvasive nature [24]. Unlike static baseline predictors, these trajectories capture the dynamic evolution of disease activity. Clusters 4 and 5 were strongly associated with poor long‐term outcomes, suggesting that PMS (a simple, noninvasive metric) can be a reliable surrogate for tracking disease course in daily practice [25]. However, it has been reported that almost half of patients with UC in symptomatic remission show endoscopic activity, supporting the need for frequent endoscopic evaluation [26]. Nevertheless, the clear prognostic separation indicates that the trajectory‐based approach allows for the early identification of high‐risk patients who may benefit from “top‐down” or accelerated step‐up strategies, moving beyond a one‐size‐fits‐all approach.

Importantly, clusters associated with poorer outcomes showed higher PMS distributions as early as the 1‐year timepoint, indicating that clinically relevant divergence emerges early in the disease course. However, we do not propose a simplified cutoff or prediction rule based on early PMS in the current study, as it was not designed for predictive model development or validation.

A major strength of this study is its design as a prospective multicenter inception cohort, allowing standardized assessment of baseline disease activity and minimizing heterogeneity in disease duration. The strict diagnostic criteria for moderate‐to‐severe UC and the use of a prespecified case report form also ensured data consistency. The involvement of 30 academic centers under a uniform national health insurance system further reduced regional variations in treatment access.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, recruitment from tertiary academic centers may have introduced a referral bias toward more severe cases. However, in the Korean healthcare system, patients with moderate‐to‐severe UC are almost exclusively referred to tertiary centers, likely mitigating this bias. Furthermore, clinical practice patterns are similar across institutions in Korea [27]. Second, almost 40% of patients did not complete the prespecified 5‐year visit. Although such attrition is common in long‐term real‐world observational studies [28], the pattern in our cohort largely reflects the Korean clinical pathway in which patients achieving stable remission are frequently transferred from tertiary centers to local clinics or discontinue scheduled research visits while continuing routine care. Therefore, our estimates of disabling disease may be conservative, yet they reflect the reality of managing complex cases in tertiary care. Ideally, linking our data with national health insurance claims data could capture the outcomes of patients transferred to primary clinics, which remains an area for future research. Third, given potential genotypic and phenotypic differences, our study results need to be validated in patients with UC from different Asian countries [5, 6, 20, 29]. In addition, external validation of the identified trajectory clusters using independent longitudinal datasets was not performed and remains an important future research need. Furthermore, fecal calprotectin was not routinely collected in this cohort, limiting the ability to incorporate objective biomarker‐based disease activity assessment. Finally, specific complications and medication‐specific adverse events were not systematically captured as separate variables, limiting analyses of complication incidence and drug‐specific safety profiles.

In conclusion, this prospective inception cohort study delineates the contemporary disease course of moderate‐to‐severe UC in Asia. We highlighted a clinically important divergence—outcomes were structurally benign, with very low colectomy rates, yet clinically burdensome with high rates of disabling disease. The novel disease trajectories identified offer a valuable framework for risk stratification, guiding clinicians toward more personalized and timely interventions to improve long‐term quality of life.
