Localized unilateral wedge-shaped defect of a single mandibular denture: a case report
Authors: Jingyu Qi, Jiaqiao Chen, Yongwu Wang
Published: 2026

Background

Wedge-shaped defects are a common type of non-carious cervical lesion of dental hard tissues, with a reported prevalence of approximately 20–35% in the general population, particularly among middle-aged and older adults. Their etiology is generally considered multifactorial and has been associated with toothbrushing abrasion, acidic erosion, structural vulnerability of the cervical tooth region, and sustained occlusal stress resulting from functional or parafunctional loading [1–5]. Improper oral hygiene practices, demineralization processes, and excessive stress concentration at the cervical region have all been implicated in the development of these lesions. However, these mechanisms primarily explain wedge-shaped defects occurring in natural teeth.

Tooth loss may impair oral function, esthetics and phonation, and is commonly managed with dental prostheses. Single mandibular dentures (SMDs) remain an economical and widely used treatment option for edentulous or partially edentulous patients. Nevertheless, SMDs are associated with several clinical complications, including loss of retention, soft tissue irritation or ulceration, loss of artificial teeth, and fracture of denture base, which together account for approximately 70–80% of SMD-related clinical complications [6, 7].

In addition to biological and occlusal factors, both prosthesis-related and patient-related variables may contribute to the development of surface defects in removable dentures. Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA), the most commonly used denture base material, eis susceptible to surface degradation during long-term intraoral service. Mechanical cleaning procedures, particularly toothbrushing with abrasive dentifrices, may increase surface roughness and accelerate material wear. Patient-related factors, including excessive brushing force, improper cleaning techniques, and inadequate denture maintenance, may further exacerbate such deterioration. Furthermore, unfavorable storage conditions may affect the physical stability and long-term mechanical properties of acrylic denture materials.

Taken together, denture material properties, maintenance practices, and patient behavioral habits should all be considered as potentially relevant contributors when interpreting surface alterations in removable prostheses. However, these wear-related mechanisms would be expected to affect broader denture surfaces rather than produce a localized lesion. Wedge-shaped defects remain rarely reported in SMDs, particularly when presenting in a unilateral pattern. The present report describes an unusual case of a localized unilateral wedge-shaped defect in an SMD and discusses its possible etiological mechanisms.

Case presentation

A 58-year-old female patient presented to our hospital with a chief complaint of a several-day history of aching discomfort in the left maxillary posterior teeth. The patient reported experiencing sensitivity to hot and cold stimuli in the same region. A clinical examination was performed, and wedge-shaped defects were identified in the left maxillary first and second molars. No wedge-shaped defects were observed in the remaining natural teeth. We carefully inquired about the patient’s lifestyle habits. The patient did not report a preference for acidic foods or symptoms of teeth grinding. The patient reported occasional improper tooth-brushing habits. The patient reported no history of systemic diseases and no long-term medication use. In addition, no relevant medical records indicating systemic comorbidities were identified in our institutional records for this patient. However, we acknowledge that no further comprehensive systemic examinations or external medical record verification were performed as part of this case report. Therefore, the assessment of the patient’s general medical condition was primarily based on patient-reported history and available institutional information, which represents a limitation of this study.

Furthermore, examination of the corresponding mandibular dental arch revealed an edentulous ridge restored with a removable complete denture. However, this denture exhibited marked wear of the artificial teeth and denture base material, accompanied by poor retention and bilateral instability. A distinct, long wedge-shaped defect was identified on the denture base. This defect, approximately 3 mm in depth with sharp margins, was located at the contact area between the denture border and the interdental papillae in the region corresponding to teeth 32–36. The patient denied any history of bruxism or external traumatic injury to the denture but reported a long-standing habit of unilateral mastication, primarily on the left side (Figs. 1 and 2).

The patient had worn the prosthesis for approximately 5 years. The denture was fabricated from conventional heat-polymerized polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) resin without reinforcement. Regarding daily oral hygiene practices, the patient reported predominantly using a mechanical cleaning regimen, consisting of daily brushing of the denture with a toothbrush and toothpaste. The brushing technique was non-standardized, with limited attention to brush orientation or controlled cleaning motion. No routine use of chemical denture-cleansing agents was reported, and the prosthesis was consistently stored in cold water overnight. In addition, the patient acknowledged occasional application of moderate to excessive brushing force during cleaning, particularly when perceived stains or surface irregularities were present. This combination of prolonged prosthesis use, mechanical cleaning habits, and suboptimal maintenance practices may have contributed to progressive surface degradation of the denture base over time.

A simplified occlusal examination was performed using articulating paper placed bilaterally in the posterior regions. Upon evaluation of the occlusal markings, more pronounced contact intensity was observed on the left posterior teeth compared with the contralateral side, suggesting an asymmetrical distribution of occlusal contacts. However, no evident lateral mandibular deviation or skeletal shift was detected clinically. The patient exhibited a habitual preference for unilateral mastication, rather than a true occlusal discrepancy or functional mandibular displacement. The stability of the removable prosthesis under functional loading appeared reduced, indicating a potential imbalance in occlusal force distribution. This finding may reflect localized overload associated with preferential chewing patterns rather than a structural occlusal disharmony. It should be noted that the most reliable assessment of occlusal force magnitude would require the use of dedicated occlusal force measurement systems, such as pressure-sensitive sensors or computerized occlusal analysis systems. Unfortunately, such quantitative assessment was not performed in the present study, and the influence of occlusal force magnitude could not be fully evaluated.Salivary pH was measured chairside using pH indicator strips (MN, 92150, PH-fix6.0-7.7, GER), and the results were found to be within the normal physiological range. This finding suggested an absence of overt acidic oral environment that could contribute to accelerated chemical degradation of the denture base material.

A multifaceted treatment approach was subsequently implemented following comprehensive clinical evaluation. Management included restoration of the maxillary molar defects using composite resin, occlusal adjustment to reduce asymmetrical occlusal contacts, behavioral instruction to discourage unilateral mastication, and recommendation for future replacement of the single mandibular denture because of advanced occlusal wear and compromised prosthesis stability. Occlusal adjustment was performed to reduce asymmetrical occlusal contacts identified during clinical examination. Articulating paper examination demonstrated relatively heavier occlusal contacts on the left posterior region, corresponding to the side of the observed prosthetic defect.

Following treatment, the patient reported improved masticatory comfort and perceived functional balance after occlusal adjustment and behavioral instruction. At the 3-month follow-up visit, no progression of the existing defect or development of new wedge-shaped lesions was observed. A simple occlusal examination was performed, consistent with the initial clinical assessment. However, long-term follow-up was not available because the patient subsequently relocated to another region for an extended period, resulting in loss of follow-up. The patient was advised to maintain bilateral mastication, adopt appropriate denture hygiene practices, and attend regular recall visits for prosthesis maintenance and occlusal reassessment.

Discussion

Strictly speaking, the lesion observed in the SMD does not fulfill the classical histological definition of a non-carious cervical lesion in natural teeth. However, its distinct wedge-shaped morphology and its occurrence at a stress-concentrated junction under unfavorable occlusal conditions show similarities to the clinical features described in abfraction-related wedge-shaped defects. Although direct extrapolation from natural teeth to prosthetic materials should be made with caution, this morphological similarity provides a useful framework for discussing the possible mechanisms underlying this unusual lesion.

Wedge-shaped defects are known to have a multifactorial etiology, primarily involving abrasive tooth brushing, acidic erosion, and the presence of structurally compromised enamel and dentin at the cervical region. Furthermore, this region is susceptible to biomechanical stresses from occlusal loading, a process known as abfraction. Although prevalent in natural dentition, wedge-shaped defects are rarely documented in SMD.

Firstly, bruxism is a recognized etiological factor for wedge-shaped defects. This parafunctional activity, characterized by involuntary clenching or grinding of the teeth, may induce occlusal imbalance, premature contacts, and excessive lateral forces, resulting in stress concentration in the cervical region and subsequent loss of dental hard tissue [8, 9]. Occlusal surface wear has also been proposed as an indirect indicator of excessive occlusal loading associated with bruxism [10]. However, bruxism was considered less likely to be the primary etiological factor in the present case. The patient denied a history of bruxism, and no clinical signs suggestive of generalized bruxism-related tooth wear were observed. In addition, the wedge-shaped defects were confined to the left side rather than showing the more generalized or bilaterally distributed pattern commonly associated with bruxism. Although bruxism cannot be completely excluded, the available clinical findings suggest that it was unlikely to be the predominant cause of the observed lesion.

Secondly, the toothbrush abrasion theory proposes that certain extraoral mechanical habits are important etiological factors in the development of wedge-shaped defects. Vigorous horizontal tooth brushing with excessive force has been considered a major contributing factor because it produces concentrated abrasive wear in the cervical region. Epidemiological studies have shown a significant association between horizontal brushing and the prevalence of wedge-shaped defects, whereas other brushing techniques have not demonstrated a similar relationship [11]. In the present case, the patient reported occasional improper tooth-brushing habits; however, the lesion exhibited a localized unilateral distribution rather than the generalized or bilateral pattern more commonly associated with toothbrush abrasion.

Acid erosion is another recognized etiological factor. Frequent exposure to dietary acids or gastric reflux may demineralize enamel and dentin, increasing their susceptibility to mechanical wear and the subsequent development of wedge-shaped defects. However, the patient reported no preference for acidic foods, had no symptoms suggestive of gastric reflux, and salivary pH was within the normal physiological range. Moreover, acid erosion typically affects multiple teeth rather than producing an isolated unilateral lesion. Therefore, although toothbrush abrasion and acid erosion may have contributed to the lesion, neither appeared to represent the primary etiological factor in the present case.

Finally, occlusal factors appeared to represent one of the most plausible etiological contributors in the present case. The unilateral distribution of the wedge-shaped defect in the SMD, together with the patient’s long-standing habit of unilateral mastication and the relatively heavier occlusal contacts observed in the left posterior region, may suggest the presence of chronic asymmetrical functional loading. This observation is consistent with the abfraction theory, which proposes that excessive or misdirected occlusal forces generate stress concentration in the cervical region. Repeated cyclic loading may induce alternating tensile and compressive stresses, leading to microcrack initiation, propagation, and ultimately structural breakdown when the fatigue limit of the material is exceeded [12, 13]. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that the magnitude, direction, and point of application of occlusal forces continuously change during mastication, resulting in dynamic stress redistribution. Such repeated non-axial loading may increase the susceptibility of dental tissues to fatigue-related damage, particularly in regions subjected to tensile stress [14, 15]. Although the present lesion occurred in an acrylic denture rather than in natural tooth structure, the patient’s habitual unilateral mastication may have created a biomechanical environment analogous to that proposed by the abfraction theory, which could have contributed to the development of the localized wedge-shaped defect [16–18]. In the present case, articulating paper examination demonstrated relatively heavier occlusal contacts in the left posterior region, corresponding to the side of the prosthetic defect. Together with the patient’s habitual unilateral mastication, these findings may reflect long-term asymmetrical functional loading. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Quantitative assessment of occlusal force magnitude using dedicated measurement systems, such as pressure-sensitive sensors or computerized occlusal analysis, was not performed in the present case. Consequently, the relationship between occlusal loading and lesion development could only be inferred from the available clinical findings rather than objectively quantified.

This case also raises an interesting biomechanical question. In natural teeth, the cervical region is a well-recognized site of stress concentration and structural vulnerability, predisposing it to the development of wedge-shaped defects. However, this mechanism cannot be directly extrapolated to an SMD because acrylic artificial teeth are monolithic structures without an enamel–dentin junction, and occlusal forces are generally transmitted to the alveolar ridge through the denture base rather than being concentrated at the cervical region [19].

One possible explanation is that the lesion in the present case was located at the junction between the artificial tooth and the denture base resin, which may represent a mechanically vulnerable region. The prosthesis had been worn for approximately 5 years and exhibited poor retention and reduced stability during function. Consequently, functional loading may have resulted in denture movement, including slight sinking, rotation, or displacement, leading to uneven force distribution. During habitual unilateral mastication, the working-side artificial teeth may have been subjected not only to vertical loading but also to lateral and torsional forces. Repeated exposure to these non-axial forces, together with long-term material aging, may have produced localized stress concentration and fatigue-related microstructural damage, ultimately contributing to the formation of the observed wedge-shaped defect.

Additionally, the cervical region of natural teeth represents the junction between enamel and cementum. In approximately 10%–30% of individuals, incomplete coverage at this junction leaves the underlying dentin exposed, rendering this region more susceptible to abrasion and acid erosion and consequently predisposing it to the development of wedge-shaped defects. In contrast, an SMD lacks this anatomical structure, suggesting that the mechanism underlying the lesion observed in the present case may differ from that of natural teeth. The localized defect may instead be associated with chronic mechanical wear arising from functional contact between the removable prosthesis and the opposing natural dentition, age-related degradation of the denture material, or a combination of these factors. The unusual presentation described in this case highlights the need for further biomechanical and material-based investigations to clarify the mechanisms responsible for localized wedge-shaped defects in removable prostheses.

Although the precise etiology of the lesion could not be definitively established, the available clinical findings suggest that the observed defect was most likely associated with a combination of occlusal imbalance, habitual unilateral mastication, and chronic mechanical wear. Given the observational nature of this case report, a causal relationship cannot be established. This report highlights the importance of comprehensive occlusal assessment, patient behavioral evaluation, and regular prosthesis maintenance when managing unusual surface defects in removable dentures. Furthermore, this case may improve clinical recognition of localized unilateral wedge-shaped defects in single mandibular dentures and provides preliminary clinical evidence for future biomechanical and material-based investigations into their underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

This report describes a rare unilateral wedge-shaped defect in an SMD. The lesion may be associated with multiple contributing factors, including unilateral mastication, uneven occlusal loading, long-term prosthesis wear, and inappropriate denture maintenance habits. Comprehensive occlusal evaluation, proper denture hygiene, balanced mastication, and regular follow-up may help reduce the risk of similar prosthetic complications. Further studies are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms and associated risk factors of wedge-shaped defects in removable dentures.
