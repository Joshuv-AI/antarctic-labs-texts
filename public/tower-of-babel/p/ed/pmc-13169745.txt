Antidepressant discontinuation in France from an intersectional perspective: a discrete-time survival analysis within the MAIHDA framework
Authors: Eugenia Alcalde, George Leckie, Emmanuel Wiernik, Vladimir Jolidon, Laurent Rigal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntidepressant (AD) discontinuation before remission is common in depression care, despite guidelines recommending continuation for six months after remission to prevent relapse. Evidence suggests that socially disadvantaged groups are more likely to discontinue treatment despite higher depression severity, which may contribute to widening inequalities in mental health care. Most studies focus on single social characteristics rather than considering complex intersecting social identities. This study examines AD discontinuation over the first year of treatment across intersectional social strata.MethodsWe analysed data from AD users in the French CONSTANCES cohort (N = 44,268; 64% women) linked to the national drug reimbursement register (2007–2021). We assessed AD discontinuation using discrete time survival analysis within a Multilevel Analysis of Individual Heterogeneity and Discriminatory Accuracy (MAIHDA) framework across 36 intersectional social strata based on unique combinations of gender, education, generation, and migration background.ResultsSocial inequities in AD discontinuation between intersectional strata slightly increased over time. Men or women intersecting lower education, younger generations, and migrant backgrounds had a 44% predicted probability of remaining on treatment at month 1 and 12% at month 11. Men or women with higher education, non-migrant status, and belonging to Generation X or the Boomers, had a 66% predicted probability of remaining on treatment at month 1 and 28% at month 11, indicating longer treatment durations.ConclusionsWhile AD discontinuation may sometimes reflect remission, it is also shaped by intersectional social contexts. Our findings show that AD discontinuation is socially patterned, and this could inform comprehensive policies and programs aimed at supporting individuals from social strata that may experience more difficulties in sustaining treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27208-4.Keywords: Discontinuation, Intersectionality, Antidepressants, Survival analysis, MAIHDA

Introduction

Engaging in a new antidepressant (AD) treatment for a sufficient amount of time to ensure recovery is a major concern in depression care. While guidelines recommend that AD should be continued for at least six months after remission to prevent relapse (e.g., NICE, French National Authority for Health), several studies from Western countries [1–3] have reported that discontinuing treatment prematurely is a common phenomenon. An earlier French study found that around 72% of newly prescribed patients discontinued treatment within the first six months after initiation, of which 45% only had a single 28-day dispensation [2]. Defining an optimal treatment duration remains challenging. Given guideline recommendations and considering that the median duration of a depressive episode in the general population ranges from 3 to 6 months, depending on severity [4, 5], one would expect AD treatment to last at least 9 to 12 months. Still, these estimations are very general and may not accurately reflect systematic differences in episode and treatment durations for minority or marginalized groups for whom there is a lack of information on these aspects.

High rates of early discontinuation suggest two possible scenarios. First, ADs may be prescribed for mild symptoms, where they are no more effective than a placebo [6]. In such cases, users might discontinue treatment because they experience little to no noticeable improvement, and their symptoms may disappear naturally over time. Second, users may be reluctant to use AD even when prescribed. Reasons for this reluctance include, but are not limited to, concerns about stigma [7, 8], concerns about side effects [9, 10], negative experiences with providers [11–14], and perceived ineffectiveness [11]. Even though these perceptions are not necessarily tied to a specific social group, they may be more pronounced for AD users at the bottom of the social hierarchy. Evidence suggests that individuals with low income [2, 15], a low level of education [9], non-Western immigrant backgrounds [9, 16], and older age [9] tend to discontinue treatment earlier than their more advantaged counterparts. Importantly, while groups at the bottom of the social hierarchy are more likely to experience depression [17], early discontinuation may increase their burden by prolonging the untreated progression of symptoms [18].

When articulating depression care with social inequities, we recognize the need for an intersectional approach rather than isolating social dimensions. Indeed, social identities are not reducible to a single dimension, and overlooking this can neglect the interplay of many systems of oppression in individuals' existence. Intersectionality theory, first coined and conceptualized by Black feminist scholars, emphasizes the complexity of intersecting power relations at the core of human experiences and social relations [19]. As an analytical tool, intersectionality seeks to understand how categories such as ‘race,’ class, gender, ethnicity, and age – among others – interrelate and mutually shape one another [20]. In recent years, intersectionality theory has attracted significant interest in social epidemiology and other fields (e.g., [21, 22].

Applied to our case, we believe an intersectional approach allows for an in-depth understanding of how categorisation systems create specific contexts of oppression or opportunities in which individuals navigate depression care. Importantly, intersecting strata are not considered as individual ‘risky’ identities that require paternalistic health education. Instead, intersecting strata are seen as the social, political, and economic contexts where individuals with depressive symptoms may face treatment barriers. In this context, rather than framing AD discontinuation in terms of low or high adherence, which can imply a negative valence by placing sole responsibility on the individual for taking or not taking medication [8], we limit our analysis to the time-to-discontinuation and interrogate how underlying systems of power and oppression, such as classism, sexism, ageism, and racism intersect and shape depression care experiences.

In recent years, multilevel analysis of individual heterogeneity and discriminatory accuracy (MAIHDA) has been proposed as a promising approach to identify inequalities between subgroups of individuals defined by intersecting social identities (i.e., intersectional strata) [23, 24]. While individuals within these strata may share similar social experiences, considerable heterogeneity also exists within and between groups. Methodologically, MAIHDA places the social context (i.e., social stratification) as a group level in a multilevel model. Conceptually, this avoids the treatment of societal factors, such as the systems of oppression, as individual-level characteristics [25]. Until recently, MAIHDA has mainly been applied in cross-sectional designs. A recent paper has extended the MAIHDA approach to studying longitudinal trajectories of individual outcomes [26], treating time occasions (level-1) as nested in individuals (level-2), who are in turn nested within social strata (level-3).

This study adopts a different longitudinal approach — discrete-time survival analysis — to examine which social strata gradually discontinue a new AD prescription over a 12-month period in a French population-based sample. We extend previous studies focusing on independent social factors or clinical profiles in the early discontinuation of AD by identifying which intersecting strata are more likely to drop out of treatment prematurely. In the clinical context, this information could help clinicians enhance communication with groups of patients who may experience or perceive barriers in depression care. From a proportionate universalism perspective [27], and as argued by other scholars [28], identifying these groups can inform policymakers on how to implement evidence-based programs and policies to improve depression care for all, while scaling them proportionally to the level of disadvantage.

Methods

Participants

This study is based on CONSTANCES, a population-based cohort of adults randomly selected from the general French population. Enrolment occurred between 2012 and 2021, reaching over 200,000 individuals aged 18–69 covered by the National Health Insurance Fund (NHIF). The participation rate of the CONSTANCES cohort was estimated at 7.3% [29], corresponding to the proportion of invited persons who agreed to join the cohort among those eligible. Participants completed a self-administered questionnaire covering socio-demographic and health-related information and underwent medical examinations at affiliated Health Screening Centres (HSC) at inclusion. Detailed information about CONSTANCES is available elsewhere [30]. A unique anonymized identifier links the cohort to the French National Health Data System (FNHDS), containing comprehensive outpatient records, including reimbursed drug dispensations.

For the current study, we obtained access to AD dispensation data for CONSTANCES participants available from 2007 to 2021. We selected participants with at least one AD dispensation, including those whose first AD dispensation occurred after July 1, 2007, and who had no recorded dispensations in the six months preceding the first month of AD dispensation (i.e., to construct a left-truncated cohort). Only participants with complete sociodemographic data (specifically migration background and education level, see below) were considered. We excluded students at cohort inclusion (see below) and participants whose first dispensation occurred after June 1, 2021. In the case of multiple depressive episodes during follow-up, only the first was considered.

Measures

Outcome and discrete-time follow-up

We extracted data on AD dispensations from the FNHDS. In France, clinical guidelines recommend that new AD treatments be prescribed for an initial period of at least three months, with a follow-up visit expected to renew or adjust the prescription. Each dispensation usually covers 28 days, requiring users to refill their prescription monthly. Only in exceptional cases can individuals obtain more AD medications at once. Based on this, we defined AD discontinuation (0 = no discontinuation; 1 = discontinuation) as a gap of more than 56 days between dispensations (i.e., 28 for the current dispensation plus 28 days without a refill) or, in the case of single dispensations, discontinuation was assumed if no second prescription was filled. Switching AD medicines was not considered a discontinuation, as the outcome captured any dispensed AD at each time point.

To model discontinuation over time, we constructed a panel dataset in a person-period format. Participants were followed monthly from the second month of their AD treatment (i.e., we refer to this as “month 1” as this indicates the first month in which they were at risk of discontinuing their AD treatment) for up to 11 months (i.e., corresponding to a year after initiation) or until they discontinued treatment. Participants with a single dispensation, for example, had one row indicating discontinuation in the second month after treatment initiation, while those continuing treatment for longer contributed as many rows as they filled in their treatment, up to a maximum of 11 rows. Although participants initiated treatment at different calendar years between 2007 and 2021, we did not model period effects explicitly. We therefore implicitly assumed no systematic changes in AD prescribing or discontinuation patterns over time.

Social variables and stratification

To build our intersectional stratification, we used sex, educational attainment, migration background, and birth cohort as measured at inclusion. In particular, we assumed that sex and educational attainment were stable between the time they answered the CONSTANCES questionnaire and AD initiation, even if we acknowledge that this cannot be certain. For sex, we assumed that all participants had a static gender identity. As many life-course studies suggest, mid-adults have relatively static educational attainment [31]. Therefore, we considered that the information collected at inclusion was their highest qualification achieved. For this reason, we excluded students, as they may report lower educational attainment at cohort inclusion, potentially introducing misclassification bias.

The CONSTANCES database provides a binary variable for “sex,” which was used as the basis for the random sampling scheme. We used this binary categorisation as a proxy for the gender identity of men and women. For education, we grouped participants into two categories: lower education (up to 12 years of education—secondary level) and higher education (completed post-secondary). This dimension was used as a measure of socioeconomic position. Participants were asked to indicate their region of origin, selecting from a list of eight regions of the world. They were then asked the same question regarding the geographical area of their ancestors. Another question asked about their nationality. We used the answers to these three questions to categorize people according to their migration background, following previous demographic research [32]. The resulting categorisation divided the participants into a majority group (French-born individuals without an immigrant background, except for the grandchildren of immigrants, immigrants from Europe, and French citizens born abroad), an immigrant group (non-French citizens born outside Europe and French citizens born in overseas departments), and a descendant group (individuals born in France to parents with a non-European immigrant background). This categorisation was used to distinguish white French and those from other majority backgrounds, from first- and second-generation immigrant participants.

We extracted birth years to categorize individuals into different generational groups. We chose to include generation in the stratification because members of a birth cohort (generation) share historical and social contexts at comparable ages, which may likely shape cohort-specific attitudes towards mental health and, more specifically, AD use. Moreover, differences across birth cohorts may stem from differential exposure to stressors and their cumulative effects over the life course. For instance, many studies conducted during COVID-19 have demonstrated that “boomers” are more resilient than younger generations [33], likely because of differences in coping style and adaptive behaviour shaped by cumulative events at broader societal levels (e.g., recessions and crises) and multiple lived experiences at the personal level (e.g., bereavement, material losses). Further, we grouped some generational categories due to the small number of cases, resulting in three modalities: “silent/boomer”, “X”, and “Y/Z”. Our stratification resulted in 2 X 2 X 3 X 3 = 36 strata, all of which included sufficient participants (minimum strata size was n = 56, see Supplementary Table 1).

Only sex and date of birth were fully complete in our database, as both variables were provided by the CONSTANCES administrators. Missing values reached up to 3.5% for education and geographic origin.

Control variables

Since AD medications have varying levels of acceptability and tolerability [34], discontinuation may be more prominent in some medication types than others. We adjusted our models for different classifications of ADs to account for potential confounding effects related to the type of prescribed molecules. We categorised the molecules into three groups: the reference group of serotonin-selective reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs); monoamine oxidase inhibitors and tricyclics; and other ADs.

The full model (see below) was also adjusted for age at the initiation of AD, which was treated as a fixed characteristic of the participants. Further information on this variable can be found in the Supplemental Materials.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using R 4.3.0. We applied a multilevel discrete-time survival analysis within a longitudinal MAIHDA framework. Briefly, survival analysis refers to methods used to examine the time until an event of interest occurs [35]. The event may not be observed for all subjects before the study ends; such subjects are considered censored. In our study, this applies to participants who continued AD treatment beyond the 11-month observation period, i.e., those who had not discontinued and remained on treatment at least into the twelfth month. Survival models estimate the hazard function (i.e., the probability of discontinuation at time t, given survival until t) and the survival function, which represents the probability of remaining on treatment up to time t. The survival function is cumulative, as it depends on hazard estimates across all previous time intervals.

Initial models revealed that a quadratic functional form for the baseline hazard improved model fit and was retained throughout the analysis. The month variable (and its squared term) was centred at month 6 to facilitate computation. We present the two final models: an unadjusted model with intersectional strata-specific hazard functions and a fully adjusted model that includes the main effects and interactions with time of the intersection defining variables (gender, educational attainment, migrant background, and generation). Full details are given in the Supplemental Materials. We describe the most salient points here.

The strata-specific hazard functions were specified by including a stratum-level random-intercept effect and a stratum-level random-slope effect on the linear component of time. The variability of the intercept and slope effects was captured by the intercept and slope variances. We additionally included an individual-level random-intercept effect to allow for frailty; that is, with all else being equal, some individuals may be more likely to discontinue than others due to unobserved individual heterogeneity [36].

The partially adjusted model was used to compute the “dynamic” Variance Partitioning Coefficient (VPC). In MAIHDA cross-sectional studies, the VPC indicates the magnitude of outcome variability attributable to differences between intersectional strata. In other words, the VPC expresses the discriminatory accuracy (DA) of the stratification in distinguishing individual differences in AD discontinuation between strata. In our case, a single VPC would not capture the dynamics of our stratification over time; however, it is possible to calculate the VPC as a function of time [26]. We therefore report the VPC for each month, interpreting it as the percentage of individual variability in AD discontinuation attributable to mean differences at the strata level at that point in time.

The full model allowed us to examine the degree to which the intersectional inequalities at each month could be explained by the additive main effects of gender, educational attainment, migrant background, and generation. Specifically, we calculated the “dynamic” proportional change in variances (PCV) associated with adding the intersection-defining variables to the model. That is, we report the PCV for each month. The PCV ranges from 0 to 1. The higher the PCV, the more the patterning of intersectional inequalities follows an additive pattern at that point in time. For example, a PCV of 0.9 would suggest that 90% of the variation in intersectional inequalities follows an additive structure of the stratifying variables, while a 10% is non-additive, that is, unexplained variation due to interactions between two or more intersection-defining variables.

The full adjusted model also provided adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) for the main effects of gender, educational attainment, migrant background, and generation, and how these evolve over time. Finally, based on this model, we calculated the mean predicted hazard and survival function for AD discontinuation for each stratum at each month. These predictions are based on the full linear predictor, which includes both the main effects of the covariates, their interactions with time, and the random intercept and random slope for time at the stratum level, while setting the individual-level random intercept to zero. We plotted the survival function for each stratum, illustrating the probability that discontinuation had not yet occurred at each monthly interval.

For sensitivity analyses, we estimated the same models using dummy variables for months 1 to 11, instead of the linear and quadratic month terms.

Results

Overall, the variability attributable to the stratification followed a U-shaped trend over time, ranging from 1% at month 1 to almost 2% at month 11. The adjusted model explained most of the between-strata variance (PCV = 97.8%) at month 1, implying that inequalities in AD discontinuation at this stage mainly followed an additive pattern. However, the PCV decreased to around 82% from month 7 onwards (Fig. 1), suggesting that the patterning of intersection inequalities becomes less additive over time. This implies that not only do inequalities increase over time, but unmodelled interactions between two or more of gender, educational attainment, migration background, and generation play an increasingly important role in defining these inequalities as time proceeds, accounting for about 18% of the variation in the hazard of discontinuation from month 7 onwards.

For simplicity, we present our results at month 1 (i.e., single dispensations) and month 11 (under treatment after one year). These two occasions can be seen as a way to distinguish early discontinuers from continuers. Table 2 shows the top five strata with AD discontinuation's highest and lowest predicted survival functions at month 1, based on the fully adjusted model. At month 1, the predicted hazard of AD discontinuation ranged from 34 to 56% across all strata. The strata with the highest probability of discontinuation were lower-educated women and men with immigrant backgrounds and belonging to the youngest generations (“Y/Z”) or Generation “X” (54%−56%). In contrast, those with the lowest probabilities of discontinuation at month 1, with few exceptions, were highly educated women and men from the majority group and belonging to Generation “X” or the “Boomer” generation (34%−36%).

Discussion

Using data from a drug reimbursement register linked to a large population-based sample, this study explored AD discontinuation over the first year of prescription from an intersectional perspective. We adopted a MAIHDA approach combined with discrete-time survival analysis and used participants’ unique combinations of educational attainment, gender, migration background, and generation from a general population-based sample. Overall, all strata tended to discontinue gradually as months passed and showed low probabilities of AD use by the end of the first prescription year. This is not surprising, as we expect people to stop AD after at least six months of symptom remission. What is surprising, however, is that despite being unable to ascertain the reason for discontinuation (e.g., remission or early quitting), we found that discontinuation is not only socially patterned but also that social inequities between strata tend to slightly increase over time. Another way to put it is that the probabilities of remaining under treatment after the first dispensation (i.e., month 1) differed by approximately 22 percentage points between the least and most disadvantaged social strata. After one year of treatment (i.e., month 11), this difference was 16 percentage points. In this sense and using different indicators within the MAIHDA framework (i.e., the VPC and the predicted survival probabilities per stratum), our findings contribute new evidence to the compelling body of research on social inequities in depression care.

Although comparisons with other studies are limited by methodological differences, some of our findings align with previous research. For example, in our study, the percentage of single dispensations per stratum (i.e., observed, not predicted by the model) ranged from 37 to 60% (not shown), which is comparable with a previous French study based on a representative sample from the FNHDS which documented that 40% of AD users had only one dispensation [3]. Interestingly, that 60% figure, the upper bound in our study, corresponds to immigrant women with lower education and belonging to Generation “X”, while the lower bound (37% to 40%) corresponds to strata composed of men and women from the majority group (i.e., the French non-migrant group). This suggests that the study of Fagot et al., using the representative sample of the FNHDS, merely reflects AD use in the majority group and may not capture other social realities. On that point, our findings contribute to unmasking how other social strata navigate depression care and, in particular, to demystifying the assumption of a homogeneous population navigating such care. Furthermore, our findings are also consistent with previous studies on AD discontinuation that examined social position indicators independently. For example, using data from the STAR*D study, Warden et al. (2009) also found that younger individuals and those with racialized backgrounds were more likely to discontinue treatment earlier [37].

To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply a longitudinal MAIHDA framework to analyse AD discontinuation over the first treatment year. The VPCs from Model 1 over time were low; however these values are comparable to those reported in other MAIHDA cross-sectional studies using mental health outcomes [38, 39]. Importantly, and as reflected by a lower PCV over time (i.e., unmodelled intersectional differences increased from the 7th month onwards), these low VPCs do not rule out specific intersectional experiences in depression care. Rather, they indicate that our strata-defining variables only captured a small portion of the variability in our outcome lying between strata. Another way to put it is that these low VPCs indicate that the discriminatory accuracy of our models to distinguish individuals who might discontinue early across strata was relatively weak, as early discontinuers may also belong to the most socially advantaged groups. That said, our method aligns with the global need to expand our knowledge of how social stratification systems shape individuals’ experiences in navigating mental health care. Traditional regression models with interaction terms or collapsed social categories would have limited our ability to identify specific intersectional strata that may face particular challenges in the depression continuum of care. A major advantage of the MAIHDA framework is that it generates predictions for every stratum instead of relying upon a comparative approach that requires an arbitrary reference group. Furthermore, the partial pooling used to generate these predictions has been shown to be more accurate than attempting to directly estimate the relationship in each stratum [40].

We acknowledge that AD intake depends on many factors, including personal attitudes (e.g., agency, reluctance, etc.) and clinical factors such as severity, comorbidities, and side effects. We arguably oriented our analyses on the social contexts that shape AD discontinuation, rather than assessing associations between determinants and this event. Our findings reveal two distinct patterns among AD users, defined as early discontinuers and continuers, which are shaped by the unequal distribution of resources and opportunities across different social positions. On the one hand, social identities intersecting a lower educational attainment, racialized backgrounds, and younger generations, among both men and women, tend to use ADs for short periods. On the other hand, social identities intersecting a higher education attainment, membership in the majority population, and belonging to Generation “X” or the Boomers, regardless of gender identity, use AD for relatively longer periods. Importantly, assuming that AD treatment was an appropriate option given the severity at onset, our findings suggest that strata identified as early discontinuers may experience greater difficulty in continuing treatment. Such disparities warrant further attention, as the course of untreated symptoms can lead to worse depression outcomes [37]. One possible explanation is that the partnership with clinicians, which is key in depression care [41] for evaluating severity over time and obtaining a renewal, may be hindered by intersecting forms of domination and oppression. Indeed, the asymmetrical context of a medical encounter can lead clinicians to overlook patients from different backgrounds in treatment decisions [42]. Not to mention potential subtle (or overt) forms of discrimination toward users whose intersecting identities do not align with clinicians' social positions. For example, a previous French study documented that GPs tend to prescribe ADs more frequently to lower-educated users under the assumption that psychotherapy does not suit them [43]. If this were the case in our sample, and strata intersecting lower education were prescribed ADs for mild symptoms, premature discontinuation might reflect their inefficacy for this symptom intensity [6], but primarily driven by implicit bias. Nevertheless, the asymmetry with healthcare providers is not only the result of social class discordance, as studies report ageism and racism towards patients from providers as barriers to communication and support [42, 44]. Therefore, from an intersectional perspective, when these systems interlock in the clinical encounter, the quality of received care, in terms of support or communication about AD, may be even more affected. From the patient’s perspective, this asymmetrical relation with prescribers may contribute to premature dropout, especially if they lack information about AD functioning and feel discredited to express their concerns during consultation. On the contrary, these strata may also deliberately discontinue their AD treatment as a form of resistance to the top-down prescribing approach of clinicians. In this sense, we acknowledge that discontinuation may also stem from forms of negotiation or agency in these strata [45], whose experiences cannot be solely reduced to their intersectional position in clinical encounters.

Moving forward to explore the mechanisms underlying how intersectional experiences shape AD discontinuation among early discontinuers, we propose a step-by-step additive approach that builds a more complex picture. Our finding that younger generations are more likely to discontinue at earlier stages than older generations likely reflects younger people’s reluctance toward mental health treatment [11]. It has been shown that younger generations struggle more with accepting their diagnoses and the need for help, and may perceive depression as a personal weakness [46]. As such, they may refuse ADs out of fear of stigma. From an intersectional perspective, younger generations, especially “Y” and “Z”, who have lower education levels, may face more financial barriers to affording the out-of-pocket expenses for ADs. In France, ADs are reimbursed by the national health insurance at 65%, and the remaining costs are covered through complementary insurance. Although most people have complementary insurance, those without it tend to be more socially disadvantaged. While a free complementary scheme (Complémentaire Santé Solidaire) exists, and is granted on an income basis, some eligible disadvantaged individuals remain unenrolled [47]. Further, since these identities also intersect with racialized backgrounds, we believe that discontinuation is likely shaped by the interlocking systems of sexism, racism, classism, and ageism. In recent years, media and societal views on newly arrived young immigrants in France have fuelled the idea that immigrants “take advantage of” the welfare system and are a financial burden (e.g., the reader could refer to attempts from the right-wing sector to suppress or minimize the 'Aide Médicale d’État,' a health solidarity program for immigrants with irregular administrative status). Such narratives not only vehicle institutional discrimination [48] and legitimize interpersonal discrimination but also contribute to increased internalized racism [49]. Hence, it is not surprising that racialised young women and men with a lower level of education feel discouraged from continuing depression care. Likewise, it could also be that, due to the effects of social devaluation and discrimination, these strata may refuse the label of 'depressed', because of its potential interactive effects with their social identity. This aligns with previous research showing that high levels of self-stigma are associated with the discontinuation of medications [50, 51].

Finally, we found that older birth cohorts were more prone to continuing AD, as opposed to the younger generations. Since older birth cohorts tend to have worse mental health [52], partly due to the cumulative effects of biographical and historical hardships over the life course, but also because of age-related discrimination (ageism) [53], their continued use of AD follows an expected trajectory. As a reminder, the top two strata with the highest probability of using AD for over a year were men and women from the Silent/Boomer birth cohorts with higher educational attainment and belonging to the majority group. These strata grew up in post-war France, a period marked by economic growth and considerable booms in innovative biotechnologies, particularly the unexpected discovery and commercialization of the first AD generations in the 1950s. Their acceptance of AD may also reflect broader upper-class social norms around coping with depression, as higher social positions tend to have greater dispositions to seek mental healthcare, as sociological scholarship has documented [54].

Implications

Our results underscore the need for programs and policies that aim to improve depression care not only for the most disadvantaged, but through a proportionate universalism approach, reaching everyone while adjusting support to varying levels of need. Considering the asymmetrical dynamics in clinical encounters, programs aimed to improve clinicians’ communication skills and addressing implicit biases could help fill this gap. As documented by Aggarwal et al. in their review, some effective communication strategies that promote continued mental health care among racial and ethnic minorities include, but are not limited to, incorporating users’ views of illness and care in a collaborative partnership [42]. Notably, French clinical guidelines for depression recognize the importance of the clinician–patient therapeutic alliance (e.g., by recommending that clinicians explain how ADs work and inform patients on issues related to stigma and discrimination) [55]. However, in practice, prescribing decisions are often guided by clinicians’ own explicit or implicit beliefs and strategies [56]. Hence, guidelines alone do not appear sufficient and additional training on implicit bias and comprehensive communication strategies is necessary, particularly when supporting patients from diverse social backgrounds. Yet, as argued by other scholars (e.g., [57]), addressing health inequalities extends beyond the clinical encounter and the healthcare system. Efforts towards increasing awareness, not only about depression and treatments, but also about the pervasive effects of social stratification systems, need to persist. We argue that, when planning policies, programmes and prevention campaigns, it is essential to articulate an intersectional lens informed by lived experience, in order to avoid neglecting those who are often marginalised.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, as with many studies based on drug register data, we lacked information about the severity and the AD indication. As mentioned, it is difficult to distinguish between AD discontinuation due to symptom remission, premature dropout, or whether discontinuation is driven by clinicians or patients. Nevertheless, our findings can be interpreted as an attempt to map intersectional contexts of AD treatment, rather than focusing on clinical causes underpinning discontinuation. In line with this, we also lacked information on other factors that may influence discontinuation, such as tolerability, side effects, co-therapy, or acceptability from the users’ perspective. Overall, we acknowledge that unmeasured clinical factors, including AD indication, symptom severity, treatment tolerability or acceptability, and the decision to stop treatment, may also be associated with patients’ social intersectional positions. Their absence from our models may therefore have introduced some residual confounding. As a result, differences in the probability of AD discontinuation across social strata may partly reflect unmeasured clinical factors rather than social context alone, and should be interpreted with caution. However, since we found two distinct patterns that clearly polarised strata, we believe that broader societal and contextual forces likely still played a substantial role in shaping how participants managed their treatment. We assumed that data (i.e., social identities) reported at CONSTANCES inclusion were constant over a span of 14 years (i.e., the span we had access to the data). While we selected variables that are typically static in adulthood, changes may have occurred, particularly for gender identity or educational attainment. Additionally, we collapsed some modalities due to small sample sizes, which may have obscured specific social contexts. In the adjusted model, the random-effects structure showed signs of singularity (i.e., a near-perfect correlation between variance components). This is a known issue that can arise in multilevel models when working with complex data structures. Although the model converged and the fixed-effect estimates remained stable, the random-effects parameters should be interpreted with caution. We used a maximum likelihood (ML) approach for the estimation; unfortunately, to the best of our knowledge, ML models do not provide confidence intervals for stratum-specific predicted values when using the clog-log link function. In comparison with the French general population, our sample included a higher proportion of women (~ 64% vs. 51.6%), was more educated [58], and overrepresented by the majority group (91% in our sample compared with an estimated of 80.6% in the French general population [32]). These patterns are consistent with previous studies based on the CONSTANCES cohort that have shown that participants are healthier and more highly educated than the general French population [59, 60], and may therefore limit the representativeness of our sample. Finally, our findings may not be generalizable to other healthcare settings, especially those with different AD reimbursement systems.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study shows that, contrary to clinical guidelines, many people discontinue AD earlier than expected, and that this phenomenon is socially patterned. Social strata intersecting gender, a lower level of education, an immigrant background, and a younger generation are found to discontinue AD at relatively early stages. These strata may not benefit from full support throughout their treatment journey, underscoring, to some extent, the interlocking effects of classism, racism, and ageism within depression care. Our findings could be used to implement comprehensive policies and programs to support these strata that may experience more difficulties in sustaining treatment. Still, all strata could benefit from programs that help improve depression care. Clinical guidelines could also incorporate and acknowledge the social heterogeneity within the depressed population, not from a paternalistic perspective, but from an inclusive and comprehensive one that gradually adjusts efforts according to the level of disadvantage.
