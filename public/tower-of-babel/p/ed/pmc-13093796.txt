Muscle MRI and Muscle Ultrasound Applications in MND/ALS: Academic Insights and Clinical Opportunities
Authors: Alison Toomey, Jana Kleinerova, Ee Ling Tan, We Fong Siah, Peter Bede
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThere is an unmet need for the clinically relevant ALS biomarkers to facilitate an accurate diagnosis in suspected cases, monitor disease progression and evaluate response to therapy in clinical trials. While the MND/ALS literature is dominated by innovative brain studies, motor disability in ALS is primarily driven by neurogenic muscle change impacting mobility, dexterity, respiratory and bulbar function.MethodsWith the intention of raising awareness of muscle‐derived imaging markers in ALS, a systematic review has been conducted. Study designs, imaging methods, data interpretation frameworks, and cohort characteristics were systematically evaluated to identify innovative approaches and barriers to clinical implementation.ResultsA total of 219 studies were screened and 73 original studies selected for systematic review; 37 muscle MRI studies and 36 studies using ultrasound, PET or CT. All of the selected studies successfully captured ALS‐associated muscle degeneration and their methods included the evaluation of muscle dimensions (thickness/volumes n = 34), ‘acute’ denervation (water content, n = 15), fasciculation counts (n = 14), ‘chronic’ neurogenic change (fat content, n = 21), metabolic changes (n = 4), diffusion alterations (n = 8) and echo intensity changes (n = 13).ConclusionsDespite the huge impact of lower motor neuron dysfunction on the patients' independence, survival and quality of life, muscle imaging is a glaringly overlooked frontier of MND/ALS research. This is a missed opportunity, as a variety of non‐invasive quantitative muscle imaging techniques have been successfully used in other neurological conditions; these protocols are easy to implement on commercial MRI and ultrasound platforms and recent studies have demonstrated their ease of use and potential clinical utility.Keywords: amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, motor neuron disease, MRI, muscle, muscle imaging

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis is a progressive neurological condition causing considerable physical disability and eventually respiratory weakness [1]. Treatment options are limited and recent therapy development efforts, such as the implementation of ASO therapies target specific genotypes [2]. Despite the optimism around antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) therapies, the vast majority of patients with ALS have no family history of ALS and harbour no known ALS‐associated genetic variants. These patients are typically labelled as ‘sporadic patients’ ALS and have limited therapeutic options. Clinical trial designs in ALS are conservative [3] and continue to rely on clinically‐administered instruments such as the revised ALS functional rating scale (ALSFRS‐r), various bulbar measures [4], respiratory indices [1] and often use survival as clinical trial end‐points [5]. Despite the recognition of comorbid frontotemporal [6], cerebellar [7, 8], extrapyramidal [9] dysfunction in ALS, validated cognitive [10], spasticity [11], upper motor neuron [12], and gait impairment scales are seldom administered [5, 13]. A wealth of biofluid [14, 15] and imaging biomarkers [16, 17] have been proposed over the years to capture and track progressive changes in the clinical setting, but these have not been integrated in pharmacological trial designs. Wet biomarkers in ALS include serum, cerebrospinal fluid and urine markers that are thought to be associated with disease stages and considered to distinguish ALS from other neurodegenerative conditions [15, 18]. Some wet biomarkers, such as neurofilament light chain (NfL) levels, do not only distinguish patients from controls, but are excellent prognostic indicators and may be particularly suitable for clinical trial applications [19]. Surrogate imaging markers of ALS encompass a range of spinal cord [20, 21, 22], white matter [23, 24], cortical grey matter [25], subcortical [26, 27, 28], network integrity [29, 30] and neurometabolic [31, 32] measures. Despite the potential monitoring role of some of these markers, and their ability to detect progressive changes over relatively short follow‐up intervals [16], they are not used in clinical trials. Muscle imaging is a new and relatively underexplored frontier of ALS imaging. While a number of innovative Muscle MRI [33] studies have been published in ALS, the majority of imaging studies in ALS continue to focus on cerebral and spinal cord alterations [21]. This is a missed opportunity, as quantitative muscle imaging is widely used in a range of neuromuscular conditions and ALS is associated with relentlessly progressive muscle dysfunction and muscle atrophy. While classically regarded as a downstream phenomenon due to denervation and neurogenic change, primary myopathic processes have been repeatedly implicated [34]. Progressive lower motor neuron dysfunction in ALS leads to relentless muscle wasting and results in impaired dexterity, respiratory weakness, gait impairment and bulbar dysfunction. Skeletal muscle mitochondrial dysfunction has been suggested by some ALS studies, but contested by other [35].

There is a consensus among ALS neurologists that lower motor neuron dysfunction drives clinical disability and disproportionately contributes to the decline in revised ALS Functional rating scale (ALSFRS‐r) scores. This is supported by insights from PLS, where physical disability remains relatively moderate despite long symptom duration and dramatic brain changes [36, 37]. In light of the central role of LMN dysfunction in defining motor disability and the technical challenges associated with spinal anterior horn imaging in vivo [20, 21, 38], muscle imaging is an academically and clinically relevant frontier of ALS biomarker development. It is also noteworthy that ALS is associated with a very specific pattern of muscle atrophy with the selective involvement of specific muscles among muscles innervated by the same nerve from the same spinal root. This is referred to as the split‐hand and split‐leg phenomena, a muscle involvement pattern so characteristic of ALS that it is often considered a diagnostic cue on initial clinical review [39]. The split hand sign is commonly regarded as the manifestation of divergent corticomotoneuronal factors [40, 41] and often specifically evaluated by neurophysiology studies. Despite its diagnostic utility and specificity to ALS, this phenomenon is seldom assessed on muscle imaging [42].

Beyond its relevance in ALS, muscle imaging seems particularly suitable to detect and track progressive muscle wasting and neurogenic change in lower‐motor neuron (LMN) predominant motor neuron diseases (MNDs) [43] such as Spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy (SBMA) or Kennedy's disease [44], Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) [45], post‐polio syndrome (PPS) [46], monomelic forms of MND [47], Hirayama disease [48, 49]. While varying degrees of cerebral involvement have been described in Kennedy's disease [50], SMA [51] and post‐polio syndrome [16, 52], the reported patterns are either anatomically inconsistent [44], or not confirmed by other studies [38]. Even increased cerebral integrity metrics were detected in some LMN conditions and interpreted as possible compensatory changes [38, 53, 54]. As disability in these LMN‐predominant conditions is primarily defined by neurogenic change and ensuing muscle atrophy [44, 45], the development and implementation of muscle‐based imaging techniques may be particularly applicable to these phenotypes.

While the vast majority of ALS and MND imaging studies continue to focus on brain and spinal cord alterations, significant progress has been made in quantitative muscle imaging in other neurological conditions. Accordingly, we have conducted a systematic review of existing muscle imaging initiatives in motor neuron diseases, focusing on clinical utility, biomarker potential and potential clinical trial applications. We review the main findings of pioneering muscle imaging studies in MND to systematically appraise the methodological lessons of existing studies, identify stereotyped shortcomings and define research priorities and future directions.

Methods

A formal literature review search was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) recommendations to systematically evaluate recent developments in muscle imaging in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis and other motor neuron diseases using the PubMed repository (last accessed on 1st September 2025). The following primary search terms, ‘Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis’, ‘Motor Neuron Disease’, ‘Kennedy's disease’, ‘Primary Lateral Sclerosis”, ‘Spinal Bulbar Muscular Atrophy’, ‘ALS’, ‘MND’ were individually paired with ‘muscle MRI’, ‘muscle Imaging’, ‘muscle ultrasound’, ‘muscle CT’, ‘biomarker’, ‘quantitative’ and in an additional search paired with the keywords ‘split hand’, ‘tongue’, ‘diaphragm’, ‘first dorsal interosseous”, ‘paraspinal’, ‘neck extensor’, ‘limb girdle’, ‘psoas’, ‘intercostal’. A supplementary search was conducted with the keywords ‘ALS’/”MND” paired with ‘muscle’ and ‘machine‐learning’/‘AI’. Our search was limited to studies reported in English that involved human subjects. All papers were screened by title and abstract and the full text of selected articles was then reviewed. The inclusion criteria included: (1) original research articles investigating muscle alterations by imaging in ALS/MND (2) innovative case series using novel imaging techniques were also considered. Animal studies, editorials, opinion pieces, previous reviews, conference abstracts, case reports, studies reported in other languages than English were excluded. The reference lists of screened and selected articles were also reviewed to identify additional original research papers. We have only critically reviewed original research studies utilising quantitative imaging techniques, but studies reporting imaging alterations on visual inspections were also indexed for discussion. Following the selection of the relevant original research studies the following information was systematically retrieved from the papers: cohort size (n), control population (none, healthy controls, mimics, independent patient cohort etc.), study design (prospective, retrospective, cross‐sectional, convenience sample, longitudinal, case report, cohort study), muscle imaging methods (US, transducer array, MRI, pulse sequence, scanner, field strength), quantitative versus qualitative data interpretation (manual segmentation, semi‐quantitative approaches, visual inspection) anatomical focus (whole‐body, hand, neck, tongue, diaphragm, other), supporting clinical data (genetics, EMG, clinical exam, MRC score, functional rating scale, biopsy, alternative imaging modality, post mortem data), availability of clinical information (medication, weight, height, feeding tube in situ, catabolic state) and main study conclusions. Based on the retrieved information, strengths and innovation of specific studies are highlighted. Stereotyped shortcomings of recent studies are also discussed to inform the desirable study design of future muscle studies in MND.

Results

As outlined in our flow chart (Figure 1), a total of 219 studies were initially identified and screened. Following the exclusion of animal studies, non‐research papers, such as editorials, reviews, and opinion pieces, and studies that did not perform muscle imaging, a total of 73 studies were selected for systematic review. A total of 37 studies have implemented muscle MRI (Table 1) and 36 studies used ultrasound, PET, or CT‐based muscle imaging modalities (Table 2). Three‐quarter of the muscle MRI studies identified (75%) were cross‐sectional. with only 25% implementing a longitudinal design. The vast majority of the studies were multimodal (81%) and quantitative (84%). Less than one‐third of the studies (27%) had supporting genetic information included in their reports. Only 2 out of the 37 studies implemented ML‐type analyses either for segmentation or diagnostic classification purposes. Only 4 out of the 37 studies were multicentre studies; the rest had a single‐centre design. As expected, single centre studies included smaller cohorts, with an average sample size of 37 participants (range 7–79, SD 20.85), and the included ALS groups ranged between 2 and 60 (Mean 19.5, SD 13.45). The average number of patients with SMA was 18.5 (range 10–31), patients with PPS 17 (range 15–19), participants with SBMA 30 (range 21–39), and controls 19 (range 5–40). Multicentre studies operate with large overall samples, M = 658 (range 178–1169), with ALS participants ranging from 96 to 206 (M = 151). With respect to target muscles, 65% assessed limb muscles, 19% evaluated the tongue, 10% implemented whole‐body approaches, 8% studied neck muscles and only 5% appraised respiratory muscle groups. From a methods perspective, 57% of muscle MRI studies evaluated fat content, 41% measured water content and oedema, 22% implemented diffusion‐based techniques, and 41% measured various dimension outputs, such as muscle thickness, volumes, and cross‐sectional areas.

The 36 non‐MRI studies included 31 US, 4 CT and one PET study. The majority of these (83%) were cross‐sectional, 17% longitudinal and only 6% multicentre. The average number of participants in these studies was 98 (range 6–310). The average number of patients with ALS was 65 (range 6–261), patients with SMA 32 (range 31–33), patients with PPS 46.5 (range 45–48), and controls was 51 (range 10–123). Nearly two‐thirds of the studies (64%) evaluated limb muscles, 22% bulbar and facial muscles, 14% respiratory muscles, and only 8% neck muscles. The vast majority of US studies (94%) implemented quantitative techniques, and only 2 studies used semi‐quantitative approaches such as the Heckmatt scale to grade muscle echogenicity and atrophy. Nearyl half of the US studies (48%) evaluated muscle dimensions such as thickness or volumes, 45% measures fasciculations, and 41% assessed muscle echo intensity.

Discussion

Our review identified a number of promising and innovative approaches to detect, measure and interpret muscle changes in motor neuron disorders. A range of diverse methodologies have been successfully piloted in several MND phenotypes, demonstrating that meaningful quantitative data can be acquired in this patient population. The overarching conclusion of the reviewed studies is the feasibility of non‐invasive muscle integrity assessments in MNDs and the demonstration that the imaging indices derived from muscle imaging correlate with clinical scores and neurophysiology metrics. While the studies indicate a putative biomarker potential and a scope to develop muscle‐derived imaging measures into viable monitoring and diagnostic tools, stereotyped shortcomings have also been identified. These need to be addressed by purpose‐designed future studies. About half of the identified studies were MRI‐based (Table 1), adding to the ever‐growing literature of MRI‐derived biomarkers and these techniques could potentially be added to existing brain and spinal cord MR protocols. The other half of the studies were ultrasound‐based (Table 2) which is attractive as it can potentially be performed in a sitting or upright position, is not affected by claustrophobia or orthopnoea, and could potentially be integrated in neurophysiology protocols as an add‐on measurement.

Current and Emerging Muscle Imaging Techniques

The most commonly used muscle imaging methods in ALS/MND include the evaluation of muscles dimensions (thickness/volumes), changes associated with ‘acute’ denervation (water content, T2 signal with fat suppression, STIR), fasciculation metrics (US or MRI), ‘chronic’ neurogenic change (fat content, DIXON), metabolic changes (MRS/PET), diffusion alterations (DWI/DTI) and echo intensity changes (US). T2‐mapping evaluates T2 relaxation times and helps to detect oedema. Gadolinium‐enhanced MRI is often used in the clinical setting, but typically avoided in research and biomarker development initiatives. Dixon MRI distinguishes water and fat signals, enabling fat fraction quantification in specific muscles. Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) models water movement restriction providing valuable insights on muscle microstructure, fibre integrity and organisation. Muscle Spectroscopy (MRS) accurately measures metabolite concentrations relevant to muscle functions, such as creatine and lipids and depending on the resolution of the spectra may reflect on mitochondrial function. Beyond routine structural protocols, a number of functional muscle MRI (mfMRI) approaches have also been developed which can appraise muscle deformation during passive lengthening and active contractions [55]. T2w techniques rely on increased muscle water transverse relaxation time (T2) during muscle activation to map muscle activity during a task. T2 mapping for example facilitates quantitative muscle activation measurements to evaluate the extent of muscle recruitment. Spin echo (SE) and multi‐spin echo (MSE) sequences are commonly used in mfMRI to acquire T2‐weighted images and generate T2 maps. Sodium MRI has already been successfully used in brain imaging in ALS, [56] it can be also used for functional muscle imaging by measuring sodium concentration alterations within a specific muscle during activity. mfMRI can be has been used to map muscle activation patterns during a specific tasks and can measure the extent of muscle recruitment. mfMRI can not only identify and localise muscle dysfunction in muscle injuries but has also been implemented in metabolic myopathies. While early applications of mfMRI were intended to decipher the biomechanics of specific tasks under the physiological circumstances, it may be used to assess changes in activation and recruitment patterns following specific interventions such as rehabilitation.

Achievements of Recent Muscle Imaging Studies

In addition to the routinely used muscle imaging techniques (wT2, DIXON, STIR), a number of innovative imaging modalities have been implemented in recent studies. Fat fraction measurements have been consistently utilised as a promising marker of myopathy in ALS and a range of functional and dynamic imaging modalities have also been successfully implemented. Spectroscopy based studies, such as those implementing Phosphorus‐31 magnetic resonance spectroscopy (31P MRS) [57] detected metabolic muscle alterations suggestive of mitochondrial dysfunction. Skeletal muscle 31P MRS Skeletal enables the non‐invasive evaluation of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation both at rest and exercise proving dynamic metabolic insights. Acquisition protocols using respirator gating and spirometry‐controlled image acquisition [58] enabled the high‐quality imaging of respiratory muscles. Combined US and MRI approaches have been successfully integrated in single protocols [59] and the combination of MRI and neurophysiology techniques led to particularly innovative studies [60]. The evaluation of signal variance, motor unit activity maps, percentage of fasciculating muscles post nerve stimulation generated unprecedented spatiotemporal information on motor unit shape, distribution and recruitment sequence [61]. Similarly, ‘fasciculation imaging’ has been successfully undertaken by pioneering MRI studies based on the number of detected signal voids normalised to muscle volume and acquisition times [60, 61]. Not only did the resulting MUMRI‐derived fasciculation rates correlate with SEMG, but MUMRI offered greater anatomical coverage and apparent sensitivity. Despite the innovation of MRI‐based fasciculation imaging [60, 61], US‐based techniques have consistently demonstrated the reliability of detecting and measuring fasciculation rates in ALS [62, 63, 64]. In light of the widespread availability and overall tolerability of US compared to MRI, US‐based fasciculation quantification is probably better suited for clinical use. As a departure from manual muscle segmentation, reliance on anatomical landmarks and atlas‐based semi‐automatic parcellation, AI‐assisted muscle segmentation techniques have been successfully implemented by several recent studies [65, 66]. Automated segmentation approaches have already been extensively used in brain studies of ALS [29] for thalamic, brainstem, and amygdala segmentation, and they not only expedite the parcellation of specific regions but eliminate the need for interrater reliability assessments, multi‐site training and validation, visual anatomical landmark recognition, facilitating robust, multi‐site multicentre studies. Another achievement of recent muscle imaging studies is the evaluation of genotype‐associated changes. This has not only been shown by high‐quality SBMA studies [67, 68] but the muscle imaging correlates of specific genotypes, such as VCP variants [69] have also been demonstrated. One of the most exciting clinical aspects of recent muscle imaging initiatives is the ascertainment of clinical associations. Most of the published studies demonstrated associations between muscle integrity indices and various clinical scores such as MRC, Mercuri scores, IBMFRS, SBMAFRS, ALSFRS‐r, hand‐held dynamometry, 10MWT, 6MWD and ALSFRS‐r [58, 68, 70]. Associations with disease duration have also been explored by several studies [64, 71, 72] which help to model progressive changes in clinically heterogeneous data sets. The most interesting studies, however, are those adopting a longitudinal design [73], especially those acquiring multi‐timepoint data overall sequential acquisitions [72, 74]. These studies capture progressive FF increases over time in the soleus and TA [72] and increased T2 signal in the TA, quadriceps, hamstrings and gastrocnemius [74]. The demonstration of progressive muscle metric alterations over relatively short follow‐up intervals supports the monitoring potential of muscle derived imaging indices. While the vast majority of imaging studies focus on limb and skeletal muscles, significant advances have taken place in the imaging of respiratory muscle. Respiratory involvement is a key facet of MND with implications for survival, quality of life, independence and the early recognition of respiratory dysfunction is crucial for initiating timely interventions [1]. Recent muscle studies have not only characterised dynamic diaphragmatic changes but have successfully evaluated intercostal and accessory muscle involvement also [58]. Respiratory muscle dysfunction has been detected prior to discernible PFT alterations [75] and it has been suggested that respiratory muscle imaging may be particularly useful in those with bulbar impairment who find spirometry difficult. While ALS is typically presented either as a limb‐onset (spinal‐onset) or bulbar‐onset disease, respiratory‐onset is a relatively rare but recognised entity, where respiratory muscle imaging may be particularly useful.

Some researchers highlight that ‘deep’ muscles, not readily accessible to EMG, may be quantitatively evaluated with ease using MRI‐based methods [76], while others suggest the role of muscle MRI in identifying subclinical involvement for pre‐EMG mapping to facilitate an earlier diagnosis [77]. Muscles not readily accessible or routinely sampled on EMG, such as the iliopsoas [76, 78] have been successfully imaged by several research groups and shown significant differences from controls. Paraspinal muscle groups have been evaluated by MRI [69], CT [79], and US [80]. Tongue imaging studies are of particular interest as patients presenting with isolated bulbar impairment with no limb findings often pose a diagnostic challenge early in the course of the disease, and genioglossus EMG is not well tolerated and the tongue may be difficult to relax during neurophysiology study. Methods therefore that can reliably detect neurogenic change in the tongue are of practical utility. A number of innovative tongue studies have been published evaluating T1 signal [59], echointensity alterations [70], evaluating tongue dimensions, [59] performing automated tongue segmentation [65, 66], detecting tongue fasciculations by ultrasound [63] etc. These studies represent a clear departure form visually inspecting anatomical scans for the ‘bright tongue sign’ which is not particularly sensitive, reliable or specific to ALS. A number of studies implemented whole‐body protocols [74, 77, 81] and argued that it may identify subclinical involvement or guide pre‐EMG mapping [77].

In addition to studies attaching diagnostic utility to muscle MRI in MNDs, some regard it as a putative prognostic indicator with regards to survival and overall prognosis [79, 82, 83, 84]. Longitudinal studies continued to capture progressive changes such as increasing FF and a decline in CSA and RMA even when ALSFRS‐r plateaued or exhibited a flooring effect [73] Some studies assert that muscle MRI techniques can reliably simultaneously capture both early (oedema/T2) and chronic (fatty infiltration/FF, atrophy/CSA, RMA) muscle denervation changes [73] A number of pioneering machine‐learning studies have also been published, either using muscle imaging metrics for diagnostic classification [85, 86], or deep‐learning techniques for accurate anatomical segmentation [65, 66]. In a non‐ALS study, a random forest supervised model was implemented to accurately identify muscular dystrophies based on fatty replacement patterns [86]. An US‐based fasciculation detection approach sampling 8 muscles promises high diagnostic performance in both early and late stage ALS [85]. An innovative study suggested that ML‐based body composition analyses may identify individuals who would benefit from early nutritional support [87] Another deep‐learning based study proposed that deep learning interpretation of CT‐derived imaging measures enable pulmonary function predictions, including tracheostomy‐free survival [84] Our literature review has also identified a number of studies successfully pooling multicentre data and meaningfully integrating their findings [59, 65] The obvious benefit of multicentre studies, beyond their ability to generate large data sets, is demonstrating that acquisition protocols can be harmonised across centres and potentially used in a multicentre clinical trial setting. From an academic standpoint, specific imaging markers have been linked to specific aspects of the disease such as FF to disease progression, wT2 to disease activity, diffusion metrics to fibre size and intracellular deposits, and that MRI may capture early disease‐specific features before irreversible fat replacement [88] In light with emerging phylogenetic hypotheses [40] some muscle imaging studies suggest that muscle groups with strong corticomotneuronal drives are preferentially affected by ALS and this pattern can be clearly detected on imaging [81] There is a prevailing notion that neuronal networks supporting evolutionarily ‘novel’ skills such as vocalisation, grip, fine finger movements, bipedalism may be particularly vulnerable to the neurodegenerative processes fuelling ALS. These findings are consistent with the conceptualisation of split‐hand and split‐limb phenomena [39] and the strikingly selective involvement of hand muscles on recent muscle imaging studies [42].

Several innovative studies have evaluated muscle changes in non‐ALS motor neuron diseases, such as SBMA [44], various SMA subtypes and post‐polio syndrome [52] which is very informative as these studies not only confirm the utility of muscle imaging but identify distinctive changes in conditions which have a longer course than ALS. Increased fat content and decreased CSAs were described in SBMA [67] and it has been proposed that mMRI readily differentiates between SBMA and ALS based on fat infiltration [68]. Studies of SMA revealed increased FF & FA, and decreased MD & T2 and correlations with motor function [89]. It has been suggested that limb muscles are affected earlier than oro‐bulbar muscles in SMA and that calf fat content may be the best indicator of disease severity in SMA [90]. A number of innovative ultrasound studies have also been conducted in SMA [91]. The identified inclusion‐body myositis studies have revealed T1, [92] T2, DWI [88] alterations. The relevance of IBM studies stems from the fact that IBM may present with neurogenic changes on EMG and is sometimes a differential diagnostic consideration during the work‐up for ALS. Unfortunately, we have not identified case series looking at muscle imaging changes in Hirayama disease or primary lateral sclerosis. While Hirayama disease has a high prevalence in many Asian countries and is readily recognised on flexion cervical MRI, it is a rare entity in most European countries and may initially be misdiagnosed as ALS [49]. Primary Lateral Sclerosis also poses diagnostic challenges and may resemble ALS early in the course of the disease [37]. Despite overlapping clinical and cerebral changes in PLS and ALS [36], there is very limited lower motor neuron involvement in PLS [93] and one would wonder if the whole‐body MRI protocols used in ALS studies could demonstrate this in PLS and potentially aid clinical decision making.

Shortcomings and Opportunities

Despite the innovation and the suggestion of clinical utility of recent muscle imaging studies in MNDs, considerable shortcomings have been identified (Table 3). The pathological and histological substrate of specific biophysical signal alterations (FF, FA, MD etc.) is seldom evaluated and most studies are not supported by complementary muscle biopsies or post mortem data. This could be addressed by animal model studies so we have a clearer understanding of what specific MRI alterations mean in histopathological terms. Existing animal model studies of ALS often focus on spinal cord and brain alterations but the targeted assessment of muscles both in vivo by imaging and histologically post mortem may contribute important insights. Only a portion of studies use control populations and these often merely rely on healthy controls. For an imaging marker to be clinically useful they need to distinguish a specific condition, such as ALS, from neurological controls and mimic syndromes, therefore future studies need to include mimics or neurological controls to demonstrate the specificity of changes to ALS/MND. For example, a proposed muscle imaging‐marker should readily distinguish changes in ALS from those observed in MMN, IBM or multi‐level radiculopathies to be clinically useful. The vast majority of published studies are descriptive and operate with comparative models contrasting ALS with various control groups; the prognostic value of specific muscle metrics is seldom explored in the identified imaging studies [79, 83, 84].

Stereotyped shortcomings of recent muscle imaging studies, considerations and opportunities for future study designs.

Many of the innovative muscle studies presented are marred by the scarcity of clinical data. The most commonly reported variables are total ALSFRS‐r, disease onset and MRC scores, but these offer limited information on the involvement of specific muscles. ALSFRS‐r and MRC are poor proxies of the functional impairment experienced by patients with ALS. It is noteworthy that gait impairment, bulbar dysfunction and impaired dexterity are not solely driven by upper‐ and lower‐motor neuron dysfunction, but confounded by coexisting cerebellar and extrapyramidal deficits [9, 94, 95, 96]. Symptom duration and EMG correlates of the assessed muscle seem essential. The confounding effects of catabolic state, BMI, dysphagia, and other morphometric variables such as height are seldom considered. It seems crucial to distinguish whether the detected muscle changes in a population are due to neurogenic change or nutritional factors, catabolic processes and poor swallow. At the very least, indicating if participants had bulbar onset disease, have a feeding tube in place and what their BMI is essential. Small cohorts, retrospective design, convenience samples with considerable clinical heterogeneity are obvious shortcomings of many of the reviewed studies, and only purpose‐designed, prospective, well‐powered, longitudinal studies with ‘disease controls’ can truly reflect the monitoring and diagnostic utility of a specific muscle imaging modality. The practicalities, such as how long it takes to acquire the data in proposed protocols, are surprisingly underreported. Whole‐body muscle imaging of studies seems interesting, but experience in imaging patients with ALS suggests that patients with sialorrhoea, respiratory compromise, and orthopnoea cannot tolerate long MRI protocols. It seems therefore crucial to transparently report the acquisition time of a proposed protocol, as studies operating with long protocols are likely to introduce a considerable bias to less severely affected, early‐stage or milder phenotypes. Most studies don’t report the number of patients who were invited to participate but couldn’t tolerate scanning, and the attrition rates of the few longitudinal studies are high. All of these factors limit the practical utility of muscle imaging in the clinical setting and in pharmaceutical trials. Expected attrition rates, tolerability challenges, issues related to orthopnoea, claustrophobia, sialorrhoea and scan duration need to be carefully considered when proposing a complex academic protocol for clinical or pharmacological trial use. The added value of muscle imaging compared to gold standard tests such as EMG is seldom discussed. Despite the suggestion of muscle MRI identifying sub‐clinical or pre‐clinical changes [77], the comparative detection sensitivity of imaging and neurophysiology‐based techniques in ascertaining neurogenic change needs to be established. The financial aspects, costs associated with attendance to an imaging facility, data storage and analyses are seldom reported, despite their impact on clinical applicability.

While bulbar dysfunction is a pathognomonic facet of ALS with considerable quality of life implications, [4] pharyngeal and buccal muscles are seldom assessed by quantitative imaging modalities. Existing imaging studies of bulbar dysfunction invariably limit their focus to the tongue. Despite the involvement of pharyngeal constrictors as well as the stylopharyngeus, salpingopharyngeus, palatopharyngeus muscle groups in ALS, these have not been specifically evaluated by MRI studies to date. With the exception of a few studies [82], buccal, facial and temporal muscles are also understudied despite their contribution to poor oral control and sialorrhoea.

There are only a handful of studies implementing multi‐site, multi‐centre designs, which is a missed opportunity. Imaging protocols that can be harmonised across sites can potentially be used in clinical trials; therefore, ‘standardisability’ of a proposed technique is an important consideration, which is seldom addressed. Standardisation of US‐derived techniques in particular may be challenging and robust training, test–retest validation and inter‐rater reliability testing is needed for the multi‐centre implementation of a proposed US technique. While innovative CT‐based body composition studies have been published [84, 87], from a biomarker perspective, a clinical trial standpoint, and for longitudinal implementation, there is a clear preference to avoid unnecessary ionising radiation exposure or at least reduce cumulative exposure. Accessibility considerations are also likely to limit the routine use of PET‐based techniques [78]. Semi‐quantitative assessments, visual inspection, and radiological scoring systems have been reported, but semi‐quantitative have now been superseded by quantitative protocols in brain and spinal imaging. There is a clear preference for robust, reliable, observer‐independent quantitative data analysis frameworks instead of visual signal intensity rating, manual segmentations, fat content estimations, etc. Similar to brain data pipelines, there is also a clear preference for open‐source, validated academic software instead of ‘black‐box’, in‐house commercial analysis pipelines to add transparency and credence to data interpretation.

The identified studies only assess symptomatic cohorts meeting diagnostic criteria. To demonstrate the academic and practical utility of a proposed technique, the inclusion of early‐phase, peri‐diagnostic or presymptomatic cohorts seems important. Presymptomatic brain and cord studies in asymptomatic carriers of specific genetic variants have contributed significantly to our understanding of disease evolution and accruing disease burden [97, 98], but no such studies have been conducted using muscle MRI to date. Another shortcoming of existing studies is the selective focus on a few imaging markers (T1, T2, FF, DWI etc.) instead of the critical appraisal of a comprehensive panel of biophysical variables so that the detection and monitoring potential of specific markers can be juxtaposed. The systematic assessment of a large panel of imaging markers on the same subjects would help to select the most sensitive and clinically useful indices for clinical and clinical trial use and create a streamlined protocol. The few longitudinal studies don’t comment on potential flooring, ceiling, and ‘saturation’ effects which impedes translation for clinical and pharmaceutical trial use. Analogous to the concept of cognitive reserve, [10] the notion of ‘motor reserve’ has been proposed by several groups [99, 100] suggestive of a certain degree of physiological redundancy which helps to withstand some degree of degenerative change in the motor system without overt or immediate motor manifestations. Specific aspects of motor reserve such as athleticism [101], metabolic profiles [102], adaptive processes [103] etc. remain poorly defined, but it is increasingly accepted that premorbid motor network integrity may alter motor manifestations of the disease [99, 100, 103].

Existing studies, while sometimes controlling for disease duration, typically operate with convenience samples that are admixed from a symptom duration viewpoint. The early recruitment and follow of a clinically, genetically and phenotypically homogenous cohorts, with comparable disease duration through multiple timepoint would be desirable to characterise disease trajectories. In the absence of muscle biopsies or post mortem muscle data, the identified studies do not comment on histological correlates, and the proposed molecular and histological substrates of diffusivity, water content, and signal intensity alterations are merely hypothetical. Imaging animal muscle denervation models may help to establish the histological correlates of MRI alterations. Animal model muscle MRI studies have been conducted in Pompe's disease, [104] Duchenne [105], myotonic dystrophy, and other conditions. Some of these studies report associations between in vivo MRI measures and post vivo histopathology data. Correlations between diffusion parameters, autophagic markers, and fibre size have been described in Pompe mice models, for example [104]. While numerous animal model imaging studies have been carried out in ALS, the vast majority of these explore cerebral and spinal cord pathology [106]. Most studies use descriptive statistics to describe group‐level characteristics, either cross‐sectional traits or longitudinal disease trajectories of a specific condition, phenotype or genotype. While this is of interest academically, in the clinical setting, the priority is the interpretation of data from a specific individual. Accordingly, single‐subject data interpretation frameworks, z‐scoring or ML‐based subject categorisation into relevant diagnostic, phenotypic and prognostic groups is required. At the very least, evaluating the discriminatory power of specific measures between neurological conditions and reporting AUC in ROC analyses would be desirable.

There is a striking paucity of gene association muscle studies. With the exception of the large VCP study [69] and a few Kennedy's studies [67], the muscle imaging correlates of specific genetic variants have not been explored in ALS to date. With very few exceptions [81], aetiological, developmental and phylogenetic factors, the substrate of preferential muscle vulnerability and split limb phenomena are not considered or explored, despite the wealth of imaging and complementary clinical data generated by the above studies. There is a glaring absence of accompanying serum markers, fasting lipids, metabolic markers, albumin and creatinine in most muscle MRI and US studies [87]. One study suggests that SMI values may reflect denervation‐related muscle loss rather than nutritional status [71], but statistical adjustments for bulbar disability [4], nutritional status, BMI, height, etc. are seldom considered [87, 90]. Medications and information about oral nutritional supplements are also omitted by most studies, despite the potential confounding effect of malnutrition, catabolic state, dysphagia, neuropsychological and endocrine aspects of decreased appetite in ALS [107]. It is also noteworthy that large imaging consortia in ALS (Nisals, CALSNIC, etc.) have been successful in pooling harmonised and non‐harmonised brain data sets and have generated considerable novel insights into brain involvement and disease propagation patterns, but none of these initiatives have considered acquiring muscle data. The identified shortcomings of existing muscle imaging studies offer valuable lessons for the design of future studies and are relatively easily addressed to maintain the momentum of muscle imaging in MNDs and build on the success of recent initiatives (Table 3).

The Prospects of Muscle Imaging in MNDs

The assessment of muscles not readily accessible or routinely sampled by EMG, such as the diaphragm, intercostal muscles, psoas, or bulbar muscles, is a clear advantage of non‐invasive imaging modalities. Furthermore, EMG and comprehensive neurophysiology may not be available in smaller hospitals or for patients with advanced disease who cannot travel, while muscle imaging data can be acquired on local MRI platforms and transferred to other centres for data interpretation. A number of robust methods exist for individual‐subject data interpretation, from z‐scoring approaches contrasting single data sets to age‐, sex‐, height‐matched normative data, to more complex classification and ML‐frameworks. Existing data suggest that muscle MRI may potentially identify early, ‘subclinical’ changes, fatty infiltration, etc., which may not reveal themselves on clinical assessment in athletic individuals. Another practical benefit of muscle MRI and US is that high‐quality data can be acquired non‐invasively and patients who are anticoagulated and cannot undergo EMG can also be evaluated. Machine‐learning frameworks have been extensively applied to cerebral and spinal MRI data sets to accurately categorise individual patients into relevant diagnostic, prognostic and phenotypic subgroups. It is conceivable that muscle data can be meaningfully integrated into these models for accurate diagnostic categorisation. Conclusions

Quantitative muscle imaging modalities offer a powerful set of tools for assessing muscle integrity, providing objective information about muscle composition, fat and water content, microstructure, function and metabolism in vivo. The review of muscle imaging initiatives in MND suggests that muscle imaging may distinguish ALS from other neuromuscular conditions, may expedite the diagnosis in selected cases, has the potential to monitor disease progression objectively and with further development, may have a putative role in future clinical trial applications. From an academic standpoint, muscle imaging may shed light on disease mechanisms, the substrate of the split‐hand phenomenon, the impact of corticospinal motoneuronal drive, mitochondrial dysfunction, gene‐mitochondria interactions, and clarify if primary myopathic processes or neuromuscular junction dysfunction may contribute to the clinical syndrome of ALS. The momentous progress made in muscle imaging in MND and other neuromuscular conditions suggests that muscle imaging will be firmly integrated in future clinical and academic protocols and has potential utility in future clinical trial designs. Academic developments, advances in scanner technology, AI/ML based data interpretation and the availability of robust open‐source analysis pipelines fuel the relentless progress in biomarker development in MDN with tangible clinical utility. These advacnes enable the meaningful interpretation of imaging data from a single individual, categorizing them into relevant diagnostic, phenotypic and prognostic categories.
