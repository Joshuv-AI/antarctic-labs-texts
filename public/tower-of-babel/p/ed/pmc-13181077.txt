Double flower is associated with defects in a Phantastica-like gene in Catharanthus roseus
Authors: Koichi Tomomatsu, Takuya Tsuji, Tomotsugu Koyama, Tenta Segawa, Eiichiro Ono, Kenichi Suzuki, Keisuke Matsui, Akihiro Itai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFloral morphological traits, such as double-flowering, are important aspects of the commercial value of ornamental plants. Commercially available double-flower Madagascar periwinkle (Catharanthus roseus), cv. ’Sunny Princess’, exhibits a unique morphology in which normal floral organs, including pistils and stamens, are retained, while additional petals develops at the base of the corolla. The aim of this study was to elucidate the molecular basis of double-flowering in C. roseus. Quantitative trait locus (QTL) analysis of F₂ populations derived from crosses between single- and double-flower plants revealed that the PHANTASTICA-like (PHAL) gene is associated with the trait. Two PHAN homologs were identified in C. roseus (CrPHAN and PHAN-like (PHAL)). Double-flower plants consistently harbored a frameshift mutation immediately downstream of the PHAL initiation codon, likely resulting in a loss of function. Additionally, a nonsense mutation in PHAL was identified in double-flower cultivars with different genetic backgrounds, as further evidence of the role of this gene. These findings demonstrate that the loss of PHAL function is associated with double flowering in C. roseus. Introduction

Floral structure is a key trait in ornamental cultivars, particularly double-flower cultivars with increased petal numbers1. The genetic basis of double flowering has often been explained by the ABC model, which was first proposed for Arabidopsis and Antirrhinum to describe the combinatorial activity of the three classes of homeotic genes in floral organ identity2,3. Sepals are specified by class A genes, petals by class A and B genes, stamens by class B and C genes, and carpels by class C genes, with mutual antagonism between the A and C functions2,3. Mutations in these genes frequently give rise to double-flower phenotypes. For example, class C mutations in Arabidopsis lead to petaloid stamens and carpels3,4, whereas class B mutations in petunias and tulips induce double blossoms5. In roses, this trait has been linked to misregulation of class B and C genes6,7. Additional mechanisms include altered miRNA regulation8, ectopic expression of the transcription factor SEPALLATA3 (SEP3)9, and changes in the signaling of hormones, such as auxin10.

Madagascar periwinkle, Catharanthus roseus (Apocynaceae), native to Madagascar, is valued for its tolerance to heat, drought, and pollution, making it one of the most widely used bedding plants in the United States11,12. In addition, the plant has some medicinal value, as it produces terpenoid indole alkaloids, which are known to have pharmaceutical properties in the treatment of cardiac disorders and certain cancers13. Morphologically, C. roseus resembles a cleistogamous flower14, and although it is self-compatible, self-pollination rarely occurs because of the spatial separation of the anthers and stigmatic secretions15,16. Cross-pollination typically requires long-tongued pollinators such as butterflies and hawkmoths14. However, autonomous self-pollination occurs in certain cultivars, where pistil elongation enables contact between the stigma and anthers14,15,17. A group of double-flower cultivars characterized by additional petals arising from the corolla base has been reported (Fig. 1A, B)18. These cultivars retain fertile stamens and carpels; the results of inheritance studies have suggested that these traits are controlled by a single recessive gene18. Furthermore, similar to Matthiola, double flowering in C. roseus is associated with leaf twisting18,19. Notably, unlike the ABC model-based double flowering, which is usually caused by the homeotic conversion of non-petal organs, double flowering in C. roseus involves all four floral organs, although each retaining their identity, suggesting a distinct genetic mechanism.

Although double-flower C. roseus cultivars are available, the molecular basis of their unique phenotypes remains unclear. The objective of this study was to clarify the relationship between the MYB transcription factor and the double-flower trait in C. roseus. To investigate this mechanism, we crossed a commercially available double-flower cultivar, ‘Sunny Princess’, with single-flower lines and obtained segregating F₂ populations. Through quantitative trait locus (QTL) analysis we identified a mutation in a gene, which encodes an MYB transcription factor (PHANTASTICA-like gene, CrPHAL).

Material & Methods

Plant material

All plant materials used in this study were proprietary hybrid populations generated from commercially available C. roseus cultivars. A double-flower line of C. roseus (V20-113-15) and three wild-type single-flower lines (V20-30-2, V20-36-3, and V20-50-1) were used as the principal material (in this study, the single-flower phenotype was defined as the wild type in C. roseus). In 2022, three crosses were performed using V20-113-15 as the seed parent and each of the three wild-type lines as the pollen parents. F₁ seeds were harvested and sown, and the resulting seedlings grown to maturity. Eight single-flower F₁ lines (V22-135, V22-138, V22-139, V22-140, V22-143, V22-150, V22-151, and V22-152) were selected and subsequently self-pollinated to generate F₂ seeds. In 2024, 742 F₂ seedlings derived from these eight lines were evaluated for floral phenotype.

In addition, the following commercially available varieties of C. roseus were analyzed: the double-flower varieties ‘Sunny Princess Pink’, ‘Sunny Princess Blue’, ‘Sunny Princess White’, ‘Mira Cherry Pink’, ‘Souryo Usuzakura’, and ‘Solaris’, as well as two single-flower varieties, ‘Fairy Star White’ and ‘Fairy Star Pastel Pink’.

QTL-seq analysis

To identify genomic regions associated with the two-petaled flower trait, QTL-seq analysis was performed using a modified QTL-seq pipeline20,21. The script used in this study is described at https://github.com/SegawaTenta/modifiedQTL-seq. Genomic DNA libraries of the double-flower parent, the F1 individual, and the double- and single-phenotype bulks were prepared using the Swift 2 S Turbo Flexible DNA Library Kit (Swift Biosciences, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Paired-end sequencing was performed on the DNBSEQ-G400RS platform (MGI Tech, China). The obtained paired-end reads were aligned to the C. roseus reference genome (ASM2450571 v1)22 using BWA-MEM (v0.7.18)23. The resulting alignments were sorted and indexed with SAMtools (v1.20)24. SNP calling and pileup generation were performed using BCFtools (v1.20)25. Genome-wide sequencing depth and coverage breadth were calculated using Mosdepth (v3.12) with 100 kb windows26. Because the single-flower parent was unavailable, SNP filtering was conducted using genotype information from the double-flower parent and the F1. SNPs were retained if they were homozygous in the double-flower parent and heterozygous in the F1, thereby ensuring segregation in the progeny. SNP-index values for each bulk and ΔSNP-index were calculated using the Python scripts implemented in modified QTL-seq pipeline. Sliding window analysis was applied to calculate the average SNP-index and ΔSNP-index across the genome. Confidence intervals (95% and 99%) were estimated by simulation as implemented in modified QTL-seq. Genomic regions exhibiting the ΔSNP-index exceeding the simulation-based confidence thresholds were defined as candidate QTL regions.

Marker-assisted fine mapping

To narrow the genomic region associated with the double-flowering trait, we conducted traditional marker mapping in the F₂ population of line V22-150 using the PACE genotyping assay system (3CR Bioscience, UK)27. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) or insertion-deletions (indels) between the parents of V22-150 were used as PACE markers. The resulting narrowed region was analyzed using SnpEff to predict the functional effects of variations in the predicted genes28.

Molecular cloning and sequence analysis

PCR primers for amplifying the full-length C. roseus PHAL and CrPHAN homologs were designed based on the genome sequence of ASM2450571v1. Molecular cloning and sequence analyses were performed as described in a previous report29. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 300 ng of total RNA using ReverTra Ace (Toyobo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Genomic DNA was extracted from young leaves using a NucleoSpin Plant II kit (Macherey-Nagel, Germany). PCR reactions contained 1.25 U of Tks Gflex DNA polymerase (Takara Bio, Japan),0.2 µM of each primer, and either 1 µL of first-strand cDNA (for RT-PCR) or 10 ng of genomic DNA (for genomic PCR) as a template. The thermal cycling program consisted of an initial denaturation at 94℃ for 2 min, followed by 30 cycles of 98℃ for 15 s, 60℃ for 20 s, and 68℃ for 30 s. PCR products were cloned using the Zero Blunt TOPO PCR Cloning Kit (Invitrogen, USA) and sequenced. The primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

PACE SNP genotyping

Genotypes of the CrPHAL locus were determined using the PACE SNP genotyping assay27. A marker detecting the single-base deletion in CrPHALm1 was developed and applied to the V22-150 F₂ population. Genotyping results were compared with the floral phenotypes to test for concordance. The primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Phylogenetic analysis

The C. roseus sequence M9H77_15500 was retrieved from the NCBI database, used as the outgroup, and selected as the third-highest BLAST hit against PHAL and CrPHAN. The sequence data used for phylogenetic analysis are listed in Table S3.

The amino acid sequences were aligned with MAFFT v7.526 using the L-INS-i strategy30. Ambiguously aligned positions were removed with trimAl v1.5 using the ‘automated1’ procedure31. Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic inference was conducted using RAxML-NG v1.2.1, with the LG + F + G4 substitution model32. Branch support was assessed with 1,000 non-parametric bootstrap replicates, and Felsenstein bootstrap proportion values were reported. The best-scoring ML tree was used for the figure shown in Fig. 4.

Phylogenetic tree of PHAN-related genes. M9H77_15500 was used as an outgroup. Neighbor-joining tree is shown with bootstrap values (%) on the branches (1,000 replicates). Scale bar indicates 0.05 amino acid substitutions per site.

Gene expression analysis (qRT-PCR)

qRT-PCR was performed to examine the expression of CrPHAL and CrPHAN in young leaves and flower buds (St1-St2, Fig. 1D) from two individuals representing each genotype (D/D, S/D and S/S) of C. roseus. Total RNA was extracted from plant tissues using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 200 ng of total RNA using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (Toyobo) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative PCR was performed using THUNDERBIRD SYBR qPCR Mix (Toyobo) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). Gene expression levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method. Expression values were normalized to actin gene as the internal control gene. Each sample was analyzed in three replicates (n = 3). The primer sequences are listed in Table S1. The actin was registered in GenBank as KAI5672647.1.

Phenotypic analysis of the Arabidopsis thaliana as1 mutant

AS1 is a homolog of PHAN in A. thaliana. Wild-type plants of A. thaliana (Col-0) and AS1 loss-of-function mutants (as1; CS146 (Ler/Col) and CS3374 (Col) provided by Arabidopsis biological resource center (ABRC)) were grown at 22℃ until flowering and subsequently transferred to in controlled chambers under a cycle of 16 h light/8 h dark at 12℃, 22℃, and 32℃ for floral morphology observation. The number of floral organs was recorded and compared to that of the wild-type. Leaf morphology was observed at 22℃. CS146 and CS3374 possess a frameshift mutation caused by the deletion of a guanine nucleotide at position 691 of the AS1 gene, resulting in loss of function. These have been reported to exhibit the characteristic leaf dwarfing phenotype of as1 mutants33,34.

Statistical analysis

The goodness-of-fit for the segregation ratios was assessed using the chi-square test in Microsoft Excel. For multiple comparisons, Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test was performed using Microsoft Excel.

Results

Morphology of double flowers in C. roseus

Phenotypic observations were conducted on the double-flower C. roseus. The floral organs of the double-flower lines consisted of five sepals, five petals, five stamens, and one pistil, which were identical to those of the wild-type single-flower plants. However, the double-flower lines produced five additional petals, one at the base of each original petal (Fig. 1A–C). At early developmental stages (St1 and St2), these additional petals were visible in the floral buds (Fig. 1D, Fig. S1). In addition, the all double-flower lines exhibited characteristic leaf twisting caused by partial inhibition of leaf elongation from the juvenile to mature stages (Fig. 1E, Table S4).

Inheritance of the double-flower trait in C. roseus

The floral phenotypes of F₁ and F₂ progeny from crosses between single- and double-flower lines are shown in Table S5. All F₁ progeny exhibited the wild-type single-flower phenotype, indicating that the double-flower trait is controlled by a recessive allele. In the F₂, double-flowered plants segregated at a frequency of 20–27% across eight combinations. A chi-square test supported a 3:1 segregation ratio (single: double), confirming the Mendelian inheritance of the trait (Table S5). Moreover, F₂ double-flower plants tended to have smaller corollas than the wild-type plants (data not shown).

QTL-seq and marker-assisted fine mapping identified PHANTASTICA -like as a candidate gene for double-flowering

To identify genomic regions associated with the double-flower trait, QTL-seq analysis was performed using an F2 population (V22-150, 104 individuals). Double-flower and single-flower individuals from the F2 population were separately bulked, and whole-genome sequencing was conducted along with the double-flower parent and the F1 plant. The obtained reads were aligned to the C. roseus reference genome sequence, and SNPs at positions that were homozygous in the double-flower parent and heterozygous in the F1 SNPs were called (Fig. S2). Although the alignment to the reference genome was relatively uniform, heterogeneity in SNP density was observed, likely due to numerous regions with high homology between the parental genomes (Fig. S3, Fig. S4, Table S6). For the identified SNPs, the ΔSNP index between the double-flower bulk and the single-flower bulk was calculated and plotted. To assess regional allele frequency shifts rather than variation at individual SNPs, sliding window–averaged ΔSNP-index values were calculated across the genome. A single locus on chromosome 1 showed sliding window–averaged ΔSNP-index values exceeding the 95% confidence interval (Fig. 2, Fig. S4). Although several regions with locally high ΔSNP-index values were observed, none of these regions fell outside the 95% confidence interval of the sliding window–averaged ΔSNP-index. Consequently, the region spanning 60–70 Mb on chromosome 1 was selected as a candidate region. For three SNPs located around 60–70 Mb on chromosome 1 (SNP9, SNP11, and SNP12), all F2 individuals were genotyped using the PACE genotyping system. Comparison with the phenotypes narrowed the candidate causal region to between SNP11 and SNP12 (69.0–70.3 Mb) (Table S7). Furthermore, in 12 individuals showing recombination between SNP11 and SNP12, genotypes of two additional SNPs (SNP11-1 and SNP11-2) located between SNP11 and SNP12 were determined. By comparing these genotypes with the phenotype, the candidate causal locus was further narrowed down to a 650 kb region between SNP11 and SNP11-2 (69.0–69.6 Mb) (Fig. 2). The association between this locus and the phenotype is consistent with the observed 3:1 Mendelian segregation pattern. This region contains 82 genes, of which 10 were predicted by SnpEff to harbor potentially functional mutations (Table S8). Among them, only M9H77_05480 was predicted to have a disruptive effect (classified as “HIGH” impact by SnpEff). M9H77_05480 had a single-nucleotide deletion at position 91 bp, causing a frameshift mutation in the double-flower lines (Fig. 3A). BLAST searches identified M9H77_05480 as an MYB transcription factor orthologous to PHAN in Antirrhinum and tomato and AS1 in Arabidopsis. This gene was designated PHANTASTICA-like (CrPHAL), and the mutant allele harboring the frameshift was named CrPHALm1.

To validate the association, a PACE marker detecting the single-base deletion was applied to 104 individuals of the V22-150 F₂ population. Among these, 20 single-flower plants were homozygous for the wild-type allele and 61 were heterozygous; as well, all 23 double-flower plants were homozygous for CrPHALm1 (Fig. S5, Table S4). The concordance observed between genotype and phenotype suggests that contributions from other genomic regions to the double-flower trait in C. roseus are limited, supporting that CrPHAL is as a primary determinant of this phenotype.

Sequence analysis of CrPHAL

Based on cDNA sequence analysis of CrPHAL transcripts from the mutant line, a frameshift mutation caused by the deletion of a thymine nucleotide at position 91 bp was identified in CrPHALm1. This mutation introduces a stop codon at amino acid position 61, located within the R2 HTH MYB-type domain (1–171 bp), which are the major functional domains of CrPHAL. Consequently, normal protein translation is inhibited, suggesting that CrPHALm1 represents a loss-of-function mutation. Therefore, while the wild-type CrPHAL encodes a 397-amino-acid protein, CrPHALm1 encodes only a truncated protein of 61 amino acids (Fig. 3A).

Analysis of CrPHAL in commercial double-flower cultivars

Commercial double-flower cultivars were first screened using PACE markers targeting the CrPHALm1 deletion. ‘Sunny Princess Pink’, ‘Sunny Princess Blue’, and ‘Sunny Princess White’ (Suntory Flowers Ltd.), as well as ‘Mira Cherry Pink’ (Kitajima Engei), carried the same deletion identified in CrPHALm1. In contrast, no mutation was detected in ‘Souryou’ (Sakata Seed) or ‘Solaris’ (Hanabiyori) using the CrPHALm1-specific marker. To determine whether alternative mutations were present in CrPHAL, full-length cDNA sequences were analyzed in these cultivars. Sequencing revealed nonsense mutations at position 13 bp in both cultivars, designated as CrPHALm2 (Table S9 and Fig. 3A). These mutations introduce premature stop codons and are predicted to generate severely truncated proteins, consistent with loss of function. Collectively, these results indicate that independent loss-of-function alleles of CrPHAL underlie the double-flower phenotype in commercial cultivars.

Characterization of C. roseus PHAN-like genes

Genome database analysis identified a second PHAN-like homolog, annotated as M9H77_03614, in addition to CrPHAL. Subsequent phylogenetic analysis revealed that M9H77_03614 clustered more closely with canonical PHAN genes than with CrPHAL. Based on this relationship, the gene was designated CrPHAN. CrPHAL encodes 397 aa and CrPHAN encodes 350 aa, sharing 48% sequence identity. Compared to CrPHAN, CrPHAL contained insertions of 17, 18, and 18 amino acids at positions 124, 189, and 362 aa, respectively, downstream of the R2R3 MYB-type HTH DNA-binding domain (Fig. 3B). Although the double-flower C. roseus lines carry loss-of-function mutations in CrPHAL (e.g., CrPHALm1 and CrPHALm2), no apparent loss-of-function mutations, such as frameshift or nonsense mutations in CrPHAN compared with the single-flower variety.

Phylogenetic analysis of PHAN-like genes

Phylogenetic analysis of the PHAN-like sequences revealed that CrPHAL and CrPHAN are classified into different clades (Fig. 4). The PHAL clade and its homologs encompass other genera in the family Apocynaceae, and Antirrhinum and Nicotiana genus, whereas the PHAN clade contains CrPHAN and its homologs, including Arabidopsis AS1, Antirrhinum PHAN, Nicotiana NtPHAN, tomato LePHAN, and RS2 homologs of maize and rice, which are known to play crucial roles in establishing leaf adaxial–abaxial polarity and morphogenesis. These results indicate that species of Apocynaceae, as well as Nicotiana and Antirrhinum, possess two PHAN homologs, one belonging to PHAL clade and the other to PHAN clade. This suggests that Arabidopsis, tomato, maize and wheat have one PHAN gene, on the other hand some plant species may possess two copies of the PHAN gene.

Expression of CrPHAL and CrPHAN in young leaves and flower buds

We investigated the expression patterns of CrPHAL and CrPHAN in C. roseus lines representing each genotype (D/D, S/D, and S/S) (Fig. 5). Two independent lines were analyzed for each genotype, and qRT-PCR was performed in triplicate for each sample. Expression of both genes was detected in young leaves and flower buds at the St1 and St2 across all genotypes. For both CrPHAL and CrPHAN, expression levels tended to be higher in flower buds than in young leaves. In flower buds, both genes showed significantly higher expression levels in the double-flower genotype (D/D) compared to the single-flower genotypes (S/D and S/S).

Phenotypic analysis of the Arabidopsis thaliana as1 mutant

Temperature changes have been shown to express cryptic developmental phenotypes in multiple regulatory genes such as PHAN35–38. Based on these findings, we examined whether A. thaliana as1 mutant might display a double-flower phenotype under different growth temperatures. To evaluate the functional conservation of PHAN homologs, A. thaliana AS1 and loss-of-function mutants (as1, CS146, and CS3374) were analyzed. Loss-of-function as1 mutants (CS146, CS3374) were examined at 12℃, 22℃, and 32℃. Unlike CrPHALm1 mutants, as1 mutants did not develop additional petals at any of the temperatures tested. At 12℃, a slight but nonsignificant reduction in petal number was observed (Fig. S6A-C, Table S10). The number of stamens tended to be reduced in both mutants, with CS146 showing a significant reduction at 32℃. The number of sepals and pistils did not differ significantly from what was seen in the wild-type. This suggests that AS1 is not involved in regulating petal number in A. thaliana, or that its potential effects remain suppressed even under altered temperature conditions35–38. As previously reported, as1 mutants exhibited reduced leaf blade development, resulting in smaller leaves (Fig. S6D-F)39.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the phenotype, inheritance, and molecular basis of double- flowering in C. roseus. QTL analysis of segregating F₂ populations revealed that double-flower plants consistently carried a single-base deletion immediately downstream of the CrPHAL start codon, designated CrPHALm1, which likely causes loss of function. The PACE marker for CrPHALm1 co-segregated perfectly with the double-flower phenotype, supporting its involvement in trait determination. This study demonstrated that PHAL was associated with for the double-flower trait in C. roseus. Since the increase in petal number in C. roseus occurs without homeotic conversion of floral organs, this phenotype can’t be readily explained by the classical ABC model alone. Therefore, our results suggest a different molecular basis for double flowering that could contribute to the diversification of floral forms in ornamental breeding.

Double-flower C. roseus plants exhibit reduced leaf elongation, leading to leaf twisting from the juvenile to adult stages18. Similar phenotypes have been reported in PHAN-deficient mutants of Arabidopsis, Antirrhinum, tomato, maize, and Nicotiana sylvestris, where suppression of PHAN function resulted in altered leaf morphology and polarity39–46. In Antirrhinum and tomato, PHAN maintains the adaxial polarity of the leaf primordia, and its loss leads to abaxialization42,46. In contrast, N. sylvestris antisense NSPHAN lines retain initial adaxial–abaxial polarity but exhibit delayed palisade development and disrupted cell division in the adaxial mesophyll, suggesting a role in leaf blade maturation rather than polarity establishment41. Arabidopsis as1 mutants develop shorter, twisted leaves39, and tomato antisense SlPHAN lines display twisted leaves with rough surfaces and knobby protrusions47. Regarding floral organ morphology, it has been reported that in tomato antisense SlPHAN line, the number of petals increases while the number of stamens decreases47. Similarly, in the 35 S-LeT6 line overexpressing KNOX, a gene negatively regulated by PHAN, an increase in petal number and decrease in stamen number were also observed48. These findings suggest that the suppression of PHAN in tomato leads to KNOX overexpression, indicating its potential involvement in increasing petal number. In Antirrhinum, dominant mutants Hirzina-d153 and Invaginata-d1 display ectopic expression of KNOX due to transposon insertions, leading to spur-like floral structure49. The phenotype of the loss of function of CrPHAL in C. roseus exhibited additional petals and characteristic leaf twisting, similar to PHAN suppression and KNOX overexpression in tomatoes, but did not show a reduction in stamen number observed in those cases.

Analysis of the commercial cultivars revealed an additional allele, CrPHALm2, carrying a nonsense mutation 13 bp downstream of the initiation codon. Similar to CrPHALm1, this mutation was associated with double-flowering and leaf twisting but not stamen reduction. This finding further supports that the loss of CrPHAL function underlies double-flowering in C. roseus. The results indicate that CrPHAL in C. roseus shares partially overlapping functions with AS1 and PHAN in Arabidopsis, Antirrhinum, tomato, and Nicotiana. However, CrPHAL may also have distinct roles from those previously reported, as the loss of CrPHAL causes the formation of additional petals without alterations in stamen development.

Phylogenetic analysis indicated that C. roseus possesses two PHAN homologs, CrPHAL and CrPHAN, whereas Arabidopsis, maize, and tomato contain only one. CrPHAN clustered with AS1, PHAN, RS2, NtPHAN, and LePHAN, which regulate leaf polarity and growth, whereas CrPHAL grouped in a clade distinct from these genes. Although functional data for this PHAL clade are lacking, the conservation of PHAN–KNOX regulatory modules across Lycophyta and Monilophyta50 suggests that PHAL may contribute to the interaction and regulation of KNOX.

Expression analysis in young leaves and flower buds of C. roseus showed that CrPHAL and CrPHAN were expressed in all materials examined, regardless of genotype. Notably, in flower buds, lines exhibiting the double-flower phenotype (D/D) tended to show higher expression levels of both CrPHAL and CrPHAN compared to single-flower lines (S/D and S/S). In the D/D genotype, CrPHAL does not produce a functional protein due to the frameshift mutation, yet the transcription of CrPHAL and CrPHAN appears to be maintained. In contrast, in the S/D and S/S genotypes, these genes may be subject to negative feedback regulation by normal CrPHAL protein during floral development. However, the underlying regulatory mechanisms for these expression differences remain unclear. Loss-of-function mutations in PHAN-clade genes such as AS1 in Arabidopsis and PHAN in Antirrhinum caused a significant loss of leaf blade formation, as shown in Fig. S6 and previously reported39,40,46. In contrast, a decrease or loss of the PHAL clade in C. roseus leads to partial inhibition of leaf elongation, resulting in curled leaves which appears less severe from a horticultural perspective. These findings suggest that the PHAN clade CrPHAN plays a key role in leaf blade formation in C. roseus. However, the functions of PHAL clade homologs have not yet been reported in Antirrhinum or Nicotiana. Further analyses of CrPHAN and CrPHAL homologs in other plants are necessary to clarify the functions and relationships between these two types of PHAN homologs. Our initial findings indicate that loss of function of CrPHAL results in additional petal formation and leaf twisting without the loss of stamens in C. roseus, suggesting functional differentiation between CrPHAL and CrPHAN.

PHAN has been reported to influence phenotypes in a temperature-dependent manner35. To determine whether the as1 mutant of A. thaliana exhibits floral phenotypes similar to the extra petal formation observed in C. roseus under different temperature conditions, we examined the flower morphology of as1 plants grown at various temperature ranges. No increase in petal number was detected at 12℃, 22℃, or 32℃. Similarly, increased petal number and strong reduction in stamens observed in the tomato genes Antisense SlPHAN and LeT6 were absent in as147, 48. Leaf abnormalities, such as reduced elongation and twisted morphology, were observed, consistent with those reported in C. roseus PHALm1 and PHALm2 mutants, tomato Antisense SlPHAN47, and Antirrhinum PHAN-deficient lines35,46. These results suggest that, although PHAN homologues share conserved roles in leaf development across species, their functions in floral organ formation have diverged after gene duplication.

In conclusion, C. roseus harbors two PHAN homologs, and functional loss of CrPHAL is associated with double-flowering with additional petals and twisted leaves but does not affect stamen number. In contrast, PHAN suppression in tomatoes and Arabidopsis produces different floral and foliar outcomes, highlighting the lineage-specific diversification of PHAN and PHAL functions. Although the presence of PHAL clade homologs has been suggested in species such as Antirrhinum and Nicotiana, this study provides the first report linking loss of function of a PHAL clade gene to a defined phenotype. The leaf twisting observed in C. roseus due to loss of CrPHAL function was relatively mild compared to the phenotype reported in loss-of-function mutants of PHAN clade genes such as AS1, LePHAN, and NtPHAN. Futhermore, phylogenetic analysis revealed that members of the PHAL clade are absent in some plant species. Taken together, these findings suggest that while CrPHAN, belonging to the PHAN clade, plays a major role in leaf blade growth in C. roseus. Rather, CrPHAL may have a supplementary or partially redundant role, contributing to the robustness of leaf development through functional compensation. Although the precise developmental pathways remain unclear, future analyses of CrPHAL, CrPHAN, and KNOX expression during floral primordia formation, combined with transformation and genome editing approaches, are essential for clarifying their roles. Given the high commercial value of double-flowering, our findings not only elucidate the genetic basis in C. roseus but also suggest that PHAL mutations could be exploited to generate novel ornamental valuable traits in other flowering plants, either alone or in combination with mutations in genetic pathways regulating floral organ identity as described in the ABC model. Difficulty in crossing floral homeotic mutants (such as agamous mutants) often arises because of the absence of reproductive organs. However, in the case of the PHAL mutation, breeding the double-flowering trait is feasible by crossing, increasing the opportunity to develop double-flowering varieties with various flower colors and plant types.

D, double-flower allele; S, single-flower allele.
