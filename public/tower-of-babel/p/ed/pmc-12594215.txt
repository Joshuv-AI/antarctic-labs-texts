Highly Sensitive Field Detection Technology for Anthrax Based on the CRISPR/Cas13a System
Authors: Jiao Fan, Luyao Huang, Jiahui Chen, Yaxuan Jiang, Xinying Du, Ligui Wang, Kanghui Ding, Jun Huang, Shaofu Qiu, Hongbo Liu, Hongbin Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn this study, we established a highly sensitive on‐site detection technology for Bacillus anthracis . Firstly, we integrated Multiple Enzyme Isothermal Rapid Amplification (MIRA) with the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) /associated protein 13a (CRISPR/Cas13a) detection system to develop a highly sensitive CRISPR/Cas13a assay. After testing crRNA selection, MIRA primers, reaction temperature, and CRISPR detection conditions, the CRISPR/Cas13a detection system employing dual crRNAs achieved a detection limit of 1000 copies/mL for B. anthracis . Quantitative analysis was additionally attempted. Compared with other common respiratory pathogens, the assay demonstrated high specificity. In clinically simulated samples, all 20 positive specimens were correctly identified, and all 13 negatives were unambiguously classified as negative. Based on these findings, we established a CRISPR point‐of‐care testing technology. By developing a CRISPR point‐of‐care testing device together with a tested lyophilised reagent system, the device achieved a detection limit of 250 copies/mL and delivered results within 30 min. All positive samples were accurately identified, and every negative sample was classified as negative. Consequently, this study presents a highly sensitive and portable technology for on‐site detection of B. anthracis . It holds significant value for on‐site detection of emerging infectious diseases.

Introduction

Anthrax is an animal‐borne disease caused by B. anthracis , a Gram‐positive Bacillus that can survive for long periods in soil as spores (Moayeri et al. 2015). The epidemiology of anthrax is closely tied to the climate, geography, and animal husbandry practices of an area. Globally, anthrax is most prevalent in the arid and temperate regions of Eurasia, Africa, and North America, particularly where even‐toed ungulates are raised (Deka et al. 2022; Tan et al. 2022). The oedema factor encoded by the CYA gene in B. anthracis is an exotoxin that requires calmodulin for activation to stimulate adenylyl cyclase, disrupting the host cell signal transduction pathways (Collier 2009; Drum et al. 2002). Consequently, the detection of CYA is crucial for preventing and controlling anthrax (Goncharova et al. 2021).

In nucleic acid detection, quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) is the most widely used method. However, its sensitivity has yet to fully meet the demands of on‐site diagnostics. It requires lengthy sample preparation and dependence on bulky instruments, rendering it unsuitable for field use or resource‐limited settings. (Matero et al. 2011). The clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats and their associated proteins (CRISPR‐Cas) system, a novel tool for gene editing and nucleic acid detection, has gained attention for its high specificity and sensitivity in infectious disease prevention and control (Abbott et al. 2020). In the CRISPR/Cas13a system, Cas13a is an RNA‐guided ribonuclease. Upon forming a complex with its cognate CRISPR RNA (crRNA), Cas13a scans for and binds complementary target RNA sequences. This target recognition induces a conformational change within the Cas13a protein, activating its collateral cleavage activity. Once activated, Cas13a non‐specifically cleaves any nearby single‐stranded RNA (ssRNA) molecules present in the reaction mixture. The reporter ssRNA probe is dual‐labelled: one terminus carries a fluorophore, while the other carries a quencher molecule. When the Cas13a‐crRNA‐target complex is formed and collateral cleavage is activated, the reporter probe is cleaved. This cleavage physically separates the fluorophore from the quencher, resulting in a detectable fluorescent signal. This mechanism forms the basis of our highly sensitive detection approach (Gootenberg et al. 2017). Existing CRISPR/Cas12 systems that couple RPA with B. anthracis detection can achieve single‐copy sensitivity only at the cost of prolonged assay times or labour‐intensive workflows, making them unsuitable for field deployment (Lyu et al. 2022; Patnaik et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2022). Our study focuses on developing a highly sensitive yet field‐ready platform for the detection of B. anthracis.

Accurate nucleic acid quantification is essential for the detection of many pathogens. qPCR, recognised as the gold standard for nucleic acid quantification, primarily relies on internal controls to quantify pathogen nucleic acids (Vandenberg et al. 2021). However, in the standard curve method, the preparation of standards lacks uniformity, and nucleic acid standards are prone to degradation. Additionally, preparing new standards introduces errors into the quantitative analysis. When testing samples, the need to measure internal controls simultaneously reduces the number of samples that can be processed in a single batch, thereby decreasing the efficiency of high‐throughput detection. Quantitative analysis using the CRISPR/Cas system has gained significant importance (Shen et al. 2023).

Point‐of‐care testing combined with lyophilisation technology is suitable for single‐person field detection in remote areas. Lyophilisation significantly extends the shelf life of the reagents (Qu et al. 2010; Rybnicky et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2023). It offers rapid and convenient testing without the need to send samples back to the laboratory, thereby avoiding losses and contamination during transportation and enabling faster results. In the field of on‐site detection, amplification‐free CRISPR assays integrated with chip‐based electrochemical readouts have been reported, yet their portability remains limited (Liu et al. 2021). Visual detection offers a convenient readout, but the results are highly subjective and easily affected by ambient light (de Puig et al. 2021). When small portable devices are employed, smartphone‐assisted interpretation is required, which reduces operational convenience in the field (Wang et al. 2024). To overcome these limitations, we have developed a CRISPR‐based portable platform that enables truly equipment‐free detection under field conditions.

In this study, we developed a highly sensitive CRISPR‐based assay targeting the CYA gene of B. anthracis . By testing the MIRA isothermal amplification and CRISPR/Cas systems, we explored the quantitative analysis of pathogen nucleic acids. Based on this foundation, we established a CRISPR point‐of‐care testing technology that integrates a portable, rapid‐response reader with a lyophilised CRISPR reagent system, improving the limit of the CRISPR point‐of‐care testing technology and reducing reliance on large instruments, thereby providing direction for on‐site pathogen nucleic acid diagnostics.

Materials and Methods

Design and Screening of crRNA and Primers

Downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/), 43 full‐length sequences of B. anthracis (2403 bp) were analysed using the MEGA7 software (www.megasoftware.net/show_eua). Following alignment, a 704 bp conserved sequence from B. anthracis (GenBank: MW198516.1, position: 1283–1987) was chosen. The conserved sequences of the CYA gene were separately constructed into the pUC57 plasmids as positive controls (Tianyihuiyuan Biotechnology Co. Ltd.). Detailed protocols for the screening of crRNA and primers, including reagent formulations and instrument settings, are provided in Supporting Information S1 (Section S.1).

Establishment of CRISPR/Cas13a Fluorescence Detection System

The CRISPR/Cas13a trans‐cleavage fluorescence detection was conducted in a 25 μL reaction system. 100 nM Cas13a protein, 12 ng/μL crRNA, 2 mM rNTP Mix, 800 nM Cas13a report probe, 1× Cas13a 10× buffer, 10 mM Taurine, 15 U/μL T7 RNA Polymerase, 8 U/μL RNase inhibitor, 20 mM MgCl2, 10 μL of MIRA amplification product, nuclease‐free water to a final volume of 25 μL. The reaction, mediated by CRISPR/Cas13a, was incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Fluorescence signals were detected every 2 min using an ABI 7500 (Applied Biosystems) instrument.

Evaluation of the Detection Limit for Dual‐crRNAs CRISPR/Cas13a System

The CYA gene recombinant plasmid was used as the amplification template and diluted with DEPC water to five different concentrations: 106, 105, 104, 103, 500, and 100 copies/mL. Subsequently, MIRA amplification, CRISPR/Cas13a fluorescence detection, and immunochromatographic test strip evaluation were performed on these dilutions.

Specificity Evaluation of CRISPR/Cas13a

MIRA isothermal amplification of standard samples of B. anthracis and Haemophilus influenzae, Bacillus subtilis, Bacillus thuringiensis, Bacillus cereus, and Bacillus licheniformis performs CRISPR/Cas13a fluorescence detection and immunochromatographic strip evaluation.

qPCR Detection

The experiments were performed using the B. anthracis qPCR kit (TianJinSha Biotechnology Co. Ltd.). Detailed protocols for the screening of qPCR detection, including reagent formulations and instrument settings, are provided in Supporting Information S1 (Section S.2).

Simulated Sample Preparation

The plasmid was diluted to 104 copies/mL and mixed with human throat‐swab material; nucleic acids were then extracted to serve as positive simulated samples. In total, 33 simulated samples were prepared: 20 positives were generated by adding the extracted nucleic acid, and 13 negatives were generated by adding nuclease‐free water.

Optimisation of CRISPR/Cas13a Detection System

Detection was performed using tested MIRA temperature and time parameters along with CRISPR system concentrations. Detailed protocols for the testing of the CRISPR/Cas13a detection system, including reagent formulations and instrument settings, are provided in Supporting Information S1 (Section S.3).

Establishment of CRISPR Quantitative Technology

The positive plasmid of the B. anthracis CYA gene was diluted in a gradient to concentrations of 108, 107, 106, 105, 104, and 103 copies/mL. By calculating the linear relationship between the different gradient concentrations of the positive plasmid and the CRISPR‐fluorescence values and qPCR, quantitative analysis of the CRISPR.

Lyophilisation Technology Development

The CRISPR detection system was lyophilised using 5% mannitol, 5% sucrose, 2.5% pullulan, and 0.1 M HEPES (Bao et al. 2023). Detailed protocols for the testing of lyophilisation technology, including reagent formulations and instrument settings, are provided in Supporting Information S1 (Section S.4).

CRISPR‐Point‐of‐Care Testing

The basic CRISPR‐point‐of‐care testing consists of two components: a portable point‐of‐care testing device and a microchip. Based on the selected MIRA amplification primers and crRNA, MIRA freeze‐dried beads and CRISPR detection freeze‐dried beads were prepared. The CRISPR point‐of‐care testing device detection limit was evaluated, and the detection time was assessed. The specificity was evaluated using standard control samples of B. anthracis , H. influenzae, B. subtilis, B. thuringiensis, B. cereus, and B. licheniformis . Stability was assessed using low‐concentration positive plasmids. Nucleic acids were extracted from clinical simulated samples and subjected to qPCR detection. The same nucleic acids were also tested on the CRISPR point‐of‐care testing device. Real samples were evaluated based on the colour of the indicator lights on the on‐site detection device, and the nucleic acids were further tested by qPCR.

Results

Sequence Alignment

A total of 43 B. anthracis CYA gene sequences were downloaded from the Nucleotide database of the NCBI (Figure S1). Additionally, one full‐length reference genome sequence from each of the five Bacillus species was obtained (Figure S2). These sequences were aligned using MEGA7 software to identify conserved regions within the B. anthracis CYA gene. The full genome sequence of B. anthracis (GenBank: M24074.1) served as the reference genome. The conserved CYA gene sequence was then inserted into the pUC57 plasmid vector to construct a recombinant plasmid. By further aligning the full genome sequences of the five Bacillus species with the B. anthracis reference genome, we identified specific sequences unique to the B. anthracis CYA gene.

Design and Screening of crRNA and Primers

Eleven crRNAs with detection of the conserved sequences of the B. anthracis CYA gene (Table S1). Corresponding crRNAs were designed and transcribed, then purified. Six forward primers and six reverse primers were designed from the conserved region of the CYA gene (Table S2). MIRA amplification was performed using a plasmid containing the CYA gene at a concentration of 105 copies/mL, and 2% agarose‐gel electrophoresis revealed that primer pair F4/R3 (lane 19) yielded the highest amplification efficiency (Figure S3A,B). When the F4/R3 amplicon was used to test 11 crRNAs CRISPR‐fluorescence assay, crRNA4 reached the highest fluorescence signal within 30 min (Figure 1A,B). Non‐overlapping, high‐fluorescence crRNA4, crRNA8, crRNA9, and crRNA11 were selected for multiplexed dual‐crRNA evaluation. The dual‐crRNA set comprising crRNA4 and crRNA9 exhibited the best performance (Figure 1C,D).

Optimisation of the CRISPR System

Based on the CRISPR/Cas13a fluorescence signal, the optimal amplification temperature was found to be 37°C, and the optimal amplification time was 40 min (Figure S4C,D). Methodologically optimal CRISPR/Cas13a detection conditions were a final concentration of 100 nM Cas13a protein, 12 ng/μL crRNA, and 20 nM MgCl2 (Figure S4E–G). Among three designed Cas13a fluorescent probes (Table S3), probe 3 yielded the strongest fluorescence signal for detecting the B. anthracis CYA gene and was selected as the fluorescence detection probe (Figure S4A). For the CRISPR/Cas13a‐based immunochromatographic strip, Cas13a test strip probe 2 showed colour at both the T line and the C line and was selected as the detection probe (Figure S4B).

The Lower Detection Limit of the Tested CRISPR/Cas13a System

Gradient dilutions of B. anthracis plasmids were used as amplification templates. Using primer F4/R3, the combination of crRNA4 and crRNA9 was used for Cas13a fluorescence detection; a distinguishable signal was observed at 1000 copies/mL, and a detectable signal was observed at 500 copies/mL, but with markedly larger variability (Figure 2A,B). In the Cas13a immunochromatographic test paper assay, both the T line and C1 line showed colour at 1000 copies/mL. At 500 copies/mL, the T line and C1 line also showed colour, but with lighter intensity (Figure 2C).

Specificity of CRISPR/Cas13a for CYA Detection

We performed CRISPR‐fluorescence detection using 105 copies/mL of the B. anthracis plasmids and other bacterial strains, including H. influenzae , B. subtilis, B. thuringiensis, B. cereus , and B. licheniformis . The results demonstrated that the fluorescence signal for B. anthracis was significantly higher than that for the other pathogens (Figure 3A,B). Using an immunochromatographic strip for the specific evaluation, it was observed that only the T line and C line of the test paper showed colour for B. anthracis (Figure 3C).

Lyophilisation Technology Development

Based on the CRISPR/Cas13a fluorescence signal, the concentrations were 40 nM for Cas13a protein, 3 U/μL for the probe, and 1.28 μM for T7 RNA polymerase (Figure S5A–C). The established system was prepared as lyophilised beads and in situ lyophilised beads for the evaluation of lyophilisation effects. The CRISPR lyophilised beads showed fluorescence signals close to those of the liquid system, but further validation is needed to assess the performance of the lyophilised system over longer storage periods. In contrast, the CRISPR in situ lyophilised beads exhibited reduced activity compared to the liquid system. Future work will consider outsourcing the preparation of lyophilised beads to a third‐party company (Figure S5D–G).

Development of Portable CRISPR On‐Site Detection Technology

According to the CRISPR‐point‐of‐care testing workflow diagram (Figure 6A). The fundamental principle involves that when the MIRA amplification product button is pressed, the sample is pushed by the internal pressure of the chip into the front end, where the CRISPR freeze‐dried beads are located. The CRISPR/Cas13a fluorescence reaction specifically recognises the pathogen sequence, causing the free fluorescent probes to be non‐specifically cleaved, thereby generating a fluorescent signal. After the CRISPR‐point‐of‐care testing device acquires the raw fluorescent signal, the filtered fluorescent signal passes through an optical filter and is received by a sensor located at the bottom of the device, which then amplifies the signal. The detection result is reported by an indicator light based on the increase in fluorescence intensity over a given period. The positive plasmid caused the device indicator light to turn red, while the negative control caused the indicator light to turn green. The MIRA lyophilised beads and CRISPR detection lyophilised beads were assembled into the CRISPR point‐of‐care testing chip. It was found that adding the positive plasmid caused the indicator light to turn red, while adding enzyme‐free water caused the indicator light to turn green (Figure 6B–C). The positive plasmid was diluted to concentrations ranging from 108 to 500 copies/mL. The CRISPR point‐of‐care testing device achieved a detection limit of 250 copies/mL—substantially lower than the 1000 copies/mL reported for fluorescence‐based assays. We attribute this heightened sensitivity to two factors. First, the CRISPR reaction mixture in the cartridge was tested by lyophilisation, preserving maximal enzymatic activity. Second, the device's algorithm classifies a sample as positive whenever its fluorescence exceeds the preset threshold at any time during the 30‐min read window, rather than relying solely on an endpoint measurement. In standard qPCR, positivity is defined by a statistically significant difference between the endpoint fluorescence of the sample and that of the negative control after a prolonged, stable reaction. By detecting any threshold crossing within 30 min, the portable platform effectively lowers its limit of detection compared with conventional qPCR instrumentation.

CRISPR‐point‐of‐care testing detection results. (A) Principle and workflow of the CRISPR‐point‐of‐care testing; (B) Results of the CRISPR‐point‐of‐care testing device using a liquid system; (C) Results of the CRISPR‐point‐of‐care testing device using lyophilised beads.

The positive plasmid was diluted to three concentration levels (high, medium, and low). The MIRA isothermal amplification lyophilised beads were reacted for 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 min, respectively. The shortest detection cycle was found to be 30 min. When compared with five other pathogens, good specificity was observed. The required number of simulated samples was calculated to be greater than 26. Therefore, 33 samples were selected for stability evaluation, and good stability was demonstrated. An evaluation of 29 simulated samples showed consistent detection results with qPCR (Figure S6A–E; Table S4).

Discussion

B. anthracis is valued as a biological warfare agent for the presence of lethal strains, the ability of spores to survive for long periods at low temperatures, and the ease of transmission of nucleic acids (Pohanka 2020). The anthrax nucleic acid detection method is mainly qPCR. The CRISPR/Cas13a system has been used for the detection of various pathogens, such as SARS‐CoV‐2 (Gootenberg et al. 2017; Fozouni et al. 2021). In this study, we successfully developed a CRISPR/Cas13a‐based assay for the detection of the B. anthracis CYA gene. By employing dual‐crRNA to enhance sensitivity, we achieved a limit of detection of 1000 copies/mL in both CRISPR‐fluorescence and CRISPR‐lateral‐flow formats. Our CRISPR approach significantly outperforms the previously reported qPCR method targeting the B. anthracis 16S gene (Braun et al. 2021), and markedly reduces dependence on sophisticated laboratory infrastructure (Tian et al. 2022).

By testing the CRISPR/Cas13a detection system, we explored its potential for quantitative analysis. In the tested platform, both the CRISPR‐generated fluorescence signal and the qPCR signal exhibited a linear relationship with the concentration of the B. anthracis plasmid. Comparative analysis revealed a linear correlation between the CRISPR fluorescence and qPCR signals, demonstrating that the CRISPR/Cas13a platform possesses quantitative capabilities comparable to qPCR.

Although conventional qPCR is regarded as the gold standard for quantification, its reliance on internal controls can introduce variability in results. Recent studies have integrated aptamer‐based molecular recognition with the CRISPR‐Cas12a system for AFP quantification (Jia et al. 2023). Yet non‐specific binding between aptamers and ssRNA can compromise accuracy. Our approach eliminates both the need for internal references and the risk of non‐specific artefacts, thereby offering a promising alternative for quantitative CRISPR‐based analysis.

Based on the highly sensitive CRISPR/Cas13a system, we investigated its potential for portable on‐site diagnostics. By integrating freeze‐drying technology, we significantly enhanced the field applicability and thermal stability of the CRISPR/Cas13a platform. Through the optimisation and lyophilisation of the MIRA and CRISPR/Cas13a assays into single‐dose microspheres, we eliminated the need for cold‐chain storage and mitigated enzyme activity loss caused by repeated freeze–thaw cycles. This innovation ensures room‐temperature stability during transport and storage, enabling sensitive, field‐deployable testing without performance compromise—an advantage over conventional liquid CRISPR reagents and complex qPCR master mixes.

We leveraged a CRISPR/Cas13a‐based ultrasensitive detection system to develop a CRISPR point‐of‐care testing technology. The device applies dual‐channel microchip houses lyophilised MIRA microspheres and CRISPR detection microspheres in separate but connected compartments. The user simply adds extracted nucleic acid to the chip's ‘large inlet’, presses a button to drive the sample into the reaction chambers, and reads the colour‐coded indicator lights for a clear, visual result. The dual‐channel layout allows an on‐chip parallel replicate, enhancing accuracy, while the light‐based readout minimises the subjective errors common in other visual assays. In our study, the point‐of‐care device achieved a limit of detection of 250 copies/mL and successfully detected clinical mock samples with CT values ranging from 20 to 40. Testing of 29 mock samples showed 100% concordance with qPCR. The total assay time is only 30 min. Compared with traditional amplification‐free point‐of‐care testing methods and qPCR, our system eliminates dependence on laboratory infrastructure and electrochemical instrumentation (Tian et al. 2022; Hu et al. 2022), offering a versatile platform for on‐site control of infectious diseases—simply by swapping MIRA primers and crRNA to target new pathogens.

Applicability of CRISPR‐based field diagnostics. First, the technology is economically viable: simply by swapping primers and crRNAs, it can be rapidly redeployed against diverse pathogens, ensuring scalability and driving unit costs down steeply with increased production volumes. Second, the POCT device replaces costly PCR thermocyclers with inexpensive resistive micro‐heaters, markedly reducing capital expenditure relative to qPCR. Finally, room‐temperature‐stable lyophilised reagents eliminate the logistical burden and expense of cold‐chain distribution, substantially lowering the total cost of ownership. This combination of performance, adaptability, and affordability positions the platform to deliver transformative impact across a wide range of settings.

CRISPR‐point‐of‐care testing technology still faces certain limitations. Currently, the device can only detect a single pathogen and cannot achieve the detection of multiple pathogens. In the future, it will still be necessary to continuously explore devices for detecting multiple pathogens to meet the detection needs in complex environments. The portable on‐site detection device still requires manual nucleic acid extraction by operators in the field. In the future, we plan to develop a portable nucleic acid extraction device. After field staff collect swabs or blood samples, they can add them to a small nucleic acid extraction device to obtain nucleic acids that match the CRISPR‐point‐of‐care testing device. The extracted nucleic acids can be directly added to the large well of the device to achieve an integrated small portable device for sample processing, amplification, and detection. This will further enhance the usability of the device and increase the application value of the technology in actual public health events.

Conclusion

Our study established a highly sensitive CRISPR/Cas13a detection system based on the CYA gene of B. anthracis. First, the detection limits for dual‐crRNAs fluorescence and immunochromatographic strip tests for B. anthracis were 1000 copies/mL, lower than the detection limits of qPCR. By testing the MIRA amplification and CRISPR detection processes, we developed quantitative techniques for B. anthracis . Second, leveraging a CRISPR‐based ultrasensitive assay, we established a CRISPR point‐of‐care testing platform using lyophilised CRISPR microspheres and a portable device. It enhances the on‐site adaptability of CRISPR/Cas detection, providing a rapid and portable method for on‐site pathogen nucleic acid detection and an effective approach for the prevention and control of infectious diseases.
