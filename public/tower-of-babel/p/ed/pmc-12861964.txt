Developmental, normal, and abnormal anatomy of the coronary sinus
Authors: Robert H Anderson, Diane E Spicer, Damian Sanchez-Quintana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe coronary sinus is the persisting remnant of the left horn of the developing systemic venous sinus. During the fifth week of human development, the channel is incorporated, with its own walls, into the left atrioventricular groove. Sprouts from the newly formed sinus then provide the basis for formation of the great, middle, and small cardiac veins.Postnatally, the sinus provides drainage of the greater part of the venous return from the heart. The left superior caval vein itself largely regresses, with only its terminal part persisting as the oblique vein of the left atrium. Union of the oblique vein and the great cardiac vein serves to mark the distal extent of the sinus, with its proximal boundary formed at its entrance to the right atrium, guarded by the Thebesian valve. The great cardiac vein itself forms the base of the left ventricular summit, with this area initially described as a triangle by Brocq and Mouchet. In the setting of congenital persistence of the left superior caval vein, the sinus, and its right atrial orifice, are usually dilated. An imperforate Thebesian valve produces atresia of the sinus, with the persisting left superior caval vein then providing a conduit for retrograde passage of cardiac venous return to the right atrium. Rarer malformations are so-called "unroofing" of the sinus, and aneurysmal dilation to form diverticulums in the inferior wall of the ventricular mass. The sinus can also be abnormal, or absent, in the setting of isomerism.Keywords: Left sinus horn, Vein of Marshall, Great cardiac vein, Left ventricular summit, Left atrioventricular junction, Left superior caval vein

Introduction

The coronary venous sinus, not to be confused with the two aortic valvar sinuses which give rise to the right and left coronary arteries, is the channel which, in the normal heart, collects the majority of the venous return from the walls of the cardiac chambers and returns it to the cavity of the right atrium [1]. The channel is of significance to electrophysiologists as a conduit to the left atrioventricular junction, either for left ventricular pacing [2], or as an alternative route to ablate accessory atrioventricular pathways [3]. Its continuation as the great cardiac vein provides direct access to the summit of the left ventricular myocardial cone [4], increasing its clinical importance. In this review, we provide an account of its development and its normal anatomy, offering also a brief review of the changes observed when the channel, either as a solitary entity or duplicated, is present in congenitally malformed hearts.

Development of the coronary sinus

At the end of the fourth week of post-conceptual human development, recognised as stage 10 in the series of phases of development established by those working at the Carnegie Institute, the developing heart is a straight tube, with symmetrical venous tributaries entering caudally, and paired arterial trunks exiting cranially [5]. At the next stage of development, with each stage approximating to two days of development, the caudal connections of the symmetrical venous tributaries can be recognised as the hepato-cardiac channels. By Carnegie stage 12, when the embryo is about 30 days old, the venous tributaries still retain their right-left symmetry. It is now also possible to recognise that, on each side, separate veins bring back the blood to the heart from the yolk sac, as the vitelline veins, from the placenta as the umbilical veins, and from the body of the embryo itself as the cardinal veins. Each set of veins, furthermore, has joined to form bilateral conduits well described as the horns of the systemic venous sinus, the latter manifold also known as the sinus venosus (Fig. 1A). Within the next two days of developing, during the thirteenth stage in the series established by the Carnegie Institute, the left sinus horn has undergone a remarkable change. Remaining as a conduit draining the left vitelline, umbilical, and cardinal veins, the channel has lost its direct communication with the cavity of the left half of the developing atrial component of the primary heart tube. Instead, along with the right sinus horn, it has been remodelled so as to open exclusively into the right half of the cavity of the developing atrial component, which can now be recognised as the morphologically right atrium. The change occurs concomitantly with the formation of collateral channels that serve to shift the majority of venous return, including the returns from the vitelline and umbilical plexuses, from the left to the right side of the embryo. Similar changes occur within the cardinal venous systems such that the venous returns from the embryo itself are directed exclusively to the developing right atrium. With these changes, the left sinus horn, retaining its own discrete walls, has become incorporated within the junction developing between the left atrium and the ventricular mass (Fig. 2). With continuing growth over the subsequent weeks of embryonic life, with the left sinus horn now incorporated within the left atrioventricular groove as the coronary sinus, endothelial sprouts extend from its cavity. They will become the major venous tributaries bringing back the blood from the greater parts of the walls of the cardiac chambers to the cavity of the right atrium. The first tributary formed in this fashion is the middle cardiac vein, which occupies the developing inferior interventricular groove [6,7]. This is followed by formation of the great cardiac vein, in the anterior interventricular groove, and the small cardiac vein in the right atrioventricular groove (Fig. 3A). When first seen, the developing veins are no more than endothelial tubes. They can be recognised in the human heart by Carnegie stage 17. By Carnegie stage 22, which is very close to the end of the embryonic period, with the embryo itself in the eighth week of development, the veins have developed their smooth muscle coverings. During the same period, the greater part of the left superior caval vein, which was formed from the left cranial cardinal vein, loses its lumen and atrophies, becoming ligamentous. Its proximal patent component can then be recognised as the coronary sinus in the left atrioventricular groove. Subsequent to the atrophy of its distal extrapericardial component, a component of the intrapericardial part retains its patency as the oblique vein of the left atrium, also known as the vein of Marshall [8]. The site of union between the oblique vein and the great cardiac vein then serves to identify the distal extent of the coronary sinus [9]. Others, however, use the presence within the venous conduit thus formed with the great cardiac vein of a prominent venous valve, the valve of Vieussens, as the marker of the distal extent of the coronary sinus [10]. The proximal boundary of the sinus, at its entrance to the right atrium, is usually marked by another prominent valve. This is the Thebesian valve. It is the persistent inferior part of the embryonic right venous valve. The right and left venous valves themselves are first seen as the right and left sinus horns become incorporated into the developing morphologically right atrium during Carnegie stage 13 (Fig. 2A). The cranial part of the right venous valve, which guards the right atrial opening of the inferior caval vein, persists to limited extent as the Eustachian valve, (Fig. 3B). The caudal part, which guards the orifice of the coronary sinus (Fig. 3B), initially formed the lateral boundary of the left sinus horn as it was incorporated, as part of the systemic venous sinus, into the morphologically right atrium (Fig. 3). The cranial part of the embryonic left valve of the systemic venous sinus can sometimes be recognised in the human heart as fenestrated remnants on the surface of the oval fossa. The caudal part usually disappears in its entirety. As we will show when discussing congenital malformations, this part of the embryonic valvar system may persist should the Thebesian valve itself be imperforate, blocking the entrance of the coronary sinus to the morphologically right atrium.

The anatomy of the coronary sinus

As should now be obvious from our account provided of cardiac development, the coronary sinus serves as the collecting channel for the greater part of the venous return from the heart itself [11]. Not all of the cardiac venous return, however, is received by the coronary sinus. The venous blood from the parietal wall of the right ventricle returns directly to the right atrial chamber through the anterior cardiac veins. A significant part of the blood from the subendocardial components of the chamber walls also returns directly to the cavities of the different chambers through the Thebesian system of small veins. As already described, nonetheless, the majority of the coronary venous blood returns to the coronary sinus through the greater, middle, and small cardiac veins, with additional marginal and inferior veins draining the blood from the walls of the left ventricle (Fig. 3A). The sinus itself occupies the rightward two-thirds of the mural part of the left atrioventricular junction. The leftward one-third of the junction contains the great cardiac vein, with this venous tributary coursing from the anterior interventricular groove to empty into the sinus (Fig. 4A). The walls of the sinus itself are myocardial, and are discrete from the adjacent myocardial walls of the left atrium (Fig. 4B). Although the walls of sinus and left atrium are separate entities, it is usually the case that myocardial strands extend between the two parts [12]. The myocardium covering of the sinus then extends for variable distances, of up to 1 cm, onto the walls of the great cardiac vein [13]. In their extensive study of the coronary sinus and its venous tributaries, Von Ludinghausen and colleagues pointed to the variability in both the presence and location of the valve of Vieussens, emphasing that the site of union with the oblique vein provided the best anatomical marker of the distal extent of the sinus [13].

As it courses through the left atrioventricular groove, the channel formed by the great cardiac vein and the coronary sinus has a relatively intimate relationship to the left atrial vestibule. This relationship was examined in detail by El Maasarany and colleagues [1]. Dividing the mural component of the left atrioventricular junction into six segments, they observed some degree of variability in the adjacency of the venous conduit to the left atrial vestibule. In general, nonetheless, the furthest distance between the venous cavity and the hinge of the mural leaflet of the mitral valve, of around 1 cm, was found at the entrance of the sinus to the right atrium. The distance then gradually decreased as the channel extended distally, with the shortest distance, of around half a centimeter, found in the region of the supero-lateral end of the zone of apposition between the mural and aortic leaflets of the mitral valve. This, of course, is the area in which the great cardiac vein ascends from the anterior interventricular groove to enter the left atrioventricular groove. Now frequently described as the left ventricular summit, the area was previously described by Brocq and Mouchet [14] in terms of a triangle bounded by the diverging major branches of the main stem of the left coronary artery, with the great cardiac vein forming the base of the triangle (Fig. 5A). Those emphasising the area as the left ventricular summit have pointed to its importance as a frequent site of origin of left ventricular focal arrhythmias [15]. This, in turn, points to the significance of the venous conduit as providing the means to reach the area so as to ablate the abnormal rhythms. By the same token, the location of the coronary sinus within the left atrioventricular junction points to its value in providing a route for ablating accessory atrioventricular connections (Fig. 5B).

Abnormal anatomy of the coronary sinus

The most frequent abnormality of the coronary sinus is found when there is persistence of the left superior caval vein (Fig. 6A) [16]. In this setting, the sinus itself is usually dilated, along with its opening into the morphologically right atrium (Fig. 6B). Often found in association with other congenital cardiac malformations, the arrangement can exist in isolation. It is the isolated examples that are likely to be of greatest significance to the electrophysiologist. In that the venous return is to the morphologically right atrium, the lesion in itself does not produce haemodynamic problems. Its presence, nonetheless, is often unexpected. This can be particular significant should the right superior caval vein be absent, although it is very unusual to find this combination in isolation [17]. The persisting left superior caval vein, therefore, is usually found in concert with a normal right superior caval vein. Although it has been suggested that, usually, there is a bridging brachiocephalic vein in this setting [18], this has not been our experience. Also of note is when the persistent left superior caval vein serves as the drainage of the abdominal venous blood when the inferior caval vein is interrupted. This combination is again usually found in the setting of other congenital malformations, in particular left isomerism [19]. It can, nonetheless, be found as an isolated finding, albeit that the drainage is usually via the right superior caval vein [20]. If unrecognised, it can be disconcerting if, during cardiac catheterisation from the inferior caval vein, the catheter suddenly turns away from the heart and ascends into the thorax in parallel fashion.

The other relatively frequent anomaly of the coronary sinus is atresia of its right atrial orifice (Fig. 7A) [21]. In this setting, there is again persistence of the left superior caval vein. The venous channel then serves as the conduit for coronary venous blood to reach the right atrium in retrograde fashion, coursing via the brachiocephalic to the right superior caval vein (Fig. 7B). The atresia is the consequence of presence of an imperforate Thebesian valve, likely due to fusion during developing of the caudal parts of the right and left embryonic venous valves (Fig. 2). The finding is of obvious significance to the electrophysiologist, since its presence will preclude the possibility to catheterise the coronary sinus, and will remove the option directly to reach the greater part of the coronary venous system.

In this setting, as shown in panel B, the persistent left superior caval vein then serves as the conduit for the return of the cardiac venous blood draining to the coronary sinus to the right atrium via the brachiocephalic vein.

The rarest congenital abnormality of the coronary sinus is often described in terms of its "unroofing". The term is less than ideal, since the lesion represents a communication between the cavity of the coronary sinus and the adjacent cavity of the left atrium (Fig. 8A). The communication thus produced can vary markedly in its size and its location [21,22]. It is difficult to explain the presence of the communication, irrespective of its size, from the stance of morphogenesis. It used to be thought that a shared wall was found between the sinus and left atrium. We now know this is not the case, and that each part has its own discrete wall. This implies that, prior to the dissolution of both walls, the sinus must have been incorporated in usual fashion within the left atrioventricular groove. In presence of the communication, and again irrespective of its size, the orifice of the coronary sinus functions as an interatrial communication (Fig. 8B) [23]. The lesion is usually found in association with persistence of the left superior caval vein. In its extreme form, the caval vein opens to the atrial roof between the orifice of the left superior pulmonary vein and the mouth of the left atrial appendage. An interatrial communication at the mouth of the sinus, nonetheless, with absence of the walls of the sinus itself, can be found in absence of a persistent left superior caval vein.

Discussion of the abnormalities of the coronary sinus would be incomplete without mention of its aneurysmal enlargement, with this process producing a diverticulum in the inferior wall of the ventricular mass at the cardiac crux. The lesion was first described in 1983, as seen in an infant with a ventricular septal defect, and in a young man dying suddenly [24]. Subsequent histological examination of comparable cases revealed the presence of extensive myocardial communications between the ventricular wall of the diverticulum and the adjacent atrial vestibule, with the investigation also identifying an association with ventricular pre-excitation [25]. Shortly thereafter, electrophysiologists confirmed the association by ablating the accessory connection, and curing the arrhythmia [26]. It is now recognised that such accessory connections, when associated with coronary sinus diverticulums, account for around one-tenth of cases of inferior paraseptal bypass tracts [27].

Isomerism and the coronary sinus

In all the discussions offered to this stage, it has been presumed that the coronary sinus is occupying the left-sided inferior atrioventricular groove. This, of course, is the usual arrangement, as found in the greater part of the population, who have so-called "situs solitus". In a very small number of individuals, who have a mirror-imaged arrangement of their organs, or so-called "situs inversus", the coronary sinus will occupy the right-sided atrioventricular groove. In such patients, who can be otherwise entirely normal, the overall anatomy will also be the mirror-image of those as described above, as will be the arrangements should the coronary sinus itself be congenitally malformed. Patients with mirror-imaged atrial arrangement, however, make up a remarkably small proportion of those who have congenitally malformed hearts. A much larger number of patients, albeit still small when compared to those with usual atrial arrangement, is found in the setting of isomerism. Much confusion currently exists in the literature with regard to malformations of the coronary sinus in the setting of isomerism. The patients with right isomerism, for example, have been suggested to exhibit the extreme form of "unroofing" of the coronary sinus. This is not true. The coronary sinus is a component of the morphologically left atrioventricular groove. In the setting of right isomerism, both atrioventricular junctions will be morphologically right. One of the universal features of individuals having right isomerism, therefore, is total absence of the coronary sinus. This does not mean that there is absence of a left-sided superior caval vein. On the contrary, most patients with right isomerism have bilateral superior caval veins [28]. But each superior caval vein is morphologically right, opening to the roof of an atrium which possesses a morphologically right appendage (Fig. 9). In this setting, the pectinate muscles extend round both atrial vestibules, meeting together at the crux of the heart. In consequence of the absence of the coronary sinus, the coronary veins drain directly into the atrial vestibules [29]. When each superior caval vein joins its atrial chamber, it enters adjacent to a terminal crest. Reflecting the isomerism of the appendages, there is also isomerism of the sinus node, with each node occupying the terminal groove adjacent to the crest of the appendage (Fig. 9) [30,31].

The arrangement is quite different in the setting of left isomerism. In this situation, it is again frequent to find superior caval veins on both sides. This time, however, each caval vein is morphologically left. When bilateral veins are present, each joins its atrial chamber between the morphologically left atrial appendage and the pulmonary veins, but with no formation of a terminal groove (Fig. 10). It is not anatomically possible for each vein to drain into its atrium through a separate orifice comparable to that found in the normal morphologically right atrium. Indeed, in many instances the caval veins open directly to the atrial roof, but again in absence of a terminal crest [28]. These details are unlikely to be of direct relevance to those undertaking electrophysiological procedures in the adult population, since they are found in hearts that are themselves significantly malformed at birth (Fig. 10). The features do, nonetheless, add to the understanding of the anatomy and development of the normal coronary sinus.

Conclusions

Knowledge of the development of the venous pole of the heart now provides the basis for understanding the anatomy of the normal coronary sinus, which is the manifold for drainage of the greater part of the cardiac venous return. An appreciation of the normal anatomy then provides the foundation for recognising the abnormal features to be observed when the sinus is congenitally malformed. This, in turn, provides the necessary information for the electrophysiologist to assess the value of the sinus and its tributaries as the portal for his, or her, interventional procedures.
