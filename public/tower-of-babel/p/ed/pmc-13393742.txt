Dynamics of facial skin microbiota in female college students during menstruation: comparative analysis between regular and irregular menstrual cycles with identification of phase-specific microbiota
Authors: Haiyue Liu, Jiayi Wang, Feng Li, Wanzhen Yang, Yixuan Zheng, Yifei Yin, Qiaochu Wei, Guolin Hong, Zuheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Previous studies demonstrate that many women experience menstrual cycle-related skin symptoms during their periods. While the exacerbation of skin conditions during menstruation is well-documented, the underlying biological mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Although an association between skin microbiota and the menstrual cycle have been proposed, to gain clearer insights into this change, we finally enrolled 95 subjects and implemented one-to-one matching in the present study. Facial skin microbial samples were collected from female college students during both menstrual and intermenstrual periods, followed by 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. The facial skin microbiome shifted with menstrual cycle regularity, primarily driven by the irregular menstrual group. Proteobacteria predominated over Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, and Bacteroidetes across all samples. At the genus level, Sphingomonas, Bradyrhizobium, Streptococcus, and Corynebacterium were most abundant. However, Firmicutes and Actinobacteria increased significantly in the irregular menstrual group, with Acinetobacter, Haemophilus, Streptococcus, and Corynebacterium exhibiting peak abundance in this group. Multiclass random forest analysis distinguished irregular menstrual samples from all others, achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.82 using bacterial OTUs; Aeromonas caviae (OTU_423025) and Sphingomonadaceae (OTU_4391560) were identified as the model’s most important OTUs. Reduced intra-kingdom interactions were observed in subjects with irregular cycles. Notably, in the irregular menstrual group, three co-occurring OTUs including Streptococcus (OTU_523025), Gemellaceae (OTU_858896), and Staphylococcus (OTU_4345285) were lost. Furthermore, PICRUSt2 analysis predicted upregulation of genes involved in fatty acid, carbohydrate metabolism and signal transduction (e.g., K01462, K01104, K17686) and downregulation of transport (K01991) and antioxidant related genes (K00799) in the irregular menstrual cycle group. The study indicates that irregular menstruation is accompanied by alterations in the structure of the skin microbial community, with such structural changes primarily occurring during the menstrual phase. Additionally, distinctive features of the facial skin microbiota were observed in the Irregular-Menstrual group.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04567-z.

Introduction

The menstrual cycle involves cyclical physiological changes in the endometrium, orchestrated by the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis and characterized by dynamic hormonal fluctuations [1]. Previous research indicates that many women experience skin symptoms associated with the menstrual cycle [2]. Although the exacerbation of skin diseases during menstruation had been noticed, the underlying related factors remain to be fully elucidated.

Therefore, more in-depth explorations have been conducted. Firstly, hormonal fluctuations during the menstrual cycle may serve as one of the contributing factors to alterations in skin condition. Studies on estrogen’s role in skin physiology correlate estrogen levels with increased epidermal thickness [3], higher dermal hydration [4], enhanced stratum corneum barrier function [5], and accelerated wound healing [6]. Previous studies confirmed that the skin is one of the target organs of sex hormones, with an abundance of estrogen receptors present in both the dermis and epidermis [7]. Estrogen can exert its effects not only by binding to estrogen receptors (ERs) on the skin surface, but also through coupling with membrane-associated endoplasmic reticulum and cytoplasmic signal transduction proteins within the cell [8]. Estrogen is also essential for skin hydration, and its replenishment can ameliorate manifestations of skin aging [9].

On the other hand, the onset of multiple dermatological conditions demonstrates correlation with peak progesterone levels. Dermatological conditions exacerbated during the perimenstrual period include acne, psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, and irritant contact dermatitis, with polymorphic erythema potentially also being implicated [10]. The underlying mechanism may be related to the decline in immune and barrier functions caused by cyclical fluctuations in estrogen or progesterone. During the menstrual cycle, women’s skin may exhibit some alterations. For example, the correlation between atopic dermatitis and menstrual cycle fluctuations is well-documented [11]. Niamh TM et al., in their systematic review, concluded that premenstrual exacerbation of atopic dermatitis, a phenomenon consistently documented in clinical practice, typically manifests during the week preceding menstruation [12]. In addition, Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is more prevalent in females, and the severity of the condition appears to fluctuate with menstrual cycle changes, suggesting a potential role of sex hormones in its pathogenesis [13].

An early study has explored estrogen dermatitis in women with chronic skin disorders who experience periodic exacerbation or premenstrual dermatitis symptoms, including itching, urticaria, eczema, and papulovesicular eruptions. Among these women, half were sensitive to estrogen, and nearly one-third of the study population showed worsening of skin lesions during the premenstrual period [14].Research also indicates that dietary preferences can shift throughout the menstrual cycle. For instance, the preference level for sucrose peaks during the late follicular phase, which may also indirectly contribute to physiological changes in the skin [15].

Therefore, the changes in endocrine hormones may subsequently alter the structural characteristics of the skin surface microbial community may be one of the causes of its skin symptoms. Previous study on urban Chinese women have reported an increased proportion of Firmicutes, Acinetobacter, Staphylococcus, and Cutibacterium in the skin microbiota of patients with irregular menstruation [16]. On the other hand, another study on adolescent girls have found that the vaginal microbiota of girls begins to exhibit characteristics similar to those of adults long before the onset of menarche [17]. Additionally, the vulvar microbiota closely resembles the vaginal microbiota, with Lactobacillus spp. dominating the perineal microbiota during early to middle adolescence [17]. Meanwhile, higher levels of Gardnerella vaginalis, which may be associated with bacterial vaginosis in adults, were detected in about one-third of the participants [17]. Another study also suggests that alterations in the vaginal microbiota are associated with cyclical hormonal changes [18].

Hormonal changes might be associated with alterations in skin microbiota, which in turn might trigger skin-related symptoms. The onset or exacerbation of dermatological symptoms during menstruation causes varying levels of distress for women. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the differences in facial skin surface microbial community structures among female college students during menstruation, and analyze some potentially associated lifestyle factors, so as to provide potential intervention targets for hormone-related skin discomfort.

Materials and methods

Study population and sample collection

We recruited 131 volunteers from the Xiang’an Campus of Xiamen University. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) use of anti-inflammatory drugs, antibiotics, glucocorticoids, or immunosuppressants that significantly affect human microbiota within the last three months; (2) previous radiotherapy or chemotherapy within the past six months; (3) Severe dermatological conditions; (4) acne grade 3 or higher; (5) use of cosmetics containing antibiotics or hormones within the last three months; (6) participation in any clinical trials within the last three months; (7) pregnancy or amenorrhea lasting several months. All the samples were collected from the right cheek area beside the subject’s nostril, covering a region measuring 3 cm by 3 cm. After applying exclusion criteria, 27 participants were lost to follow-up because they provided only one sample, and 9 were excluded from the analysis due to insufficient sequencing depth in one or both samples. A total of 95 female college student volunteers were finally enrolled, with an age range from 17 to 29 years. In addition, we confirmed the basic information of the participants through a questionnaire, which was included in the supplementary materials.

Irregular menstrual cycles were defined as: menstrual cycles that vary by more than 7 days, or periods shorter than 2 days or longer than 7 days, or excessive bleeding (requiring changing pads every 1–2 h) or scanty bleeding (spotting), or severe dysmenorrhea. The samples were divided into four groups based on the regularity (regular vs. irregular) and phase of the menstrual cycle (menstruation vs. interval menstruation). This study was conducted at the School of Public Health, Xiamen University, and the First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University, with ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University (No.: 2023 − 109). All enrolled volunteers provided written informed consent.

To ensure sample integrity and prevent contamination, all participants discontinued the use of skincare products and cosmetics within 8 h before sampling. To maintain sample concentration, participants refrained from washing their faces before sampling. Researchers wore sterile gloves and used specialized cotton swabs. Each swab was dipped in sterile saline solution and vigorously rubbed over the sampling sites for at least 15 strokes using rotating motions. To control for environmental interference, blank control groups were established by exposing unused swabs to air for 5 s before placing them back into their original Eppendorf tubes (RNase- and DNase-free). For each volunteer, skin surface samples were collected during both the menstrual and intermenstrual phases. After sampling, the swabs were immediately stored. The samples were transported in a cryobox to a -20 °C freezer and then transferred to a -80 °C freezer within 12 h.

DNA extraction, bioinformatics and statistical analysis

The swab sample DNA was extracted using a magnetic bead-based bacterial DNA extraction kit (Findrop Biosafety Echnology (Guangzhou) Co. Ltd., Guangzhou, China), with all operations strictly following the manufacturer’s protocol. A segment of the hypervariable 16S rRNA gene was amplified via the conserved V4 region-specific primers 515F (5’ GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA 3’) and 806R (5’ GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT 3’) [19]. The reaction sequence included initial heating at 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 52 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s. A final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min was used to complete the process. Following sequencing on the Illumina iSeq PE250 sequencing platform (Novogene, Beijing, China). PCR amplification was performed on an ABI ViiA7 thermocycler.

Reagent controls for extraction (no swab) and PCR amplification (no template, ddH2O only) were included in the experiment. No PCR product bands were observed in the reagent controls. Three DNA extraction controls and one PCR negative control were subsequently sequenced to identify any potential contaminating bacterial species. The main OTUs and their number of sequencing reads in the reagent controls were presented in Table S1. None of the bacterial OTUs present in the reagent controls have > 100 read counts.

The demultiplexed and quality-filtered sequencing reads were subjected to close-reference operational taxonomic unit (OTU) picking using QIIME 1.9.1 [20]. Previous studies have shown that OTU-based and variable structural gene sequence (ASV)-based analyses have strong consistency in capturing alpha diversity, beta diversity, and taxonomic patterns at the genera level, supporting the reliability of the findings [21, 22]. The obtained sequences were de-multiplexed, trimmed of primers and barcodes, and filtered for ambiguous bases or primer mismatches, according to the BIPES protocol [23]. Chimeras were screened out and removed using UCHIME in de novo mode [24]. Sequences were then clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) using USEARCH in closed‑reference mode with default parameters. Reference-based alignments of representative sequences were performed with PyNAST against Greengenes 13_8 [25]. The Ribosome Database Project (RDP) classifier assigned representative 16 S sequences to taxa using the default database [26]. To account for differences in sequencing depth, all samples were rarefied to an even depth of 4210 reads.

Alpha diversity of each sample was evaluated using two indices: PD_whole_tree (phylogenetic diversity), and Shannon index (incorporating species richness and evenness). These two complementary indices were selected to provide a comprehensive evaluation of microbial community diversity [27]. Beta diversity comparisons between sample groups were calculated using unweighted UniFrac distance metrics, which quantify community dissimilarity considering phylogenetic relationships. Adonis analysis was used to estimate the level of dissimilarity in microbial composition to describe the association between facial skin microbiome variation and lifestyle. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) implemented in QIIME 1.9.1 software package was employed to visualize microbial community structural differences among samples. Bacterial community composition profiles were compared using stacked bar charts generated at different taxonomic levels. Functional inference of the microbiome was performed using PICRUSt2 analysis [28]. Multiple KEGG orthologs were compared, and FDR correction (Benjamini–Hochberg) was applied with a threshold of FDR < 0.2.

The methodology of machine learning, co-occurrence networks and procrustes analysis

Random forest analysis has constructed four types of prediction models. The dataset is stratified and divided into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%). Apply 10‑fold cross‑validation in the training set and repeat this process 100 times to ensure stability. Five hundred decision trees were implemented to evaluate the importance of features by reducing the accuracy rate on average. Model performance was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC) with an one‑vs‑all strategy. All analyses were completed in R (version 4.2.2) using the randomForest (v4.7-1.1) and pROC (v1.18.0) packages.

Microbial co‑occurrence network analysis was conducted as follows. OTUs with an average abundance greater than 0.5% across all samples were selected for downstream analysis, and ultimately 40 OTUs were obtained. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient (ρ ≥ 0.4) was calculated to evaluate the co-occurrence and mutual relationship among OTUs, and the control of multiple hypothesis testing (FDR < 0.05) was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

Significant OTU pairs were then used to construct an undirected network using the igraph package (v1.6.0) in R version 4.2.2, where nodes represented OTUs and edge weights corresponded to correlation coefficients. Procrustes analysis was conducted on the regular cycle group and the irregular cycle group respectively to evaluate the consistency of spatial relations between two principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) ordinations (intermenstrual vs. menstrual groups). The Procrustes sum of squares (m²) between paired samples was obtained through the vegan::procrustes () function with symmetric = TRUE. Statistical significance was assessed using 999 Monte Carlo permutation tests via the protest() function. Visualization was performed in ggplot2, generating a biplot in which gray lines represent residual vectors of paired samples, with 68% confidence ellipses for inter‑group differences.

Results

Characteristics from volunteers included in the study

Facial skin microbiota was collected from 131 volunteers, and after applying exclusion criteria, performing sequencing, and removing low-quality DNA samples, we enrolled 95 subjects. The characteristics of the 95 female college student volunteers were obtained from the questionnaire (Supplemental material). The mean BMI was 20.87 ± 3.06 kg/m². Regarding lifestyle habits, 81/95 (85.3%) participants reported going to bed after 00:00 a.m., and 68/95 (71.6%) reported sleeping 6–8 h daily. All participants had mild acne (grades I–II). Specifically, during the menstrual phase, 57 participants were classified as grade I and 38 as grade II; during the intermenstrual phase, the numbers were 58 and 37, respectively. Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table S2.

Microbiota composition in different groups and stages

A total of 190 facial skin microbiota samples were analyzed, collected from 95 female volunteers aged 17 to 29 years. The samples were divided into four groups: Regular−Interval, Regular−Menstrual, Irregular−Interval, and Irregular−Menstrual groups. The alpha diversity of the skin microbiome in the four groups was analyzed based on the Shannon index and PD_whole_tree index. There was a significant difference between Regular−Menstrual and Irregular−Menstrual groups in Shannon index (p < 0.05, Kruskal-Wallis), while similar results were also observed in PD_whole_tree index (p < 0.01, Kruskal-Wallis). In addition, the alpha diversity of the Irregular-Menstrual group was significantly higher than that of the Regular-Interval group (Shannon index: p < 0.01; PD_whole_tree index: p < 0.01; Dunn-Bonferroni test), as well as the Regular-Menstrual group (Shannon index: p < 0.05; PD_whole_tree index: p < 0.01). However, no significant differences in alpha diversity were observed between Regular−Interval and Irregular−Interval groups (Fig. 1a). These findings indicate that alterations in facial microbiota composition between young women with regular and irregular menstrual cycles occur during specific phases of the menstrual cycle.

Next, beta-diversity differences among groups were assessed using Adonis (PERMANOVA) based on unweighted UniFrac distances, and visualized using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) (Fig. 1b). Adonis based on unweighted UniFrac distances revealed a significant difference in facial microbial composition among the four subgroups (Regular-Menstrual, Regular-Interval, Irregular-Menstrual, and Irregular-Interval; R² = 0.0199, p < 0.01), suggesting a small but statistically significant effect of menstrual cycle regularity and phase on facial microbial composition. Pairwise comparisons further showed that the only significant difference was observed between the Irregular-Menstrual and Regular-Menstrual groups (R² = 0.0179, p < 0.001), while no significant differences were detected between the two regular subgroups, between the two irregular subgroups, or between the Interval subgroups across cycle regularity groups. When participants were grouped solely by menstrual cycle regularity (regular vs. irregular, regardless of menstrual phase), Adonis also revealed a significant difference between the two groups (R² = 0.0081, p < 0.01), with the distinct separation supported by the PCoA plot (Fig. 1b). These findings are consistent with previous reports [16]. These findings suggest that women with irregular menstrual cycles may exhibit a more distinct facial microbiota structure compared to those with regular cycles, potentially contributing to dysbiosis-related alterations in facial skin condition through certain pathway disruptions.

Furthermore, analysis of taxonomic composition plots at the phylum level revealed that Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, and Bacteroidetes constituted a substantial proportion of the bacterial community, collectively accounting for over 96% of the total (Fig. 1c). Proteobacteria had relative abundances of 75.83% (Regular-Interval), 76.89% (Regular-Menstrual), 71.87% (Irregular-Interval), and 61.86% (Irregular-Menstrual), while Firmicutes showed abundances of 11.97%, 10.12%, 13.06%, and 18.27% in the respective groups (Fig. 1c).

Genus-level analysis revealed intergroup distribution differences among 15 key bacterial genera (Fig. 1c). Overall, all groups of skin microbiota were dominated by Sphingomonas, Bradyrhizobium (a symbiotic nitrogen-fixing bacterium), Streptococcus, and Corynebacterium at the genera level. First, Sphingomonas exhibited a decreasing trend across the Regular-Interval, Regular-Menstrual, Irregular-Interval, and Irregular-Menstrual groups, with relative abundances of 42.80%, 38.95%, 35.65%, and 26.17%, respectively. Bradyrhizobium showed increased abundance during menstrual periods compared to interval periods, with relative abundances of 5.25%, 6.04%, 4.59%, and 6.29% in the respective groups. In addition, Streptococcus (5.40%, 3.98%, 6.00% and 7.64%) and Corynebacterium (4.40%, 6.25%, 6.52% and 8.43%) demonstrated a progressive upward trend from the Regular-Interval to the Irregular-Menstrual group. We next investigated the relationship between beta diversity of the facial skin microbial community and lifestyle factors. Adonis analysis revealed significant correlations between several variables and the facial skin microbiota (unweighted UniFrac distance, p < 0.05), including acne grade and atopic predisposition in the interval subgroup, as well as menstrual regularity in the menstrual subgroup (Figure S2).

Facial skin microbiota and function KO (KEGG Orthology) differences among these groups

Subsequently, we compared the most important bacteria among the four groups. Notably, the distributions of both Proteobacteria and Firmicutes differed significantly among the four groups (Kruskal-Wallis test, p < 0.01; Fig. 2a). In addition, opportunistic pathogens such as Acinetobacter, Haemophilus, and Corynebacterium reached their highest proportions in the Irregular-Menstrual group, being significantly more abundant than in the other three groups (Acinetobacter: p < 0.05, Haemophilus: p < 0.01, Corynebacterium: p < 0.05; Kruskal-Wallis Dunn-Bonferroni test). Similarly, Ralstonia, Neisseria, Streptococcus, and Veillonella also showed the highest prevalence in the Irregular-Menstrual group (p < 0.05, Kruskal-Wallis test) (Fig. 2b). PICRUSt2 functional prediction inferred that in the menstrual phase of the irregular cycle group, multiple genes involved in fatty acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, and signal transduction (e.g., K01462, K01104, K17686, etc.) were upregulated, whereas genes related to substance transport (K01991) and glutathione‑mediated antioxidant defense (K00799) were downregulated. These functional differences suggest that changes in the metabolic and transport capabilities of the skin microbiota might be associated with hormonal fluctuations during the menstrual cycle.

Discriminative OTUs by random forest modeling

In order to further explore the characteristics of the bacterial communities among different groups, Random Forest modeling was conducted using 51 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) selected based on inter-group Kruskal-Wallis tests (raw p < 0.05) and prevalence exceeding 10% in the cohort. Multi-class ROC curve analysis demonstrated that microbial community features could effectively distinguish the Irregular-Menstrual group, with the Random Forest model achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.82 (Fig. 3a). In addition, the Random Forest model identified 20 key OTUs, and their inter-group distribution differences are visualized in a bubble plot (Fig. 3b, c). Among them, Aeromonas caviae (OTU423025; Mean Decrease Accuracy = 9.12), Sphingomonadaceae (OTU4391560; Mean Decrease Accuracy = 8.02), Lautropia (OTU887694; Mean Decrease Accuracy = 3.36), Veillonella parvula (OTU114821; Mean Decrease Accuracy = 3.10), and Granulicatella (OTU1059729; Mean Decrease Accuracy = 3.08) contributed most significantly to the classification (Fig. 3c). Additionally, the bubble plot reveals significant intergroup differences in specific OTUs (Fig. 3b). First, Corynebacterium (OTU: 1015518) exhibited significantly higher abundance in the Irregular-Menstrual group compared to another two regular menstrual groups (p < 0.01). Second, Granulicatella (OTU: 1059729) showed the highest relative abundance in the Irregular-Menstrual group, with significant elevation compared to the Irregular-Interval group (p < 0.05) and both regular menstrual groups (both p < 0.01). Third, Veillonella parvula (OTU: 114821) and Veillonella dispar (OTU: 4475758) were significantly more abundant in the Irregular-Menstrual group than in the regular groups (p < 0.01 for both comparisons for V. parvula; p < 0.05 for both comparisons for V. dispar). Moreover, Sphingomonadaceae (OTU: 4391560) and Sphingomonas (OTU: 4396025) displayed peak abundance in the Irregular-Interval group, with significant elevation over the other three groups (all p < 0.05) (Fig. 3b). The findings indicate potential distinctive features in the facial skin microbiota composition of Irregular-Menstrual group.

Co-occurrence networks reveal ‘hub’ OTUs in facial skin microbiota

To investigate ‘hub’ OTUs, we conducted co-occurrence analysis using discriminative OTUs identified across these groups. Microbial co-occurrence networks were generated based on significant Spearman correlations (R > 0.4). The (Fig. 4a) regular interval, (Fig. 4b) regular menstrual, (Fig. 4c) irregular interval, and (Fig. 4d) irregular menstrual groups respectively present the co-occurrence networks of facial skin microbiota. In the regular menstrual subgroup, the menstrual phase network exhibited significantly greater complexity compared to the non-menstrual phase. This complexity pattern persisted in the irregular menstrual subgroup, where the menstrual phase network also demonstrated greater complexity than its non-menstrual counterpart (Fig. 4).

The Regular-Interval group network included 39 nodes and 138 edges, with 11 nodes each connecting to more than 10 nodes. Among these, ten nodes belonged to the Proteobacteria phylum: Sphingomonadaceae (OTU: 4312153) ranked first with 18 node connections, and Sphingomonas (OTU: 580992) ranked second with 17 connections, with major negative correlations contributed by Sphingomonas (OTU: 580992). One node from the Actinobacteria phylum, Corynebacterium (OTU: 495067), connected to 14 nodes, including 2 negative correlations with Proteobacteria nodes, 1 positive correlation with a Proteobacteria node, and 11 positive correlations with non-Proteobacteria nodes. This module appeared to represent an interaction pattern of skin-associated commensal microorganisms. Additionally, a module showing mutual co-occurrence relationships among three microorganisms: Streptococcus (OTU: 523025), Gemellaceae (OTU: 858896), and Staphylococcus (OTU: 4345285) was identified in this group.

Additionally, the Regular-Menstrual group contains a network of 38 nodes and 196 edges, with 21 nodes exhibiting connections to more than 10 other nodes. Among these, 18 nodes belong to the phylum Proteobacteria, with Sphingomonas (OTU: 580992) (containing 24 edges) and Sphingomonadaceae (OTU: 4312153) (19 edges) demonstrating particularly high interaction frequencies with other microbial taxa. Another three nodes from the phylum Firmicutes include Streptococcus (OTU: 523025) (15 edges), Gemellaceae (OTU:858896) (16 edges), and Staphylococcus (OTU:4345285) (13 edges), which form a positively correlated subnetwork. Notably, the opportunistic pathogens Acinetobacterm (OTU: 4449458) (11 edges) and Haemophilus (OTU: 341460) (13 edges) emerged as a high-connectivity nodes in this group’s microbial interaction network.

On the other hand, the Irregular-Interval group network consists of 35 nodes and 101 edges, with 6 nodes connected to more than 10 others. Gemellaceae (OTU: 858896) (14 edges) from the phylum Firmicutes exhibited the highest connectivity. All remaining nodes originated from the phylum Proteobacteria. Additionally, the co-occurrence relationships module involving Streptococcus (OTU: 523025), Gemellaceae (OTU: 858896), and Staphylococcus (OTU: 4345285), which formed a positively correlated subnetwork in Regular-Menstrual group, was also observed in this group.

In addition, the Irregular-Menstrual group network comprises 28 nodes and 93 edges, with 10 nodes originating from the phylum Proteobacteria. The dominant microbial interaction patterns in this group are exclusively contributed by Proteobacteria, forming a more homogeneous yet intricate microbial interaction network. Notably, the co-occurrence relationships module involving Streptococcus (OTU: 523025), Gemellaceae (OTU: 858896), and Staphylococcus (OTU: 4345285), which was previously observed in other groups, has disappeared in this group.

Procrustes analysis of facial microbial community

Since we conducted two samplings on the same cohort of individuals, we employed Procrustes analysis to compare microbial community consistency between menstrually regular and irregular groups across different menstrual phase samples (Figure S1). In the menstrually regular individuals, Procrustes analysis revealed no significant congruence between microbial community structure and reference configuration (M² = 0.221, P = 0.877, permutation test). For the menstrually irregular group, although the result did not reach the conventional significance threshold (M² = 0.107, P = 0.092), the effect size suggested a potential trend of association, might reflecting partial consistency in community structure influenced by menstrual irregularity.

Discussion

The menstrual cycle is a natural process that every woman experiences in her lifetime. However, irregular cycles may cause various physical and mental health issues, such as polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), anxiety, and depression [29, 30]. Various studies have already shown that irregular menstrual periods can cause various skin conditions [16, 31], but there is still little research on the relationship between regular menstrual cycles and skin health. Recently, some studies have reported a connection between menstrual regularity and skin microbiota [16]. However, they did not account for participant matching in their research design. To further explore this relationship, we conducted one-to-one paired comparisons within our study population.

Similar to previous study [16], we observed differences in facial skin microbiota between subjects with regular menstrual cycles and those with irregular menstrual cycles. Further analysis revealed that during the menstrual phase, there were significant differences in microbial community structure between participants with regular and irregular menstrual cycles, whereas such disparities were not observed during the intermenstrual phase. At the genus level, Sphingomonas was identified as the dominant bacteria on the facial skin of young female college students. Its relative abundance exhibited a decreasing trend in both individuals with irregular menstrual cycles and during the menstrual phase. Previous literature indicates that Sphingomonas extract exhibits two key anti-aging mechanisms: it mitigates intrinsic skin aging through inhibition of cellular senescence pathways and simultaneously demonstrates skin-restructuring capabilities that enhance tissue integrity [32]. Another study suggest that Sphingomonas strain exhibited high resistance to UV irradiation and effectively reduced reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in human keratinocytes [33]. These findings suggest that women with irregular menstrual cycles may exhibit a depletion of beneficial skin bacteria. Furthermore, the ROC curve’s demonstration of the capacity to distinguish the irregular menstrual group suggests that the facial skin microbiota during menstruation in individuals with irregular cycles exhibits unique characteristics. Thus, skin microbial biomarkers hold promise as adjunctive diagnostic tools for menstrual cycle disorders, although larger-scale sample validation may be required.

Furthermore, we observed a significant increase in the relative abundance of Streptococcus and Veillonella in young women with irregular menstruation. This observation is consistent with previous studies linking vaginal microbiome imbalance to hormonal fluctuations [34]. For example, Group A Streptococcus can cause skin and soft tissue infections and may promote psoriatic inflammation through immune pathways [35, 36]. Additionally, Streptococcus species such as S. pyogenes, sometimes, can lead to deep tissue infections [37]. Another study has also shown that Streptococcus causes damage to the skin by upregulating arginine catabolism [38]. Consequently, these processes might potentially worsen skin conditions. On the contrary, some species from Streptococcus instead exhibit protective or therapeutic effects. For example, extracellular vesicles derived from Streptococcus mutans play a role in wound healing in mouse skin [39]. Notably, the disappearance of co-occurrence relationships among Streptococcus, Gemellaceae, and Staphylococcus in irregular menstruation also suggests that cyclical changes in Streptococcus might be associated with skin symptoms.

On the other hand, although Veillonella species typically exist as commensal bacteria in the human microbiota and are seldom recognized as clinically significant pathogens, research has established correlations between these microorganisms and liver disease progression [40, 41]. Furthermore, approximately 50 years ago, research already discovered that endotoxin derived from Veillonella can contribute to skin inflammation and induce the Shwartzman reaction [42]. Another bacterium worthy of attention is Aeromonas caviae, which serves as a significant factor in identifying participants with menstrual irregularities. Aeromonas caviae also appears to be a pathogenic bacterium capable of inducing soft tissue infections [43], and even necrotizing fasciitis [44].

In addition, regular menstrual cycle groups exhibited more complex and interrelated microbial networks in our study. High network connectivity and complexity contribute to the enhancement of the stability and resilience of the community [45]. In contrast, irregular menstrual cycle groups showed reduced connectivity and the complete loss of a co-occurring module (Streptococcus, Gemellaceae, Staphylococcus). Reduced network complexity or module loss might indicate weakened stability and increased fragility [46]. Therefore, these topological differences suggest that menstrual cycle regularity might associated with distinct skin microbial ecological states.

Additionally, the predicted upregulation of K01462 (enoyl-CoA hydratase) in the irregular menstrual cycle group might indicate hormone-driven lipid metabolism shifts [47], while the predicted downregulation of K00799 (glutathione S-transferase) might suggest impaired microbial antioxidant capacity [48]. Another study also suggests that the predicted downregulation of the ABC‑2 type transporter (K01991) may impair the microbial uptake or efflux of hormone metabolites [49], thereby weakening the bidirectional crosstalk between the skin microbiota and host hormonal signals. This disruption may partially be associated with the altered skin microbiota homeostasis observed in women with irregular menstrual cycles. However, these predicted functional differences should be interpreted cautiously and require validation through metagenomic or metabolomic approaches in future studies.

Alterations in the skin microbiota of young college students with menstrual irregularities may be associated with facial skin symptoms. Fluctuations in hormone levels may be associated with changes in the skin microbiota, which might exert effects through direct invasion, immune-mediated mechanisms, or metabolites. Indeed, previous studies involving gut microbiota have suggested that intestinal flora may remotely influence the skin by altering metabolites [50]. However, this study constitutes a descriptive observational investigation which lacks validation of causal relationships.

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size and the exclusive focus on female college students within a specific age range may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, we did not conduct a more detailed analysis of the menstrual cycle phases, including menstruation, the follicular phase, ovulation, and the luteal phase. Third, variations in participants’ dietary habits and lifestyle factors might potentially affect facial skin microbiota. Although research on the effects of diet on skin microbiota is limited, one study has demonstrated that dietary energy intake can influence the skin microbiota of yaks [51], whereas different dietary components do not seem to affect that of rainbow trout [52]. While most of the dietary intake among our study population came from university cafeterias, their exact diets were difficult to pinpoint, and analyzing the specific components of the foods proved challenging. Therefore, future research on the impact of diet on skin microbiota is likely to be of great interest. Another limitation is that measuring participants’ hormone levels would have strengthened the conclusions. However, the cyclic changes in estrogen and progesterone are the established cause of menstruation, indirectly suggesting an association between hormonal fluctuation and microbial variation. Since we did not measure clinical skin parameters, the microbial findings in this study cannot be directly applied to infer skin health outcomes, and future studies need include clinical skin parameters to establish a direct connection.

In addition, given the low biomass of skin samples and the potential influence of environmental microbiota, several methodological limitations should be considered. The samples were collected from college students in a university setting, where environmental exposure to airborne bacteria (e.g., Proteobacteria such as Sphingomonas and Methylobacterium) may have occurred during sampling [53]. This is inherent to field‑based skin microbiome studies. Additionally, the V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene targeted in this study has been reported to exhibit differential amplification efficiency across bacterial phyla, and this primer set may amplify Proteobacteria more efficiently than other phyla [54]. Nevertheless, given the dynamic sampling design with paired samples collected from the same individuals across menstrual phases, our comparative findings may still offer some preliminary insights. Finally, while OTU‑based and ASV‑based methods show strong consistency for major ecological metrics, ASV‑based methods (e.g., DADA2) may offer higher taxonomic resolution and should be considered in future investigations [21, 22].

Conclusions

In this descriptive study, we found that facial skin microbiota differed between individuals with regular and irregular menstrual cycles. Women with irregular menstruation are more likely to exhibited distinct microbial features, including altered taxonomic composition and reduced microbial interactions. These preliminary observations suggest that menstrual cycle regularity may be associated with skin microbial composition. Further research is needed to validate these findings and explore their biological implications.
