Identification of the BAHD gene family in Korla fragrant pear and functional characterization of PsBAHD6 in resistance to pear Valsa canker
Authors: Wang Lanfei, Yu Mingyang, Jia Yuanyuan, Wang Hao, Zhao Keke, Liu Can, Fan Weifan, Li Xin, Zeng Junkai, Wu Xiao, Zhang Shaoling, Bao Jianping
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study performed genome-wide identification and functional characterization of BAHD genes in Korla fragrant pear and investigated their roles in the defense response against Valsa canker. A total of 42 PsBAHD members were identified. Evolutionary and collinearity analyses indicated that this gene family is evolutionarily conserved across Rosaceae species and has been subjected to strong purifying selection. Expression profiling under pathogen infection revealed that several PsBAHD members, especially PsBAHD6, exhibited time-specific expression following pathogen infection. Subcellular localization showed PsBAHD6 is localized in both the nucleus and cell membrane, suggesting potential dual-site biological functions in the pear's response to Valsa canker. Functional analysis revealed that overexpression of PsBAHD6 in pear callus enhanced resistance to Valsa canker, reducing lesion area by 39.3% to 55.2% compared with the wild type (p < 0.05). This was accompanied by increased activities of antioxidant enzymes—SOD by 40.2% to 72.8%, POD by 97.8% to 135.8%, and CAT by 47.3% to 77.8% (p < 0.05)—along with significantly elevated phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) activity, which is closely associated with disease resistance, and a marked reduction in H2O2 accumulation. In contrast, silencing PsBAHD6 via VIGS in Korla fragrant pear seedlings resulted in increased disease susceptibility upon pathogen challenge. This was associated with decreased activities of antioxidant enzymes: SOD declined by 20.5% to 26.2%, POD by 34.2% to 42.3%, and CAT was significantly inhibited, dropping by 33.1% to 47.8% (p < 0.05). PAL activity also decreased by 25.3% to 30.8% (p < 0.05), leading to an increase in H2O2 levels. These findings suggest that PsBAHD6 likely functions as a key positive regulator of Valsa canker resistance in Korla fragrant pear. The results provide insights into a theoretical basis for understanding the disease resistance mechanism of BAHD family members in pear and could offer valuable genetic resources for breeding Valsa canker-resistant Korla fragrant pear cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08479-w.Keywords: Korla fragrant pear, BAHD acyltransferase, Valsa canker

Introduction

Korla fragrant pear (Pyrus sinkiangensis Yü), a distinctive and valuable fruit native to Xinjiang, China, is renowned both domestically and internationally for its unique flavor and superior quality. It has emerged as a vital industry and makes substantial contributions to local economic growth [1]. However, pear canker disease, caused by pathogenic fungi, severely hinders the sustainable development of the industry [2, 3]. In major production areas such as Korla in Xinjiang, the disease incidence can reach 50% to 60% [4], resulting in significant economic losses to the local pear industry.

Upon entering the phloem of pear trees through wounds, the pathogen responsible for Valsa canker alters the expression of genes related to cell wall degradation and oxidative stress, thereby disrupting host cell structure. The pathogen subsequently proliferates rapidly within the phloem, obstructing sieve tubes and impairing vascular bundle function. This results in the formation of canker lesions, severely impedes nutrient transport, weakens tree vigor, causes branch dieback, and can ultimately lead to whole-plant mortality [5, 6]. Currently, the control of disease remains heavily reliant on chemical agents, which increasingly raise concerns over environmental pollution, pesticide residues, and pathogen resistance. Therefore, in-depth identification of pear genes associated with canker resistance and systematic elucidation of their stress-response mechanisms are crucial for breeding high-resistance cultivars and promoting sustainable industry development.

The BAHD acyltransferase superfamily, named after its four initially characterized enzymes (BEAT, AHCT, HCBT, and DAT), is one of the most prevalent and functionally diverse groups of acyltransferases in plants [7]. Members of this superfamily are defined by two highly conserved motifs, HXXXD [8] and DFGWG [9], which have been identified in a wide range of plant species, including Leonurus japonicus [10], Allium fistulosum [11], and Musa acuminata [12]. BAHD superfamily members play a key role in plant defense by participating in multiple biological processes, including secondary metabolic modification, synthesis of extracellular barrier substances, and the production and transduction of defense-related signaling molecules [13]. Specifically, through catalyzing acylation reactions, this family modifies the solubility, stability, and bioactivity of various metabolites such as flavonoids, anthocyanins, alkaloids, and phenylpropanoids, thereby significantly diversifying plant secondary metabolites and enhancing both defense capacity and environmental adaptability [13]. As integral components of the phenylpropanoid pathway, BAHD family members directly regulate the biosynthesis of disease-resistant substances such as lignin and chlorogenic acid, while promoting the synthesis of cutin and suberin, thereby strengthening the physical barrier against pathogen invasion [14–16]. In the plant defense system, the BAHD acyltransferase family is not only involved in secondary metabolism but also plays a significant role in immune regulation. EPS1 is a gene in Arabidopsis thaliana that encodes a BAHD acyltransferase-like protein. It positively regulates resistance to bacterial pathogens by promoting salicylic acid (SA) accumulation [17]. In rice, certain OsBAHD acyltransferase genes showed elevated expression in genotypes susceptible to the sheath blight fungus Rhizoctonia solani, suggesting their involvement in mediating host susceptibility, potentially through pathways like brassinosteroid signaling [18]. During tomato fruit ripening, BAHD acyltransferase family member participates in converting the bitter and toxic α-tomatine into the non-bitter and low-toxicity esculeoside A by catalyzing the acetylation of hydroxytomatine, confirming the key role of the detoxification metabolism of glycoalkaloids in tomato fruits [19]. This metabolic conversion not only reduces the bitterness and toxicity of the fruit, improving its palatability, but also reflects the important function of the BAHD family in regulating defense compound metabolism and quality formation during plant development.

In addition, certain members are involved in the synthesis of defense signaling molecules, including polyamine conjugates and hydroxycinnamic amides [20]. Acylation-mediated modification of these signaling molecules alters their chemical properties, which in turn affects their mobility within plant tissues and the activation of downstream defense responses [21]. These mechanisms collectively improve pathogen recognition, accelerate the propagation of localized defense signals, and ultimately support the establishment of systemic resistance. Although extensive reports on the involvement of BAHD family members in plant defense, studies focusing on the BAHD family in pear and its functional role in canker disease resistance remain limited.

This study uses Korla fragrant pear as the research material to systematically identify members of the BAHD family, analyze their sequence characteristics and evolutionary relationships, and examine the expression patterns of this family under canker stress. Key BAHD genes associated with canker resistance will be screened and functionally validated. The research aims to deepen the understanding of the BAHD family in pears, provide candidate genes and a theoretical foundation for molecular breeding of canker-resistant Korla fragrant pear, and further enrich the research framework on the disease-resistance functions of the pear BAHD family.

Results

Identification and basic characteristic analysis of the BAHD gene family in Korla fragrant pear

A total of 42 BAHD acyltransferase family members were identified in the Korla fragrant pear genome and systematically designated PsBAHD1 through PsBAHD42, revealing significant heterogeneity in gene distribution. Among them, Chr17 was an enriched region for PsBAHD genes, containing 12 members; Chr9 also exhibited clustered distribution of multiple members, comprising 9 members. The formation of these gene clusters, particularly those located on Chr17 and Chr9, suggests the occurrence of gene duplication events. To understand the protein physicochemical characteristics of the BAHD family in the Korla fragrant pear, we analyzed the amino acid length, molecular weight (MW), and isoelectric point (pI) of all 42 PsBAHD genes (Supplementary Table S3). The amino acid lengths varied significantly, ranging from 325 to 536, with an average of approximately 460 amino acids. Molecular weights spanned from 35,561.3 to 59,215.2 Da, with most members falling between 45,000 and 55,000 Da. Based on their predicted pI values, the proteins were categorized as acidic (pI < 7) and basic (pI ≥ 7) proteins, with 31 and 11 members identified in each group, respectively.

To elucidate the evolutionary relationships of the BAHD family in Korla fragrant pear, we constructed a phylogenetic tree. The 42 PsBAHD members were classified into five branches (GroupⅠ to Ⅴ) (Fig. 1-B). Among them, GroupⅠ was the largest, containing 17 members, followed by Groups III, V, IV, and II with 13, 6, 4, and 2 members, respectively. A total of twenty conserved motifs (motifs 1–20) were identified, and their composition and distribution exhibited clear branching specificity (Fig. 1-B). Notably, Motif 1 and Motif 2, which are present in all branches, encompass the characteristic HXXXD catalytic motif and the DFGWG structural motif of the BAHD family, thereby constituting the structural foundation for the enzyme's catalytic activity. Gene structure analysis further showed that all PsBAHD members possess continuous coding sequences (CDS), with little variation in CDS lengths, reflecting strong conservation of the core catalytic domain (Fig. 1-B). In contrast, the untranslated regions (UTRs) showed marked diversity, as only a subset of members contain UTRs.

Evolution and collinearity analysis of the BAHD gene family

Members of the BAHD acyltransferase gene family from the Korla fragrant pear, white pear, Western pear, apple, and Arabidopsis thaliana were systematically identified to construct a multi-species phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2-A). A total of 208 genes were included: 42 from Korla fragrant pear, 35 from white pear, 37 from Western pear, 47 from apple, and 47 from Arabidopsis thaliana. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that these genes are grouped into 14 branches (Ⅰ-ⅩⅣ). Branch I further subdivided into 5 subbranches (I-A to I-E), suggesting fine-scale evolutionary diversification. The conserved clustering pattern across species reflects the evolutionary stability of the BAHD family, while the presence of well-defined subclades implies functional divergence, with different sublineages likely performing distinct biochemical roles or exhibiting specific expression profiles. To investigate the evolutionary dynamics of the BAHD family in Korla fragrant pear, a whole-genome collinearity analysis was conducted (Fig. 2-B). A total of 23 pairs of duplicated genes were identified (Supplementary Table S4). Among these, 16 pairs (69.6%) are fragment duplications distributed across 9 chromosomes, indicating that large-scale genomic fragment replication has been the main driver for family expansion. The remaining 7 pairs (30.4%) were classified as tandem duplications. Further comparative analysis of the non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rate for these PsBAHD gene pairs (Supplementary Table S5) showed that all 23 pairs exhibited Ka/Ks values below 1 (ranging from 0.0535 to 0.6982), indicating that this gene family has been subject to extensive purifying selection during evolution. Notably, 19 of these pairs displayed Ka/Ks values lower than 0.5, reflecting strong functional constraints. These findings strongly support the highly conserved evolution of the PsBAHD family genes, implying that their protein functions may be critical for plant adaptability. To systematically investigate the evolutionary trajectory of the BAHD gene family within Rosaceae and beyond, we performed a cross-species collinearity analysis (Fig. 2-C). Starting with Korla fragrant pear, the analysis was sequentially extended to Western pear, apple, and Arabidopsis thaliana. The results showed that Korla fragrant pears and Western pears share 56 pairs of collinear genes, Western pears and apples share 39 pairs, and apples and Arabidopsis thaliana share 12 pairs. Notably, the number of collinear genes between the two pear species was significantly higher than those between other species, aligning with their closer phylogenetic relationship. We also observed "one-to-many" collinearity relationships, in which a single gene in Korla fragrant pears corresponded to multiple homologs in Western pears. For example, PsBAHD3 in Korla fragrant pears forms collinear pairs with several genes in Western pears, including PcBAHD20, PcBAHD3, PcBAHD33, and PcBAHD9. These patterns suggest that fragment replication events derived from a common ancestral genome likely occurred during pear evolution, resulting in gene copy number expansion and functional divergence. Furthermore, by examining the core highly conserved gene members, we identified 44 gene pairs that remained collinear across all four species (Fig. 2-C). Mapping these core conserved genes onto the phylogenetic tree revealed that 12 pairs were significantly enriched in the I-E branch (Fig. 2-A). This result provides genomic-structural evidence that the I-E branch represents an evolutionarily conserved and functionally important lineage in plants.

Analysis of Cis-acting elements in the promoter regions of BAHD family genes in Korla fragrant pear

To characterize the regulatory potential of BAHD genes in Korla fragrant pear, the 2000 bp sequences upstream of the start codons (ATGs) of all family members were systematically scanned for cis-acting elements. The results showed that the promoter regions of PsBAHD genes are rich in diverse elements associated with hormonal responses, as well as biological and abiotic stresses (Fig. 3). Hormone-response elements were particularly abundant, with motifs related to methyl jasmonate (MeJA) and abscisic acid (ABA) being the most prevalent. In addition, abiotic stress response elements, such as the MBS element for drought response, the LTR element for low-temperature response, and the anaerobic-induced ARE element, were detected in the majority of promoters. Several elements linked to biotic stress and defense responses were also identified, including the elicitor-responsive element AS-1 and the fungus-inducible W-box, indicating a regulatory capacity of PsBAHD genes under pathogen challenge.

Expression pattern of the BAHD family gene in Korla fragrant pear during Valsa canker infection

To elucidate the potential roles of PsBAHD genes in the defense response of Korla fragrant pear against Valsa canker, we performed qRT-PCR to assess the expression levels of all 42 PsBAHD family members in leaf tissues at 0, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 hpi with the pathogen (Fig. 4-A). The results revealed distinct expression patterns among PsBAHD genes during infection. Notably, PsBAHD6 exhibited a consistent and pronounced upregulation from 48 to 120 hpi, suggesting its sustained and potentially central role in mid- to late-stage defense. Furthermore, it was also observed that PsBAHD8, PsBAHD12, and PsBAHD22 exhibited significant upregulation at specific time points. PsBAHD8 displayed moderate induction accompanied by an early peak, PsBAHD12 fluctuated after peaking at the early 48-h mark and PsBAHD22 showed a brief spike at 24 h. In contrast, PsBAHD3, PsBAHD17, PsBAHD24, PsBAHD27 and PsBAHD33 were markedly downregulated after inoculation and remained at low levels after 24 hpi, indicating possible roles in early stress regulation or inhibited by the pathogen. It is noteworthy that the promoter of the PsBAHD6 gene contains cis-regulatory elements such as jasmonic acid-responsive elements, abscisic acid-responsive elements, and W-boxes, which provides bioinformatic evidence for the gene's potential responsiveness to corresponding hormonal and pathogen stimuli.

Subcellular localization of PsBAHD6

Given the significant response of PsBAHD6 during pathogenic bacterial infection, we conducted a subcellular localization assay to pinpoint its expression region. A fusion construct of PsBAHD6 with green fluorescent protein (GFP) was generated and transiently expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves (Fig. 5). Fluorescence imaging revealed that the PsBAHD6-GFP signal was predominantly localized to the nucleus. Additionally, though less intense than the nuclear signal, distinct fluorescent signals were consistently observed in the cell membrane region, suggesting partial association with the cell membrane. This dual localization pattern implies that PsBAHD6 may function in distinct cellular compartments, potentially participating in nuclear transcriptional regulation, while also playing a role in cell membrane-localized immune signal recognition and related signal transduction regulation.

Enhanced resistance to Valsa canker in PsBAHD6-overexpressing callus and its physiological mechanism

To explore the functional role of PsBAHD6 in resistance to Valsa canker, we generated transgenic pear callus overexpressing PsBAHD6 (Fig S1). Stable transgenic lines (OE3, OE7, OE10) together with wild-type (WT) callus were selected for further analysis (Fig. 6-A). Upon inoculation with the Valsa canker, the lesion area was significantly smaller in all overexpression lines compared to WT, with reductions of 39.3%−55.2% (Fig. 6-B), indicating that PsBAHD6 overexpression enhances resistance to the pathogen. Following inoculation with Valsa canker bacteria, SOD activity increased by 40.2%−72.8% in both transgenic and WT callus (Fig. 6-C). POD, which participates in both H2O2 scavenging and lignin polymerization, rose markedly by 97.8%−135.8% (Fig. 6-D). CAT activity also increased significantly, by 47.3%−77.8% (Fig. 6-E). Although H2O2 accumulated upon infection, its content was significantly lower in overexpressing callus than in WT after inoculation (Fig. 6-F). These results suggest that PsBAHD6 effectively maintains oxidative homeostasis under biotic stress by coordinately enhancing multiple antioxidant enzymes, thereby alleviating oxidative damage. Furthermore, PAL activity increased markedly in overexpression lines compared to WT after inoculation (Fig. 6-G), suggesting that PsBAHD6 overexpression activates the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway, which may promote the synthesis of defense-related compounds such as lignin and thus restrict pathogen progression.

B Lesion area quantification of WT and three independent OE lines using Image J. C-G Physiological indices measured in callus before and after inoculation, including SOD activity, POD activity, CAT activity, H₂O₂ content, and PAL activity. Significant differences were determined by Duncan's multiple range test (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001)

Silencing PsBAHD6 compromises resistance to Valsa canker and disrupts related physiological pathways

To further validate the role of PsBAHD6 in disease resistance, we generated PsBAHD6-silenced lines of Korla fragrant pear seedlings using VIGS technology. The silencing efficiency was confirmed by RT-qPCR (Fig S2). Following inoculation with Valsa canker bacteria, the silenced lines (pTRV-PsBAHD6−5, −8, and −11) showed significant larger lesion areas compared to the empty‑vector control (pTRV) (Fig. 7-A and B). We next measured key physiological indicators in leaves before and after inoculation to explore how PsBAHD6 regulates defense responses. Analysis of ROS metabolism indicated that the antioxidant system was significantly impacted in silenced plants after inoculation. Compared with the pTRV control, SOD activity decreased by 17.5%−26.2% in the three pTRV-PsBAHD6 gene-silenced lines (Fig. 7-C). POD activity was reduced by 34.2%−42.3% (Fig. 7-D), and CAT activity declined by 33.1%−47.8% (Fig. 7-E). This overall weakening of the antioxidant enzyme system directly diminished ROS‑scavenging capacity, leading to significant H₂O₂ accumulation in silenced plants (Fig. 7-F). Thus, PsBAHD6 silencing disrupts ROS metabolic homeostasis under pathogen stress. The phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway was also notably affected. After inoculation, PAL activity in silenced lines decreased by 25.3%−30.8% compared to the control (Fig. 7-G). These results indicate that PsBAHD6 contributes to resistance against Valsa canker by coordinating ROS metabolism and activating the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

In this study, 45-day-old, healthy-growing Korla fragrant pear seedlings were selected as experimental materials and placed in a pear seedling growth chamber maintained at a constant temperature of 25 °C under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod. These seedlings were grown from seeds extracted from mature fruits of the Korla fragrant pear, which were sourced from the pear resource nursery of Tarim University in Xinjiang, China. This plant material represents a widely cultivated horticultural variety. For the purpose of this study, the collection of wild plant resources or endangered species listed in the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES) was not involved. The plant material was formally identified by Professor Bao Jianping. No voucher specimen was deposited for this study, as the plant material is a widely cultivated variety obtained from a recognized germplasm resource nursery (the pear resource nursery of Tarim University). For genome-wide gene family identification, the reference genome of Korla fragrant pear was obtained from the Pear Genome Database (http://pearomics.njau.edu.cn/about_us). Pathogenic strains for Valsa canker were isolated and purified from symptomatic bark tissue of infected Korla fragrant pear trees. Based on morphological characteristics and molecular sequencing analysis, the pathogenic isolates were identified as Valsa pyri (Supplementary Fig. S1). In subsequent inoculation experiments, the identified Valsa pyri pathogen was cultured upside down on potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium at 25 °C in the dark for 7 days. For inoculation, a sterile punch (2 mm in diameter) was used to collect the most vigorous fungal plugs from the edge of the colony. These plugs were then placed directly onto pre-made slight wound sites and onto plates spread with callus, with the mycelium-facing side downward. After inoculation, the plants were maintained under suitable temperature and humidity conditions, and disease development was observed and recorded at regular intervals.

Genome-wide identification of the BAHD gene family

Genome-wide identification and sequence analysis of the BAHD gene family

The Hidden Markov Model (HMM) [22] file of the BAHD-specific domain (PF02458) was downloaded from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org) [23]. Candidate BAHD proteins were initially identified in the Korla fragrant pear genome using hmmsearch tool. To ensure accurate identification, all candidate sequences were subsequently validated through the CDD database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd) [24] to confirm the presence of a complete and intact BAHD domain (PF02458). All subsequent analyses in this study were based on these high-confidence BAHD genes. The ExPASy ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam) was then employed to calculate the physicochemical properties of BAHD family proteins, including amino acid length, molecular weight, and isoelectric point.

Chromosomal localization and gene structure analysis

The chromosomal positions of BAHD genes were obtained from the pear genome annotation file. The chromosomal distribution of genes was visualized using TBtools (version v2.376) software [25]. Based on the genome annotation file, the exon–intron structure of BAHD genes was analyzed, and plotted with TBtools for comparative analysis.

Multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis

The BAHD protein sequences from representative plants including Pyrus bretschneideri, Pyrus communis, Malus domestica, and Arabidopsis thaliana, were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Together with the identified pear BAHD sequences, all sequences were first aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm for multiple sequence alignment. Subsequently, a phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the alignment using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method in MEGA 12 software. The robustness of the tree topology was assessed with bootstrap analysis using 1000 replicates. The evolutionary tree was further edited and annotated using the iTOL tool (https://itol.embl.de) [26]. Conserved motifs in the BAHD protein sequences were identified using MEME software [27], with the maximum number of motifs set to 20 and motif width ranging from 6 to 100 amino acid residues. The identified motifs, along with known functional domains and the phylogenetic tree, were integrated and visualized using TBtools.

Gene duplication and collinearity analysis

Gene duplication events within the BAHD family were analyzed with MCScanX software. Conserved syntenic blocks across the pear genome were identified via BLASTP alignment with an E-value threshold of 1e-10 using MCScanX [28]. Duplication events and collinearity patterns were visualized using Circos and TBtools, respectively. Protein sequences of European pear, apple, and Arabidopsis thaliana were downloaded from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). MCScanX software was used to perform whole-genome collinearity analysis between Korla fragrant pear and each of these species, and to identify homologous BAHD gene pairs. The Ka/Ks values were calculated using KaKs_Calculator 2.0 to assess selective pressure. Generally, Ka/Ks > 1, = 1, and < 1 indicate positive selection, neutral evolution, and purifying selection, respectively.

Valsa canker stress treatment and verification of disease resistance function

qRT-PCR analysis for gene expression patterns

Healthy, uniformly grown seedlings of Korla fragrant pear were selected for inoculation with the Valsa canker pathogen. Inoculated leaves were collected at 0, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96 and 120 h post-inoculation (hpi). All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80℃ until RNA extraction. Total RNA was isolated using a commercial kit, and cDNA was synthesized with a reverse transcription kit. Gene-specific primers for all BAHD acyltransferase family genes in Korla fragrant pear were designed using Primer-BLAST (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/), an online primer design tool provided by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (Supplementary Table S, S2 and Fig. S4). All primers used for qRT-PCR were validated for specificity, and the specificity of amplification products was further confirmed by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis, with all products exhibiting a single band of the expected size. qRT-PCR was performed on a real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR system using SYBR Green Premix, with the Tublin gene serving as the internal control. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method [29].

Determination of physiological and biochemical indicators

The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT), and phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), along with the hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) content, were measured using commercial assay kits (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Subcellular localization by transient expression in tobacco

Young, fully expanded leaves from approximately 5-week-old Nicotiana tabacum plants were used for transient transformation. The coding sequence of PsBAHD6 was fused to green fluorescent protein (GFP) and cloned into a plant expression vector. The resulting recombinant plasmid was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. The bacterial suspension was infiltrated into tobacco leaves using a needle‑less syringe, with the 35S: GFP empty vector serving as a control. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. After 48–72 h of incubation, fluorescent signals were observed under a laser confocal microscope.

Generation of transgenic pear callus

Single colonies of Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 harboring the PsBAHD6-GFP (Supplementary Fig. S2) construct were cultured in LB liquid medium supplemented with appropriate antibiotics at 30 °C with shaking (220 rpm) for 2 days until turbid. The bacteria were then transferred to 60 mL of fresh antibiotic-resistant LB medium with an inoculum size of 1% to 2%. After reaching an OD600 of 0.6–0.8, once activated, the cells were collected by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 5 min and resuspended in CMS liquid medium. Acetosyringone was added to the resuspension to reach a final concentration of 100 µM, and the suspension was incubated at 28 °C with gentle shaking (120 rpm) for 45 min. Wild-type pear callus (from the Pear Research Center Laboratory of Nanjing Agricultural University) that had been pre-cultured for 15 days was used for transformation. The callus pieces were immersed in the activated bacterial suspension and agitated gently at 28 °C with 120 rpm for 20 min. After removing the excess bacterial solution using sterile filter paper, the callus tissue was evenly spread on the co-culture medium and incubated in the dark for 2 days. Subsequently, the tissue was transferred to a selective medium CMS (containing 200 µL/L hygromycin and 750 µL/L cephalosporin antibiotics) and cultured in the dark in a constant-temperature tissue culture room for approximately one month. Emerging resistant callus tissue was sub-cultured onto fresh selective medium for further proliferation [30].

Validation of virus-induced gene silencing

A 300-bp fragment from the coding region of PsBAHD6 gene was selected based on silencing-efficiency prediction via the SolGenomics VIGS tool (https://vigs.solgenomics.net) and cloned into the pTRV2 vector (Supplementary Fig. S3). Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 carrying the recombinant or empty vectors was cultured in a 20 mL suspension with shaking. The infiltration buffer was prepared as follows (per 100 mL): 5 mL MES stock solution, 5 mL MgCl2 stock solution, 90 mL distilled water, and 75 µL acetosyringone. Bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min, resuspended, and adjusted to an OD600 absorbance of 0.6–0.8. The bacterial suspension was then mixed with infiltration buffer and activated at 28 °C for at least 1 h. For plant infiltration, the mixture of pTRV1 and pTRV2-PsBAHD6 was used as the experimental group, while the mixture of pTRV1 and empty pTRV2 served as the control. Seedlings of Korla fragrant pear with uniform and healthy growth were selected. The activated bacterial suspension was infiltrated into three basal leaves per seedling using a needle-less syringe [30]. The experimental group pTRV-PsBAHD6 and the control group pTRV) contained at least 15 independent seedlings, and all experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates. After infiltration, plants were kept in darkness for 24 h and then maintained under normal growth conditions for approximately one week. One week later, assess the silencing efficiency by qRT-PCR. Select experimental seedlings with a silencing level greater than 50% compared to the control group for subsequent inoculation treatment.

Statistical analysis

All experiments in this study were independently repeated at least three times (n ≥ 3 biological replicates). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 31.0) software and Duncan's multiple comparison test was employed to assess the significance of differences between groups. GraphPad Prism 9.0 software was used for graph generation, and lesion areas were quantified using ImageJ. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The Korla fragrant pear, a premium fruit tree native to Xinjiang, China, faces significant challenges in its industrial development due to Valsa canker. This disease not only stunts seedling growth and reduces fruiting rates but also compromises fruit quality, leading to substantial economic losses [31]. One essential plant defense strategy against pathogens is the biosynthesis of antibacterial compounds through secondary metabolic pathways [32]. The BAHD acyltransferase family, which plays a central role in plant secondary metabolism, regulates the synthesis, stability, and bioactivity of these antibacterial compounds by catalyzing acylation reactions [13, 33].

The BAHD acyltransferase family regulates biological activity mainly through the acylation of secondary metabolites, including anthocyanins and terpenoids, and thereby participates in diverse plant physiological processes such as disease resistance and defense responses [8, 34]. Following inoculation with a Valsa canker pathogen, qRT-PCR-based expression profiling of 42 BAHD family members revealed a significant up-regulation of PsBAHD6, suggesting its potential central role in the defense response of Korla fragrant pear against Valsa canker. To further explore the function of PsBAHD6 in disease resistance, we generated transgenic pear fruit callus overexpressing PsBAHD6 and Korla fragrant pear seedlings with specifically silenced PsBAHD6. In the overexpression system, lesion area significantly decreased after inoculation, accompanied by marked upregulation of the antioxidant enzyme system and lower H2O2 content compared to WT. In contrast, silencing of PsBAHD6 led to enlarged lesions, decreased activities of SOD, POD, and CAT, and substantial accumulation of H2O2. The simultaneous enhancement of SOD and CAT directly mitigates oxidative stress caused by superoxide anions and hydrogen peroxide, thereby suppressing uncontrolled lipid peroxidation and programmed cell death [35, 36]. Moreover, the significant upregulation of POD not only aids in H2O2 removal but also provides essential substrates [37–39] for subsequent lignin polymerization in the cell wall. Therefore, in PsBAHD6-overexpressing materials, the efficiency of H2O2 removal and utilization may be enhanced, which reduces nonspecific oxidative damage while potentially directing H2O2 toward cell wall reinforcement. We compared the changes in key physiological indicators (SOD, POD, CAT, PAL activities; H2O2 content) between PsBAHD6-overexpressing callus and PsBAHD6-silenced seedlings following infection by Valsa canker. In both systems, the direction of PsBAHD6-mediated changes was completely consistent (Figs. 6 and 7). Overexpression of PsBAHD6 in callus led to increased activities of SOD, POD, CAT, and PAL, along with reduced accumulation of H2O2 after pathogen infection. Conversely, silencing of PsBAHD6 in seedlings resulted in decreased activities of these enzymes and elevated H2O2 levels. This interaction pattern suggests that PsBAHD6 positively regulates both antioxidant capacity and phenylpropanoid metabolism in response to Valsa canker.

The significant activation of phenylpropanoid metabolism linked to the ROS module resulted in a notable increase in PAL activity in the flesh callus of overexpressed pear fruits after inoculation, whereas PAL activity decreased in silenced pear seedlings. As the rate-limiting enzyme in phenylpropanoid metabolism, PAL typically dictates the flux of defense-related metabolites such as lignin, flavonoids, chlorogenic acid, and phenolic amines [40, 41]. PsBAHD6 not only regulates the ROS homeostasis but also enhances structural and chemical defenses by promoting PAL-controlled metabolism flux. This aligns with observations in fruit tree pathosystems, where increased activity of phenylpropane-related enzymes correlates with enhanced lignification and improved resistance in pear fruits following pathogen infection [42, 43]. Notably, the lignin polymerization process mediated by POD depends on H2O2 as the oxidation substrate, while PAL determines the supply of lignin precursors. These two processes synergistically interact in time and space, forming a complete closed-loop system encompassing substrate supply, oxidation polymerization, and wall layer curing [39]. Based on these findings, we speculate that PsBAHD6 promotes rapid cell wall fortification, thereby limiting pathogen spread. BAHD enzymes link phenylpropane metabolites with cell wall structural units, enabling the metabolites to act as an antioxidant/antibacterial molecules and to be esterified into the cell wall, thereby strengthening the physical barrier.

In this study, the BAHD acyltransferase gene family in Korla fragrant pears was identified, revealing 42 members. Studies have reported the number of BAHD genes varies significantly not only across different species but also among cultivars within the same species, including 116 in poplar [44], 55 in barley [45], 46 in bananas [12], and 81 in white pear [46]. The variation in BAHD gene copy members across species likely reflects evolutionary differences in metabolic requirements and lineage-specific patterns of gene family expansion or contraction. Members of the PsBAHD family are unevenly distributed on chromosomes, a pattern similar to the distribution characteristics of the BAHD family in other cultivated varieties of Pyrus [46]. Phylogenetic analysis categorized 42 PsBAHD genes into five evolutionary branches, consistent with the classification of BAHD families into 5 to 10 branches in other species [13, 47]. Each branch features a specific conserved motif composition, including the typical HXXXD catalytic motif and DFGWG structural motif. This structural conservation is also observed in BAHD families of Arabidopsis thaliana [48] and Allium fistulosum [11], indicating that the core catalytic architecture is largely preserved across species [49, 50]. The BAHD acyltransferase family plays a crucial role in plant stress and defense responses across species. In rice, for example, the BAHD gene STBR1 confers dual resistance to both salt-alkali stress and blast disease by scavenging hydrogen peroxide through stabilizing the atypical catalase CATA [51]. This finding provides direct evidence for the crucial role of this enzyme class in plant disease defense. Similarly, in Arabidopsis, the BAHD member EPS1 is essential for disease resistance, as its loss of function impairs salicylic acid accumulation and thereby enhances susceptibility to bacterial and fungal pathogens [17]. The conserved yet functionally diverse roles of BAHD genes across plant species suggest that PsBAHD6 may also perform a distinct function in disease resistance mechanisms. Promoter analysis identified an abundance of stress- and defense-related cis-elements, such as MeJA- and ABA-responsive motifs, as well as fungal-induction-related W-box and AS-1 elements, supporting regulated involvement in defense responses [45]. Collinearity analysis indicated that segmental duplication has driven family expansion, with Ka/Ks values < 1 for all duplicated pairs, reflecting strong purifying selection and functional conservation a pattern also observed in Leonurus japonicus [10].

The main limitations of the current study lie at both the molecular and physiological levels. At the molecular level, constrained by experimental conditions, we have not yet been able to systematically elucidate the interacting proteins and transcriptional regulatory network of PsBAHD6. At the physiological level, its disease-resistant activity has only been validated under specific pathogen strains and controlled environments. Its broad-spectrum efficacy against different pathogenic strains, effectiveness in complex field environments, and regulatory conservation across different pear cultivars remain to be further clarified. Therefore, follow-up research will focus on two main directions: On one hand, it is necessary to establish platforms for interaction screening and transcriptomic analysis to deeply unravel the molecular interactions and downstream regulatory network of PsBAHD6. On the other hand, a comprehensive evaluation of the stability and applicability of its disease-resistant function should be conducted under conditions involving multiple pathogen species, various cultivars, and semi-natural field environments. This will provide a more comprehensive and reliable basis for its practical application in disease-resistant pear breeding.

Conclusion

This study systematically identified 42 BAHD gene family members in Korla fragrant pears. Bioinformatics analyses indicated function conservation within this family, and cis-regulatory element profiling of their promoters suggested a close association with stress responses. A key gene, PsBAHD6, which exhibited a marked response to Valsa canker infection, was successfully identified. Subcellular localization revealed that PsBAHD6 is localized to both the nucleus and plasma membrane. Evidence from stable overexpression and VIGS demonstrates that PsBAHD6 significantly enhances resistance to canker disease by synergistically activating the antioxidant enzyme system (SOD, POD, CAT) to maintain oxidative homeostasis and promoting PAL to facilitate the synthesis of defense-related compounds. Thus, our data suggest that PsBAHD6 may play an important role in pear resistance to Valsa canker through the crosstalk between ROS metabolism and the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway. This study provides a theoretical foundation and valuable genetic resources for breeding disease-resistant varieties.
