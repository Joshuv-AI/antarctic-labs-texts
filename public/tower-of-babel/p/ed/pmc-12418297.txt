Addressing the Increasing Rate of Caesarean Sections in an Australian Healthcare System: A Systems Science Approach to Identify Stakeholder Perspectives
Authors: Siobhan O'Halloran, Susan Perlen, Anna Chapman, Sominsky Luba, Samantha L Dawson, Rachael Cusworth, Laura Alston, Vidanka Vasilevski, Linda Sweet, Peter Vuillermin, Alison M Hutchinson, Pregnancy Research and Translation Ecosystem Investigator group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCaesarean section (CS) rates are increasing globally. We aimed to understand stakeholders' perspectives on factors driving CS in pregnancy care to inform areas for intervention. Stakeholders from five health services participated in three Group Model Building workshops to identify the drivers of CS and intervention opportunities. A Causal Loop Diagram was developed to identify priority action areas. Workshop transcripts were thematically analyzed to understand the factors influencing CS rates and to identify areas for interventions to improve CS decision‐making. Participants identified 29 drivers that were grouped into four priority areas for action: mothers' health and confidence, clinicians providing care, clinical monitoring and intervention, and health system issues. Five major themes emerged from the transcripts: health system issues, maternal factors, clinical factors, clinical management, and guidelines and adherence. Intervention ideas included: feedback data use across health services, improving the quality of data, and modifying data entry prompts. The factors driving CS rates related to clinicians, maternal service users, the health system, and the health service. A standardized audit and feedback tool to assess, monitor, and compare CS rates within health systems may address CS overuse.Keywords: caesarean section, infants, midwives, pregnancy care, pregnancy care clinicians, systems science, systems thinking, women

Introduction

Caesarean section (CS) is a surgical intervention that, when medically indicated, can reduce the risk of harm to both the mother and infant (World Health Organization 2015). However, CSs are associated with longer hospital stays, increased risk for future pregnancies, bladder injuries, psychological distress, anesthetic complications, and obstetric shock (Sandall et al. 2018). In addition, CSs are associated with higher rates of infection and slower recovery for women compared with having an uncomplicated vaginal birth (Bodner et al. 2011). For term infants, CSs are associated with higher rates of neonatal intensive care admission, lower rates of breastfeeding initiation, and increased rates of childhood asthma (Sandall et al. 2018).

Globally, the CS rate is estimated to be 21%, and in developed countries, CSs account for approximately 27% of births (Betran et al. 2021). By 2030, the global average CS rate is projected to increase to approximately 29%, with more than 38 million caesarean births (Betran et al. 2021; World Health Organization 2021). In Australia, in 2020, 36% of all women gave birth via CS, which is comparable to other high‐income countries such as Ireland (31%) and the United States (33%) (European Perinatal Health 2018). In the Australian state of Victoria, where our study is based, the CS rate has increased from 37.2% in 2019 to 39.2% in 2021 (Safer Care Victoria 2022). Notably, research has found that CS rates above 19% are not associated with improvements in maternal and neonatal mortality (Molina et al. 2015). Unsurprisingly, the increasing CS rates have raised concerns about using this procedure when not medically justified and have become a key focal point for preventative health (Chapman et al. 2019), with the World Health Organization calling for a reduction in the rates of unnecessary CSs (World Health Organization 2018).

The factors driving the increase in CS rates are complex. A change in maternal socio‐demographic characteristics, an increase in pre‐existing medical conditions (i.e., diabetes), the availability of health professionals with the necessary expertise, differences in professional practice approaches (Betrán et al. 2018), and in some women, a fear of labor pain and physical damage during vaginal birth (Stoll et al. 2017) have all been identified as maternal, social, and clinical contributing factors.

Given the continued rise in CS rates across the globe, there is a collective drive to better understand the views and experiences of women and health professionals, as well as the complexity of the health system that leads to the high use of CS. Recent evidence has shown that prioritizing a vaginal birth, together with enhanced communication and adequate resourcing, may reduce CS rates according to the views of pregnant Irish women and clinicians (Gallagher et al. 2022). In England, findings from health care professionals and women showed that an acceptable and improved care pathway should include providing key birthing information, appropriate referrals, and improved timing of CS decision‐making (Kenyon et al. 2016). Whereas in Sweden, interviewed health professionals emphasized that the mode of delivery is a professional decision, with shared decision‐making taken into consideration through information and support (Fredriksson et al. 2024). To date, researchers have not reported the comprehensive system of factors from the perspectives of stakeholders that may be driving the increased CS rates in Victoria, Australia. Methods

Study Design

The Pregnancy Research and Translation Ecosystem (PRT‐E) is a research program involving six health services (predominantly regional/rural services with one metropolitan service) delivering pregnancy care in the southwest of the state of Victoria, Australia. The Australian Government Modified Monash Model (MMM) classification was used to define metropolitan, regional, and rural areas (Australian Government Department of Health and Aged Care 2024). The overall study methodology has been described elsewhere (O'Halloran, Cusworth, et al. 2025; O'Halloran, Sunder, et al. 2025). A participatory systems science‐based approach called Group Model Building (GMB) was used to develop a shared understanding of the complexity of CS, to allow for the visualization of the non‐linear relationships between cause and effect and for the codesign of potential intervention solutions. Systems science participatory methods are acknowledged as useful tools for engaging stakeholders, and the GMB approach has previously been applied to understand complex problems such as childhood obesity (Allender et al. 2015; Bolton et al. 2022; Finegood et al. 2010; Savona et al. 2023), local government and community health issues (Browne et al. 2021; O'Halloran et al. 2022, 2024) and retail food environments (Vargas et al. 2024; Whelan et al. 2021).

Participants

The PRT‐E study team, comprised of clinicians specializing in pregnancy care and transdisciplinary researchers, with expertise in systems science, mental health, and nutrition, conducted this research and was responsible for participant recruitment, facilitation of workshops, data analysis, and reporting. The Pregnancy Stakeholder Working Group (SWG) included transdisciplinary researchers from the PRT‐E study team and at least one midwife, obstetrician, pediatrician, or psychiatrist involved in pregnancy care from each of the six participating health services and maternity service users. Any health professionals who were involved in pregnancy care at the six participating health services were eligible to join the SWG and were then invited to join the SWG via email by the PRT‐E study team. SWG members were eligible to participate if they were ≥ 18 years of age and had access to online platforms for workshops. Additionally, health professionals had to have experience working in pregnancy or maternity care services. Maternity service users needed to be receiving or had recently received pregnancy care through one of the six health services. SWG attendance at the workshops was dependent on workplace demands and competing responsibilities (O'Halloran, Cusworth, et al. 2025; O'Halloran, Sunder, et al. 2025). The Barwon Health Research Ethics Committee approved the study (Project Number: 21/162). Participants provided written consent before participating in the first workshop.

Data Collection

To co‐design a visual representation of stakeholder perspectives on the drivers influencing the use of CS, we used methods from systems science: GMB and Causal Loop Diagramming (Hovmand 2014). The GMB involved capturing a shared understanding of the factors driving the complexity of CS rates with members of the SWG who were able to attend the workshops by creating a Causal Loop Diagram (CLD) using a systems software program called Systems Thinking in Community Knowledge Exchange (STICKE Version 3). The CLD enabled the visualization of the complex range of drivers and the non‐linear cause‐and‐effect connections. From the diagram, drivers that could be prioritized for action and future interventions to support improvement in maternity care were identified.

Between March and July 2022, the PRT‐E study team and members of the SWG completed three, approximately 2‐h online GMB workshops using the Zoom platform (Zoom Video Communications Inc., San Jose, California, United States). The GMB workshop female facilitators (L.A. and S.O. [both of whom were part of the PRT‐E study team], were research fellows who had experience in conducting systems science workshops and had a track record in this area) used specific scripts from the GMB Scriptapedia handbook (including graphs over time, connection circles, model review, and action ideas) to guide the workshop discussion process (Hovmand et al. 2012; Hovmand 2014). The audio from each workshop was recorded, and the recording was transcribed by SmartDocs Transcription Services (Glen Iris, VIC, Australia). Notes were taken during the workshops and were later used to review the CLD.

To begin the development of the CLD during Workshop 1, the group was asked the question: “what factors do you think are driving the rates of CS in your areas?” This question prompted group discussions and allowed for the initial development of the CLD. At Workshop 2, the revised diagram was presented to the group, where the SWG confirmed the modifications, further refined the diagram, and discussed the identified CS rate drivers in‐depth. Workshop 3 involved a review of the refined CLD, and once participants confirmed that the final diagram was an accurate representation of the key drivers of CS rates within the region, then potential actions/interventions to address the rates were developed.

Data Analysis

To understand stakeholder perspectives on the challenges associated with CS in pregnancy care, a thematic analysis of the GMB transcripts was performed by S.O., A.C., and S.P. using NVivo version 12. All transcriptions were read independently for content familiarization, and themes and subthemes were identified and organized via an inductive process (Braun and Clarke 2006). Differences in theming were discussed to achieve consensus. Verbatim quotes support the presentation of the themes and subthemes.

Results

Participants

All participants were female and represented five of the six health services recruited to PRT‐E. One obstetrician, 11 midwives, and one maternity service user participated in at least one GMB workshop. Due to the nature of the workplace, including clinical activity, shift work, and other work commitments of the clinical staff on the day, representatives from each health service varied across workshops. The minimum number of participants required for each GMB workshop was five (this is based on GMB recommendations; Hovmand 2014). Stakeholders' Perspectives of Key Drivers of CS Rates

Five major themes emerged from the analysis of transcripts from the GMB workshops: (1) health systems issues, (2) maternal factors, (3) clinician factors, (4) clinical management, and (5) guidelines and adherence (Figure 2). Across the five themes, 14 sub‐themes were identified and are described within the respective themes in the following sections.

Major themes and subthemes that were identified from the analysis of the workshop transcripts.

Theme 1: Health System Issues

Health System and Service Variability

Participants spoke of the variation across the health system in terms of differences between hospitals. For example, lack of access to specialist staff was identified as a reason for hastened decisions to conduct a CS: “We don't have paediatricians at all. So, our doctors would pull out sooner if they've got concerns rather than what someone with a paediatrician in the building would. So that's sort of where our [CS] rates might differ to a bigger hospital.”

Lack of continuity of care, antenatally and during labor, was identified as an influence on CS rates, which some participants considered a health system issue. One participant stated: “I think it comes down to the health system because they need to implement it [continuity of care].” Similarly, another participant said, “Yeah, that's the outcome, but I think the actual initiative is the health system, that's how it's organised and the workforce.”

Multidisciplinary Team Roles/Roles and Responsibilities

Participants described the benefits of working in a multidisciplinary team when caring for a woman in hospital during labor. A midwife said, “That's what I find as a midwife; it is a lot more team oriented. Everyone has a role, and that is respected, as opposed to working in a [hierarchical] system under a doctor.” Another participant identified the importance of the multidisciplinary team when caring for a woman who is having a CS: “There'll be a whole team around the care of that woman.” Others talked about roles and responsibilities within the team: “I think a greater understanding of the roles each of us can play would actually be really, really useful.”

Time

Participants discussed the rising rate of CS over recent years and agreed that there are times when CSs are required. It was also acknowledged that timing was important when deciding to perform a CS, for instance, if it is very late at night or to expedite the birth of the baby in a timely manner. “I think there are some times where it's just due to it being late at night, ‘let's get this done’. And our doctors very rarely offer a trial of scar, they just recommend caesars to women. So, I think there's definitely room for improvement.” Another participant said, “you're right about the time, like, I mean really within a hospital, if you're 24 hours and you've used so many methods of induction of labour, you're straight to Caesar.” A different participant talked about decisions to progress to CS being made too hastily, without giving the woman time to dilate and labor naturally: “I think a lot of ours, if we gave them just that bit longer, they would dilate.”

Theme 2: Maternal Factors

Maternal Knowledge (Education) and Understanding

Participants described how an emergency CS can be traumatizing for women as it is often unexpected and deviates from their birth plan. Regarding personal beliefs, one participant mentioned that beliefs can lead to a fear of birth. They said, “We've had a few women who have come in with a belief system and have had some serious adverse outcomes as a result of that”. Another participant suggested that sometimes women request medical interventions to suit their lifestyles, using induction as an example. “It's not just all medically‐driven, like we'll have women who want to be induced to fit around certain social things that are happening, or their partners are on leave, and so yeah, I think the goal post might have changed. A participant agreed, noting that women also request a CS, Yeah, because most women, absolutely, most women come in and go, ‘I want a caesarean section. I am not birthing this baby vaginally’. So the number of women who want a vaginal birth is vanishingly small. And yes, the numbers are going up.”

One participant described how, during antenatal appointments, unless there is a major concern about a woman's birth, there are missed opportunities to provide sufficient information about birthing options. Acuity was another contributing factor mentioned, where a high acuity situation can lead to less discussion and information provision: “Some women, they don't want all of that decision‐making discussion or education. They just want it done for them [CS], and that's fine.”

Maternal Risk Profile

One participant shared her experience, where women were told that they needed to be induced because their baby was large, which could lead to a third‐ or fourth‐degree tear, or that they could have a still‐birth or shoulder dystocia (where shoulders become wedged during a vaginal birth) leading to neonatal hypoxia. In these instances, a CS was offered as an option to mitigate the risks. The participant explained that most women would choose to have a CS after hearing that information because it was perceived as less risky. “When you're talking about risk to your baby, like as soon as someone mentions that there's going to be a risk to your baby, like it's really hard to then sort of try and stay calm and make a decision.”

Theme 3: Clinical Factors

Clinician Education and Training/Resources

Participants spoke of the importance of clinical education around CS and the need for gaps in knowledge and skillsets to be identified and targeted for improvement, (e.g., delivery via vaginal breach births) rather than providing a one‐size‐fits‐all approach to education and training. Others spoke of the benefit of working closely with a more experienced clinician: “Doing actually more shadowing of a more senior doctor when you're an RMO [Resident Medical Officer] is potentially going to not only increase your [birthing] experience but also your confidence in the information that you're providing.” Another participant talked about staff training, stating: “If we had a safe service, I think more women would choose it [vaginal breach births]. But we are saying to them,” “Look, our staff aren't necessarily trained to do this.” So yeah, there's a gap in how to get there, but I think if we were saying, “We feel safe to provide this service as long as you're aware of the risks here, here, and here, but our staff are trained.”

Clinician Skills, Experience, and Confidence

Participants talked about clinician confidence and gave an example of working with junior midwives and a junior doctor who are not very confident in how labor is progressing, which may result in a CS. Others indicated that improving clinician confidence and skills was necessary, stating, “If we can influence any small part, that is going to be a big gamechanger, so the confidence and skill are whether they bail out and go for a Caesar rather than have the confidence to sit and let these women continue [with a vaginal birth]. So, I think we probably shouldn't underestimate that one. That's probably going to have the biggest ripple effect. If we can make a small dent in that [clinician confidence], it should have better outcomes.”

Theme 4: Clinical Management

Labor Management and Clinical Monitoring

Participants highlighted the importance of optimizing antenatal care to address risk factors for birth complications such as fetal malposition or malpresentation (when the fetus is not in the ideal position for birth) “And if we can prevent them coming into labor in an OP [Occiput Posterior] position, then life would be sweet.” Another added, “I think we've got to backpedal all the way back to 34 weeks and start talking about your fetal positioning and preventing breech more than down the line because also offering more ECV [external cephalic version] if it [breech position] does happen to occur as well would be ideal before we get to the point where we're thinking of moving ahead with vaginal breeches.”

Clinical Interventions

Participants indicated that improving the assessment of women prior to recommending induction of labor may reduce the need for unnecessary interventions leading to CS. One said, “it's to do with her cervix basically – so it's to do with does she need a couple more days? Is that going to be of any harm to leave her a couple of more days to try and get her body a little bit closer to being ready for the priming?” Another participant said, “It could be related to clinician experience as to the priming and getting women into labour, so maybe that's something we can focus on, the experience of starting, putting a balloon [catheter] in or giving Prostin [an induction agent] and things like that.” A participant put it another way, “And like we mentioned before, there's the timelines as well. You know, we're wanting people to not spend hours of their labor in hospital, but obviously, as soon as you try to control that, you're messing with the physiology. So, it's difficult working in a hospital, obviously, with the time constraints and staffing constraints. But I think acknowledging that then if you're not waiting for someone to go through the full labor process, that you are messing with the physiology and then increasing the risk of C section.”

Models of Care (Continuity of Care)

Participants agreed that continuity of care positively impacts a woman's confidence in the birthing process and leads to better outcomes for women that are at risk of CS. Continuity of midwifery care was described as the gold standard in terms of minimizing interventions. Participants spoke positively of continuity of care in labor and midwife‐led models of care: “I think it comes back to the continuity of care because if you're seeing the same person, you're not repeating your history over and over again. Which takes up your time. And I feel that continuity is the key, kind of ensuring that discussion [around birth options and possible outcomes] is kind of a continuous thing that happens throughout the care cycle, and it's not as time‐ consuming because you know the woman and you have that relationship. You know her history and then when it comes to making these decisions, it's not as time‐consuming.”

However, participants also added that fragmented care (a model of antenatal/birthing/postnatal care involving a range of health professionals instead of one person/one team) is often the norm for women. One participant stated, “Antenatally as well, even with the obstetric doctors because—I don't know how other places function, but ours here may not see the same—if they need to see a doctor, they won't see the same doctor, like they'll be lucky to see them twice.”

Framing the Problem

Risk aversion was a key contributor to decision‐making around CS: “One reason I think that is, you know, people are always frightened, this kind of fear of medico‐legal stuff. But that's not really what it is. It's fear of someone having a terrible outcome, and you're feeling like you could have done something differently. And the way that we, you know, the things that feed into clinician fear are a little bit different to perception of safety.” Another participant highlighted the challenge in deciding to perform a CS, “But there's a lot of fear in decisions as well in that if I let her labour for another 4‐hours and then, you know, an adverse outcome happens, then I'm going to be asked why I didn't do a caesarean earlier.”

Decision‐Making and Communication

Participants suggested that effective communication is key when talking with women about their birth, which involves exchanging perspectives and actively discussing issues to facilitate decision‐ making, rather than a one‐way conversation where birthing information is provided by the clinician to the woman. A participant reflected, “When I was later in my pregnancy, my daughter was always quite small, and then my fundal height was measuring low, according to the obstetrician. And so, she basically said that, you know, there's risks to your baby. We recommend induction. There was no explanation of the risks of induction.”

Participants also emphasized the importance of how the information is worded and delivered, so women feel empowered and can make informed decisions about their birth: “And I think we forget that actually the role is to provide enough information to birthing people to make an informed decision.” Being certain that the woman understands the information rather than presuming that she does was also mentioned.

Theme 5: Guidelines and Adherence

Guidelines and Evidence

Participants identified a need for clinical practice guidelines, the adherence to those guidelines, and auditing to ensure guideline adherence: “I think another point might just be in terms of having clear evidence‐based clinical practice guidelines, but also just, I guess mapping them against Safer Care Victoria's ones and identifying any gaps between the services compared to Safer Care Victoria.”

Another participant said, “Then I've got ones [guidelines] that are targeted towards health professionals, so a lot of them have to do with combining the use of clinical practice guidelines with some kind of secondary options, so you've got your mandatory second opinion where some hospitals introduced that.”

Audit and Feedback

Participants discussed the difficulty in auditing adherence to guidelines. “I feel like it's hard to try and even do that just at [our hospital], try to do an induction audit. … God, the data collection part is so tiring.” Another added that promoting adherence to guidelines would ensure a systematic approach for decision making and the advice women receive regarding recommendations for birth, “I think in the US [United States] they've done a few audits on adherence to – like when they've put all these bundles and adherence to guidelines, some of the hospitals have implemented reviewing these things and then giving the staff feedback and things like that. Especially if you're going to have like a standardised path‐based management, labour or induction, and you want it to be systematic for everybody.” Another spoke about the importance of audit and feedback, which could prompt clinicians to reflect on their own practices: “Yeah, and I guess the other thing is audit and feedback can be done at the unit level but also at the individual level, so the individual clinicians get feedback on their rates compared to everybody else, so that's often the powerful intervention.”

Discussion

This is one of the first studies to apply a systems thinking approach to understand stakeholders' perspectives on the factors contributing to CS use, identify areas for action, and identify potential interventions to reduce CS rates in the region. Notably, the key local factors driving CS rates identified by our participants spanned several levels, from the health system and the health service levels to the individual clinician and maternal levels, which adds to the global research focused on reducing CS rates (Chapman et al. 2019; Panda et al. 2018, 2022). Our findings revealed five common themes among the drivers of CS rates in southwest Victoria: health systems issues, maternal factors, clinical factors, clinical management and guidelines, and adherence. Of the 14 subthemes, which all played an important role in decisions to progress to CS, according to our participants, five were a primary focus: health system and service variability; maternal knowledge (education) and clinical understanding; clinician skills, experience, and confidence; models of care; guidelines and evidence. The three key recommendations for interventions to optimize CS rates identified by our participants were new findings and included: (1) data use across the health service, (2) data quality, and (3) improved data entry prompts. According to our participants, these are fundamental in addressing the increasing CS rates across the region. Our findings echo the Australian Maternity Services Strategy, highlighting the importance of woman‐reported data, maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality data, and data linkage to ensure the delivery of safe and quality maternity care delivery (Australian Government Department of Health 2019).

In the context of the subtheme health systems and service variability, the Australian Maternity Services Strategy specifies that women should have access to appropriate maternity care in their geographic location; yet the strategy is yet to be fully realized in rural Australia (Australian Government Department of Health 2019) Timely access to healthcare and maternity services in rural areas is limited and less reliable compared with regional and metropolitan areas (Kildea et al. 2015; Kornelsen and Stoll 2023; Kozhimannil et al. 2022). This is partly due to the limited availability of health professionals with the necessary skills and confidence to deliver maternity services (Kozhimannil et al. 2016; Longman et al. 2017). On the one hand, the lack of access to health professionals skilled in the conduct of CSs can lead to risks for women and infants and require their transfer to other health services. However, even with access to doctors skilled in CSs, lack of access to pediatric specialist services or after‐hours theater services and anesthetists may affect the threshold for CS versus expectant labor management (Kornelsen et al. 2023; Panda et al. 2022). In the absence of specialist expertise, balancing these presents a dilemma for health professionals in rural and regional settings. Other barriers to implementing the strategy include a lack of rural community consultation, access to appropriate governance of these services, and overestimation of risks (Longman et al. 2017).

Regarding the subtheme maternal knowledge (education) and the influence this has on CS rates of women's requests and preferences for CS, the findings from the literature are mixed (Panda et al. 2020). Authors of a systematic review of midwives' and obstetricians' perceptions of factors influencing decisions to perform a CS found that women's requests had a prominent influence on the decision (Panda et al. 2018). On the other hand, the findings of three other reviews indicated that women's requests for CS had a minimal influence on CS rates (Gamble and Creedy 2000; Mazzoni et al. 2011; McCourt et al. 2007). Panda et al. (2020) suggested that the differences may be explained by variations in the perceptions of women and health professionals, health professionals' views about risk influencing women's requests, and health professionals construing concerns expressed by women as a request for CS (Panda et al. 2020).

Our participants spoke of risk aversion in the context of clinician skills, experience, and confidence and the impact this has on CS rates, which has been explored in the literature. For example, a review of the evidence on behavioral factors relating to fear of litigation as a driver for CSs concluded that interactions between cognitive factors (in the form of ambiguity aversion and biases), social and cultural factors (pressure from women, social norms, and a blame culture), and environmental factors (legal, insurance, medical and professional, media) led to fear of litigation as the impetus for CSs (Elaraby et al. 2023). Furthermore, in a study investigating woman‐physician interactions in decision‐making about CS, vaginal births were not preferred when interactions occurred between risk‐averse physicians and women who were anxious and lacked confidence in the physician's willingness to support a normal vaginal birth (Doraiswamy et al. 2021). The researchers concluded that insufficient communication and challenges in decision‐making contributed to the high CS rates.

In midwifery, continuity of care models comprise care delivery led by a midwife or group of midwives throughout the antepartum and intrapartum periods. In most models, the care extends to the postpartum period (Bradford et al. 2022). Midwife‐led continuity of care models are typically available to women with low‐risk pregnancies, although some services offer all‐risk models. Participants in our study identified that lack of continuity of care as potentially influencing CS rates, which is consistent with the literature. For example, authors of a Cochrane review of 17 studies comparing midwifery continuity of care models with other models of care on outcomes for women and infants concluded that women receiving midwifery continuity of care models were more likely to have a spontaneous vaginal birth and less likely to undergo a CS (Sandall et al. 2024). The authors also found that women receiving midwifery continuity of care were more likely to report positive experiences during the pregnancy, labor, and postpartum periods. In addition, research conducted in a rural Australian hospital to evaluate women's experiences of a Midwifery Group Practice model of maternity showed that women were highly satisfied with the care received (Whitburn et al. 2024). Implementing midwifery continuity of care in regional and rural settings is thus an important strategy for improving quality of care, shared decision‐making, and potentially reducing CS rates (Brundell et al. 2022).

Regarding guidelines and evidence, variation in clinical practice regarding CS was discussed by our participants, which is perhaps unsurprising, as practice variations can occur when clear guidance about CS decision‐making is lacking. For example, following a systematic analysis of 49 clinical guidelines for planned CSs showed that indications for CS were generally consistent. However, there was little guidance on shared decision‐making and the recommended timing for CS (Coates et al. 2020). Ensuring that clinical guidelines are consistent and adhered to is likely to overcome variability in practice and encourage women to make informed decisions regarding CS with their health providers (World Health Organization 2018). This approach may also structure interactions with women who request CS because whether a woman's request is adhered to is dependent on a range of clinician and woman‐related factors (Panda et al. 2020).

Strengths and Limitations

Key strengths of our study include the novel use of systems thinking approaches, which allowed pregnancy care clinicians to consider the use of CS as a complex systematic problem and thereby identify the driving factors at multiple levels, ranging from health system factors to maternal and clinician factors. Our methodology was underpinned by systems science and co‐design approaches that allow participants to have an equal opportunity to contribute to the discussions. The presentation of the CLD to the stakeholders during each workshop was an iterative process that enabled the stakeholders to confirm or refute the representation and interpretation of the discussions. Analyzing the GMB workshop transcripts allowed for in‐depth insights into areas of concern and priorities for action. Lastly, we included representatives from several health services in the south‐west region of Victoria, which allowed for diverse perspectives about CS use to be shared during the GMB workshops. Our findings are consistent with local and international research regarding the complex factors contributing to CS, and we expect that these findings will be applicable in other high‐income countries with similarly designed hospital systems. However, we do acknowledge that the factors driving CS use may be different in low‐and middle‐income countries.

Our study limitations included the low number of rural and regional obstetric participants and the low number of maternity service users who attended the workshops. We acknowledge that input from a greater number of women currently receiving or having recently received pregnancy care would have strengthened the findings. We also acknowledge that the inclusion of mostly midwives likely impacted our findings and that their role in pregnancy care is different from that of obstetricians; thus, their perspectives regarding the overuse of CS in the Australian health care system also likely differed. We did not collect data on the length of time participants had served in their professional roles and recognize that this is also a limitation.

Implications for Future Practice

Although it is widely accepted that current CS rates have gone beyond reasonable justification of need, effective interventions to optimize CS births have shown to be elusive (Betrán et al. 2018). According to our participants, auditing adherence to guidelines and providing timely information to clinicians for decision making to improve patient care and outcomes are important quality improvement processes. One potential intervention option is to implement a repeatable audit and feedback system for aggregating de‐identified CS‐related data from health providers and summarizing and presenting it in a useful format. A classification system that breaks down variation and provides the key action back to a health professional as a strategy to change clinical behavior could be used within the auditing cycle (Boatin et al. 2018). The Robson Classification, which is recommended by the World Health Organization (2017), is the global standard for assessing, monitoring, and comparing CS rates within and between health care facilities and applies obstetric characteristics to group all women admitted for delivery into one of ten mutually exclusive groups (Boatin et al. 2018; World Health Organization 2017). Routinely collected maternity data could be extracted from perinatal databases at health settings and audited using the Robson Classification to provide feedback to health professionals in clinical audit cycles. Implementing a repeatable, useful breakdown of CS rates could allow for benchmarking, inform future interventions, and enable the monitoring of CS use trends over time.

Conclusion

In this study, the views of our participants illustrated that optimizing the use of CS in our region was a complex healthcare challenge arising broadly from maternal, clinician, and health system factors. The key interconnected drivers identified included shared decision‐making approaches, access to specialists, clear and consistent clinical guidelines, and midwifery continuity of care models. Intervention strategies should include reviewing CS‐related data, auditing, and providing feedback to health professionals in clinical audit cycles to reduce CS rates and contribute to improving maternity care experiences and outcomes.
