Nursing competency readiness for integrated primary health care implementation: a multi-province cross-sectional study in Indonesia
Authors: Wiwin Wiarsih, Endang Triyanto, Megah Andriany, Lita Heni Kusumawardani, Kinantika Nur Dewanti, Muhamad Jauhar, Utami Rachmawati, Indah Permata Sari, Ummi Hani, Maula Maratus Sholikhah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntegrated Primary Health Care (IPHC) reform in Indonesia requires a competent nursing workforce capable of delivering coordinated, life-course-oriented, and community-linked primary care. However, evidence on factors associated with nurses’ competency readiness for IPHC implementation remains limited. This study aimed to assess nursing competency readiness for IPHC implementation and examine its associations with knowledge, perceived IPHC implementation, stakeholder support, and professional development needs.MethodsA facility-based analytical cross-sectional study was conducted among 687 nurses recruited through non-probability consecutive sampling from selected public primary health care centres in 15 Indonesian provinces. Data were collected using a structured self-administered questionnaire assessing demographic and professional characteristics, IPHC knowledge, perceived IPHC implementation, stakeholder support, professional development needs, and nursing competency readiness. Reliability testing, descriptive statistics, Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests, Spearman correlation, multiple linear regression, and supplementary logistic regression were performed.ResultsThe mean nursing competency readiness score was 102.32 (SD 15.48). Using the sample median score of 108 as a supplementary analytical cut-off, 53.4% of nurses were classified as having higher competency readiness. Competency readiness was positively correlated with stakeholder support (rho = 0.577), perceived IPHC implementation (rho = 0.496), and professional development needs (rho = 0.474) (all p < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, stakeholder support showed the largest standardized association with competency readiness (β = 0.400), followed by perceived IPHC implementation (β = 0.243) and professional development needs (β = 0.231). The model explained 50.6% of the variance in competency readiness. Supplementary logistic regression using the median-based readiness category showed a similar pattern of associations.ConclusionNursing competency readiness for IPHC implementation was more strongly associated with implementation-related and stakeholder-support factors than with demographic, educational, or knowledge-related characteristics. These findings suggest that implementation environment, stakeholder engagement, and professional development may be relevant areas for workforce planning, but their effects on nursing competency readiness should be tested in longitudinal, mixed-methods, or intervention studies using stronger competency measures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-05010-2.

Introduction

Primary health care (PHC) remains the foundation of equitable, efficient, and sustainable health systems. International evidence consistently shows that PHC-oriented systems are better positioned to improve population health, reduce avoidable health service use, and support progress toward universal health coverage [1]. Recent PHC reforms across countries have increasingly emphasized people-centred care, service integration, multidisciplinary teamwork, digital transformation, and stronger community engagement. These priorities have become more urgent in the context of demographic transition, multimorbidity, non-communicable diseases, persistent communicable diseases, and increasing expectations for accessible and continuous care [2].

Integrated care has emerged as a key strategy to reduce fragmentation and improve coordination across service levels, professional groups, and community-based health systems [3]. Contemporary integrated care frameworks highlight the importance of clinical, professional, organizational, functional, normative, and system-level integration. However, implementation of integrated care remains complex, particularly in low and middle-income countries, where resource constraints, workforce shortages, limited coordination, and variable local implementation capacity may hinder reform progress [4]. Evidence from PHC implementation studies suggests that successful reform requires not only policy commitment but also local actor collaboration, workforce readiness, organizational support, and context-sensitive implementation strategies [5, 6].

Indonesia has introduced Integrated Primary Health Care (IPHC), as part of its broader health system transformation [5]. Operationally, IPHC in Indonesia reorganizes primary care around life-course service clusters and strengthens the linkage between Puskesmas, auxiliary health posts, Posyandu, community health workers, families, and communities. The reform emphasizes promotive and preventive care while maintaining curative, rehabilitative, and palliative functions. It also requires stronger local area monitoring, digital reporting, village-level health dashboards, family visits, home visits, and coordinated follow-up across service levels. For nurses working in Puskesmas, these operational changes expand expectations beyond routine clinical tasks to include population-based assessment, family- and community-oriented care, chronic disease follow-up, care coordination, health education, documentation, and collaboration with community actors. IPHC aims to reorganize primary care services around life-course needs, strengthen promotive and preventive care, and improve linkages between Puskesmas, auxiliary health posts, community-based services, families, and individuals. A recent mixed-methods study in Indonesia showed that readiness for primary health care integration varies across health posts and is influenced by human resources, infrastructure, financing, service organization, and community participation [7]. These findings indicate that IPHC is not merely an administrative restructuring but a complex implementation process that requires competent and supported frontline health workers.

Nurses are central to PHC and integrated care implementation. Across countries, nurses working in primary care contribute to health assessment, health education, chronic disease management, care coordination, screening, follow-up, self-management support, community engagement, and inter-professional collaboration [8]. Systematic reviews have shown that registered nurses in primary care can contribute positively to patient outcomes, system-level outcomes, patient satisfaction, preventive care, and chronic disease management [9]. Nurse-led and nurse-coordinated interventions have also shown potential benefits in hypertension management, self-management support, care coordination, and multidisciplinary primary care [10].

Despite this potential, the role of nurses in primary care is not always clearly defined. International evidence shows considerable variation in job titles, education requirements, competency expectations, and role enactment among nurses working in primary care [11]. Barriers to optimizing nurses’ roles include regulatory limitations, lack of role clarity, insufficient education, limited training opportunities, organizational resistance, and inadequate stakeholder support [12]. These barriers are particularly relevant in countries undergoing PHC reform, where policy changes often move faster than workforce preparation and professional development systems [13].

Competency readiness is therefore a critical issue in IPHC implementation. Nursing competency is generally understood as the integration of knowledge, skills, attitudes, clinical judgment, communication, ethical practice, collaboration, leadership, and quality improvement required to deliver safe and effective care [14]. For primary care nurses, competency must extend beyond individual clinical care to include population health, family-centred care, community engagement, chronic disease management, digital health, inter-professional collaboration, care coordination, and equity-oriented practice [15]. Recent reviews also emphasize the need for context-sensitive competency frameworks that reflect changing health system needs, digital transformation, demographic shifts, and evolving nursing roles [16, 17].

In the context of IPHC, nurses’ competency readiness may be influenced by several interrelated factors. Educational background may shape foundational knowledge and professional preparation, while years of work experience may influence practical judgment and familiarity with Puskesmas-based services. However, education and experience alone may not be sufficient. Knowledge of IPHC, perceived implementation of IPHC in the workplace, and stakeholder support are likely to play important roles in shaping readiness for integrated practice. Implementation science frameworks also suggest that innovation characteristics, inner setting, outer setting, individual characteristics, and implementation process are important determinants of successful implementation [18]. Stakeholder engagement is particularly important because integrated care requires collaboration among health professionals, managers, local government, community health workers, and service users.

Professional development needs are another important dimension of workforce readiness, but their interpretation requires careful theoretical framing. In a simple deficit model, higher professional development needs may indicate gaps in competence. However, in the context of an ongoing health system reform such as IPHC, perceived development needs may also reflect nurses’ awareness of new role expectations, exposure to reform activities, engagement with changing service delivery models, and recognition of continuing learning requirements. Nurses who are more familiar with the operational demands of IPHC may be more capable of identifying areas in which further development is needed, such as care coordination, family- and community-based care, chronic disease management, digital reporting, inter-professional collaboration, and service improvement [19]. Therefore, professional development needs were examined in this study not only as a potential indicator of competency gaps, but also as a marker of perceived learning demand and reform-related professional engagement.

Although IPHC is a major primary care reform in Indonesia, existing empirical studies have largely focused on health post readiness, service integration, infrastructure, financing, human resources, community participation, or general implementation readiness. These studies provide important system- and facility-level information, but they do not sufficiently explain how nurses themselves perceive their competency readiness for IPHC-related roles. In particular, previous evidence has not clearly examined how individual knowledge of IPHC, perceived implementation in the workplace, stakeholder support, and perceived professional development needs are associated with nurses’ competency readiness [20]. This gap is important because IPHC implementation depends not only on the availability of facilities and policy guidance, but also on the readiness of frontline nurses to perform expanded primary care functions. From an implementation perspective, perceived implementation reflects the practice environment in which nurses are expected to enact IPHC-related roles, while stakeholder support reflects leadership, inter-professional, community, and administrative conditions that may enable or constrain role performance. Knowledge of IPHC is necessary for understanding reform expectations, but knowledge alone may be insufficient if the workplace does not provide opportunities, support, and systems for implementation.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess nurses’ competency readiness for IPHC implementation among nurses working in selected Indonesian public primary health care centres and to examine its associations with demographic characteristics, educational background, IPHC knowledge, perceived IPHC implementation, stakeholder support, and professional development needs. The findings are expected to inform nursing workforce development, IPHC training strategies, and primary care reform implementation in Indonesia.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a facility-based analytical cross-sectional design to examine nursing competency readiness for Integrated Primary Health Care implementation in Indonesia. This design was selected because the study aimed to assess competency readiness and identify demographic, professional, knowledge-related, implementation-related, stakeholder-support, and professional-development factors associated with nurses’ competency readiness at a single point in time.

Study setting

The study was conducted in public primary health care centres, locally known as Puskesmas, across 15 provinces in Indonesia: Jakarta, Central Java, Yogyakarta, East Java, North Sumatra, West Sumatra, Bengkulu, South Kalimantan, South Sulawesi, West Sulawesi, Bali, East Nusa Tenggara, West Nusa Tenggara, Maluku, and Papua. Puskesmas are the main public primary care facilities responsible for delivering promotive, preventive, curative, rehabilitative, and community-based health services. Puskesmas are expected to reorganize service delivery across life-course needs, strengthen community-based services, and improve coordination between health centres, auxiliary health posts, community health workers, families, and local stakeholders. The study did not aim to estimate nationally representative prevalence of nursing competency readiness, but rather to examine individual-level associations between IPHC-related factors and competency readiness among nurses working in selected public primary health care facilities.

Participants and sampling

The target population was nurses working in Indonesian public primary health care settings. Province was included only as a descriptive contextual variable and was not interpreted as evidence of regional representativeness. The accessible population consisted of nurses employed at participating Puskesmas during the study period. Participants were eligible if they were registered nurses working at a Puskesmas, had been employed in the current primary care setting for at least three months, were involved in clinical, administrative, community-based, or IPHC-related service activities, and agreed to participate in the study.

Nurses were excluded if they were on long-term leave during data collection, were students or temporary trainees, or submitted incomplete questionnaires that could not be used for analysis. This study used a non-probability consecutive sampling approach. Probability sampling was not used because a complete sampling frame of all eligible nurses working in Puskesmas involved in or exposed to IPHC implementation was not available to the research team. Participating Puskesmas were selected purposively based on their status as public primary health care centres, their involvement in or exposure to IPHC implementation, accessibility through collaborating professional and institutional networks, and permission from the relevant Puskesmas or local health authorities.

Within each participating Puskesmas, nurses who met the eligibility criteria were invited consecutively to complete the questionnaire during the data collection period from October to December 2025. Recruitment continued until data collection was completed. A total of 687 eligible nurses from selected public Puskesmas across 15 provinces were included in the final analysis.

The final analytical dataset retained respondents’ province but did not retain facility-level identifiers. Therefore, the exact number of individual Puskesmas, facility-level response rates, and clustering of nurses within facilities could not be verified from the de-identified analytical dataset. For this reason, the analysis was conducted at the individual nurse level, and no facility-level or multilevel analysis was performed. The findings should therefore be interpreted as facility-based evidence from selected public primary health care settings rather than as nationally representative estimates.

Variables

The primary dependent variable was nursing competency readiness for IPHC implementation. Competency readiness was primarily analysed as a continuous total score. For supplementary descriptive and logistic regression analyses, the competency readiness score was also categorized into higher and lower competency readiness using the median score of the study sample as the analytical cut-off. This classification was used only for secondary analysis and should not be interpreted as a validated clinical or professional competency threshold. The main independent variables were: IPHC knowledge; perceived IPHC implementation; stakeholder support; and professional development needs. The covariates included age, sex, employment status, educational level, length of work experience, and province or Puskesmas location. Educational level was analyzed according to the categories available in the dataset and was also interpreted in relation to vocational and professional nursing preparation where relevant.

Instrument and measures

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire developed for this study, except for the nursing competency readiness component, which was adapted from the Holistic Nursing Competence Scale developed by Takase and Teraoka [21]. The researcher-developed sections of the questionnaire were constructed to reflect the operational components of IPHC implementation in Indonesian primary health care, the study objectives, and relevant literature on primary care nursing, integrated care, implementation readiness, stakeholder support, and professional development. Items were developed to capture four domains: IPHC knowledge, perceived IPHC implementation, stakeholder support, and professional development needs.

The initial item pool was reviewed by the research team and subject-matter experts in community health nursing, primary care, nursing management, and health service implementation. The review focused on content relevance, clarity, comprehensibility, contextual appropriateness for Puskesmas-based nursing practice, and alignment with the operational context of IPHC implementation. Items were revised for wording, redundancy, and contextual fit based on this review. A formal item-level or scale-level content validity index was not calculated. A formal cognitive interview, pilot test, and full cultural adaptation study were also not conducted. The questionnaire was administered in Indonesian. The English-language version provided as Additional file 1 was prepared for reporting purposes. These measurement limitations are acknowledged in the Discussion.

Demographic and professional characteristics

Demographic and professional variables included age, sex, employment status, educational level, length of work experience, and workplace location. Age and length of work experience were treated as continuous variables. Sex, employment status, educational level, and workplace location were treated as categorical variables.

IPHC knowledge

IPHC knowledge was measured using 25 dichotomous items. Each item was scored according to the predetermined scoring protocol, and the total knowledge score was calculated by summing all item scores. Higher scores indicated greater factual knowledge of IPHC. Because the knowledge items were dichotomous and covered multiple content areas, the knowledge measure was treated as a knowledge index rather than a unidimensional psychometric scale.

Perceived IPHC implementation

Perceived IPHC implementation was measured using 28 Likert-type items assessing nurses’ perceptions of IPHC implementation in their workplace. The items covered aspects of service organization, integration of care, role implementation, community linkage, service coordination, follow-up, and reporting mechanisms. Total implementation scores were calculated by summing all item scores, with higher scores indicating better perceived IPHC implementation.

Stakeholder support

Stakeholder support was measured using 18 Likert-type items assessing perceived support from relevant internal and external stakeholders. These included support from Puskesmas leadership, colleagues, district health authorities, community health workers, local government, and community actors. Total stakeholder support scores were calculated by summing all item scores, with higher scores indicating stronger perceived stakeholder support.

Professional development needs

Professional development needs were measured using 21 items assessing nurses’ perceived need for further development related to IPHC implementation and primary care service delivery. The items reflected areas such as IPHC orientation, care coordination, family and community-based care, chronic disease management, communication, reporting, inter-professional collaboration, and service improvement. Total scores were calculated according to the scoring protocol, with higher scores indicating greater perceived professional development needs.

Nursing competency readiness

Nursing competency readiness was measured using a competency assessment instrument adapted from the Holistic Nursing Competence Scale developed by Takase and Teraoka [21]. The instrument reflects a holistic view of nursing competence, including the integration of knowledge, skills, professional attitudes, clinical judgment, communication, ethical practice, and role performance in nursing care. In the present study, the competency readiness score was treated primarily as a continuous variable because the adapted instrument was used to assess perceived readiness rather than to make a formal professional competency certification decision.

The original Holistic Nursing Competence Scale does not provide an IPHC-specific cut-off for classifying nurses as competent or not competent in the context of Indonesian primary health care reform. Therefore, the categorical classification used in this study was not derived from the original scale and was not a validated clinical, diagnostic, or professional credentialing threshold. For supplementary descriptive and logistic regression analyses only, respondents with competency readiness scores of 108 or higher, corresponding to the sample median, were classified as having higher competency readiness, whereas those with scores below 108 were classified as having lower competency readiness. The continuous competency readiness score remained the primary outcome in the main analysis.

Validity and reliability assessment

The item-level dataset contained 25 IPHC knowledge items, 28 IPHC implementation items, 18 stakeholder support items, and 21 professional development needs items. Two variables in the item-level dataset were not part of the total score calculation and were excluded from the reliability analysis. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha for Likert-type scales and KR-20/Cronbach’s alpha for the dichotomous knowledge items. Corrected item-total correlations were also examined for the Likert-type scales.

Exploratory factor analysis was conducted to examine factorability and preliminary dimensional structure of the researcher-developed measures. Sampling adequacy was assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin statistic, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was used to examine whether the correlation matrix was suitable for factor analysis. Factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.0 were examined to describe the preliminary dimensional structure of each measure.

The IPHC implementation, stakeholder support, and professional development needs scales demonstrated excellent internal consistency. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.970 for the IPHC implementation scale, 0.981 for the stakeholder support scale, and 0.976 for the professional development needs scale. Corrected item-total correlations ranged from 0.574 to 0.811 for IPHC implementation, 0.725 to 0.925 for stakeholder support, and 0.701 to 0.876 for professional development needs, indicating strong item performance across these constructs. Although these values indicate high internal consistency, the very high Cronbach’s alpha coefficients may also suggest item redundancy or construct overlap. Therefore, the reliability findings were interpreted as preliminary evidence of internal consistency rather than as evidence of full psychometric validation. Further validation is needed to examine item redundancy, dimensionality, and criterion validity.

The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin values indicated adequate to excellent sampling adequacy: 0.715 for IPHC knowledge, 0.965 for IPHC implementation, 0.973 for stakeholder support, and 0.968 for professional development needs. Exploratory factor analysis suggested a multidimensional structure for IPHC knowledge, with nine factors having eigenvalues greater than 1 and the first factor explaining 12.5% of the variance. This supported treating the knowledge measure as a content-based knowledge index rather than as a unidimensional psychometric scale. For the IPHC implementation scale, three factors had eigenvalues greater than 1, with the first factor explaining 56.0% of the variance. The stakeholder support scale showed a dominant one-factor structure, with the first factor explaining 76.8% of the variance. The professional development needs scale showed a two-factor structure, with the first factor explaining 69.7% of the variance. Because these factor analyses were exploratory, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of dimensional structure. Confirmatory factor analysis, test–retest reliability, and criterion validity testing were not performed and should be considered in future validation studies.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected between October 2025 and December 2025. Permission to conduct the study was obtained from Puskesmas authority in Indonesia. Eligible nurses were informed about the study objectives, procedures, voluntary participation, confidentiality, and their right to withdraw. Written or electronic informed consent was obtained before participants completed the questionnaire. The questionnaire was administered using self-administered format. Completed questionnaires were checked for completeness. Data were coded, cleaned, and prepared for statistical analysis. The analytical dataset contained no missing values for the variables included in the final analysis.

Data management

The original data were stored in SPSS format. Before analysis, all variables were examined for coding accuracy, value labels, missing values, range errors, and consistency between item-level scores and total scores. Continuous variables were inspected using descriptive statistics, histograms, skewness, kurtosis, and normality tests. Categorical variables were examined using frequency distributions. The final analytical dataset included 687 respondents. No missing values were identified in the variables used for the main analysis. Therefore, complete case analysis was applied.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS statistical software. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Common method bias was considered because the main variables were collected using a self-administered questionnaire at a single time point. Procedural steps were used to reduce this risk, including voluntary participation, anonymous analysis, separation of questionnaire sections, neutral item wording, and assurance that there were no right or wrong responses. As statistical diagnostics, inter-construct correlations and multicollinearity indicators were examined. The absence of extremely high correlations among the main constructs and variance inflation factor values below 3 suggested that severe common-method overlap was unlikely. However, no marker-variable technique or common latent factor model was used; therefore, common method bias could not be completely ruled out.

Descriptive analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize respondent characteristics and main study variables. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation, median, and observed range. Descriptive analyses were performed for age, sex, employment status, educational level, length of work experience, province or workplace location, IPHC knowledge, perceived IPHC implementation, stakeholder support, professional development needs, and nursing competency readiness.

Bivariate analysis

Bivariate analyses were conducted to examine differences in competency readiness across respondent characteristics and categorical study variables. For two-group comparisons, Mann–Whitney U test was used. For comparisons involving more than two groups, Kruskal–Wallis test was used. Differences in competency readiness were examined by sex, educational level, employment status, length of work experience category, province or workplace location, knowledge category, IPHC implementation category, stakeholder support category, and professional development needs category.

Correlation analysis

Spearman rank correlation analysis was used to examine the relationships between continuous study variables and nursing competency readiness. Correlations were assessed between competency readiness and age, length of work experience, IPHC knowledge, perceived IPHC implementation, stakeholder support, and professional development needs. Spearman correlation was selected because several variables showed non-normal distributions.

Multiple linear regression

Multiple linear regression was conducted as the primary multivariable analysis to identify factors independently associated with nursing competency readiness. The competency readiness total score was entered as the dependent variable. Independent variables included age, length of work experience, sex, educational level, employment status, IPHC knowledge, perceived IPHC implementation, stakeholder support, and professional development needs.

The regression model was specified as follows:

Nursing competency readiness = age + length of work experience + sex + educational level + employment status + IPHC knowledge + perceived IPHC implementation + stakeholder support + professional development needs.

Nursing competency readiness = age + length of work experience + sex + educational level + employment status + IPHC knowledge + perceived IPHC implementation + stakeholder support + professional development needs.

Model assumptions were assessed by examining residual distribution, linearity, homoscedasticity, and influential observations. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor values. Variables with variance inflation factor values below 5 were considered acceptable, and values below 3 were interpreted as indicating low risk of multicollinearity. The model fit was evaluated using R² and adjusted R². Unstandardized regression coefficients, standardized beta coefficients, standard errors, p-values, and 95% confidence intervals were reported.

Supplementary logistic regression

A supplementary binary logistic regression analysis was conducted using the median-based competency readiness category as the outcome, classified as higher versus lower competency readiness. The same independent variables used in the linear regression model were entered into the logistic regression model. Results were reported as odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals, and p-values. Model performance was evaluated using pseudo-R² statistics and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. This supplementary analysis was conducted only as a sensitivity analysis to examine whether the factors associated with the continuous competency readiness score showed a similar pattern when the outcome was categorized using a sample-based median split.

Results

Participant characteristics

In the Table 1, a total of 687 nurses were included in the analysis. The mean age of participants was 38.37 years (SD 7.02), with a median of 38 years (IQR 33–43). The mean length of work experience was 11.34 years (SD 8.13), with a median of 10 years (IQR 5–16). Most participants were female (537, 78.2%), and 150 (21.8%) were male. Regarding educational background, 277 nurses (40.3%) had completed diploma-level nursing education, 55 (8.0%) had completed bachelor-level nursing education, 341 (49.6%) had completed the professional nursing programme, 13 (1.9%) had completed master-level nursing education, and one participant (0.1%) had completed doctoral-level nursing education. Most participants were government employees (509, 75%), while 178 (25%) were non-government employees. The largest proportion of participants came from South Sulawesi (213, 31.0%), followed by East Nusa Tenggara (96, 14.0%) and West Nusa Tenggara (71, 10.3%).

Descriptive findings for main study variables

Descriptive statistics of the main study variables are presented in Table 2. The mean knowledge score was 22.53 (SD 1.80), and 376 participants (54.7%) were categorized as having good knowledge of IPHC. Knowledge scores were clustered toward the upper end of the possible score range. The mean knowledge score was 22.53 out of 25, with a median of 23 and an interquartile range of 21–24. This distribution indicates a possible ceiling effect and limited variability in the knowledge measure, which may partly explain the weak and non-significant association between knowledge and competency readiness.

The mean IPHC implementation score was 103.66 (SD 27.51), with 358 participants (52.1%) reporting good IPHC implementation. The mean stakeholder support score was 64.21 (SD 11.67), and 363 participants (52.8%) perceived stakeholder support as supportive. The mean professional development needs score was 58.96 (SD 9.99), with 416 participants (60.6%) classified as needing professional development. The mean nursing competency readiness score was 102.32 (SD 15.48), with a median of 108. Using the sample median as a supplementary analytical cut-off, 367 participants (53.4%) were classified as having higher competency readiness (Table 2).

Nursing competency readiness

There were no statistically significant differences in competency readiness scores by sex, educational pathway, employment status, or knowledge category. Competency readiness was comparable between nurses with diploma-level education and those with bachelor-level or professional nursing education (median 108 [IQR 93–115] versus 108 [IQR 97–114], p = 0.804). Nurses reporting better perceived IPHC implementation had higher competency readiness scores than those reporting lower perceived implementation (median 113 [IQR 106–115] versus 102 [IQR 83–110], p < 0.001). Nurses reporting higher perceived stakeholder support also had higher competency readiness scores than those reporting lower stakeholder support (median 114 [IQR 107–115] versus 101 [IQR 83–108], p < 0.001).

Nurses reporting higher perceived professional development needs had higher competency readiness scores than those reporting lower perceived professional development needs (median 112 [IQR 104–115] versus 100 [IQR 80–109], p < 0.001). This counterintuitive finding should be interpreted cautiously because the professional development needs measure may capture perceived learning demand, reform exposure, engagement, or awareness of IPHC complexity rather than training deficit alone.

Supplementary logistic regression

A supplementary binary logistic regression analysis was conducted using the median-based competency readiness category as the outcome, coded as higher versus lower competency readiness. This analysis was performed only as a sensitivity analysis to examine whether the direction of associations was broadly consistent with the primary linear regression model. The median-based category was not a validated competency threshold and should not be interpreted as a clinical, diagnostic, or professional classification.

Consistent with the linear regression findings, perceived IPHC implementation (adjusted OR 1.023, 95% CI 1.015 to 1.031, p < 0.001), stakeholder support (adjusted OR 1.074, 95% CI 1.049 to 1.099, p < 0.001), and professional development needs (adjusted OR 1.055, 95% CI 1.031 to 1.080, p < 0.001) were associated with higher odds of being classified as having higher competency readiness. Demographic and educational variables were not significantly associated with the median-based readiness category. Because this supplementary analysis dichotomized a continuous outcome and did not include calibration assessment, predefined classification thresholds, or internal validation, the logistic regression findings should be interpreted only as supportive evidence and not as a clinical prediction model.

Overall, the findings indicate that nursing competency readiness for IPHC implementation was more strongly associated with implementation-related and support-related factors than with demographic or educational characteristics. Stakeholder support showed the largest standardized association, followed by perceived IPHC implementation and professional development needs. These findings should be interpreted in light of the cross-sectional, self-reported design and the possibility of common-method bias. In particular, the positive association between professional development needs and competency readiness should be interpreted cautiously because this measure may reflect perceived learning demand, reform exposure, or professional engagement rather than training deficit alone.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study examined nursing competency readiness for Integrated Primary Health Care implementation and assessed its associations with knowledge, perceived IPHC implementation, stakeholder support, and professional development needs. Three key findings emerged. First, slightly more than half of nurses were classified as having higher competency readiness using a sample-based median split. This finding should be interpreted as a relative distribution of readiness within the study sample rather than as evidence of formal professional competence or certification status. Second, competency readiness was not significantly associated with demographic and professional characteristics such as age, sex, employment status, educational level, length of work experience, or province. Third, IPHC implementation, stakeholder support, and professional development needs were consistently associated with competency readiness in bivariate and multivariable analyses. Among these factors, stakeholder support showed the largest standardized association with competency readiness.

These findings should be interpreted cautiously. The study used a cross-sectional and self-reported design; therefore, the direction of the observed associations cannot be established. The results do not demonstrate that stakeholder support, perceived IPHC implementation, or professional development needs cause higher competency readiness. Rather, they indicate that nurses who reported more supportive implementation conditions and greater perceived development needs also tended to report higher competency readiness. These relationships may reflect actual links between practice context and readiness, but they may also reflect shared perceptions, common-method variance, or differences in nurses’ exposure to IPHC-related reform activities [5].

Interpretation of competency readiness in the context of IPHC

The finding that 53.4% of nurses were classified as having higher competency readiness using a sample median split should be interpreted as a relative distribution of readiness within this study sample, rather than as evidence that these nurses had achieved a validated professional competency threshold. IPHC represents a major reorientation of primary care delivery in Indonesia, moving services toward life-course-based, integrated, community-linked, and prevention-oriented care. In such a reform, competency cannot be limited to clinical task performance. Nurses are expected to contribute to health promotion, disease prevention, screening, home-based follow-up, care coordination, community engagement, documentation, and inter-professional collaboration.

This broader role is consistent with international evidence showing that primary care nurses contribute to patient outcomes, system outcomes, chronic disease management, self-management support, and service coordination [22]. Systematic reviews have shown that registered nurses in primary care can improve patient satisfaction, enablement, quality of life, self-efficacy, health behaviours, and system-level outcomes when their roles are appropriately integrated into primary care teams. However, the same body of literature also emphasizes that the contribution of nurses depends heavily on role clarity, organizational support, regulatory context, and team-based practice infrastructure [23].

Therefore, the moderate level of competency readiness found in this study should not be interpreted simply as a weakness of individual nurses. Rather, it reflects the early-stage challenge of translating a national reform into routine professional practice. Similar implementation challenges have been reported in studies of primary health care integration and role expansion in other settings, where workforce readiness depends on alignment between policy expectations, training systems, leadership support, and service delivery infrastructure [24].

Stakeholder support as the largest standardized association of nursing competency readiness

Stakeholder support showed the largest standardized association with competency readiness. This finding suggests that nurses who perceived greater support from Puskesmas leadership, colleagues, district health authorities, community health workers, local government, and community actors also tended to report higher competency readiness. This association is plausible because IPHC implementation requires role clarification, inter-professional coordination, community linkage, documentation, follow-up, and local service adaptation. Nurses working in more supportive environments may have more opportunities to understand, practise, and enact IPHC-related roles. However, this finding should not be interpreted as proof that stakeholder support strengthens competency readiness. Because both stakeholder support and competency readiness were measured by self-report at the same time, reverse interpretation and shared-method effects are possible. Nurses who perceive themselves as more ready may also evaluate their work environment more positively. Similarly, nurses who are more engaged with IPHC activities may be more aware of available support and more likely to report higher readiness [25]. Longitudinal or intervention studies are needed to determine whether strengthening stakeholder support leads to measurable improvements in nursing competency readiness.

Perceived IPHC implementation was also positively associated with competency readiness. This may indicate that nurses working in settings where IPHC activities are more visible or more established have greater exposure to integrated service delivery, community linkage, care coordination, family visits, digital reporting, and inter-professional collaboration. Such exposure may be relevant to perceived readiness. Nevertheless, the association may also operate in the opposite direction: nurses with higher perceived readiness may be more likely to recognize or report IPHC implementation in their workplace. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence of an association between perceived implementation context and self-reported readiness, not as evidence of causal influence.

The result is also consistent with evidence from primary care nursing literature. An integrative review on the implementation of nurses’ roles in primary care found that role implementation is facilitated by multidisciplinary teamwork, recognition of nurses’ contributions, organizational readiness, and context-sensitive support [26]. Conversely, lack of stakeholder engagement, weak inter-professional, unclear role boundaries, and insufficient managerial support can limit nurses’ ability to perform expanded primary care roles effectively [27]. In the context of IPHC, this means that strengthening nursing competency cannot be reduced to training individual nurses; it must include active engagement of organizational and community stakeholders.

A critical implication is that stakeholder support should be treated as a core implementation strategy, not as an external background factor. Puskesmas leaders and district health offices need to provide explicit role expectations, supportive supervision, regular feedback, adequate resources, and mechanisms for inter-professional coordination. Without these supports, competency development may remain fragmented and dependent on individual motivation.

IPHC implementation and competency readiness

The second major predictor of competency readiness was perceived IPHC implementation. Nurses who reported better IPHC implementation had higher competency readiness scores and higher odds of being classified as having higher competency readiness in the supplementary analysis. This finding suggests a reciprocal relationship between implementation exposure and competency. On one hand, better implementation may provide nurses with more opportunities to practice IPHC-related tasks [11]. On the other hand, competent nurses may perceive and contribute to better implementation because they are more familiar with IPHC functions, service coordination, and community-based care.

This finding is consistent with evidence from Indonesia showing that readiness for primary health care integration varies across health posts and is influenced by human resources, infrastructure, financing, service organization, and community participation. It also aligns with international evidence that primary care transformation requires more than policy directives; it requires operational changes at the facility level, including workflow redesign, team-based care, coordination mechanisms, and local adaptation [28].

The association between IPHC implementation and competency readiness is particularly important because it implies that competency is developed through practice environments, not merely through pre-service education. Nurses who are exposed to functioning IPHC systems may gain practical experience in life-course service delivery, risk identification, community linkage, and inter-professional collaboration [29]. Conversely, in settings where IPHC implementation remains incomplete or poorly coordinated, nurses may have fewer opportunities to internalize the competencies required for integrated care.

This finding supports the need for implementation-based capacity building. Rather than relying only on classroom-based training, IPHC competency development should include on-the-job mentoring, case-based learning, reflective supervision, peer learning, and performance feedback within actual Puskesmas workflows. Such strategies are more likely to bridge the gap between knowledge and practice.

Professional development needs and competency readiness

Professional development needs were positively associated with competency readiness. This finding should be interpreted cautiously because, conceptually, a higher need for professional development may be expected to indicate a competency gap. In the present study, however, the professional development needs measure may not only have captured a deficit in competence, but also nurses’ awareness of the complexity of IPHC implementation, exposure to new service expectations, and perceived priority for further capacity building.

One possible explanation is that nurses with higher competency readiness were more able to recognize the expanded roles required in IPHC, including care coordination, family- and community-based care, chronic disease management, digital reporting, inter-professional collaboration, and service improvement. In this sense, higher professional development needs may reflect professional insight, engagement with reform, and awareness of evolving practice demands rather than low competence alone. Another explanation is possible construct overlap: nurses who were more exposed to IPHC implementation may have simultaneously reported higher competency readiness and greater perceived need for further development.

This interpretation is consistent with recent literature emphasizing that primary care nursing roles are evolving rapidly and require continuous professional development to respond to demographic, technological, and service delivery changes [16]. This finding has an important implication for policy and education. Professional development needs should not be viewed only as evidence of a competency gap. They should also be used as an entry point for designing targeted workforce development. In IPHC implementation, continuing professional development should be needs-based, role-specific, and linked to actual service delivery challenges. Priority areas may include integrated life-course care, family and community assessment, chronic disease management, digital health reporting, care coordination, leadership, and inter-professional communication.

The non-significant role of knowledge

One unexpected finding was that knowledge was not significantly associated with competency readiness. This result deserves careful interpretation. It does not mean that knowledge is unimportant. Rather, it suggests that knowledge alone may be insufficient to produce competency readiness in the context of complex primary care reform. Competency requires the integration of knowledge, skills, attitudes, judgment, communication, collaboration, and contextual application. A nurse may understand IPHC conceptually but still face difficulty applying it in practice if the workplace lacks role clarity, stakeholder support, workflow integration, supervision, or inter-professional coordination. This distinction between knowing and doing is widely recognized in implementation science, where implementation outcomes depend not only on individual knowledge but also on organizational context, leadership, resources, and implementation climate [30].

The absence of a significant association between knowledge and competency may also reflect measurement issues. If the knowledge score had limited variability, ceiling effects, or focused mainly on factual recall, it may not have captured applied knowledge required for IPHC practice. Future studies should consider using case-based knowledge assessment, scenario-based decision-making, or objective structured competency evaluation to better measure applied IPHC knowledge. This finding strengthens the argument that IPHC training should move beyond information transfer. Training should not merely explain what IPHC is; it should develop the ability to perform IPHC-related functions in real service contexts. Competency-oriented training should include problem-solving, case simulation, inter-professional exercises, community assessment, and supervised practice.

The knowledge score also showed evidence of a possible ceiling effect. Participants had a high mean knowledge score relative to the maximum possible score, and the interquartile range was narrow. This limited variability may have reduced the ability to detect an association between knowledge and competency readiness. In addition, the knowledge items assessed factual understanding of IPHC and may not have captured applied knowledge, clinical reasoning, or decision-making required for integrated primary care practice. Future studies should consider scenario-based or case-based knowledge assessment to better capture applied IPHC knowledge.

Educational level and competency readiness

Another important finding was the absence of a significant difference in competency readiness by educational level. This result is particularly relevant because early framing of this study considered comparing vocationally and professionally prepared nurses. The current findings suggest that formal educational level alone may not be sufficient to explain readiness for IPHC implementation. This does not reduce the importance of nursing education. Rather, it indicates that the competencies required for IPHC may be highly practice-dependent and shaped by exposure to implementation, organizational support, and continuing professional development. In contexts of health system reform, pre-service education may provide foundational competence, but reform-specific readiness often requires in-service training, role adaptation, and learning within the workplace.

International literature supports this interpretation. Primary care nursing roles vary considerably across countries in terms of job titles, education requirements, scope of practice, and competency expectations. Recent reviews also suggest that evolving primary health care demands require competency frameworks that are continuously updated and linked to real-world practice needs [31]. Therefore, the lack of significant educational differences in this study may indicate that IPHC competency is not automatically produced by educational credentials but must be developed through system-supported practice. For Indonesia, this finding is strategically important. It suggests that IPHC workforce development should not rely solely on differentiating nurses by educational level. Instead, it should define IPHC-specific competencies, assess readiness across all nursing groups, and provide structured professional development pathways for both vocationally and professionally prepared nurses.

Demographic and employment characteristics

Age, sex, employment status, length of work experience, and province were not significantly associated with competency readiness. These findings further reinforce the argument that competency readiness is shaped more by implementation and support systems than by demographic attributes. Length of work experience, in particular, did not appear to be a meaningful predictor. This suggests that experience in primary care does not automatically translate into readiness for integrated primary care reform. This finding is plausible because IPHC introduces new expectations that may differ from previous routines. Experienced nurses may have deep familiarity with Puskesmas operations, but they may still require support to adapt to new service integration models, life-course clusters, data use, team-based care, and community linkage. Conversely, less experienced nurses may demonstrate readiness if they receive adequate support, exposure, and training [32].

The non-significant role of province should also be interpreted cautiously. Provinces with very small sample sizes were retained in the descriptive table to provide transparent information about the geographical distribution of respondents. However, they were not used to support regional inference. The study was not designed to compare provinces or estimate province-level effects. Therefore, province should be interpreted only as a contextual descriptor, and the main findings should be understood at the individual nurse level. While provincial differences may exist in implementation capacity, this study may not have been designed to detect complex regional variation. Province-level analysis may be limited by unequal sample distribution, unmeasured district-level factors, and the absence of facility-level readiness indicators. Future studies should incorporate multilevel modelling and facility-level variables to examine how local implementation context affects nursing competency readiness.

Implications for IPHC implementation and nursing workforce policy

From a practical perspective, the findings indicate that competency readiness may need to be considered alongside the broader practice environment in which nurses work. Role clarification, supportive supervision, inter-professional communication, community linkage, and opportunities for workplace-based learning may be relevant components of IPHC workforce development. These areas should be evaluated in future implementation studies to determine whether they contribute to measurable improvements in nursing competency, service delivery, and patient or community outcomes.

The findings also suggest that professional development programmes should be designed carefully. Because higher professional development needs may reflect several constructs, including perceived learning demand, reform exposure, motivation, or competency gaps, needs assessment tools should distinguish these dimensions before they are used to guide training policy. Future workforce development programmes should therefore combine self-reported needs assessment with objective or supervisor-rated competency assessment, facility-level readiness indicators, and qualitative exploration of nurses’ experiences in IPHC implementation.

Stakeholder support may be considered as part of workforce planning. Puskesmas leaders, district health offices, professional organizations, and community stakeholders need to create an enabling environment for nurses to perform integrated care roles. This includes role clarification, supervision, supportive leadership, team communication, adequate resources, and recognition of nursing contributions. IPHC competency development should be embedded in implementation processes. Training should be linked to actual service workflows, including family assessment, home visits, screening, referral coordination, follow-up care, digital reporting, and community engagement. Facility-based mentoring and supportive supervision may be more effective than one-time training.

Professional development should be targeted and differentiated. Not all nurses require the same training. Development programs should be based on competency assessment and should prioritize domains with the greatest implementation relevance. These may include care coordination, inter-professional collaboration, chronic disease management, life-course care, digital health, and community-based nursing [33]. Competency frameworks for IPHC nursing practice should be developed or adapted. Such frameworks should define the expected competencies of nurses in integrated primary care and should be aligned with Indonesia’s PHC transformation agenda. A competency framework would also support curriculum development, in-service training, performance appraisal, and career development [34]. The findings suggest that policymakers should avoid assuming that education level alone determines readiness. While formal education remains important, IPHC readiness appears to depend more on practice exposure, implementation maturity, stakeholder support, and continuous development. Workforce policies should therefore combine educational strengthening with workplace-based implementation support [3, 35].

Implications for nursing education

The findings also have implications for nursing education institutions. Nursing curricula should increasingly prepare graduates for integrated, community-oriented, and team-based primary care. This includes competencies in population health, family-centred care, chronic disease prevention, digital health, care coordination, health coaching, inter-professional collaboration, and community engagement. For professional nursing education, IPHC can be used as a contextual model for strengthening primary care nursing competencies. Simulation, community-based learning, case-based learning, and inter-professional education should be expanded to prepare nurses for reform-oriented practice. For vocational nursing education, based on the Ministry of Health Republic of Indonesia (2023), curricula should ensure that graduates possess practical competencies relevant to Puskesmas-based integrated care, including screening, health education, documentation, home visits, and community follow-up. Continuing professional development should also bridge the gap between pre-service education and reform-specific practice. In-service programs should not be limited to technical orientation; they should build adaptive competence, problem-solving capacity, and leadership in primary care transformation [36, 37].

Strengths of the study

This study has several strengths. First, it used a relatively large sample of 687 nurses, providing sufficient statistical power for bivariate and multivariable analyses. Second, the study examined competency readiness using a multidimensional implementation perspective, incorporating individual characteristics, knowledge, IPHC implementation, stakeholder support, and professional development needs. Third, the use of both linear and logistic regression strengthens the robustness of the findings by showing consistent predictors across continuous and categorical outcome models. Fourth, the study addresses an important evidence gap in Indonesia’s primary care transformation by focusing specifically on nurses, a key workforce group in IPHC implementation.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inference. Although perceived IPHC implementation, stakeholder support, and professional development needs were associated with nursing competency readiness, the direction of these relationships cannot be established. The observed associations should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that implementation conditions, stakeholder support, or professional development needs directly improve competency readiness.

Second, all main variables were collected using a self-administered questionnaire at a single time point. This creates a risk of self-report bias and common-method bias, which may have inflated the associations among perceived IPHC implementation, stakeholder support, professional development needs, and competency readiness. Although procedural steps were used to reduce this risk and the inter-construct correlations and variance inflation factor values did not indicate severe overlap or multicollinearity, these diagnostics cannot rule out common-method bias. No marker-variable technique or common latent factor model was used.

Third, the study used a non-probability facility-based sampling approach. Participating Puskesmas were selected purposively, and eligible nurses were recruited consecutively from participating facilities. Therefore, the sample should not be considered nationally representative of all primary care nurses in Indonesia. In addition, the final de-identified analytical dataset retained respondents’ province but did not retain facility-level identifiers. Consequently, the exact number of participating Puskesmas, facility-level response rates, non-response rates, and clustering of nurses within facilities could not be verified. Facility-level clustering and multilevel effects could not be examined. Although respondents came from 15 provinces, the province distribution was highly uneven, and province-level comparisons should not be interpreted as evidence of regional differences or national generalizability.

Fourth, the researcher-developed IPHC knowledge, perceived IPHC implementation, stakeholder support, and professional development needs measures require further psychometric validation. Item-level data were available for these researcher-developed measures, allowing internal consistency assessment and exploratory factor analysis. However, these analyses should be interpreted as preliminary psychometric evidence rather than full validation. Formal content validity indexing, cognitive interviewing, pilot testing, confirmatory factor analysis, test–retest reliability, and criterion validity testing were not completed. The very high Cronbach’s alpha values for several Likert-type scales may also indicate item redundancy or construct overlap.

Fifth, the competency readiness measure was adapted from a generic self-report nursing competence instrument and was not originally developed as an IPHC-specific competency measure. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as self-reported competency readiness rather than objectively verified IPHC competency. The original Holistic Nursing Competence Scale does not provide an IPHC-specific validated cut-off for classifying nurses as competent or not competent in Indonesian primary health care settings. The higher/lower readiness classification used in this study was based on the sample median and was used only for supplementary analysis. The continuous competency readiness score should therefore be regarded as the primary outcome.

Finally, possible construct overlap should be considered. Perceived IPHC implementation, stakeholder support, professional development needs, and competency readiness were all assessed through self-report and may partly capture related perceptions of reform exposure, engagement, organizational support, role clarity, or professional confidence. In particular, the professional development needs measure may not reflect training deficit alone; higher scores may also indicate perceived learning demand, awareness of IPHC complexity, exposure to reform activities, or professional growth orientation. Future studies should use validated IPHC-specific instruments, objective or supervisor-rated competency measures, longitudinal designs, and facility-level data to clarify these relationships.

Future research

Objective competency measures were not included in this study. Future research should consider supervisor-rated competency, direct observation, structured clinical or community-based performance assessment, chart or documentation audit, simulation-based assessment, objective structured clinical examination, and facility-level performance indicators. These approaches would provide stronger evidence on actual nursing competency for IPHC implementation and reduce reliance on self-reported readiness.

Future studies should validate IPHC-specific competency thresholds using objective performance indicators, supervisor assessment, or criterion-based competency evaluation. Future research should use longitudinal or mixed-methods designs to examine how nursing competency evolves during IPHC implementation. Studies should also include facility-level readiness indicators, supervisor-rated competency, objective performance indicators, and qualitative exploration of how nurses experience role transition in IPHC. Multilevel analysis would be particularly valuable to distinguish individual-level competency determinants from facility- and district-level implementation factors.

Conclusion

This study found that self-reported nursing competency readiness for IPHC implementation was associated with perceived IPHC implementation, stakeholder support, and professional development needs, whereas demographic, employment, educational, and knowledge-related characteristics showed weaker or non-significant associations. Stakeholder support showed the largest standardized association with competency readiness, but the cross-sectional and self-reported design prevents conclusions about causality or directionality. The positive association between professional development needs and competency readiness should also be interpreted cautiously because this measure may reflect perceived learning demand, reform exposure, professional engagement, or awareness of IPHC complexity rather than training deficit alone. These findings suggest that implementation environment, stakeholder engagement, role clarity, interprofessional collaboration, and professional development may be relevant areas for workforce planning, but their effects on nursing competency readiness should be tested in longitudinal, mixed-methods, and intervention studies using objective or supervisor-rated competency measures.
