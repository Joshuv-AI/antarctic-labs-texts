Real-world effectiveness and safety of adjuvant atezolizumab in pathological stage IIA–IIIB non-small cell lung cancer following curative surgery
Authors: Akifumi Nakamura, Nobuyuki Kondo, Seiji Matsumoto, Masaki Hashimoto, Ayumi Kuroda, Kyoshiro Takegahara, Koji Mikami, Toshiyuki Minami, Kozo Kuribayashi, Kazuhiro Kitajima, Takashi Kijima, Soichiro Funaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative atezolizumab is approved as adjuvant therapy for programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)-positive non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) in Japan, based on the phase III IMpower010 trial. However, real-world data on this therapeutic approach remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the real-world effectiveness and safety of adjuvant atezolizumab in patients with pathological stage II–III NSCLC.MethodsThis single-institution retrospective cohort study included patients with completely resected pathological stage IIA–IIIB [Union for International Cancer Control/American Joint Committee on Cancer (UICC/AJCC) staging system version 8] NSCLC who received at least one cycle of adjuvant atezolizumab after platinum-based chemotherapy between January 2021 and December 2024. Clinical characteristics, treatment completion, disease-free survival (DFS), overall survival (OS), and adverse events (AEs) were evaluated. Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method.ResultsAmong 646 patients who underwent curative-intent surgery, 25 met the eligibility criteria and received adjuvant atezolizumab. PD-L1 expression assessed using the 22C3 assay was <1% in 1 patient, 1–49% in 11, and ≥50% in 13; notably, the patient with PD-L1 <1% on 22C3 testing was found to be positive on SP263 testing. Median follow-up was 27.4 months. Median DFS was not reached; the 2-year DFS rate was 63.8%, with comparable outcomes between PD-L1 subgroups (PD-L1 1–49%: 72.9% vs. ≥50%: 57.7%; P=0.37). AEs occurred in 60.0%, including two grade 3 events. No grade 4–5 toxicities or treatment-related deaths were observed. Fifteen patients (60.0%) completed 1 year of treatment.ConclusionsIn this real-world cohort, adjuvant atezolizumab demonstrated favorable tolerability and clinically meaningful DFS across PD-L1 expression subgroups, including patients with PD-L1 1–49%. These findings are descriptive in nature and should be interpreted with caution, nevertheless, they provide real-world evidence supporting the potential clinical utility of adjuvant atezolizumab in appropriately selected patients with resected stage II–III NSCLC.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), atezolizumab, adjuvant chemotherapy (AC), programmed death-1 inhibition, adverse events (AEs)

Highlight box

Key findings

• In this real-world cohort of patients with completely resected pathological stage II–III non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), adjuvant atezolizumab following platinum-based chemotherapy demonstrated clinically meaningful disease-free survival (DFS), with a 2-year DFS rate of 63.8%.

• DFS outcomes in patients with programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression of 1–49% were comparable to those in patients with PD-L1 ≥50% (2-year DFS: 72.9% vs. 57.7%, P=0.37).

• Treatment was generally well tolerated, with manageable immune-related adverse events and no treatment-related deaths.

What is known, and what is new?

• The phase III IMpower010 trial established adjuvant atezolizumab as a standard option for PD-L1-positive resected stage II–IIIA NSCLC, showing pronounced benefit in tumors with PD-L1 ≥50%.

• However, real-world evidence remains limited, particularly regarding outcomes in patients with lower PD-L1 expression.

• This study provides real-world data showing that patients with PD-L1 1–49% may also derive substantial benefit from adjuvant atezolizumab, supporting its broader clinical applicability beyond highly selected trial populations.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Restricting adjuvant atezolizumab use solely to patients with PD-L1 ≥50% may exclude potentially eligible high-risk patients.

• PD-L1 expression should be considered alongside clinical and pathological features when selecting candidates for adjuvant immunotherapy.

• These real-world results support the integration of atezolizumab into routine perioperative decision-making for appropriately selected patients with resected stage II–III NSCLC, highlighting the need for further multi-institutional studies with longer follow-up.

Introduction

Background

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer (LC) cases and remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide (1). Surgical resection is the standard treatment for early-stage NSCLC; however, recurrence rates remain high among patients with pathological stage II–III disease (2). Although adjuvant chemotherapy (AC) provides modest survival benefits, overall prognosis remains poor (3). Recently, immune checkpoint inhibitors have emerged as promising therapeutic options across multiple stages of NSCLC (4). The phase III IMpower010 trial demonstrated a significant disease-free survival (DFS) benefit with adjuvant atezolizumab, a programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitor, following platinum-based chemotherapy in patients with PD-L1-positive resected stage II–III NSCLC (4). Therefore, atezolizumab has been approved for adjuvant use in several countries, including Japan.

Rationale and knowledge gap

Clinical trials often enroll highly selected patient populations, and evidence regarding the real-world effectiveness and safety of adjuvant atezolizumab remains limited. Understanding how these findings translate into routine clinical practice is crucial for optimizing treatment strategies.

Objective

This study aimed to evaluate the real-world effectiveness and safety of adjuvant atezolizumab in patients with pathological stage II–III NSCLC following curative-intent surgery, providing insights into its clinical utility outside the controlled setting of clinical trials. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1-2564/rc).

Methods

Study design and ethics statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee of Hyogo Medical University, Nishinomiya, Japan (No. 4726) and informed consent was obtained from all individual participants.

Patient data, eligibility criteria, and treatment strategy

We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of patients who underwent surgery for LC at a single institution between January 2021 and December 2024. Inclusion criteria were: age ≥18 years, completely resected pathological stage IIA‒IIIB NSCLC according to the 8th edition of the Union for International Cancer Control/American Joint Committee on Cancer (UICC/AJCC) staging system, prior treatment with adjuvant platinum-based doublet chemotherapy, and initiation of adjuvant atezolizumab with at least one administered cycle. Patients with pathological stage 0, IA, IB, or IV disease (according to the 8th edition of the UICC/AJCC staging system); those who underwent non-curative resection; those who did not receive platinum-based AC; and those who did not receive adjuvant atezolizumab were excluded.

Atezolizumab (1,200 mg/body) was administered intravenously on day one of each 21-day cycle for up to 1 year, or until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. Treatment completion was defined as receiving 16 cycles, corresponding to approximately 1 year of therapy. Patients who received atezolizumab were identified through the institutional database. Patient demographics, tumor characteristics, and treatment details were collected via electronic medical record review.

Follow-up and diagnosis of recurrence

Patients were followed up every 3 weeks to 3 months until death or last contact. Each visit included a physical examination, blood tests, and chest radiography or computed tomography. A multidisciplinary cancer board diagnosed recurrence. Upon confirmation of recurrence, subsequent treatment was administered based on the clinical status of the patient.

Evaluation of outcomes and statistical analyses

We retrospectively analyzed medical records to evaluate overall survival (OS), DFS, and treatment safety. OS was defined as the time from atezolizumab initiation to death from any cause or last follow-up. DFS was defined as the time from adjuvant atezolizumab initiation to radiologically confirmed recurrence, death from any cause, or last follow-up. OS and DFS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP version 14 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). To evaluate safety, adverse events (AEs) were graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0 (CTCAE v5.0) guidelines. The data cut-off date was September 15, 2025.

Results

Study flowchart

The study flowchart is presented in Figure 1. Between January 2021 and December 2024, 646 consecutive patients underwent curative-intent surgery for LC. Of these, 518 had pathological stage 0, IA, or IB disease; 11 had stage IV disease; and 117 had pathological stage II–III disease, who were typically eligible for platinum-based AC. Among the 117 patients with pathological stage II–III disease, 50 received platinum-based and 67 did not. Of the 67 patients who did not receive platinum-based AC, five received UFT and two received S-1. Twelve patients with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-positive tumors received adjuvant osimertinib, and one received adjuvant pembrolizumab. Eight patients underwent R2 resection and were subsequently treated as having unresectable disease. Four patients were diagnosed with small-cell lung cancer. Of the remaining 35 patients who were observed without further treatment, the reasons included poor performance status (PS; 11 cases), patient refusal (9 cases), comorbidities (5 cases), advanced age (5 cases), and other reasons (5 cases).

Among the 50 patients who received platinum-based AC, regimens included cisplatin (CDDP) plus vinorelbine (VNR) in 34 patients, CDDP plus docetaxel (DOC) in 3, CDDP plus pemetrexed (PEM) in 1, carboplatin (Cb) plus paclitaxel (PTX) in 4, Cb plus gemcitabine (GEM) in 4, Cb plus S-1 in 2, Cb plus PEM in 1, and Cb plus VNR in 1 (Table 1). After completing platinum-based AC, 25 patients ultimately received treatment with atezolizumab. Among the 25 patients who did not receive adjuvant atezolizumab, the reasons were as follows: PD-L1 negativity (six patients), treatment before the approval of atezolizumab (six patients), poor PS (three patients), early recurrence (three patients), patient refusal (three patients), adjuvant alectinib for anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)-positive tumors (two patients), adjuvant osimertinib for EGFR-positive tumors (one patient), and interstitial lung disease (ILD) during AC (one patient).

Efficacy evaluation

Kaplan-Meier curves for overall DFS and DFS stratified by PD-L1 expression after atezolizumab administration are presented in Figure 3. The median DFS was not reached, and the 2-year DFS rate was 63.8%. The 2-year DFS rates by PD-L1 expression were 72.9% and 57.7% for the 1–49% and ≥50% groups, respectively (P=0.37). Among the 20 patients who received adjuvant CDDP plus VNR followed by atezolizumab, the median DFS was not reached, and the 2-year DFS and OS rates were 64.2% and 87.7%, respectively.

Safety evaluation

Toxicities were retrospectively assessed for all patients using the CTCAE v5.0. AEs are summarized in Table 4. A total of 21 AEs occurred in 15 patients (60.0%), including 2 (8.0%) grade 3 AEs. No grade 4 or 5 AEs, febrile neutropenia, or treatment-related deaths were observed. The most common AEs were adrenocortical insufficiency in 4 patients (16.0%), chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy in 3 (12.0%), thyrotoxicosis in 3 (12.0%), and dermatitis in 3 (12.0%). Grade 3 chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy occurred in one patient after the first cycle of atezolizumab and led to treatment discontinuation. Four patients permanently discontinued atezolizumab due to AEs: grade 3 peripheral neuropathy, grade 3 hepatic dysfunction, grade 2 thyrotoxicosis, and grade 1 ILD.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the real-world use of AC for pathological stage II–III completely resected NSCLC at a single Japanese university hospital, with a particular focus on the efficacy and safety of atezolizumab. Among patients who underwent surgical resection for lung cancer, approximately 1 in 5 (18.1%) had pathological stage II–III disease, for which platinum-based AC is generally indicated. Of these patients, 42.7% received platinum-based AC, and atezolizumab was subsequently introduced in approximately half of those who received platinum-based AC (50.0%). Notably, among patients treated with atezolizumab, the proportions of those with low and high PD-L1 expression were nearly equal. The immune-related AEs observed were consistent with those in previous reports, and most were manageable without necessitating treatment discontinuation. Although the median follow-up duration was relatively short at 27.4 months, patients with low PD-L1 expression demonstrated DFS comparable to that of patients with high PD-L1 expression. Our findings provide important insights into the current clinical practice and may help clarify the potential role of atezolizumab in the adjuvant setting.

The IMpower010 trial established the role of adjuvant atezolizumab following platinum-based chemotherapy in patients with resected stage IB–IIIA NSCLC (4). Its recently reported 5-year follow-up confirmed durable clinical benefit, particularly in stage II–IIIA disease (5,6). Among patients with PD-L1 TC expression ≥1%, atezolizumab significantly prolonged median DFS compared with best supportive care [68.5 vs. 37.3 months; hazard ratio (HR) 0.70, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.55–0.91], whereas OS showed a favorable but non-significant trend (HR 0.77, 95% CI: 0.56–1.06) (5). The benefit was most pronounced in the PD-L1 ≥50% subgroup, with 5-year DFS rates of 65.1% vs. 44.5% (HR 0.48, 95% CI: 0.32–0.72) and 5-year OS rates of 82.7% vs. 65.3% (HR 0.47, 95% CI: 0.28–0.77) (5). Based on these results, the Japanese Lung Cancer Society Clinical Practice Guidelines weakly recommend adjuvant atezolizumab monotherapy following cisplatin-based chemotherapy for patients with completely resected pathological stage IIB–IIIA (8th edition) NSCLC whose TCs exhibit PD-L1 expression ≥50% (7).

In accordance with current guidelines, platinum-based AC is required before atezolizumab initiation (8-10). Accordingly, understanding how this recommendation is implemented in real-world practice is of considerable importance.

In the LACE meta-analysis, AC demonstrated a significant survival benefit in patients with stage II–III NSCLC, with an overall HR of 0.89 and an absolute 5.4% improvement in 5-year OS (3). CDDP plus VNR has been widely used as the standard adjuvant regimen (11). Consistently, in the IMpower010 subgroup analysis, the CDDP plus VNR subgroup showed the most favorable DFS, with an HR of 0.67 (4). However, in real-world clinical practice, many patients are ineligible for cisplatin, and the effectiveness of carboplatin-based regimens as alternative treatments has been reported (12). In our study, carboplatin-containing regimens were also frequently used, and among patients with high PD-L1 expression, atezolizumab demonstrated particularly strong efficacy. These findings suggest that carboplatin-based regimens may serve as an effective bridge to subsequent atezolizumab therapy in this population.

Conversely, in recent years, several pivotal clinical trials have evaluated perioperative systemic therapies for NSCLC. These include adjuvant immunotherapy in IMpower010, neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in CheckMate 816, and perioperative approaches such as KEYNOTE-671 and AEGEAN (13-15). Targeted approaches have also demonstrated substantial benefit, as shown in ADAURA with adjuvant osimertinib for EGFR-mutated NSCLC and ALINA with adjuvant alectinib for ALK-positive disease (16,17). Collectively, these studies have introduced multiple potentially practice-changing regimens into clinical practice, resulting in a rapidly evolving and crowded therapeutic landscape with overlapping indications and ongoing debate regarding the optimal standard of care (10). Within this evolving landscape, several issues concerning adjuvant atezolizumab administration have emerged.

The first issue concerns whether patients with clinical stage II–III NSCLC should be treated with neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy or undergo upfront surgery followed by AC with atezolizumab. A key point in resolving this discussion is that pathological assessment before treatment is critically important for guiding decisions regarding perioperative therapeutic strategies (10). In the recent IASLC consensus by Spicer et al., the panel recommended prioritizing neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy for clinically resectable stage III NSCLC, whereas treatment strategies for stage II disease remain in equipoise between perioperative approaches and upfront surgery followed by adjuvant therapy (10). A key message from this consensus is the critical role of multidisciplinary decision-making and comprehensive preoperative biomarker assessment, including EGFR and ALK alterations as well as PD-L1 expression (9). Notably, the panel emphasized that patients with actionable EGFR or ALK driver mutations should not receive immune checkpoint inhibitors in either the neoadjuvant or adjuvant setting, underscoring the need to tailor perioperative management based on molecular profiles (10).

The second issue is whether adjuvant atezolizumab is appropriate for patients with low PD-L1 expression. In an exploratory IMpower010 subgroup analysis, patients with PD-L1 TC 1–49% demonstrated a clear DFS benefit (HR 0.91, 95% CI: 0.65–1.27), suggesting that the overall DFS advantage in the PD-L1 ≥1% population is largely driven by those with higher PD-L1 expression (5). However, as highlighted by Marmarelis et al., PD-L1 is an imperfect and dynamic biomarker, with substantial intratumoral heterogeneity, discordance between primary and metastatic sites, assay-to-assay variability, and temporal changes with prior therapy (18-20). In IMpower010 specifically, the low-sensitivity SP142 assay was used for enrollment, whereas the more sensitive SP263 assay was used for analysis, further complicating the strict application of PD-L1 thresholds in routine practice (4). Given these limitations, small numerical differences between PD-L1 subgroups should be interpreted with caution, as restricting atezolizumab to PD-L1 ≥50% risks denying potential benefit to high-risk patients with PD-L1 1–49% (18). Marmarelis et al. therefore recommend integrating PD-L1 status with clinical risk factors (such as tumor size, extent of nodal involvement, comorbidities, and PS) and considering adjuvant atezolizumab for all patients with PD-L1-expressing stage II–IIIA NSCLC, particularly given the clinically meaningful DFS improvements and the inconsistent pattern of PD-L1-stratified benefit observed across adjuvant immunotherapy trials (such as greater DFS benefit in the PD-L1 1–49% subgroup than in PD-L1 ≥50% in PEARLS/KEYNOTE-091) (18,21,22). Similarly, in our cohort, approximately half of the patients had tumors with PD-L1 expression of 1–49%. Despite the small sample size, DFS outcomes in these patients were comparable to those of patients with PD-L1 ≥50%. In addition, most patients were able to complete the planned course of therapy, suggesting that adjuvant atezolizumab may confer meaningful benefit even in this PD-L1 1–49% subgroup.

This study has some limitations. First, the single-center, single-arm, retrospective design introduces potential biases, including selection bias and unmeasured confounders, which may affect external validity. Our study population may not be representative of populations in North America or Europe. In addition, analyses according to PD-L1 expression were exploratory and descriptive in nature. Given the small sample size, caution is required when interpreting comparisons of efficacy between patients with PD-L1 expression of 1–49% and those with PD-L1 expression ≥50%. Second, the retrospective design introduces variability in follow-up schedules, potentially causing discrepancies between the actual timing of recurrence and the recorded date of diagnosis, which could affect recurrence assessments. Third, the study was concluded before all survival data could be collected, and the follow-up period on all patients after atezolizumab initiation was relatively short, resulting in immature DFS and OS. Further research with larger patient cohorts, longer follow-up, and multi-institutional participation is warranted to confirm these findings. Fourth, although several major clinical guidelines, including those from the American Society of Clinical Oncology, European Society for Medical Oncology, and the Japan Lung Cancer Society, recognize cisplatin-based chemotherapy as the evidence-established standard for AC in resected NSCLC, carboplatin-based regimens are sometimes used in real-world practice depending on renal function, neurotoxicity, and PS (6,9,23). While carboplatin-based AC has been proposed as an alternative option in selected clinical situations, the supporting evidence remains limited and it is not currently considered a standard recommended regimen (23). Therefore, the inclusion of patients treated with carboplatin-based regimens in the present cohort may have influenced the interpretation of results. In addition, because this study included cases treated before adjuvant osimertinib became widely available, a small number of patients with EGFR-mutated tumors received adjuvant atezolizumab, which differs from current clinical practice and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusions

In this real-world cohort of patients with resected stage II–III NSCLC, adjuvant atezolizumab following platinum-based chemotherapy was generally well tolerated and was associated with clinically meaningful DFS. DFS outcomes observed across PD-L1 expression subgroups, including patients with PD-L1 expression of 1–49%, are descriptive in nature and should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, these findings provide real-word insights into the feasibility and potential clinical utility of adjuvant atezolizumab in appropriately selected patients after curative-intent surgery and chemotherapy.
