Breaking the storm: canakinumab as salvage therapy for refractory macrophage activation syndrome in pediatric systemic lupus erythematosus—a case report
Authors: Tarun Selvarajan, Lavanya Kannekanti, Ashlyn Green, Alexandra Zamora Smith, Sarwat Umer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMacrophage activation syndrome (MAS) complicating pediatric systemic lupus erythematosus (cSLE) is a life-threatening hyperinflammatory state driven by dysregulated cytokine release. A recent systematic literature review identified only a limited number of cSLE-MAS cases treated with IL-1 inhibitors, with variable efficacy, underscoring the need for evidence-based salvage strategies when first-line biologic therapy fails.Case summaryWe report a 17-year-old female with cSLE who developed secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis/MAS fulfilling HLH-2004 criteria approximately 17 months after initial diagnosis, in the setting of medication nonadherence and nutritional failure. The MAS course was refractory to intravenous immunoglobulin, pulse methylprednisolone, dexamethasone, cyclosporine, and intravenous anakinra, and was further complicated by a secondary Clostridioides difficile superinfection precipitating a MAS flare with ferritin peaking at > 10,000 ng/mL. Following transfer to our institution, subcutaneous canakinumab at standard weight-based dosing produced prompt defervescence, sustained ferritin normalization, and clinical stabilization permitting transition to outpatient immunomodulation.ConclusionsThis case highlights canakinumab as a viable salvage therapeutic option for patients with biologic-refractory cSLE-MAS. Emerging evidence supports an expanding role for selective IL-1β blockade in pediatric lupus-associated hyperinflammatory syndromes; prospective studies are needed to define optimal dosing, sequencing, and treatment duration.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex autoimmune disease with heterogeneous manifestations across multiple organ systems. Pediatric SLE (cSLE) presents additional diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to higher disease activity, more severe renal and cardiac involvement, and increased incidence of life-threatening complications compared with adult-onset disease [1, 2].

Macrophage activation syndrome (MAS) represents a severe complication of cSLE, occurring as a manifestation of secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) driven by unchecked IL-1and IL-6 mediated cytokine release [3]. The reported incidence in pediatric lupus ranges from 0.7% to 4.6%, though this may underestimate true prevalence due to diagnostic overlap with active SLE flares [2, 4]. Untreated MAS carries significant mortality, necessitating rapid recognition and escalation of immunosuppression when conventional therapy proves insufficient [5].

Anakinra (recombinant IL-1 receptor antagonist) has been used as biologic salvage therapy in selected cases of refractory MAS; however, a recent systematic literature review of MAS treatment worldwide identified only a limited number of cSLE-MAS cases treated with IL-1 inhibitors, and the efficacy of this approach was variable across patients [6, 7]. Treatment failures occur in a subset, whether due to inadequate biochemical response despite multi-agent therapy, concurrent infectious triggers that preclude further immunosuppressive intensification, or practical limitations in critically ill patients. In such scenarios, alternative IL-1 targeted strategies may be warranted [8].

Canakinumab, a fully human monoclonal antibody selectively targeting IL-1β, has been successfully employed in MAS complicating systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA) and in refractory hyperferritinemic syndromes more broadly [9, 10]. Although systematic data in cSLE-MAS are limited, emerging case-level evidence supports its potential as a salvage biologic agent when anakinra fails or transition to a longer-acting maintenance agent is clinically indicated. We present a case of severe, biologic-refractory cSLE complicated by MAS that achieved rapid clinical and biochemical resolution following subcutaneous canakinumab initiation.

Case presentation

A 17-year-old female with congenital dilated cardiomyopathy presented with a two-week prodrome of progressive dyspnea, fatigue, and arthralgias. Laboratory evaluation revealed profound symptomatic anemia (hemoglobin 4.2 g/dL), thrombocytopenia (platelets 35,000/µL), and evidence of hemolysis. Serologic testing demonstrated positive antinuclear antibody (ANA, titer 1:1280, homogeneous pattern), anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) and anti-Smith (anti-Sm) antibodies, and markedly suppressed complement (C3 < 20 mg/dL; C4 undetectable). Urinalysis showed 4 + proteinuria without hematuria. Transthoracic echocardiography documented a new dilated cardiomyopathy with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of approximately 45%, representing deterioration from her established baseline of 55%. A diagnosis of SLE was established per 2019 EULAR/ACR classification criteria [11]. Initial therapy consisted of intravenous methylprednisolone pulse followed by oral prednisone and mycophenolate mofetil; hydroxychloroquine was deferred due to concerns regarding cardiotoxicity in the setting of pre-existing cardiomyopathy.

The patient’s subsequent course was complicated by severe depression, medication nonadherence, nutritional failure, and loss to follow-up. Hospitalization for refeeding syndrome was complicated by profound electrolyte disturbances, respiratory failure requiring PICU admission, and a witnessed cardiopulmonary arrest with multisystem organ dysfunction, resulting in significant interruption of SLE-directed immunosuppression. Approximately 17 months after initial SLE diagnosis, the patient developed a hyperinflammatory syndrome fulfilling HLH-2004 diagnostic criteria [12], characterized by severe pancytopenia (platelets 4,000/µL; WBC 1.97 × 10³/µL; hemoglobin 8.8 g/dL), markedly elevated ferritin, hypofibrinogenemia (fibrinogen 95 mg/dL; reference range 200–400 mg/dL), hypertriglyceridemia (triglycerides 287 mg/dL), elevated soluble IL-2 receptor (sIL-2R 1,250 U/mL), and hemophagocytosis on both peripheral blood smear and bone marrow biopsy, consistent with MAS secondary to active SLE. NK cell activity was not tested; however, the patient fulfilled at least six of eight HLH-2004 criteria (fever, trilineage cytopenias, hypertriglyceridemia, hypofibrinogenemia, hemophagocytosis, and elevated sCD25), satisfying the diagnostic threshold of five or more criteria.

Initial management included intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG; 1 g/kg × 2 doses), pulse methylprednisolone (10 mg/kg/day IV, escalated to twice daily given persistent MAS activity), and mycophenolate mofetil (progressively escalated to 1 g twice daily). Intravenous anakinra was initiated at 100 mg twice daily and escalated to 100 mg three times daily; the intravenous route was selected given the severity of illness and concern for impaired subcutaneous absorption in a critically ill, nutritionally depleted patient [13]. This produced partial clinical improvement with resolution of fever and improved oral intake; however, persistent cytopenias and hyperferritinemia indicated incomplete MAS control, prompting transition to dexamethasone (up to 10 mg/m²/day orally) and addition of cyclosporine (3 mg/kg/day), reflecting its established role in HLH management. Upon planned anakinra discontinuation in anticipation of canakinumab transition, ferritin initially continued to trend downward; however, the patient subsequently developed a MAS flare with ferritin rising rapidly to a peak of > 10,000 ng/mL, precipitated by concurrent Clostridioides difficile colitis diagnosed during this episode. Anakinra was restarted alongside oral vancomycin; the patient became afebrile and inflammatory markers improved, though ferritin remained substantially elevated.

After transfer to our institution, the patient received IV canakinumab 4.4 mg/kg with dramatic improvement within 48 h, including defervescence, ferritin decline to 4,200 ng/mL, improving inflammatory markers, and marked clinical recovery, with ferritin reaching 800 ng/mL by day 14 and continued hematologic and clinical stabilization. Prednisone was successfully tapered from 1.5 to 0.5 mg/kg/day without relapse (Fig. 1). She was maintained on canakinumab (IV loading followed by 2.2 mg/kg subcutaneously every 4 weeks), mycophenolate mofetil, and gradual steroid taper, with hydroxychloroquine avoided due to cardiomyopathy. At 3-month follow-up, she remained clinically stable with normalized labs and no recurrence of hyperinflammation.

Discussion

This case illustrates successful canakinumab rescue in a critically ill adolescent with cSLE-associated MAS refractory to a comprehensive, stepwise immunosuppressive escalation [1]. The clinical course was exceptionally complex, encompassing multiorgan SLE involvement, severe psychiatric comorbidity, nutritional failure, critical illness, and MAS fulfilling HLH-2004 criteria [12], with a secondary flare precipitated by Clostridioides difficile superinfection. In this context, persistent hyperferritinemia and incomplete MAS control despite multi-agent therapy, compounded by an emerging infectious trigger, prompted a strategic transition to canakinumab rather than further anakinra dose escalation.

IL-1 blockade is a cornerstone in the management of MAS and related hyperferritinemic syndromes in children [8]. Anakinra is favored for its rapid onset and flexible dosing, with pediatric series reporting response rates of approximately 50–90% in MAS complicating various rheumatic diseases, including cSLE [3, 6]. However, a recent systematic literature review conducted by Baldo and colleagues to inform the METAPHOR project identified only a limited number of cSLE-MAS cases treated with IL-1 inhibitors and documented variable efficacy, with no randomized comparative data available [7]. The intravenous route of anakinra administration, selected in our patient given severity of illness and concern for impaired subcutaneous absorption in a critically ill, thrombocytopenic patient, has been well-described in pediatric MAS/HLH cohorts at doses up to 15 mg/kg/day [13]. The decision to transition to canakinumab rather than escalate anakinra further was individualized, reflecting incomplete biochemical response, the complicating infectious trigger, and the advantage of a longer-acting agent suited to outpatient continuation.

Canakinumab, a fully human monoclonal antibody directed against IL-1β, provides sustained cytokine neutralization with a half-life of approximately 26 days and has demonstrated efficacy in sJIA-associated MAS [9, 10]. In our patient, subcutaneous canakinumab at standard weight-based dosing produced rapid defervescence, a sustained ferritin decline, and hematologic recovery, permitting meaningful steroid tapering and transition to outpatient care. Maintenance subcutaneous canakinumab combined with mycophenolate mofetil and low-dose prednisone was associated with sustained MAS remission at three-month follow-up without recurrence. These observations support the biological plausibility and clinical utility of selective IL-1β blockade as salvage therapy when anakinra fails or when transition to a longer-acting outpatient-compatible agent is indicated in cSLE-MAS [8].

Limitations include the single-patient design, short follow-up duration, and concurrent administration of multiple immunosuppressive agents, which precludes definitive attribution of improvement exclusively to canakinumab. Nonetheless, the temporal relationship between canakinumab initiation and rapid clinical and biochemical improvement is compelling [6]. Prospective studies are needed to define optimal dosing, route of administration, duration of therapy, and long-term safety of canakinumab in cSLE-associated MAS [8].

Conclusion

This case report demonstrates successful rescue therapy with subcutaneous canakinumab in a patient with severe, biologic-refractory MAS complicating pediatric SLE. The rapid clinical and biochemical response following canakinumab—characterized by prompt defervescence, sustained ferritin normalization, hematologic recovery, and maintained disease control at three-month follow-up—supports its potential utility as salvage therapy when first-line IL-1 blockade fails or is suboptimally tolerated, and when transition to a longer-acting maintenance agent is clinically indicated. While prospective controlled studies are needed to establish optimal treatment protocols, canakinumab represents a valuable therapeutic option in the management of biologic-refractory cSLE-MAS. Clinicians caring for pediatric lupus patients should consider canakinumab in cases demonstrating inadequate response to anakinra, particularly when rapid disease control is critical to prevent organ damage or mortality. Further research is urgently needed to define comparative efficacy, optimal dosing strategies, treatment duration, and long-term safety of IL-1 inhibition approaches in this vulnerable patient population.
