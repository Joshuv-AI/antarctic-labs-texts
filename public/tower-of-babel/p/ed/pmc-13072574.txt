Association between emotional intelligence and triage accuracy among emergency nurses: a cross-sectional descriptive correlational study
Authors: Hamed Gholizad Gougjehyaran, Hossein Ebrahimi, Reza Nematollahi Maleki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmotional intelligence (EI) is a critical non-technical skill that plays an important role in nurses’ performance, particularly in high-pressure environments such as emergency departments. Accurate triage decision-making is essential for ensuring patient safety and optimal resource allocation. Background

Triage is a fundamental process in emergency care aimed at rapidly identifying patients who require immediate or time-critical interventions, prioritizing treatment based on clinical urgency rather than order of arrival, and ensuring appropriate allocation of resources such as staff, space, and equipment [1, 2]. Effective triage contributes to patient safety and reduction of morbidity and mortality, particularly during periods of high demand or resource constraints [3, 4].

Since the early 1990s, several countries have developed triage systems. One such system is the ESI, which was first introduced in 1999 (first edition) by two emergency medicine specialists, Richard Wuerz and David Eitel [5]. In this method, the triage nurse classifies patients based on two criteria: the severity of the condition and the resources required. The first criterion is determined by the presence of life- or limb-threatening conditions, danger signs, and vital signs, while the second is based on the nurse’s clinical judgment. The unique feature of the ESI system, distinguishing it from other triage models, is the nurse’s role in estimating the diagnostic and therapeutic resources needed for each patient [6, 7].

Several key characteristics are essential for the effective performance of triage nurses, including strong clinical knowledge, decision-making skills, and excellent communication abilities. Research indicates that triage nurses with extensive experience and specialized training demonstrate higher levels of accuracy in triage assessments [6, 8]. Their ability to rapidly analyze patient information and make informed decisions plays a crucial role in determining the appropriate level of care in a timely manner. Accurate triage not only enhances patient safety but also improves overall workflow in emergency settings, potentially reducing patient wait times and optimizing resource allocation [9, 10]. Evidence also indicates that triage accuracy varies across studies, with agreement with the correct triage level typically reported at around 60% to 80%, while under-triage rates are estimated at approximately 5% to 15% and over-triage rates at about 15% to 30% [11].

One of the key factors that significantly influences the accuracy of nurses’ decisions is emotional intelligence. Emotional intelligence is a multifaceted array of interrelated emotions, social competencies, skills, and facilitators that affect an individual’s capabilities [12, 13]. It is a practical and essential nursing skill at all stages of patient care and a critical factor in improving the quality of nursing services. Research shows that emotional intelligence plays an important role in enhancing nurse–patient communication, and nurses with high emotional intelligence demonstrate more effective clinical interactions [14, 15]. Individuals with higher levels of emotional intelligence can more easily identify coping strategies, implement effective actions to resolve problems, and find efficient solutions [16]. This not only facilitates communication among nurses but also contributes to improving the quality of care and patient recovery [17]. Previous studies conducted in emergency settings have mainly examined the general relationship between emotional intelligence and outcomes such as job stress, burnout, and communication, and evidence suggests that higher levels of emotional intelligence are generally associated with lower job stress and reduced burnout, as individuals with greater ability to regulate emotions and cope with psychological pressures are better able to adapt to the intense emotional demands of emergency care [18–20].

Given that previous research has primarily focused on structural and skill-related factors such as experience, training, and workload, the role of psychological factors particularly emotional intelligence has received less attention, despite the fact that emergency departments are characterized by emotional pressures, uncertainty, and the need for rapid decision-making. Moreover, existing evidence on the relationship between emotional intelligence and nurses’ professional outcomes suggests that this capability may influence information processing, stress management, and the quality of clinical judgment. Therefore, examining the relationship between emotional intelligence and triage accuracy can help address this knowledge gap and provide a basis for designing educational and managerial interventions aimed at improving decision-making quality and enhancing patient outcomes in emergency settings.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional descriptive correlational study was conducted between July and October 2025 to explore the relationship between emotional intelligence and triage accuracy among emergency nurses.

Study setting

The study was conducted in selected hospitals, including Imam Reza, Shahid Madani, Sina, and Taleghani Hospitals. These hospitals were chosen to represent a diverse range of emergency departments and to enhance the generalizability of the fin dings.

Participants

The participants of this study were emergency nurses from the selected hospitals.

Inclusion criteria

Nurses working in the emergency department, at least six months of clinical experience in emergency care, and willingness to participate in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Nurses on leave during the study period, and nurses with incomplete questionnaire responses.

Sampling method

In this study, a proportionate stratified random sampling method was used to select participants. This method was chosen to ensure a balanced distribution of nurses across hospitals and to enhance the generalizability of the findings. The sample size was proportionally allocated according to the number of emergency nurses working in each hospital. After determining the stratum for each hospital, samples were selected from emergency nurses using simple random sampling based on a random numbers table. Nurses were stratified based on characteristics such as gender, work experience, or department (general emergency, specialized emergency), and then randomly selected from each stratum.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on previous similar studies reporting a correlation coefficient of 0.3 between emotional intelligence and clinical performance, with a 95% confidence level and 90% statistical power. Using the Fisher Z transformation formula for Pearson correlation, the required sample size was estimated to be approximately 113 participants. Considering a potential 20% attrition rate, the final sample size was increased to 145 emergency nurses to ensure sufficient statistical power.

Evaluation tool and data collection

three questionnaires were used for data collection in the present study, including the demographic questionnaire, Siberia Schering Emotional Intelligence Questionnaire, and Triage Accuracy Questionnaire. Eligible emergency nurses were approached during their work shifts, and the study objectives were explained to them. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation.

Demographic questionnaire

The first part was a demographic questionnaire that gathered information such as age, gender, years of work experience, educational level, and other relevant variables for examining the baseline characteristics of the participants.

Siberia Schering Emotional Intelligence Questionnaire

The second tool was the Siberia Schering Emotional Intelligence Questionnaire, which consists of 33 items. The items assess various dimensions of emotional intelligence, including self-motivation, self-awareness, self-regulation, empathy (or social awareness), and social (communication) skills. Each participant receives one total score and five separate scores corresponding to the five subscales. Responses are rated on a five-point Likert scale: always, often, sometimes, rarely, and never. In some questions, selecting option A is scored as 5, while options B, C, D, and E are scored as 4, 3, 2, and 1 respectively. In some other questions, the scoring is reversed. Specifically, in items 1, 3, 9, 12, 13, 14, 18, 20, 22, 28, 31, and 33, selecting option A scores 5 points, and the subsequent options score 4 to 1 in order. For the rest of the items, the scoring is reversed. The maximum total score is 165. Scores are interpreted as follows: scores between 132 and 165 indicate excellent emotional intelligence, 99 to 131 indicate good emotional intelligence, 66 to 98 indicate average, and 33 to 65 indicate low emotional intelligence. The operational definition of self-awareness is the score obtained from items 6, 10, 12, 14, 24, 27, 32, and 33. Self-regulation is defined by the total score of items 2, 5, 11, 16, 18, 23, and 30. Empathy is measured by the scores of items 3, 4, 17, 22, 25, and 29. Social skills are assessed through items 7, 8, 13, 19, and 28. Self-motivation is measured by items 1, 9, 15, 20, 21, 26, and 31 [21, 22]. The questionnaire has demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties in various studies. Reported Cronbach’s alpha coefficients range from 0.83 to 0.85, indicating good internal consistency. Construct validity was supported by a correlation coefficient of 0.63 with the Cooper smith Self-Esteem Inventory [23]. In a study by Farzadnia in 2012, Cronbach’s alpha for the subscales was reported as 0.84 [24]. Similarly, in a study by Bani Hashemian et al. in 2010, the overall reliability was reported as 0.85, confirming the appropriateness of the instrument for research purposes [22].

Triage accuracy questionnaire

For assessing triage accuracy, 20 standardized clinical scenarios were selected from the Emergency Severity Index (ESI) Version 4 manual, which is a valid and widely recognized tool for evaluating decision-making in triage. The scenarios were administered individually to each participant in a controlled environment. Each scenario was presented in written form, and participants were asked to determine the appropriate triage level within 60 to 90 s to simulate real-world triage conditions. The total duration of the assessment for each participant was approximately 20 to 30 min. The scenarios were translated from the triage manual by an emergency medicine specialist to ensure accuracy and clinical appropriateness.

The scoring procedure was clearly defined to ensure transparency in evaluation. Each of the 20 ESI scenarios was scored as correct (1) or incorrect (0) based on the degree of agreement between the participant’s response and the standard answer key extracted from the official ESI Version 4 manual. The total score was calculated by summing the item scores. The answer key was determined according to standard ESI guidelines. An independent observer supervised the testing sessions to ensure that instructions were delivered uniformly, time limits were followed, and no external assistance was provided to participants.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages.

Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the associations between total emotional intelligence, its subscales, and triage accuracy. 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for correlation coefficients were estimated using Fisher’s z transformation.

Multivariable associations were examined using multiple linear regression analysis with the enter method. Emotional intelligence (total score and subscales) was entered as the main independent variable, and triage accuracy as the dependent variable. Covariates including age, gender, work experience, and employment status were included based on theoretical relevance and prior evidence suggesting their potential influence on clinical decision-making and triage performance.

Missing data were examined prior to analysis. Because the proportion of missing values was minimal, complete-case analysis (listwise deletion) was applied.

Model assumptions were assessed before interpreting regression results. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance statistics. Residual plots were examined to assess homoscedasticity and linearity, and the normality of residuals was evaluated using histogram and normal probability plots. Influential observations were assessed using standardized residuals and Cook’s distance. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 145 emergency nurses were initially approached to participate in the study. Of these, 140 nurses completed all components of the questionnaires, yielding a completion rate of 96.5%. The mean age of participants was 32.8 ± 6.4 years, and their mean emergency department work experience was 7.3 ± 4.8 years. The majority of participants were female (68.6%), and most held a bachelor’s degree in nursing (84.3%).

Regarding employment status, 71.4% were full-time nurses, and 28.6% were contractual or part-time staff. Most participants worked in general emergency departments (62.1%), while the remainder were assigned to specialized units such as trauma or cardiac emergency (37.9%). Among the demographic variables, age (β = 0.17, p = 0.022) and work experience (β = 0.19, p = 0.017) were significant predictors of triage accuracy, suggesting that nurses with higher age and longer clinical experience demonstrated greater accuracy in triage decision-making. In contrast, gender (p = 0.192) and education level (p = 0.070) did not show significant associations with triage accuracy.

With regard to emotional intelligence components, self-awareness (β = 0.25, p = 0.006) and self-motivation (β = 0.21, p = 0.019) were identified as significant predictors. These findings indicate that nurses with higher levels of self-awareness and motivation were more likely to make accurate triage decisions. However, the dimensions of self-regulation, empathy, and social skills did not yield statistically significant effects in the final model (p > 0.05).

Overall, the regression analysis highlights that both demographic characteristics particularly age and work experience and specific dimensions of emotional intelligence play an important role in predicting triage accuracy among emergency nurses.

Discussion

The present findings indicate that the mean triage accuracy among emergency nurses was 78.2%, with under-triage (12.5%) occurring slightly more frequently than over-triage (9.3%). These results are consistent with previous studies on triage accuracy in emergency departments. For instance, a review reported triage nurses’ accuracy rates ranging from 59.3% to 82%, with under-triage being a common issue. Similarly, a study found under-triage rates of 16.7% and over-triage of 30.9% in certain settings, highlighting variations but aligning with our observed patterns [25, 26]. The slightly higher rate of under-triage compared with over-triage observed in our study is consistent with evidence suggesting that under-triage remains a persistent challenge in emergency departments, potentially reflecting cautious resource allocation or uncertainty in borderline clinical presentations.

In the current study, total emotional intelligence (Total EI) showed a significant positive correlation with triage accuracy (r = 0.41, p < 0.001). This finding aligns with a substantial body of prior research that has identified emotional intelligence as an important predictor of clinical performance among emergency nurses. For example, a study on emergency room nurses demonstrated positive correlations between emotional intelligence and the quality of clinical decision-making abilities. Additionally, research among nurses found a significant positive correlation between EI and job performance, with coefficients supporting moderate associations similar to ours [27, 28]. Our findings extend this evidence by demonstrating that emotional intelligence remains independently associated with triage accuracy even after adjusting for demographic and professional factors. Importantly, self-awareness and self-motivation emerged as significant independent predictors in the multivariable model.

In the multiple linear regression analysis, self-awareness (β = 0.25, p = 0.006) and self-motivation (β = 0.21, p = 0.019) emerged as independent significant predictors of triage accuracy. These results are supported by studies showing significant relationships between self-awareness, self-motivation, and job performance in health professionals, including nurses. Another investigation highlighted that nurses with higher EI subscales like self-awareness demonstrate enhanced clinical decision-making in high-stakes settings. Interestingly, although empathy showed a significant bivariate correlation, it did not remain an independent predictor in the final model. This may suggest that, in the high-pressure environment of emergency triage, rapid decision-making and internal emotional control (self-awareness and self-motivation) take precedence over processing patients’ emotions, as noted in related work on emotional calibration in clinicians [29, 30]. Similarly, self-motivation may reflect intrinsic engagement and sustained attention, which are essential for maintaining vigilance in high-workload settings such as emergency departments. These mechanisms may explain why these dimensions showed stronger associations than other emotional intelligence components. Regarding demographic variables, age and years of clinical experience were independent predictors of triage accuracy. This finding is entirely consistent with the existing literature; numerous studies have shown that longer clinical experience is associated with higher triage accuracy and better decision-making. The lack of significant effects for gender and educational level is also in line with many previous studies that found no strong influence of these variables on triage accuracy [25, 31, 32].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design restricts the ability to infer causality between emotional intelligence and triage accuracy; longitudinal studies are needed to confirm this relationship. Second, the use of self-report measures for assessing emotional intelligence may introduce response bias, as participants may overestimate or underestimate their abilities. Third, triage accuracy was evaluated using standardized scenarios, which may not fully capture the complexity and unpredictability of real clinical triage situations. Fourth, although the sample was representative of major hospitals in Tabriz, the findings may not be generalizable to nurses working in other regions or healthcare systems with different triage protocols or organizational cultures. Finally, factors such as workload, fatigue, and environmental stressors which may also influence triage accuracy were not measured and should be explored in future studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the results of this study confirm that emotional intelligence particularly the dimensions of self-awareness and self-motivation together with clinical experience, are key determinants of triage accuracy among emergency nurses. These findings provide a strong foundation for developing targeted educational programs aimed at enhancing emotional intelligence (especially self-awareness and intrinsic motivation) in emergency nursing staff, which may ultimately contribute to reducing triage errors and improving patient outcomes.
