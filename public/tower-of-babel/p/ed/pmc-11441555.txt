QnAs with Zhijian “James” Chen: Winner of the 2024 Albert Lasker Basic Medical Research Award
Authors: Matthew Hardcastle
Published: 2024

The presence of DNA in the cytoplasm of a cell can be a sign of microbial infection or cellular dysfunction. When cells sense cytosolic DNA, an immune response is triggered. The role of DNA in triggering immune reactions was recognized prior to the discovery of DNA’s role as the genetic material (1). However, the molecular mechanism responsible for sensing cytosolic DNA remained unknown until 2012, when Zhijian “James” Chen, a biochemist at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center and an Investigator at Howard Hughes Medical Institute, and his colleagues discovered the signaling molecule cyclic guanosine monophosphate–adenosine monophosphate (cyclic GMP-AMP or cGAMP) (2) and the enzyme cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) (3). For this achievement, Chen has been awarded the 2024 Lasker Basic Medical Research Award.

Zhijian “James” Chen. Image credit: UT Southwestern.

The DNA sensing pathway Chen uncovered begins when cGAS detects and binds to cytosolic DNA. In response, cGAS triggers the formation of cGAMP, which binds to Stimulator of Interferon Genes (STING) proteins. STING proteins in turn trigger the activation of transcription factors, which move to the nucleus of the cell to induce the production of type-I interferons and other cytokines, resulting in an immune response. Beyond providing insight into the innate immune response of cells, Chen’s discovery of cGAS and the mechanism of DNA sensing has enabled subsequent research into a range of topics including autoimmune and inflammatory diseases (4), cellular senescence (5), and antitumor immunity (6). PNAS spoke with Chen about his award-winning work.

Why is it important for cells to be able to sense DNA in their cytoplasm?

In eukaryotic cells, DNA is usually in the nucleus and in the mitochondria. Our cytoplasm is largely free of DNA. So we use this system to detect microbial infections. The advantage is that the vast majority of microorganisms have DNA as their essential genetic material, whether it’s viruses, bacteria, or parasites. Even for retroviruses, like HIV, the viral RNA is reverse-transcribed into DNA. The sensing of this microbial DNA provides a very effective mechanism to detect infections.

The danger of that is that our cells also have DNA, and cGAS activation by DNA is independent of DNA sequence. Our strategy is to keep our DNA in the nucleus and the mitochondria. However, under some pathological conditions, our own DNA can get into the cytosol, either from the nucleus or from the mitochondria, and that can also trigger an immune response. In some cases, it can lead to autoimmune diseases and inflammatory disorders.

How did you become interested in the mechanisms of cytosolic DNA sensing?

Before we started working on this, we were actually working on an RNA-sensing pathway: How the immune system detects RNA viruses, like influenza virus, coronaviruses, etc. We discovered a protein that we call mitochondrial antiviral-signaling (MAVS) (7), which is a mitochondrial protein that is very important for immune defense against RNA viruses. Then, we were interested in whether MAVS is also important for immune sensing of cytoplasmic DNA. It turns out that it is not required. It's dispensable (8). This suggested there is another pathway, the DNA sensing pathway. So we started to look into that.

At the time we started working on this, there were actually quite a few putative DNA sensors that had been reported. They were published in a number of papers, and different groups actually identified different DNA sensors. It was kind of confusing; I mean, they cannot all be right. We did some experiments to try to test whether they are involved in DNA sensing. In our hands, they were not required. So, then, we decided to take an unbiased approach to identify this DNA sensor. Our approach was to use biochemistry. We set up a cell-free system that can detect this DNA-sensing activity. It took us about two years to get this assay to work.

What we did was to set up this cell-free system that could recapitulate what’s happening inside cells, and then we could fractionate the cell extract to identify the DNA sensor. Before we did the fractionation, we asked if this DNA sensor was a protein or not. We heated up the cell extract and spun down the denatured proteins and asked whether the supernatant still had this activity. We found that, to our surprise, the activity was heat resistant. In other experiments, we showed that it was resistant to proteinase K treatment and resistant to nuclease treatment. That suggests that it’s a small molecule.

From the same cell-free system, we found there was another activity that was required, suggesting that two factors are responsible for this DNA sensing activity: One is a small molecule, and the other is probably an enzyme that makes this small molecule. We identified both. It turns out the small molecule is cyclic GMP-AMP (2). We call it cGAMP. Then, we tried to purify the enzyme, which was also quite difficult. Eventually, we found that it’s an uncharacterized protein at the time, and we named this protein cGAS or cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (3).

What is the significance of understanding DNA sensing, beyond the cellular immune response?

The aberrant activation of this pathway can lead to many different types of autoimmune diseases and inflammation (4). More and more diseases have been linked to activation of this pathway. These range from rare, monogenic diseases, such as Aicardi–Goutières syndrome or type 1 interferonopathies. There are more common diseases like lupus and neurodegenerations like Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, ALS, etc. The exciting possibility is that inhibition of this pathway could provide treatments for these diseases.

We and others have shown that this pathway plays an important role in cellular senescence (5) and antitumor immunity (6). When cancer cells are engulfed by dendritic cells, the activation of the cGAS–STING pathway by tumor DNA in the cytoplasm of dendritic cells is very important for activation of the adaptive immune system, including priming and recruitment of cytotoxic T cells to kill tumor cells. So, now, there’s also a lot of interest in activating this pathway, hopefully in tumors specifically, to enhance cancer immunotherapy.

What are your next steps in this research?

We have been working on dissecting the signaling mechanisms in the cGAS–STING pathway. For example, exactly how is cGAS activated within the cytoplasm by DNA? And how does activation of cGAS lead to activation of STING? How does STING activate the downstream pathway? There are quite a number of interesting questions regarding signal transduction. We are also very interested in developing agonists and antagonists of this pathway for the treatment of human diseases that range from cancers to neurodegeneration.

This pathway originated in bacteria (9). There’s a very large family of cGAS-like proteins in bacteria, and the major function of bacterial cGAS is for antiphage immune defense, much like the CRISPR system. The bacterial cGAS system is quite sophisticated (10–12). There’s a lot of diversity in terms of the cyclic nucleotides that bacterial cGAS produce, the effector molecules that bind to these cyclic nucleotides, and then the downstream events once these effector molecules bind to the cyclic nucleotides. The hope is that by understanding how bacteria use this cGAS system to fight infection, we’ll be able to uncover new mechanisms of immune response, maybe in mammalian cells.

What does it mean to you to receive the Lasker Award?

It’s a tremendous honor. I think it’s really a recognition of the work that has been done, not only by students and postdocs in my lab but also by many scientists in this field. Although cGAS was discovered not long ago, just a little bit over a decade, there has been tremendous progress. This is really through the collective effort of many labs throughout the world. I think this is a recognition of the work by the community.
