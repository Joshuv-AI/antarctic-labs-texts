Accuracy and heterogeneity of histological analysis for detecting periprosthetic joint infections
Authors: Jiazheng Xu, Lihua Zhang, Yunhao Tang, Zhigang Song, Wei Chai, Rui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntraoperative frozen section histology is a cornerstone for diagnosing periprosthetic joint infection (PJI), yet its reported accuracy varies significantly. Previous studies have primarily focused on the accuracy of histopathological diagnosis for PJI, with analysis of heterogeneous sources remaining insufficient. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of frozen sections for PJI and, specifically, to analyze where the discrepancies may exist.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 171 patients (89 PJI, 82 Aseptic) undergoing revision hip or knee arthroplasty (2015–2023). PJI was defined strictly by International Consensus Meeting (ICM) major criteria to avoid incorporation bias. Aseptic failures were confirmed by a lack of infection signs and > 2 years of survival without antibiotic usage. Diagnostic performance of frozen section in the diagnosis of PJI was evaluated. Histological heterogeneity was quantified by analyzing the concordance rate across three distinct tissue specimens per case. We further compared diagnostic accuracy using different interpretation strategies: requiring either one, two, or three positive samples to confirm the diagnosis. Additionally, inter-observer agreement was assessed by two independent pathologists re-evaluating 81 sections.ResultsThe overall sensitivity was 71.9% (95% CI 61.4–80.9%) and specificity was 75.6% (95% CI 64.9–84.4%), with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.738 (95% CI 0.672–0.803). Diagnostic performance was consistent between hip and knee revisions (p = 0.829). Notably, histological heterogeneity was prevalent: among confirmed PJI cases, only 44.0% (37/84) exhibited consistent positive results across all three specimens, whereas 73.7% of aseptic cases were consistently negative. Among the three specimens, if consider at least one specimen had >5 neutrophils per high power field in 5 high power fields (×400) as histology positive, the sensitivity and specificity was 70.2% (59.3–79.7%) and 73.7% (62.3–83.1%), respectively. Furthermore, inter-observer agreement was moderate (Kappa = 0.581), suggesting that diagnostic interpretation varies between pathologists.ConclusionIntraoperative frozen section analysis provides reliable diagnostic accuracy for patients meeting ICM Major Criteria, with consistent performance across hip and knee revisions. However, the significant heterogeneity existed in both surgical sampling and pathological interpretation. Standardization from the surgeon’s intraoperative sampling to the pathologist’s result interpretation stage may offer a potential solution to this problem.Keywords: Periprosthetic joint infection, Diagnosis, Frozen section, Intraoperative histology, Arthroplasty

Background

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is a catastrophic complication after total joint arthroplasty (TJA) that severely affects patients’ quality of life and imposes a substantial financial burden on the healthcare system [1]. Accurate diagnosis is the prerequisite for choosing an effective therapeutic strategy. However, the diagnosis of PJI remains challenging.

PJI can manifest with a wide spectrum of clinical features [2]. Although the diagnosis based on major criteria (e.g., sinus tract) is definitive, such signs are relatively rare [3, 4], and microbiological cultures are not always positive [5, 6]. Consequently, the diagnostic process often relies on a combination of biomarkers, including serum and synovial fluid analysis [7]. However, serum markers can be confounded by concurrent systemic inflammatory diseases [8, 9]. Moreover, in “dry tap” cases where synovial fluid cannot be aspirated, standard synovial-based tests are unavailable [10, 11]. In these ambiguous scenarios, intraoperative histopathology provides valuable guidance for decision-making.

As a criterion for PJI, neutrophil count in frozen section analysis has been adopted by major guidelines [12–14]. However, its reported diagnostic sensitivity fluctuates wildly, ranging from 43% to 100% [15–17], suggesting significant underlying heterogeneity. Theoretically, heterogeneity may exist in any part of the histopathological analysis. First, whether the hip and knee share similar accuracy in histopathological analysis needs further evidence. Second, the surgeon’s ability to obtain infected tissue directly affects the histopathological results. However, what is the consistency among the tissues which surgeon take from a same case, and how many positive samples are more suitable for the PJI diagnostic criteria are still not clear. Finally, what is the consistency of the results reported by different pathologists is also seldom evaluated.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to provide a detailed assessment of the accuracy and these possible aspects of heterogeneity in the histological diagnosis of PJI.

Method

Study design and patient selection

This study was approved by our Institutional Review Board (2024KY090-KS001). We specifically selected patients undergoing primary revision total knee or hip arthroplasty at our institution between January 2015 and January 2023, and did not include those undergoing the second stage of a two-stage exchange.

To avoid incorporation bias, the definition of PJI was strictly based on the major criteria of the International Consensus Meeting (ICM) [12]: (1) presence of a sinus tract communicating with the prosthesis, or (2) two positive cultures of the same organism. Patients were classified as “Aseptic Failures” only if they met the following strict criteria: (1) no clinical or microbiological evidence of infection (ICM criteria negative); and (2) successful clinical follow-up of at least 2 years without subsequent infection-related failure or antibiotic suppression.

A total of 245 potentially eligible cases were screened. After excluding 74 cases with ambiguous diagnoses (meeting neither major PJI criteria nor strict aseptic criteria), 171 patients (89 PJI and 82 Aseptic) were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Cases without therapeutic antibiotics and who did not fail within 1 year were classified into the Aseptic Cases. Standardized sampling protocol and histopathological analysis

Intraoperative frozen sections were obtained from representative periprosthetic regions, following established histopathological sampling strategies(pseudocapsule, interface membrane, and synovium) aimed at maximizing diagnostic certainty [18]. Fresh tissue samples were immediately sent for intraoperative frozen section analysis. Histological evaluation was performed by pathologists. For each tissue specimen, the number of polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) was quantified under high-power magnification (×400, High-Power Field [HPF]). The mean neutrophil count was calculated from 10 separate fields. A specimen was defined as “positive” if the mean count met the diagnostic threshold (evaluated at ≥5 PMNs/HPF) [12, 14].

Assessment of inter-observer reliability

To assess the reproducibility of neutrophil counting, an independent inter-observer reliability analysis was conducted. A random subset of 81 histological slides (collected between 2020 and 2023) was re-evaluated by two blinded specialized pathologists. Both reviewers were masked to the clinical diagnosis and the original pathology report. Inter-observer agreement was quantified using Cohen’s kappa coefficient.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2) with the epiR and pROC packages. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test, and categorical variables using the Chi-square test. Diagnostic performance metrics—sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV)—were calculated with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve was generated, and the Area Under the Curve (AUC) was calculated. To address the statistical comparison of diagnostic performance: Comparison of AUCs between different subgroups was performed using DeLong’s test. All figures were generated via Prism (version 9.3.1). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Overall diagnostic accuracy and impact of joint type

We first evaluated the diagnostic performance of frozen sections using the standard threshold (≥ 5 PMNs/HPF). In the combined cohort, the sensitivity was 71.9% (95% CI 61.4–80.9%) and specificity was 75.6% (64.9–84.4%), with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.738(95% CI 0.672–0.803) (Fig. 2; Table 3).

When stratified by joint type, no significant difference in diagnostic performance was observed between hip and knee revisions (AUC: Hip 0.733 vs. Knee 0.740, p = 0.829), confirming that frozen section criteria are equally applicable to both joints.

Quantification of Histological Heterogeneity

A total of 160 patients (84 PJI, 76 Aseptic) underwent the complete three-site sampling protocol. In the PJI group, only 37 patients (44.0%) exhibited consistent positive results across all three sampled tissues. This implies that in the remaining 56% of confirmed infections, the inflammatory response was “patchy,” and at least one tissue sample yielded a false-negative result. In contrast, the aseptic group showed higher consistency, with 56 patients (73.7%) demonstrating consistent negative results across all three specimens (Fig. 3).

While the overall discriminative ability (AUC) was statistically comparable across these criteria, requiring multiple positive samples significantly increased the rate of missed diagnoses (false negatives) due to the heterogeneous nature of the infection.

Inter-observer reliability

To assess reproducibility, 81 histopathology slides were independently re-evaluated by two specialized pathologists (Table 5). Diagnostic concordance was achieved in 64 cases (79.0%), with both observers agreeing on 31 positive and 33 negative results. However, discrepancies occurred in 17 cases: Observer 1 identified 7 slides as positive that Observer 2 classified as negative, whereas Observer 2 identified 10 positive slides that Observer 1 deemed negative. The resulting Cohen’s Kappa coefficient was 0.581 (p < 0.01), indicating a moderate level of agreement. This result underscores the inter-observer variability inherent in manual histopathological interpretation.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the accuracy and heterogeneity of histological analysis in diagnosing periprosthetic joint infection (PJI). We found that the diagnostic accuracy of frozen section analysis was consistent between hip and knee joints. However, tissue samples taken from the same case showed considerable inconsistency. Furthermore, even the interpretation of the same tissue sample exhibited only moderate agreement among different observers.

Based on our data, the overall sensitivity and specificity of intraoperative frozen sections for the diagnosis of PJI were 71.9% (61.4–80.9%) and 75.6% (64.9–84.4%), respectively, which are slightly lower than some of reported in previous studies. For instance, Higuera Rueda et al. [17] reported that intraoperative frozen section histology yielded high specificity (98.8%) and accuracy (94.0%) when matched to the MSIS criteria. This discrepancy is likely attributable to incorporation bias. Previous studies often utilized the MSIS/ICM criteria (which include histology) as the reference standard; this inherently inflates the diagnostic performance. In contrast, we strictly selected cases meeting the “major criteria” (sinus tract or positive cultures) as the reference. We acknowledge that by strictly adhering to the Major Criteria resulted in the exclusion of culture-negative infections. Therefore, our accuracy metrics reflect the performance of frozen sections in ‘confirmed’ rather than ‘ambiguous’ cases. Additionally, in our subgroup analysis, we found that the hip and knee joints had similar accuracies (AUC: 0.75 vs. 0.73). This finding supports the current guidelines, which do not separate diagnostic thresholds for hip and knee joints.

The most significant finding of our study is the quantification of histological heterogeneity, a factor often overlooked in traditional diagnostic accuracy studies. The infection-induced inflammatory response is not diffusely or uniformly distributed within the joint capsule; rather, it manifests as a “patchy” or multifocal process [19]. Our data reveals that among confirmed PJI cases, only 44% (37/84) exhibited consistent positive findings across all three sampled tissues. This suggests that if a surgeon were to rely on a single biopsy from a non-inflamed area, the diagnosis would likely be missed.

For this reason, multi-site sampling, such as pseudocapsule, interface membrane, and neosynovial tissue were recommended according to previous studies [19, 20]. Similarly, Sigmund et al. [19] demonstrated that obtaining 3–6 tissue specimens is essential to achieve the desired diagnostic yield. Our results provide data validation for these recommendations. We specifically analyzed the optimal definition of a “positive case” when three tissue samples are available. While the overall diagnostic accuracy (AUC) remained consistent across different thresholds, requiring only a single positive specimen provides the most balanced trade-off. This approach optimizes sensitivity to ensure cases are not overlooked, without a prohibitive decrease in specificity, making it a more pragmatic choice for clinical screening.

Furthermore, we observed moderate inter-observer agreement (Kappa = 0.581) for neutrophil counting, indicating that manual assessment is susceptible to subjective interpretation. This variability is consistent with established limitations in histopathological diagnosis. Krenn et al. [21] have noted that traditional histological criteria often suffer from limited reproducibility, necessitating standardized consensus classifications to improve consistency. Moreover, this issue is not unique to PJI; Elmore et al. [22] reported that significant diagnostic disagreement is a persistent challenge among pathologists in various clinical contexts. These findings suggest that the subjectivity inherent in manual visual assessment is a contributing factor to the diagnostic variability observed in our study. The coexistence of extensive wear debris can hinder the differentiation between Krenn Type I (wear-induced) and Type II (infectious) patterns, as dark metal deposits or birefringent polyethylene particles may be misidentified as polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNs) by the pathologist [21].

This study is subject to limitations inherent to its retrospective design. First, while our strict reliance on the ICM major criteria—specifically excluding histopathology—may have excluded some ‘gray-zone’ cases, this approach was necessary to avoid incorporation bias and ensure that the diagnostic utility of histology was not artificially overestimated. Second, the histopathological findings in this study were obtained intraoperatively. While some studies now support preoperative biopsy diagnosis [23], whether the results of this study can be applied to preoperative biopsy requires further validation. Third, as this study focused exclusively on hip and knee arthroplasties, the generalizability of our conclusions to shoulder or elbow PJIs remains to be established. Last, the sample size, while sufficient for our primary objectives, remains relatively modest, further multicenter studies are warranted.

Conclusion

Intraoperative frozen section analysis provides reliable diagnostic accuracy for PJI, with consistent performance across hip and knee revisions. However, the significant heterogeneity existed in both surgical sampling and pathological interpretation. Standardization from the surgeon’s intraoperative sampling to the pathologist’s result interpretation stage may offer a potential solution to this problem.
