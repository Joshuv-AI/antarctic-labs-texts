Characterization of skin microbiome profile before and during radiation therapy and its correlation to the occurrence and severity of radiation dermatitis
Authors: R. Abdah-Bortnyak, H. Brand, R. Finkelshtein, G. Perry, D. Shwartz, M. Brandwein, A. Katz, M. Nuriel-Ohayon, N. Shental, M. Ben-Simon, S. Billan, S. Luder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose/objective(s)The purpose of study is to characterize the skin microbiome profile of breast cancer patients before and during radiation therapy, and evaluate the relationship between the microbiome profile and the severity of acute radiation dermatitis (aRD).Materials/methodsIn this observational, single-center, single-arm study, breast cancer patients received RT at Rambam Health Care Campus from November 2020 to July 2021. Skin assessments and skin microbiome samples were collected from all patients before, weekly during RT, after completion of the treatment. The outcome measures were: aRD grade, skin microbiome composition at baseline and during RT.Results640 skin samples were collected from 86 patients, bacterial DNA was extracted, and 16S rDNA was amplified and sequenced.At mid-treatment, the bacterial family Clostridiaceae was unique to patients later diagnosed with moderate/severe acute dermatitis, and present in 30% of the samples (p-value = 0.002038). An unknown species of Anaerococcus, designated Anaerococcus US436, was unique to patients in the moderate/severe aRD group at mid-treatment (21.67% in this group, p-value = 0.002038).To increase sample size and reduce noise, we collected all treated samples from all time points and compared the microbiome composition between the groups of dermatitis severity. The Clostridiaceae family and Clostridium genus remained significantly enriched in the moderate/severe group (p-value = 0.002095 and 0.004269, respectively), as well as the Anaerococcus genus and its unknown species (p-value = 0.001825 and 0.007104, respectively).The treated samples for each patient were summed up, and each bacterium was examined for at least one appearance per patient during the treatment. Granulicatella elegans was enriched in the moderate/severe group (p-value = 0.000512), and Bacillus thuringiensis was enriched in the mild group (p-value = 0.000595).ConclusionA bacterial composition associated with moderate/severe aRD was identified: Clostridium, Anaerococcus US436, and Granulicatella elegans.

Introduction

Acute radiation dermatitis (aRD) is a common adverse effect of radiation therapy (RT), that ranges from mild rash to severe ulceration [1], [2], arising from the inherent risk of injury of healthy tissue proximal to the RT treated zones. Repeated RT exposure inevitably impacts rapidly proliferating cells in the outer skin layer, which results in epidermal peeling and shedding. At the same time, inflammatory and vascular responses are triggered on injury of capillary cells or capillary dilation [3], [4]. Increased melanin production and its migration to more superficial layers of the epidermis, underlie pigmentary changes common to the condition [4]. With increasing impairment of epidermal barrier function, subdermal lymphatics and hair follicles are directly damaged, which can lead to moist desquamation, skin necrosis and/or epilation. Furthermore, increasing skin permeability leaves the area susceptible to infection, which can further aggravate the reaction by upregulating inflammation and skin damage. As many as 95% of patients undergoing breast RT may experience some form of aRD, part of them may suffer from significant radiation-induced skin injury. While a reduced total dose of radiation and use of an advanced mode of radiation delivery may help mitigate the severity of radiation effects on the skin, aRD remains one of the most common side effects of RT [1], [5], [22]. Apart from delivered radiation, dose and fractionation schedule, other factors, such as proximity of skin to the treated area, the size of the area exposed to radiation and the concurrent use of radiosensitizing agents can affect the likelihood of aRD. For this reason, aRD is particularly common among patients with cancers of the breast, skin, head and neck, vulva, anus, sarcoma, as the treated areas are close to the skin and with limited options for the skin sparing [6], [7], [8], [9]. Patient-specific factors, such as malnutrition, smoking, obesity, vascular or connective tissue comorbidities, and certain genetic alterations can predispose patients to increased risk and severity of aRD [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]. Skin reactions related to aRT usually manifest within 1–3 weeks of radiation, persist for the duration of RT, and may require several weeks to improve after completion of therapy [2]. Signs and symptoms of aRD, which are generally scored using the RT Oncology Group (RTOG) or the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI CTCAE) standardized grading systems [17], [18], include red rash (erythema), itching, flaking or peeling skin (dry desquamation), blisters and wet peeling skin (moist desquamation), pigmentation changes, development of ulcers, pain, discomfort, burning, and general irritation. The above symptoms of aRD have a profound impact on the quality of patient's life, due to pain, discomfort and diminished aesthetic appearance. In some patients, these effects may restrict movement of an affected limb, impeding activities of daily living, and lead to loss of independence and self-care ability [2]. In addition, it may be the cause of premature interruption of RT, resulting in inadequate treatment of malignant disease [2], [10], [19].

No standardized approach currently exists for prevention and treatment of radiation-induced cutaneous toxicities, largely due to the scarcity of rigorous evidence and confirmatory studies [20].

Supporting skin care during breast radiotherapy includes good hygiene by washing the skin daily with soap and water; gently pat dry; avoid rubbing, shaving, using heat and cooling pads; avoid sun exposure; use a moisturizer; use an appropriate dressing/product to reduce further trauma and infection [21].

The systematic review, published by Multinational Association of Supportive Care in Cancer (MASCC) showed that photobiomodulation therapy, Mepitel® film, mometasone furoate, betamethasone, olive oil, and oral enzyme mixtures demonstrated promising results across multiple RCTs mainly as aRD prevention methods [22].

Recently published ESTRO practical recommendations conclude that standard of care for aRD remains a key of challenge and calls for continued research on this topic [23].

Since currently, there is no established standard of care for aRD, it is crucial to identify patients prone to developing severe reactions, enabling personalized treatment adjustments to prevent such reactions.

The human microbiome is dynamic, and changes associated with health and various disease conditions have been described and mechanistically investigated. The microbiome of normal human skin was found to have a high diversity and high interpersonal variation [24], [25].

The changes in the composition of skin microbiome and the correlation with numerous disease states, raising the possibility that the microbiome profile could be used as a biomarker and be translated into prognostic, diagnostic, therapeutic and preventive measures in the context of personalized and precision medicine. The profile of skin microbiome can potentially serve as a predictive indicator of the patient's suitability for treatment. Those identified as needing intervention can undergo a pre−/co treatment aimed at modifying the microbiome. This modification aims to optimize the composition of the microbiome, ensuring that patients are primed for the most effective treatment. Being a tractable entity, the microbiome is a prime target for manipulation to influence health and disease processes. In most cases, but especially on the skin, the microbiota is an easily accessible target for therapeutic intervention and diagnostic testing [24].

The purpose of this investigation is to characterize the skin microbiome profile of breast cancer patients before and during RT and evaluate the relationship between the microbiome profile and occurrence and severity of aRD. Such characterizations can lead to potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets that can be used for the prognosis, prevention, and treatment of this condition. Currently limited data exists regarding the effect of the skin microbiome profile on the severity of aRD. A prospective, longitudinal study aims to analyze the association of skin microbiome with aRD showed that breast cancer patients who developed mild aRD showed significantly higher relative abundance of particular taxa such as Ralstonia, Truepera, Methyloversatilis genera and lower abundance of particular taxa including Staphylococcus, Corynebacterium genera in comparison to patients who developed severe RD [26]. Another study showed that low colonization of skin with Staphylococcus epidermidis, Staphylococcus hominis, Cutibacterium acnes before radiotherapy was associated with the development of severe aRD in breast cancer patients [27].

Identifying biomarkers and skin microbiome profiles associated with aRD occurrence and the expected severity of the disease may be used to guide management and treatment and help to decide what will be the optimal treatment approach [28]. For example, identifying the patients with high risk to develop higher grades of aRD can also help to start treatment for dermatitis as a prevention step. The skin microbiome profile may also help to predict whether the patient is in an optimal state for RT treatment, and patients in need may receive a microbiome-modifying pre−/co-treatment to edit the microbiome so that its constituents can best prepare the patient for optimal treatment, for example by using topical products that could be designed to specifically shape microbial communities.

Methods

Patients, treatment

This study is a single-center, observational, prospective study, included women ≥18 years old with histologically confirmed unilateral breast malignancy, underwent breast conserving therapy, and scheduled to receive adjuvant radiation therapy at Rambam Health Care Campus.

Screening of the consented patients performed after CT-based planning before the RT. Eligible patients with breast cancer received three dimensional conformal RT (3D-CRT) over 18 daily fractions of 2.5Gy (5 fractions per week) to a total dose of 45 Gy to the whole breast with additional 4–5 fractions of 2.5Gy to the tumor site (boost) if needed, resulting in a total dose of 55–57.5 Gy. Skin assessments conducted by the treating physician and nurse using the RT Oncology Group (RTOG) scoring system at baseline, weekly assessments during the RT, end-of-therapy check (EOT) and on follow-up visit 2 weeks after EOT.

For the purpose of the analysis, each patient was assigned the maximum score of aRD severity measured during her treatment. Patients were divided into two severity groups to increase the power of analysis: mild dermatitis (Grade 0–1) and moderate/severe dermatitis (Grade 2–4).

Microbiome composition, samples collection and storage

Skin microbiome samples collected from all patients at several points: before RT, during RT, at the end of treatment and at follow-up visits.

The collection of skin culture samples is a non-invasive procedure. Following receiving written informed consent, a sterile swab that has been dipped in a saline-mild detergent solution (0.15 NaCl, 0.1% Tween20), gently rubbed on a 2*2 area of the skin on the 3 different areas. The target areas were skin regions within the RT field, control site of the healthy breast, a control area on the forehead. Microbiome samples coded, anonymized, stored in a − 20C freezer, and have been transferred to laboratory.

DNA extraction and microbiome analysis

The microbiology samples have been analyzed at Hylabs (Israel). Samples were defrosted and bacterial DNA was extracted. Human DNA was not analyzed. The bacterial DNA 16S marker gene amplified and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq instrument. Sequence reads processed with CLC or QIIME and statistical analysis carried out with R. Following DNA extraction, the samples have been destroyed.

Statistical evaluation and analysis

The results tabulated and analyzed using appropriate statistical techniques to investigate the association between the microbiome profile (diversity and community composition) and the degree of aRD (RTOG grading scale) in breast cancer patients. To describe the data, descriptive statistics, mean, and standard error were used. Data analysis performed using SPSS (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). For statistical analysis, we considered the presence or absence of each bacterium as a dichotomous variable across patients or samples divided into clinical groups (according to the severity of dermatitis, time points compared, or breast compared). For each variable, bacteria were included in the following analysis only if their presence in at least one clinical group reached at least 10% comprising at least three patients or samples. To detect species differentiating between clinical groups, either Fisher's exact test or Chi-squared test was applied when appropriate according to contingency tables, and false discovery rates (FDR) were calculated according to the Benjamini-Hochberg correction method (bar for significance was set to FDR < 0.25). Given the inherent sparsity of microbiome datasets and the exploratory focus of this analysis, we adopted an FDR threshold of 0.25 to minimize Type II errors. By prioritizing discovery-phase sensitivity, this threshold ensures that potentially relevant biological associations are preserved for further investigation rather than being prematurely excluded by overly conservative corrections.

When matched samples from the same individual were compared (i.e., treated vs. contralateral breast, two samples from different time points), McNemar's test was applied instead (followed by FDR calculation). Either way, this procedure was carried out for each taxonomic level from species to phylum. Specific bacteria showing significant enrichments were also examined for their relative abundance in patients during radiation treatment. For bacterial signatures, relative risks (RR), odds ratios (OR), and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated.

Alpha diversity measures were calculated using the R package ‘phyloseq’. Shannon diversity indices were compared using either the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (to compare treated vs. contralateral breast samples), or the Friedman test for repeated measures (for changes in diversity during radiation treatment).

To note, in all the following tables, “%” in the header represents the decimal fraction and not the actual percentage.

Results

Overview of the patients and samples

Eighty-six patients treated from November 2020 to July 2021 included to the study.

Thirty-six patients received radiation therapy to unilateral breast for total dose of 45 Gy in 18 fractions and 50 patients were subjected to an additional radiation dose to the lumpectomy bed (boost).

Mild aRD (G 0–1) developed 25 (29%) patients, and 61 (71%) patients suffered from moderate (G2)/severe (G3) acute dermatitis, including 49 patients with G2 and 12 patients with G3, were no cases of G4 aRD.

Six hundred forty (640) skin samples were collected from 86 breast cancer patients before and during the course of RT, bacterial DNA was extracted, and 16S rDNA was amplified and sequenced. Thirty-two of the samples were taken from the foreheads of the patients at baseline before the commencement of radiation (for control), and all other samples were collected from the treated and contralateral breasts.

15 technical control samples were sequenced for bacterial DNA. As most species in control samples were also prevalent in the case samples, and many of them are known human skin commensals, we assumed that case samples probably contaminated the controls and not vice versa, and decided to keep all species in the following analyses.

Six misidentified samples and two samples that yielded less than 1000 reads were excluded from the analysis. Samples were analyzed from the following time points: baseline before treatment commenced (visit 2), mid-treatment (visit 4 or 4b), end of treatment (visit 5).

When we examined the 48 samples positive for Clostridium from the moderate/severe group, we noticed that they belonged to 33 patients, while the 7 positive samples from the mild group belonged to 7 different patients. This finding might insinuate that the presence of this bacterium might be an isolated event in the mild group, compared to a repetitive phenomenon potentially harboring a biological meaning in the moderate/severe group. To follow this lead, we summed up all the treated samples for each patient, and examined each bacterium, for at least one appearance per patient during the treatment (Fig. 7). The Clostridiaceae family and Clostridium genus did not pass our bar for FDR anymore (p-value = 0.01936 and 0.02757, respectively). However, in light of their significance in our previous findings, we hypothesized that the presence of Clostridium or Anaerococcus US436 at least once during the treatment might bear clinical significance. Hence, we united the Clostridium genus with the Anaerococcus US436 and examined which patients were positive for at least one of them at least once during treatment. The rate in the moderate/severe group was significantly higher compared to the mild group (67.2% vs. 36%, p-value = 0.007715), and this union was significantly associated with moderate/severe aRD (OR, 3.644; 95% CI, 1.373–9.672; p = 0.0094) and with a higher risk for moderate/severe aRD (RR, 1.476; 95% CI, 1.072–2.032, p = 0.0170).

A further examination of the presence of either of these bacteria showed that in the mild group, 9 patients were positive for either Clostridium or Anaerococcus US436, with one being positive for both. In 6 of these patients, these bacteria only appeared in the last treatment (visit 5). Conversely, in the moderate/severe group, only in 4 of the 41 patients who were positive for either Clostridium or Anaerococcus US436, these bacteria first appeared on visit 5. In fact, 17 of these patients were positive for at least one of these bacteria before treatment. In the moderate/severe group, out of 20 patients who were negative for both bacteria, 8 were diagnosed with moderate/severe aRD only on visit 6 (follow-up visit), from which skin microbiome samples were not sequenced (hence microbiome composition is unknown). Out of a total of 27 patients who were first diagnosed with moderate/severe aRD on visit 6, 20 were diagnosed with a maximal score of 2. This means that up to and including visit 5 while their microbiome composition is known, their aRD was considered mild. If we exclude these samples, the union of these bacteria would be even more significantly enriched in the moderate/severe group (p-value = 0.005616).

Granulicatella elegans, which was found to be enriched in the group of patients with moderate/severe aRD when all samples were collected per patient, descends from the Firmicutes phylum similarly to Clostridium and Anaerococcus. Many patients from both dermatitis groups were positive for either Granulicatella elegans, Clostridium or Anaerococcus US436. However, all 7 patients who were positive for all three, belonged to the moderate/severe group, and 50.8% of the patients in the moderate/severe group were positive for at least two out of the three, compared to only 8% of the mild group. Moreover, the presence of at least two bacteria out of these three was significantly associated with moderate/severe aRD (OR, 11.88; 95% CI, 2.57–54.86; p-value = 0.0015) and with a significantly increased risk for moderate/severe aRD (RR, 1.66; 95% CI, 1.29–2.13, p-value = 0.0001). A closer examination of the presence of these bacteria in patients over time, showed that 10 out of 25 patients (40%) in the mild group were negative for all three, and 9 out of the 15 remaining patients, were positive for only one out of the three, in one visit. Nevertheless, only 7 out of the 61 patients (11.5%) in the moderate/severe group were negative for all three bacteria; All 7 were assigned with a dermatitis severity score of 2, and 2 of them were only diagnosed with moderate/severe aRD on visit 6, from which skin microbiome samples were not sequenced. Only in 3 patients with moderate/severe aRD, the first appearance of these bacteria was noted in visit 5 (one of them missing the sample from visit 04), and 29 patients from this group were already positive for at least one before treatment, on visit 2.

One other bacterial species showed a significant enrichment when all samples were collected per patient: Bacillus thuringiensis, enriched in the group of patients with mild aRD. Only 2 patients from the moderate/severe group were positive for this bacterium; one of them was also positive for all three bacteria associated with moderate/severe aRD (Granulicatella elegans, Clostridium and Anaerococcus US436) while the other patient was positive for one of the three. In the mild group, 8 patients were positive for Bacillus thuringiensis: 3 of them were negative for all three bacteria associated with moderate/severe aRD, 4 were positive for one of the three, and one patient was positive for two. Thus, we hypothesized that if the presence of Granulicatella elegans, Clostridium and Anaerococcus US436 can contribute to moderate/severe aRD, Bacillus thuringiensis might interrupt their postulated activity, especially when only one or two of them are present (to note, all four bacteria descend from the Firmicutes phylum).

To statistically examine the validity of this hypothesis, we looked for patients who were both positive for at least two of the bacteria associated with moderate/severe aRD, and negative for Bacillus thuringiensis. We found only one patient with mild aRD (4%) answering this criterion, compared to 31 patients with moderate/severe aRD, 50.8% of this group. Statistically, this signature was highly significantly associated with moderate/severe aRD (OR, 24.8; 95% CI, 3.15–195.05; p-value = 0.0015) and with an increased risk for moderate/severe aRD (RR, 1.744; 95% CI, 1.363–2.231, p-value <0.0001).

An alternative signature might be the presence of at least one of the bacteria associated with moderate/severe aRD, along with the absence of Bacillus thuringiensis. It appeared in more patients with mild aRD (40%) compared to the previous signature, but possibly explained more cases with moderate/severe aRD (88.5%). Unsurprisingly, this composition was more robustly associated with moderate/severe aRD (OR, 11.57; 95% CI, 3.77–35.56; p-value <0.0001) but slightly less with increased risk for moderate/severe aRD (RR, 2.652; 95% CI, 1.426–4.933, p-value = 0.0021).

Discussion

This was a single center, observational study aimed to explore the correlation between the skin microbiome profile of breast cancer patients before and during RT to the occurrence and severity of aRD.

Bacterial associations with dermatitis severity

Our study identified specific bacterial associations with the severity of aRD in patients undergoing treatment. At baseline, the presence of the Flavobacterales order and the Flavobacteriia class was significantly associated with later diagnoses of moderate/severe aRD, distinguishing patients with moderate/severe form from those with mild aRD (although more than 50% of the patients who later diagnosed with mild aRD also share this bacteria). Conversely, the presence of the Thermomicrobia class and its parent phylum, Chloroflexi, was unique to patients later diagnosed with mild aRD. Mid-treatment revealed a distinct association between the Clostridiaceae family, particularly the Clostridium genus, and the development of moderate/severe aRD. This association persisted even on the additional visit (visit 4b) for patients who received a radiation boost. However, caution is warranted in interpreting findings of visit 4b due to smaller sample sizes. Notably, at the last treatment visit (visit 5), Roseomonas genus enrichment was significantly associated with moderate/severe aRD in patients who did not receive a radiation boost, underlining the dynamic nature of bacterial associations throughout the treatment course. While these findings imply distinct bacterial associations at each visit individually, no discernible trend in bacterial associations was observed over the course of the RT treatment. These findings offer valuable insights into potential microbial markers for dermatitis severity, though further research is warranted to validate and extend these observations.

Bacteria significantly enriched at specific time points

We managed to identify notable bacterial shifts at specific time points, particularly in the relative abundance of the Clostridium genus and Anaerococcus sp. genus. Both patient groups, regardless of dermatitis severity, experienced a general reduction in the relative abundance of the Clostridium genus during radiation treatment. Notably, among patients who received a radiation boost, those in the moderate/severe aRD group exhibited a higher relative abundance of Clostridium, which may indicate a distinctive response in this subgroup. Furthermore, the appearance of Anaerococcus sp., specifically the unknown species Anaerococcus US436, was unique to the moderate/severe aRD group at mid-treatment, showcasing its potential relevance in dermatitis severity. Although this species did not surpass our threshold for false discovery rate (FDR), its consistent presence in the moderate/severe group, particularly in cases with a radiation boost, suggests a potential association with moderate/severe aRD.

A bacterial signature associated with dermatitis severity regardless of treatment time point

We further aimed to uncover bacterial markers associated with dermatitis severity, irrespective of the treatment time point. Notably, the Clostridiaceae family and Clostridium genus maintained significant enrichment in the moderate/severe aRD group, as did the Anaerococcus genus and its unknown species. Delving deeper into the Clostridium genus, we observed a higher relative abundance in the moderate/severe group, particularly among patients who received a radiation boost. Further investigation revealed intriguing patterns; patients in the moderate/severe group exhibited repetitive occurrences of Clostridium, suggesting potential biological significance compared to isolated events in the mild group.

To strengthen our findings, we conducted an in-depth patient-level analysis, focusing on the presence of specific bacteria during treatment. Granulicatella elegans, from the Firmicutes phylum akin to Clostridium and Anaerococcus, exhibited significant enrichment in the severe group. The combined presence of Clostridium or Anaerococcus US436 at least once during treatment emerged as a potential clinical marker of moderate/severe aRD, further supported by statistical associations and increased risk.

Examining patients over time revealed distinctive patterns. The presence of Granulicatella elegans, Clostridium, and Anaerococcus US436 collectively heightened the risk of moderate/severe aRD. Intriguingly, Bacillus thuringiensis, enriched in the mild group, seemed to interrupt this association when present in conjunction with the mentioned bacteria. Statistically significant findings, such as the absence of Bacillus thuringiensis along with specific bacteria associated with moderate/severe aRD, emerged as potential markers of dermatitis severity.

It is crucial to note that this composition was not identified as predictable. We strongly advocate for the validation of these results in an alternative patient cohort, coupled with a more in-depth exploration of the included bacteria to unveil their biological functions.

The skin microbiome of the treated breast is different from the skin microbiome of the contralateral breast

Skin samples were additionally collected from the contralateral breast of each patient. Remarkably, distinct microbiome compositions were evident in the skin of the matched breasts even before radiation treatment, featuring the presence of Ralstonia insidiosa and Caulobacter US7 in the contralateral breasts while being absent in the treated breasts. This distinctive microbiome pattern persisted at the mid-treatment stage, although Ralstonia insidiosa also appeared in the treated breast of one patient, and Caulobacter US7 showed reduced prevalence. Subsequent visits revealed diminishing differences between the breasts, attributable to decreased sample sizes resulting from patient grouping based on the administration or absence of an additional radiation boost. These findings underscore the significant and persistent differences in the skin microbiome compositions of both breasts, manifesting even before radiation, and further evolving during the course of treatment.

Modifications in the skin microbiome during radiation treatment

Modifications in the skin microbiome were evident during radiation treatment, as observed by comparing the pre-treatment microbiome composition at visit 2 to that during treatment at visit 4. Significant changes were noted, with distinct taxa becoming prevalent in the patient population during treatment, particularly in the group with moderate/severe aRD. Notably, the contralateral breast also exhibited significant modifications during treatment, suggesting influences beyond radiation, such as systemic treatment. The treated breast demonstrated more prominent microbiome composition changes between visits 4 and 4b, especially for patients receiving an additional boost dose. Similarly, substantial differences were observed between visits 4 and 5, the last radiation treatment, in the treated breast compared to the contralateral breast. Overall, our observations highlight substantial alterations in the skin microbiome over the course of treatment. However, given the significant changes observed in both breasts and the non-uniformity of additional treatments, such as systemic and topical treatment, the specific impact of radiation treatment on these changes remains unclear.

Alpha diversity and bacterial richness

In the course of radiation treatment, bacterial diversity generally remained comparable between the treated breast and the matched contralateral breast, except for samples obtained at visit 4, where the healthy breast exhibited greater microbiome diversity. In contrast to our findings, Ramadan and colleagues [27] observed a substantial decrease in bacterial diversity (Shannon, Chao1, and observed species) in cancer patients experiencing RT-induced dermatitis compared to their healthy controls. The difference in outcomes could be attributed to the different control groups employed in the studies. In our research, the contralateral breast of breast cancer patients served as the control group, whereas Ramadan and colleagues utilized healthy subjects as their control group. Moreover, the similar bacterial diversity observed in our study between the treated and contralateral breasts might be due to the potential exposure of the healthy breast to a certain radiation dose. Another factor to consider is the potential impact of systemic therapy, which may have contributed to a decrease in the bacterial diversity of the contralateral breast.

Regarding the treated breasts, we did not find any variations in bacterial diversity observed over time. The differences in bacterial diversity between matched breast samples and during treatment were mostly non-significant. The disparity observed between breasts at mid-treatment is likely attributed to a temporary increase in diversity in the contralateral breast. The clinical significance of this modification remains uncertain.

This study had a limitation in that we designated the contralateral breast as the control, acknowledging the possibility of radiation affecting both the treated and healthy breasts, and consequently influencing the microbiome in the contralateral breast. Despite recognizing this limitation, we reasoned that the contralateral breast would serve as a more suitable control group compared to obtaining skin samples from healthy breasts of healthy women. Our rationale was to minimize the impact of natural microbiome variations between individuals. Therefore, we regard this limitation as minor.

The study did not adjust the clinical and other treatment related confounding factors, and thus the multivariable analysis for the influence of these parameters on the microbiome was not performed; this can represent another study limitation.

Conclusions

The human skin harbors millions of bacteria, fungi, archaea, and viruses, collectively playing a pivotal role in human immunological and physiological functions and influencing the development of skin diseases. Recent theories underscore the significance of dysbiosis in the skin microbiome in cancer treatment-related skin toxicities, particularly due to interconnections between inflammation processes and skin microbes.

Existing literature reveals alterations in the diversity and abundance of the skin microbiome during cancer treatments, including RT. These changes encompass reduced diversity, an elevated Proteobacteria/Firmicutes ratio, and an increased presence of pathogenic Staphylococcus aureus [28].

The scientific literature presents challenges in obtaining consistent data, possibly attributed to the inherent diversity in the skin microbiome across individuals. Additionally, cancer patients undergo multiple treatments, such as chemotherapy, hormonotherapy and biologic treatment, alongside RT, potentially influencing the microbiome profile. Therefore, isolating the sole impact of RT on the skin microbiome of cancer patients proves challenging due to these multifaceted factors.

In current study, we identified a bacterial composition associated with moderate/severe aRD. However, the literature evidence on the impact of RT on the skin microbiome is limited and further exploration and confirmation of the findings is necessary.

There is the raising evidence about effectiveness of bacterial decolonization (BD) and the role of the microbiome in colonization resistance. The randomized clinical trial suggests that BD is effective for RD prophylaxis [29].

Our findings are important for potential interventions in reducing skin reactions by preserving the epidermal barrier and stabilizing microbial balance to prevent and manage aRD.
