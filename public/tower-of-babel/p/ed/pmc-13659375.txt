Introduction

Spinal dysraphisms comprise a heterogeneous group of congenital malformations resulting from incomplete closure of the neural tube during embryonic development and are currently classified into open and closed forms according to the Orphanet classification system [1]. Open spinal dysraphysm, particularly myelomeningocele and related subtypes, is the most common form and is associated with lifelong motor and neurological disabilities [2]. Despite major advances in prenatal diagnosis, imaging characterisation and foetal surgery, accurately predicting functional outcomes remains a significant clinical challenge [3, 4, 5]. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of prenatal imaging features, including lesion morphology and associated cranial findings, in refining prognostic assessment and candidate selection for foetal surgery [6, 7, 8].

The severity of motor impairment in open spinal dysraphism is influenced by multiple mechanisms. First, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage through the spinal defect contributes to the development of Chiari II malformation, which includes hindbrain herniation and is often associated with hydrocephalus. Second, the ‘second‐hit’ hypothesis describes progressive injury to exposed neural tissue from the amniotic environment [2, 9]. More recently, a ‘third‐hit’ mechanism has been proposed, where traction on the placode and nerve roots, particularly in myelomeningocele, may further impair motor function [8, 10, 11, 12]. These insights underscore the importance of assessing both structural and functional aspects of the lesion during prenatal evaluation.

While the anatomical level of the spinal defect is routinely assessed by ultrasound, it does not always correlate with motor function [13]. Functional assessment through foetal limb movement or indirect signs such as talipes may provide better prognostic information, but is not systematically studied or applied in clinical practice [5]. Associated cranial findings, including ventriculomegaly and Chiari II‐related abnormalities, are also increasingly recognised as important markers of disease severity and neurological outcome [6, 7].

The main objective of this study was to evaluate the discrepancy between anatomical and motor levels during prenatal ultrasound assessment in foetuses with open spinal dysraphism. First, we aimed to quantify this difference and determine how frequently the motor level is better, equal to or worse than the anatomical level at the time of ultrasound evaluation. Second, we examined prenatal factors associated with greater or lesser discrepancies, adjusting for anatomical level given that lower defects inherently limit the potential for large motor‐anatomical gaps. Third, we explored the association between anatomical level and other ultrasound findings.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This observational retrospective cross‐sectional single‐centre study was carried out at Hospital Universitari Vall d'Hebron, Barcelona, and included a review of ultrasound and clinical data collected between January 2011 and December 2022. The study focused on the first prenatal ultrasound assessment performed in the Foetal Medicine Unit for foetuses diagnosed with open spinal dysraphism. The local Ethics Committee, Comité de Ética de Investigación con Medicamentos y comisión de proyectos de investigación del Hospital Universitari Vall d'Hebron (CEIm‐VHIR), approved the study (PR(AMI)541/2022) on December 16th 2022. Due to its retrospective design, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee.

Participants

The study population consisted of foetuses diagnosed with open spinal dysraphism. Inclusion criteria required a gestational age of at least 15 + 0 weeks. Exclusion criteria included the presence of pathogenic or likely pathogenic chromosomal, other severe structural malformations, closed spinal dysraphism, lesions without hindbrain herniation, cases in which the specific defect type had not been documented, or cases where either the anatomical or motor level was not documented.

To identify cases meeting the inclusion criteria, we conducted a search in Viewpoint using the terms ‘spina bifida’, ‘neural tube defect’, ‘myelomeningocele’, ‘myeloschisis’, and ‘myelocele’.

Clinical Protocol

Following the prenatal diagnosis of a spinal dysraphism, pregnant women were referred to the Foetal Medicine Unit for comprehensive evaluation. A detailed ultrasound scan was performed to assess foetal anatomy, biometric parameters, placental location and cervical length. Prenatal classification of spinal dysraphism was based on ultrasound findings, including both direct and indirect signs. Open spinal dysraphism was suspected in the presence of cranial findings associated with Chiari II malformation, including abnormal posterior convexity of the cerebellum with obliteration of the cisterna magna (‘banana sign’), and flattening of the frontal bones (‘lemon sign’), as well as spinal findings suggestive of an exposed neural placode [6, 14, 15].

Open defects were further categorised as either myelomeningocele (characterised by a cystic lesion) or myeloschisis (identified by a flat lesion). The anatomical level was defined as the highest non‐closed posterior vertebral arch visible on ultrasound. This assessment was performed in axial views, using the twelfth thoracic vertebra (T12), identified by the last rib, as a reference point.

The motor level was assigned based on the most caudal active muscle group identified through dynamic ultrasound assessment, using the following standardised sequence: L1 (psoas muscle—hip flexion), L2 (adductors—hip adduction), L3 (quadriceps—knee extension), L4 (hamstrings/gluteus—knee flexion), L5 (anterior tibialis—ankle dorsiflexion) and S1 (gastrocnemius, soleus, gluteus—ankle plantarflexion, hip extension and external rotation) [13]. Our group previously published a study demonstrating the reproducibility of this technique for prenatal motor level assessment.

All ultrasound examinations were conducted by physicians with specific training in the prenatal assessment of spinal defect levels, ensuring consistent and accurate evaluation of both anatomical and motor function [16].

In all cases of spinal dysraphism, invasive genetic testing was recommended as part of the standard prenatal diagnostic evaluation.

Variables

The primary outcome variables were the anatomical and motor levels of the spinal defect, as assessed by prenatal ultrasound. The discrepancy between these two levels was calculated by subtracting the anatomical level from the motor level. A positive value indicated that the motor level was better (more caudal) than the anatomical level, while a negative value indicated a worse (more cranial) motor level relative to the anatomical level.

Additional ultrasound variables related to the spinal dysraphism included the type of defect, the width of the posterior horns of the lateral ventricles and associated structural anomalies such as kyphosis, scoliosis, hemivertebra and talipes equinovarus (clubfoot).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the study population. Continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), while categorical variables were presented as absolute numbers and percentages. To enable quantitative comparison, consecutive numerical scores were assigned to each vertebral level, with C1 = 1, C2 = 2, C3 = 3 and so on.

Linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association between the motor‐anatomical discrepancy and potential influencing factors, including type of defect, gestational age at diagnosis, ventricular width and associated anomalies (e.g., scoliosis, talipes).

Because the anatomical level inherently limits the possible magnitude of discrepancy with motor level, particularly in more caudal lesions, anatomical level was included as an adjustment variable in the multivariable regression analysis to control for this constraint.

To analyse the association between anatomical level and other ultrasound findings, we performed univariate linear regression analyses with anatomical level as the dependent variable and each ultrasound variable as the independent predictor.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and a two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants

A total of 247 foetuses with spinal dysraphism were assessed during the study period. Of these, 60 cases were excluded for the following reasons: closed or intermediate defect (n = 20), gestational age < 15 weeks (n = 11), missing assessment of motor level (n = 14), presence of pathogenic chromosomal abnormalities (n = 9), additional major structural anomalies (n = 4), type of defect not described (n = 1). Therefore, 187 cases were included in the final analysis.

Invasive genetic testing was performed in 150 cases. Chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) was carried out in 119 cases, while karyotyping was performed in 38 cases; 31 underwent karyotyping alone and 7 had both karyotype and CMA. Karyotyping identified one balanced translocation and one balanced inversion. CMA additionally detected a maternally inherited 9p13.2 duplication in the same case with the balanced translocation, as well as two pathogenic copy number variants (CNVs) in pregnancies that were continued: one involving a deletion in the 15q25 region and another involving a deletion in the 1q21.1–q21.2 region.

Demographic Characteristics

The median maternal age was 32.8 years (IQR, 28.7–36.6), and the median body mass index (BMI) was 23.8 kg/m2 (IQR, 21.6–27.5). Of the included pregnancies, 95 (50.8%) were multiparous. Information on mode of conception was available for 163 cases, of which 20 (12.3%) were conceived via assisted reproductive techniques (ART). Smoking status was available for 169 women, and 17 (10.1%) were reported smokers.

Ultrasound Assessment

The median gestational age at assessment was 21.4 weeks (IQR, 20.3–22.4). In this cross‐sectional study, the anatomical and motor levels corresponded to the first ultrasound assessment performed in the Foetal Medicine Unit at our centre, during the same examination. Longitudinal changes in the discrepancy between anatomical and motor levels during gestation or in the postnatal period were not assessed.

Five foetuses (2.7%) had associated anomalies: two with agenesis of the ductus venosus, one with unilateral renal agenesis, one with horseshoe kidney and one with a persistent left superior vena cava.

Among the 187 included cases, 128 (68.4%) were diagnosed with myelomeningocele, and 59 (31.6%) with myeloschisis.

The anatomical level of the lesion ranged from C7 to S3, with L4 being the most frequently affected level (Figure 1). The motor level ranged from L1 to S1, and the most common motor level was S1 (Figure 1).

In terms of brain findings, the median width of the posterior horn of the lateral ventricles was 9.6 mm (IQR, 7.7–12.0). A hindbrain herniation was present in all 187 cases. Kyphosis was observed in 15 cases (8.0%), scoliosis in 5 cases (2.7%), bilateral talipes in 22 cases (11.8%) and unilateral talipes in 10 cases (5.4%).

Comparison Between Myelomeningocele and Myeloschisis

The discrepancy between anatomical and motor levels was greater in foetuses with myeloschisis than in those with myelomeningocele (median 3 levels [IQR 2–4] vs. 2 levels [IQR 1–3], p = 0.005). In contrast, foetuses with myelomeningocele showed a more impaired motor level overall (p = 0.002). No significant differences were observed between both defect types regarding anatomical level distribution (p = 0.391) or other ultrasound characteristics (Table S1).

Anatomical Versus Motor Level

The median difference between anatomical and motor levels was two vertebral segments, with the motor level being more caudal (better) than the anatomical level. The range of differences extended from −3 (motor level worse than anatomical) to +15 (motor level better).

In most cases (159/187, 85.0%), the motor level was lower (better) than the anatomical level. Specifically, in 124 cases (66.3%), the motor level was two or more vertebral segments below the anatomical level, while in 35 cases (18.7%), it was one level lower. No discrepancy was observed in 19 cases (10.2%). In nine cases (4.8%), the motor level was worse than the anatomical level: four were one level worse and five were two levels worse.

Association Between Anatomical Level and Anatomical—Motor Discrepancy

We explored whether the anatomical level of the spinal lesion influenced the magnitude of the discrepancy between anatomical and motor levels. A clear trend was observed in which higher anatomical lesions (i.e., more cranial levels) were associated with greater discrepancies, with motor levels extending more caudally. In contrast, foetuses with lower anatomical levels (e.g., sacral) showed limited differences between anatomical and motor function (Figure 2).

Compared to myelomeningocele, foetuses with myeloschisis had significantly larger discrepancies, with motor levels positioned more caudally relative to the anatomical defect (adjusted coefficient: 0.78, p < 0.001), suggesting better‐than‐expected motor function.

Other clinical variables including gestational age at ultrasound, male sex and ventricular width were not independently associated with the difference between levels.

However, talipes and kyphosis showed significant associations. Foetuses with unilateral talipes had smaller discrepancies (adjusted coefficient: −0.80, p = 0.032), while those with bilateral talipes showed an even smaller discrepancy between anatomical and motor levels (−1.64, p < 0.001), indicating that the motor level was closer to the anatomical level and suggesting more severe motor impairment. Kyphosis was also significantly associated with a smaller motor–anatomical gap (adjusted coefficient: −1.45, p = 0.001). No significant association was found for scoliosis or hemivertebra after adjustment.

Ultrasound Characteristics According to Anatomical Level

Higher anatomical levels were more likely to present with ventriculomegaly (p < 0.001), kyphosis (p < 0.001), scoliosis (p < 0.001) and hemivertebra (p < 0.001). We did not find an association with talipes (p = 0.539), foetal sex (p = 0.122) or gestational age at diagnosis (p = 0.373) (Table S2).

Discussion

Main Findings

In this large retrospective cohort of 187 foetuses with prenatally diagnosed open spinal dysraphism, we observed a frequent discrepancy between the anatomical level of the lesion and the functional motor level assessed by prenatal ultrasound. In over 80% of cases, the motor level was more caudal (better) than the anatomical level, with a median difference of two vertebral segments. This reinforces the notion that anatomical imaging alone may underestimate preserved motor function in a substantial proportion of affected foetuses.

Notably, the magnitude of this discrepancy was significantly associated with the anatomical level: higher lesions were linked to greater motor‐anatomical differences, while lower lesions (e.g., sacral) showed minimal or no discrepancy.

After adjusting for anatomical level, lesion type remained a significant predictor: foetuses with myeloschisis had larger motor‐anatomical differences compared to those with myelomeningocele. The motor level in myeloschisis extended more distally, indicating better‐than‐expected in utero neuromuscular function.

Importantly, we observed that talipes deformities and kyphosis were independently associated with smaller anatomical–motor gaps, likely reflecting more advanced functional impairment. Bilateral talipes was associated with the most limited motor level relative to anatomy, highlighting its potential value as a prenatal marker of severe neuromuscular compromise.

These findings suggest that integrating detailed functional assessment into standard anatomical evaluation adds valuable prognostic information and may improve prenatal counselling and surgical planning for open spinal dysraphism.

Interpretation in Light of Other Evidence

Our findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that anatomical level does not always predict neurological function in foetuses with spinal dysraphism. However, few studies have systematically quantified this discrepancy or analysed its determinants. The large proportion of cases with better‐than‐expected motor levels supports the value of direct foetal motor assessment, especially when counselling families or selecting candidates for foetal surgery [5, 13, 14]. The greater discrepancy observed in myeloschisis compared to myelomeningocele may reflect differences in lesion morphology or the root stretching that occurs in myelomeningocele, where the nerve roots are suspended and potentially tractioned within the meningeal cyst (‘third‐hit hypothesis’) [8, 10, 11, 12]. This mechanical tension may contribute to more impaired motor function in myelomeningocele despite similar anatomical levels.

The observed inverse relationship between anatomical level and discrepancy aligns with the biomechanical limitations imposed by caudal lesions. Lower anatomical levels naturally restrict how much more distal the motor level can be, which is why we included anatomical level as an adjustment variable in the multivariable model. This approach allowed us to isolate true associations from those confounded by lesion position alone.

The association between musculoskeletal anomalies such as bilateral talipes and kyphosis with reduced anatomical–motor differences likely reflects more severe neurological compromise. These features are clinically recognisable and may offer valuable adjuncts in assessing motor prognosis in foetuses undergoing evaluation for open spina bifida repair.

The association between ventriculomegaly and motor function has been previously described. Biggio et al. found that foetuses with ventriculomegaly were less likely to be ambulatory. Furthermore, they found a significant association between the lesion level and the lateral ventricle width [17]. In our study we found that ventriculomegaly was associated with the anatomical level, being more likely with higher lesions, but after adjusting for the anatomical level, it was not correlated with the difference between the anatomical and motor level. It is reasonable to think that foetuses with a greater difference between anatomical and motor level are those with a higher anatomical level; therefore, this finding could be influenced by the higher anatomical level rather than by the fact that the motor level is more or less impaired. In a more recent study Corroenne et al. [18] did not find an association between ventriculomegaly and functional level.

Our data are consistent with previous studies that have found an association between higher anatomical levels and more brain findings [19, 20, 21]. Patients with high anatomical lesions (thoracic or upper lumbar) tend to have more frequent and more severe forms of hydrocephalus and Chiari II malformation.

Strengths and Limitations

A major strength of this study is the large, single‐centre cohort spanning over a decade, which allowed consistent data acquisition and minimised inter‐operator variability. The assessments were conducted by highly experienced sonographers using a standardised and validated protocol for foetal motor evaluation, enhancing the reliability and reproducibility of motor level assignment [16].

However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. Foetal motor assessment by ultrasound relies on visualisation of spontaneous movements in a single imaging plane, which may not fully capture all motor activity. Although strategies such as external stimulation and maternal glucose intake were used to enhance foetal activity, these are not foolproof. This introduces the possibility of underestimating motor function, particularly in foetuses that are less active during the examination. Furthermore, the evaluation is limited to motor levels down to S1, precluding accurate detection of function at more distal sacral levels (e.g., S2–S3), which may lead to under appreciation of better‐than‐expected function in some cases.

Another limitation is the inability to assess the strength of detected movements, which could significantly affect postnatal functional capacity, particularly ambulation. Finally, the retrospective design may introduce selection or information bias, although the uniform protocol and prospective data recording mitigate this risk.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that in foetuses with open spinal neural tube defects, the motor level assessed by prenatal ultrasound is frequently more caudal than the anatomical level, with a median difference of two vertebral segments. This discrepancy was observed in over 80% of cases, confirming that anatomical assessment alone may underestimate residual motor function.

The extent of the anatomical–motor discrepancy was influenced by both lesion type and lesion height. Greater differences were observed in higher anatomical lesions and in foetuses with myeloschisis compared to myelomeningocele, suggesting better‐than‐expected motor preservation in these subgroups. Conversely, musculoskeletal anomalies such as bilateral talipes and kyphosis were associated with smaller discrepancies, reflecting more severe motor compromise.

Additionally, anatomical level was significantly associated with several ultrasound findings, including ventriculomegaly and vertebral anomalies, highlighting the complexity of structural involvement in higher lesions.

These results support the use of foetal motor evaluation as an important complement to anatomical imaging in the prenatal assessment of spinal dysraphism, particularly for counselling and surgical planning. Further validation against long‐term postnatal functional outcomes would strengthen its prognostic role and clinical applicability.
