The KiwiDiab First National Paediatric Diabetes Benchmarking Audit: A Report on Children and Adolescents With Diabetes in Aotearoa New Zealand 2023
Authors: Jonathan Williman, Martin de Bock, Ben Wheeler, Vicki Cunningham, Fran Mouat, Yiing Yiing Goh, Maryam Mussa, Mark Shaw, Jeremy Armishaw, Rhian Teaz, Nicola Pereira, Oliver Grupp, Philip Moore, Pauline Giles, Esko Wiltshire, Gilli Lewis, Wendy Hunter, Caroline Griffin, Erin Southgate, Ryan Paul, Hamish Crocket, Craig Jefferies, KiwiDiab Diabetes Data Network
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo compare demographics, therapeutics and outcomes in children and adolescents with diabetes in Aotearoa, New Zealand from the KiwiDiab network.MethodsAll cases of type 1 diabetes (T1D) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) from the 13 regional centres that provide care were collated for the 2023 calendar year.ResultsThere were 1849 children and adolescents with diabetes: 1642 (88.8%) had T1D, mean age at diagnosis 9 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 6–12), mean age in clinic 12 years (IQR: 9–14); 47% were female and 69% identified as NZ European, 16% Māori and 8.3% Pacific. Overall, 23% of children and adolescents with T1D achieved an HbA1c level < 53 mmol/mol (7.0%). The mean (±standard deviation) HbA1c for any insulin pump use was 59 ± 11 mmol/mol (7.5% ± 1.0%) compared to 72 ± 19 (8.7% ± 1.8%) for those on multi‐daily injections (MDI) and 96 ± 25 (11.0% ± 2.3%) on twice‐daily (BD) insulin (p < 0.001). For the 23% of those using automated insulin delivery, mean HbA1c was similar across ethnic groups. However, HbA1c was higher for those on MDI or BD of Pacific or Māori versus European or Asian ethnicity (p < 0.001). There were 151/1849 (8.2%) children and adolescents with T2D: mean age at diagnosis 13 years (IQR: 12–14), age in clinic 14 years (IQR: 12–15); 61% were female, 38% Māori and 42% Pacific.ConclusionsThis inaugural national benchmarking report demonstrates the differences in diabetes technology usage and HbA1c levels for children and adolescents in Aotearoa, New Zealand. This highlights the need for ongoing benchmarking activities on a regular basis.Keywords: benchmarking, diabetes mellitus, network, quality improvement, registry

Introduction

Aotearoa New Zealand has an estimated incidence of type 1 diabetes (T1D) of 19 per 100 000 children and adolescents (aged < 15 years), with most receiving care at secondary and tertiary healthcare centres. International consensus statements advocate for data collection and between‐centre benchmarking to enhance outcomes for individuals with diabetes [1, 2]. Examples of international benchmarking include the Australasian Diabetes Data Network (ADDN) within Australasia and SWEET, which is centre‐specific and global in scope. However, until more clinical centres in Aotearoa New Zealand began collecting clinical data, there was no capacity for paediatric benchmarking within the country.

KiwiDiab was established in 2019 by initiating sites that were eager to link data from existing databases and begin benchmarking activities. As a result, additional sites have joined the initiative with the goal of creating a national dataset to facilitate benchmarking across the country. By 2023, over 95% of paediatric diabetes sites were linked, enabling the first national benchmarking report for children and adolescents through the KiwiDiab diabetes network, all united by a shared data dictionary, ethics framework and governance structure. This collaboration has already led to the publication of national data on the prevalence and incidence of T1D in Aotearoa [3], as well as on the use of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) devices within this population [4]. This report presents the findings from the inaugural national benchmarking activity for the KiwiDiab diabetes network.

Methods

This national descriptive study presents health data of children and adolescents with diabetes in Aotearoa New Zealand, routinely collected by local paediatric diabetes services and sourced via the KiwiDiab network (University of Otago Human Ethics Committee reference number HD18/098). Information provided included individual demographic and clinical characteristics (gender, ethnicity, diabetes type and comorbidities) and health status at the time of check‐ups with diabetes services (date of visit, age, height, weight, HbA1c, occurrence of adverse events, current insulin regime and use of CGM). Where multiple ethnicities were recorded for an individual, ethnicity was prioritised in order of Māori, Pacific, Asian or European. Those of other ethnicities (including Middle Eastern, Latin American, African, other not further defined) have been included within the European category [5].

All individuals identified with at least one visit to a paediatric diabetes service in 2023 were included in the analysis. For each eligible individual, the last recorded visit in 2023 and, if reported, all visits occurring in the preceding 12‐month period were included. Visits where the individual was aged 18 or older were excluded. Data from multiple visits per individual was summarised by taking the arithmetic mean (age, body mass index, HbA1c), or last recorded observation (insulin regimen, use of CGM). Individual‐level data was summarised (as counts and percentages, or medians and interquartile ranges [IQRs]) by groups of interest for presentation in tables and figures.

Differences in HbA1c across ethnicities were explored by fitting generalised additive models with combined insulin regimen and CGM use included as a categorical dependent variable, age at visit as a restricted cubic spline and health region as a random effect. Incidence of T1D in those aged less than 16 years was calculated using 2023 population data obtained from the StatsNZ Infoshare web tool (https://infoshare.stats.govt.nz/, table reference: DPE056AA) or by ethnicity, using data downloaded from the Populations web tool (21 October 2024, https://tewhatuora.shinyapps.io/populations‐web‐tool/) based on customised population estimates and projections provided by Stats NZ to Health New Zealand.

Children and Adolescents With T1D

The majority of children and adolescents cared for by paediatric centres had T1D (n = 1642, 89%). Within the T1D cohort, 69% were of European ethnicity, with Māori accounting for the largest proportion of other ethnicities (16%) (Table 1). Table 2 depicts insulin regimen by ethnicity, demonstrating that multi‐daily injection (MDI) and insulin pump therapy were almost exclusively used—49% for each. Technology usage was highest in those of European ethnicity with risk ratios for insulin pump therapy of 1.7 (95% CI 1.4, 2.1) versus Māori or Asian and 2.2 (95% CI: 1.5, 3.2) versus Pacific and for CGM usage of 1.5 (95% CI: 1.3, 1.8) versus Māori or Asian and 2.4 (95% CI: 1.8, 3.4) versus Pacific. Regional variation exists for the distribution of insulin regimen, with the proportion of insulin pump use ranging from 29% to 73% (Figure S1).

There was considerable variation in individuals' glycaemic measurements, with the median HbA1c at 62 mmol/mol (IQR: 54–74, minimum: 30, maximum: 132 mmol/mol). Median: 7.8%, IQR: 7.1–8.9, minimum: 4.9, maximum: 14.2%. This wide range underscores the diverse control of diabetes within the population. Fewer than one‐quarter (23%) of individuals had an HbA1c below the 2023 ISPAD target of 53 mmol/mol (7.0%) [6]. The insulin regimen and glucose monitoring modality were associated with the lowest HbA1c levels, with insulin pumps and CGM showing the most favourable results. When used together in an automated insulin delivery system, the lowest HbA1c levels were observed (Figure 1).

Ethnicity was identified as a predictor of HbA1c, with Asian and European groups generally showing more favourable levels. However, ethnic disparities in HbA1c were lesser in those using insulin pumps and CGM. HbA1c levels increased with age after 8 years, with a more pronounced rise observed amongst children of Māori and Pacific ethnicities (test for difference in age effect by ethnicity, p = 0.026). This trend highlights the need for targeted interventions to address the growing challenges in diabetes management for these groups as they age (Figure S2).

HbA1c across regional centres is shown in Figure S1 and is further divided by insulin regimen in Figure 2, demonstrating that those using insulin pumps had the healthiest HbA1c on average regardless of region. In 2023, there were 186 newly diagnosed cases of T1D. Of these, 183 were aged 15 years or younger, likely representing all newly diagnosed cases within that age group. There is regional variation for individuals aged 16 years or older, with some being managed by paediatric services while others are referred to adult diabetes services.

Including all under 16 years of age, the national annual incidence of T1D was 17.5 (95% CI: 15.1–20.3) per 100 000 persons. Incidence rates were highest amongst those of European ethnicity, at 25 (95% CI: 20–30) per 100 000 person‐years, while the rates for Māori, Pacific and Asian ethnicities were closer to 10 per 100 000 person‐years.

Children and Adolescents With T2D

There were 151 with T2D, with 42 diagnosed in 2023 giving a national annual incidence of type 2 diabetes of 4.0 (95% CI: 2.9–5.5) per 100 000 persons. The majority of children and adolescents with T2D were Māori and Pacific (38% and 41%), respectively (Table 1). Of these, 69 (46%) were recorded as taking insulin, with only one (0.7%) on an insulin pump and three (2.6%) using CGM. The median HbA1c for children and adolescents was 57 mmol/mol (IQR 46–73), with 61 (41%) having an HbA1c of less than 53 mmol/mol, and 43 (29%) less than 48 mmol/mol.

Discussion

This report presents a snapshot of diabetes care in New Zealand in 2023. Importantly, these data provide a national landscape of diabetes care and outcomes at a time when access to automated insulin delivery was limited, with CGM unfunded and insulin pumps only funded for those meeting restrictive non‐evidence‐based eligibility criteria. On 1 October 2024, full funding of diabetes technology was introduced with non‐restrictive criteria, where anyone with T1D, neonatal, monogenic, T3C and atypical inherited diabetes could be prescribed an insulin pump and CGM free of charge by their supportive multidisciplinary team. As such, the 2023 data set serves as a baseline marker against which the impact of this funding change may be measured. This verifies the utility of having comprehensive national data for monitoring changes over time and identifying healthcare priorities. Producing this report required dedicated data collection by regional centres and national coordination to integrate and analyse this information.

On the surface, the landscape of diabetes care in NZ appears to have changed little since October 2021 [3, 4]. Reported insulin pump usage has marginally increased from 43% to 49%, and CGM remains at 69%, with persistent differences by ethnicity. Data were not sufficiently detailed to determine the ratio of real‐time to intermittently scanned CGM, but these sub‐categories of CGM are only of historic interest as from 2024 all funded CGM were real‐time. Automated insulin delivery appears to be growing, with at least 320 individuals using their pumps in hybrid closed loop mode (compared to 28 in 2021). We anticipate a marked increase in both insulin pump and CGM use since the 1 October 2024, funding change. For example, a large increase in CGM was observed in Australia with universal funding [7]. A notable difference is that insulin pumps are also fully funded in Aotearoa New Zealand but not in Australia (except for a minority based on age and financial hardship criteria), and therefore we would anticipate a greater proportion of New Zealanders benefiting from automated insulin delivery than Australia.

Our benchmarking data does show that there remain systemic barriers for improved health outcomes in our most vulnerable ethnicities—Māori and Pacific peoples. Short‐ and long‐term complications of diabetes are known to be higher in Māori and Pacific people [8, 9, 10]. Paediatric data from New Zealand has shown that advanced diabetes technologies significantly reduce predicted differences in HbA1c based on ethnicity [4]. However, at 1 October 2024, there is an estimated 550 children and adolescents with T1D not yet on CGM and 900 not on insulin pump. With approximately 200 new cases each year and an under‐resourced workforce, there will be significant challenges in supporting children and adolescents and their families in transitioning to advanced diabetes technologies, particularly for those who already face barriers in accessing healthcare services [11, 12]. Future benchmarking reports that capture the post 1 October 2024, diabetes landscape will facilitate the KiwiDiab network to examine the impact of this policy change on equity.

It is striking that almost one in five children and adolescents newly diagnosed with diabetes had type 2, of whom approximately one third are Māori and half are of Pacific ethnicity. This presents an additional workforce challenge for paediatric diabetes clinics, adapting to the growing diversity amongst the young people they serve.

There is a global trend to increasing use of diabetes technologies. International benchmarking by SWEET shows pump usage increasing relatively slowly, from an estimated 42.9% in 2013 to 60.2% in 2022. Conversely, CGM use increased rapidly from 18.7% in 2016 to 81.7% in 2022. There is wide variation between countries as SWEET is an international society with data being contributed from countries with very poorly resourced health system [13]. Of note, in Sweden where there is universal insulin pump and CGM funding, almost all (98%) Swedish youth with T1D were on CGM and two‐thirds were on insulin pumps in 2018–2019 [14]. The Swedish example shows that high uptakes of diabetes technology enabled through public funding are associated with substantial diabetes population level improvement in glycaemic outcomes.

The strengths of this study are its nationwide coverage and comprehensive reporting of demographic characteristics and HbA1c. Unfortunately, recording of technology usage was variable across regions and too imprecise to allow detailed analysis at this time; therefore, we were unable to delineate manual pump therapy from automated insulin delivery. However, the next benchmarking reports will be enabled to have this data as outlined above. The cross‐sectional nature of the study prohibits close examination of changes over time. A limitation of our dataset is the low numbers of those with non‐T1D, preventing detailed analysis for this group. At the time of data collection, there was no national registry of young people with diabetes, and as such, we may have missed a small number who were not referred to secondary or tertiary care—but this is unlikely to be true for those with T1D.

Conclusions

This first benchmarking report from KiwiDiab provides a snapshot of the diabetes landscape in Aotearoa New Zealand for children and adolescents living with diabetes. KiwiDiab is working on a centralised data collection system (national registry) for more efficiency and standardisation of data collected. We will continue to benchmark data, with the vision of improving the lives of people with diabetes (T1D and T2D), and to improve equitable outcomes.
