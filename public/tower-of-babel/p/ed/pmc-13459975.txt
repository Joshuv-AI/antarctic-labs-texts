Adaptive Functioning in Clients With Intellectual Disabilities or Borderline Intellectual Functioning and Psychiatric Comorbidity
Authors: Femke Jonker, Samantha Bouwmeester, Peter de Looff, Robert Didden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAssessment of adaptive functioning is part of the classification of intellectual disability and borderline intellectual functioning and important in estimating support needs. Adaptive functioning may be impaired in clients with psychiatric comorbidity. We investigated relationships between adaptive functioning and psychiatric comorbidity in individuals with intellectual disabilities or borderline intellectual functioning using the ADAPT.MethodADAPT scores of clients with comorbid psychiatric disorders were compared with those of clients without comorbidity (N = 4376).ResultsIn clients with intellectual disabilities, ASD was associated with lower ADAPT scores while depressive mood disorder was associated with higher ADAPT scores. We found a negative relationship between number of psychiatric disorders and mean total ADAPT score.ConclusionWhen determining the severity of an intellectual disability or using the V‐code borderline intellectual functioning, ASD and multiple comorbidities must be taken into account to prevent an intellectual disability or borderline intellectual functioning from being incorrectly classified.

Background

Adaptive skills are skills that people learn throughout their lives to meet demands and expectations of their environment and society (Tassé 2021). Since the DSM‐5 (APA 2013), more emphasis is placed on determining deficits in adaptive functioning when classifying an intellectual disability. The degree of impairment in adaptive functioning now determines the severity of an intellectual disability, while the role of IQ has diminished. For the use of the V‐code borderline intellectual functioning, the requirement of an IQ score between 71 and 84 has expired. The V‐code is now used if there is a reason for support or if borderline intellectual disability affects treatment prognosis. However, with the elimination of the IQ criterion, there is no criterion left for determining borderline intellectual functioning. A recent meta‐analysis by Orío‐Aparicio et al. (Orío‐Aparicio, López‐Escribano, and Bel‐Fennelós 2025; Orío‐Aparicio, Bel‐Fenellós, and López‐Escribano 2025) shows that people with borderline intellectual functioning, like those with intellectual disabilities, may experience difficulties in adaptive functioning across conceptual, social, and practical domains. In previous research with the ADAPT (Jonker et al. 2021), we also demonstrated that clients with borderline intellectual functioning experience conceptually similar problems in adaptive functioning as clients with mild intellectual disabilities. Furthermore, this study shows that the functioning problems of people with borderline intellectual functioning are less severe than the functioning problems of clients with mild intellectual disabilities. Measuring adaptive functioning is therefore of great importance for both classifications. In addition, compared to IQ, adaptive functioning can improve with training and is more informative about the type and intensity of support a person needs to successfully participate in society (APA 2022; Schalock et al. 2021). Unlike in many other countries (Orío‐Aparicio, López‐Escribano, and Bel‐Fennelós 2025; Orío‐Aparicio, Bel‐Fenellós, and López‐Escribano 2025), clients with borderline intellectual functioning in the Netherlands can access specialised care for people with mild intellectual disabilities if, in addition to a low IQ, they also experience difficulties with adaptive functioning. These difficulties can lead to social, societal, and emotional problems (Wieland and Zitman 2016), and providing support for these deficits can lead to clients' improved functioning, participation, and well‐being (Schalock et al. 2021).” In the Dutch context, we see that a part of the clients with borderline intellectual functioning therefore benefit more from the support paradigm (Schalock et al. 2021) in specialised care for clients with intellectual disabilities than from the treatment paradigm of mental healthcare.

Limitations in adaptive functioning also occur in people without intellectual disabilities or borderline intellectual functioning. For example, research into people with autism spectrum disorders (ASD) has consistently revealed limitations in adaptive functioning, whereby the level of adaptive functioning was one to two standard deviations below their IQ score (Matthews et al. 2021; Tamm et al. 2021). In clients with ASD and intellectual disability, differences between IQ and adaptive functioning are smaller (Alexander and Reynolds 2020). However, there is little consensus about which domain(s) of adaptive functioning are impaired in ASD (see Matthews et al. 2021).

Although less extensive, research in adult people with schizophrenia or ADHD has also revealed deficits in adaptive functioning. Lower adaptive functioning in adults with schizophrenia is associated with negative symptoms (e.g., apathy, executive function problems), cognitive impairments and communication problems, while no association has been found between adaptive functioning and positive symptoms (Adelsky et al. 2011; Bowie and Harvey 2008; McGurk et al. 2000; Velligan et al. 2002). In ADHD, lower adaptive functioning in (young) adults is mainly associated with inattention/disorganisation and less with hyperactivity/impulsivity (Stavro et al. 2007). In adults with intellectual disability, no differences in adaptive functioning were found between individuals with ADHD and those without ADHD (Di Nuovo and Buono 2007).

Research into deficits in adaptive functioning in other psychiatric disorders (with and without intellectual disabilities) is rare. The study by Di Nuovo and Buono (2007) among clients with intellectual disability showed higher adaptive functioning in some areas for clients with mood and/or personality disorder than for clients without such comorbidity. In a study by Didden et al. (2009) among clients with mild intellectual disability or borderline intellectual functioning and severe behavioural problems, no difference in adaptive functioning was found between clients with and without substance use disorder. In clients with (profound to) severe intellectual disabilities, Matson et al. (2006) found no difference in adaptive functioning between clients with and without bipolar mood disorder or another psychiatric disorder. However, these studies involved small and highly selective samples containing only small numbers of subjects with a specific psychiatric disorder. No correction was made for other variables that may be associated with adaptive functioning (see below), and no research was conducted at subscale level.

As it is known that persons with ASD, ADHD and schizophrenia have often problems in adaptive functioning problems as a part of the disorder. We hypothesised that adaptive functioning might also be related to other comorbid psychiatric disorders. For instance, it is the authors' experiences in clinical practice that clients with depressive mood disorder may lie in bed more often and neglect their friendships; clients with addiction may care less about self‐hygiene and keep their house less tidy; and clients with cluster B personality disorder (borderline, narcissistic, antisocial and histrionic personality disorders) may make less responsible choices and hang out with peers who show risky behaviours. Next to this, some studies have revealed more adaptive functioning deficits when there are multiple disorders compared to individuals with one disorder or no disorder at all in addition to an intellectual disability (Matson et al. 2009; Ward et al. 2022). This possibly implies a relationship between number of psychiatric disorders and level of adaptive functioning.

Research into the associations between psychiatric disorders and adaptive functioning deficits is important to prevent the incorrect classification of an intellectual disability or borderline intellectual functioning. If a comorbid psychiatric disorder is related to lower adaptive functioning, then the probability of a false positive classification of an intellectual disability or borderline intellectual functioning may increase. Thus, psychiatric comorbidity should be considered when assessing (the severity of an) intellectual disability or borderline intellectual functioning. In addition, knowledge about relationships between psychiatric disorders and adaptive functioning may inform caregivers about the type and intensity of (extra) support clients with psychiatric comorbidity need.

The aim of this study was to investigate associations between adaptive functioning and psychiatric disorders in a large sample (N = 4376) of clients with moderate to mild intellectual disabilities or borderline intellectual functioning. For each disorder (i.e., ADHD, ASD, psychotic disorder, anxiety disorder, mood disorder, personality disorder and substance use disorder) we explored whether there are adaptive functioning deficits compared to clients without any psychiatric comorbidity. In the analyses, relationships were corrected for gender, age and IQ as research with the ADAPT (see Jonker et al. 2021; Jonker and Nijman 2021) shows that men with intellectual disabilities or borderline intellectual functioning have significantly lower ADAPT scores than women, ADAPT scores increase until ages 30–34 and then slowly decrease, and ADAPT scores increase when IQ increases (see Jonker et al. 2021; Jonker and Nijman 2021), thus these factors need to be adjusted for. To investigate unique effects of psychiatric comorbidity analyses and their interaction effects were corrected for these three variables. In case a unique effect of a disorder was found on total ADAPT score, post hoc analyses were done on the eight ADAPT subscales. This provided insight into specific areas in which these clients have extra support needs. Second, we also investigated whether there was an association between adaptive functioning deficits and number of psychiatric disorders (also corrected for gender, age and IQ). Multiple psychiatric disorders imply more serious psychiatric problems, but the question was whether this also would mean more severe adaptive functioning deficits when corrected for gender, age and IQ. We hypothesised that (1) total ADAPT scores would be lower in participants with a specific psychiatric disorder compared to clients without any disorder, and that (2) there would be a negative relationship between number of psychiatric disorders and total ADAPT score. Before examining both hypotheses, we explored associations between ADAPT scores and gender, age and IQ.

Method

Participants and Setting

Between 2018 and 2021, 4376 ADAPTs were collected in more than 200 organisations for intellectual disability, mental health care and forensic care in the Netherlands. The initiative to participate in the data collection came—following a request from the first author—from the psychologists responsible for the clients. In most institutions, an explanation was provided in advance regarding adaptive skills and how to complete the ADAPT. Additional data, such as gender, age, results of the most recent IQ test and current psychiatric comorbidity, were provided by the psychologists who submitted the ADAPT data. Clients for whom the ADAPT was completed were 16 years or older. It was assumed that these clients would meet the criteria for an intellectual disability or borderline intellectual functioning before testing, based on information from the intake. Note that this assumption was not always confirmed after completing an IQ test and the ADAPT, which explains that there are also clients with relatively higher cognitive and adaptive functioning in our dataset. The ADAPT is originally intended for measuring adaptive functioning in clients with mild intellectual functioning and borderline intellectual functioning. We included clients with moderate intellectual disability and below‐average intellectual functioning in the dataset because we felt it necessary to provide the clinician with broader IQ ranges than for mild intellectual disability and borderline intellectual functioning. Our rationale behind this is that the DSM‐5‐TR states that IQ data in the lower regions of the theoretical normal distribution are less reliable (APA 2022). We also observed in our dataset that the majority of clients had a disharmonious intelligence profile. Since the clinician is assigned a greater role in classifying the severity of an intellectual disability and the determination of the V‐code borderline intellectual functioning, we wanted to provide clinicians with the information needed to make sound judgements regarding the classifications. The setting in which clients resided varied from outpatient/ambulant to closed and secured units. The type of care consisted of housing support and/or clinical treatment (see Table 1). Clients with a physical or sensory impairment were excluded because these conditions can seriously influence adaptive functioning (see Van der Aa et al. 2023).

Procedure

Permission to collect anonymised data was obtained from the Ethics Committee Social Sciences (ECSS) of Radboud University at Nijmegen (ECSW‐2018‐122). Through meetings, conferences, and the website specifically created for the study, organisations were approached to participate. After an organisation expressed interest in participating, the first author provided background information on adaptive functioning and verbal and/or written instructions on how to complete the ADAPT. The ADAPT was completed by professional caregivers and adult family members who had reasonable knowledge into the daily functioning of the client in multiple contexts, such as home, work and social contacts (For more details on the procedure, see Jonker et al. 2021).

The ADaptive Ability Performance Test (ADAPT)

Following the changes in the DSM‐5 for determining intellectual disability and borderline intellectual functioning (APA 2013), the ADAPT was developed in 2016 (Jonker et al. 2016). The ADAPT is an observational instrument for assessing adaptive skills covering the conceptual, social and practical domain. The development of the ADAPT was necessary because of a lack of up‐to‐date instruments that incorporate the changes in the DSM‐5 and contemporary adaptive skills, such as internet banking and using a mobile phone. Since no changes have been made to criteria on intellectual disability and V‐code regarding borderline intellectual functioning in the DSM‐5‐TR (APA 2022), no further changes to the ADAPT were made. The ADAPT, developed for clients aged 16 and older, consists of 62 items, divided over eight factors: Basic self‐care, hygiene and responsible eating; Household skills; Society skills; Social alignment; Applying academic skills; Dealing with money, mail and insurance; Daily structure and daily schedule; Making responsible choices. Examples of items of the ADAPT are (The client…): −Wears clean clothes−Shops for a meal−Travels independently by public transport−Uses internet banking−Makes adequate contact with new people−Fills in a form or sends a readable email.

Each item is scored on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 1: Does not perform the skill, even with help, to 5: Performs the skill completely independently. A proxy scores what a client actually does, not what s/he potentially can do (but does not always do). The total score ranges between 62 and 310 and higher scores indicate higher adaptive functioning. Several studies have shown that the internal consistency of subscale and total scores were (very) high and inter‐rater reliability was good. Concurrent and predictive validity were (very) good. Positive associations were found between total ADAPT score and IQ, educational level, level of social–emotional functioning and age (see Jonker et al. 2021; Kruisdijk et al. 2019; Nijman et al. 2017).

Demographic and Other Characteristics

IQ was mainly assessed with the WAIS‐IV‐NL, and WISC‐V‐NL. The most recent IQ score and DSM classification were collected from the client's file. Psychiatric classifications were divided into major disorder groups: ASD, ADHD, psychotic disorders, anxiety disorders and stressor related disorders, depressive mood disorders, personality disorders (cluster A, B, and C), and substance use disorders. Psychiatric classifications were established by a psychiatrist or healthcare psychologist, psychotherapist or clinical psychologist. Data on gender, age, level of education and living situation were also collected from the client's file.

Statistical Analyses

SPSS version 29 was used for the statistical analyses. Demographic and diagnostic characteristics were analysed using descriptive statistics. First, it was explored (using t‐tests, regression analysis and Pearson correlations) if we could find the relationships between adaptive functioning and gender, age and IQ as were reported in our earlier studies. Second, general linear models were used: (a) to test differences in total ADAPT scores between clients with a specific psychiatric disorder and those without, corrected for gender, age and IQ; (b) to test post hoc differences in ADAPT subscale scores for disorders where a unique effect was found for the total scores and corrected for gender, age and IQ; and (c) to investigate the relationship between number of disorders and total ADAPT scores. Assumptions for the regression analyses were met.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

Demographic data of the sample of 4376 clients are available in Table 1. Men were overrepresented compared to women, which can be explained by the participation of forensic facilities in which men (also those with intellectual disabilities or borderline intellectual functioning) are overrepresented. An IQ score was available for slightly more than half of the clients (i.e., 2575), with approximately 95% of clients having an IQ below 85. Most clients lived in a 24‐h care or treatment setting.

Diagnostic Characteristics

About half of the clients had one or more psychiatric disorders (Table 2), of which ASD was the most common. A residual category (named ‘other’) of classifications mainly concerned attachment disorders, aggression‐related disorders, and genetic disorders, among others. This category was not considered in the analyses because of the limited number of clients per separate condition. About two‐third of clients with comorbidity had one classification, about a quarter of clients with comorbidity had two classifications, and less than 10% had three or more classifications.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate whether psychiatric comorbidity in clients with moderate to mild intellectual disability or borderline intellectual functioning was associated with adaptive functioning as assessed with the ADAPT. Data were collected in 4376 clients of whom approximately half were classified with one or more psychiatric disorders. All major psychiatric disorder groups were represented in the dataset, including classifications that, to our knowledge, had not been previously studied on their relationship with adaptive functioning. The main findings were that ASD was related to lower adaptive functioning while depressive mood disorder was related to higher adaptive functioning compared to clients without psychiatric disorder. Next to this, a negative association was found between number of comorbid disorders and adaptive functioning. Our study also extends the studies in the introduction by correcting for gender, age and IQ that may be related to adaptive functioning. As far as we know, no studies have included (all these) corrections when analysing relationships between psychiatric disorders and adaptive functioning. In particular, gender has not been taken into account when exploring relationships between a given disorder and adaptive functioning.

Comorbid Psychiatric Disorders Are Related to Lower ADAPT Scores

Our first hypothesis stated that lower ADAPT scores would be found in individuals with a comorbid psychiatric disorder. The hypothesis could only be accepted for ASD as the results showed that ASD was associated with lower ADAPT scores. Post hoc analysis at subscale level showed that mean scores were significantly lower on most of the ADAPT subscales. It is difficult to compare results at subscale level with those of other studies as their results are inconclusive about which domain(s) of adaptive functioning are impaired in ASD (Matthews et al. 2021).

The average difference of 22.76 ADAPT points between clients with ASD in comparison to clients without comorbidity may be clinically relevant when assessing (the severity of) intellectual disability or borderline intellectual functioning. If this difference is not taken into account when assessing individuals with ASD, there is a risk of false positive classification of (the severity of) an intellectual disability or borderline intellectual functioning. For example, the mean difference between clients with a mild intellectual disability (which in DSM‐5‐TR terms makes one standard deviation difference with a moderate intellectual disability) and those with a moderate intellectual disability is 20 ADAPT points (Jonker and Nijman 2021).

Contrary to our expectations, we found higher adaptive functioning levels in clients with comorbid depressive mood disorder compared to clients without comorbidity. However, the outcomes of the present study confirm those of an earlier study by Di Nuovo and Buono (2007) who investigated a small number of clients with comorbid depression. The relatively high level of adaptive functioning (relative to IQ) observed in clients with comorbid depression may be explained as follows: Depression has a negative impact on cognitive functioning, including attention and memory problems, executive functions, processing speed, and overall IQ (Rhee et al. 2024). These limitations may persist even after recovery from depression (Rhee et al. 2024). It is possible that the clients with (comorbid) depression in our study were incorrectly classified as having borderline intellectual functioning or a mild intellectual disability based on decreased IQ, even though their adaptive functioning was not or only minimally affected. Unfortunately, our data do not provide information on the onset of depression or the date of the IQ test, so we are unable to determine whether the low IQ was already present prior to these clients' (first) depressive episode. A longitudinal study of the premorbid and postmorbid adaptive functioning of people with depressive mood disorder might provide an answer to the hypothesis that in clients with depressive mood disorder, post‐morbid adaptive functioning remains the same or is less affected than IQ, compared to premorbid adaptive functioning. If it turns out to be true that depression causes a decline in IQ but adaptive functioning remains relatively intact, this would differ from psychotic disorders, in which both IQ and adaptive functioning decline (see also the section below).

The fact that most single psychiatric comorbidities are not related to adaptive functioning deficits suggests that comorbid psychiatric disorders, except ASD, do not potentially hinder the assessment of the severity of intellectual disability or borderline intellectual functioning. Below, we will discuss two other disorders (i.e., psychotic disorder, ADHD) in more detail because of the differences between our findings and those of previous studies.

In contrast to other studies as well as our own clinical experience, no relationship was found between psychotic disorders and (lower) adaptive functioning in clients with intellectual disability or borderline intellectual functioning. A possible explanation is that not only adaptive functioning deficits are related to psychotic disorders, but also intellectual functioning deficits. Results from meta‐analyses show that individuals who later developed schizophrenia (Woodberry et al. 2008) have an average premorbid 8‐point IQ deficit compared to individuals who not later developed schizophrenia, and a 14‐to 21‐point IQ deficit among first‐episode and chronic schizophrenia clients (Heinrichs and Zakzanis 1998; Mesholam‐Gately et al. 2009; Reichenberg and Harvey 2007). The intellectual functioning has deteriorated as a result of the psychotic disorder. When classifying (the severity of) an intellectual disability or borderline intellectual functioning in clients with psychosis, the premorbid level of both IQ and adaptive functioning should be examined to decide whether there is a congenital or an acquired disability (i.e., C criterion of DSM‐5‐TR classification of intellectual disability). Another explanation for the discrepancy between our results and those of other studies may involve gender differences. On average, men (with and without intellectual disability or borderline intellectual functioning) show lower ADAPT scores than women (Jonker et al. 2021; Table 4). Also, psychotic disorders are about four times more common in men than in women (Lewis and Buchanan 2015). In studies cited in the introduction, men were overrepresented and none of the analyses conducted in these studies were corrected for gender. The deficits in adaptive functioning found in studies on clients with psychotic disorders could therefore partly be the result of gender instead of a unique effect of the diagnosis.

Finally, in contrast to previous research, we did not find a relationship between (lower) adaptive functioning and ADHD after correcting for gender, age and IQ. As with psychotic disorders, adaptive functioning deficits in ADHD can (partly) be explained by gender differences than by the unique effect of diagnosis, since ADHD is about two times more common in adult men than in adult women (Danielson et al. 2018; Kessler et al. 2006). Men were overrepresented in the studies cited in the introduction section of this article.

Future research should focus on adaptive functioning in clients with a psychiatric disorder without intellectual disabilities. For example, in ASD in children it has been found that the differences between IQ (intellectual potential functioning) and adaptive functioning (observed functioning) are smaller as IQ is lower (Ward et al. 2022). In clients with higher IQ, psychiatric disorders could play a greater role in adaptive functioning problems.

The Higher the Number of Psychiatric Disorders, the Lower the ADAPT Scores

Although most single comorbid disorders are not related to deficits in adaptive functioning, a negative relationship was found between number of disorders and problems in adaptive functioning. The effect per additional disorder is small. In an earlier study we found that 10 ADAPT points or more produces a clinically relevant difference (Jonker et al. 2021). As our regression analysis shows that each additional disorder results in a decrease of total ADAPT score of 3.85, a clinically relevant difference in adaptive functioning deficits occurs if clients have 3 or more comorbid psychiatric disorders.

Strengths and Limitations

There are several strengths and limitations that should be noted. A strength of this study is that the data were collected at more than 200 facilities and that the organisations were spread across all major regions of the Netherlands. Another strength is that all DSM‐5(−TR) classifications were represented, which has not been considered in studies before. Next to this, most classifications were well‐represented in terms of number of participants. A final strength is that we corrected for age, gender and IQ in the analyses to explore the unique effects of disorders.

Results of our study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. One of the limitations is that—although obtained through certified diagnosticians—we do not know how the DSM classifications were set and whether valid instruments and methods were used for the classification. Related to this, we did not collect data on the severity of psychiatric disorders which may be associated with adaptive functioning. For example, in ASD it has been shown that the more severe the disorder, the more severe the adaptive functioning deficits (Matson et al. 2009). Finally, the influence of psychiatric comorbidity was examined in clients with an IQ range between 40 and 99, in which the group of clients with a mild intellectual disability and borderline intellectual functioning was particularly represented. The results can therefore not be generalised to clients with average or higher IQ or to clients with more severe intellectual disability.

Conclusion

Since the introduction of the DSM‐5, the severity of an intellectual disability is primarily determined on deficits in adaptive functioning. Deficits in adaptive functioning that lead to a need for professional support can be a reason to use the V‐code borderline intellectual functioning in case of lower intelligence (APA 2022). Because psychiatric comorbidity is common in clients with intellectual disability or borderline intellectual functioning, it is important to investigate to what extent psychiatric comorbidity is related to deficits in adaptive functioning. This study suggests that we must take psychiatric comorbidity into account when assessing or classifying intellectual disability or borderline intellectual functioning.
