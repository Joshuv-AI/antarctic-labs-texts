Efficacy and safety of intravenous Levetiracetam as first-line treatment for neonatal clinical seizures: A single-center study (2021−2023)
Authors: Mojtaba Movahedinia, Seyed Reza Mirjalili, Narges Abedi Ardekani, Farimah Shamsi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal seizures demand urgent treatment to mitigate neurodevelopmental risks. This study evaluates intravenous levetiracetam's efficacy and safety as first-line therapy for seizures in term neonates.MethodsA retrospective analysis included term neonates with clinical seizures admitted to a NICU (2021–2023), excluding metabolic/other predefined etiologies. All received intravenous levetiracetam initially. Statistical analysis (SPSS v.19) used chi-square and paired t-tests.ResultsThe study included 40 neonates, of whom 12 (30 %) were male and 28 (70 %) were female. Tonic seizures were the most frequent type (40 %), followed by myoclonic and mixed seizures (22.5 % each). Hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) was the predominant etiology, accounting for 50 % of cases. No significant association was found between seizure type and the number of doses required (p = 0.051) or seizure recurrence within 48 h (p = 0.485). Additionally, the number of doses administered did not significantly impact seizure recurrence after 48 h (p = 0.63).ConclusionIntravenous levetiracetam demonstrated efficacy in controlling neonatal seizures, with no reported drug-related adverse effects. More than 70 % of neonates required only a single dose, and nearly 85 % remained seizure-free within 48 h. While these findings suggest favorable outcomes with levetiracetam, larger, prospective studies are necessary to further substantiate its efficacy and safety profile in this population.Keywords: Neonatal seizures, Intravenous levetiracetam, Hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, Antiepileptic therapy, Seizure control

Introduction

Neonatal seizures are a critical neurological emergency, frequently signaling significant underlying pathologies, such as hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, infections, or metabolic disturbances. [1] Their timely and effective management is crucial, as unaddressed seizures can result in acute brain injury and increase the risk of long-term neurodevelopmental impairments. [2], [3] Despite the pressing need for optimal therapeutic strategies, neonatal seizure management continues to face significant challenges, including limitations in available pharmacological options and the risk of adverse drug effects in this vulnerable population. [4]

Effective and safe treatment of neonatal seizures is critical to prevent long-term neurological deficits. [5] Traditional first-line antiepileptic drugs (AEDs), such as phenobarbital, have been associated with limited efficacy and potential neurotoxicity, leading to increased interest in newer AEDs like levetiracetam. [6], [7].

Phenobarbital has long been the mainstay treatment for neonatal seizures. However, its limited efficacy in achieving seizure control and potential for neurotoxic side effects have fueled growing interest in identifying safer and more effective alternatives. [8] Among second-generation antiepileptic drugs, levetiracetam has emerged as a promising candidate due to its distinct pharmacological properties, including high bioavailability, minimal hepatic metabolism, and low risk of drug interactions. [9] Unlike traditional antiepileptic medications, levetiracetam acts on synaptic vesicle protein 2 A (SV2A), which is associated with its broad-spectrum antiepileptic efficacy and neuroprotective effects. [10] While intravenous levetiracetam is widely used in older children and adults, its application in neonates remains under investigation. [11] The neonatal population presents unique pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic challenges, necessitating rigorous evaluation to establish dosing, efficacy, and safety profiles. [12]

Recent evidence highlights the potential efficacy of intravenous levetiracetam in neonatal seizure management. A prospective study by Abend et al. (2011) reported seizure control in 56 % of neonates when levetiracetam was used as a second-line agent. [13] Similarly, a 2021 meta-analysis by Qiao MY et al. compared levetiracetam with phenobarbital, demonstrating comparable efficacy between the two drugs. Notably, the meta-analysis found that levetiracetam was associated with a lower incidence of adverse effects, suggesting it may offer a safer alternative to phenobarbital. [8]

The safety profile of levetiracetam in neonates has been a focal point of recent research. In contrast to phenobarbital, which is associated with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes, levetiracetam has shown a more favorable safety profile. A phase IIb randomized controlled trial by Sharpe et al. (2020) compared levetiracetam and phenobarbital for the treatment of neonatal seizures, reporting that phenobarbital achieved a significantly higher seizure resolution rate at 24 h (80 % vs. 28 %, P < 0.001). Although increasing the dose of levetiracetam improved efficacy marginally (by 7.5 %), phenobarbital remained more effective. While adverse events were more common with phenobarbital, the difference was not statistically significant. [14] Maitre et al. (2013) retrospectively analyzed 280 infants treated for neonatal seizures. Greater phenobarbital exposure was associated with worse neurodevelopmental outcomes, including reduced BSID scores and increased cerebral palsy risk. [15] Levetiracetam showed milder effects on development. The findings suggest levetiracetam may be less detrimental than phenobarbital for long-term neurodevelopment. [16] Rao et al. compared the efficacy of levetiracetam and phenobarbital for treating neonatal seizures associated with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE). Despite the study's relatively small sample size and retrospective design, the findings suggest that levetiracetam is a promising alternative to phenobarbital for managing neonatal seizures in this context. However, a large-scale randomized controlled trial is required to validate these results and establish robust clinical guidelines. [17]

Head-to-head comparisons between levetiracetam and phenobarbital have underscored their respective advantages and limitations in the treatment of neonatal seizures. While phenobarbital remains the most commonly used first-line therapy, its association with limited efficacy and potential neurodevelopmental toxicity has prompted a growing interest in alternatives such as levetiracetam. Despite promising results from preliminary studies, several critical gaps persist in the literature. Large-scale randomized controlled trials are necessary to conclusively establish levetiracetam's efficacy and safety as a first-line therapy for neonatal seizures. Furthermore, additional research is warranted to refine dosing strategies, identify predictors of therapeutic response, and elucidate the drug's potential neuroprotective mechanisms. Importantly, understanding the long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes associated with levetiracetam use in neonates is essential for guiding its integration into clinical practice. This study seeks to evaluate the efficacy and safety of intravenous levetiracetam as a first-line treatment for clinical seizures in term neonates admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) at Shahid Sadoughi Hospital, Yazd. By addressing these critical questions, the study aims to contribute valuable data to inform clinical practice and enhance evidence-based guidelines for neonatal seizure management.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted at the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) of Shahid Sadoughi Hospital, Yazd, Iran, between April 2021 and March 2023. The study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of intravenous levetiracetam as a first-line treatment for clinical seizures in term neonates. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board (Ethics ID: IR.SSU.MEDICINE.REC.1403.101). Parental consent was secured for all participants, and data confidentiality was maintained throughout the study.

Study population

Neonates admitted to the NICU with clinical seizures confirmed by bedside video-EEG monitoring were included. Inclusion criteria were as follows: Age < 1-month, Clinical seizure confirmed by specialized clinical judgment, Admission to the NICU or pediatric wards, Completion of a comprehensive diagnostic and therapeutic evaluation during the neonatal period, Administration of intravenous levetiracetam as a first-line treatment. Exclusion criteria included neonates with seizures caused by: Reversible metabolic disturbances (e.g., hypoglycemia, hypocalcemia, hypo−/hypernatremia, hypomagnesemia), Pyridoxine-dependent seizures or non-ketotic hyperglycemia, Opioid withdrawal, Prematurity (gestational age < 37 weeks), Renal impairment (serum creatinine ≥1 mg/dL).

Data collection

The hospital's Health Information System (HIS) was used to identify eligible neonates. Demographic details, clinical presentation, diagnostic evaluations, and treatment responses were extracted from electronic medical records and entered into structured questionnaires. Blood samples were obtained on admission to evaluate electrolytes, liver enzymes, ammonia, blood gas analysis, CBC, BUN, and creatinine levels.

Intervention protocol

All neonates received intravenous levetiracetam as the first-line antiepileptic drug; none were administered levetiracetam as second-line therapy. Persistent seizures despite the maximum dose of levetiracetam (60 mg/kg) were managed with second-line agents (phenobarbital or phenytoin).

Intravenous levetiracetam was administered as the initial antiepileptic drug, with a loading dose of 40 mg/kg followed by maintenance doses adjusted to clinical response and gestational age. The protocol included slow infusion of 20 mg/kg diluted with normal saline. If seizures persisted or recurred within 15 min, additional doses of 20 mg/kg were given, up to a total of 60 mg/kg. Maintenance doses of 20–60 mg/kg/day were divided into two intravenous administrations over 48 h.

Subsequently, oral levetiracetam was prescribed for 1–3 months in seizure-free neonates.

If seizures persisted or recurred after the maximum levetiracetam dose (60 mg/kg), intravenous phenobarbital (20 mg/kg) or phenytoin (20 mg/kg) was administered as second-line therapy.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was seizure cessation, defined as the resolution of clinical seizures within 24 h of levetiracetam administration without additional anticonvulsants. A favorable response was defined as seizure control within two hours of the initial dose and no recurrence within 48 h. Adverse effects, including hypotension, bradycardia, respiratory depression, and sedation, were systematically documented and managed.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 19.0. Descriptive statistics summarized baseline characteristics. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests, and continuous variables were evaluated using paired t-tests. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study adhered to ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was conducted with the approval of the Ethics Committee at the Faculty of Medicine of Shahid Sadougi University of Medical Sciences, Yazd (ID IR.SSU.MEDICINE.REC.1403.101), and written informed consent was obtained from the parents of the infants. No additional costs or interventions were imposed on participants.

Results

Newborn characteristics

A total of 40 newborns were included in the study. Gender distribution showed that 70 % were female (n = 28), while 30 % were male (n = 12). Regarding the type of delivery, 52.5 % of the newborns were delivered via cesarean section (n = 21), and 47.5 % via vaginal delivery (n = 19). The most common head circumference range was 33.0–<34.0 cm, observed in 51.28 % of newborns (n = 20). Other ranges were 32.0–<33.0 cm (23.07 %, n = 9), 34.0–<36.0 cm (20.51 %, n = 8), and 36.0–<39.5 cm (5.12 %, n = 2). Most newborns had normal birth weights (2500–3499 g), accounting for 60 % (n = 24). Low birth weight (<2500 g) was observed in 10 % (n = 4), and high birth weight (3500–4499 g) in 5 % (n = 2). Short stays (1–3 days) were reported for 25 % of newborns (n = 10), moderate stays (4–7 days) for 40 % (n = 16), extended stays (8–15 days) for 22.5 % (n = 9), and prolonged stays (>16 days) for 12.5 % (n = 5). Seizure Onset and Types: Age of Seizure Onset: Early-onset seizures (1–5 days) were the most prevalent, affecting 62.5 % of newborns (n = 25). Intermediate onset (6–20 days) and late onset (21–30 days) were observed in 25 % (n = 10) and 12.5 % (n = 5) of cases, respectively. Seizure Types: Tonic seizures were the most frequent, accounting for 40 % (n = 16), followed by mixed seizures (22.5 %, n = 9) and myoclonic seizures (22.5 %, n = 9). Subtle and colonic seizures each accounted for 7.5 % (n = 3). (Table 1).

Seizure etiology

The primary etiology of seizures in the study population was hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, accounting for 50.0 % of cases (n = 9). Other identified causes included infections (16.6 %, n = 3), structural brain abnormalities (11.1 %, n = 2), suspected metabolic causes (11.1 %, n = 2), hematoma (5.5 %, n = 1), and intracerebral hemorrhage (5.5 %, n = 1). (Table 1).

Clinical response and maintenance dosing patterns in neonatal seizure management

The majority of patients (77.5 %, n = 31) responded to the first dose of the medication, while responses to the second and third doses were observed in 20 % (n = 8) and 2.5 % (n = 1) of patients, respectively. The most commonly prescribed maintenance dose was 20 mg/day, accounting for 80 % (n = 32) of patients, followed by 40 mg/day (15 %, n = 6), 30 mg/day (2.5 %, n = 1), and 10 mg/day (2.5 %, n = 1). (Table 2).

Prevalence of seizures, abnormal MRI findings, and blood test results in neonatal seizure

Seizures were reported in 15.38 % (n = 6) of the study population, whereas 84.62 % (n = 33) did not experience seizures, with statistical analysis showing no significant association (χ2 = 6.38 × 10−7, P = 0.9994). Abnormal MRI findings were observed in 51.52 % (n = 17) of cases, while 48.48 % (n = 16) showed normal results, with no statistically significant association (χ2 = 3.11 × 10−7, P = 0.9996). Similarly, abnormal blood test results were recorded in 30 % (n = 12) of participants compared to 70 % (n = 28) with normal results, again without significant association (χ2 = 0.00, P = 1.0000). Seizure type and dose response

The response to various seizure types and doses is detailed in Table 5. For subtle seizures, all cases (100 %, n = 40) responded to the first dose, with no responses observed for the second or third doses (P = 0.051). Among cases of tonic seizures, the majority (68.8 %, n = 11) achieved seizure control with the first dose, while 31.3 % (n = 5) required a second dose. No responses were recorded for the third dose. In colonic seizures, responses were distributed across doses, with 66.7 % (n = 2) responding to the first dose and 33.3 % (n = 1) responding to the third dose; no responses were observed for the second dose. For myoclonic seizures, most cases (88.9 %, n = 8) responded to the first dose, and 11.1 % (n = 1) required a second dose, with no response observed for the third dose. In cases of mixed seizures, the majority (77.8 %, n = 7) responded to the first dose, while 22.2 % (n = 2) responded to the second dose, with no responses observed for the third dose. (Table 4).

Resolution patterns of different seizure types within 48 hours

The association between seizure type and resolution after 48 h is summarized in Table 5. Subtle seizures resolved completely in all cases (100 %, n = 3) within 48 h (P = 0.485). For tonic seizures, 18.8 % (n = 3) of cases experienced persistence, while 81.3 % (n = 13) were seizure-free after 48 h. Among cases with clonic seizures, 33.3 % (n = 1) exhibited persistence, whereas 66.7 % (n = 2) resolved within 48 h. Myoclonic seizures persisted in 22.2 % (n = 2) of cases, with 77.8 % (n = 7) resolving within the same timeframe. Finally, all cases of mixed seizures (100 %, n = 9) resolved without seizures after 48 h. (Table 5).

Medication dose and seizure resolution within 48 hours

The effect of the number of doses on seizure resolution was evaluated (P = 0.63). Among patients who received one dose, seizures persisted in 12.9 % (n = 4), while 87.1 % (n = 27) achieved resolution. For those who received two doses, seizures persisted in 25.0 % (n = 2), with 75.0 % (n = 6) achieving resolution. Notably, all patients who received three doses (100 %, n = 1) experienced seizure resolution. (Table 5).

Quantitative factors associated with seizure resolution within 48 hours

The association of various quantitative factors with seizure resolution within 48 h is summarized in Table 5. The mean age of seizure onset was similar between newborns with persistent seizures (7.7 ± 8.8 days) and those without seizures after 48 h (8.8 ± 8.2 days), with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.88). Newborns with persistent seizures had a lower mean birth weight (2518.3 ± 558.5 g) compared to those without seizures (2874.4 ± 461.1 g), though the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.09). Similarly, the mean head circumference was slightly smaller in newborns with persistent seizures (32.8 ± 0.87 cm) compared to those without seizures (33.5 ± 1.2 cm), with no significant association (P = 0.19). The mean duration of hospitalization was also comparable between the two groups (8.3 ± 7.0 days for persistent seizures vs. 7.9 ± 7.2 days for no seizures), with no significant difference (P = 0.90). (Table 5).

Discussion

This study demonstrates the efficacy and safety of intravenous levetiracetam as a first-line treatment for neonatal clinical seizures. Early-onset seizures, primarily tonic in nature, were most common, with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) identified as the leading etiology. Levetiracetam showed a high response rate, with 77.5 % of neonates achieving seizure control after the first dose, and most cases resolving within 48 h. Differences in resolution rates among seizure types were noted, but overall outcomes were favorable. Quantitative factors such as birth weight, head circumference, and age of seizure onset showed no significant association with treatment outcomes, and diagnostic findings from MRI and blood tests were not predictive of seizure resolution. These results support the use of levetiracetam as an effective and manageable option in neonatal seizure management, warranting further studies to confirm and expand on these findings.

The findings of this study corroborate and extend existing evidence on the use of intravenous levetiracetam in neonatal seizure management. Similar to previous studies, our results highlight the high efficacy of levetiracetam, with 77.5 % of neonates achieving seizure control after the first dose and most seizures resolving within 48 h. This aligns with efficacy rates reported by Abend et al. (2011) [13] and Kreimer et al. (2019) [18], and khan et al. (2021) [19] where response rates to levetiracetam as a first-line treatment were between 70 % and 85 %. The favorable response in our study adds to the growing consensus on the drug's effectiveness in early seizure control. Also, a 2016 study by Venkatesan et al. found that levetiracetam effectively treated seizures in neonates with HIE, with 84 % (27 out of 32) achieving seizure cessation after administration. [20]

In terms of seizure type, our findings are consistent with studies indicating higher resolution rates for subtle and mixed seizures compared to tonic and clonic seizures. Sharpe et al. (2020) found that subtle seizures responded better to treatment, likely due to differences in pathophysiology, while our study observed higher resolution rates for tonic seizures (81.3 % within 48 h) compared to earlier reports. In their phase IIb trial, phenobarbital was more effective than levetiracetam for neonatal seizures, achieving 24-h seizure freedom in 80 % of cases versus 28 %. Dose escalation improved levetiracetam's efficacy slightly (by 7.5 %), but phenobarbital remained superior. Although adverse effects were more common with phenobarbital, they were not statistically significant. The study highlights phenobarbital's efficacy but underscores the need for further research on levetiracetam dosing and long-term outcomes. [14]

HIE was the leading cause of neonatal seizures in our study (50 %), consistent with Tekgul et al. (2006), and the high efficacy of levetiracetam in HIE-related seizures underscores its growing clinical utility. [21] MRI abnormalities were observed in 51.52 % of cases and blood test abnormalities in 30 %, aligning with Pisani et al. (2012), but neither were predictive of seizure resolution. [22] Similarly, quantitative factors such as birth weight, head circumference, and seizure onset age showed no significant association with treatment outcomes, consistent with findings by Rao et al. (2019). [17] These results highlight that while diagnostic tools like imaging and laboratory tests are essential for identifying underlying etiologies, they offer limited prognostic value for clinical responses to antiepileptic therapy.

Quantitative factors such as birth weight, head circumference, and seizure onset age showed no significant association with seizure resolution in our study. These findings align with those of Rao et al. (2019), who also reported limited predictive value for these factors in determining treatment outcomes. [17] Falsaperla et al. (2019) observed complete seizure control in 75 % of neonates within 6 h of levetiracetam administration, particularly highlighting its efficacy in neonates with HIE. [23] Our results similarly identified HIE as the most common etiology of seizures, with high response rates to levetiracetam, further supporting its role as a reliable option for this subgroup. The alignment of these findings across different studies strengthens the evidence for levetiracetam's utility in treating neonatal seizures, particularly in HIE-associated cases.

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis by Kumar et al. found that levetiracetam may be non-inferior to phenobarbital for managing neonatal seizures, with a better safety profile and marginally better short-term neurodevelopmental outcomes. [24] Additionally, a 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis by Qiao et al. reported no difference in efficacy between levetiracetam and phenobarbital in treating neonatal seizures, but noted that levetiracetam had a lower incidence of side effects. [8] These studies suggest that levetiracetam is at least as effective as phenobarbital, with a more favorable safety profile. Our study contributes to this growing consensus, demonstrating a favorable safety and efficacy profile, with no significant adverse effects reported during the study period. While phenobarbital remains a widely used AED in neonates, its association with side effects such as respiratory depression and sedation limits its utility. In contrast, levetiracetam's ability to achieve rapid and sustained seizure control with fewer side effects positions it as a strong candidate for first-line therapy. Another study by Falsaperla et al., evaluated levetiracetam as a first-line treatment for neonatal seizures, reporting that all patients responded to treatment without major side effects, suggesting levetiracetam is effective and safe in this context. [25] Additionally, Qiao meta-analysis concluded that while there was no significant difference in efficacy between levetiracetam and phenobarbital for neonatal seizures, levetiracetam was associated with fewer adverse effects, indicating a better safety profile. [8] These studies collectively suggest that levetiracetam is generally well-tolerated in neonates, with only mild and transient side effects reported. Importantly, our study supports this safety profile, as no significant adverse effects were observed.

However, research has explored the neurodevelopmental effects of antiepileptic drugs, including levetiracetam. For instance, a 2007 study found that while several antiepileptic drugs induced region-specific cell death in the immature brain, levetiracetam did not exhibit this effect, even at high doses. [26] Additionally, a 2020 case report detailed behavioral abnormalities in a patient following levetiracetam administration, emphasizing the need for vigilance regarding potential psychiatric side effects. [27] These findings highlight the importance of further longitudinal studies to comprehensively assess the long-term safety of levetiracetam, particularly concerning neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Recent evidence highlights the potential of intravenous levetiracetam in neonatal seizure management. Abend et al. (2011) [13] reported a 56 % seizure control rate when used as a second-line agent, while a 2021 meta-analysis by Qiao et al. [8] found its efficacy comparable to phenobarbital, with fewer adverse effects. However, a randomized controlled trial by Sharpe et al. (2020) [14] demonstrated phenobarbital's superior efficacy, achieving 80 % seizure resolution at 24 h compared to 28 % for levetiracetam. Despite this, phenobarbital's higher side effect profile, including neurodevelopmental risks (Maitre et al., 2013), [15] supports levetiracetam as a safer alternative for long-term outcomes. Additionally, studies like Roa et al. [17] underscore levetiracetam's promise in managing HIE-associated seizures, emphasizing the need for large-scale trials to refine clinical guidelines.

In summary, the results of our study reinforce the efficacy and safety of intravenous levetiracetam in neonatal seizure management, aligning with findings from previous studies. However, variations in dosing protocols, patient populations, and study designs across the literature underscore the importance of conducting large-scale, randomized controlled trials to establish standardized treatment guidelines and further validate its long-term safety and efficacy.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths

This study utilized a retrospective design, allowing for the analysis of real-world clinical data, which enhances the generalizability of findings. The inclusion of diverse seizure etiologies and variations in treatment protocols provides valuable insights for clinical practice.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The single-center design and relatively small sample size may restrict the generalizability and statistical power of the results. The retrospective nature introduces potential biases, including variability in data recording and unmeasured confounding factors. Additionally, the lack of long-term follow-up precludes assessment of levetiracetam's impact on neurodevelopmental outcomes. The study did not directly compare levetiracetam with other antiepileptic drugs like phenobarbital, nor did it investigate biomarkers or genetic factors influencing treatment response. Future research addressing these limitations through multicenter, randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes, standardized protocols, and extended follow-up is essential to validate and expand upon these findings.

Conclusion

This study reaffirms the efficacy and safety of intravenous levetiracetam as a first-line treatment for neonatal clinical seizures, demonstrating high rates of seizure control and minimal adverse effects. These findings align with existing evidence and further underscore levetiracetam's potential as a superior alternative to traditional antiepileptic drugs, particularly for neonates with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy. However, the lack of significant predictive markers for treatment outcomes highlights the need for further research into optimizing treatment strategies. Future multicenter randomized controlled trials are essential to validate these results, establish standardized dosing protocols, and evaluate long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes associated with levetiracetam use in neonates.

Future directions in neonatal seizure management with Levetiracetam

This study underscores the potential of intravenous levetiracetam in managing neonatal seizures while highlighting areas requiring further research to optimize its clinical application. Multicenter randomized controlled trials are essential to confirm its efficacy and safety across diverse clinical settings and populations. Comparative studies against standard antiepileptic drugs, such as phenobarbital and phenytoin, should evaluate both immediate seizure resolution and long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes. Additionally, research must focus on establishing optimal dosing regimens tailored to specific subgroups, such as neonates with HIE. Longitudinal studies assessing cognitive, motor, and behavioral development are needed to clarify levetiracetam's impact on long-term neurodevelopment. Investigations into biomarkers and genetic predictors of treatment response could enable personalized therapy, enhancing efficacy while minimizing adverse effects. Understanding the mechanisms of levetiracetam's action in the developing brain and its interaction with various seizure etiologies is critical. Studies focusing on neonates with comorbidities, such as metabolic disorders or respiratory distress, are necessary to evaluate safety and efficacy in high-risk populations. Integrating advanced neuroimaging and EEG techniques could offer deeper insights into levetiracetam's effects on brain activity and seizure networks, further refining its role in neonatal care. By addressing these research priorities, future studies will pave the way for evidence-based guidelines and improved outcomes for neonates with seizures.
