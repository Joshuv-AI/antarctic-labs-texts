Fractional spatiotemporal Hahnfeldt tumor model with convergence analysis and optimal control
Authors: Engin Can
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The mathematical modeling of tumor-immune interactions is pivotal for decoding the complex, multistage processes of cancer progression, yet classical integer-order models often fail to capture the hereditary properties and spatial heterogeneity intrinsic to malignant growth. This research presents a novel fractional spatiotemporal Hahnfeldt tumor model that integrates Caputo fractional derivatives with diffusion mechanisms to characterize memory dependent tumor vascular dynamics. Unlike standard formulations, this model explicitly accounts for the “biological memory” of the immune system and the "sub-diffusive" invasion patterns of tumor cells. We provide a rigorous mathematical analysis, establishing the non-negativity and boundedness of solutions, followed by a stability analysis of the Adams–Bashforth–Moulton predictor–corrector scheme. Numerical simulations reveal that lower fractional order inherently reproduce clinical phenomena such as tumor dormancy and delayed therapeutic response, which are mathematically inaccessible to integer-order counterparts. Furthermore, a fractional optimal control framework, solved via the Forward–Backward Sweep Method, demonstrates that adaptive chemo-immunotherapy significantly outperforms monotherapies by leveraging the system’s memory to sustain remission. These findings offer a theoretically sound and clinically relevant computational tool for predicting long-term treatment outcomes in heterogeneous tissue environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41810-x.

Introduction

Mathematical oncology offers a quantitative framework for comprehending tumor proliferation, forecasting therapeutic results, and refining treatment approaches. Early studies of tumor growth used classical models like the exponential, logistic, and Gompertz growth laws1,2. The Hahnfeldt model3 was the first to connect tumor growth to angiogenic processes in a way that was based on biology. This model is still an important part of cancer modeling because it uses a mechanistic framework to combine tumor volume and vascular carrying capacity4,5.

But integer-order differential equations can’t fully describe the memory and hereditary traits that are inherent in biological systems. Processes with long-term dependencies, delayed feedback, and cumulative toxicity control how tumors, the immune system, and drugs interact. All of these are naturally represented by fractional calculus6–10. The Caputo fractional derivative, specifically, facilitates biologically significant initial conditions, thereby permitting the realistic modeling of systems characterized by temporal memory11–14. Fractional-order tumor models have demonstrated the capacity to replicate extended therapeutic responses and non-exponential decay patterns identified in clinical and experimental investigations15–17.

Despite significant advancements in oncology, the dynamics of tumor-immune competition remain biologically intricate due to uncontrolled growth, sequestered locations, and heterogeneous behaviors. Classical mathematical models often oversimplify these mechanisms, neglecting the multi-stage nature of immune surveillance and the time-delayed responses inherent in biological tissues. To address such complexities, recent researchers have employed diverse mathematical frameworks. For instance, the spatiotemporal dynamics of glioma-immune interactions18,19 and the role of immunotherapy in brain tumor abrogation20,27,28 have highlighted the need for spatial modeling. Similarly, the impact of distributed time delays22,23 and impulse therapy effects24 has been investigated to understand bifurcation and oscillatory dynamics in tumor proliferation25,26. Furthermore, quantifying the role of specific immunotherapeutic agents, such as T11 target structures, has provided dynamical perspectives on malignant progression21. Building upon these foundations, this study introduces a fractional-order approach to unify these aspects memory, delay, and spatial diffusion into a single comprehensive framework, providing a more realistic prediction of tumor dormancy and therapy resistance.

Tumor development is heterogeneous both temporally and spatially. Cancer cells grow, invade, and move through tissues, which causes them to change shape and spread to other parts of the body. Spatio-temporal models that include diffusion and transport phenomena give a more complete picture of how tumors invade29–31. Recent developments in fractional diffusion theory indicate that sub-diffusive processes more precisely characterize anomalous cell migration in heterogeneous tissue environments32–34. Consequently, fractional spatio-temporal systems can encapsulate both memory and heterogeneity, two fundamental characteristics of authentic tumor dynamics. Recent computational studies have further expanded this field; for instance35, analyzed cancer-immune interactions in avascular environments, while36 explored the chaotic dynamics of cancer models with singular kernels. Additionally, numerical analyses of time-fractional models with varying net killing rates have highlighted the importance of accurate discretization schemes37.

In parallel with mechanistic modeling, recent computational advancements have demonstrated the immense potential of integrating fractional calculus with machine learning and hybrid intelligence algorithms to address complex medical challenges. Significant progress has been made in developing hybrid predictive frameworks for systemic diseases; for instance, fractional-order models combined with machine learning have been effectively utilized for heart disease prediction38, Parkinson’s disease classification39, and diabetes risk assessment40. The versatility of these hybrid approaches extends to dermatology and reproductive medicine, as evidenced by studies enhancing psoriasis prediction41 and optimizing in vitro fertilization success rates through dual-approach artificial neural networks and comprehensive numerical analyses42,43. Furthermore, sophisticated techniques such as quantum-inspired neural fuzzy sliding mode control have been applied to model physiological processes like intraocular pressure in glaucoma44. Within the specific domain of oncology, fractional-order models incorporating time delays have also provided deeper insights into lung cancer dynamics45. Collectively, these studies underscore a paradigm shift towards data-driven fractional modeling, providing a rich, contemporary context for the mechanistic spatio-temporal framework proposed in this research.

Optimal control theory makes these models even more useful for predicting and treating diseases. The Pontryagin Maximum Principle has been extensively utilized to establish dosing regimens for chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy46–48. When applied to fractional systems, it produces memory-dependent control laws that account for drug accumulation and immune response latency49–51. Fractional optimal control formulations thus connect mathematical accuracy with clinical flexibility.

Even with these improvements, not many studies have brought together fractional dynamics, spatio-temporal structure, and strict numerical convergence theory into one framework. This study addresses the existing gap by formulating a fractional spatio-temporal Hahnfeldt model, conducting a theoretical convergence analysis of the ABM predictor–corrector scheme, and implementing fractional optimal control in the context of combined chemo-immunotherapy.

Model formulation

Overview

To describe the dynamics of tumor growth, angiogenesis, and therapy response, we extend the classical Hahnfeldt tumor-vascular model into a fractional spatio-temporal framework.

The original Hahnfeldt model3 represents the temporal evolution of tumor volume V(t) and vascular carrying capacity K(t) as7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{dV}{{dt}} = \lambda V\ln \left( \frac{K}{V} \right),\frac{dK}{{dt}} = bV - dK$$\end{document}where λ is the tumor growth rate, b is the rate of angiogenic stimulation, and d is the rate ofvascular degradation.

This system captures the balance between tumor expansion and vascular support but neglects spatial heterogeneity and biological memory. We therefore extend it in two key directions: (i) introducing fractional temporal derivatives to model memory effects, and (ii) including diffusion terms to represent spatial invasion.

Biological interpretation

The fractional derivative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}^{C}D_{t}^{\alpha }$$\end{document} introduces biological memory, reflecting delayed response of tumor and vascular systems to external therapy and microenvironmental changes. The diffusion terms \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_{V} \Delta V$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_{V} \Delta K$$\end{document} model spatial invasion and vascular remodeling, enabling simulation of heterogeneous tumor patterns. The control terms \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$- u_{1} (t)V$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$- u_{2} (t)V$$\end{document} represent cytotoxic and immune-mediated killing, respectively, acting multiplicatively on the tumor population.

Beyond mathematical generalization, the fractional order \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha \in (0,1]$$\end{document} serves as a quantitative index of ‘system memory’ or persistence in biological processes. In the context of pharmacokinetics, a lower \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} value reflects a deviation from standard exponential decay, representing a 'tissue-binding’ effect where the therapeutic agent remains active in the tumor microenvironment longer than predicted by classical clearance rates14. Similarly, regarding the immune component,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} captures the ‘immune memory’ of cytotoxic T-cells. A fractional derivative implies that the immune response at time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document} is weighted by the history of tumor-immune interactions, simulating the sustained activation and gradual exhaustion of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, rather than an instantaneous reaction to current tumor density.

Model reduction and comparison

When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_{V} = D_{K} = 0$$\end{document}, the model reduces to a fractional ODE system, suitable for temporal studies without spatial diffusion. When additionally \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha = 1$$\end{document}, it becomes the classical integer-order Hahnfeldt model, providing a direct baseline for comparison in later simulations. Thus, the proposed system generalizes both the temporal and spatio-temporal behavior of tumor growth under therapeutic influence.

Theoretical framework

This part looks at the math behind the suggested fractional spatio-temporal Hahnfeldt model, with a focus on the Adams–Bashforth-Moulton predictor–corrector (ABM-PC) method, its stability, and how it converges. The conversation moves from temporal fractional ODEs to their spatio-temporal PDE extensions.

Adams–Bashforth-Moulton predictor–corrector scheme

Consider the Caputo initial value problem.

Remark on Proofs

The proofs for existence, uniqueness, and convergence (Theorems 4.1–4.3) follow standard analytical techniques for fractional differential equations involving the generalized Grönwall inequality and Banach fixed-point theorem. Detailed proofs are omitted here for brevity and can be found in standard foundational texts such as Diethelm27 and Li & Zeng29.

Hence, for sufficiently small h, the method preserves the asymptotic stability of the continuous fractional system.

Stability under perturbations

If the fractional Hahnfeldt system is perturbed by bounded additive noise,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}^{C}D_{t}^{\alpha } y(t) = f(y) + \varepsilon g(t,y),$$\end{document} then the deviation between perturbed and nominal trajectories satisfies \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left| {y_{\varepsilon } (t) - y_{t} } \right| \le C\varepsilon$$\end{document} for small \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon$$\end{document}.

This shows that the model and the numerical scheme are very stable even when biological parameters are not known for sure.

The analysis shows that the ABM-PC method gives a stable and consistent numerical base for simulating fractional tumor models. Because the method is so simple, it can be easily combined with spatial diffusion and optimal-control modules. This makes it possible to simulate large-scale heterogeneous tumor growth while still being theoretically sound. These convergence guarantees validate the application of the ABM-PC solver in the ensuing numerical and control experiments.

Numerical stability and verification

The numerical convergence was verified by halving the time step from h = 0.01 to h = 0.005, resulting in relative error reduction consistent with the theoretical order \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(h^{1 + \alpha } )$$\end{document}. No numerical instability was observed for 0.7 ≤ α ≤ 1.0. Spatial resolution tests confirmed second-order accuracy with respect to Δx. Hence, the ABM-PC scheme is both accurate and robust for fractional PDE tumor models.

While the fractional ABM-PC scheme provides superior biological accuracy, it incurs a higher computational cost compared to integer-order solvers. Due to the non-local nature of the fractional operator, the solver must store and process the entire history of the solution at each step, leading to a complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(N^{2} )$$\end{document}(or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(N\log N)$$\end{document} with fast convolution algorithms) compared to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(N)$$\end{document} for classical ODE methods. However, for the time scales considered in tumor growth (days to weeks), this additional cost is negligible on modern computing architectures.

Results and discussion

The numerical experiments delineated in Section "Numerical simulations" validate that the proposed fractional spatio-temporal Hahnfeldt model proficiently encapsulates both the biological memory and spatial heterogeneity of tumor dynamics. In this section, we analyze these results through the lens of theoretical frameworks53,54 and prior research in mathematical oncology.

Impact of fractional dynamics on tumor growth

Fractional-order modeling provides a powerful mechanism for describing delayed yet persistent tumor responses. As observed in Fig. 2, lower fractional orders (α < 1) lead to slower initial tumor proliferation but sustain long-term activity. This behavior stems from the nonlocal property of the Caputo derivative: the rate of change at time t depends on the entire history of the system. Biologically, such memory effects correspond to cell-cycle heterogeneity, delayed vascular remodeling, and immune adaptation, which cannot be reproduced by memoryless (integer-order) models55,56.

This outcome corresponds with recent studies by57,58, which demonstrated that fractional models produce more precise long-term forecasts for tumor regression and recurrence.

Role of spatial diffusion

The introduction of spatial diffusion terms (DVΔV and DKΔK) enables modeling of tumor invasion and vascular dispersion across heterogeneous tissues. As shown in Fig. 3, diffusion produces nonuniform tumor density patterns resembling invasion fronts seen in histopathological data. The diffusion-induced heterogeneity becomes particularly evident when combined with fractional dynamics, leading to slower yet spatially persistent invasion.

From a biophysical perspective, these findings correspond to the sub-diffusive migration mechanisms identified in live-cell imaging studies17,19. Consequently, the fractional spatio-temporal formulation encapsulates both the temporal memory and spatial intricacy of tumor progression, representing a crucial advancement towards realistic modeling.

Comparison with the classical Hahnfeldt model

The classical Hahnfeldt model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( {\alpha = {1}} \right)$$\end{document} accurately describes short-term angiogenic interactions but fails to reproduce long-term persistence and therapy-induced dormancy. In contrast, the fractional model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( {\alpha = 0.{8}} \right)$$\end{document} produces delayed responses and extended low-activity phases (Fig. 5), consistent with the tumor dormancy hypothesis discussed by Byrne4 and Bellomo et al.5. The inclusion of fractional memory essentially introduces an implicit feedback delay, stabilizing the tumor vasculature subsystem and preventing unrealistically fast regrowth.

Quantitatively, fractional simulations reduce the steady-state tumor volume by approximately 25–30% compared to the classical case, under identical therapy protocols.

This demonstrates that fractional effects are not merely numerical artifacts but have measurable biological implications.

Therapeutic optimization and control insights

The integration of fractional optimal control provides deeper insight into therapeutic strategies. As summarized in Table 3, the combined chemo-immunotherapy protocol achieves the strongest suppression of tumor growth. Fractional dynamics enhance the persistence of therapeutic effects even after drug withdrawal, owing to memory-dependent control laws in the fractional Pontryagin framework.

This property has direct clinical significance; it implies that fractional-order dynamics naturally model drug retention, immune memory, and post-treatment inhibition, features that are difficult to include in standard ODE-based models.

The results are consistent with the findings of Rehman et al.47 and Sweilam et al.48, who reported similar prolonged suppression in fractional cancer control models59,60.

Sensitivity and robustness

The parameter analysis (Section "Spatio-temporal diffusion patterns") shows that fractional models are less sensitive to small perturbations in biological parameters (λ, b, d). This robustness originates from the smoothing effect of fractional derivatives, which distribute local perturbations over past states.

Consequently, the model remains stable under parameter uncertainty, making it a reliable framework for personalized therapeutic optimization where patient-specific variability is inevitable.

Conclusion

This study established a unified fractional spatio-temporal Hahnfeldt model to elucidate the memory-dependent dynamics of tumor-vascular interactions. By integrating Caputo fractional derivatives with spatial diffusion, we demonstrated that biological memory significantly influences tumor dormancy and therapeutic resistance phenomena that integer-order models fail to capture adequately. Theoretical analysis confirmed the non-negativity, boundedness, and stability of the proposed system, validating the robustness of the Adams–Bashforth-Moulton numerical scheme.

Quantitative simulations revealed that lower fractional orders \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\alpha < 1)$$\end{document} correlate with delayed tumor regression and extended remission phases, offering a mathematical explanation for the “sleeping tumor” hypothesis. Furthermore, the application of fractional optimal control theory highlighted that adaptive chemo-immunotherapy provides superior long-term suppression compared to monotherapies, minimizing drug toxicity while maximizing immune surveillance.

Ultimately, this framework bridges the gap between mathematical abstraction and clinical reality. It suggests that incorporating fractional memory and spatial heterogeneity is essential for the next generation of predictive oncology models, particularly for designing personalized treatment schedules that account for patient-specific immune history and invasion patterns.
