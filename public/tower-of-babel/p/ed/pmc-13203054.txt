Encephalomyocarditis virus evades IFN-mediated antiviral response by RNF149 targeting JAK1 for ubiquitination and degradation
Authors: Peng Ma, Qi Xie, Yixiao Yang, Yujing Song, Mingquan Liu, Jianting Li, Linjie Li, Zheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEncephalomyocarditis virus (EMCV), an important zoonotic pathogen, causes an acute disease characterized primarily by encephalitis and myocarditis. Interferon (IFN) activates the JAK-STAT signaling pathway to induce the expression of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), resulting in antiviral effects. However, the mechanism through which EMCV evades the immune system via the IFN-mediated JAK-STAT signaling pathway remains poorly understood. Here, we identified an E3 ubiquitin ligase, RNF149, that is upregulated in EMCV-infected cells. Overexpression of RNF149 inhibited type I IFN-mediated JAK-STAT signaling pathway activation and its antiviral response, enhancing viral replication. Knockout of RNF149 promoted ISGs expression. Notably, RNF149 interacted with JAK1 and downregulated its protein expression through the E3 ubiquitin ligase. RNF149 promoted the K27- and K33-linked ubiquitination of JAK1, which promoted JAK1 degradation through the proteasome pathway. Taken together, these data describe a negative regulatory mechanism involving RNF149 in interferon antiviral activity and provide insights into the mechanism by which EMCV evades host antiviral immunity. These results provide a new strategy for treating viral infections.KEYWORDS: Encephalomyocarditis virus, RNF149, interferon, JAK1, ubiquitination

Introduction

Encephalomyocarditis virus (EMCV) is an important zoonotic pathogen that belongs to the Cardiovirus genus within the Picornaviridae family [1,2]. It is a nonenveloped, single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus with a genome of approximately 7.8 kb and contains a single open reading frame (ORF) and 5’ and 3’ untranslated regions [3]. EMCV infection causes an acute disease characterized primarily by encephalitis and myocarditis in mammals, rodents, and even primates [4]. Currently, EMCV is globally distributed and has a broad host range. It has been isolated from various wildlife species, including pigs, rodents, and primates, as well as humans, indicating its potential risk to public health and safety [5,6].

The innate immune system is the first line of host defense against viral infection, and interferons (IFNs) are recognized as essential components of the innate immune response [7]. Type I IFN (IFNI) exerts its antiviral effect by binding to the interferon receptor complex, IFNAR1/ IFNAR2, on cell surface [8–10]. The JAK-STAT signaling pathway primarily consists of three components: tyrosine kinase-associated receptors, Janus kinases (JAKs), and signal transducers and activators of transcription (STATs) [8,11]. In signal transduction, Upon IFN-I engagement, IFNAR1/IFNAR2 activate JAK1/TYK2, which phosphorylates STAT1 and STAT2, promoting their heterodimerization. The STAT1-STAT2 dimer then recruits IRF9 to form the ISGF3 complex, which translocates to the nucleus and binds to interferon-stimulated response elements (ISREs) [12]. This binding drives many intrinsic antiviral ISGs, such as 2”−5”-oligoadenylate synthetase (OAS) and Myxovirus resistance protein 1 (MX1) [13]. These ISGs can rapidly inhibit and clear intracellular viruses.

Picornaviruses possess mechanisms to antagonize the antiviral JAK-STAT signaling pathway, enabling immune evasion and persistent survival within the host. In enterovirus 71 (EV71) -infected cells, the 2A protein induces the degradation of the interferon receptor IFNAR1, thereby blocking IFN-mediated phosphorylation of JAK1, TYK2, STAT1, and STAT2 and antagonizing the host IFN response [14]. EV71 infection can also induce the proteasomal degradation of JAK1 to prevent its phosphorylation in MRC-5 and RD cells [15]. Similarly, foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV) VP3 can induce JAK1 degradation, disrupting the formation of the JAK1 complex via the lysosomal pathway [16]. FMDV VP3 also interacts with JAK1/2 to block STAT1 phosphorylation, dimerization, and nuclear translocation [17]. Therefore, the inhibition of the JAK-STAT pathway following picornavirus infection involves numerous complex mechanisms [18]. However, research on EMCV in this context remains scarce.

RNF149 belongs to the RING finger protein family. Its structure contains a conserved N-terminal RING domain, which has been demonstrated to possess E3 ubiquitin ligase activity [19]. This activity enables RNF149 to catalyze the conjugation of ubiquitin (Ub) or ubiquitin-like molecules to substrate proteins, thereby regulating diverse biological processes, such as intracellular protein degradation and signal transduction [20,21]. For instance, in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, the major methyltransferase cotransporter MFSD4A recruits RNF149 to mediate the ubiquitination and degradation of EPHA2, thereby inhibiting the proliferation, migration, and invasion of nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells [22]. Additionally, RNF149 acts as a pivotal regulator in antiviral innate immunity. RNF149 functions in antiviral responses through K27- and K33-linked ubiquitination to degrade IRF3, thereby decreasing IFN-β production [23]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that RNF149 performs its protein degradation function and regulates the antiviral response through its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. Specifically, the function of RNF149 in the IFN-JAK-STAT signaling cascade has not been defined.

In this study, we investigated the regulatory role of RNF149 in IFN-mediated innate antiviral immunity. RNF149 was observed to interact with JAK1 and promote its K27- and K33-linked polyubiquitination, thereby downregulating JAK1 protein levels, suppressing IFN-mediated production of downstream ISGs, and diminishing the host antiviral response. Furthermore, the 1–559 aa region of JAK1 was identified as the critical domain for RNF149-induced polyubiquitination. The regulatory effects of RNF149 on viral infection were observed to depend on its E3 ubiquitin ligase. Our findings indicate that RNF149 plays a pivotal role in suppressing the JAK-STAT pathway during EMCV infection to regulate viral replication, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for restoring antiviral innate immunity.

Materials and methods

Cells, viruses, and antibodies

The A549 cell line is a human non-small cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC) cell line derived from a lung adenocarcinoma and can be used for EMCV infection experiments [24]. The A549 cell lines were kindly provided by Dr. Xiaoxin Fang (First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University, China). HEK293T cells were previously maintained by our laboratory. All cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Cellmax, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cellmax, China) and 100 IU/mL penicillin‒streptomycin solution (Cellmax, China). All cells were cultured in an incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2.

EMCV strain PV21 was purchased from ATCC (GenBank accession no. X74312). Recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus expressing green fluorescent protein (VSV-GFP) and Sendai virus (SeV) was kindly provided by Prof. Xin Cao (Jilin Agricultural University, China).

The anti-RNF149 mouse monoclonal antibody (D197155-0100) was purchased from Sangon Biotech (China) and used at a 1:3000 dilution for Western blotting. The JAK1 monoclonal antibody (66466–1-Ig), beta-actin monoclonal antibody (66009–1-Ig), MYC tag polyclonal antibody (16286–1-AP), DYKDDDDK tag polyclonal antibody (20543–1-AP), and His-tag polyclonal antibody (10001–0-AP) were purchased from Proteintech (China). For Western blot analysis, these primary antibodies were used at the following dilutions: JAK1 at 1:2000, beta-actin at 1:5000, MYC tag at 1:3000, DYKDDDDK tag at 1:3000, and His-tag at 1:3000. The mouse anti-rabbit IgG LCS (A25022) and goat anti-rabbit IgG HCS (A25222) secondary antibodies were purchased from Abbkine Scientific (China) and both were used at a 1:5000 dilution for Western blotting.

EMCV infection and viral titer determination

The encephalomyocarditis virus (EMCV) was propagated in A549 cells cultured in DMEM supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS). When extensive cytopathic effect (CPE) was observed, cells were harvested, freeze-thawed three times, and clarified by centrifugation. The supernatant was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C.

Viral titers were quantified using a TCID50 (50% tissue culture infectious dose) assay. Briefly, 10-fold serial dilutions of EMCV were inoculated into A549 cells seeded in 96-well tissue culture plates at 5,000 cells per well. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 4–6 days post-infection (dpi), after which cytopathic effects (CPE) in each well were observed under light microscopy. The viral titer was determined by counting wells exhibiting obvious CPE and calculated using the Reed – Muench method [25].

Plasmids

Full-length Homo sapiens RNF149 was amplified from HEK293T cells and cloned into pcDNA3.1-Flag and pEGFP-C2. Full-length JAK1 was amplified from HEK293T cells and cloned into pcDNA3.1-Myc and pcDNA3.1-mcherry.

To investigate the functional domains and E3 ligase activity of RNF149, we generated five mutants. Two point mutations were introduced in the RING-finger domain: C269A/C272A, which inactivates the ubiquitin ligase [26]. Additionally, H289A, a conserved residue critical for ligase activity [21]. RNF149 truncation mutants RNF1491–221, RNF14967–310, and RNF149176–400 were constructed to delete specific regions. All mutants were cloned into the pcDNA3.1-Flag vector. JAK1 mutants JAK11–559, JAK1560–1154, and JAK1851–1154 were cloned into pcDNA3.1-Myc. Myc-TYK2, Myc-STAT1, Myc-STAT2, Myc-IRF9, His-Ub-WT, His-Ub-K6, His-Ub-K11, His-Ub-K27, His-Ub-K29, His-Ub-K33, His-Ub-K48, His-Ub-K63, His-Ub-K27R, and His-Ub-K33R, pIFN-β-Luc, pISRE-Luc, pNF-κB-Luc, pSTAT1-Luc and pRL-TK were purchased from Miaoling Biotechnology (China). All plasmids were verified by Sanger sequencing (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) before being used in transfection experiments.

Transfection and dual-luciferase reporter assays

To evaluate the effect of RNF149 on the activation of key promoters in the type I interferon signaling pathway, dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed. The HEK293T cells were plated in 24-well plates (1 × 105 cell/mL) for 24 h. Then, co-transfected with Luciferase reporter plasmids pIFN-β-Luc, pISRE-Luc, pNF-κB-Luc, or pSTAT1-Luc and pRL-TK plasmid, along with the indicated amount of control plasmid or plasmids expressing RNF149 used Lipofectamine 8000 (Beyotime Biotech, Beijing, China) with the indicated amount of expression construct, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. At 24 h post-transfection, the cells were untreated or treated with, IFN-α or IFN-β for another 12 h. Following lysis, the activities of the firefly and renilla luciferase reporters in the whole-cell extracts were assessed using a commercial dual-luciferase assay kit (Beyotime, Beijing, China) as per the manufacturer’s guidelines. The relative promoter activity was calculated as the ratio of firefly luciferase activity to Renilla luciferase activity.

Differential expression analysis and GO and KEGG enrichment analysis

Gene expression levels between the two groups (three biological replicates per group) were compared using the DESeq2 package (v1.20.0) in R [27]. This method models count data with a negative binomial distribution and adjusts for multiple testing using the Benjamini – Hochberg procedure. Genes with an adjusted P‑value < 0.05 and | log2 (fold change) |≥1 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Differentially expressed genes were analyzed using the GO database with a significance threshold of p < 0.05 to obtain the distribution of target genes across the three functional categories: cellular component (CC), molecular function (MF), and biological process (BP), thereby assessing gene enrichment. The KEGG database was used to analyze the functions of the target genes, also with a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

VSV-GFP replication assay

To evaluate the effect of RNF149 knockdown on VSV replication, HEK293T cells were seeded in 24-well plates (1 × 105 cell/mL) and transfected with siRNA targeting RNF149 (siRNF149) or control siRNA using Lipofectamine 8000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 hpt, cells were infected with vesicular stomatitis virus expressing green fluorescent protein (VSV-GFP) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01. The virus was allowed to adsorb for 2 h at 37 °C, after which the inoculum was removed and cells were washed twice with PBS and cultured in fresh DMEM containing 2% FBS. At 12 h post-infection, GFP fluorescence, which directly reflects viral replication, was visualized and photographed using a fluorescence microscope.

Confocal fluorescence microscopy

The cells were seeded on glass coverslips in 35-mm cell culture dishes and cultured overnight. Thereafter, the HEK293T cells were co-transfected with pEGFP-C2-RNF149 and pcDNA3.1-mcherry-JAK1. After 24 h, the cells were washed three times with cold phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with DAPI at 37°C for 10 min and washed with cold PBS. Finally, images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus FV3000).

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Quantitative real-time PCR was then conducted using a Bio-Rad system with ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech) in a 20-μL reaction mixture containing specific primers. The amplification profile consisted of an initial 10-min denaturation at 95°C, and 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s, 60°C for 20 s, and 72°C for 15 s. Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and analyzed via the 2^(–ΔΔCT) method. Refer to Table 2 for the primer sequences.

Protein extraction and Western blot

Following collection, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer. The lysates were combined with SDS-PAGE loading buffer and heat-denatured at 95°C for 10 minutes. Proteins were resolved on 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels at 120 V for 100 minutes and then electrophoretically transferred onto a 0.22 µm PVDF membrane (Beyotime) at 200 mA for 1.5 hours and used M5 HiClear Prestained Protein Ladder (MF212, Mei5bio, China) as standard marker. The membrane was blocked with 1% BSA in TBST for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by an overnight incubation with a primary antibody (1:1000) at 4°C. After three TBST washes, the membrane was incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:2000) for 2 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were ultimately visualized using the BeyoECL Moon chemiluminescent system (Beyotime) after a final series of washes.

Co-immunoprecipitation assays

The HEK293T cells (1 × 105 cells/mL) cultured in 24-well plates were co-transfected with the following plasmids: Flag-RNF149, Flag-RNF1491–221, Flag-RNF14967–310, or Flag-RNF149176–400, and Myc-JAK1; Myc-JAK11–559, Myc-JAK1560–1154, or Myc-JAK1851–1154 and Flag-RNF149 using Lipofectamine 8000. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, cells were lysed on ice for 30 minutes using RIPA lysis buffer (500 µL/well) containing 1 mM PMSF (Beyotime). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 30 minutes at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. A 25% of the supernatant was reserved as input, and the remainder was subjected to co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) with anti-Flag agarose affinity beads (Beyotime). Prior to use, the beads (50 µL) was washed with cold TBS after a brief centrifugation. The pre-treated gel was incubated with the lysate overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Finally, the beads were washed extensively with cold TBS, and the immobilized proteins were eluted for Western blot analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software (student t-test, GraphPad Prism software, GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

RNF149 expression is upregulated by EMCV

The RNF family is the largest family of E3 ubiquitin ligases that contain a RING domain and mediate protein ubiquitination is essential for virus-host interactions. To analyze the RNF gene expression pattern following EMCV infection, we used two cell lines, HEK293T and A549, with EMCV infection. At 24 hours post-infection with EMCV, the RNF gene mRNA expression level was measured by RT-qPCR. The results revealed that RNF6, RNF19B, RNF24, RNF123, RNF149, RNF157, RNF182, and RNF186 were upregulated in both EMCV-infected A549 (Figure 1(A)) and HEK293T cells (Figure 1(C)). RNF14, RNF43, RNF152, RNF207, RNF208, and RNF224 were downregulated by EMCV (Figure 1(B,D)). Interestingly, RNF149 expression was significantly upregulated upon EMCV infection. To explore the RNF149 protein expression level, we also used EMCV to infect A549 and HEK293T cells. Afterward, the RNF149 protein level was measured by Western blotting at 6 h, 9 h, 12 h, and 24 h post-infection. Western blotting revealed that EMCV infection increased RNF149 protein expression at different time points in A549 (Figure 1(E)) and HEK293T (Figure 1(F)) cells, similar to the changes observed at the transcriptional level. These results indicate that RNF149 is a potential host factor for the regulation of viral replication.

RNF149 promotes EMCV replication

To better understand the function of RNF149 in viral replication, we designed siRNAs targeting RNF149 to knockdown its expression in A549 cells, RNF149 protein levels decreased after transfection with RNF149 siRNA. siRNA-4# efficiently downregulated RNF149 protein expression (Figure 2(A)). Compared with siRNA-ctrl, knockdown of RNF149 reduced the replication of EMCV in A549 cells. The viral titers was significantly reduced after siRNA transfection (Figure 2(B)). To further verify this phenomenon, we knocked down RNF149 in HEK293T cells by transfection with siRNF149, which revealed that the absence of RNF149 also suppressed EMCV replication in HEK293T cells (Figure 2(C)). Furthermore, recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus expressing green fluorescent protein (VSV-GFP) was introduced into cells to assess VSV replication via fluorescence microscopy. The GFP levels in cells was significantly repressed in HEK293T cells transfected with RNF149 siRNA (Figure 2(D)). Additionally, the level of VSV mRNA was inhibited (Figure 2(E)).

To verify the function of RNF149 in viral replication, RNF149 was overexpressed in A549 and HEK293T cells, which were subsequently infected with EMCV. RNF149 overexpression promoted EMCV replication in both A549 cells (Figure 2(F)) and HEK293T cells (Figure 2H) and increased the viral titers in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 2(G,I)). Overall, we demonstrated that RNF149, as a regulatory gene in viral replication, can promote viral replication.

RNF149 inhibits type I IFN-mediated signaling

To elucidate whether the RNF149 protein may regulate IFN-mediated signaling pathway, HEK293T cells were transfected with IFN-β, ISRE and NF-κB-luc in addition to RNF149 expression plasmids and stimulated with Sendai virus (SeV) before being subjected to a dual-luciferase assay. The results of this assay revealed that the RNF149 protein inhibited SeV-induced IFN-β (Figure S1A), ISRE (Figure S1B) and NF-κB (Figure S1C) promoter activity in a dose-dependent manner. RNF149 protein expression was then detected by Western blotting (Figure S1D).

We hypothesized that RNF149 promotes viral replication through inhibition of the IFN-mediated downstream antiviral response. To verify this hypothesis, HEK293T cells were transfected with plasmids containing RNF149, ISRE and STAT1 promoter region and stimulated with recombinant IFN-α or IFN-β protein. The dual-luciferase assay results revealed that RNF149 overexpression inhibited the activation of the ISRE and STAT1 promoters triggered by IFN-α (Figure 3(A)) or IFN-β (Figure 3(B)) in a dose-dependent manner. Additionally, IFN-triggered transcription of ISGs was measured by RT‒qPCR analysis. The results indicated that RNF149 overexpression significantly inhibited the expression of MX dynamin-like GTPase 1 (MX1) (Figure 3(C)), interferon-stimulated gene 15 (ISG15) (Figure 3(D)), 2”−5”-oligoadenylate synthetase-like (OASL) (Figure 3(E)), and interferon-stimulated gene 56 (ISG56) (Figure 3(F)) mRNA levels. Recombinant IFN-α and IFN-β proteins are well-known antiviral drugs used clinically to treat viral infections. Therefore, we investigated whether the RNF149 protein inhibits the antiviral effect of IFN-α and IFN-β in vitro. We first transfected the RNF149 plasmid and then infected the cells with VSV-GFP. Subsequently, the cells were treated with recombinant IFN-α and IFN-β proteins. Treatment with IFN-α and IFN-β proteins significantly suppressed VSV-GFP replication in HEK293T cells. However, overexpression of RNF149 led to an increase in green fluorescence intensity, indicating that RNF149 promotes VSV replication (Figure 3(G)). Taken together, these data suggest that the RNF149 protein is a generally negative regulator of the IFN-triggered antiviral response.

RNF149 deficiency promotes JAK-STAT signaling pathway activation

To further investigate the regulatory role of RNF149 in the antiviral response, RNF149−knockout HEK293T cells (RNF149-KO) were constructed using CRISPR-Cas9 technology. RNF149 protein expression was measured by Western blotting (Figure 4(A)). To investigate the role of RNF149 in EMCV replication, the RNF149-KO and wild-type cells (RNF149-WT) were infected with EMCV. Compared with wild-type cells, knockout of RNF149 reduced the replication of EMCV and viral titers (Figure 4(B,C)). RNF149 inhibits the JAK-STAT signaling pathway through JAK1

To determine the adaptor protein through which RNF149 regulates JAK-STAT signaling pathway inhibition, we transfected RNF149-overexpressing cells with plasmids encoding JAK1, TYK2, STAT1, STAT2, or IRF9. RNF149 overexpression inhibited the activation of the ISRE promoter triggered by JAK1 (Figure 5(A)) but not TYK2 (Figure 5(B)), STAT1 (Figure 5(C)), STAT2 (Figure 5(D)), or IRF9 (Figure 5(E)) expression. Next, we hypothesized that RNF149 interacts with JAK1. To test this hypothesis, we co-transfected RNF149 with JAK1 and performed coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments, which indicated that JAK1 specifically interacted with RNF149 (Figure 5(F)). Confocal microscopy confirmed that RNF149 and JAK1 were colocalized in the cytoplasm (Figure 5(G)). To further assess the impact of JAK1 on EMCV replication and ISG induction, we then overexpressed JAK1 significantly inhibited EMCV replication (Figure S3A). Moreover, in RNF149-KO cells, JAK1 overexpression markedly upregulated ISG expression, include MX1 (Figure S3B), ISG15 (Figure S3C), OASL (Figure S3D), and ISG56 (Figure S3E). Collectively, these data demonstrate that JAK1 is the specific target of the RNF149 protein.

RNF149 degradation of JAK1 depends on E3 ubiquitin ligase activity

It has been reported that RNF149, as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, mediates the degradation of target proteins, thereby regulating signaling pathways. In humans, the RNF149 protein belongs to the PA-TM-RING family, which consists of a protease-associated (PA) domain and a RING finger domain separated by a transmembrane (TM) domain [29]. Next, we co-transfected RNF149 truncation mutants with JAK1 and performed coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments. Co-IP experiments indicated that JAK1 specifically interacted with the RNF149 RING finger domain (Figure 7(B)). To verify the RING domain function of RNF149, HEK293T cells were transfected with plasmids expressing RNF149 truncation mutants, the ISRE or STAT1 promoter and then stimulated with recombinant IFN-α or IFN-β protein. The results revealed that the overexpression RING domain inhibited the activation of the ISRE and STAT1 promoters triggered by IFN-α (Figure 7(C)) or IFN-β (Figure 7(D)).

In the RING-finger domain, mutations in residues C269/C272 mediate the inactivation of ubiquitin ligases [26]. Additionally, it has been reported that residue H289 in the RING domain is highly conserved and necessary for ubiquitin ligase activity, and mutation of this histidine to alanine (H289A) results in loss of ubiquitin ligase activity [21]. Next, we constructed RNF149-C269A/C272A and RNF149-H289A plasmids and transfected them into cells, after which protein expression was measured by western blotting (Figure 7(E)). Furthermore, we co-transfected mutant Myc-JAK1 into HEK293T cells. As shown in the results, overexpression of RNF149 significantly reduced the protein level. However, through mutations in C269/C272 and H289, which led to the loss of ubiquitin ligase activity, the protein levels of JAK1 were restored (Figure 7(F)). Furthermore, upon overexpression of the C269A/C272A and H289A mutants, the inhibitory effect of RNF149 on ISRE and STAT1 promoter activity was alleviated (Figure 7(G,H)). Collectively, these results indicate that the regulatory effect of RNF149 on JAK1 depends on its ubiquitin ligase activity.

RNF149 promotes the K27- and K33-linked ubiquitination of JAK1

Protein degradation is among the primary strategies for regulating protein function. At least three systems are involved in protein degradation: the ubiquitin – proteasome pathway, the autophagy – lysosomal pathway, and the caspase-mediate pathway. To investigate the specific pathway through which RNF149 promotes JAK1 degradation, we used specific inhibitors targeting distinct degradation pathways. We found that the proteasome inhibitor MG132 completely blocked RNF149-mediated JAK1 degradation, whereas the autophagy inhibitors 3-MA and BAf A1 and the broad-spectrum caspase inhibitor Z-VAD had no such effect (Figure 8(A)). Moreover, MG132 inhibited RNF149-mediated endogenous JAK1 degradation (Figure 8(B)). These findings indicate that RNF149 mediates JAK1 protein degradation by regulating ubiquitination. To confirm whether RNF149 can ubiquitinate JAK1, we performed a protein ubiquitination assay. The results revealed that RNF149 overexpression significantly increased the ubiquitination of JAK1 (Figure 8(C)).

The 1–559 aa region of JAK1 is critical for efficient RNF149-mediated degradation

JAK family proteins feature seven conserved regions, known as JAK homology (JH) domains [30]. The C-terminal portion encompasses the JH1 (amino acids 851–1154) and JH2 (amino acids 560–851) domains: JH1 is a canonical tyrosine kinase domain, and JH2 represents a pseudokinase domain implicated in regulatory functions [31,32]. The N-terminal half of JAK1, spanning JH3 to JH7, includes a predicted FERM domain and a putative SH2 domain [30]. Previous studies have shown that the N-terminus of JAK1 contains a ubiquitin-like domain [31]. To determine which specific region of JAK1 is required for its ubiquitination and degradation, we designed and generated the following constructs: JAK11–559, JAK1560–1154, and JAK1851–1154 (Figure 9(A)). Next, we co-transfected JAK1 mutants with RNF149 and performed Co-IP experiments, which indicated that the JAK11–559 region specifically interacted with RNF149 (Figure 9(B)). JAK1-1-559aa, but not JAK1-560-1154aa or JAK1-851-1154aa, was extensively degraded by RNF149 (Figure 9(C)). These results confirmed that RNF149 can induce K27- and K33-linked ubiquitination of JAK1. Therefore, we next analyzed which type of ubiquitination could be induced by RNF149 on JAK1-1-559aa. Similarly, overexpression experiments revealed that RNF149 significantly promoted the K27- and K33-linked ubiquitination of JAK1-1-559aa (Figure 9(D)). In summary, we demonstrate that the JAK1-1–559 aa region is the critical domain for RNF-mediated JAK1 degradation through K27- and K33-linked ubiquitination.

Discussion

EMCV is a significant zoonotic virus with a broad host range, which can cause acute myocarditis and potentially heart failure in severe cases. EMCV can counteract the inhibitory antiviral response of the host’s innate immune system through various pathways, such as 3Cpro, 2C, and VP3, thereby promoting immune evasion and enhancing transmission [33]. The current understanding of immune evasion and pathogenesis in picornaviruses remains limited, with different viruses in this family utilizing diverse mechanisms to subvert host antiviral responses. Research has shown that members of the RNF protein family can directly or indirectly participate in virus-host interactions and mediate viral immune escape by coordinating host antiviral immune responses [19]. In this study, we revealed a novel function of the E3 ubiquitin ligase RNF149 in IFN-mediated antiviral immunity during EMCV infection. Notably, RNF149 knockdown was effective at limiting the replication of both EMCV and VSV-GFP, whereas its overexpression promoted viral replication. These findings suggest that the expression level of RNF149 may play a crucial role in viral replication, but the molecular mechanism through which RNF149 regulate the EMCV infection needs to be further refined.

We observed that RNF149 was significantly upregulated in different cell lines after EMCV infection. Notably, EMCV infection typically induces a near-complete shutoff of host Pol-II-dependent mRNA synthesis [34]; therefore, the upregulation of RNF149 expression appears to contradict this phenomenon. However, host transcriptional shutoff is not uniform across all genes; certain genes, particularly those involved in stress responses and immune regulation, may be transiently upregulated even under conditions of global translational repression. For example, EMCV infection has been shown to upregulate TMEM39A in HEK293 and C2C12 cells and DDX56 in HEK293T and A549 cells [35,36]. In fact, the phenomenon of viruses inducing the expression of host genes to facilitate their own replication is well documented. The low MOI used in our study allowed for multistep viral replication and enabled us to capture dynamic changes in host gene expression; under low MOI conditions, viral spread occurs gradually, resulting in a more nuanced host transcriptional response compared to high MOI infections, where the shutoff is more rapid and overwhelming. Furthermore, Li et al. reported that infection both DNA and RNA viruses induced the expression of RNF149 and that RNF149 may promote viral replication by suppressing IFN-β production [23]. These findings support the notion that RNF149 upregulation is a general host response to viral infection.

The antiviral response induced by IFN-I poses a major threat to viral survival, and viruses can use various immune evasion mechanisms to inhibit the JAK-STAT signaling pathway [37]. Previous studies have confirmed that IFNs can suppress the replication of multiple viruses, including coronaviruses and alphaviruses [38]. Therefore, we demonstrated that RNF149 inhibits type I interferon-triggered antiviral responses and promotes viral proliferation. RNF149 significantly inhibited the promoter activity of IFN-β, ISRE, and NF-κB induced by SeV. It was found that RNF protein can regulate the IFN-mediated signaling pathway. Following IFN-I stimulation, RNF2 directly binds to STAT1 in the nucleus, suppressing the expression of ISGs and impairing the antiviral response [39]. Additionally, after infection with SeV or VSV, the interaction between RNF31 (HOIP) and STAT1 is enhanced, which inhibits the antiviral activity of IFN-I [40]. Our findings demonstrate that RNF149 limited the IFN-induced activation of ISRE and STAT1 promoters, and restricted the production of downstream ISGs (MX1, ISG15, OASL and ISG56). Next, RNA-seq revealed that the DEGs in RNF149-knockout cells are enriched primarily in the JAK‒STAT signaling pathway. The ISGs induced by EMCV infection are significantly upregulated in RNF149-knockout cells, and RNF149 deficiency rescued the activity of viral induced ISRE and STAT promoters, suggesting that the negative regulatory effect of RNF149 on JAK-STAT pathway may be a critical mechanism for immune escape in EMCV. Furthermore, RNF149 markedly inhibited IFN-I mediated antiviral activity and enhanced VSV-GFP replication, suggesting that RNF149 may serve as a broad-spectrum immunosuppressive regulator not only in the context of EMCV but also for other RNA viruses. However, our study mainly employed overexpression systems, which may not fully recapitulate endogenous physiological conditions; future validation using knock-in or inducible expression models will be of great value.

In the IFN-mediated JAK-STAT signaling pathway, the adaptor protein JAK1 is a cytoplasmic protein that binds to the cytoplasmic domain of the transmembrane IFN-I receptor [12]. JAK1-deficient mice die at birth and exhibit defects in lymphopoiesis, reduced thymocyte numbers, and a lack of response to IFN-I [41]. In vitro, transcriptome analysis of JAK1-deficient mouse hematopoietic stem cells revealed that its absence significantly affects the expression of STAT1 and STAT2, as well as the expression of IRF family members, leading to reduced sensitivity to IFN-I [42]. Currently, numerous viruses have been found to employ JAK1 targeting as a strategy for immune evasion. During infection by picornaviruses such as FMDV, EMCV, and EV71, the viral 3D protein suppresses the JAK1-mediated antiviral response to facilitate immune escape [18]. Additionally, the picornavirus, Seneca Valley virus (SVV) has been shown to antagonize the type I interferon response through its 3C protease, which targets the STAT1-STAT2-IRF9 complex and KPNA1 signals [43,44]. Similarly, the polymerase protein PB2 of influenza A virus (IAV) negatively regulates JAK1 by mediating its polyubiquitination and degradation, thereby attenuating the interferon-induced antiviral response [45]. In our previous study, we demonstrated that infectious bronchitis virus Nsp14 interacts with JAK1 and induces its degradation, leading to the inhibition of IFN-mediated antiviral activity [28]. In our study, following stimulation by adaptor proteins of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, RNF149 specifically suppressed JAK1-mediated ISRE and ISG activation. Moreover, RNF149 interacted with JAK1 but not TYK2, STAT1/2, or IRF9. Interestingly, overexpression of RNF149 significantly reduced the protein levels of both endogenous and exogenous JAK1 without affecting its transcript level. These findings indicate that RNF149 promotes the degradation of the JAK1 protein.

Our finding that RNF149 promotes JAK1 degradation enriches the growing understanding of how EMCV targets the JAK-STAT pathway. Beyond inhibiting JAK-STAT signaling downstream of the IFN-α/β receptor, EMCV also actively suppresses the transcriptional production of type I interferons. Several EMCV proteins target upstream IFN‑I induction pathways: the 3C protease cleaves TANK [46], the 2B and 2C proteins antagonize RLR signaling [47,48], and VP2 degrades the RLR sensors MDA5 and MAVS [49]. In addition, EMCV can inhibit IFN production by hijacking host proteins [36,50]. Thus, EMCV employs a dual‑pronged immune evasion strategy: first, it limits the amount of IFN‑I produced by infected cells; second, it impairs cellular responsiveness to any residual IFN‑I by degrading JAK1 via RNF149. This dual‑layered suppression ensures a robust blockade of the antiviral response, with direct targeting of JAK1 serving as an additional fail‑safe mechanism. Our findings on RNF149‑mediated JAK1 degradation therefore complement the known upstream inhibitory mechanisms and provide a more comprehensive view of how EMCV evades host immunity. Nevertheless, our findings are primarily derived from in vitro experiments in immortalized cell lines. Whether RNF149-mediated JAK1 degradation plays a similarly critical role in vivo, particularly during EMCV infection in animals, remains to be explored.

The RING-finger protein family (RNF), whose structure contains a common N-terminal RING-finger domain, is the largest known family of E3 ubiquitin ligases and mediates ubiquitination modifications [19]. Ubiquitination is a widely occurring form of posttranslational modification (PTM) in cells. The process of ubiquitination involves the covalent attachment of ubiquitin to target proteins, achieved through the sequential actions of ubiquitin-activating enzymes (E1), ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes (E2), and ubiquitin ligases (E3) [51]. Ubiquitination modification provides a key mechanism through which viruses antagonize the host JAK-STAT signaling pathway. This modification primarily involves strategies such as substrate mimicry, competitive binding to block E3–substrate interactions, encoding E3s, and hijacking host E3s 51. The RING-finger domain participates in the ubiquitination mediated by RNF proteins [20]. When bound to two zinc atoms, the RING domain forms a RING finger structure similar to the zinc finger structure [52]. This structure provides the structural basis for E2 binding, creates a relatively stable binding region, and effectively mediates the ubiquitination process [52]. Therefore, we constructed RNF mutants that resulted in the inactivation of ubiquitin ligase activity on the basis of previous reports [21,26]. When RNF149 lacked ubiquitin ligase activity, its suppressive effects on both ISRE and STAT1 promoter activity were significantly reduced. These findings support the conclusion that RNF149 mediates the ubiquitination and degradation of JAK1, thereby aligning with its known function within the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway.

The ubiquitination process involves the attachment of one or more ubiquitin molecules to the target protein, which can regulate the activity of functional proteins in time and space and play a decisive role in controlling many biological processes within cells [51]. Ubiquitination results in the formation of polyubiquitin chains by linking any of the seven lysine residues of ubiquitin, K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, and K-63, or the amino-terminal methionine Met-1 of ubiquitin to the carboxy-terminal glycine residue of another ubiquitin molecule [51,53]. RNF167 belongs to the PA-TM-RING subfamily of the RING-finger protein family, which includes RNF149 [54]. RNF167 mediates the degradation of RIG-I/MDA5 through K6- and K11-linked polyubiquitination [55]. Moreover, RNF149 suppresses the antiviral innate immune response by mediating K27- and K33-linked ubiquitination of IRF3 [23]. Here, we showed that RNF149 promotes the K27- and K33-linked ubiquitination of JAK1, thereby mediating its degradation. While we have demonstrated that RNF149 specifically interacts with JAK1 and promotes its K27- and K33-linked ubiquitination, the precise molecular determinants governing substrate selectivity within the JAK family have yet to be fully elucidated. Resolving these questions in future work will further strengthen the conclusion that RNF149 functions as an important negative regulator of type I interferon signaling.

In summary, we propose a working model in which EMCV upregulates RNF149 protein expression to evade the IFN-mediated antiviral response. The RNF149 protein targets JAK1 for degradation through the proteasome pathway, thereby blocking the JAK-STAT signaling pathway (Figure 10). The protein-mediated degradation of JAK1 is crucial for inhibiting the antiviral interferon response, which provides insights into the immune evasion mechanism of EMCV and contributes to the prevention of cross-species transmission risks.

A working model of the role of RNF149 protein in the regulation of IFNs-mediated JAK-STAT signaling pathway to promote EMCV replication.
