Mitigating lipid oxidation in meat products using nano-formulated essential oils and plant extracts: a systematic review
Authors: Amirhossein Abedini, Samira Shokri, Leila Mirmoghtadaie, Saeedeh Shojaee-Aliabadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lipid oxidation limits the shelf life and quality of meat products. Although essential oils and plant extracts are natural antioxidants, their application is restricted by poor stability and solubility. Nanotechnology enhances their efficacy through controlled release. This systematic review, based on predefined keywords and PRISMA-guided screening, evaluated the effects of nanoformulated essential oils and plant extracts on primary and secondary oxidation indices in meat. Analysis of 62 studies showed that nanoemulsions, nanoliposomes, nanocapsules, and nanocoatings consistently reduced peroxide value (PV) and thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS). For example, chili seed oil nanoemulsion lowered TBARS from 2.42 to 0.42 mg/kg; nanoencapsulated thyme oil (50–70 mg/kg) and cinnamon nanoemulsion effectively retarded oxidation. PV reductions ranged from 20 to 70%, and shelf life was extended by 3–60 days with improved sensory acceptability. In conclusion, nanoencapsulation of essential oils and plant extracts is an effective natural strategy for improving oxidative stability in meat products.

Introduction

Meat has been historically recognized as a nutrient-dense food, and its consumption is supported by both cultural traditions and human metabolic adaptations, including the efficient digestion of energy-dense foods (Leroy et al., 2023). Despite recent concerns regarding the health and environmental impacts of meat production, particularly in developed countries (Lescinsky et al., 2022; Parlasca & Qaim, 2022), the nutritional bioavailability of amino acids, vitamins, minerals, and fatty acids in meat justifies its continued role as a major dietary component (Prates, 2025; Smith et al., 2022). Consequently, meat production and processing have expanded considerably, especially in developing nations, increasing both product diversity and consumer access. Based on the OECD-FAO Agricultural Outlook, global per capita meat consumption in 2025 is estimated at around 28.5 kg per person per year and is projected to increase slightly to approximately 29.3 kg by 2034, representing a modest growth of about 3% over the decade (OECD/FAO, 2025). At the same time, total meat consumption is expected to rise significantly, with poultry increasing by about 21%, sheep meat by 16%, beef by 13%, and pork by 5%. Nearly 45% of this growth will occur in upper middle-income countries, while regions with rapid population expansion particularly Africa are projected to experience a 33% increase in total meat consumption (OECD/FAO, 2025). Despite this overall growth, per capita consumption trends vary across regions. In high-income countries, intake is expected to stagnate or slightly decline due to growing health, environmental, and animal welfare concerns. In contrast, demand growth is concentrated in emerging economies. A key global trend is the shift toward poultry, which is expected to account for 62% of the additional meat consumed and provide 45% of total meat protein by 2034, driven by its affordability, favorable nutritional profile, and lower environmental impact compared to red meats. Meanwhile, per capita consumption of beef and pork is projected to remain stable or decline in many regions (OECD/FAO, 2025) However, this expanded production has brought food safety and quality preservation to the forefront of consumer concerns (Das et al., 2019). Among various deterioration pathways, lipid oxidation represents a primary threat to meat quality, as it generates hydroperoxides (measured as Peroxide Value, PV) and secondary products such as aldehydes and ketones (assessed via Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances, TBARS), leading to rancidity, off-flavors, and potential health risks (Domínguez et al., 2019; Fu et al., 2022). Therefore, controlling oxidative reactions during production, storage, and distribution is a critical priority for the meat industry. Synthetic antioxidants have been widely used to mitigate lipid oxidation (Kumar et al., 2015). Nevertheless, growing public awareness of the potential toxicity, carcinogenicity, and metabolic disturbances associated with synthetic additives has driven the search for natural alternatives (Abedini et al., 2022; Abedini et al., 2024). Essential oils and plant extracts, rich in phenolic compounds, terpenes, and flavonoids, have emerged as promising natural antioxidants due to their generally recognized as safe (GRAS) status and strong radical-scavenging activity (Abedini et al., 2023; Pateiro et al., 2018; Smaoui et al., 2022). Despite their potential, the direct application of free essential oils and extracts to meat matrices faces significant limitations, including high volatility, low water solubility, chemical instability, and rapid degradation. Nano-encapsulation including nanoemulsions, nanoliposomes, and nanostructured lipid carriers has recently been proposed as a strategy to overcome these limitations by improving bioavailability, enabling controlled release, and protecting active compounds from environmental stressors. Several primary studies have evaluated the efficacy of nano-formulated essential oils and extracts in retarding lipid oxidation in various meat products (Rather et al., 2024; Kaur et al. 2024b; Azizi et al., 2024; Karimi et al. 2025; Khalili et al., 2025).

In reviewing related articles, Shu et al. (2026) comprehensively reviewed next-generation edible coatings and films, focusing on intelligent design, natural active ingredients, encapsulation methods, and industrial scalability. The present systematic review complements that work by specifically and quantitatively evaluating the effects of nanoformulated essential oils and plant extracts on primary and secondary oxidation indices (PV and TBARS) in meat and meat products. While Shu et al. addressed broad material functionality and smart preservation systems, the present study narrows the focus to lipid oxidation chemistry and antioxidant efficacy, providing a detailed synthesis of quantitative outcomes. Together, these two reviews offer a more complete understanding of natural-agent-based meat preservation from both material design and oxidative stability perspectives (Shu et al., 2026).

However, a comprehensive synthesis of the underlying mechanisms, comparative efficacy of different nano-carrier systems, and the influence of study heterogeneity (e.g., meat type, EO concentration, storage conditions) is currently lacking. Therefore, this systematic review aims to fill this gap by critically investigating the mechanisms by which nano-formulated essential oils and extracts inhibit lipid oxidation in meat and meat products, and by providing a structured overview of the existing evidence base.

Methods

This systematic review followed PRISMA guidelines. All procedures (literature search, inclusion/exclusion criteria application, and data extraction) were independently performed by two authors to ensure rigor and reduce bias.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in English on July 6, 2025. No time restrictions were applied in this systematic review. The databases selected for the search included PubMed, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, and Scopus. The search strategy employed the following keywords: (Nano OR “Nano-essential oil” OR “Nano-extract”) AND (meat OR fish OR chicken OR beef) AND (oxidation OR “Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances” OR TBARs OR “peroxide value” OR PV). In total, 2118 articles were retrieved. Initially, titles and abstracts were screened, and studies not meeting the inclusion criteria were excluded. Subsequently, the full texts of the remaining articles were carefully reviewed by two independent authors (A.A., and S.S.) to extract relevant data.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The two reviewers (A.A., and S·S) searched the keywords in databases independently. Articles were excluded if they were non-original papers such as reviews, book chapters, conference proceedings, short communications, patents, or editorials; if their scope was irrelevant, for example studies not focused on nano-formulated essential oils or nano-extracts or those applied to non-meat products; if they did not assess lipid oxidation or related indicators such as TBARS or peroxide value; if they used conventional essential oils or extracts without nano-encapsulation; if they were not published in English; if they provided insufficient methodological details or lacked extractable data; or if they were duplicate records retrieved from different databases. Inclusion criteria for this systematic review included original articles that investigate nano-formulated essential oils, oils and extracts effects on lipid oxidation in Meat and meat products. All publications that were according to inclusion criteria were considered.

Results

A total of 2118 articles were retrieved from PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and ScienceDirect. After the removal of 498 duplicates, the titles and abstracts of the remaining records were screened, leading to the exclusion of 1492 articles. Subsequently, 128 full-text papers were assessed for eligibility, and the quality of the studies was independently evaluated by two reviewers. Finally, 62 studies met the inclusion criteria. The database search and selection process are illustrated in Fig. 1. Among the 62 studies incorporated in this systematic review, the majority originated from Iran (50%) and China (20.4%). Regarding meat type, red meat accounted for 31.2% of studies, fish/seafood for 26.8%, poultry for 16%, while the remaining categories were less represented. Nanoemulsions were the predominant carrier system (54.8%), followed by nanoliposomes (12.9%) and other nano-encapsulation forms. Thyme/oregano essential oil was the most frequently investigated bioactive compound (19.4%), with cinnamon and clove oils also commonly used. Storage durations were predominantly between 4 and 20 days under refrigerated conditions (2–8 °C), with some studies extending up to 60 days and − 20 °C. Concentrations of essential oils typically ranged from 0.1% to 2%, while plant extracts were applied at 500–2500 ppm. This synthesis reveals concentrated research efforts in Iran, China, Egypt, India, Turkey, Brazil, Tunisia, Belgium, Australia, Poland, Canada, Iraq, New Zealand, and Pakistan on nanoemulsion- and nanoliposome-based delivery of essential oils (including thyme, cinnamon, clove, oregano, cumin, saffron, garlic, bay laurel, and lemon grass) and plant extracts to extend the refrigerated shelf-life of various meat products such as poultry, fish, beef, mutton, pork, lamb, ostrich, shrimp, quail, sausage, and cooked ham.

The descriptive results of screened manuscripts

At this stage, 62 articles were chosen for the systematic review. For each included study, information regarding the country of origin, type of meat, essential oil or extract applied, dosage, storage or shelf-life conditions, funding, and corresponding references was collected in Table 1.

Discussion

Integration of the 62 summarized studies (Table 1) provides strong quantitative evidence that nano-encapsulation significantly enhances the antioxidant efficacy of essential oils and plant extracts in meat products, particularly regarding PV and TBARS.

From a statistical perspective, out of 62 studies, 38 reported PV as an oxidation index. Among these, 33 studies (86.8%) demonstrated that nano-encapsulated treatments significantly (p < 0.05) reduced PV compared to free essential oils or control groups, with a mean reduction of 54.2 ± 11.3% (range: 30–70%). Notably, in 29 of these studies, the PV in nano-treated samples remained below the acceptable limit for meat products (e.g., <10 meq O₂/kg) throughout the entire storage period.

Regarding TBARS, which was reported in 52 out of 62 studies, the inhibitory effect was even more pronounced. In 48 studies (92.3%), nano-encapsulation significantly (p < 0.05) lowered TBARS values, with an average reduction of 61.7 ± 14.2% (range: 38–89%) compared to controls. Importantly, in 41 studies, the TBARS levels in nano-treated samples remained below the sensory threshold for rancidity (0.5–2 mg MDA/kg) until the end of storage, whereas control samples exceeded this threshold within the first half of the storage period in most cases.

Only four studies (for example, Kaur et al., 2024b on crossbred pastes; Dghais et al., 2023 on minced beef) reported no significant difference between free and nano-encapsulated forms, which may be attributed to the specific meat matrix, low initial oxidation, or insufficient encapsulation efficiency. Overall, these integrated data confirm that nano-encapsulation consistently improves oxidative stability in meat products by delaying primary (PV) and secondary (TBARS) lipid oxidation products.

Oxidation

Primary oxidation (PV)

The collected studies consistently show that nano-formulations of essential oils and plant extracts markedly suppress the formation of primary lipid oxidation products, as indicated by PV (Table 1). For instance, in rainbow trout fish stored for 21 days at 4 °C, whey-based coatings enriched with nano-dill essential oil reduced PV to 11.5 mEq/kg compared with 20.9 mEq/kg in the control (Azizi et al., 2024). This pronounced effect can be mechanistically explained by the ability of whey protein to act as a barrier to oxygen penetration while the nano-dill oil ensured controlled release of monoterpenes with radical-scavenging activity directly at the muscle surface. Similarly, ostrich meat treated with an 8% nanocomposite coating containing Myrtus communis oil exhibited a PV of only 1.67 mEq/kg, markedly lower than the untreated group (Karimi et al., 2025). In this case, encapsulation enhanced the stability of the oil's bioactive terpenoids, preventing their evaporation and degradation, while also improving their solubility and distribution across the muscle matrix, which increased their accessibility to lipid radicals.

In sea bass fillets treated with nanoemulsions from various edible oils, PV remained well below the acceptability threshold of 20 mEq O₂/kg during 10 days of storage (Özogul et al., 2016). The nanoemulsion droplets provided large interfacial areas, enabling antioxidants to localize at the oil–water boundary exactly where lipid oxidation initiates thus preventing hydroperoxide generation. Comparable reductions were observed in sardine and trout fillets, where nano-encapsulation of cumin or Zataria multiflora oils significantly delayed peroxide formation, extending shelf life beyond untreated counterparts (Mohammad Hashemi et al., 2023; Homayonpour et al., 2021). This outcome is linked to the improved partitioning of encapsulated phenolics into lipid bilayers, which interrupts the initiation step of oxidation by neutralizing lipid alkyl radicals (Murphy, 2001).

In red meats, nano-encapsulated pomegranate peel extract effectively lowered PV in mutton mince (Khalili et al., 2025), while nanoliposome-encapsulated bay leaf extract prevented PV from exceeding the 5 mEq/kg threshold in beef stored for 16 days (Tometri et al., 2020). In these cases, the liposomal carriers not only acted as protective shells that stabilized phenolics against degradation but also facilitated their slow release into the aqueous-lipid interface, prolonging antioxidant activity throughout storage (Rudzińska et al., 2024).

Notably, cinnamon and curcuma oil nanoemulsions in Tunisian minced beef preserved extremely low PV values, demonstrating the strong efficacy of curcuma nanoemulsion (0.085 mg/kg) compared with the rapid rise in untreated controls (Fig. 3) (Dghais et al., 2023). Here, the improved solubilization of curcuminoids in the nanoemulsion format maximized their interaction with lipid radicals, while their encapsulation protected them from photodegradation (Iqbal et al., 2020). This finding is particularly significant because it illustrates a dose-sparing effect: the nanoemulsion achieved maximal protection at a fraction of the concentration (0.085 mg/kg) that would be required for free curcuma oil, underscoring the enhanced bioavailability of nano-formulated phenolics at the site of oxidation.Fig. 3(A) The impact of cinnamon and turmeric extracts, applied as essential oil (EO) and nanoemulsion (NE), on thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) levels in minced meat (Dghais et al., 2023). The data from processed meat products further refine our mechanistic understanding. In cooked ham, a hybrid nanoclay/clove essential oil (30%) bionanocomposite maintained MDA stable at 0.1 mg/kg for 28 days, whereas the film without nanoclay showed a gradual increase (de Sousa Cândido et al., 2025). This suggests that the nanoclay component provides an additional physical barrier and possibly a surface charge effect that retards oxygen diffusion independently of the essential oil. Similarly, in sausages, chitosan films with nanoencapsulated garlic essential oil (2% v/v) achieved a remarkably low PV of 0.37 and TBARS of 0.47 after 50 days (Esmaeili et al., 2020). Such long-term stability (50 days) indicates that nano-encapsulation can preserve antioxidant activity well beyond the typical 2–3 weeks' storage period, a critical factor for commercial applications. Conversely, a dose-dependent threshold effect was observed in low-fat minced pork, where nano tea seed oil microcapsules (NTM) at 50–70% reduced TBARS, but 90% NTM only marginally improved oxidation (1.90 vs. 2.09 mg/kg control) (Yu et al., 2024). This implies that excessive encapsulation material (propolis/lecithin) may physically hinder the release or interaction of antioxidants with lipid radicals, indicating an optimal loading range exists.

Taken together, these results confirm that nano-encapsulation not only stabilizes essential oils and extracts against volatilization and degradation but also ensures controlled release of active phenolics at critical sites of oxidation. By improving their solubility, protecting against early inactivation, and facilitating positioning at lipid-aqueous interfaces, nano-formulations effectively delay hydroperoxide accumulation. However, a nuanced interpretation of the compiled data reveals four key principles governing efficacy: (1) The biphasic nature of PV (rise then fall) means that nano-formulations primarily extend the lag phase and delay the peak, but do not prevent eventual hydroperoxide decomposition; (2) There exists an optimal loading range (e.g., 50–70% for tea seed oil) beyond which excess encapsulation material may hinder antioxidant release; (3) Matrix specificity is critical-high-fat products (Wagyu) benefit more from nanoemulsions, while leaner products may respond equally to free oils; and (4) The choice between rapid-release (nanoemulsion) and sustained-release (NLC, liposome) formats must be tailored to the oxidation kinetics of the specific meat or seafood product. However, variability across studies such as differences in encapsulation methods (nanoemulsions, liposomes, nanocomposites), storage temperatures, and meat types highlights the importance of matrix-specific optimization.

Secondary oxidation (TBARS)

Secondary oxidation, measured by TBARS, further supports the protective role of nano-formulated essential oils and extracts (Table 1). In chicken breast cubes, nano-emulsified chili seed oil maintained TBARS as low as 0.42–0.66 mg/kg by day 15, whereas the control exceeded the rancidity threshold of 2 mg/kg (Fig. 3) (Rather et al., 2024). This reduction is attributable to the enhanced dispersibility of chili seed oil polyphenols in the muscle matrix, enabling them to quench lipid peroxyl and alkoxyl radicals before they decompose into malondialdehyde and other secondary aldehydes (Schaich, 2022).

Similar reductions were observed in Wagyu pastes, where nanoemulsions of rosemary and mānuka oils produced stronger inhibitory effects than free oils or nanocapsules (Kaur et al., 2024b). Mechanistically, the smaller droplet size in nanoemulsions increased the surface-to-volume ratio, improving interaction between antioxidants and free radicals. Additionally, encapsulation minimized phase separation, which is common with free oils, thereby ensuring a more homogeneous distribution of active compounds throughout the lipid-rich matrix (Perteghella et al., 2023). However, a deeper examination of the data reveals that the relationship between PV (primary oxidation) and TBARS (secondary oxidation) in the presence of nanoformulations is not always linear. For example, in ostrich meat treated with an 8% nanocomposite coating containing Myrtus communis essential oil, PV was remarkably low (1.67 mEq/kg), yet TBARS was relatively higher (1.11 mg MDA/kg) (Karimi et al., 2025). This apparently contradictory finding reveals a critical mechanistic insight: encapsulated bioactive compounds may strongly inhibit the initiation phase of oxidation (hydroperoxide formation), but if the scavenging system cannot neutralize all alkoxyl and peroxyl radicals, some hydroperoxides still decompose into secondary aldehydes. Thus, TBARS is a more direct indicator of cumulative oxidative damage during late storage stages, whereas PV primarily reflects the lag phase of oxidation.

Seafood products again demonstrated pronounced benefits. Rainbow trout fillets treated with whey-based nano-dill essential oil exhibited TBARS of 5.23 mg MDA/kg at day 21 versus 8.34 mg/kg in controls (Azizi et al., 2024). The whey protein carrier provided a film barrier against oxygen and pro-oxidant diffusion, while the encapsulated dill terpenes offered continuous radical scavenging, preventing hydroperoxide breakdown into aldehydes (Cinelli et al., 2014). Likewise, sardine fillets with cumin oil nanocoatings showed TBARS as low as 0.033 mg/kg, maintaining sensory acceptability up to 16 days, while controls became unacceptable after one week (Homayonpour et al., 2021). In fattier fish such as rainbow trout, additional data indicate that the type of nanocarrier plays a decisive role. Nanoemulsion of Zataria multiflora essential oil kept TBARS below the rancidity threshold (2 mg MDA/kg) (Hashemi et al., 2023), whereas in the same species, thymol-loaded nanogelatin (NG-T-N) reduced TBARS to 1.45 mg/kg (Hashemi et al., 2022). This difference in efficacy can be attributed to distinct release kinetics: nanogelatin provides a more gradual release, which is better suited for controlling oxidation in high-fat tissues with large surface area, while nanoemulsions offer a rapid initial effect but may have lower durability over extended storage.

Red meats and processed products demonstrated parallel trends. In beef burgers, thyme oil nanoencapsulation reduced TBARS by up to 89.6%, maintaining values under 0.5 mg/kg for 8 days, while controls exceeded this threshold by day 2 (Ghaderi-Ghahfarokhi et al., 2017). Encapsulation ensured a slower, sustained release of thymol and carvacrol, prolonging their radical-scavenging capacity throughout storage. Cooked ham packed with clove oil bionanocomposite films preserved stable TBARS (0.1 mg/kg) for 28 days, compared with rising values in conventional packaging (Cândido et al., 2025). In this context, the nanocomposite films acted not only as carriers but also as semi-permeable barriers, limiting oxygen diffusion into the product while gradually releasing eugenol (Kocira et al., 2021). Similarly, nano-encapsulated sour tea extract in chicken nuggets limited TBARS accumulation and prolonged acceptability to day 9 (Bahrami Feridoni & Khademi Shurmasti, 2020). The protective effect was linked to the ability of encapsulated anthocyanins to stabilize against pH and light-induced degradation, ensuring prolonged antioxidant functionality (Tan et al., 2021).

In poultry meat, extensive data reveal a non-linear dose-response relationship. In chicken breast fillets, nanoliposomal garlic essential oil (2%) reduced TBARS to approximately 1685 μg/kg by day 14 compared with 2206 μg/kg in controls (Kamkar et al., 2021). However, increasing concentration from 1% to 2% produced no significant difference (p > 0.05), indicating a saturation concentration beyond which additional nanocarriers only have a diluting effect. In contrast, in quail meat, nanoencapsulated thyme oil (50 mg/kg) significantly reduced TBARS compared with the same concentration of free oil (Abd El-Maged et al., 2024), demonstrating that the advantage of nanoformulation is more pronounced in meats with lower saturated fat content. Despite differences in formulations, doses, and storage times, the overall evidence shows that nanoencapsulation consistently retards secondary oxidation by slowing hydroperoxide breakdown into aldehydes and ketones. Mechanistically, this effect arises from enhanced radical scavenging, improved solubility of hydrophobic bioactives, localization at lipid-aqueous interfaces, and physical barrier properties of nano-carriers, all of which synergistically reduce secondary oxidation product accumulation.

Mechanism of action

Inhibition of meat oxidation by nano-encapsulated essential oils (nano-EOs) and extracts involves a multifaceted approach combining chemical and physical mechanisms (Gonçalves et al., 2025). The primary chemical action is the antioxidant activity of phenolic compounds such as thymol, carvacrol, and capsaicin. These compounds directly scavenge lipid radicals (LOO•, L•) by donating hydrogen atoms or electrons, which terminates the oxidation chain reaction (Fig. 4) (Gonçalves et al., 2025). This leads to a significant reduction in primary oxidation products, as measured by PV. Furthermore, nano-EOs can chelate pro-oxidant metal ions like Fe and Cu, thereby inhibiting Fenton-type reactions that convert hydroperoxides into more reactive free radicals (Van Genuchten & Peña, 2017). This slows down the formation of secondary oxidation products, such as aldehydes and ketones, which are measured by TBARS (Papastergiadis et al., 2012). Some compounds also quench singlet oxygen and inhibit pro-oxidant enzymes, further reducing the initiation of oxidation (Papastergiadis et al., 2012). The superior antioxidant performance of nano-encapsulated EOs over their free forms, consistently observed across 62 included studies, can be mechanistically explained through several interconnected pathways. First, the nanoscale size of these formulations (typically 100–200 nm) provides an extremely high surface-area-to-volume ratio, which allows nano-antioxidants to concentrate precisely at the oil-water interface within meat matrices the critical site where lipid oxidation is initiated by free radicals and transition metals (Rather et al., 2024). Free EOs, in contrast, form larger droplets with limited interfacial contact, resulting in lower antioxidant efficiency. For instance, nanoencapsulated thyme oil in beef burgers reduced TBARS by 71–89% compared to free oil at identical concentrations (Ghaderi-Ghahfarokhi et al., 2016). Second, nano-carrier systems including nanoemulsions, nanoliposomes, and nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) physically encapsulate bioactive compounds, protecting them from degradation by heat, oxygen, and light while enabling sustained release throughout storage (Esmaeili et al., 2020; Sedaghat Doost et al., 2019). Thymol nanoemulsions, for example, maintained antioxidant activity in raw chicken breast for 30 days at 4 °C, whereas free thymol would rapidly evaporate (Sedaghat Doost et al., 2019). Third, different nano-carriers exhibit distinct release kinetics that influence their suitability for specific applications. Nanoemulsions provide rapid release ideal for short-term preservation, while NLCs offer slower, sustained release for extended storage (Borhani et al., 2024). Nanoliposomes are particularly effective for hydrophilic plant extracts such as pomegranate peel and bay leaf extracts (Khalili et al., 2025; Tometri et al., 2020), and nanocomposite coatings provide a dual function creating a physical oxygen barrier while actively delivering antioxidants to the meat surface (Karimi et al., 2025; Shiravani et al., 2024). Fourth, the antioxidant potency varies significantly by EO type: thyme and oregano oils (rich in thymol and carvacrol) exhibit the strongest chain-breaking activity due to their phenolic hydroxyl groups, followed by cinnamon and clove oils (Ghaderi-Ghahfarokhi et al., 2016; Xiong et al., 2020). Many nano-EOs also provide synergistic antimicrobial effects, as spoilage microorganisms produce pro-oxidant enzymes that accelerate lipid oxidation (Hussain et al., 2025). Despite considerable heterogeneity among the 62 studies including variations in EO concentrations (0.05% to 25%), meat substrates (fish, poultry, beef, pork, lamb), and storage conditions (4 to 60 days) the collective evidence robustly supports that nano-encapsulation consistently enhances oxidative stability across diverse meat matrices. This generalizability, together with the mechanisms of targeted delivery, controlled release, carrier-dependent kinetics, and differential EO potency, confirms nano-formulations as a superior and versatile strategy for extending the shelf-life of meat products.Fig. 4Mechanism of Nano-formulated essential oils or extracts in preventing lipid oxidation in meat.Fig. 4

Another important issue identified from the synthesized studies in Table 1 is the presence of several contradictions that have direct implications for understanding the underlying mechanism of action of essential oils in meat products. Despite the general consensus that nano-encapsulation enhances the oxidative stability of meat products, the present systematic review identified notable contradictions that preclude a universal ranking of nano-delivery systems. Specifically, the comparative efficacy of nanoemulsions versus nanocapsules (including nanostructured lipid carriers, NLCs) varied substantially depending on the meat matrix, storage duration, and release kinetics.

A striking contradiction emerged from the data synthesized in Table 1. In the study by Kaur et al. (2024b) on Wagyu meat pastes, nanoemulsions were reported as the most effective formulation in reducing TBARS, outperforming both free essential oils and nanocapsules. The authors attributed this superiority to the smaller particle size and higher physical stability of nanoemulsions, which facilitated more rapid interaction with lipid substrates. However, paradoxically, in the same study, when the intervention was applied to crossbred meat pastes, no significant differences were observed among free essential oils, nanoemulsions, and nanocapsules (Kaur et al., 2024b). This within-study contradiction strongly suggests that the intrinsic properties of the meat matrix such as marbling fat content, fatty acid profile, and endogenous antioxidant capacity can completely override the technological advantages of nano-encapsulation.

An additional layer of contradiction is provided by Borhani et al. (2024), who compared thymol-loaded nanoemulsions with thymol-loaded nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) in fresh chicken meat. Although nanoemulsions exhibited faster thymol release due to their smaller droplet size (105.7 nm) and higher encapsulation efficiency (99%), the NLC-coated samples paradoxically achieved lower PV and superior sensory scores during storage. The authors explained that while nanoemulsions provide a burst release of the active compound, NLCs offer a more sustained release profile, which appears to be more beneficial for inhibiting lipid oxidation over extended refrigeration periods (Borhani et al., 2024). This finding contradicts the assumption that faster release equals better antioxidant protection and instead supports the hypothesis that prolonged, controlled release is critical for long-term oxidative stability.

Furthermore, the study by Dghais et al. (2023) on minced beef meat demonstrated that both free curcuma essential oil and its nanoemulsified form maintained equally low TBARS values (0.086 and 0.085 mg/kg, respectively) after 24 h, with no significant difference between the two. This suggests that in very short-term storage (e.g., 24 h), the benefits of nano-encapsulation may not be apparent, as the endogenous antioxidants in fresh meat or the rapid application of the product may overshadow any nano-specific effects (Dghais et al., 2023).

Taken together, these contradictory results indicate that nanoemulsions are not universally superior to nanocapsules, nor is the “nano” form always better than the free form. The choice of nano-delivery system must be tailored to (1) the specific meat matrix (e.g., high-fat Wagyu vs. lean crossbred), (2) the intended shelf-life (short-term vs. long-term storage), and (3) the desired release profile (burst vs. sustained release). Researchers should exercise caution when generalizing findings from one meat product to another, and future systematic reviews should consider matrix-specific subgroup analyses to resolve these contradictions.

Sensory properties

Beyond biochemical indices, sensory attributes provide critical confirmation of the preservative efficacy of nano-formulated essential oils and extracts. In multiple studies, treated samples consistently retained higher scores for color, odor, texture, and overall acceptability compared to controls. For instance, ostrich meat coated with 8% Myrtus communis nanocomposite achieved an acceptability score of 4.6, significantly outperforming controls that showed noticeable rancidity (Karimi et al., 2025). The improved sensory stability is directly linked to reduced lipid oxidation, since rancid aldehydes and ketones are primary drivers of off-flavors. The encapsulation matrix also helped retain the oil's volatile terpenes, preventing rapid aroma loss (Koyama & Heinbockel, 2020). Likewise, sardine fillets treated with nano-cumin oil coatings remained acceptable up to 16 days, whereas controls were rejected after 7 days (Homayonpour et al., 2021). This improvement reflects the direct suppression of oxidation-derived volatiles but also the protective effect of nanoemulsions on myofibrillar proteins and pigments. By limiting oxidative cross-linking and myoglobin degradation, the coatings preserved texture and color, two attributes critical to consumer perception (Homayonpour et al., 2021).

In chicken nuggets enriched with nanoencapsulated sour tea extract, sensory attributes such as taste and odor were preserved despite a slight reduction in color scores, maintaining overall consumer acceptance throughout storage (Bahrami Feridoni & Khademi Shurmasti, 2020). Anthocyanins contributed antioxidant effects but also influenced color, illustrating the delicate balance between functional efficacy and sensory neutrality. Similarly, beef burgers containing nano-thyme oil-maintained odor and color quality up to 8 days, while controls lost acceptability after just 2 days (Ghaderi-Ghahfarokhi et al., 2017). The encapsulation prevented rapid depletion of thymol and carvacrol, prolonging their protective activity against oxidative off-flavors (Ricardo-Rodrigues et al., 2024).

Fish products benefited substantially as well: whey-based nano-dill oil coatings kept rainbow trout sensory-acceptable up to 21 days, contrasting with unacceptable controls by the same timepoint (Azizi et al., 2024). Mechanistically, this outcome reflects the sustained antioxidant release at the muscle surface, which not only delayed rancidity but also stabilized tissue integrity and water-holding capacity, both of which contribute to positive texture perception (Azizi et al., 2024).

Overall, the convergence of chemical indices (PV, TBARS) and sensory outcomes strongly supports nanoencapsulated essential oils as effective natural preservatives in meat systems. Their dual action delaying oxidative spoilage while maintaining consumer-relevant quality traits can be mechanistically traced to (i) localization of antioxidants at lipid interfaces, (ii) gradual and sustained release of active compounds, (iii) barrier effects against oxygen and pro-oxidants, and (iv) stabilization of pigments and proteins against oxidation (Syed A Hussain et al., 2025). Together, these properties explain why encapsulated formulations consistently outperform free essential oils in both chemical stability and sensory preservation.

Future perspectives

The application of nanoencapsulation for preserving meat and seafood products has shown significant promise; however, future research must focus on translating these laboratory successes into scalable, safe, and regulatory-compliant technologies. One important direction is the optimization of green and food-grade encapsulation materials, such as biopolymers and biodegradable lipids, to ensure sustainability and consumer acceptance. Advanced delivery systems, including multilayer nanocarriers and stimuli-responsive nanostructures, could provide targeted and controlled release of antioxidants and antimicrobials in response to pH, temperature, or oxidative stress, thereby extending shelf life more efficiently. Another emerging avenue is the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) in the design and optimization of nanoformulations. AI-driven modeling can predict the physicochemical behavior, stability, and release kinetics of nanocarriers under different storage conditions, drastically reducing experimental trial-and-error. Moreover, data-driven algorithms could help identify optimal combinations of encapsulating materials and bioactive compounds, enabling the creation of tailored nanodelivery systems for specific meat products. Furthermore, combining nanoencapsulation with intelligent packaging technologies represents a promising strategy. Packaging materials embedded with nanosensors could monitor oxidation markers in real time while simultaneously releasing encapsulated bioactives, allowing dynamic protection of food products. From a mechanistic standpoint, the application of omics-based approaches (proteomics, metabolomics, lipidomics) will also provide deeper insights into the interactions between nanocarriers, bioactives, and food matrices.

Despite these advances, several drawbacks of current nano–essential oil applications must be acknowledged and addressed. Sensory issues remain a primary concern, as essential oils (e.g., cinnamon, clove, thyme, or curcuma) often impart strong flavors and odors that can alter the organoleptic properties of meat and seafood products, potentially reducing consumer acceptability. Even at nanoencapsulated doses, residual off-flavors or aroma release during storage may limit practical use. Additionally, cost-related challenges persist. The production of nanoemulsions and nanocarriers requires specialized equipment (e.g., high-pressure homogenizers, ultrasonication systems), energy-intensive processes, and high-purity food-grade biopolymers and surfactants, which can significantly increase final product costs. These economic barriers hinder industrial scalability, particularly for price-sensitive meat and seafood markets.

Therefore, based on current research, several general conclusions can be drawn. First, nanoencapsulation consistently improves the physicochemical stability and controlled release of essential oils compared to free forms, leading to enhanced antioxidant and antimicrobial efficacy. Second, the choice of encapsulating matrix (e.g., polysaccharides, proteins, or lipids) critically determines nanoparticle stability, bioavailability, and interaction with the food matrix. Third, the efficacy of nanoencapsulated systems is highly product-dependent, and formulation parameters must be optimized for each meat or seafood type. Consequently, refined guidance for future studies includes: (i) mandatory sensory evaluation using standardized panels to establish acceptable concentration thresholds; (ii) life-cycle cost analysis and techno-economic assessments to identify feasible production pathways; (iii) development of synergistic blends or masking agents to mitigate off-flavors without compromising bioactivity; and (iv) validation under real industrial storage and distribution conditions rather than laboratory settings alone.

Equally important, long-term toxicological studies and comprehensive risk assessments are essential to address concerns about nanoparticle migration, bioaccumulation, and consumer health. Establishing global regulatory frameworks and harmonized standards will be crucial for industrial adoption. Ultimately, the future of nanoencapsulation in meat preservation lies in the convergence of nanotechnology, AI-driven design, sustainability, safety, and cost-sensory balance, paving the way toward more resilient and healthier food systems (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Future perspectives.Fig. 5

Conclusion

Evidence gathered from multiple studies in the systematic review demonstrates that incorporating plant extracts and essential oils in nano-enabled forms such as nano-emulsions, nan-ocapsules, nano-liposomes, and nano-coatings substantially diminishes lipid oxidation in meat and meat products and contributes to prolonged shelf life. These nano-delivery systems improve the stability of bioactive compounds and facilitate their controlled release, thereby reducing TBARS and peroxide values during storage and limiting the formation of undesirable odors and flavors. Among the evaluated systems, nano-coatings and nano-emulsions showed the strongest antioxidant effects, particularly in turkey and beef, where nano-encapsulated thyme, oregano, or clove oil maintained PV and TBARS at consistently low levels for more than 28 days while preserving key sensory traits such as color, texture, and flavor. Furthermore, nano-encapsulation of beetroot and annatto extracts exhibited notable oxidation inhibition during long-term cold storage, with nano-treated samples showing markedly lower MDA levels well below the 2 mg/kg acceptable limit compared with untreated controls over a 60-day period at 4 °C. Overall, these results indicate that nano-formulations, especially nano-coatings and nano-emulsions, represent an efficient natural preservation approach that enhances oxidative stability, supports sensory quality, and extends the shelf life of meat products more effectively than traditional, non-nano forms of plant extracts and essential oils. As a general conclusion from current research, nano-encapsulation consistently outperforms free essential oils in mitigating lipid oxidation (long time), although efficacy varies with delivery system type and storage duration, with nano-coatings and nano-emulsions providing the most robust protection. Nevertheless, current nano-essential oil applications present notable drawbacks, including residual off-flavors and odors that may compromise the natural sensory profile of meat products, as well as high production costs associated with specialized equipment, energy-intensive processes, and food-grade biopolymers, which remain significant barriers to industrial scalability. Accordingly, refined guidance for future research mandates the integration of standardized sensory evaluation panels and techno-economic assessments alongside antioxidant efficacy studies, alongside the development of cost-effective, green nano-formulations and synergistic masking strategies to balance oxidative stability with consumer acceptability. Despite the comprehensive findings of this systematic review, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, heterogeneity across the included studies in terms of meat matrices, nanoformulation types, essential oil concentrations, storage conditions, and oxidation assessment methods precluded a meta-analytic synthesis of the data. Second, the majority of studies evaluated oxidative stability over relatively short storage periods (typically less than 60 days), limiting conclusions regarding long-term efficacy and stability of nanoformulations. Third, potential migration of nanomaterials from packaging or coatings into meat matrices and associated safety implications have not been systematically investigated. Fourth, industrial scalability remains underexplored, as most studies were conducted at laboratory scale without techno-economic assessments of production costs, energy requirements, or regulatory approval pathways.
