Electronic Visit Use and Resolution Rates for Adult Outpatient Conditions in an Integrated Care Setting
Authors: Tracy A. Lieu, Jie Huang, Khai Tran, Dao Nguyen, Mary E. Reed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study reports on how often patients in an integrated care setting use electronic visits for common outpatient conditions and if the rates of resolution differ from telephone, video, or office visits.

Introduction

Electronic visits (e-visits) are an emerging mode of providing convenient access for outpatient primary care conditions. Such visits have been defined as asynchronous patient messages requiring medical decision-making and at least 5 minutes of clinician time over 7 days.1 E-visits have been made available by some large health care systems as well as by some direct-to-consumer companies as an adjunct or competitor to traditional outpatient care. E-visits can be accessed by patients online 24 hours, 7 days per week without an appointment, and may consist of unstructured secure messages2 or structured questionnaires.

Understanding patterns of e-visit use and outcomes is important because telehealth services have grown rapidly in recent years.3,4 If used effectively, e-visits might help address the ongoing national shortage of primary care physicians because they offer patients convenient access to care. E-visits may speed clinicians’ decision-making by gathering information in writing, especially if structured formats are used. Although the use of e-visits has been increasing,4 scant evidence is available on their outcomes. One study raised concern that clinicians in systems offering direct-to-consumer e-visits may prescribe antibiotics inappropriately.5 Little information exists from settings where e-visits are integrated within patients’ own medical groups.

This study was designed to address gaps in knowledge about the use and outcomes of structured e-visits in settings where patients can choose among multiple modes of care for common outpatient conditions. Its aims were to (1) compare the percentage of initial visits made via e-visits, telephone, video, or office appointments for 4 common outpatient conditions; (2) compare rates of related follow-up visits within 7 days among visit types; and (3) identify patient characteristics associated with e-visit and follow-up visit use.

Methods

Design, Setting, and Population

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at Kaiser Permanente Northern California (KPNC), where The Permanente Medical Group provides care for 4.6 million patients. Kaiser Permanente (KP) is an integrated health care system in which regional medical groups provide comprehensive inpatient and outpatient care for defined populations of members under capitated arrangements with the health insurance system, which handles employer-based commercial insurance, Medicaid, and Medicare.

The study population was defined based on initial outpatient visits (e-visits, telephone or video appointment visits, or physician’s office visits) by persons aged 18 years and older between January 1 and December 31, 2024, for 4 prespecified conditions. Data from 2023 and 2025 were used to analyze selected baseline variables, such as comorbidities, and follow-up visits. To enable complete data capture, initial outpatient visits were excluded from this study if the patient was not an active member or was not continuously enrolled during the 12 months prior to and 1 month after the initial visit.

The Research Determination Committee for KPNC determined that this project did not meet the definition of research involving human participants per 45 CFR 46.102(d) and was exempt from review. This report follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cross-sectional studies.

Electronic and Other Visits

At KPNC, adult patients can freely access e-visits on the home screen of the electronic health record (EHR) patient portal. Clinicians and staff may steer patients to the online location verbally or by sending the webpage link via secure message. Within KP, e-visits are defined as structured online interactions for prespecified conditions in which patients are asked closed-ended questions in a predetermined sequence to gather information related to the chief concern and potential diagnosis. An example is shown in eFigure in Supplement 1. E-visits are available for more than 25 conditions selected based on being common and amenable to a structured decision workflow (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). The algorithm underlying each e-visit uses each patient’s responses to determine eligibility for diagnosis and treatment by a regional virtual medical center physician. Those who are not eligible (for example, if the symptoms may require immediate attention or a direct examination) may receive on-screen instructions to contact the regional call center to schedule a visit. For eligible patients, a virtual medical center physician uses the information collected by the e-visit questions to determine the appropriate diagnosis and action (advice, a prescription medication, and/or a laboratory order) and a follow-up secure message is sent to the patient. Physicians handle e-visit requests from 6 am to 10 pm and patients are told they can expect to receive replies within 4 hours; in reality, the response time is usually 30 to 60 minutes.

Patients can choose other visit modalities, including telephone, video, and office visits, by using the online EHR portal or the regional call center to schedule an appointment. In addition, for selected conditions such as urinary tract infection (UTI) symptoms, patients who contact the regional call center may receive a diagnosis and clinical action via a structured telephone treatment protocol with a nurse working with a physician. In all visit modalities, including e-visits, eligibility for diagnosis and treatment is determined by a physician after relevant information is collected.

Data Sources and Variables

International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes in EHR data (eTable 2 in Supplement 1) were used to identify all adult outpatient encounters during the study period for 4 conditions: UTI, seasonal allergy, acne, and international travel. These conditions were selected based on being common, affecting adults of varying ages, and having the potential to lead to follow-up care beyond the initial encounter. Conditions were selected to represent a range of issues so that commonalities and contrasts could be identified. The UTI e-visit was introduced in 2018, seasonal allergy in 2020, acne in 2022, and international travel in 2021. The international travel e-visit provides a predeparture risk assessment for travel-related illnesses based on personal medical history, destinations, and planned activities. Travelers at low risk and medium risk are provided vaccines and medications, while those at highest risk for travel-related illnesses are advised to follow up with travel medicine specialists. Encounters were defined as visits that resulted in a diagnosis; thus, e-visits and other visits that did not lead to a diagnosis were not included in the analysis.

An initial or index visit was defined as one without a visit or medication for the condition of interest (acne or seasonal allergy) within the prior 365 days. Follow-up visits for the same or related conditions were identified using the original ICD-10 codes supplemented by related codes (eTable 2 in Supplement 1). For UTI, e-visits were only available for females; thus, analyses of UTI were restricted to females for all visit types.

Patient race and ethnicity and preferred spoken language were drawn from computerized data based on self-report. Each patient’s community-level socioeconomic status was based on the Neighborhood Deprivation Index for the geocode for their census tract. The Comorbidity Point Score, version 2, comorbidity score was calculated for each patient based on a standard algorithm.6

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were conducted in unadjusted and adjusted forms using the initial visit as the unit in the primary analysis because a given patient could have made visits for more than 1 of the conditions studied. Adjusted analyses of the percentage of visits made via e-visits or other types of visits used multinomial logistic regression adjusting for patient characteristics, including age group, sex, race or ethnicity, language spoken, insurance type, Neighborhood Deprivation Index quintile, comorbidity score, having ever previously used an e-visit, and having used the KP mobile portal in the prior 365 days. Analyses to identify patient characteristics associated with the likelihood of using an e-visit vs all other types of visits used logistic regression. Analyses of the rates of follow-up visits used modified Poisson regression and adjusted for the patient characteristics listed in the Data Sources and Variables section. To simplify interpretation, we also calculated adjusted percentages of different types of visits and adjusted rates of follow-up visits via marginal standardization of regression model estimates using the Stata margins postestimation command. Missing data (0.17% for language, 0.51% for insurance type, and 0.01% for Neighborhood Deprivation Index) were imputed using the mode. We did not conduct an analysis explicitly comparing the 4 conditions studied because they have inherent clinical differences.

The primary analysis included e-visits, telephone appointment visits, video appointment visits, and physician’s office visits, but it did not include call center encounters because these were not offered for 3 of the 4 conditions evaluated. UTI was eligible to be handled via call center encounters; thus, a secondary analysis was conducted that included these encounters along with other visit types.7 Data analyses were performed using Stata, version 17.0 (StataCorp). Statistical significance was set at 2-sided P < .05.

Results

E-Visit Use and Patient Characteristics

The study sample contained 73 560 total initial visits (86% by females; 41% by participants aged 18-39 years), including 34 895 e-visits, 20 647 telephone visits, 3135 video visits, and 14 883 office visits (Table 1). Of the total initial visits, 47 264 were for UTI, 15 646 for seasonal allergy, 6426 for acne, and 4224 for international travel. Among the 4 conditions studied, e-visits comprised the highest percentage of visits for UTI (58% [27 268 of 47 264]), followed by international travel (56% [2377 of 4224]), seasonal allergy (24% [3718 of 15 646]), and acne (24% [1532 of 6426]) (Figure 1).

Follow-Up Visit Rates and Risk Factors

Follow-up visits for related diagnoses within 7 days after e-visits occurred infrequently for all conditions (adjusted percentages were 2.48% for UTI, 2.11% for acne, 6.45% for seasonal allergy, and 0.12% for international travel) (Table 2). For UTI and international travel, follow-up visit rates were lowest after e-visits compared with other visit types. In contrast, for acne and seasonal allergy, such follow-up visits tended to occur less frequently after office visits compared with e-visits, telephone, or video visits. Follow-up visits after e-visits were most commonly telephone or video appointments (eTable 4 in Supplement 1).

Secondary Analysis

The secondary analysis identified 18 466 call center encounters with a UTI diagnosis by a regional call center physician. When these were included in the analysis, the percentages of UTI encounters made via each modality shifted to 41.48% via e-visits, 28.09% via call center encounters, 23.85% via telephone visits, 1.49% via video visits, and 5.08% via office visits. In adjusted analyses that included call center encounters, the patient characteristics associated with using an e-visit for a UTI relative to another type of visit were unchanged from the primary analysis. E-visit use remained more likely among patients aged 18 to 29 years, White, English speaking, commercially insured, had low comorbidity scores, previously ever used an e-visit, or used the health system’s mobile portal in the prior 365 days (eTable 6 in Supplement 1). The risk of a related follow-up visit for UTI within 7 days was not significantly different after a call center encounter than after an e-visit (adjusted percentages, 2.7% vs 2.4%; ARR, 1.10; 95% CI, 0.98-1.25).

Discussion

High percentages of patients in this study setting selected asynchronous structured e-visits rather than telephone, video, or office visits for the 4 common outpatient conditions evaluated. The popularity of e-visits was observed in a system where patients had timely access to all types of visits. E-visits had high rates of point-of-contact resolution, as seen in the low rates of follow-up visits for the same condition within 7 days. E-visit resolution rates were similar to or better than telephone, video, or office visits for 3 of the 4 conditions studied.

This study adds substantive new knowledge by comparing e-visits with other types of visits for the same conditions. In the population studied, e-visit use was relatively robust across patient groups with varying demographic characteristics. For UTI, e-visits accounted for more than 50% of visits by nearly all subgroups except for patients with the highest comorbidity scores and those aged 65 years and older (even in this age group, e-visits comprised 30% of visits). The use of e-visits compared with other visit types for UTI was higher in adults aged 30 to 39 years, White, English speaking, commercially insured, and who had low comorbidity scores. These patterns are similar to those seen in a prior study of e-visits9 but differ from patterns in a recent study of other telemedicine modalities.10 Of note, after controlling for other variables, e-visit use was not associated with socioeconomic status.

It was not surprising that e-visit use was higher among patients who had previously used an e-visit and among patients who had used the KP mobile portal during the past year. Studies have observed that patient experience with health information technology is an indicator of propensity to use such technology for future health needs.11 Experience using telemedicine, or health care services in general, might also account for the unexpected finding that individuals aged 30 to 39 years were more likely to use e-visits than those aged 18 to 29 years for 3 of the 4 conditions studied.

This study’s findings on point-of-contact resolution showed low follow-up visits within 7 days after e-visits for all 4 study conditions. However, the fact that e-visits had lower follow-up visit rates than other visit types for UTI should not be interpreted as meaning that e-visits were more effective. Patients were able to self-select their visit modalities, and if those with lower acuity preferentially selected e-visits, their lower follow-up visit rates might have been due to selection bias. For seasonal allergy, the fact that e-visits had higher follow-up visit rates than other visit modalities may reflect the fact that e-visits had been recently introduced and had not yet been refined at the time of the study.

The popularity of e-visits in this study exemplifies the growth of virtual care, in which an array of telemedicine modalities, including secure portal messages and video visits, are used to enhance convenient access for patients and may improve efficiency of care. Careful quality monitoring is needed as these new modalities are introduced,8 and changes in incentives for use may have differential effects in different subgroups of patients.12 The e-visits in this study’s integrated care setting differ greatly from e-visit platforms provided by direct-to-consumer companies, which are often not integrated with the clinicians or systems serving patients’ other primary care needs. In contrast, the e-visits in this study were created and delivered by the same medical group responsible for the comprehensive care of the patients who selected them. They were designed to address individual patients’ clinical circumstances by directing those with potentially higher acuity or complexity to other visit modalities.

Limitations

This study should be interpreted with several limitations in mind. First, it was conducted in an integrated health care system where patients had financial coverage for care and good access to all types of visits. These findings may not be generalizable to populations who are uninsured or who experience other barriers to outpatient care. Second, while the KPNC population is diverse in race and ethnicity and is largely representative of the general population, the lowest-income and highest-income groups may be underrepresented.13 Third, the data analyzed did not include visits that were not completed or did not lead to a clinician’s diagnosis of 1 of the 4 conditions studied, but similar exclusion criteria were applied to all modalities. In some of the e-visits that did not lead to a diagnosis by a virtual care physician, the algorithm led to the patient being instructed to contact the health care system’s call center to seek a scheduled telephone, video, or physician’s office visit; the outcomes of these visits would be a useful topic for future study. Fourth, the follow-up visit use evaluated was only a proxy for actual clinical outcomes. Fifth, in evaluating resolution, this study did not count secure messages that patients may have sent to their primary care physicians via the electronic patient portal in follow-up of e-visits or other types of visits. However, a previous study in this setting suggested that rates of such messages after e-visits are low.9

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study, high percentages of patients chose structured asynchronous e-visits rather than telephone, video, or office visits for common outpatient conditions. Point-of-contact resolution rates for e-visits were high and comparable with other types of visits. E-visits may be a useful mode of providing highly convenient access for patients and helping clinicians deliver care for common conditions.
