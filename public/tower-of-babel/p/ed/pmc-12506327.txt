The treatment success rate and associated factors among pulmonary bacteriologically confirmed and pulmonary clinically diagnosed drug susceptible TB coinfected with HIV in Teso Sub-Region, North-Eastern Uganda 2020–2021. a retrospective study
Authors: Saadick Mugerwa Ssentongo, Kenneth Kwenya, Bonniface Oryokot, Ronald Opito, Susan Alwedo, Gerald Ochieng, Abraham Oluka, Peter Sekiranda, Lilian Onega, Miya Yunus, Mugisha Kenneth, Baker Bakashaba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis (TB) remains a global public health burden, with 500,000 people dying of Tb in 2021. People living with HIV have an 18 times higher risk of having TB, which is associated with poor treatment outcomes. This study aimed to determine the treatment success rate and associated factors among Pulmonary Clinically Diagnosed and Bacteriologically Confirmed TB individuals coinfected with HIV in the Teso region, Northeastern Uganda.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study in Twenty-two (22) accredited diagnostic and Tuberculosis Unit health facilities located in 11 districts of the Teso region, Northeastern. Data was collected from the health facility’s Tb treatment register using a data abstraction tool in February 2024 for individuals with drug-susceptible tuberculosis who started Tb treatment from January 2020 to December 2021. Additional information relating to the patients was collected from the Client Care Cards. The primary outcome was treatment success (Yes and No). Continuous Data was summarized into mean and standard deviation and categorical variables into proportions and frequencies. Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to determine factors associated with TSR and reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR). A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsA total of 801 individuals were included in the analysis, with a median age of 42 years (interquartile range 33–52). Among these, 61.67% (n = 494) were aged 15–49. The majority of individuals, 53.43% (n = 428), were males, with 67.92% (n = 544) having PBC. Among all participants, 40.5% (n = 325) had been on treatment for more than 5 years. The Overall treatment success rate was 84.39%. Factors that were associated with successful TSR were receiving services at a specialized clinic (adjusted Odds Ratio (aOR) : 2.10, 95%CI; 1.03–4.23, P = 0.041), having New TB (aOR: 3.30, 95%CI; 1.17–9.39, P = 0.025) and relapsed TB (aOR: 9.71, 95%CI; 2.03–46.24, P = 0.004) and having viral load suppression (aOR :2.85, 95%CI; 1.64–4.97, P = 0.001).ConclusionReceiving treatment in a specialized HIV clinic, having New and relapsed TB, and having a suppressed viral load were associated with a successful treatment rate. There is a need to scale up integrated TB/HIV differentiated services, ensure Viral load suppression through adherence support for the TB/HIV coinfected clients, and increase Tb awareness to achieve the end TB Strategy by 2030.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11618-7.Keywords: Tuberculosis, HIV-TB coinfection, Treatment outcome

Introduction

Despite being preventable and curable, Tuberculosis (TB) remains a Global public health burden. In 2021, an estimated 10.6 million people fell ill with TB [1]. With the WHO Africa Region accounting for 23% (2.5 million people) of the incident cases, and around 500,000 dying of Tb in 2021 [2]. The risk of developing Tb is 18 times higher amongst people living with HIV (PLHIV) than in the HIV-negative general population [3].

Despite the overall decline in TB/HIV Co-infection rates over the years, 6.7% of the reported Tb Cases in 2021 globally were TB/HIV Co-infected with the majority coming from the WHO Africa, even though the global Treatment Success Rate (TSR) for Drug Susceptible Tb patients has improved from to 88% in 2022 from 87% in 2021, that of TB/HIV coinfected individuals remains much lower at 79% in 2022 [4]. The coronavirus (COVID-19) pandemic significantly affected Tb services, leading to an increase in TB deaths from 2019 (1.34 million deaths) to 2021 (1.42 million deaths), with an estimated 187,000 TB-related deaths amongst the HIV Positive in 2021 [1].

Uganda remains among the 30 WHO-designated TB/HIV high-burdened countries that accounted for 87% of the cases of Tb globally, of whom 29,000 were coinfected with HIV in 2021 [1]. Despite the increase in the proportion of TB/HIV infection cases identified from 31.7% in 2021/2022 to 35% in 2022/2023, The TSR for TB/HIV Coinfected remains suboptimal below 90% target. However, there was slight improvement from 83.4% in 2021/2022 to 86.7% in 2022/2023 with 6200 TB-related deaths amongst the PLHIV [5–7]. This is similar to findings in Mozambique, where an ad study by Nacarapa found that of 9,655 patients analyzed, 82.8% had TB/HIV co-infection, but only 73.2% of patients had a favorable treatment outcome [8].

In 2015, WHO launched the End Tb Strategy to reduce TB-related deaths by 35% in 2020 and 75% by 2025, with an overall reduction of 95% by 2030. Similarly, the National Tb and Leprosy Strategic Plan 2020/2021–2024/2025 of the Ministry of Health Uganda, focuses on improving the Treatment success from 72% to over 90% by 2024/2025 [9, 10]. However, to achieve this, determinants of TSR among TB/HIV individuals need to be well understood to design respective interventions. Teso region, in North-Eastern Uganda, has a high-burden of TB but with a low HIV prevalence; however, the TSR and its determinants among TB/HIV coinfected remain unclear. This study aimed to determine the treatment success rate and associated factors among Pulmonary Clinically Diagnosed and Bacteriologically Confirmed individuals coinfected with HIV in Teso region, Northeastern Uganda.

Materials and methods

Study design. We conducted a retrospective study that included a collection of quantitative data from the health facility Tb treatment register using a data abstraction tool, and we also collected additional information relating to the patients from the Client Care cards. The data was accessed and collected by 15th March 2025. All Individuals with Pulmonary Bacteriologically Confirmed (PBC) and Pulmonary Clinically Diagnosed (PCD) Drug Susceptible Tb coinfected with HIV who started TB treatment from January 2020 to December 2021 were included in the study. Data on Individuals with treatment outcomes were considered. The findings were reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [11].

Study setting

The study was conducted in all Twenty-two (22) accredited diagnostic and Tuberculosis Unit health facilities located in 11 districts of the Teso region that include Katakwi, Amuria, and Kapelebyong, bordering Karamoja and have pastoralist communities. Kaberamaido, Serere, Kalaki, and Kumi are surrounded by water bodies, leading to significant engagement in fishing activities. Moreover, Bukedea, Kumi, Ngora, Soroti City and District, Kaberamaido, and Kalaki are all intersected by the Great North Road, resulting in frequent population movement and non-resident populations seeking healthcare within the region.

The combined population of these districts was estimated to be 2,462,344 in 2024 [12], with one of the lowest HIV/AIDS prevalence rates in Uganda at 4.2% [13]. Additionally, this is the fifth poorest region in Uganda, with a multidimensional poverty rate of 56%, 10.7% of the population have ever had an acute respiratory infection, and 5.2% have moderate smoking behavior [14–16]. The districts are largely rural and the majority of residents are subsistence mixed farmers, keeping goats and cattle and tending crops for a livelihood.

The health facilities were purposively selected because they were accredited to offer TB treatment services in the Teso region. Each facility serves as the TB diagnostic and treatment unit (DTU). Within each TB unit, there is a TB focal person who is either a nurse or a clinical officer. The facility focal persons are supervised by the District TB and Leprosy Supervisor (DTLS), an expert responsible for TB treatment and management at the district level. All these Health facilities receive support from the AIDS Information Center (AIC), funded by the U.S. President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) through the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) to enhance high-quality TB/HIV services through a health system strengthening grant to implement.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All individuals notified with Pulmonary Bacteriologically Confirmed and Clinically diagnosed Drug Susceptible Tb patients with a documented HIV Positive status in the Unit TB registers were included in the study. Individuals reported in the 22 Targeted DTUs from January 2020 to December 2021 were included in the study.

Individuals with Extra-pulmonary TB of any form, those with Drug-resistant TB, and those who were reported to be HIV-negative or of unknown HIV status were excluded from this study.

Study variables

Exposures: Demographic characteristics (age, gender), disease classification categorized as bacteriologically confirmed (laboratory diagnosis with urine LAM, Gen Xpert or Microscopy), clinically diagnosed (based on clinical assessment and chest x-ray diagnosis), and [17]TB treatment type categorized as new or retreatment, point of referral (Facility or community), GeneXpert results (Positive, Negative, and unknown).

The primary outcome was TB treatment Success is defined as treatment completion (completing treatment without evidence of failure but with no record to show that sputum smear or culture results in the last month of treatment and on at least one previous occasion were negative, either because tests were not done or because results are unavailable) and/or Cure (negative smear microscopy at the end of treatment and on at least one previous follow-up test at 2 months or 5 months) [18].

Treatment failure is when an individual remained smear-positive after 5 months of treatment, defaulted (patients who interrupted their treatment for two consecutive months or more), died, Lost to follow-up (A Tb patient who did not start treatment or who completed more than 1 month of treatment and was interrupted for 2 consecutive months or more) [18].

The HIV positive was determined as individuals with drug-susceptible TB having an HIV-positive test result (both the newly tested positive and the Known HIV-positive; individual already confirmed to be living with HIV as documented in the TB unit register) at the time of TB treatment initiation after TB diagnosis by either GeneXpert, urine Tuberculosis lipoarabinomannan or clinically diagnosed by a health worker [19, 20].

Data management and analysis

Using a designed data abstraction tool, data were extracted on both the exposure and outcome variables from the health unit TB registers from the time started on TB treatment until the treatment outcome was reached. The collected data was entered into a Microsoft Excel sheet and then exported to Stata Statistical Software: Release 15 (College Station, TX: StataCorpLLC.) where it was cleaned, validated, and analyzed [21].

Descriptive statistics of the study participants were summarized using proportions for categorical variables, means, and standard deviations for continuous variables with normal distribution and medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables with skewed distributions. The association between treatment success (Yes/No) and the categorical variables were determined using the Chi-square or Fisher exact test, and associations between treatment success and numerical variables were assessed using the student t-test or Mann–Whitney U. Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to determine factors associated with treatment success as adjusted odds ratios [22]. All variables significant (p < 0.05) at bivariate analysis were included in binary logistic regression analysis at the multivariate level. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Recruitment of participants in the study

Of 827 individuals, we excluded 19 with extrapulmonary Tuberculosis and 7 with rifampicin-resistant Tuberculosis. A total of 801 individuals with TB and HIV positive were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1.)

Social demographic characteristics:The median age of all participants was 42 years (interquartile range 33–52). Among these, 81.77% (n = 655) were identified at public health facilities. Only 6.99% (56 participants) were aged 0–14 years, while 61.67% (n = 494) were aged 15–49 years (Adults). The majority of individuals, 53.43% (n = 428), were males, with 67.92% (n = 544) having PBC. Discussion

In this study, we aimed to determine the treatment success rate and associated factors among Pulmonary Clinically Diagnosed and Bacteriologically Confirmed Patients treated in Teso region, Northeastern Uganda. We found suboptimal treatment success among Individuals with TB and HIV. The treatment success was significantly associated with individuals receiving care from a specialized Facility, having TB relapse, and suppressed viral load. The findings of this multicenter study add to the existing literature and inform the targeted intervention to improve the suboptimal TB treatment outcome. The finding further strengthens the call for having differentiated and integrated service delivery for PLHIV with TB.

The treatment success rate among the Tb HIV patients was suboptimal at 84.39%, below the national and WHO Tb target of 90% [23]. This is similar to studies done in Ethiopia and Malaysia [24, 25]. The low TSR is explained by the pill burden of People living with HIV, and the drug interaction between the anti-TBs and the antiretroviral therapies, also HIV tends to weaken the immune system, increasing the vulnerability to TB and the progression of latent TB, slow response, and consequently low treatment outcomes [24, 26–29].

The treatment success was much higher among females as compared to males. These findings are similar to the studies done Uganda [30, 31] and India [32]. These differences are due to social behaviors where women have better health seeking behaviour more likely be enrolled on treatment timely, and adhere to treatment.

In this study, individuals attending special clinics were more likely to achieve treatment success compared to those at public health facilities. This is consistent with findings in Ethiopia, where private clinics (TSR: 98.1%) had a 1.03-fold higher treatment success rate than the Public health center (TSR: 95.3%) [33, 34]. This difference may be attributed to the integrated and differentiated service delivery model for TB/HIV care at the TASO Center of Excellence in Soroti. These models combine facility and community-based support, including e counselor and expert clients (peers) attached to each of the TB/HIV individuals to support them adhere to treatment and active follow-up [35].

In this study we individuals with relapsed Tb were 9.71 times more likely to have a treatment success. This is consistent with findings in Benin, which reported (RR: 1.06, 95 CI: 1.02–1.10) [36]. Similarly, New Tb cases were 3 times more likely to achieve treatment success compared to patients returning after loss to follow up (LTFU), consistent with studies from Tanzania [37] India [38] and in Ethiopia, a two times likelihood of achieving treatment success (aOR: 2.91; 95% CI: 1.17–7.28) [8]. This disparity may better adherence to the treatment and leading to improved outcomes [39, 40]. These findings underscore the need for improved treatment retention strategies, such as multi-month dispensing, Psychosocial support for patients to complete treatment, avoid interruptions, and improve health outcomes [39].

We found that Individuals with a suppressed viral load had a higher treatment success as compared to the non-suppressed (aOR: 2.85, 95% CI: 1.64–4.97); this is similar to findings in a clinical trial done in South Africa, where Tb patienst with unsuppressed viral load had 9.28-fold higher rates of significant treatment failure (95% CI: 1.53–56.61) [41]Similarly, studies from Botswana [42] and Nigeria (Olalere et al.) demonstrated increased odds of poor outcomes among virally unsuppressed (aOR = 1.677, 95% CI: 1.036–2.716) as compared to those with suppression [43]. While the magnitude of association varied across studies, this consistency highlights the need for sustained direct observed therapy and intensive adherence counseling among TB/HIV coinfected individuals to take their Antiretroviral drugs, achieve viral load suppression, reduce the incidence of TB, and improve overall treatment outcomes.

Limitations and strengths

This study has some limitations; First, it used retrospective data, which may have been incomplete or lacked certain variables. Second, not all HIV-positive individuals were included in the study due to service interruptions due by COVID-19. Despite these limitations, the study has notable strengths. Being a multicenter study, done in all TB diagnostic and treatment facilities in the Teso region, including facilities of different capacities (health center IIIs, regional referral hospitals, district general hospitals, and Health center IVs) provides a robust representation of diverse healthcare settings. The large sample size of data collected over a two-year (2020–2021) period enhances the generalizability of the findings. This study contributes valuable evidence to the literature on TB-HIV treatment outcomes and reflects outcomes in resource-limited rural settings.

Conclusion

This study revealed a suboptimal treatment success rate among individuals with HIV-TB coinfection in the Teso region. The key factors associated with a higher treatment success rate included receiving treatment in a specialized HIV clinic, being newly diagnosed with TB, having relapsed TB, and having a suppressed viral load. To improve the outcomes in this high-risk population, we recommend scaling up differentiated TB services delivery both in the community and facility, ensuring universal ART access and TB preventive therapy, and implementing combined virtual and physical adherence support for the TB/HIV coinfected. Future studies should further investigate TB treatment success rate among people living who experience relapse of TB, particularly comparing those who received TB preventive therapy, versus those who did not. Additionally, controlled trials are needed to evaluate the long-term effectiveness of TPT in HIV-positive individuals, including its impact on relapse rates and sustained TB prevention.
