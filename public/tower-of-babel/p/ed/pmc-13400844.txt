Dynamic hematologic recovery patterns to guide clinical differentiation along the dengue–hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis hyperinflammatory spectrum: A case report
Authors: Reynard Laysandro, Aqilla Katrita Zaira Nugroho, Widya Eka Nugraha, Andri Sanityoso Sulaiman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDengue infection may trigger a hyperinflammatory response that closely resembles secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), making therapeutic decision-making particularly challenging in patients who fulfill multiple HLH-associated diagnostic criteria. Distinguishing self-limited dengue-associated immune activation from progressive HLH requiring immunomodulatory therapy remains an important clinical dilemma. We report a 19-year-old woman presenting with prolonged fever, pancytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, marked hyperferritinemia, hypofibrinogenemia, hypertriglyceridemia, elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor levels, and a high HScore, initially raising strong suspicion for secondary HLH. Bone marrow examination demonstrated mildly hypocellular marrow without overt hemophagocytosis; however, this finding was interpreted in conjunction with the overall clinical course rather than as an exclusion criterion for HLH. Despite fulfilling several HLH-associated parameters, the patient remained clinically stable and demonstrated spontaneous defervescence, progressive reticulocyte recovery, rapid platelet rebound, and a marked decline in ferritin levels during supportive treatment alone, resulting in complete clinical and hematologic recovery without corticosteroids or HLH-directed therapy. This case illustrates that dynamic longitudinal assessment may provide greater clinical value than isolated diagnostic thresholds when evaluating dengue-associated hyperinflammation. New findings

This report highlights that dengue-associated hyperinflammation may transiently fulfill multiple diagnostic criteria for secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), creating substantial diagnostic uncertainty despite a high HScore. Rather than relying on isolated laboratory thresholds or the absence of hemophagocytosis, this case emphasizes the value of integrating longitudinal clinical evolution with dynamic hematologic recovery, including rapid platelet rebound, progressive reticulocytosis, and spontaneous ferritin decline. These recovery patterns may help clinicians identify clinically stable patients who can be safely observed while remaining vigilant for features suggestive of evolving HLH requiring immunomodulatory therapy.

Introduction

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a life-threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome caused by uncontrolled activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes and macrophages, resulting in excessive cytokine release, progressive cytopenias, multiorgan dysfunction, and, if left untreated, high mortality. Although traditionally classified as either primary (familial) or secondary, current evidence suggests that HLH is better understood as a clinicopathologic syndrome representing dysregulated immune activation triggered by a wide range of infectious, malignant, or autoimmune conditions rather than a single disease entity [1], [2], [3], [4], [5].

The diagnosis of secondary HLH in adults remains particularly challenging because no individual clinical feature or laboratory marker is sufficiently specific. Instead, diagnosis relies on the integration of multiple clinical, biochemical, and hematologic findings, including persistent fever, cytopenias, hepatosplenomegaly, hyperferritinemia, hypofibrinogenemia, hypertriglyceridemia, elevated soluble interleukin−2 receptor (sCD25), impaired natural killer (NK) cell activity, and, when present, hemophagocytosis on tissue examination [2], [4], [5], [6], [7]. Importantly, contemporary guidelines emphasize that neither fulfillment of HLH−2004 criteria nor the demonstration of hemophagocytosis alone should dictate therapeutic decisions. Rather, both diagnostic interpretation and treatment should be guided by the overall clinical trajectory and the likelihood of progressive immune dysregulation [4], [7], [8], [9].

Dengue is one of the most common arboviral infections worldwide and remains a major public health problem in tropical and subtropical regions. While most patients experience a self-limited illness, severe dengue may induce profound immune activation characterized by macrophage activation, cytokine release, endothelial dysfunction, coagulopathy, and transient bone marrow suppression. These mechanisms produce clinical and laboratory abnormalities that substantially overlap with those observed in HLH, including prolonged fever, pancytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, hyperferritinemia, hypofibrinogenemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and hepatic injury [10], [11], [12], [13]. Consequently, distinguishing severe dengue-associated hyperinflammation from secondary HLH remains one of the most difficult diagnostic challenges encountered in dengue-endemic settings.

Rather than representing two entirely separate entities, accumulating evidence indicates that dengue-associated hyperinflammation and secondary HLH exist along a continuum of immune activation [10], [11], [14], [15]. At one end of this spectrum, immune activation resolves spontaneously with supportive care as viral infection improves. At the other, persistent macrophage activation progresses to fulminant HLH requiring prompt immunomodulatory therapy. Because patients at different points along this continuum may initially satisfy similar diagnostic criteria, isolated laboratory abnormalities or probability scores, including the HScore, may not adequately distinguish those who require immediate HLH-directed treatment from those who can be safely managed with close observation [4], [6], [8], [9].

Recent systematic reviews of dengue-associated HLH have highlighted persistent uncertainty regarding the optimal diagnostic approach in adults. Although many reported cases fulfill multiple HLH-associated criteria, relatively little attention has been given to the dynamic evolution of clinical and hematologic recovery after presentation [10], [16]. Most published reports emphasize static laboratory thresholds, whereas practical markers indicating spontaneous resolution of immune activation remain poorly defined. Consequently, clinicians are frequently faced with a therapeutic dilemma when patients appear to satisfy diagnostic criteria for HLH but remain clinically stable and begin to improve without immunosuppressive therapy [4], [8], [10].

Here, we describe a young woman with dengue-associated hyperinflammation who fulfilled multiple HLH-associated diagnostic parameters, including a high HScore, yet recovered completely with supportive management alone. Rather than proposing a strict distinction between dengue and HLH, this case illustrates how integrating longitudinal clinical evolution with dynamic hematologic recovery including platelet rebound, progressive reticulocytosis, and declining ferritin levels may complement existing diagnostic frameworks when evaluating patients along the dengue associated HLH hyperinflammatory spectrum. We further discuss how these recovery patterns may assist clinicians in identifying patients who can be safely observed while maintaining vigilance for those who develop progressive immune dysregulation requiring HLH-directed therapy.

Case presentation

A 19-year-old Asian woman presented with an 11-day history of persistent high-grade fever accompanied by nausea, vomiting, anorexia, watery diarrhea, diffuse abdominal pain, generalized myalgia, arthralgia, and progressive fatigue. During the course of illness, she developed spontaneous epistaxis and petechial rash involving both upper and lower extremities. Initial evaluation at a local healthcare facility resulted in empirical treatment for suspected dengue fever and typhoid infection; however, her symptoms progressively worsened, prompting referral to a tertiary referral hospital (Table 1).

Investigation

Because the combination of prolonged fever, pancytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, marked hyperferritinemia, and hepatic dysfunction raised concern for secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), an extensive diagnostic evaluation was undertaken to identify potential infectious, hematologic, autoimmune, and inflammatory etiologies.

Initial hematologic investigations demonstrated pancytopenia involving all three cell lineages, with leukopenia (2.1 × 10⁹/L), neutropenia (absolute neutrophil count 0.9 × 10⁹/L), thrombocytopenia (47 × 10⁹/L), and mild anemia (hemoglobin 11.9 g/dL) (Table 6). Peripheral blood smear examination showed dysplastic leukocyte morphology with hypolobulated nuclei and pseudo Pelger-Huët anomaly involving approximately 15% of leukocytes, without circulating blast cells (Fig. 2). Although these findings raised concern for bone marrow pathology, they were interpreted cautiously in the context of acute systemic inflammation.

Biochemical testing demonstrated severe hepatocellular injury, reflected by markedly elevated AST (1303 U/L), ALT (424 U/L), and lactate dehydrogenase (1006 U/L), while bilirubin levels and renal function remained preserved (Table 3). Viral hepatitis serologies (hepatitis A, B, and C) and HIV testing were negative, reducing the likelihood of alternative infectious causes for the observed hepatic injury.

Further evaluation of the hyperinflammatory state revealed profound hyperferritinemia (21,210 ng/mL), hypertriglyceridemia (202 mg/dL), hypofibrinogenemia (125.4 mg/dL), and prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (52.5 s), fulfilling several laboratory features commonly encountered in HLH (Table 3). Mild hypokalemia was also noted. Autoimmune screening, including lupus anticoagulant, anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies, and complement levels (C3 and C4), showed no evidence of an underlying autoimmune disorder.

Serologic testing for dengue demonstrated concurrent positivity for both anti-dengue IgM and IgG antibodies (Table 3). Although virological confirmation by NS1 antigen or reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was unavailable because of the timing of presentation, this serologic profile was compatible with either late primary dengue infection or secondary dengue infection. Given that secondary dengue is associated with enhanced immune activation and a greater risk of severe inflammatory manifestations, this finding was considered clinically relevant during diagnostic interpretation and is further discussed below.

Electrocardiography demonstrated sinus rhythm with T-wave inversion in leads V1-V3 without evidence of conduction abnormalities or ventricular hypertrophy (Fig. 3). Chest radiography showed no cardiopulmonary abnormalities (Fig. 4). Abdominal ultrasonography confirmed mild hepatomegaly (right hepatic lobe craniocaudal diameter 16.2 cm) and mild splenomegaly (craniocaudal diameter 14 cm; anteroposterior diameter 8 cm), consistent with the physical examination findings.

Because persistent pancytopenia together with a high HScore raised substantial concern for secondary HLH, bone marrow examination was performed primarily to exclude hematologic malignancy and to evaluate for hemophagocytosis. The biopsy demonstrated mildly hypocellular marrow (approximately 40% cellularity) with preserved trilineage hematopoiesis, normal myeloid-to-erythroid ratio, scattered small hypolobated megakaryocytes, and no evidence of marrow fibrosis. No overt hemophagocytosis was identified (Table 5). However, this finding was interpreted as one component of the overall diagnostic assessment rather than sufficient evidence to exclude HLH, recognizing that hemophagocytosis is neither universally present nor specific for the diagnosis.

To further characterize immune activation, serum soluble interleukin−2 receptor (sCD25) concentration was measured and found to be elevated at 2154 U/mL, indicating significant immune activation but remaining below the diagnostic threshold proposed in the HLH−2004 criteria (>2400 U/mL) (Table 5). Integrating these findings, the patient fulfilled multiple HLH-associated clinical and laboratory parameters and had an HScore of 215, corresponding to an estimated 93–96% probability of secondary HLH (Table 4). Nevertheless, interpretation of these results was guided by the patient's overall clinical course rather than isolated diagnostic thresholds.

Clinical decision making

Despite fulfilling multiple HLH-associated clinical and laboratory criteria, including prolonged fever, bicytopenia progressing to pancytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, hyperferritinemia, hypofibrinogenemia, hypertriglyceridemia, elevated soluble interleukin−2 receptor levels, and an HScore of 215 corresponding to an estimated 93–96% probability of secondary HLH (Table 4), the multidisciplinary clinical team elected to continue close supportive management rather than immediately initiate corticosteroids or HLH-directed immunosuppressive therapy.

This decision reflected the recognition that diagnostic criteria are intended to support, rather than replace, clinical judgment. At the time of assessment, the patient remained hemodynamically stable without vasopressor requirement, respiratory failure, neurological deterioration, progressive hepatic failure, or other evidence of rapidly evolving multiorgan dysfunction. Although laboratory findings strongly suggested a hyperinflammatory state, her overall clinical condition appeared disproportionally preserved compared with the severity typically encountered in fulminant secondary HLH.

Equally important, serial reassessment during hospitalization demonstrated early signs of spontaneous recovery. Defervescence was accompanied by rapid platelet recovery, progressive reticulocyte response, stabilization of leukocyte counts, declining serum ferritin concentrations, improvement in coagulation parameters, and gradual resolution of hepatic injury. These dynamic changes suggested improving immune regulation rather than persistent hyperinflammation. Consequently, continued close observation with supportive treatment was considered an appropriate and safer strategy while maintaining a low threshold for therapeutic escalation should clinical deterioration occur.

The patient was monitored through frequent clinical assessment and serial laboratory evaluation. Corticosteroids, etoposide, intravenous immunoglobulin, or other HLH-directed therapies were reserved as contingency options if persistent fever, worsening cytopenias, progressive organ dysfunction, rising inflammatory markers, or other features suggestive of uncontrolled immune activation had developed. However, none of these warning signs emerged during hospitalization. Instead, both clinical and laboratory parameters improved consistently, allowing complete recovery without immunomodulatory therapy.

This management strategy does not imply that immunosuppressive therapy should be withheld in all patients with suspected dengue-associated HLH. Rather, it highlights the importance of integrating diagnostic criteria with longitudinal clinical evolution and dynamic hematologic recovery when determining whether supportive management or HLH-directed treatment represents the most appropriate therapeutic approach.

Follow up and clinical outcomes

During hospitalization, serial clinical and laboratory reassessment demonstrated a coordinated pattern of spontaneous recovery rather than progressive deterioration. At admission (illness day 12), the patient remained febrile and pancytopenic, with marked hyperferritinemia, hypofibrinogenemia, coagulopathy, and severe hepatocellular injury (Table 3, Table 6). Despite these abnormalities, she remained hemodynamically stable and did not develop respiratory failure, neurological impairment, shock, or other evidence of evolving multiorgan dysfunction.

By hospital day 2 (illness day 13), the only bleeding manifestation was a single episode of epistaxis, with no recurrent hemorrhagic events thereafter. Serial complete blood count monitoring showed fluctuating cytopenias, but early evidence of hematologic recovery became apparent. Platelet counts increased from 47 × 10⁹/L on illness day 11–252 × 10⁹/L by illness day 14 and continued to rise to 407 × 10⁹/L by illness day 19 (Table 6, Fig. 5). Concurrently, the immature platelet fraction gradually declined, consistent with normalization of platelet production.

Longitudinal hematologic trends during the clinical course. Serial measurements demonstrate progressive platelet recovery and a marked rise in absolute reticulocyte count, indicating bone marrow recovery. White blood cell and hemoglobin levels showed mild fluctuations throughout hospitalization. The observed hematologic recovery pattern supported dengue-associated cytopenia rather than progressive hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis.

Absolute reticulocyte counts increased progressively from 20,200/µL on illness day 11–62,100/µL on illness day 19, indicating preserved marrow responsiveness and active compensatory erythropoiesis (Table 6, Fig. 5). In contrast, leukocyte and absolute neutrophil counts fluctuated near the lower limit of normal throughout hospitalization, with intermittent neutropenia persisting despite overall clinical improvement.

Biochemical parameters followed a similar recovery trajectory. AST and ALT decreased substantially between illness day 12 and day 16, while lactate dehydrogenase levels also declined markedly (Table 3). Total bilirubin remained within normal limits throughout the observation period. Coagulation abnormalities improved in parallel, with activated partial thromboplastin time normalizing by day 16 and fibrinogen levels increasing from 125.4 mg/dL to 180.4 mg/dL.

Markers of hyperinflammation demonstrated partial but significant resolution. Serum ferritin decreased from 21,210 ng/mL on illness day 12–5407 ng/mL on illness day 16, while triglyceride levels remained only mildly elevated (Table 3, Fig. 6). Inflammatory biomarkers, including C-reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and procalcitonin, remained normal or minimally elevated, and repeated microbiological investigations, including blood and stool cultures, remained sterile.

Dynamic hematologic and clinical recovery trends during hospitalization. Serial monitoring demonstrated progressive platelet recovery, declining ferritin levels, and resolution of fever over time. The rapid platelet rebound and spontaneous ferritin decline without immunosuppressive therapy supported a reversible dengue-associated hyperinflammatory response rather than progressive hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis.

Clinically, fever resolved after the first day of admission and did not recur during the remainder of hospitalization. By hospital day 4 (illness day 15), hepatomegaly and splenomegaly were no longer palpable on physical examination, although petechiae and rash persisted transiently. The patient continued to improve without corticosteroids, etoposide, intravenous immunoglobulin, or other immunomodulatory therapy.

On reassessment at illness day 16, the calculated HScore had decreased from 215 to 109, corresponding to a substantially lower probability of HLH (Table 4). Given the sustained clinical stability, progressive platelet rebound, reticulocyte recovery, improving liver function, normalization of coagulation parameters, and declining ferritin levels, supportive management was continued. The patient was discharged on hospital day 8 (illness day 19) in good clinical condition with ongoing hematologic recovery.

Discussion

This case illustrates the diagnostic uncertainty encountered when dengue infection presents with marked immune activation fulfilling multiple HLH-associated criteria. At presentation, the combination of prolonged fever, pancytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, profound hyperferritinemia, hypofibrinogenemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and elevated soluble interleukin−2 receptor (sCD25) generated a high probability of secondary HLH based on established diagnostic frameworks [1], [3], [6], [7], [8], [17], [18]. However, the subsequent clinical course demonstrated spontaneous resolution of fever, progressive hematologic recovery, declining ferritin levels, and improving hepatic injury with supportive care alone. Rather than supporting a simple distinction between dengue-associated hyperinflammation and "true" HLH, this case highlights the concept that infection-associated immune activation exists along a continuum, in which patients may transiently satisfy multiple HLH-associated criteria without developing progressive immune dysregulation requiring HLH-directed therapy [8], [10], [11], [12], [19].

Dengue virus is increasingly recognized as an important trigger of secondary HLH as well as HLH-like hyperinflammatory syndromes [10], [11], [16]. Severe dengue is characterized by excessive activation of T lymphocytes and macrophages, leading to dysregulated cytokine production, endothelial dysfunction, and systemic inflammatory injury [10], [11], [12]. Consequently, prolonged fever, cytopenias, hyperferritinemia, coagulopathy, and hepatosplenomegaly may occur in both severe dengue and secondary HLH, creating substantial diagnostic overlap [10], [13], [16]. Rather than representing two discrete entities, accumulating evidence suggests that dengue-associated immune activation spans a spectrum ranging from self-limited hyperinflammation to fulminant secondary HLH requiring immunomodulatory therapy [11], [14], [15]. This conceptual framework is clinically important because therapeutic decisions should be guided not only by fulfillment of diagnostic criteria but also by the evolution of the patient's clinical and laboratory course.

The biological basis for this overlap lies in shared inflammatory pathways. Severe dengue and secondary HLH are both characterized by excessive production of proinflammatory cytokines, including interferon-γ, interleukin−6, interleukin−10, and tumor necrosis factor-α, which amplify macrophage activation, endothelial injury, and systemic inflammation [5], [8], [10], [11], [12], [20]. These cytokine networks contribute to many of the laboratory abnormalities common to both conditions, including cytopenias, hyperferritinemia, coagulopathy, and hepatic dysfunction. Consequently, fulfillment of several HLH-associated criteria during acute dengue infection should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of irreversible hemophagocytic disease. Instead, these findings should be interpreted within the broader clinical context, incorporating serial reassessment of disease trajectory rather than relying solely on isolated laboratory thresholds [4], [8], [9], [10], [21].

Another important aspect of this case is the interpretation of the dengue serologic profile. Concurrent positivity of anti-dengue IgM and IgG is compatible with either a late primary infection or a secondary dengue infection, depending on the timing of specimen collection relative to symptom onset [13], [22]. Because this patient presented on the twelfth day of illness, after more than one week of persistent fever, both scenarios remain biologically plausible. However, the combination of simultaneous IgM and IgG positivity, prolonged fever, profound hyperferritinemia, and marked immune activation raises the possibility of secondary dengue infection, which has been consistently associated with a more intense inflammatory response through antibody-dependent enhancement and exaggerated cytokine production [10], [13], [16].

From a clinical perspective, distinguishing between late primary and secondary dengue would not have substantially altered the immediate management in this patient, but it provides important pathophysiologic context for the observed hyperinflammatory phenotype. Secondary dengue infection is recognized to increase the risk of severe disease manifestations, including plasma leakage, coagulopathy, hepatic injury, and, in rare cases, secondary HLH or HLH-like syndromes [10], [13], [16], [22]. Nevertheless, serologic findings alone should not be used to infer progression toward HLH because both primary and secondary dengue infections may transiently fulfill several HLH-associated laboratory criteria during periods of intense immune activation. Accordingly, interpretation of dengue serology should be integrated with the patient's clinical trajectory and serial laboratory evolution rather than considered in isolation.

The diagnosis of secondary HLH remains particularly challenging because currently available diagnostic tools are intended to support, rather than replace, clinical judgment. Although the HLH−2004 criteria and HScore provide structured approaches for estimating the likelihood of HLH, both rely on combinations of clinical and laboratory findings that are not specific to hemophagocytic syndromes and may also occur during severe systemic infections, including dengue [4], [6], [7], [8], [9], [17], [19]. Consequently, patients with severe infection may transiently fulfill multiple HLH-associated criteria despite having a self-limited inflammatory response. This limitation is especially relevant in dengue-endemic regions, where fever, cytopenias, hyperferritinemia, hypofibrinogenemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and hepatosplenomegaly frequently coexist during the critical phase of illness [10], [11], [21]. Rather than serving as definitive diagnostic tests, the HLH−2004 criteria and HScore should therefore be interpreted within the broader clinical context, incorporating serial reassessment of disease evolution and response to supportive management.

An important implication of this approach is that bone marrow findings should also be interpreted cautiously. Although hemophagocytosis remains one of the HLH−2004 criteria, its absence neither excludes HLH nor reliably distinguishes infection-associated hyperinflammation from progressive hemophagocytic disease because hemophagocytosis may be absent early in the disease course and is not specific to HLH [4], [8], [9], [17], [23]. In the present case, bone marrow examination was primarily valuable for excluding hematologic malignancy and demonstrating preserved marrow architecture rather than for confirming or refuting HLH. The subsequent interpretation therefore relied on integrating marrow findings with the patient's overall clinical evolution instead of considering hemophagocytosis as an isolated determinant of diagnosis.

Our patient fulfilled several HLH-associated parameters, including prolonged fever, splenomegaly, cytopenias affecting three hematopoietic lineages, marked hyperferritinemia, hypofibrinogenemia, hypertriglyceridemia, elevated aminotransferases, and increased sCD25 levels, resulting in an HScore of 215, corresponding to an estimated HLH probability of approximately 93–96% [4], [6], [19]. However, this high probability was interpreted as a signal for intensified clinical surveillance rather than an automatic indication for immunosuppressive therapy. During the subsequent days of hospitalization, the patient remained hemodynamically stable, became afebrile, demonstrated progressive platelet recovery and reticulocytosis, showed declining ferritin concentrations, and experienced steady improvement in liver function without evidence of new organ dysfunction. Collectively, these longitudinal findings suggested resolution of immune activation rather than progression toward uncontrolled HLH [8], [9], [23], [24].

This case therefore highlights an important limitation of relying on static diagnostic thresholds alone. A high HScore should prompt careful reassessment and close monitoring, but treatment decisions should ultimately be guided by the overall clinical trajectory rather than by a single probability estimate. In patients with suspected infection-associated HLH, persistent fever, worsening cytopenias, rising ferritin concentrations, progressive organ dysfunction, or hemodynamic instability despite appropriate supportive care should heighten concern for evolving HLH and strengthen the indication for early HLH-directed therapy. Conversely, spontaneous hematologic recovery accompanied by improving inflammatory markers and clinical stability, as observed in our patient, may support continued observation with close serial reassessment while avoiding unnecessary immunosuppression [7], [8], [9], [23], [24].

One of the most instructive aspects of this case was the value of serial laboratory monitoring rather than isolated measurements. Although the patient initially fulfilled multiple HLH-associated criteria, subsequent laboratory evolution demonstrated a coordinated pattern of recovery that paralleled the favorable clinical course. Progressive platelet normalization, increasing absolute reticulocyte counts, declining ferritin concentrations, improving coagulation parameters, and gradual resolution of hepatocellular injury occurred without immunomodulatory therapy. Taken together, these findings suggested restoration of physiologic hematopoiesis and resolution of systemic inflammation rather than persistent macrophage-driven immune dysregulation [7], [8], [9], [12], [19], [21], [23], [25], [26], [27].

Rather than relying on a single biomarker, we found that the integration of these dynamic changes provided a more informative assessment of disease behavior. Platelet recovery reflected resolution of peripheral consumption and restoration of megakaryopoiesis, whereas the progressive reticulocyte response indicated preserved bone marrow reserve and active compensatory erythropoiesis. Simultaneously, declining ferritin levels and improving liver enzymes suggested attenuation of the inflammatory cascade. Although each of these laboratory findings has previously been described in dengue or HLH individually, their longitudinal interpretation as a coordinated recovery pattern has received comparatively little attention in the literature. This integrated approach may therefore provide a practical framework for distinguishing self-limited dengue-associated immune activation from patients with persistent hyperinflammation who warrant closer evaluation for evolving HLH [8], [10], [23], [24].

Among the dynamic laboratory findings, the trajectory of hematologic recovery may be particularly informative for clinical decision-making. In secondary HLH, cytopenias commonly persist or worsen because ongoing cytokine-mediated macrophage activation suppresses hematopoiesis and perpetuates peripheral cell destruction [7], [9], [21], [23]. In contrast, dengue-associated cytopenias typically begin to recover after the critical phase of infection as viral suppression resolves and bone marrow function is restored [12], [22], [25]. In our patient, platelet counts normalized rapidly, absolute reticulocyte counts increased progressively, and no new cytopenias developed despite the absence of corticosteroid therapy. These findings strongly favored reversible infection-associated immune activation over uncontrolled hemophagocytic disease.

From a practical standpoint, serial hematologic trends may complement existing diagnostic criteria when therapeutic uncertainty exists. Improvement in platelet counts, emergence of reticulocytosis, defervescence, declining ferritin concentrations, and stabilization of organ function may provide reassurance that supportive management with close observation remains appropriate. Conversely, persistent fever, worsening cytopenias, rising ferritin levels, progressive liver dysfunction, new organ failure, or hemodynamic deterioration despite optimal supportive care should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis and early initiation of HLH-directed therapy when clinically indicated [7], [8], [9], [23], [25], [27]. Although these observations arise from a single case and should not be interpreted as definitive diagnostic criteria, they highlight the potential value of incorporating disease trajectory into clinical decision-making, particularly in dengue-endemic settings where transient HLH-like laboratory abnormalities are frequently encountered.

Hyperferritinemia remains one of the most useful laboratory markers prompting consideration of HLH, yet its interpretation requires careful clinical context. Ferritin concentrations exceeding 10,000 ng/mL have been strongly associated with HLH, particularly in pediatric cohorts, although comparable elevations may also occur in severe infections and other hyperinflammatory conditions [14], [15], [18]. In dengue, marked ferritin elevation is believed to reflect intense immune activation, macrophage stimulation, and hepatocellular injury rather than being specific for irreversible hemophagocytic disease [3], [10], [11], [19], [24]. Accordingly, an isolated ferritin value should be interpreted as an indicator of inflammatory burden rather than definitive evidence of HLH.

In our patient, ferritin declined substantially from 21,210 ng/mL to 5407 ng/mL over four days without corticosteroids or other HLH-directed therapy, paralleling defervescence, hematologic recovery, and biochemical improvement. This coordinated decline supported the interpretation of a resolving inflammatory response rather than ongoing immune dysregulation. Conversely, persistent or rising ferritin levels despite appropriate supportive management, particularly when accompanied by worsening cytopenias or organ dysfunction, should heighten suspicion for evolving HLH and prompt reconsideration of immunomodulatory treatment [8], [9], [14], [23], [24].

Although both elevated soluble interleukin−2 receptor (sCD25) and bone marrow examination are incorporated into the diagnostic evaluation of suspected HLH, neither should be interpreted in isolation. Serum sCD25 reflects T-cell activation and immune dysregulation but lacks specificity, as elevated levels have also been reported in severe viral infections, including dengue, where exaggerated cytokine responses are central to disease pathogenesis [3], [4], [8], [10], [11], [17]. Likewise, bone marrow examination serves primarily to exclude alternative hematologic disorders and to provide supportive diagnostic information rather than to definitively confirm or exclude HLH. Hemophagocytosis is neither universally present nor specific for HLH and may be absent during the early phase of disease or occur in a patchy distribution [4], [9], [17], [23]. Accordingly, the mildly hypocellular marrow with preserved hematopoietic architecture and absence of overt hemophagocytosis observed in our patient were interpreted within the broader clinical context rather than as evidence against HLH. More importantly, these findings were accompanied by progressive reticulocytosis, rapid platelet recovery, declining ferritin concentrations, and continued clinical improvement, collectively indicating restoration of hematopoietic function rather than persistent macrophage-driven marrow failure. This integrated interpretation, rather than any single diagnostic test, ultimately supported continued close observation with supportive management while maintaining vigilance for potential disease progression. This case therefore illustrates that, in patients with dengue who transiently fulfill multiple HLH-associated criteria, the evolution of clinical and hematologic recovery may provide greater practical value for therapeutic decision-making than isolated abnormalities in specialized diagnostic tests [7], [8], [9], [23], [24].

The decision to defer HLH-directed immunosuppressive therapy in this patient was based on continuous clinical reassessment rather than fulfillment of diagnostic criteria alone. Although early corticosteroid-based treatment remains the cornerstone of therapy for confirmed or strongly suspected HLH because delayed treatment may result in rapid clinical deterioration and multiorgan failure, contemporary management strategies also emphasize careful evaluation of the underlying trigger, disease severity, and overall clinical trajectory before initiating immunomodulatory therapy [7], [8], [9], [14], [15], [20], [23], [24]. This distinction is particularly important in infection-associated hyperinflammatory syndromes, where indiscriminate immunosuppression may impair pathogen clearance, increase the risk of secondary infections, and expose patients with self-limited inflammatory responses to unnecessary treatment-related complications [9], [23], [24].

In our patient, sustained defervescence, hemodynamic stability, absence of neurological or other evolving organ dysfunction, together with coordinated laboratory recovery including rapid platelet normalization, progressive reticulocytosis, declining ferritin concentrations, improving coagulation parameters, and resolution of hepatocellular injury occurred without corticosteroids or other HLH-directed therapy. Collectively, these findings supported spontaneous resolution of dengue-associated hyperinflammation rather than persistent macrophage-driven immune dysregulation. Conversely, persistent or recurrent fever, worsening cytopenias, rising ferritin concentrations despite supportive care, progressive organ dysfunction, neurological involvement, hemodynamic instability, or failure of hematologic recovery should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis and strengthen the indication for early HLH-directed therapy [7], [8], [9], [23], [24]. Rather than proposing new diagnostic criteria, this case illustrates that integrating serial hematologic recovery patterns with clinical stability may complement existing HLH diagnostic frameworks and facilitate individualized therapeutic decision-making in patients with dengue-associated hyperinflammation.

A major contribution of this case is the emphasis on dynamic hematologic recovery patterns as complementary diagnostic clues in patients with dengue-associated hyperinflammation. Rather than relying on isolated laboratory abnormalities, we found that the coordinated emergence of reticulocytosis, rapid platelet recovery, declining ferritin concentrations, and improving hepatocellular injury more accurately reflected recovery from infection-induced immune activation than ongoing macrophage-driven hyperinflammation. Although each of these parameters has been described individually, their integrated longitudinal interpretation has received relatively little attention in the differentiation of dengue-associated hyperinflammation from secondary HLH. Our observations therefore support the incorporation of serial hematologic trends into clinical decision-making alongside established HLH diagnostic frameworks, particularly in dengue-endemic settings [7], [8], [9], [10], [22], [23], [24], [25].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this report describes a single patient, and the proposed diagnostic value of dynamic hematologic recovery patterns cannot be generalized without validation in larger prospective cohorts. Second, several specialized investigations recommended for HLH evaluation, including natural killer cell activity and cytokine profiling, were unavailable, although this reflects the diagnostic challenges encountered in many resource-limited settings. Third, while serum soluble CD25 was measured, serial assessment was not performed, limiting evaluation of its temporal relationship with clinical recovery. Finally, although hemophagocytosis was absent on bone marrow examination, this finding was not used to exclude HLH because its sensitivity is limited and it should be interpreted together with the overall clinical course.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the diagnostic challenge of differentiating dengue-associated hyperinflammation from secondary HLH in patients who transiently fulfill multiple HLH-associated criteria. Rather than relying on isolated laboratory abnormalities or diagnostic scores alone, interpretation of the overall clinical trajectory proved essential. Coordinated defervescence, progressive reticulocytosis, rapid platelet recovery, declining ferritin concentrations, and improving hepatic injury collectively supported a self-limited inflammatory response despite an initially high HScore. These findings suggest that serial hematologic recovery patterns may complement existing HLH diagnostic frameworks and assist clinicians in identifying patients who can be managed safely with supportive care while remaining vigilant for those who develop progressive disease requiring HLH-directed therapy. Further studies are warranted to validate these observations in larger cohorts of dengue-associated hyperinflammatory syndromes.
