The effect of the probiotic supplement—ASTARTE™—on the reduction of recurrent urinary tract infection in women aged 18-40 years: protocol for a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study
Authors: Khaled Saoud Ali Ghathian, Sofie Ingdam Halkjær, Karen Angeliki Krogfelt, Niels Frimodt-Møller, Katrine Hartung Hansen, Amalie Kruse Sigersted Frederiksen, Anne Holm, Andreas Munk Petersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUrinary tract infection (UTI) is one of the most common bacterial infections, contributing to increased antibiotic consumption and high healthcare costs. Clinical studies suggest that probiotics can be effective dietary supplements reducing the risk factors for the development of infections in the intestine and vagina. In this study, we investigate the effect of the orally ingested probiotic combination ASTARTE™ on the composition of bacteria in vaginal and fecal samples, as well as on the occurrence of bacteriologically confirmed UTIs in women of reproductive age.MethodsA randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind study evaluating the effect of the probiotic combination ASTARTE™ (Chr. Hansen A/S, Part of Novonesis Group, Denmark) on reducing the number of recurrent urinary tract infections (rUTIs) in women aged 18–40 years. Participants are randomized (1:1) to receive one daily probiotic capsule (5 × 109 CFU) containing Lactobacillus crispatus LBV 88™ (DSM22566), Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus LBV 96™ (DSM22560), Lactobacillus jensenii LBV 116™ (DSM22567), and Lactobacillus gasseri LBV 150N™ (DSM22583), or placebo, for 6 months. Assessments, including symptom questionnaires, urine culturing and microbiome analysis of vaginal and rectal samples by shotgun sequencing, are conducted at baseline, 2, 4, and 6 months. Compliance is tracked by unused capsule counts. Follow-up visits occur at 8, 10, and 12 months. The primary outcome is the incidence of symptomatic bacteriologically confirmed UTIs during the 6 months intervention period. The secondary objective is the effect of ASTARTE™ on changes in vaginal and gut microbiome.DiscussionThis randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind study investigates the effectiveness of ASTARTE™ in restoring beneficial microbiomes and reducing UTI recurrence. The findings will provide valuable evidence on the effectiveness of a non-antibiotic strategy for the prevention of rUTI as well as insights into the complex interactions between the vaginal and gut microbiome and the risk of rUTI.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT05553652. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

UTIs are among the most prevalent infections and are commonly caused by enteric bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae [1–4]. In some cases, vaginal bacteria, including Streptococcus agalactiae, may also be implicated [5, 6]. These infections contribute to high antibiotic usage and place a considerable financial burden on healthcare systems [1, 7]. UTIs encompass various conditions, including cystitis, pyelonephritis, prostatitis, urosepsis, and catheter-associated UTIs [8].

Research indicates that UTI prevalence increases with age [2, 8]. Among women over 65 years old, the prevalence is approximately 20%, which is twice as high as in the general female population, where it stands at around 11% [2–4]. It is estimated that 50% to 60% of adult women will experience at least one UTI in their lifetime, with nearly 10% of postmenopausal women reporting a UTI within the past year [2].

A study conducted in the United States suggests that sexually active women aged 18–39 years have a higher UTI risk than their non-sexually active counterparts [2]. After a first episode of a UTI, 27% of women have a confirmed recurrence within the next 6 months [2]. Factors contributing to frequent recurrences include vaginal colonization by enteric bacteria, reduced urinary flow, incomplete bladder emptying, anatomical and functional urological abnormalities, sexual activity, urinary incontinence, and low physical activity [4, 9].

A rUTI is defined as two infections within 6 months or 3 or more infections within 1 year [1, 2]. Repeated courses of antibiotics for rUTIs may disrupt the gut and vaginal microbiota and increase the risk of antibiotic resistance [10–12]. The frequent prescription of antibiotics raises concerns about the emergence of resistant uropathogenic bacteria. Many preventive measures have shown limited success and often suffer from poor patient adherence [12, 13], emphasizing the urgent need for alternative strategies beyond continuous antibiotic use.

The composition of the gut and vaginal microbiota plays a key role in UTI susceptibility [4, 14–16]. Imbalances in the gut microbiota (dysbiosis) can contribute to chronic inflammation, weakened mucosal barrier function, and increased permeability (“leaky gut”), potentially triggering systemic immune responses and elevating UTI risk [14, 17, 18]. Vaginal dysbiosis, marked by an overgrowth of enteric bacteria, raises the risk of bacterial migration to the urethra and bladder, leading to infection [4, 9]. As the vaginal microbiota naturally fluctuates over time, a decline in protective Lactobacillus species can further heighten susceptibility to UTIs [4, 15].

Several randomized clinical trials have examined the effectiveness of probiotics in preventing UTIs, with mixed outcomes influenced by treatment duration, bacterial strains used, and patient demographics [1, 16, 19–23]. It is hypothesized that consistent probiotic supplementation may help maintain a stable microbiota composition, fostering a protective, Lactobacillus-dominant microbiota in the gut, vagina, and urinary tract, thereby potentially reducing the risk of UTIs. These studies have primarily focused on comparing the incidence of symptomatic UTIs in probiotic-treated groups versus placebo groups over both intervention and follow-up periods. Probiotics are believed to help restore microbial balance, which could contribute to reducing the frequency of rUTIs. A lower recurrence rate among individuals receiving probiotics would reinforce their potential role in UTI prevention, while simultaneously aiding in reducing antibiotic use.

Existing research suggests that probiotics such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium support intestinal health by strengthening the gut mucosal barrier, enhancing immune function, and inhibiting colonization of pathogenic bacteria [1, 24–26]. Since probiotics naturally reside in the gut and vaginal microbiota, they are considered safe and may help regulate microbial communities in the urinary, vaginal, and gastrointestinal tracts, potentially reducing the risk of dysbiosis and infection. The ASTARTE™ product in our study consists of four probiotic Lactobacillus strains derived from the vaginal tract of healthy pregnant women. It has been shown to support a balanced vaginal microbiome and alleviate bacterial imbalances [27–29].

This study aims to explore whether specific combinations of Lactobacillus species can contribute to a reduction in rUTI incidence.

Trial design {8}

This study is designed as a superiority trial comparing ASTARTE™ to placebo. Women with rUTI will be invited to participate in a 6-month randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study with a 6 months follow-up period. Participants are randomly assigned 1:1 to either the probiotic group or the placebo group, and they remain in their assigned group for the duration of the study.

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting {9}

Participants will be recruited based on urine samples analyzed at the Clinical Microbiology Departments of Copenhagen University Hospitals Hvidovre and Herlev, Denmark (1,145 general practitioner clinics) and Slagelse Hospital in Region Zealand, Denmark (237 general practitioner clinics). Data on inclusion/exclusion criteria, including urine culture results, UTI frequency within 1 year before recruitment, gender, and age, will be collected from laboratory records. Women with rUTIs are invited to an information and screening visit at Copenhagen University Hospital Hvidovre. Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

Comparator in this study is a placebo capsule that is identical to the probiotic capsule in appearance, taste, and packaging to maintain blinding. A placebo control is used to account for the placebo effect, where participants may experience perceived or real improvements due to their expectations of treatment. This ensures that any observed effect is due to the probiotics and not psychological or other confounding factors.

Intervention description {11a}

Participants are given either probiotic capsules ASTARTE™ or placebo capsules respectively. One capsule is taken daily in the morning with food for 6 months.

Test product

The active product is an ASTARTE™ oral capsule containing a mixture of four probiotic strains with a combined potency of 5 × 109 CFU/capsule, containing the following:2 × 109 CFU/capsule Lactobacillus crispatus LBV 88™ (DSM22566)2 × 109 CFU/capsule Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus LBV 96 ™ (DSM22560)0.4 × 109 CFU/capsule Lactobacillus jensenii LBV 116 ™ (DSM22567)0.6 × 109 CFU/capsule Lactobacillus gasseri LBV 150 N ™ (DSM22583)

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

If a participant experiences adverse effects or complications, the intervention may be modified or discontinued to prevent harm. If the intervention proves ineffective (e.g., the participant’s condition worsens), it may be stopped or replaced with an alternative treatment. Adjustments may also be necessary if a participant cannot adhere to the protocol requirements, ensuring the study remains safe and ethical. Participants may withdraw or request changes to their intervention if they experience discomfort, unwanted side effects, or do not want to continue.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

Member of the research team provides participants with thorough information about the intervention, including its benefits, risks, and importance. Members of the research team use scheduled regular check-ins, such as phone calls, emails, or clinic visits, to track participants’ adherence and address any issues early. A member of the research team will maintain regular contact with participants throughout the intervention period. Participants will be contacted every 2 months via scheduled clinic visits, email, and telephone calls. In addition, automated reminders via SMS and email will be used to prompt capsule intake and questionnaire completion. This structured follow-up is intended to support adherence and allow early identification of any challenges.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

It is not allowed to take other probiotics during intervention and 6 months after. All medications are allowed, and the participants’ medication list will be updated continuously.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

Participation in this study is voluntary, and no compensation or other financial benefits are provided to participants.

Sample size {14}

We aim to include 738 participants based on a power calculation by Danish company SIGNIFIKANS ApS, Vedbæk, Denmark. The test statistic used is the two-sided Fisher’s Exact test, Software PASS version 12.0.10. Assuming a recurrence rate of 20% in the probiotic group versus 30% in the placebo group, with α = 0.05, β (power) = 80%, and an enrollment ratio of 1:1, the required sample size is 626 participants (313 in the placebo group and 313 in the probiotic group). Accounting for an approximate 15% dropout rate, a total of 738 participants will be enrolled (369 in the placebo group and 369 in the probiotic group). Sample size will be adjusted after an interim analysis after inclusion of 138 patients.

Recruitment {15}

Pre-study planning

The inclusion and exclusion criteria are not overly restrictive, as this can limit the eligible pool of participants. Previous studies and realistic recruitment rates were examined to establish a feasible timeline and determine the necessary resources. Laboratory data from the years 2020 to 2021 were analyzed to estimate the number of participants eligible for invitation to this study.

Efforts are made to engage with other Danish hospitals and relevant institutions while leveraging social media and online platforms. Clear and appealing messaging is used, tailored to the target demographic, and emphasizing study benefits, such as health insights or incentives. Recruitment materials will be sent via digital mail (Danish e-Boks). Potential participants are given sufficient time to review study materials and the option to contact the project phone with any questions before scheduling a screening interview. Personalized communication is maintained every 2 months to ensure participant engagement. Flexible scheduling includes phone and online conversations from 8:00 to 18:00 (Monday to Friday), with the option to collect samples from private addresses. A dedicated member of the research team and coordinators have been hired to focus on recruitment. The recruitment process is continuously assessed and refined to align with the study’s objectives.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

Prior to the start of the study, the randomization list is produced by SIGNIFIKANS ApS, Vedbæk, Denmark, using the PROC PLAN procedure in SAS version 9.4. To maintain the study blinding, the randomization list is developed and quality controlled by an external statistician. Particularities of the randomization, such as block size, are stated in the randomization list itself and will not be disclosed to the investigator or study personnel. A computer-generated random number is used to ensure unbiased assignments. The participants are randomized to either the probiotic or placebo group in a 1:1 ratio. The investigational capsules and the placebo capsules are similar in smell, taste, and appearance. All study products are packaged in identical vials with identical labelling, except for the randomization number.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

Participants are included via consecutive numbers that are randomized in advance to either the active or placebo group.

Implementation {16c}

Member of the research team enroll participants consecutively by using a computerized IT system REDCap (https://project-redcap.org/) and assign them a participant number, which is also labeled on the capsules provided. The system is also used for data storage, questionnaire, visit records, statistical analyses, etc.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

Research assistants, coordinators, and participants will be blinded. The identity of the capsules is unknown to participants, researchers, and primary investigators.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

Unblinding will be performed only when necessary for patient safety or in cases where knowledge of the treatment assignment is essential for clinical decision-making. The process will involve notifying the study sponsor and principal investigator, documenting the reason for unblinding, and accessing the randomization key using the emergency unblinding cards. All instances of unblinding will be recorded, including the date, reason, and personnel involved.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

The study involves multiple visits over the course of a year to monitor the participants’ health, collect relevant samples, and assess the participants’ adherence to the intervention. Initial visits include informed consent and screening, followed by regular follow-ups at 2, 4, and 6 months to track progress, collect data, and monitor compliance. If participants experience symptoms of a UTI, additional visits for diagnosis and treatment are included. At each visit the participant will receive the BFLUTS questionnaire on urinary tract symptoms, side effects, and UTI episodes will be retrieved from the laboratory IT register. At each visit, midstream urine samples are cultured to identify uropathogenic bacteria, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing is conducted. Rectal and vaginal swabs are analyzed for microbiome composition using shotgun metagenomic sequencing techniques. After 8, 10, and 12 months, participants will complete the BFLUTS questionnaire and report the number of UTI episodes experienced since discontinuing the capsule regimen.

Data management {19}

Data collected throughout the study will be securely stored and managed to ensure confidentiality, integrity, and compliance with ethical and regulatory standards. All participant data will be stored in a secure, encrypted REDCap system, which is designed to meet industry standards for data protection. The REDCap platform employs robust encryption protocols, both during data transmission and while at rest, ensuring that sensitive information is always safeguarded. Access to the system is strictly controlled through login authentication mechanisms, ensuring that only authorized personnel can access or modify data. REDCap provides comprehensive audit trails, enabling full traceability of all data entry, modifications, and user actions. This ensures transparency and accountability in the management of study data. Every interaction with the system, including data input, updates, and deletions, is logged with time stamps and user identification, which allows for accurate monitoring of data integrity throughout the study. Access to participant data will be restricted to authorized study team members based on predefined roles and responsibilities. Regular backups of all study data will be conducted to prevent data loss in the event of system failure. A robust data recovery plan will be in place to restore data quickly and effectively in the event of any unforeseen incidents. Data management practices will adhere to applicable data protection regulations, including the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and other relevant ethical standards. Data anonymization and de-identification procedures will be implemented where applicable to ensure participant privacy and confidentiality.

Participants will be assigned unique study IDs to avoid direct identification. Only authorized personnel will access identifiable information. Shared data will be de-identified and transferred securely. Collaborators must comply with data protection protocols. Personal data will be stored on secure servers with restricted access and encryption. Identifiable details will be stored separately from research data and linked only via study IDs. Data will be retained only as needed and securely destroyed afterward per regulatory requirements. After the study, identifiable data will be destroyed after 01.08.2032, and de-identified data may be retained for future analysis.

Plans for collection, laboratory evaluation and storage of biological specimens for genetic or molecular analysis in this trial/future use {33}

During the first introductory meeting, the study participants are thoroughly introduced to how sample collection will take place, and an additional introduction is offered at each visit to the clinic. The participant is required to hand over the samples to the laboratory on the same day it is collected to ensure the integrity and accuracy of the analysis. This timely delivery of samples minimizes potential degradation or contamination of the sample, thereby optimizing the reliability of subsequent laboratory results. After sample analysis, the remaining sample material will be frozen in 10% glycerol at −80 °C in a research biobank until the project’s completion, after which it will be destroyed if reanalysis is not required.

Mid-stream urine sample

The urine is collected to identify uropathogenic microorganisms and determine whether the participant has a UTI. After washing the genital area with water or wet wipes, midstream urine should be collected in a glass container with boric acid (SARSTEDT, Denmark). The participants collect the sample themselves, preferably as their first-morning urine. Urine culture is performed within 24 h of collection.

Rectal swab

A rectal swab (Fecal Swab, Copan, Italy) for microbiome analysis is collected for the purpose of using shotgun metagenomic sequencing techniques. A sterile swab is inserted approximately 2 cm into the rectum, gently rotated for 20–30 s, and then carefully withdrawn. The participants collect the sample themselves, which is then placed in the provided transport tube or handed over to a member of the research team during study visits. The sample is processed within 48 h and stored at −80 °C for later microbiome composition analysis.

Vaginal swab

A vaginal swab (ESwab®, Copan, Italy) for microbiome analysis is collected for the purpose of using shotgun metagenomic sequencing techniques. The participants collect the sample themselves. The swab is held in one hand, while the other hand is placed on the labia. The swab is then inserted approximately 5 cm into the vagina and rotated for 30–50 s, ensuring contact with the inner vaginal wall. The swab is then removed and placed in a transport tube. The sample is processed within 48 h and stored at −80 °C for later microbiome composition analysis.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

The primary outcome, the incidence of bacteriologically confirmed UTIs requiring antibiotic treatment, will be analyzed using a time-to-event analysis with Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests to compare differences between intervention groups. A Cox proportional hazards model will be used to estimate UTI ratios, adjusting for potential confounders such as age, previous UTI history, and baseline microbiome composition.

For the secondary outcomes, the number of UTIs within 6 and 12 months will be analyzed using Poisson or negative binomial regression models, depending on data distribution. The duration of UTI episodes will be compared between groups using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed data. Changes in vaginal and gut microbiome composition will be assessed using paired and independent t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Alpha diversity indices will be compared using the Shannon and Simpson indices, while beta diversity will be evaluated through principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, with PERMANOVA used to test for significant differences between groups.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {20b}

We conduct antibiotic susceptibility testing on pathogenic bacteria from urine and fecal samples to investigate the presence of resistant bacteria, such as Carbapenemase producing organisms (CPO), extended spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL), vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE), and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), and multidrug resistant bacteria (MDR) defined as non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories [31]. We will use disc-diffusion according to The European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) and PCR (polymerase chain reaction) test.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

We will use Multiple Imputation, which generates several datasets by imputing missing values based on observed data and its variability, and then combines the results. Multiple Imputation assumes that data are missing at random and accounts for uncertainty in the missing values. The analysis population will be defined based on protocol adherence, with compliance set at a minimum threshold of 80%. Participants who complete at least 80% of the prescribed intervention and adhere to study procedures, including sample collection and follow-up visits, will be included in the per-protocol analysis.

The intention-to-treat population will include all randomized participants, regardless of protocol adherence, following the principle of analyzing data based on initial treatment assignment. Sensitivity analyses may be conducted to assess the impact of non-adherence on study outcomes. Participants with less than 80% compliance will be categorized as protocol deviations and analyzed separately to determine their influence on the results.

Discussion

Most urinary tract infections originate from the gut microbiota, making it logical to explore the link between gut microorganisms and the development of bacteriuria and UTIs [33]. Recent findings have also challenged the traditional diagnostic and therapeutic paradigms for UTIs by highlighting the complex, symbiotic microbiome present in a healthy urogenital tract [3, 34]. These insights reveal that UTIs are not merely caused by external invasion but often arise from intricate interactions between host-associated microbial communities from gut and vagina [14, 15].

In women, vaginal microbiota significantly influence the pathogenesis of UTIs, acting as an intermediary in the journey of bacteria from the gut to the urinary tract. However, the ultimate source of the bacterial strains responsible for conditions such as cystitis and pyelonephritis is frequently the gut microbiota. This interplay between gut and vaginal microbiota demonstrates a dynamic crosstalk and interconnection between these two niches, emphasizing the need for a holistic approach to understanding and management of UTIs [35]. Such insights not only reshape our perception of UTI pathogenesis but also open new avenues for therapeutic strategies aimed at targeting these microbial ecosystems. By analyzing changes in microbiota composition across fecal, vaginal, and urinary samples, we can assess the role of probiotics in restoring or maintaining a beneficial microbiome. Furthermore, potential correlations between microbiota alterations and symptomatic improvement will be investigated to better understand its therapeutic impact.

The relationship between the gut microbiota and UTIs can be attributed to several interconnected factors. One key aspect is gut dysbiosis, which can contribute to a pro-inflammatory state within the intestine [14]. When there is an imbalance in the types and proportions of gut bacteria, it may elicit an inappropriate immune response, resulting in chronic inflammation [14, 36]. Additionally, a disrupted gut microbiota can weaken the intestinal mucosal barrier, leading to increased intestinal permeability, a condition often referred to as “leaky gut” [17, 18]. This compromised barrier allows bacteria, toxins, and other molecules to translocate from the gut into the bloodstream, potentially triggering systemic immune responses or infections in other regions, such as the urinary tract. Understanding these mechanisms highlights the complex interplay between gut health and susceptibility to UTIs. This study will assess microbiome stabilization, demonstrating whether regular intake of probiotics maintains or restores a protective lactobacilli-dominant microbiome in the gut, vaginal, and urinary tracts, which is associated with a reduced risk of UTIs. The impact of probiotics on reducing risk factors for rUTIs is assessed by primary outcomes such as the incidence of symptomatic UTIs during the intervention and follow-up periods. We aim to evaluate whether the probiotic group demonstrates significant health benefits over the placebo group. The comparative efficacy of the intervention will be assessed by comparing outcomes between the placebo group and the probiotic group. If the probiotic group exhibits fewer episodes of UTI recurrence, it will indicate the potential of probiotics as a preventive strategy.

A study by Adu-Oppong B et al. [37] confirms a significant overlap between symptomatic and asymptomatic genitourinary microbiota states. This raises the question of whether establishing a baseline for gut and vaginal microbiota in the placebo group is necessary. Such a baseline would allow us to compare microbiota states between the placebo and probiotic groups, as well as between individuals with symptomatic and asymptomatic bacteriuria, helping to identify any meaningful patterns or differences.

Shotgun sequencing is a powerful tool for microbiome analysis, offering high-resolution taxonomic and functional insights by sequencing entire genomes from a sample. However, the reliability of shotgun sequencing for microbiome analysis is affected by limitations that can impact sensitivity, specificity, and data interpretation [38]. Limitations of shotgun sequencing may include factors such as the type of reference database used, the sequencing method chosen (e.g., 16S amplicon sequencing or whole genome sequencing (WGS)), the amount of human DNA present in the sample, and other technical considerations. These limitations may influence the accuracy of identifying bacterial species and affect the comparison of microbiome composition between placebo and active groups, as well as between symptomatic and asymptomatic bacteriuria.

This study highlights the potential of probiotics as a non-antibiotic intervention to reduce recurrent UTIs, addressing concerns related to antibiotic resistance and overuse [3, 39]. To support this, we will evaluate the prevalence of uropathogenic bacteria both at baseline and after the intervention. Additionally, we will assess whether probiotics have an impact on the eradication of uropathogenic bacteria.
