Targeted nanomedicine strategies for Alzheimer’s disease therapy
Authors: Mustafa T Ardah, Baraa Mohammed Yaseen, H Malathi, Subhashree Ray, R Thyagarajan, Aman Shankhyan, Rasulbek Eshmetov, Zokir Ataullaev, Manoj Kumar Mishra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder and is characterized by amyloid-beta deposition, tau pathology, synaptic dysfunction, and progressive cognitive decline. Currently approved symptomatic therapies, including acetylcholinesterase inhibitors and the NMDA receptor antagonist memantine, provide modest and time-limited benefit and do not directly modify upstream disease drivers. This review synthesizes recent nanomedicine strategies that aim to bridge this gap by integrating biomarker-oriented nanosensors and imaging probes for earlier detection with targeted nanocarriers designed to overcome delivery barriers, particularly the blood–brain barrier, while improving pharmacokinetics and limiting off-target exposure. We highlight converging design principles, including stimulus-responsive release, receptor- and ligand-guided targeting, biomimetic coatings, and organelle-focused delivery to mitochondria and lysosome-autophagy pathways. Beyond repackaging existing agents, nano-enabled approaches are discussed in relation to amyloid and tau clearance or neutralization, redox and mitochondrial rescue, microglia-centered immunomodulation, and regenerative support for neuronal and neurovascular repair. To move beyond a descriptive overview, this review presents a stage-informed and pathology-guided framework for matching nanomedicine design to amyloid-predominant, tau-dominant, neuroinflammatory, mitochondrial, and advanced neurovascular phenotypes. We also evaluate translational constraints, including long-term safety, biodistribution, reproducibility, immunogenicity, scalable manufacturing, regulatory characterization requirements, and the trade-off between biological sophistication and clinical manufacturability. Finally, we distinguish platforms with nearer-term translational potential, such as selected lipid, polymeric, and extracellular vesicle-based systems, from exploratory multifunctional inorganic or highly complex biomimetic designs. This balanced framing clarifies where nanomedicine may realistically advance disease-modifying therapy while identifying evidence gaps that still limit translation.Graphical abstract Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Nanomedicine, Targeted drug delivery, Amyloid beta, Neuroinflammation

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease is the most frequent neurodegenerative illness worldwide [1–3]. It is defined by extracellular senile plaques composed of amyloid beta (Aβ) peptides and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles containing Tau proteins [4–6]. The leading clinical feature is a profound and steadily worsening dementia. Imaging techniques such as positron emission tomography and magnetic resonance imaging, together with fluid and tissue markers that reflect amyloidosis, tauopathy, and neurodegeneration [2, 7], support diagnosis and allow classification into preclinical disease, prodromal disease, and dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease (AD). In everyday practice, however, confirmation usually follows the recognition of cognitive decline reported by the affected person or close contacts, [7–10] and a substantial proportion of patients are still identified after meaningful neuropathology has accumulated, often at a middle or advanced stage [11]. This diagnostic lag narrows the therapeutic window and increases the likelihood that symptomatic approaches dominate clinical care. It also highlights the need for therapeutic platforms that can be adapted to disease stage, dominant pathology, and the biological barriers present at the time of intervention.

Early disease is marked by a gradual loss of cholinergic neurons, and during this window memory performance may improve when cholinergic tone is raised [2, 3, 7]. For this reason, enhancement of cholinergic signaling emerged as an initial therapeutic pathway [11, 12]. Current agents that increase cholinergic activity fall into two groups, acetylcholinesterase inhibitors and butyrylcholinesterase inhibitors [7, 13]. Tacrine once produced similar benefits but is no longer in routine use because of marked liver toxicity [14]. Memantine, a low affinity antagonist of the N methyl D aspartate receptor [11, 14], is also widely prescribed and appears to act by limiting glutamate mediated excitotoxic injury [15]. Although these medicines can slow symptom progression, [16]they do not address upstream drivers of pathology, [10, 17]and therefore postpone decline rather than provide disease modification [18].Even with emerging disease-modifying directions, clinical benefit remains variable and is frequently constrained by safety, monitoring burden, and patient selection, reinforcing the need for more precise and better tolerated strategies. This reality imposes a heavy emotional and economic load on people living with the disorder and on their families. With the continued aging of populations around the world [3, 12] the prevalence of this age related illness is expected to climb further in the coming years [19].These limitations indicate that future therapeutic design should not only improve brain delivery, but should also align the delivered payload with the pathological context, such as amyloid predominance, Tau-driven intracellular dysfunction, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial injury, or neurovascular degeneration.

Over recent decades, the medical use of nanotechnology has accelerated progress in biosensing and controlled delivery [20, 21]. Engineered materials that enhance the performance of biological components and convert signals with high efficiency have been widely used to build sensors and probes that can detect amyloidosis [21, 22] tauopathy, and neurodegeneration. Nanotechnology has also reshaped therapeutic development for AD by enabling strategies that address core translational barriers, particularly limited brain exposure, off target toxicity, and heterogeneous Blood–brain barrier (BBB) transport across disease stages [21–23]. Extracts from traditional Chinese remedies, including curcumin and ginkgolide, have drawn attention for environmentally friendly production,[24] modest cost, and multi target activity. Many studies show activity against disease related pathways in cell models, [24, 25] yet in vivo use is restricted by instability, poor solubility, low exposure after dosing, and short residence time. Nanoscale platforms can counter these drawbacks and support movement toward the clinic [26–28]. With the rise of precision medicine, targeted nanomedicines have become mainstream [29, 30] because they can limit drug deposition in healthy tissues while concentrating agents at sites of injury. For this reason, approved medicines for AD are being paired with nanoscale carriers [26, 31, 32].Reformulating donepezil as a nanomedicine can extend its half-life and improve brain targeting, increasing drug levels in the nervous system while lowering accumulation in other organs, which can boost benefit and reduce harm [29, 33]. As methods advance, nanoscale delivery now extends beyond drugs with weak in vivo properties and includes diverse therapeutic classes, including small molecules, biologics, and nucleic acid payloads, and particles that load more than one compound have enabled rational co delivery [34–36]. However, the translational value of these systems depends on more than biological activity. Platform simplicity, reproducibility, scalable production, regulatory characterization, and long-term safety must be considered alongside targeting efficiency and therapeutic potency.

At the same time, unique biological actions of these materials are being uncovered that go beyond simple carriage. Black phosphorus based constructs can act as antioxidants, [37] and graphene oxide modified with polyethylene glycol has shown protection of neural tissue through modulation of the PIP2 endoplasmic reticulum stress pathway [37, 38]. These properties can be paired with medicines to create synergistic regimens [39]. In addition, many of these materials are readily engineered and can be tuned for selective engagement through strategies such as membrane coating and chemical conjugation [40, 41] which support targeted action and maintain compatibility with living systems. Yet increasing nanoplatform complexity may also create barriers to clinical translation, including batch-to-batch variability, uncertain biodistribution, immunogenicity, difficult quality control, and challenges in manufacturing multifunctional or biomimetic systems at scale [21, 42]. Accordingly, this review connects Alzheimer’s pathobiology with nano enabled solutions across three linked goals: (i) earlier and more sensitive detection, (ii) targeted delivery and multi pathway intervention, and (iii) support for neuronal and neurovascular repair, while emphasizing translational constraints such as safety, reproducibility, and manufacturability. Rather than treating nanomedicine as a single therapeutic category, the review develops a stage-informed and pathology-guided framework for selecting strategies according to the dominant disease context. In this framework, early or amyloid-predominant disease may benefit most from BBB-preserving delivery, amyloid sensing, Aβ capture, or production-suppressing approaches; Tau-dominant disease requires intracellular delivery and autophagy-lysosome engagement; neuroinflammatory phenotypes favor microglia-targeted or immunomodulatory systems; and advanced disease with neuronal or neurovascular loss may require regenerative or repair-oriented platforms. This framework also distinguishes platforms with nearer-term clinical feasibility, such as selected lipid, polymeric, and extracellular vesicle-based systems, from more exploratory approaches, including highly complex inorganic, photodynamic, sonodynamic, and extensively engineered biomimetic designs. Taken together, nanotechnology may support more precise and mechanism-informed intervention in AD, but its clinical value will depend on whether platform design can be matched to disease biology while meeting safety, manufacturing, and regulatory requirements.

The pathophysiology of AD

Despite many years of study, the processes that initiate and drive AD progression remain debated. Several conceptual frameworks have been proposed, most commonly emphasizing oxidative stress, neuroinflammation [43–45] Aβ accumulation [46, 47] and Tau deposition [10, 48]Importantly, accumulating evidence indicates that these mechanisms can reinforce one another through feed-forward interactions. AD pathobiology therefore appears less like a single linear cascade and more like an interconnected, stage-dependent network in which the dominant driver may vary across disease stages and between individuals. Rather than detailing competing hypotheses in isolation, this section summarizes the major, disease-relevant factors that shape AD and provide mechanistic rationale for targeted intervention strategies.This distinction is important for nanomedicine design because the optimal carrier, payload, release mechanism, and cellular target may differ between amyloid-predominant, Tau-dominant, inflammatory, oxidative, and neurovascular phenotypes.

Across models, abnormal Aβ metabolism is frequently positioned as a central contributor to downstream neuronal dysfunction [10, 49]. Studies in cellular systems suggest that ferric ions can interact with amyloid precursor protein (APP) messenger RNA and modulate APP expression [49, 50]. Under physiological conditions, APP is primarily processed via non-amyloidogenic pathways, including cleavage by alpha secretase, generating fragments that are not considered pathogenic [5, 49]. In contrast, familial AD-linked mutations in presenilin genes can shift processing toward beta and gamma secretase activity, increasing generation of aggregation-prone Aβ42 species [50]. Metal ions contribute in complex ways to these events. While they can facilitate Aβ-associated injury, they also promote reactive oxygen species generation through Fenton-type chemistry and can trigger regulated cell death pathways [49, 51, 52]. Notably, the biological impact of specific ions can depend on the surrounding redox environment, highlighting that metal–Aβ interactions may be context dependent rather than uniformly detrimental. This context dependence supports the need for therapeutic systems that can respond to local biochemical conditions rather than relying only on fixed systemic exposure.

Aβ42 monomers can assemble into soluble oligomers that exert neurotoxicity through multiple, partially overlapping routes [43, 50]. First, oligomers can promote calcium dysregulation by engaging N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors and activating downstream mediators such as phospholipase C, which can provoke endoplasmic reticulum stress and ultimately trigger apoptosis [46]. Second, oligomers can compromise membrane integrity through conformational states with distinct biophysical effects [53]. Beta-sheet–rich assemblies may insert into lipid bilayers to form pore-like structures, whereas ring-shaped species can destabilize membranes during fibril growth [54, 55]. Third, oligomers can disrupt mitochondrial function, reducing mitochondrial membrane potential and increasing reactive oxygen species generation.[45, 58] Excess brain metal ions can amplify this stress by accelerating Fenton-type reactions and depleting glutathione [56, 57], thereby intensifying mitochondrial injury. These combined insults can activate caspase-3-dependent apoptosis pathways [43, 58]. The relationship between Aβ and mitochondrial dysfunction is not strictly unidirectional [43, 59], since mitochondrial impairment has been reported in settings that precede overt plaque formation, suggesting that Aβ may magnify pre-existing vulnerabilities and contribute to a self-reinforcing oxidative stress loop [43, 56]. Fourth, oligomers can damage endosomal and lysosomal membranes, enabling leakage of hydrolytic enzymes and promoting neuronal injury [60]. In parallel, oligomers can interconvert with fibrillar species that constitute a major component of plaques, although the relative toxicity of mature fibrils versus soluble oligomers remains actively debated. For therapeutic development, this diversity of Aβ species implies that amyloid-directed nanomedicines should specify whether they aim to suppress production, intercept oligomerization, neutralize soluble assemblies, promote clearance, or destabilize mature deposits.

Normally, intracellular proteostasis systems, including autophagy, help detect and clear misfolded and aggregated proteins [46]. Presenilin proteins contribute to lysosomal calcium homeostasis and the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes, so presenilin mutations can impair autophagic flux [8, 61]. Such defects favor accumulation of intracellular oligomers and fibrils, with subsequent export and extracellular deposition. These extracellular deposits can disrupt neighboring cells, including resident immune cells, thereby coupling proteinopathy to neuroinflammation and reinforcing stage-dependent feedback loops (Fig. 1).Because lysosomal and autophagy dysfunction affects both Aβ and Tau handling, it also provides a shared rationale for organelle-targeted nanocarriers, endosomal escape strategies, and platforms designed to restore intracellular clearance rather than simply deliver cargo to the brain.

The pathological mechanisms of Alzheimer’s disease include three interlinked cascades: (1) the Aβ cascade involving aggregation, Ca2⁺ imbalance, ER stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and autophagy failure; (2) the Tau cascade involving hyperphosphorylation, cytoskeletal disruption, transport impairment, and synaptic dysfunction; and (3) the neuroinflammation cascade driven by microglial activation, inflammasome signaling, and cytokine release. These pathways interact with each other, ultimately leading to neuronal death and cognitive decline

Microglia serve as immune sentinels of the central nervous system (CNS) and support local homeostasis [62, 63]. They can internalize extracellular Aβ oligomers and fibrils, while pattern-recognition receptors detect abnormal protein assemblies and activate inflammatory signaling pathways. This sensing can engage NF-kappa B signaling and, under oxidative conditions, promote recruitment of the NLRP3 complex [64, 65]. NLRP3 can then associate with ASC and pro-caspase-1 to form an inflammasome complex that supports release of pro-inflammatory mediators and sustains neuroinflammation [65, 66]. These inflammatory outputs may further accelerate pathology through feed-forward mechanisms, including increased beta secretase expression and enhanced Aβ production, as well as prostaglandin E2 mediated glutamatergic dysregulation that can exacerbate NMDA receptor linked excitotoxicity [53]. Accordingly, inflammatory AD phenotypes may require nanomedicine strategies that do more than remove protein aggregates, including microglia-biased delivery, inflammasome modulation, and controlled immunoregulatory release.

AD is also a tauopathy characterized by abnormal accumulation of modified Tau protein [23, 67]. Tau function is regulated by post-translational modifications including phosphorylation, acetylation, and glycosylation. In health, Tau binds tubulin and supports microtubule stability. In disease, zinc and ferrous ions have been linked to aberrant Tau modification, and Tau becomes hyperphosphorylated [67, 68]. This reduces Tau tubulin affinity, increases self-association, and promotes formation of Tau oligomers and eventually neurofibrillary tangles. These changes impair cytoskeletal integrity, disrupt synaptic function, and induce endoplasmic reticulum stress. Hyperphosphorylated Tau can also hinder axonal transport by interfering with dynein and kinesin. In parallel, pathological Tau can impair autophagy and lysosome function, promoting further aggregation and facilitating spread of abnormal proteins via extracellular vesicles and prion-like mechanisms [68]. Aβ and Tau pathology are tightly coupled. Aβ can activate glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta and promote Tau hyperphosphorylation, while pathological Tau can, in turn, modulate Aβ processing and amplify toxicity in vulnerable circuits [67]. Crosstalk with microglia adds further complexity. Specific Tau isoforms, including Tau 410 and Tau 441, have been reported to engage polyglutamine binding protein 1 and activate the cGAS and STING axis, thereby enhancing NF-kappa B activity and cytokine release [23]. Conversely, inhibition of NLRP3 signaling in microglia has been associated with attenuation of Tau-related injury, although the temporal ordering and causal directionality across these pathways remain incompletely resolved [64, 65]. These observations support a stage-dependent network in which Aβ proteostasis, Tau dysregulation, neuroinflammatory signaling, and oxidative mitochondrial injury amplify each other through feed-forward loops. Accordingly, therapeutic prioritization is unlikely to be uniform across patients, and the most effective strategies may require matching both mechanism and delivery to disease stage and to the dominant cellular context. These interacting cascades are summarized schematically in Fig. 1 and provide the mechanistic basis for targeted intervention strategies discussed in subsequent sections. For Tau-dominant disease, this logic particularly favors intracellular delivery systems, endosomal escape, autophagy-lysosome engagement, and careful distinction between diagnostic Tau binding and therapeutically meaningful Tau clearance.

Taken together, current evidence supports a complex and stage-dependent disease process involving Aβ species, Tau dysregulation, neuroinflammatory signaling, oxidative injury, metal ion imbalance, and impaired autophagy, among other interacting elements. Because the field still lacks a definitive hierarchy of initiating causes and universally actionable therapeutic nodes, mechanistic clarification remains essential for rational target selection and for designing delivery strategies that match disease stage and cellular context (Table 1). In this sense, biomarker-based classification should not only support diagnosis, but also guide the selection of nanomedicine strategies by identifying the dominant pathology, the likely therapeutic window, and the biological barrier most relevant to each patient group.

Targeted drug delivery using nanomedicine

The benefit of any therapy depends on more than intrinsic potency. Effective accumulation at the site of injury is essential. For this reason, evaluation of treatment for this condition should prioritize regional target engagement and pharmacodynamic effects in affected circuits rather than average exposure across the entire brain [81, 82]. Nanomedicine offers two complementary answers to this challenge. One approach uses disease-specific features or externally applied cues as gated triggers to release cargo preferentially within pathological tissue, enabling responsive release and focused delivery. The other approach designs carriers that home to relevant cells and molecular pathways by using selective biomolecules for recognition and targeting 29, 33. In practice, the strongest platforms increasingly combine both concepts within a single design, which improves specificity while reducing systemic exposure [83, 84]. For AD, however, delivery design should also be interpreted in a stage-aware manner, because BBB integrity, inflammatory tone, amyloid and Tau burden, vascular dysfunction, and neuronal loss differ across the disease continuum.

Stimulus-responsive release in the AD microenvironment

A wide range of stimulus-responsive nanomaterials now enables the design of gated nanosystems [28, 85]. These carriers can be tailored through covalent attachment or physical adsorption [86]. Depending on composition, the platforms can react to endogenous cues such as reactive oxygen species, local acidity, and hypoxia. Multiple responsive designs have emerged, including modular constructs built from cyclodextrins, cucurbiturils, and other supramolecular hosts that liberate their payload when they encounter specific disease-related proteins [66, 87, 88]. Certain metal–organic frameworks also release cargo under acidic conditions after cleavage of coordinate bonds [66]. Hallmark changes in this condition, including excess metal ions, elevated reactive oxygen species, and abnormal amyloid species, provide fertile triggers for such systems [89]. Because these platforms sense dynamic microenvironmental shifts, they support intelligent delivery that can limit overtreatment and help preserve local homeostasis. Since pathology involves many cell types and several damaging factors accumulate outside cells, on-site release can reshape the broader diseased milieu rather than a single target alone [21, 35]. However, microenvironmental signatures are not static across disease stages or brain regions, so stimulus thresholds and release kinetics should be discussed and optimized in a stage-aware manner [90, 91]. This is particularly important for multifunctional systems, because adding multiple responsive modules may improve biological precision but can also increase manufacturing complexity, analytical burden, and variability in release behavior.

BBB crossing, BBB modulation, and post-entry targeting

Targeted delivery in this condition is constrained by the BBB, which is neither uniformly intact nor uniformly disrupted across the course of disease. In early stages, barrier dysfunction can be subtle and regionally heterogeneous, whereas later stages may show greater permeability, vascular remodeling, and impaired clearance, yet still with marked spatial variability. This matters because BBB state influences not only penetration but also the balance between delivery and repair. Therefore, nanomedicine strategies can be framed as (i) BBB crossing, (ii) BBB modulation or repair, and (iii) parenchymal targeting after entry [85, 86, 92]. For BBB crossing, prevalent mechanisms include receptor-mediated transcytosis and adsorptive transcytosis, as well as transport via carrier systems that engage endothelial receptors and shuttle cargo into the brain [93]. Alternative routes such as intranasal delivery and externally assisted approaches such as focused ultrasound can also augment brain exposure in selected settings, but their stage-dependence and safety constraints should be explicitly acknowledged [85, 86, 92]. Thus, early-stage strategies may prioritize safe BBB transit and preservation of vascular function, whereas later-stage approaches may need to combine delivery with neurovascular repair, clearance support, or anti-inflammatory modulation.

Ligand-guided targeting and protein corona limitations

Although interactions between nanomaterials and proteins are well studied, it remains uncertain whether inherent affinity for membrane proteins is sufficient to achieve selective delivery under physiological protein-rich conditions [87, 94]. Notably, bundled single-walled carbon nanotubes can act as artificial neurotrophins [95, 96]. By engaging Trk receptors they modulate downstream pathways and improve learning and memory, illustrating that even unmodified materials can display targeting behavior. Important challenges persist. Circulating nanomedicines may be cleared by the immune system and by mononuclear phagocyte uptake [87, 97]. Targeting ligands can bind serum proteins and form a protein corona that blunts selectivity and alters biodistribution. Many target receptors and transporters are not unique to diseased tissue, which can drive off-target accumulation and complicate interpretation of efficacy and toxicity [95, 98]. Accordingly, studies should report not only nominal ligand identity but also corona sensitivity, competition with endogenous ligands, and off-target receptor expression in peripheral organs [87, 97]. These considerations are essential for distinguishing true disease-selective targeting from nonspecific brain exposure or generalized cellular uptake.

Biomimetic and cell-derived delivery systems

These hurdles are being addressed through biomimetic strategies that employ cell membranes, extracellular vesicles, and cell-derived shuttles as carriers. Because they arise from the body, these systems can evade immune surveillance and retain features of the source cell through membrane proteins [99]. As one example, carriers derived from regulatory T cells can suppress immunity via checkpoints such as CTLA-4 and LAG-3. After loading with drugs, vesicles and cellular shuttles may remain functional and continue aspects of their physiologic roles, enabling combined delivery and immunomodulation. Still, biomimetic systems also require careful discussion of batch-to-batch variability, immunogenicity, and scalability, which are central translational barriers for biologically derived nanomedicines [87, 97]. Neuron-selective homing remains an engineering challenge, and current approaches rely on combinatorial strategies such as lipid insertion, membrane fusion, chemical conjugation, or genetic engineering to increase neuronal tropism without sacrificing safety [100, 101]. From a translational perspective, extracellular vesicles, liposomes, and some polymeric carriers may be closer to clinical development than highly complex biomimetic or inorganic multifunctional constructs, provided that potency, identity, purity, and reproducibility can be controlled.

Intracellular and organelle-focused delivery

Once within affected cells, nanoscale drugs distribute among organelles. Considerable effort has focused on sending payloads to mitochondria, the Golgi apparatus, and the endoplasmic reticulum because energy production and protein handling are central to survival [84, 102]. Restoring or tuning these compartments can slow or reverse decline. Recent work also frames certain pathological inclusions, such as neurofibrillary tangles, as specialized condensates formed by liquid–liquid phase separation, creating new subcellular targets of interest. Although organelle-level targeting remains relatively underexplored in this condition, it offers a logical next step toward precision therapy, particularly if paired with BBB-aware delivery and stage-stratified patient selection [90, 103]. This emerging area merits deeper study (Fig. 2). For Tau-dominant or mitochondrial phenotypes, intracellular delivery, endosomal escape, lysosome-autophagy engagement, and mitochondrial targeting may be more relevant than extracellular capture alone.

Across recent reports, a consistent trend is the move from single-axis targeting toward integrated designs that couple BBB crossing or BBB support with stimulus-responsive release and multi-pathway modulation in diseased tissue [83, 84]. Because the central mechanism of the disorder has not been fully defined, highly specific delivery to a single factor may not always yield strong benefit [90, 91]. As a result, attention is shifting toward coordinated modulation of several drivers through cocktail regimens and multifunctional platforms. This shift should be balanced against the practical reality that greater biological sophistication can reduce translational feasibility if it increases formulation complexity, quality-control burden, immunogenicity, or difficulty in scale-up. Whether a system achieves sequential targeting of lesion sites, diseased cells, and molecular pathways, the ultimate goals remain the same [90, 103]. Effective therapy should prevent neuronal loss, quiet inflammatory cascades, and foster neural repair so that neurotransmitter release is maintained and function is preserved [104, 105]. The delivery principles summarized here provide the basis for a stage-informed and pathology-guided roadmap in which nanoplatforms are selected according to disease stage, dominant pathology, cellular target, and translational readiness.

Stage-informed and pathology-guided roadmap for nanomedicine selection in Alzheimer’s disease

A rational nanomedicine strategy for AD should be selected according to disease stage, dominant pathology, cellular target, and translational feasibility rather than by platform novelty alone [71, 106]. Recent biomarker-based diagnostic and staging frameworks increasingly define AD as a biological continuum that begins before overt symptoms and progresses through stages marked by amyloid deposition, Tau pathology, neurodegeneration, synaptic failure, inflammatory remodeling, and vascular dysfunction [71]. This evolving view supports a therapeutic roadmap in which nanomedicine design is matched to the dominant biological context rather than applied uniformly across all patients. Early or preclinical disease may require platforms that preserve BBB integrity, improve biomarker detection, suppress early Aβ production, or capture soluble amyloid species before irreversible circuit damage occurs. In contrast, symptomatic disease with prominent Tau pathology may require intracellular delivery, endosomal escape, and engagement of autophagy-lysosome pathways [107, 108]. Inflammatory phenotypes may benefit more from microglia-targeted, inflammasome-modulating, or immunoregulatory nanoplatforms, whereas advanced disease with neuronal loss, vascular dysfunction, and impaired repair capacity may require regenerative or neurovascular support strategies [109, 110]. The final filter highlights key translational requirements, including safety, biodistribution, reproducibility, manufacturability, regulatory characterization, and clinical scalability.

This framework also helps distinguish near-term translational candidates from exploratory concepts. Lipid nanoparticles, polymeric carriers, liposomes, and selected extracellular vesicle-based systems may be more realistic for clinical development because their composition, loading capacity, sterility, and manufacturing parameters can be standardized more readily [111, 112]. By contrast, highly complex multifunctional inorganic systems, ROS-generating photodynamic or sonodynamic platforms, and extensively engineered biomimetic constructs may offer sophisticated biological activity but often face greater barriers related to long-term retention, immunogenicity, quality control, reproducibility, and scale-up [111, 113]. Regulatory guidance for nanomaterial-containing drug products emphasizes the need to define and control critical quality attributes, including particle size distribution, morphology, surface properties, drug loading, release kinetics, stability, impurities, sterility, and batch consistency [114, 115]. For neurodegenerative applications, these issues are even more important because repeated dosing, long disease duration, limited access to human brain pharmacokinetic data, and uncertain animal-to-human biodistribution extrapolation can complicate safety assessment [29, 33, 116]. Therefore, increasing platform complexity should not be assumed to increase clinical value. In many cases, a simpler platform with reproducible manufacturing, predictable biodistribution, and measurable target engagement may have a clearer translational path than a multifunctional system with uncertain pharmacology [111, 117].

The roadmap further emphasizes that therapeutic endpoints should be matched to the intended biological context. For anti-amyloid nanomedicines, meaningful outcomes include reduced soluble oligomer burden, improved clearance, lower Aβ production, and preservation of synaptic function [69, 118]. For Tau-focused systems, relevant endpoints include intracellular target engagement, reduced pathological phosphorylation or aggregation, improved axonal transport, restored proteostasis, and functional rescue [107, 108]. For anti-inflammatory nanomedicines, efficacy should be assessed not only by reduced cytokine levels but also by preservation of beneficial microglial functions such as debris clearance and tissue surveillance [119]. For regenerative approaches, outcomes should extend beyond trophic factor delivery to include neuronal survival, synaptic integration, neurovascular stabilization, and long-term safety [112, 116]. This alignment between disease biology, delivery design, measurable endpoints, and translational readiness is essential for moving AD nanomedicine from descriptive proof-of-concept studies toward clinically actionable therapeutic development [106, 111].

Nanomedicine strategies for Tau-focused therapy

Phosphorylated Tau is tightly linked to synaptic failure and is viewed as a prime therapeutic target as well as a valuable diagnostic marker [67, 68]. Importantly, Tau is already actionable in the clinic from a diagnostic standpoint through tau PET imaging, whereas most Tau-directed therapeutics, including antibody programs, have produced mixed or negative efficacy signals in AD to date. This gap reinforces why nano-enabled Tau strategies should be discussed with clear separation of preclinical proof-of-concept versus translational readiness. Tau-focused nanomedicine is therefore most relevant for disease contexts in which intracellular Tau accumulation, impaired axonal transport, synaptic dysfunction, and defective autophagy-lysosome clearance are dominant features, rather than as a universal strategy for all AD stages [120].

A core challenge is that phosphorylated Tau closely resembles the native protein that supports essential neuronal functions, and only a limited set of antibodies, short peptides, or small-molecule motifs can discriminate disease-relevant conformers with high specificity [121]. One notable example is AT8, a mouse-derived monoclonal antibody raised against paired helical filaments of phosphorylated Tau. AT8 recognizes Tau carrying phosphorylation at two or more sites such as Ser202, Thr205, and Ser208. Its binding strength varies with the phosphorylation pattern and shows higher affinity for triply phosphorylated Tau at Ser202, Thr205, and Ser208 than for the doubly modified form, which supports its preferential recognition of pathogenic species [49, 67, 68]. Because epitope selectivity can shift across disease stage and Tau strains, it is helpful to explicitly report the Tau species targeted and the experimental model used whenever AT8 is repurposed as a targeting ligand. This point is particularly important because ligand binding to pathological Tau does not automatically prove therapeutic Tau clearance or functional rescue.

A second category of targeting agents originates from tau imaging probes that recognize paired helical filaments. Investigators introduced T808 and then optimized it to T807, a tracer scaffold developed for tau positron emission tomography with improved pharmacokinetics and BBB passage [67]. In this context, it is useful to clarify that such ligands are best validated for binding aggregated Tau in vivo for imaging, while therapeutic targeting requires additional evidence that ligand-guided carriers achieve intracellular engagement and downstream functional benefit. In addition, the Tau hexapeptide VQIINK engages Tau aggregates through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic forces and is a key sequence involved in Tau assembly and spread [122, 123]. This peptide can therefore function as a ligand to improve recognition of pathological Tau, although its affinity and selectivity should be interpreted alongside potential off-target binding to other β-sheet-rich aggregates and the impact of serum or cerebrospinal fluid protein corona formation on binding performance [124]. Accordingly, Tau-ligand functionalization should be evaluated together with intracellular delivery, endosomal escape, and target engagement assays, rather than by binding affinity alone.

From a mechanistic standpoint, nano-enabled Tau therapy can be organized into three translationally meaningful buckets: (i) capture and sequestration of pathological Tau species through nano-chaperones, (ii) modulation of Tau post-translational regulation using small molecules with improved delivery, and (iii) gene-based suppression of Tau kinases or Tau itself using nucleic acids (Table 2). These strategies differ substantially in clinical feasibility. Small-molecule nanoformulations and selected lipid or polymeric carriers may be more readily developable, whereas inorganic nano-chaperones and layered nanosheet systems remain more exploratory because of concerns about persistence, long-term toxicity, and manufacturing reproducibility.

Comparative nanomedicine platforms for Tau and Aβ targeting in AD

Clearance of phosphorylated Tau relies on intrinsic neuronal and glial proteostasis as well as upstream regulation of Tau post-translational modifications [67]. Building on new ligands, researchers have proposed nano-chaperones, which offer higher surface area and stronger capture capacity than natural chaperones to neutralize tauopathy in cell and animal models [10, 125]. Cellular entry of nanoscale therapeutics can proceed through clathrin-dependent uptake, caveolin-dependent uptake, clathrin-independent routes, macropinocytosis, and membrane fusion [23, 125]. Many constructs are trafficked into the endo-lysosomal system and can lose activity, so effective nano-chaperones often require strategies that balance endosomal escape with the fact that ultimate degradation still depends on lysosomal competence. Taking advantage of acidic compartments, one group designed pH-responsive nano-chaperones based on poly(beta-amino ester) that become protonated under low pH, which promotes endosomal escape and suppresses further aggregation of pathological Tau [126]. To address the proteostasis bottleneck, some designs combine capture with autophagy restoration, but this introduces safety and translation questions such as long-term lysosomal stress, off-target autophagy activation, and batch-to-batch reproducibility that should be stated explicitly. A key design challenge is therefore to enhance Tau clearance without chronically overstimulating autophagy or impairing normal neuronal protein turnover.

In one representative design, ceria nanoparticles were packed within mesoporous silicon particles, then coated with polyethylene glycol and decorated with AT8 to form a Tau-focused nano-chaperone [68]. This platform inhibited mTOR and activated transcription factor EB, thereby driving the autophagy-lysosome axis and clearing residual intracellular complexes of phosphorylated Tau bound to the carrier [127]. While conceptually strong, claims of Tau clearance should be tied to model-specific endpoints, for example Tau burden by immunohistochemistry, synaptic markers, and behavioral rescue, and interpreted alongside toxicity readouts such as neuroinflammation, gliosis, and long-term tissue accumulation, because inorganic cores can persist in the brain. These practical considerations are summarized in Table 2. Such systems are mechanistically attractive but should currently be regarded as preclinical and exploratory until repeat-dose biodistribution, clearance, and neurotoxicity are demonstrated in models that better approximate human disease duration.

Small molecules can also modify Tau pathology by altering post-translational states [50]. Compounds such as hydrogen sulfide donors and methylene blue have shown Tau-modulating effects, yet many candidates have limited BBB penetration and require nanoformulation for in vivo utility [128, 129]. One approach encapsulated methylene blue in a hydrophilic core and added peptides that promote barrier transit to yield a lipid nanocomposite carrying methylene blue [130, 131]. The construct maintained the ability of methylene blue to influence Akt and GSK-3β-related signaling, lowered intracellular phosphorylated Tau, and reduced apoptosis induced by okadaic acid in neuronal models [67, 68]. To strengthen translational rigor, it helps to specify whether these effects were demonstrated in primary neurons versus immortalized lines, and whether in vivo dosing achieved brain exposure consistent with the proposed mechanism. Compared with highly complex inorganic systems, lipid and polymeric nanoformulations of Tau-modifying small molecules may offer a more realistic near-term development path if their payload, release profile, and brain exposure can be standardized.

Another route to curb phosphorylated Tau formation is gene-based therapy using small interfering RNA, which silences specific transcripts with high precision. Nucleic acid drugs face rapid enzymatic degradation and immune clearance, so carriers are essential [31, 32]. Nanocarriers provide a protected microenvironment that prevents degradation in circulation and can add targeting functions [32, 132]. As a representative example, LRsGAR was created by loading siRNA against GSK-3β together with bovine serum albumin into two-dimensional magnesium–aluminum nanosheets modified with targeting peptides [133]. Intravenous dosing reduced GSK-3β messenger RNA in the hippocampus and cortex of Tau P301S mice and ultimately diminished phosphorylated Tau accumulation in the brain (Table 2). Here, the key translational inflection points are durability of knockdown, immunogenicity of the carrier, and reproducible manufacturing of layered nanosheets at scale, which should be stated alongside efficacy. Because Tau kinases also participate in physiological signaling, gene-silencing strategies require careful dose control, cell-type selectivity, and long-term monitoring for unintended pathway suppression.

Overall, Tau-focused nanomedicine should be positioned as a precision strategy for patients or experimental models with prominent intracellular Tau pathology, synaptic dysfunction, and impaired proteostasis. Its strongest rationale lies in intracellular delivery and autophagy-lysosome engagement, but its clinical translation will depend on demonstrating selective target engagement, durable functional benefit, acceptable long-term safety, and manufacturable platform design.

Nanomedicine approaches targeting pathological amyloid beta

As a central pathogenic protein in this disorder, Aβ has attracted intense interest for decades, and many mechanistic frameworks begin with the amyloid cascade [27, 154]. Therapeutic concepts that target Aβ share several design principles with Tau-directed strategies, particularly the goal of enhancing clearance through proteostasis and autophagy-related pathways [8, 155]. To avoid repetition, this section focuses on amyloid-specific nanomedicine concepts that emphasize capture, neutralization, and clearance across both central and peripheral compartments. Because Aβ species differ in solubility, aggregation state, toxicity, and anatomical distribution, amyloid-directed nanomedicine should be interpreted as a stage-sensitive strategy rather than a uniform intervention for all patients. Early amyloid-positive disease may be more suitable for production-suppressing or oligomer-intercepting platforms, whereas later symptomatic disease may require combined amyloid clearance, anti-inflammatory support, mitochondrial protection, or regenerative strategies [156].

Ligands for recognizing pathological amyloid species

Nature provides a useful template for ligands that recognize pathological amyloid species. Because toxic monomers can self-associate into oligomers and then progress toward fibrils, short amyloid-derived sequences such as KLVFFAED, KLVFF, and GGGKLVFF have been designed to mimic key aggregation motifs and competitively intercept the assembly process [157]. Beyond self-derived peptides, endogenous binding partners have also been leveraged. The ganglioside GM1 can interact with amyloid species and form complexes with distinctive structural features, which has made GM1-based designs attractive as recognition elements for targeted capture platforms [130]. However, ligand affinity for Aβ should not be equated with therapeutic efficacy unless binding is coupled to neutralization, degradation, efflux, or demonstrable reduction of toxic oligomer burden.

Importantly, discussions of lipoprotein-mediated amyloid handling should remain biologically balanced. In the periphery, HDL-like formulations enriched in apolipoprotein A I have been associated with neuroprotective patterns and amyloid-binding or efflux-related concepts, which motivates ApoA-I inspired nanocarriers in some designs. However, within the CNS, amyloid transport and clearance biology is strongly shaped by locally produced apolipoproteins, and mechanisms of CNS handling are not identical to peripheral HDL behavior [120, 130]. This distinction matters for how targeting claims are framed and for how translational expectations are set. Therefore, ApoA-I or HDL-mimetic platforms should be described cautiously as amyloid-handling or clearance-supporting systems, rather than as direct substitutes for CNS ApoE-dependent clearance biology.

Synthetic probes add further options for ligand engineering. Thioflavin T and related fluorescein-based dyes bind along the long axis of amyloid fibrils and are widely used to label fibrillar structures in aggregation studies [50, 158]. Functionalizing nanosystems with these ligands can increase affinity for fibrillar amyloid and support capture for downstream neutralization or clearance workflows [159]. Still, fibril-binding probes are often better validated for detection than for therapy, so diagnostic affinity and therapeutic benefit should be separated clearly when such ligands are incorporated into nanocarriers.

Strategy landscape and stage-aware expectations

Amyloid-directed interventions can be mapped onto several mechanistic levers: reducing production, intercepting early assembly, neutralizing oligomers, accelerating clearance, and dismantling mature fibrils. Because pathological species accumulate over time and exist in multiple conformations, strategies that focus on only one stage of the amyloid life cycle may show uneven benefit depending on disease phase [158, 160]. This reality strengthens the rationale for multifunctional nanoplatforms that combine targeting with controlled release, catalytic activity, or immune-aware clearance support. At the same time, multifunctionality should be justified by a clear biological need, because increasing platform complexity can reduce reproducibility, complicate quality control, and slow regulatory translation. Aβ-focused designs may therefore be most convincing when they define the intended disease stage, the dominant amyloid species, the route of clearance, and the measurable pharmacodynamic endpoint [161].

From a stage-informed perspective, early or preclinical amyloid-positive disease may favor Aβ production suppression, soluble oligomer capture, biomarker-guided delivery, and BBB-preserving approaches. In established amyloid pathology, capture and clearance platforms may be more relevant, particularly if they promote efflux, lysosomal degradation, or immune-compatible removal of bound aggregates. In late-stage dementia, amyloid-only strategies are less likely to restore function unless paired with interventions that address Tau pathology, neuroinflammation, synaptic loss, mitochondrial injury, or neurovascular dysfunction [162, 163].

Gene silencing and genome editing concepts

Gene silencing is a prominent upstream approach. Beta-site cleavage enzyme 1 is a key node in amyloidogenic processing [2, 7]. When siRNA against this enzyme is packaged into polymeric carriers that also incorporate BBB targeting motifs, the complexes can enter relevant brain regions, be internalized by cells via endocytosis, and release cargo to suppress translation of the target transcript. The resulting reduction in abnormal precursor cleavage can decrease generation of toxic amyloid species [164, 165]. This approach is conceptually attractive for early or amyloid-production-driven disease, but its translational value depends on durable knockdown, repeat-dose tolerability, avoidance of innate immune activation, and preservation of physiological BACE1 functions.

Genome editing represents another upstream avenue. CRISPR Cas9-based designs have been applied to amyloid pathways either by influencing secretase-related processing or by shifting APP handling toward the alpha-secretase route [166]. Given that gamma-secretase participates in physiologic processing and that complete loss of beta-secretase activity may raise safety concerns, an often-preferred direction is to increase alpha-secretase activity to divert APP processing away from toxic amyloid production. ADAM10 is a representative alpha-secretase target. Delivering catalytically inactive Cas9 directed toward ADAM10 together with a peptide-based activator such as R7L10 in a nanoparticle complex has been reported to elevate alpha-secretase activity and thereby reduce formation of pathogenic amyloid species [167, 168]. Nevertheless, genome-editing approaches remain exploratory in AD nanomedicine because long-term gene regulation, off-target editing or transcriptional effects, dosing reversibility, and scalable manufacturing require careful validation before clinical translation can be considered.

Microenvironment-responsive capture and neutralization

As disease advances, pathological amyloid burden rises and aggregation dynamics become self-reinforcing, which means that upstream suppression alone may not be sufficient [158, 160]. At this stage, capture and neutralization strategies become especially relevant. Nanochaperone-like platforms can be engineered to recognize and immobilize amyloid under disease-linked conditions. One design used an ultrasmall VP particle core assembled from N-vinyl caprolactam, aromatic N-phenyl acrylamide, and acrylic acid, then introduced a reactive oxygen species-responsive ligand, 4-hydroxy phenylboronic acid, to confer microenvironment gating [169, 170]. Under oxidative stress, cleavage of the boronic structure exposes acrylic acid groups, generating electrophilic centers that can react with nucleophilic residues such as lysine 16 in amyloid, effectively locking amyloid into a VP-amyloid complex for neutralization [158, 171]. Conceptually, this approach aligns capture efficiency with the oxidative state of diseased tissue, but it also reinforces the need to assess off-target reactivity and local toxicity in vivo when electrophilic chemistry is used [169, 170]. Such designs should be evaluated not only for amyloid binding, but also for whether the resulting complexes are cleared safely and whether reactive intermediates affect non-amyloid proteins in the aged brain microenvironment.

Peripheral sink and erythrocyte-membrane nanosponges

Another concept exploits bidirectional exchange between the CNS and circulation. Lowering peripheral amyloid levels may promote efflux from the brain, enabling a clearance strategy that can partially bypass the BBB [4, 160]. To work in blood, carriers must persist long enough to engage their targets. A common longevity tactic is cloaking nanoparticles with erythrocyte membrane, which presents self-signals such as CD47 and reduces immune clearance [6, 172]. Building on this, nanochaperone-like nanosponges can be constructed by inserting amyloid-targeting peptides such as KLVFF into erythrocyte membrane vesicles [154]. To stabilize the construct and add catalytic capacity, copper oxide components can be embedded within the membrane-coated system, yielding long-circulating platforms described in the literature as Cu_xO@EMK designs. These systems have been associated with strong amyloid adsorption and improvements in disease-relevant readouts in model settings [45, 173]. Although this approach is attractive because it reduces reliance on direct BBB penetration, its clinical translation will require clear evidence that peripheral amyloid binding meaningfully alters central amyloid burden and does not merely sequester circulating species without functional CNS benefit.

At the same time, erythrocyte membrane cloaking can introduce trade-offs. While CD47 can prolong circulation, it may also reduce uptake of amyloid-bound constructs by monocytes, potentially limiting downstream immune clearance in some contexts. Clarifying this balance, and identifying supportive strategies that maintain residence time while preserving effective immune-mediated removal, remains an important translational direction [4, 174]. Manufacturing consistency is another central issue for membrane-coated systems, because source-cell variability, membrane orientation, residual proteins, sterility, and batch-to-batch reproducibility can influence both safety and performance.

Taken together, amyloid-targeting nanomedicine strategies span upstream suppression, precision editing, stimulus-gated capture, and peripheral clearance amplification [154]. Their comparative value is likely to depend on disease stage, dominant amyloid species, and the degree to which platforms address safety constraints such as off-target binding, immune interactions, and long-term biodistribution [4, 174]. The strongest translational path for amyloid-directed nanomedicine will likely come from platforms that combine a clearly defined amyloid target with measurable target engagement, reproducible manufacturing, acceptable repeat-dose safety, and compatibility with other disease-modifying strategies. Conversely, amyloid-only nanomedicines should be interpreted cautiously in symptomatic or advanced AD, where Tau pathology, neuroinflammation, synaptic degeneration, and vascular dysfunction may already be major drivers of clinical decline.

Physicochemical nanoplatforms for amyloid disassembly

Earlier we outlined biological routes for removing toxic Aβ [50]. In parallel, physicochemical strategies have been developed to destabilize or dismantle amyloid fibrils. Broadly, these approaches rely either on controlled generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) or on localized heat, typically induced by external energy sources [175, 176]. Unlike ligand-based capture or gene-silencing approaches, these platforms act mainly through physical or chemical disruption of amyloid assemblies and should therefore be viewed as mechanistically distinct, but also more dependent on precise spatial and temporal control. ROS are hydrophilic and polar molecules that can disrupt the noncovalent interactions stabilizing amyloid assemblies. By weakening hydrophobic packing and hydrogen bonding, and by oxidizing susceptible amino acid side chains on monomeric or aggregated amyloid, ROS can shift the equilibrium toward fibril destabilization and fragmentation [89]. This concept has motivated platforms designed to promote amyloid disassembly through spatially restricted ROS generation. Photodynamic therapy is a representative strategy. Following light absorption, a photosensitizer transitions to an excited singlet state and, through intersystem crossing, transfers energy to molecular oxygen to produce singlet oxygen before returning to a lower-energy state [177, 178]. Black phosphorus nanosheet-based systems illustrate that such photochemical designs can attenuate fibril accumulation under experimental conditions. However, evidence for these systems remains largely preclinical, and reduction of fibril signal should be interpreted together with neuronal viability, inflammatory activation, long-term material clearance, and functional behavioral outcomes.

A key limitation of light-triggered approaches is tissue penetration, which is substantially lower than that achievable with ultrasound. This has increased interest in sonodynamic therapy, in which sonosensitizers generate ROS upon ultrasound exposure [169, 170]. Bismuth oxychloride nanosheets have been reported as effective sonosensitizers and have shown amyloid-reducing activity in ex vivo brain tissue settings [170]. Although ultrasound-based activation may improve penetration compared with light-based systems, sonodynamic platforms still require careful definition of acoustic parameters, exposure duration, local ROS yield, and safety margins in aged or diseased brain tissue.

Despite these encouraging findings, ROS-driven disassembly remains controversial in the context of this disorder. The disease microenvironment is already characterized by elevated oxidative stress, and further ROS generation through photodynamic or sonodynamic activation could aggravate neuronal injury and accelerate cell loss if not tightly controlled [175, 176]. Therefore, the therapeutic window, dose, and spatiotemporal confinement of ROS production require careful validation before such approaches can be credibly advanced toward translation. This limitation is especially important in symptomatic or advanced AD, where mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, impaired antioxidant defense, and neuroinflammation may already lower the tolerance of neural tissue to additional oxidative burden.

Localized heat-based strategies may offer a more controllable alternative in some contexts [89]. Photothermal approaches use near-infrared light to induce a confined temperature increase. When a photothermal agent absorbs photons and returns to the ground state through nonradiative decay, the absorbed energy is released as heat, producing local hyperthermia [177, 179]. Nanoparticles that combine guanidinocalix [7] arene with a photothermal polymer such as PDPP have been described to bind pathogenic fibrils [177]. Mild photothermal stimulation may then interfere with the conformational transition toward beta-sheet-rich structures, slow further aggregation, and facilitate partial dispersion of established fibrils, although the detailed mechanism remains under investigation. In addition, controlled mild heating has been associated with reversible opening of the BBB, which could enhance delivery when temperatures remain below thresholds that damage neural tissue [177]. This benefit must still be weighed against the intrinsic vulnerability of the aged or diseased brain to thermal and inflammatory stress. For this reason, photothermal amyloid-disassembly systems should be evaluated with strict temperature mapping, BBB integrity assessment, gliosis markers, vascular safety endpoints, and repeated-dose tolerability before being considered translationally mature.

Finally, clinical programs targeting amyloid have yielded mixed outcomes, contributing to reduced confidence in amyloid as a sole therapeutic focus. Importantly, amyloid biology is heterogeneous, with multiple toxic subtypes and conformations that complicate target selection and trial design [158, 180]. In this landscape, nanomedicine offers a wider toolkit, spanning suppression of toxic species formation to direct disruption of mature fibrillar assemblies [181]. Nevertheless, physicochemical amyloid-disassembly platforms currently remain more exploratory than lipid, polymeric, or selected extracellular vesicle-based delivery systems because they often require external activation, specialized dosing conditions, precise energy delivery, and extensive safety validation. A rational, safety-centered deployment of these physicochemical tools, ideally integrated with stage-appropriate delivery control and careful toxicity assessment, may improve the prospects of amyloid-directed interventions in clinical settings [55]. Their most realistic role may be as adjunctive or highly controlled interventions for amyloid-predominant contexts rather than stand-alone disease-modifying therapies for advanced AD, where Tau pathology, neuroinflammation, synaptic loss, and neurovascular dysfunction are also major contributors to decline.

Nanomedicine approaches to prevent neuronal apoptosis

Programmed neuronal death is a major cause of neuron loss [46]. This process is driven by activation of cleaved caspase-3 and caspase-9 and is often triggered by mitochondrial failure [43, 56]. Earlier sections addressed clearing toxic proteins. Here we focus on anti-apoptotic nanomedicine strategies that act through control of reactive oxygen species and protection of mitochondria [43, 182]. These approaches are most relevant for AD contexts in which oxidative stress, metal dyshomeostasis, mitochondrial injury, and caspase-mediated neuronal loss are prominent contributors to disease progression. However, they should be considered neuroprotective and supportive rather than stand-alone disease-modifying strategies unless they also demonstrate durable effects on cognition, synaptic preservation, and upstream pathology.

Selective delivery to neurons can be achieved by exploiting membrane receptors and proteins. Expression of these markers changes with disease state and with age. For example, levels of lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) on neurons tend to decline as pathology advances, which limits its usefulness for targeting. In contrast, the receptor for advanced glycation end products is commonly upregulated in response to amyloid pathology, allowing neuron-focused delivery using amyloid-derived peptides [133]. Additional neuron-specific targets include the trisialoganglioside known as GT1b, neural cell adhesion molecules, and fibroblast growth factor receptor one, which are relatively stable in expression [26, 27]. Peptides such as Tet one and C3 have been crafted to engage these receptors. Other targets that are abundant across brain cell types and support multi-step delivery are also used in nanotechnology [25, 33]. Two common examples are the transferrin receptor and cholinergic receptors. The transferrin pathway is present on the BBB and on neurons and can be addressed with the B6 peptide or with transferrin itself. Cholinergic receptors are found on endothelial cells, neurons, and microglia, and ligands such as RVG29 and the FG loop peptide are frequently chosen [62, 63, 128]. Using these recognition elements, carriers can be engineered to guide payloads to neurons with higher precision, but selectivity can be reduced in vivo by protein corona formation and by shared receptor expression in peripheral tissues [63]. Therefore, neuron-targeting claims should be supported by evidence of cell-type-specific uptake, intracellular target engagement, and limited peripheral accumulation rather than by ligand selection alone.

Because reactive oxygen species and mitochondrial stress reinforce one another, removing excess reactive oxygen species is essential to protect mitochondria [175, 183]. In the disease microenvironment, Fenton chemistry driven by metal ions, especially cuprous ions, accelerates generation of these species and depletes glutathione, which magnifies damage [45, 184]. Delivering metal chelators is therefore a sound therapeutic concept and has reached clinical testing. Yet small molecule chelators often fall short in living systems because of limited stability and modest capacity to bind metals. Nanomaterials that cross the BBB and present multivalent binding sites offer stronger chelation and have performed better in models [185]. For AD nanomedicine, the value of chelation-based strategies depends on whether metal binding reduces oxidative injury without disrupting physiological metal-dependent signaling, enzymatic activity, or synaptic function. This balance is particularly important because copper, iron, and zinc are not merely toxic species, but also participate in normal neuronal biology.

Some nanomaterials also display enzyme-like behavior as nanozymes that catalytically remove reactive oxygen species, adding a second line of defense. A case in point is niobium carbide in a two-dimensional form, which has a very high surface area, shows photothermal-assisted passage across the BBB, and binds metal ions [185]. In animal models of neuronal oxidative stress induced by ascorbic acid and copper ions, this material improved mitochondrial function and produced neuroprotective effects in experimental settings [56]. Similar two-dimensional materials, including black phosphorus, can cross the barrier and improve mitochondrial health in neuronal cells by chelating metals [67]. Although these findings are promising, two-dimensional inorganic nanozymes should currently be considered exploratory because their long-term brain retention, degradation products, repeat-dose safety, and large-scale reproducibility remain incompletely resolved.

A note of caution is warranted for nanozymes that contain copper, iron, and related metals. Their breakdown products, especially cuprous and ferrous ions, could in principle rekindle harmful reactive oxygen chemistry [37, 186]. The clinical risk likely depends on how these materials are processed in the body. Nanozymes designed for this condition are typically stable in physiological conditions and react directly with reactive oxygen species [49]. After toxic species have been neutralized, sustained metal ion release is unlikely. Instead, the materials are expected to move out of the brain through cerebrospinal fluid or other clearance routes. Nonetheless, long-term exposure, regional accumulation, and batch-to-batch variability remain key translational risks that require pharmacokinetic and biodistribution profiling in clinically relevant dosing regimens (Table 3) [187]. Future studies should also evaluate gliosis, vascular reactivity, lysosomal stress, behavioral outcomes, and systemic metal redistribution, because reduction of oxidative markers alone is not sufficient to establish disease-modifying benefit.

Nanomedicine strategies to prevent neuronal apoptosis in AD

Overall, anti-apoptotic nanomedicine strategies may be particularly valuable when oxidative injury and mitochondrial stress are major drivers of neuronal vulnerability. Their most realistic role may be as part of combination therapy, paired with amyloid or Tau-directed approaches, anti-inflammatory modulation, or regenerative support. From a translational perspective, simpler antioxidant-loaded lipid or polymeric systems may offer a clearer development route than highly complex inorganic nanozymes, although the latter remain scientifically attractive for catalytic ROS control. The central challenge is to preserve neuronal survival without introducing new risks related to metal imbalance, excessive ROS suppression, material persistence, or poorly controlled biodistribution.

Precision nanoplatforms for mitochondrial rescue

A more effective way to safeguard mitochondria is to deliver antioxidants directly to these organelles, which often calls for mitochondrial targeting peptides such as TPP [195]. Mitochondria-directed nanomedicine is particularly relevant for AD phenotypes characterized by oxidative stress, impaired bioenergetics, synaptic energy failure, cytochrome c release, and caspase-dependent apoptosis. In many reports these peptides are displayed on the outer surface of the carrier. This is not universally reliable, since some carriers partially disassemble while escaping lysosomes and release cargo into the cytosol, which weakens mitochondrial targeting [196]. A practical answer is to covalently link the drug to a mitochondrial homing peptide. For example, the peptide SS31, which itself has antioxidant and mitochondria-seeking properties, can be conjugated with the neuroprotective sequence S14G Humanin to form a positively charged hybrid polymer termed HNSS [133, 158]. This hybrid can be encapsulated in citraconic acid-modified PEG-PTMC, where citraconic acid lends negative charge for high loading and also drives a charge shift during endosomal to lysosomal transport that aids escape [50]. Adding the FGL peptide to the particle surface yields a construct with combined neuronal and mitochondrial tropism. Fluorescent labeling shows time-dependent lysosomal escape followed by accumulation within mitochondria, and bio-transmission electron microscopy confirms that these nanoparticles reduce mitochondrial injury under disease-like conditions [60, 87]. However, mitochondrial accumulation should be interpreted alongside functional endpoints such as ATP production, mitochondrial membrane potential, synaptic preservation, apoptosis markers, and behavioral rescue rather than localization data alone.

Beyond supplying antioxidants, a second route to lowering excess reactive oxygen species is to activate intrinsic defense programs [197, 198]. Under basal conditions, nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) binds Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) in the cytoplasm [199]. When cells face oxidative challenge, Nrf2 is normally released and moves to the nucleus to drive expression of downstream antioxidant enzymes. In the disease setting, this switch is blunted and cytoplasmic levels remain abnormally low despite oxidative stress [37, 49]. Stimulating this pathway can therefore enhance clearance of harmful species. One method encapsulates a short competitor peptide that mimics the binding motif of Nrf2 within a reactive oxygen species-responsive phenylboronic dendrimer called APBP [38, 66]. In neurons, APBP shields Nrf2 from ubiquitin–proteasome degradation and promotes its nuclear translocation, which raises antioxidant proteins and protects against amyloid-induced cell death [200]. Animal data indicate that depleting reactive oxygen species helps curb apoptosis, yet if mitochondria are already damaged, they can continue to release cytochrome c and drive the death cascade. Blocking signals from injured mitochondria is thus especially important [182]. For translation, Nrf2-activating nanoplatforms should demonstrate controlled pathway activation rather than broad chronic stimulation, because excessive or prolonged antioxidant signaling may disrupt redox-dependent cellular communication and adaptive stress responses.

A third option is to directly reprogram mitochondrial dynamics. Oxidative stress can trigger mitochondrial fusion, a protective response that donates fresh membrane to compromised organelles to limit leakage [201]. Stimulating mitochondrial biogenesis is therefore a promising tactic. In one line of work, rhein was loaded into a K8-targeted nanocarrier. After reaching the brain, rhein acted as an antioxidant through its phenolic hydroxyl group and activated the SIRT1-PGC-1α axis, which increased NRF1 and promoted mitochondrial biogenesis [202, 203]. Newly formed healthy organelles not only secrete antioxidant enzymes to neutralize intracellular reactive oxygen species but also reduce the burden of dysfunctional organelles and assist repair through fusion [26, 85]. Nevertheless, increased mitochondrial biogenesis does not necessarily indicate complete mitochondrial recovery unless it is accompanied by improved respiratory function, restored membrane potential, and removal of damaged organelles. Some studies suggest that individual cristae within a single mitochondrion can operate independently, so fusion does not always equal functional recovery. Mitophagy remains the principal route for removing damaged organelles. Compounds that stimulate mitophagy include NAD + precursors, urolithin A, actinonin, spermidine, rapamycin, and metformin, though their roles within nanotherapy for this condition are still being defined [92]. In a recent approach, bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells were engineered to overexpress the tyrosine phosphatase SHP2, producing extracellular vesicles enriched in this factor [26, 204, 205]. Treatment with these vesicles increased colocalization of lysosomes and mitochondria in diseased neurons, consistent with activated autophagosomes that engulf defective organelles. Changes in JC-1 fluorescence indicated improved mitochondrial polarization, and markers of apoptosis were reduced [30]. Extracellular vesicle-based mitochondrial rescue may be more biologically compatible than some synthetic inorganic systems, but its clinical development still depends on standardized source-cell characterization, vesicle identity, potency assays, storage stability, and batch-to-batch reproducibility.

Taken together, current nanomedicine strategies that prevent neuronal apoptosis focus on mitochondria. They quell reactive oxygen species, promote formation of new organelles, and induce mitophagy to limit activation of the caspase-dependent apoptotic cascade [22, 206]. However, the evidence base is still dominated by preclinical models, and clinical readiness hinges on dose-scaled safety, repeat-dose biodistribution, long-term neurotoxicity assessment, organelle-specific target engagement, and reproducible platform manufacturing. These approaches have produced encouraging benefits in animal models, yet confirmation of clinical value still requires rigorous testing in human studies (Table 4) [168, 207, 208]. Within the broader stage-informed roadmap, mitochondrial rescue is best positioned as a supportive strategy for oxidative or bioenergetic AD phenotypes and will likely be most effective when combined with amyloid, Tau, inflammatory, or regenerative interventions rather than used as an isolated therapeutic axis.

Nanomedicine strategies to calm neuroinflammation

Neuroinflammation is a major engine of disease progression [182, 220, 221]. The network is intricate and involves activation of resident immune cells such as microglia and astrocytes as well as recruitment of peripheral leukocytes that include lymphocytes, monocytes, and neutrophils [62, 222]. The resulting surge of pro-inflammatory cytokines can trigger neuronal pyroptosis. This section highlights strategies that modulate microglia along with non-microglial tactics that lessen inflammatory drive, while noting that most evidence remains preclinical and safety plus immunocompetence preservation are central translational constraints [62, 223, 224]. Within a stage-informed framework, anti-inflammatory nanomedicine is most relevant for AD phenotypes characterized by sustained microglial activation, elevated cytokine signaling, inflammasome engagement, astrocytic reactivity, and peripheral immune recruitment. However, inflammation in AD is not uniformly harmful; microglia and other immune cells also contribute to debris clearance, trophic support, synaptic remodeling, and host defense. Therefore, the goal should be immune recalibration rather than broad immune suppression.

Reducing immunotoxicity while improving efficacy calls for targeted delivery [129]. Since the CNS contains diverse immune cell populations, targeting often needs to be tailored to the intended cell type and disease stage. Ligand-mediated delivery to microglia commonly leverages cholinergic receptors on the microglial surface, employing many of the same ligands used for neurons [62, 221]. This overlap creates an important specificity challenge, because ligands that support CNS entry or neuronal uptake may also engage endothelial cells, neurons, astrocytes, or peripheral immune cells. As a result, microglia-targeted platforms should demonstrate cell-type-biased uptake and pathway modulation in mixed-cell or in vivo systems rather than relying only on receptor expression data.

A distinct approach is to carry drugs directly to inflammatory loci by using peripheral immune cells, their membranes, or their extracellular vesicles as shuttles. A key unknown in many biomimetic designs is the timing, location, and completeness of payload release after lesion localization, which complicates dose control and reproducibility [63, 128]. For example, some reports suggest that neutrophil extracellular traps can capture neutrophils at inflamed sites, leading to carrier degradation and prompt drug release. Membrane-coated systems that mimic macrophages or microglia can enhance localization to lesions and promote uptake by microglia, but release pathways vary and are not yet standardized across platforms [28]. In another study, carriers built from osteoclast membranes bypassed lysosomes through membrane fusion, and payloads were further exchanged between cells via exosomes [225]. Other work indicates that uptake of certain membrane-derived nanoparticles by microglia can be driven by recognition of vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 and alpha4 beta1 integrins [202, 225]. These findings underscore that endocytic routes differ among biomimetic platforms and that release modes likely vary accordingly. To improve controllability, stimulus-responsive elements can be integrated into the shell so that destabilization occurs only in inflammatory microenvironments, reducing off-target release and making pharmacology more predictable [202, 225]. For clinical development, these biomimetic approaches require standardized source-cell characterization, membrane orientation analysis, potency assays, sterility control, and batch-to-batch comparability, because small differences in membrane composition may alter biodistribution, immune recognition, and payload release.

Because microglia orchestrate much of the inflammatory response, their regulation is central [62]. Blocking intracellular pro-inflammatory signaling is therefore a major goal. Two pivotal pathways are the NF-κB axis and the NLRP3 inflammasome [226]. While many recent studies attenuate these routes indirectly by removing toxic proteins or reactive oxygen species, other levers exist. Altered riboflavin metabolism caused by loss of riboflavin kinase can activate the TNFR1-NF-κB pathway and drive excessive microglial activation [63]. The metabolite flavin mononucleotide can suppress riboflavin kinase by influencing lysine specific methyltransferase 2B, which in turn eases microglia-mediated inflammation, but poor biodistribution limits its direct use. To overcome this, flavin mononucleotide was encapsulated in human serum albumin and then cloaked with a BV2 microglial membrane to form a biomimetic particle termed MNPs@FMN [63]. This construct crossed the BBB and targeted microglia. In a mouse model, treatment improved water maze performance, lowered pro-inflammatory cytokines in the hippocampus, and restored microglial morphology toward a resting state, with parallel normalization of astrocytic reactivity [221]. Although these findings support the value of microglia-biased delivery, translation will require confirmation that the intervention preserves phagocytosis, surveillance, and tissue repair functions while reducing excessive cytokine release.

Additional regulators of NF-κB include MAPK and IκBα, which can be modulated by small molecules such as oxytocin and rhein, while TREM2 is more amenable to nucleic acid approaches that employ expression plasmids or small interfering RNA [227]. Although the NLRP3 inflammasome offers multiple intervention points that span formation of the platform, recruitment of ASC and caspase-1, activation of gasdermin D to generate GSDMD-N, and maturation of pro-interleukin-1β, nanotherapeutic studies that directly target this machinery in the disease remain limited [64]. A key design requirement is selective dampening of pathological inflammation without broadly suppressing host defense, since excessive immune silencing could impair debris clearance and increase vulnerability to infection or impaired surveillance [62, 64, 226]. Thus, anti-inflammatory nanomedicine should be evaluated with dual endpoints: suppression of harmful inflammatory signaling and preservation of protective immune functions. Biomarkers such as IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, NLRP3 activation, microglial morphology, phagocytic capacity, synaptic integrity, and behavioral performance may be needed together to judge therapeutic relevance.

Across platforms, recent trends favor (i) microglia-biased localization, (ii) multi-pathway modulation rather than single-node inhibition, and (iii) designs that combine lesion homing with controllable release. For translation, the main bottlenecks remain long-term neurotoxicity, immunogenicity, batch-to-batch variability, and the need to demonstrate durable benefit without compromising beneficial microglial functions [29]. In the broader roadmap of AD nanomedicine, neuroinflammation-targeted strategies are likely to be most useful as part of combination or phenotype-selected therapy, especially when inflammatory activation coexists with amyloid accumulation, Tau pathology, oxidative stress, or vascular dysfunction. Their clinical feasibility will depend on achieving immune recalibration, predictable exposure, reproducible manufacturing, and safety over repeated dosing in an aging population.

Peripheral immune crosstalk and brain infiltration

Chemokines released within the brain guide the entry of peripheral neutrophils, monocytes, cytotoxic T cells, helper T cells, regulatory T cells, and B cells into the parenchyma. Whereas the previous section focused primarily on resident CNS inflammatory pathways, this section addresses peripheral immune communication with the brain and its relevance for systemic-to-central nanomedicine strategies. This self-adjusting influx can help remove pathogenic mediators, yet infiltrating cells carry both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory potential, which keeps their net impact on disease progression context dependent and sometimes uncertain [223, 228]. Cytotoxic T cells and helper T cells can amplify inflammatory signaling, whereas regulatory T cells can temper it. Importantly, most mechanistic and interventional data in this area remain preclinical, and translating immune modulation to patients requires preserving beneficial surveillance while preventing harmful immunosuppression [129, 224]. This balance is particularly important in older patients, in whom immune senescence, vascular dysfunction, infection susceptibility, and comorbid systemic inflammation may alter both therapeutic response and safety.

Regulatory T cells display immune checkpoints such as CTLA-4, CD27, and LAG3 that engage CD80/CD86, CD70, and MHC class II on cytotoxic T cells and macrophages, thereby suppressing effector T cell activity and promoting macrophage apoptosis in certain contexts [229]. These features support two complementary strategies that harness regulatory T cells against neuroinflammation [129, 224]. One strategy is to create vesicles from regulatory T cell membranes so that their surface proteins deliver anti-inflammatory signals, optionally with nanoparticle cargo to combine membrane signaling with drug action [85, 230]. A key translational gap is that lesion localization and payload release kinetics in the brain are still incompletely defined for many biomimetic constructs, which complicates dose control, reproducibility, and safety assessment [62, 63, 128]. For Treg-derived vesicles, additional development barriers include donor or source-cell variability, membrane protein preservation, potency testing, sterility, storage stability, and the risk that excessive checkpoint signaling could dampen protective immune surveillance.

The second strategy is to expand peripheral regulatory T cells that are engineered for stronger accumulation and function inside the brain. For example, limited benefit from amyloid vaccination has been linked to inflammation driven by mature dendritic cells that present amyloid antigens and activate amyloid-specific helper T cells [55, 231]. Co-delivery of the vaccine with rapamycin can promote tolerogenic dendritic cells that release TGF-β and IL-10, induce amyloid-specific regulatory T cells, and shift the response toward resolution within the brain [232, 233]. Because immune tone and BBB integrity vary across disease stages, stage-aware immunomodulation and biomarker-guided stratification are likely to be necessary for consistent efficacy [131]. These approaches may be most appropriate for inflammatory or immune-reactive AD phenotypes, but they should not be assumed to benefit patients whose dominant pathology is primarily Tau-driven synaptic failure, advanced neuronal loss, or irreversible neurovascular damage.

Links between peripheral and brain inflammation extend beyond immune cells. Disturbance of the gut microbiome correlates with neuroinflammation, and microbial products, cytokines, and misfolded proteins can reach the brain through a compromised BBB, lymphatic routes, and the vagus nerve [234, 235]. The gut flora differs between transgenic disease models and controls and can track with pro-inflammatory mediators. In one approach, chitosan selenium nanoparticles carrying dihydromyricetin and decorated with a targeting ligand reshaped the gut community in disease models toward a healthier profile and reduced expression of the inflammasome component NLRP3 in both intestine and hippocampus, consistent with concurrent relief of peripheral inflammation and neuroinflammation [57]. However, causality remains difficult to prove, and future work should distinguish direct central effects from indirect benefits mediated by systemic inflammation, barrier integrity, or metabolite signaling [65, 226]. For gut-directed nanomedicine, this distinction is essential because changes in microbial composition do not necessarily demonstrate a causal therapeutic mechanism unless they are linked to defined metabolites, immune pathways, BBB effects, central target engagement, and behavioral outcomes.

The immune landscape of the CNS is complex. Once viewed as immune privileged, the brain is now recognized as accessible to peripheral cells and metabolites under pathological conditions [131]. Interplay between infiltrating components and resident regulators can either help or harm. Many immune elements remain underexplored, including astrocytes, and their interactions with proteinopathy, oxidative stress, and vascular dysfunction construct a dense network that advances disease [4, 174]. Multitarget nanomedicine may help navigate this network, but major bottlenecks for translation include long-term safety, immunogenicity, scalable manufacturing, and batch-to-batch variability, especially for biologically derived carriers (Table 5) [29, 34]. Therefore, peripheral immune nanomedicine should be positioned as a phenotype-selected and systems-level strategy rather than a generalized anti-inflammatory intervention. Its success will depend on demonstrating controllable immune recalibration, reproducible carrier identity, predictable biodistribution, and durable benefit without impairing host defense or beneficial clearance mechanisms.

Regenerative nanomedicine for stem cell repair

Because adult neurons have limited capacity for renewal, even comprehensive removal of pathogenic mediators may slow decline without restoring lost circuitry [204, 258]. Regeneration, in the strict sense, requires rebuilding functional networks, which can involve neuronal replacement, support of endogenous repair programs, and restoration of the neurovascular unit [259]. Within a stage-informed framework, regenerative nanomedicine is most relevant for advanced or repair-deficient AD phenotypes, particularly when neuronal loss, synaptic disconnection, vascular dysfunction, and impaired trophic support have become major barriers to functional recovery. A major practical constraint is that regenerative concepts are still predominantly preclinical, and their translational success will depend on controllable dosing, reproducible manufacturing, long-term safety, and compatibility with the evolving BBB and immune milieu across disease stages [260]. Therefore, regenerative platforms should be positioned as adjunctive or repair-oriented strategies rather than direct substitutes for amyloid, Tau, inflammatory, or metabolic interventions.

One route is to grow neural organoids outside the body and implant them into injured regions, but insufficient vascularization undermines survival after implantation [259]. Growth factors such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor support neuronal development, yet their levels can fall in the disease microenvironment due to proteinopathy, inflammation, and cell loss [261]. Nanocarriers have been explored to deliver growth factors, although precise control over dose, timing, and localization remains difficult [32, 164]. Off-target exposure and excessive release can cause adverse effects, including pain syndromes after nerve growth factor oversupply, which highlights the need for release kinetics that are predictable and stage aware. A complementary strategy is to deliver living cells that secrete trophic factors in response to physiological cues, thereby coupling production to local demand and potentially reducing systemic exposure [164]. However, trophic factor delivery should be assessed not only by increased neuronal survival markers, but also by synaptic integration, circuit-level function, neurovascular compatibility, and the absence of aberrant sprouting or maladaptive plasticity.

Mesenchymal stem cells are attractive because they modulate immunity and secrete trophic mediators [262]. When mesenchymal stem cells internalize iron oxide nanoparticles, breakdown to iron ions can activate the c-Jun N-terminal kinase pathway and increase expression of growth factors such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor [204]. Loaded cells also become magnetic and can be guided toward brain tissue using an external field, which can reduce off-target distribution [204]. However, translational barriers include cell-to-cell variability, batch consistency, scalability, and the need to define long-term biodistribution and safety of nanoparticle processing in vivo, particularly when repeated dosing is considered [29, 30, 128, 129]. For clinical development, stem-cell-based nanomedicine also requires standardized cell identity, potency assays, viability criteria, differentiation status, tumorigenicity assessment, immunogenicity testing, and release specifications that can be reproduced across manufacturing batches.

Exogenous stem cells and progenitors can differentiate into mature neural lineages. Classical control relies on cytokines, metabolic support, and mechanical cues, but nanomaterials can also shape lineage commitment. Circularly polarized light applied to L-type nanoparticles has been reported to induce Map2 and c-Fos expression in neural stem cells and to increase hippocampal neuron numbers in disease models, with associated gains in cognition, without introducing foreign cells [205]. Even when exogenous cells are avoided, physical stimulation strategies still require careful safety evaluation, because photostimulation parameters, tissue penetration, and off-target bioeffects can vary across models and may change with vascular and barrier pathology [29, 30, 128, 129]. In addition, reprogramming differentiated somatic cells into neurons offers a route for in situ regeneration, but this concept remains early and requires rigorous validation in this condition [230]. These approaches are conceptually attractive because they may reduce dependence on cell transplantation, but they remain exploratory until lineage specificity, durability of conversion, circuit integration, and long-term safety are demonstrated in disease-relevant models.

Because newly formed neurons remain exposed to pathogenic stressors, clearance mechanisms often need to accompany regeneration. Engineered neural stem cells that overexpress neprilysin can degrade amyloid at the membrane and through exosomes [263]. When such cells also carry a nanocomposite of retinoic acid, silver sulfide quantum dots, and a silencing sequence against SOX9, they gain a second function that promotes neuronal differentiation via retinoic acid signaling and Wnt beta-catenin pathway modulation, while near-infrared imaging from the quantum dots supports placement. This combined strategy reduced amyloid burden and neuron loss in models [264]. A key concern for translation is long-term safety and controllability of engineered overexpression programs, including unintended effects on physiological peptide processing and durability of expression, alongside manufacturing complexity and immune compatibility [265]. The broader lesson is that regenerative efficacy may require combined designs that protect new or rescued neurons from amyloid toxicity, Tau-related proteostatic stress, inflammation, and vascular insufficiency. However, each added function increases analytical complexity and may complicate regulatory characterization, release testing, and scale-up.

Neurovascular repair as a regenerative target

Beyond neurons, the disorder also involves vascular injury, including amyloid deposition in vessel walls, barrier breakdown, and disturbed flow. Dysfunction of the neurovascular unit, which includes endothelial cells, astrocytes, pericytes, microglia, and neurons, can permit entry of blood-derived factors while impairing perfusion and clearance of waste. Repair likely requires coordinated actions across multiple cell types and therefore favors co-delivery strategies [124, 266, 267]. A self-assembling system built from GM1, DMPC, a ligand for the receptor for advanced glycation end products, and apolipoprotein E3 improved endothelial, astrocytic, and pericyte coverage, increased dendritic complexity in hippocampal neurons, and improved cognition in behavioral tests [267]. These results support vascular repair as a key component of disease-modifying regimens, but translation will require careful definition of biodistribution, barrier-stage dependence, and reproducible assembly and characterization [46]. Neurovascular repair may be especially relevant in advanced AD or in patients with prominent vascular comorbidity, BBB dysfunction, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, or impaired clearance pathways. For such strategies, meaningful endpoints should include BBB integrity, perfusion, pericyte and astrocyte coverage, vascular inflammation, amyloid clearance, synaptic preservation, and cognition rather than neuronal survival alone.

Overall, regenerative nanomedicine expands the therapeutic scope of AD beyond removal of toxic proteins toward repair of neurons, synapses, and the neurovascular unit. Its strongest rationale lies in advanced or repair-deficient disease states, but its clinical pathway is likely to be more difficult than that of simpler lipid, polymeric, or small-molecule nanocarriers. The main barriers include complex product identity, potency testing, living-cell or vesicle variability, long-term engraftment or persistence, immune compatibility, tumorigenicity risk, and scalable manufacturing. For this reason, regenerative nanomedicine should be developed with cautious claims, clearly defined functional endpoints, and realistic positioning as part of combination or stage-selected therapy.

Conclusions and future directions

Nanoscale technologies have sharpened detection by improving the sensitivity and precision of sensors and molecular probes, enabling more reliable recognition of pathological amyloid species, neurofibrillary tangles, and related biomarkers [268]. Earlier diagnosis, potentially at lower overall cost, may follow and could improve outcomes by enabling timely intervention and better patient stratification. Beyond diagnostics, nanotechnology has reshaped therapeutic development by offering controllable platforms that can be engineered to cross the BBB and enrich drug exposure in affected brain regions rather than the entire brain [128, 129, 269]. Recent advances extend beyond barrier traversal and increasingly address parenchymal distribution, cellular uptake, endosomal escape, and reduced lysosomal degradation, all of which influence effective intracellular exposure and pharmacodynamic potency [60]. The next stage of AD nanomedicine should therefore move from platform-centered innovation toward stage-informed and pathology-guided therapeutic design, in which the selected carrier, payload, targeting ligand, and release mechanism are matched to the dominant disease context.

A key strength of nanomedicine is multifunctionality. Nanocarriers can deliver small molecules, biologics, and nucleic acids to pathways that limit apoptosis and dampen neuroinflammatory signaling, while some catalytic platforms such as nanozymes can reduce reactive oxygen species and thereby attenuate downstream apoptotic and pyroptotic cascades, in some cases without a conventional drug payload [32]. Importantly, neuroinflammation is not confined to microglia and astrocytes, and peripheral immune components can meaningfully shape central inflammatory tone [222]. In this context, nanovaccines and immunomodulatory designs represent a forward direction to tune lymphocyte responses and promote a more resolving immune state. Regenerative concepts further broaden the landscape, but the most plausible disease-modifying strategies will likely couple repair and remodeling with concurrent reduction of pathogenic stressors, reflecting the networked nature of disease mechanisms and the integrative capabilities of nanosystems [169]. However, multifunctionality should not be treated as an automatic advantage. Increasing biological sophistication may improve mechanistic coverage, but it can also increase formulation complexity, analytical burden, batch-to-batch variability, immunogenicity, and difficulty in scale-up. For this reason, future designs should justify each added function according to a clear therapeutic need and measurable pharmacodynamic endpoint.

A realistic translational hierarchy is also needed. Selected lipid-based systems, liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, and extracellular vesicle-based platforms may have a more plausible near-term path toward clinical development because their composition, loading behavior, sterility, release profile, and manufacturing controls can be standardized more readily. In contrast, highly complex multifunctional inorganic systems, ROS-generating photodynamic or sonodynamic platforms, extensively engineered biomimetic carriers, and cell-based regenerative products remain more exploratory. These approaches are scientifically valuable, but their clinical progression will require stronger evidence for long-term biodistribution, clearance, repeat-dose safety, immune compatibility, and reproducible manufacturing.

Despite these advances, translation remains the critical bottleneck. Many platforms remain preclinical, and their clinical potential depends on predictable safety profiles, long-term biodistribution and clearance, batch-to-batch reproducibility, scalable manufacturing, and alignment with regulatory expectations for complex nanosystems. Additional uncertainty arises because BBB integrity and transport biology can vary across disease stage, which can shift both efficacy and risk. Future studies should therefore report standardized physicochemical characterization, relevant dosing metrics, and stage-aware in vivo evaluation, alongside rigorous assessments of immunogenicity, neurotoxicity, and off-target distribution [128, 129]. Regulatory readiness will require clear definition of critical quality attributes, including particle size distribution, surface charge, morphology, drug loading, release kinetics, stability, sterility, endotoxin status, impurity profile, and batch comparability. These requirements are especially important for AD because treatment may require repeated dosing over long periods in older patients with variable BBB integrity, vascular comorbidity, and altered immune responsiveness.

Lessons from difficult-to-translate approaches should also shape future development. Promising in vitro amyloid binding, high cellular uptake, or apparent BBB penetration does not necessarily predict meaningful clinical benefit. Several recurring barriers remain important, including insufficient brain accumulation, protein corona-mediated loss of targeting specificity, poor correlation between animal biodistribution and human brain exposure, long-term retention of inorganic materials, uncertain clearance of amyloid-bound complexes, and weak reproducibility across formulations. Addressing these barriers early may help prevent the field from advancing platforms that are biologically interesting but clinically impractical.

Finally, integration of computational design and optimization is a realistic near-term opportunity. Data-driven approaches can support rational selection of materials, surface ligands, and release behaviors, potentially accelerating iteration while reducing empirical trial-and-error. Computational modeling, artificial intelligence-guided formulation screening, and biomarker-informed patient stratification may also help identify which nanomedicine designs are most suitable for amyloid-predominant, Tau-dominant, inflammatory, mitochondrial, or neurovascular phenotypes. Overall, the future of AD nanomedicine will depend less on developing increasingly complex platforms and more on building clinically testable, reproducible, mechanism-matched systems that integrate therapeutic precision with manufacturability, safety, and regulatory feasibility.
