Multiscale object detection model based on pyramid vision transformer
Authors: Ji-Won Baek, Dongjoon Suh, Kyungyong Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The sizes of objects within an image can vary significantly. In particular, in fields such as construction, manufacturing, and healthcare, where image analysis directly affects human life and safety, it is crucial to accurately detect even small objects. To address this, the present study proposes a multiscale object detection model that employs the Pyramid Vision Transformer (PVT) as the backbone network for the YOLO model. This approach compensates for the limitations of the Spatial Pyramid Pooling Feature (SPPF) used in conventional YOLO models and improves the detection accuracy for small objects. The proposed transformer-based multiscale object detection model aims to effectively capture long-range dependencies while simplifying complex pre-processing and post-processing procedures. Furthermore, it generates feature maps at various resolutions, enabling multiscale feature representation and object detection. In particular, by leveraging Global Self-Attention, the model leverages contextual information from the entire image, thereby enhancing understanding of object relationships and improving overall scene comprehension.

Introduction

Studies on artificial intelligence (AI) for image analysis are ongoing. Transformers have been introduced in natural language processing, particularly because their computational capabilities and high accuracy enable their use in image analysis1. In addition, with rapid advancements in generative models over the past three years, diffusion models have emerged as a new paradigm in object detection and image restoration2,3. For instance, studies have reported precise detection and restoration performance, even in complex backgrounds, by applying diffusion models to ship detection in remote sensing images or to de-striping infrared images4. However, such diffusion model-based approaches have the disadvantage of high computational costs and relatively slow inference times due to the need for iterative denoising5. Therefore, in construction and manufacturing sites where immediate judgment is essential, as it directly affects human life and safety, efficient models that ensure efficient performance are crucial. As a transformer uses spatial information only to a limited extent, it contributes to the identification of interactions and relationships between objects6. Therefore, as it does not emphasize the location information when such information between objects in an image is essential, its utilization is convenient. Additionally, it is not influenced by the length of the input sequence, and the output matrices are fixed in size. Therefore, regardless of the input image size, the transformer generates outputs of a consistent size and effectively performs learning7. Among image analysis methods, object detection identifies and localizes a particular object in a given scene by drawing a bounding box around it. Object detection is utilized in diverse industrial fields, such as autonomous driving, manufacturing, and healthcare. As the YOLO model employed for this purpose has a spatial limitation: only the bounding boxes associated with each grid cell are predicted, it is challenging to distinguish overlapping objects. Therefore, to enhance accuracy, the spatial relations between objects need to be understood and classified. In addition, as several down-samplings are used, inaccurate features may be extracted. Hence, a method capable of processing multiple scales needs to be developed. Defects can cause numerous problems during manufacturing processes. Concrete defects may reduce concrete strength or cause cracking, thereby degrading the strength and stability of the entire structure8. Concrete defects include cracks, spalling, efflorescence, exposure, steel defects, and paint damage. Cracks are fractures or separations that occur in concrete structures. Spalling is the peeling of concrete surfaces due to environmental deterioration. Efflorescence refers to the stain remaining on the surface of a concrete structure after a salt or material evaporates, and it occurs owing to the concrete mix. Paint damage refers to damage or erosion occurring on the surface of a structure. This happens due to environmental factors, deterioration, etc., and may reduce a structure’s protective function. Structural defects can significantly impede evacuation and rescue efforts during emergencies such as fires and earthquakes, thereby endangering not only occupants but also rescue personnel. Moreover, structural collapses and accidents caused by such defects can result in substantial loss of life and considerable property damage.

Recent investigations have identified ground subsidence and internal defects within concrete structures, such as cracking and spalling, as major contributing factors to sinkhole incidents9. Similarly, newly constructed apartment complexes have exhibited critical defects, including concrete cracking and reinforcement exposure. To mitigate these risks and enhance the structural integrity of buildings, it is imperative to establish systematic defect detection and maintenance frameworks capable of early identification and preventive intervention. Proactive defect monitoring and maintenance strategies are essential for ensuring user safety.

In this paper, a multiscale object detection model based on a pyramid vision transformer is proposed to leverage the global context capturing capability of transformers while avoiding the high computational costs associated with diffusion models. The proposed method uses a pyramid-network-based transformer model as the backbone network of an object detection model to detect defects at diverse scales. The latest State-of-the-Art (SOTA) model, YOLOv12, is utilized as the object detection model. Therefore, the accuracy of defect detection is enhanced by improving the backbone of YOLOv12 to address its limitations and by enabling detection across diverse scales. Related work

Defect detection technology trend

Defect detection involves detecting defects or faults in a product or process. This enables prompt detection of product defects and the resolution of quality-related issues during the improvement process. In addition, it enables the identification of defect causes and cost reduction through improvements to the manufacturing process. Moreover, it enables the construction of an automated detection system and improves productivity and efficiency through prompt, accurate defect detection. Defect detection methods include visual inspection, sensor-based inspection, automated inspection, and machine learning. Visual inspection utilizes image data to detect defects in products and processes. It detects abnormal parts by analyzing the shape, color, and pattern of the defects using visual and image-processing algorithms. Sensor-based inspection uses sensors to detect defects. It detects defects by analyzing physical signal measurements, such as vibration, temperature, pressure, and current10. Automated inspection uses a computer-based system to inspect a large number of products promptly and accurately. Machine-learning methods use the normal and abnormal statuses of defects to learn from large volumes of data using machine-learning algorithms11. This enables automatic feature extraction and classification, thereby improving accuracy. Wang et al.12 proposed a bridge-defect classification model for reinforced concrete by constructing a transformer-based hybrid network. In the proposed method, the multi-head attention of the transformer block classifies defects by combining and building knowledge of the same attention pooling through mutually different expression spaces of the query, key, and value. Ruggieri et al.13 proposed a machine-learning-based method for bridge-defect detection. In this method, an image-related convolutional neural network is trained to automatically recognize defects and damage related to bridge factors, such as cracks and humidity. However, this method cannot determine defect locations. Wang et al.14 proposed a deep-learning-based method for the autodetection of concrete surface defects. It is a one-stage object detection network consisting of two parts: EfficientNetB0 and a detector. EfficientNetB0 stacks several inverted bottleneck convolution blocks to reduce parameters and enhance feature extraction performance. The detector improves detection accuracy by combining low- and high-level features via up-sampling. This enables simultaneous detection of crack and exposure location and category information within an image. Figure 1 shows the structure of the defect detection model based on deep learning.

Pyramid vision transformer

In general, standard vision transformers can be applied to image classification and visual recognition tasks15, exhibiting high performance. However, they have the disadvantages of providing low-resolution output and incurring high computational and memory costs. Therefore, they are not directly applicable to pixel-level density estimations, such as object detection and segmentation, without advanced mechanisms like feature-enhanced pyramids16 or flow learning frameworks17. To solve this problem, pyramid vision transformers can be applied across various fields, including image- and pixel-level density prediction. A pyramidal vision transformer uses fine-grained image patches as inputs, which are essential for learning high-resolution representations. The computational cost of the transformer is reduced by using a pyramid, which diminishes the sequence length as the neural network becomes deeper. In addition, a spatial reduction attentional layer is applied to reduce the computational and memory costs when learning high-resolution features18. Figure 2 shows the structure of the pyramid vision transformer.

Fig. 2Structure of the pyramid vision transformer.

Structure of the pyramid vision transformer.

The pyramid vision transformer comprises four stages that generate feature maps of different sizes. All stages use a structure containing a patch-embedding layer and a transform-encoder layer. In the first stage, an input image of height×width×3 is divided into 4 × 4 × 3 patches of size (H×W)/4. The patches are then flattened and projected linearly to reconstruct a feature map of size H/4 × W/4 × C1. Thus, the feature map from the previous step is used as the input to obtain the next feature map. In addition, the spatial size of the keys and values can be reduced by applying a linear space-reducing attachment layer to the multi-head attachment layer, which is common in transformer encoders. This reduces computational and memory costs and enables image classification and object detection.

Zhou et al.19 proposed a directional SAR ship-detection model using pyramidal vision transformers. This model applies overlapping patch embedding and mixed transformer encoder modules to address the challenges of a smaller target size, dense target arrangement, and limited data for SAR ship detection, compared with the conventional pyramid vision transformer. In addition, the multiscale feature module improves the utilization of various features. This enhances the detection capability for small targets. Hu et al.20 proposed a high-resolution iterative feedback network for detecting camouflaged objects. It consists of transformer-based feature extraction, multiresolution iterative refinement, and iterative feature feedback modules. To reduce the computational cost of high-resolution feature maps, a pyramidal vision transformer was used as an image feature encoder, employing a progressively shrinking pyramid and spatially shrinking attention. Accordingly, we used a multiresolution dense enhancement module to improve the low-resolution features extracted by the transformer-based feature extractor, employing global and cross-scale strategies. Additionally, we used iterative feature feedback to regulate the feedback flow and improve the features. Thus, the proposed method can adaptively refine low-resolution features using high-resolution information via an iterative feedback scheme. Therefore, it solves the problem of detailed degradation and extracts informative high-resolution representations.

Multiscale object detection model based on pyramid vision transformer

Defects vary in size. Significant defects can be directly observed, and object detection models generally demonstrate high accuracy in detecting them. However, minor defects that cannot be visually confirmed can lead to significant problems. Therefore, a method capable of detecting objects at diverse scales needs to be developed. Recently released YOLOv12 has dramatically improved accuracy through an Attention-Centric design, going beyond the limitations of existing CNN-based detectors. However, the utilization of Global Context information remains crucial for detecting minute defects within complex backgrounds. Accordingly, this study proposes a multiscale object detection model that combines the powerful detection head of YOLOv12 with the Pyramid Vision Transformer (PVT v2) backbone. This aims to maximize detection accuracy for small objects while maintaining inference speeds applicable to industrial sites by fusing PVT’s long-range dependency learning capability with YOLOv12’s Area Attention technology. In addition, to resolve the class imbalance problem, an advanced data augmentation technique based on semantic segmentation is applied.

Data collection and pre-processing

Data pre-processing is the preparation of data for practical use in a model. In this study, the ‘Building Crack Detection’ dataset21 from AI Hub is utilized. This dataset comprises approximately 345,000 high-resolution images collected from the surfaces of real concrete structures, featuring diverse defect types, including cracks, spalling, efflorescence, and exposed reinforcement. Figure 4 shows examples of representative concrete defect types included in the dataset.

Fig. 4Images showing types of concrete defects.

Images showing types of concrete defects.

The annotation information of the collected original data follows the COCO (Common Objects in Context) format22, which has a JSON-based hierarchical structure. The COCO format includes metadata such as image ID, file path, image size, object class, and bounding box coordinates. However, YOLOv12, the latest object detection model adopted in this study, requires a label file in normalized text (.txt) format for each image file during training. Accordingly, we pre-processed the COCO-formatted JSON data and converted it into the YOLO-specific format before training. Specifically, the original bounding box coordinates were converted to the center coordinate, width, and height format required by the YOLO model, and then normalized to the range [0,1] based on the image’s total size. In addition, the consistency of the training dataset was ensured by resizing the original image to 640 × 640 resolution, matching the model’s input size, and scaling the pixel values. This systematic data preparation process provides a foundation for the model to learn defect objects of various sizes and ratios effectively.

Defect segmentation using 8-step high-precision pre-processing

Concrete image data collected from construction sites faces the challenge of clearly distinguishing defect objects from the background due to uneven lighting, shadows, and the rough surface texture of concrete in outdoor shooting environments. In particular, defects such as fine cracks or spalling are challenging to detect with general binarization techniques because their pixel intensities are similar to background noise. To overcome these environmental constraints and precisely extract only pure defect regions, this study proposes an 8-Step High-Precision Pre-processing Pipeline. The flowchart of the proposed overall process is shown in Fig. 5.

Fig. 5Proposed 8-Step Pre-processing Pipeline for Defect Segmentation.

Proposed 8-Step Pre-processing Pipeline for Defect Segmentation.

Figure 5 shows the overall flow of the proposed 8-step pre-processing pipeline. The pre-processing process is primarily divided into three stages: noise removal, contrast enhancement, and morphological correction.

First, the 3-channel RGB original image is converted to a single-channel Grayscale image to reduce data dimensions and secure computational efficiency. Then, Median Blur is applied to effectively remove sensor or dot noise while preserving as much crack-edge information as possible.

Second, CLAHE (Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization), a core technique of this study, was introduced in step 3. While general Histogram Equalization uniformly adjusts the contrast of the entire image, often amplifying noise, CLAHE divides the image into local tiles and equalizes the histogram for each region. This locally enhances the contrast of fine cracks hidden in shadowed or low-light areas, improving detectability. Subsequently, Normalization and Inversion processes are performed to readjust the pixel intensity distribution to the 0–255 range and to invert dark-defect areas into bright Regions of Interest (ROI), thereby maximizing visual features.

Third, Binarization is performed to primarily separate the object from the background based on a threshold. However, this may cause disconnection phenomena, in which fine cracks appear as dotted lines. To compensate for this, a Morphology Closing operation is applied in step 7. This operation plays a decisive role in filling cracks and securing structural continuity by dilating the object to connect adjacent pixels, then eroding it again. Furthermore, this morphological processing is essential for reconstructing the topological structure of defects, preventing the fragmentation of long, continuous cracks into multiple disconnected components, which could otherwise lead to underestimation of defect severity. Finally, Size Filtering removes fine scratches or isolated pixel clusters that are not actual defects by evaluating the area of connected components and discarding noise artifacts smaller than a predefined threshold, thereby ultimately obtaining a high-quality Clean Defect Mask.

Fig. 6Visual Results of the 8-Step Pre-processing.

Visual Results of the 8-Step Pre-processing.

Figure 6 illustrates the 8-step process applied to real concrete images to precisely segment defects. The progression demonstrates a gradual enhancement in segmentation quality, where initial noise and artifacts are systematically removed. The final mask provides a clear and accurate delineation of the crack, highlighting the effectiveness of the sequential pre-processing steps. It can be confirmed that noise is reduced and the defect area becomes clearer with each step.

Data augmentation using random synthesis

One of the primary causes of performance degradation in deep learning-based object detection models is class imbalance, where the number of defect data points is insufficient relative to the number of normal data points. General geometric augmentations (rotation, flipping, etc.) cannot fundamentally resolve the issue of data distribution. Since these conventional methods rely solely on manipulating the spatial orientation of existing samples, they fail to introduce the novel defect patterns and diverse background contexts required for robust model generalization. To address this, we propose a Semantic Segmentation-based Random Synthesis technique utilizing the high-precision masks extracted in Section Defect segmentation using 8-step high-precision pre-processing.

Constructing a multiscale object detection model based on pyramid vision transformers

Diverse object detection models exist, including Fast R-CNN, Faster R-CNN, and YOLO. Fast R-CNN extracts features of fixed size from a feature map via ROI pooling. It simultaneously predicts an object’s class and bounding box in a single network, and the overall network is trained end-to-end. Faster R-CNN uses a region proposal network to propose candidate regions for an object. It promptly proposes candidate regions for an object in an image, which are then used for object classification and bounding box regression via ROI pooling. Fast R-CNN and Faster R-CNN are two-stage models. In the first stage, the object candidate regions are proposed23,24. In the second stage, object classification and bounding box regression are performed based on the proposed areas. However, these models have the disadvantage of very low processing speeds.

Based on the comparison results shown in Fig. 10, YOLOv12 exhibited the best performance. Fast R-CNN exhibited the slowest learning speed, the lowest mAP performance, and the fewest detected objects. In addition, Faster R-CNN showed a comparatively higher speed and better performance than Fast R-CNN. On the other hand, while YOLOv8 and YOLOv12 showed comparatively high learning speeds and accuracies, YOLOv12 was evaluated as the best performer, as it is an improved version that addresses the disadvantages of YOLOv825,26. In addition, the YOLOv12 model is used because it has lower false-positive and false-negative rates than YOLOv8. However, computational complexity increased owing to the introduction of advanced attention mechanisms. Accordingly, although accurate learning is possible for training data, overfitting problems may occur. In addition, as computational complexity increases, additional learning time and substantial computing resources are required. Furthermore, increased memory usage causes problems in resource-constrained environments. Therefore, to improve YOLOv12, a transformer backbone is used to enable accurate and rapid object detection across diverse scales.

YOLOv12 breaks away from the CNN-centric design of the existing YOLO series and introduces an Attention-Centric architecture to capture global context information effectively. In particular, it uses Area Attention instead of the C2f module in the existing YOLOv8, drastically expanding the feature map’s receptive field. Area Attention divides the input image into broad areas for processing, thereby overcoming the limitations of local information processing inherent in conventional convolutional operations and enabling learning of long-range dependencies between objects. Furthermore, FlashAttention technology was optimized and applied to prevent degradation in operation speed. These attention modules are combined with the R-ELAN (Residual Efficient Layer Aggregation Network) architecture to maximize feature extraction; however, the computational cost increases exponentially with model depth. Therefore, this study aims to improve efficiency by replacing the backbone with PVT, a lightweight transformer optimized for pyramid structures, while retaining the powerful detection head of YOLOv1218.

Through self-attention, key regions of the input image’s feature map are emphasized, preserving information such as object location, size, and orientation. In addition, this approach is advantageous because it enables long-term dependency learning and flexible adaptation to diverse sizes and resolutions. To enhance the accuracy and performance of an object detection model, a vision-transformer-based backbone can be used. This provides the following potential advantages: First, because the transformer captures global context, each section of an image that interacts with another can be considered. Second, the transformer assigns a weighted value to a particular section using an attention mechanism. Therefore, it enables accurate detection of sections essential for object detection. Third, as the vision transformer partitions and processes an image into patches of fixed size, it can flexibly respond to images at diverse resolutions. To find a suitable vision transformer model to use as the backbone of an object detection model, the best model was selected by comparing the advantages/disadvantages, and parameters, among the standard vision transformer, Swin vision transformer, and pyramid vision transformer. The proposed model uses a pyramid vision transformer and YOLOv12 as the object detection model. In general, it exhibits a feature-pyramid network structure. Figure 11 shows the configuration of the pyramid-vision-transformer-based object detection model.

Fig. 11Configuration of the pyramid-vision-transformer-based object detection model.

Configuration of the pyramid-vision-transformer-based object detection model.

Figure 11 shows the pyramid vision transformer backbone network and the YOLO detection head. The pyramid vision transformer backbone network consists of Stages 1 to 3, and each stage transforms an input image into a feature map of different sizes. To extract multiscale features, an input image is converted into feature maps with different resolutions: H/4×W/4×C1, H/8×W/8×C2, and H/16×W/16×C3. Therefore, each stage extracts and encodes the features of an input image while preserving spatial arrangement information using transformer blocks and positional embeddings. The transformer blocks within each stage use self-attention to extract the global context information. Through this process, the model identifies the overall structure of an image and understands the complex relationships between objects. Patch embedding partitions an image into patches, transforms each patch into an embedding vector, and uses these vectors as inputs to a transformer. With respect to the YOLO head, the sizes of the multiscale feature maps generated by the backbone network through up-sampling and connection are adjusted, and the adjusted multiscale feature maps are connected to the feature maps of the previous stage. Therefore, the information extracted from the feature maps is integrated, enabling the model to perform a more precise detection. In addition, the feature maps integrated into the object detection stage are used to perform the final object detection using the YOLO detection head. This predicts the bounding boxes, class probabilities, and object sizes for each object in each feature map. Therefore, through pyramid-vision-transformer-based object detection, object detection can be improved in complex image environments where objects of diverse sizes and shapes coexist.

Results and performance evaluation

Multiscale object detection results based on pyramid vision transformer

The hardware and operating system used to implement the pyramid-vision-transformer-based multiscale object detection model included an AMD Ryzen 9 5990 × 12-core processor, an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090, 96 GB of RAM, Ubuntu 18.04.6, and Python 3.7. The libraries used were torch 1.7.1, etc. The optimization function used was AdamW, with a weight decay of 0.05 and a learning rate of 0.0001. To ensure a fair performance comparison, the learning environments for all models were unified in this study. The total training was set to 300 epochs with a batch size of 32, and input images were resized to 640 × 640. The optimization function used for the PVT-based object detection model was AdamW, with weight decay set to 0.05 and an initial learning rate of 0.001, using a Cosine Annealing scheduler. In addition, the design ensured generalizable performance across datasets with varying environmental variables, without requiring separate cross-dataset validation via data splitting and augmentation. Finally, a performance comparison was made with other transformer-based object detection models (DETR, conditional DETR, deformable DETR, Efficient DETR and DINO). DETR30 defines object detection through direct set prediction and uses a transformer and bipartite matching losses. It utilizes global information through self-attention and effectively detects large objects. Conditional DETR31 learns conditional spatial queries in decoder embeddings for decoder multi-head attention. This is advantageous because it enables us to focus on the range that includes the region where each cross-attention is clear, using conditional spatial queries. Therefore, as the spatial range becomes too narrow to identify a unique area for object classification and box regression, the dependency on content embedding can be alleviated. Deformable DETR32 combines the sparse spatial sampling of a convolution and the relation modeling capability of a transformer. A deformable attention module that performs attention computation only on small sampling location groups is used to preliminarily filter out key factors from the pixels of all feature maps. Such a deformable attention module is advantageous because it enables natural expansion to sum up multiscale features. Additionally, Efficient DETR33 was included as a representative modern detector that prioritizes parameter efficiency and convergence speed through dense prior initialization. DINO34 was also compared as a state-of-the-art model that improves upon previous DETR variants by introducing a contrastive denoising training strategy to stabilize the bipartite matching process. However, these models typically demand high computational resources and slow inference speeds, making them less suitable for real-time industrial applications where latency is critical. By comparing our method against these benchmarks, we aim to validate that the proposed 3-stage PVT architecture offers a superior balance between detection accuracy and computational efficiency. Conclusions

Studies are being conducted on object detection, which enables accurate detection and localization of diverse objects in images to support the identification of visual information. Although the recently introduced YOLOv12 incorporates Area Attention mechanisms to overcome the limitations of traditional CNNs, its architecture is primarily optimized for general object detection (COCO benchmark), balancing speed and accuracy. However, in the domain of concrete defect detection, where targets are often minute and exhibit irregular patterns indistinguishable from the background, YOLOv12’s native attention mechanism may still struggle to fully capture the fine-grained global context required for pixel-level precision. Furthermore, the computational complexity of YOLOv12’s backbone can be excessive when scaling up for high-resolution industrial inspection. To address this, this study proposed a multiscale object detection model that replaces the backbone with a Pyramid Vision Transformer (PVT v2). By leveraging Spatial Reduction Attention (SRA), the proposed method efficiently captures robust global dependencies tailored for small defect features while minimizing the loss of spatial details.

For object detection performance evaluation, the accuracy and recall for different object sizes were compared between the existing YOLO model and the proposed YOLO model, using mAP50 and mAP50-95. The proposed model outperformed the small YOLOv12n model. In addition, the Inference time and accuracy for different object sizes were compared between the existing YOLO model and the proposed YOLO model. The Inference time of the proposed model was relatively low. However, the accuracy remained high. As accuracy and Inference time are in trade-off, the proposed model can be used when a high-accuracy model is required. As part of the subsequent performance evaluation, the Swin Transformer and the proposed model were evaluated by replacing the YOLO model’s backbone. Based on the evaluation results, the proposed Pyramid Vision Transformer-based YOLO model achieved excellent performance. As part of the next performance evaluation, the accuracy and recall for each object size were compared across various datasets using the proposed data augmentation technique. Based on the evaluation results, the proposed model’s performance was rated excellent, as it achieved generalized performance without separate cross-dataset validation35. As the final evaluation of object detection performance, the proposed model was compared with other transformer-based object detection models (DETR, Conditional DETR, Deformable DETR, Efficient DETR and DINO). Based on the comparison results, the proposed model was evaluated as similar to Conditional DETR, Deformable DETR, Efficient DETR or as exhibiting better performance in small-object detection. Through this process, it was confirmed that the proposed model can produce results comparable to those of DETR-series models that achieved the best performance.
