Left Bundle Branch Area Pacing After Cone Repair for Ebstein's Anomaly
Authors: Mayank Yadav, Rahul Choudhary, Surender Deora, Atul Kaushik, Madhusudan Katti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEbstein's anomaly is a rare congenital heart disease characterized by apical displacement of the tricuspid valve and a small functional right ventricle. Surgical repair, particularly cone reconstruction, may be complicated by atrioventricular conduction disturbances requiring permanent pacing.Case SummaryA 47-year-old woman with Ebstein's anomaly underwent cone repair and developed complete heart block postoperatively. Because of her distorted right-sided anatomy and concerns regarding long-term dys-synchrony with right ventricular endocardial pacing, left bundle branch area pacing was performed. Despite technical challenges, successful lead implantation was achieved, resulting in narrow QRS complexes and stable pacing parameters with preserved tricuspid valve function at follow-up.DiscussionConduction system pacing provides a physiological alternative to conventional right ventricular pacing in patients with complex congenital heart disease.Take-Home MessageLeft bundle branch area pacing is feasible after cone repair and may be considered in selected patients with Ebstein's anomaly.Key words: cardiac pacemaker, congenital heart defect, Ebstein’s anomaly, valve repair

History of Presentation

A 47-year-old woman presented with episodes of palpitations and progressive exertional dyspnea over a 6-month period. Cardiovascular examination revealed splitting of the first and second heart sounds, an S3 gallop, and a holosystolic murmur best heard at the left parasternal area.

Differential Diagnosis

In a middle-aged woman presenting with palpitations, dyspnea, and systolic murmur, differential diagnoses include congenital tricuspid valve abnormalities such as Ebstein’s anomaly, primary tricuspid regurgitation, and functional tricuspid regurgitation secondary to right ventricular (RV) or right atrial dilation. Pulmonary hypertension-related right heart disease and intracardiac shunts such as atrial septal defect may present with dyspnea and right-sided volume overload.

Past Medical History

She had no comorbidities or significant past medical history.

Investigation

The electrocardiogram (ECG) showed normal sinus rhythm with a prolonged PR interval (Figure 1). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed apical displacement of the septal and posterior tricuspid valve leaflets, a markedly dilated right atrium, a small but normally functioning right ventricle, and severe low-pressure tricuspid regurgitation with preserved left ventricular systolic function (Figure 2). A diagnosis of Ebstein's anomaly with severe tricuspid regurgitation was established.

Management

After a multidisciplinary heart team discussion, the patient underwent cone repair with right atrial plication and tricuspid ring annuloplasty (Figure 3). Postoperatively, the patient developed complete heart block and became fully pacing-dependent on temporary epicardial pacing placed at the time of surgery (Figure 4). After 7 days without recovery of atrioventricular conduction, permanent pacemaker implantation was planned. Given the patient's young age and anticipated lifelong pacing dependency, a conduction system pacing strategy was selected.

A temporary pacing wire was planned to be positioned in the RV apex via the right femoral venous approach to serve as a fluoroscopic landmark for localization of the left bundle branch area. However, placement was achieved with considerable difficulty because of markedly altered right-sided anatomy, and we anticipated similar challenges during advancement of the C315 delivery sheath (Medtronic).

Two separate left extrathoracic axillary vein punctures were performed, and venous access was obtained under venographic guidance. A Medtronic C315 His fixed-curve sheath was introduced through a 7-F peel-away sheath over a 0.035-inch hydrophilic guidewire. Entry into the right ventricle proved technically challenging because of post cone repair–related distortion of tricuspid valve anatomy, resulting in recurrent slippage of the sheath–wire assembly into a markedly dilated coronary sinus and inability to advance the sheath over the wire, even after deep positioning of the guidewire into the right pulmonary artery. After multiple attempts, the sheath was successfully advanced using clockwise rotational torque combined with push–pull maneuvers.

Using the temporary RV pacing wire as a fluoroscopic landmark, the sheath position was adjusted in the right anterior oblique 30°projection and positioned at the basal RV septum (Figure 5A). A Medtronic 3830 SelectSecure lead (4.1-F, magnetic resonance imaging–compatible) was advanced through the C315 sheath. Unipolar pacing at this site demonstrated a notched W pattern in lead V1, discordant aVL and aVR, and a dominant R wave in lead II (Figure 6A); the lead was then progressively screwed into the interventricular septum under left anterior oblique 30°fluoroscopic guidance (Figure 5B).

Twelve-Lead Electrocardiograms During Lead Deployment

(A) Unipolar pacing demonstrating a notched W pattern in lead V1 with discordant aVL and aVR, consistent with left bundle branch area location. (B) Bipolar pacing after further lead advancement demonstrating a right bundle branch block–like morphology (rsR′, refers to an electrocardiographic QRS morphology characterized by a small initial r wave, followed by an S wave and a terminal R′ wave in lead V1) in lead V1, confirming left bundle branch area capture.

With further advancement, the notched W pattern evolved into a QR pattern in lead V1. The R-wave amplitude was 15 mV, the ventricular pacing threshold at a pulse width of 0.4 ms was 0.5 V, and pacing impedance was 840 Ω. Bipolar pacing demonstrated an rSR′ morphology in lead V1, with a QRS duration of 100 ms and left ventricular activation time of 64 ms measured in lead V5, remaining constant across pacing outputs ranging from 2 to 10 V (Figure 6B). Transition from nonselective to selective capture was demonstrated at 0.8 V during bipolar pacing, confirming left bundle branch area capture. Contrast injection through the sheath confirmed deep septal positioning of the lead, including the anode (Figure 7A). Given the markedly dilated right atrium, particular attention was paid to lead slack management. A generous atrial loop was deliberately created along the lateral right atrial wall to accommodate respiratory excursion and postural changes while avoiding tension across the tricuspid valve repair. The lead trajectory was carefully oriented to prevent impingement on the reconstructed tricuspid leaflets. Stability was confirmed by fluoroscopic assessment during deep inspiration and gentle traction before sheath removal.

An atrial lead (model 5076; Medtronic) was subsequently implanted in the right atrial appendage, and both leads were connected to a dual-chamber pacemaker generator (Azure XT DR MRI; Medtronic) (Figure 7B). Postprocedure ECG demonstrated an atrial-sensed, ventricular-paced rhythm with a narrow QRS complex and an rsR′ pattern in lead V1 (Figure 8). Postprocedure echocardiography confirmed deep septal positioning of the 3830 lead with left ventricular endocardial tenting, preservation of the tricuspid valve cone repair, and absence of lead-induced tricuspid regurgitation (Figure 9).

At the 7-month follow-up, atrial P-wave and ventricular R-wave amplitudes were 5 mV and 18 mV, respectively, and pacing thresholds remained stable at 0.4 V and 0.70 V at a pulse width of 0.4 ms, respectively. The patient remained clinically asymptomatic without palpitations or exertional dyspnea. Also at the 7-month follow-up, pacing morphology and QRS duration remained unchanged compared with postprocedure ECG, with stable capture thresholds and no evidence of lead-related tricuspid valve dysfunction (Figure 10). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved integrity of the tricuspid valve cone repair with no tricuspid regurgitation or stenosis, and the pacing lead remained in a stable deep septal position without interaction with the reconstructed valve apparatus.

Discussion

Approximately 4% of patients with Ebstein's anomaly develop complete heart block,1 and this risk is further increased after surgical correction or catheter ablation procedures.2 Permanent pacing in patients with Ebstein's anomaly, particularly after surgical intervention such as cone repair or tricuspid valve replacement, is technically challenging because of altered right heart and tricuspid valve anatomy.3,4 Multiple alternative pacing strategies, including transvenous, epicardial, and coronary sinus pacing, have been used in such patients.5,6 Conventional RV endocardial pacing causes electrical desynchrony and increases the risk of pacing-induced cardiomyopathy in patients requiring long term pacing. Conduction system pacing activates the native conduction system, resulting in synchronized contraction of both ventricles, and represents a better pacing strategy7,8 compared with conventional endocardial pacing. His bundle pacing, although physiological, is technically challenging and associated with concerns regarding lead stability.9 Left bundle branch pacing is form of conduction system pacing technically less challenging compared with His bundle pacing and provides stable low thresholds and deep septal anchoring.7

This case demonstrates the technical feasibility of left bundle branch area pacing in a patient with surgically altered tricuspid valve anatomy after cone repair. Although physiological pacing offers theoretical advantages, conclusions regarding long-term safety and durability cannot be drawn from a single case. Nevertheless, stable electrical parameters and absence of lead-related complications at the 7-month follow-up support short-term feasibility in carefully selected patients.

Larger studies with longer follow-up are required to determine the reproducibility, safety, and clinical benefit of conduction system pacing in patients with Ebstein’s anomaly after surgical repair. To the best of our knowledge, this represents one of the first reported cases of successful left bundle branch pacing after cone repair for Ebstein's anomaly and further supports the expanding role of conduction system pacing in complex congenital heart disease.

Conclusions

Left bundle branch area pacing was technically feasible and associated with stable pacing parameters and preserved tricuspid valve function at midterm follow-up in this patient with Ebstein's anomaly after cone repair. This approach may represent a potential pacing option in selected patients with surgically altered right heart anatomy, although larger studies with longer follow-up are needed to confirm safety and durability.
