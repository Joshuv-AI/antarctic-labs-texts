DFT study of irradiation damage-defect correlations with mechanical properties in uranium nitride
Authors: Hengfeng Gong, Yi Wang, Rongkun Yang, Jiwei Wang, Mingzhou Chen, Lixiang Wu, Jiaxiang Xue, Haixiang Hu, Guoliang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Uranium mononitride (UN) is a promising advanced nuclear fuel featuring high inherent uranium density and excellent thermal conductivity. Although density functional theory (DFT) has been extensively used to explore the fundamental properties of UN, systematic and comprehensive studies on the thermomechanical performances of irradiation-defective UN remain limited. Formation energies of typical point defects in UN are strongly dependent on local chemical environments, with Schottky defects exhibiting distinct energetic characteristics under U-rich, N-rich, and stoichiometric conditions. The introduction of these defects systematically reduces the elastic moduli of UN, while both Poisson’s ratio (σ) and Pugh’s ratio (κ) increase accordingly. Vickers hardness (HV) gradually declines with defect incorporation, as verified by empirical formula-based fracture toughness (KIC) assessments. The elastic anisotropy of defective UN is quantitatively evaluated via anisotropy factors and intuitively visualized using three-dimensional (3D) contour plots. This work further systematically calculates the melting point (Tm) and minimum thermal conductivity (κmin) of defective UN. Thermodynamically, defective UN is less stable than pristine UN at low temperatures, yet becomes stabilized at high temperatures owing to its reduced Gibbs free energy (g). The linear thermal expansion coefficient (CTE, αL) increases sharply with rising temperature and eventually plateaus at a stable value. This atomic-scale insight into radiation-induced defect mechanisms and their modulation effects on thermomechanical and thermal performances is essential for addressing key technical bottlenecks and advancing the development of next-generation nuclear reactor fuels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56593-4.

Introduction

Over the past five decades, traditional uranium dioxide (UO2) has been extensively employed in light water reactors (LWRs) owing to its high melting point, satisfactory compatibility with cladding materials, and exceptional radiation tolerance. Nevertheless, its inferior thermal conductivity leads to an escalating temperature gradient from the core to rim of the pellet, significantly impacting mechanical properties and fission product migration behaviors. Consequently, it is imperative to develop innovative nuclear fuel materials for enhancing nuclear reactor safety and economy. Uranium nitride (UN) stands out as a highly promising candidate for advanced nuclear fuels in various cutting-edge applications, including LWRs, liquid-metal-cooled Generation IV fast reactors, and high-temperature gas-cooled reactors1–4. This material exhibits exceptional thermal-mechanical properties, such as a melting temperature of approximately 2726.85 ℃5,6, a uranium density of 13.5 g/cm3, and a thermal conductivity of 25 W/(m·K) at 1600 K)7,8 values that significantly surpass those of traditional UO2 fuel (melting point: 2800 ± 15 ℃; thermal conductivity: 1.2 ~ 1.5 W/(m·K) at 1000 K and a uranium density of 9.6 g/cm3)9,10. By virtue of these superior attributes, UN emerges as a compelling candidate for next-generation nuclear fuels. Notwithstanding the widespread deployment of UO2 pellets in contemporary nuclear reactors, a critical gap persists in the experimental database concerning the in-reactor behavior of UN fuel. To establish a comprehensive understanding of its intrinsic properties prior to commercialization, systematic investigations under neutron irradiation are imperative, focusing on: (1) mechanical integrity and irradiation hardness; (2) high-temperature thermal stability and phase evolution; (3) irradiation-induced swelling kinetics; and (4) fission gas release behavior.

UN pellets are typically synthesized via conventional powder metallurgy techniques, including pressureless sintering, hot pressing, and - more recently - spark plasma sintering (SPS, also denoted as field-assisted sintering [FAST])11–13. Lian et al.11. have performed the synthesizing UN-U3Si2 composites by SPS and the micro-cracks can be significantly mitigated by controlling the cooling. Dense UN pellets with controlled microstructures and tailored grain size from large-grained to a few microns are synthesized by SPS combined with high energy ball milling12. Lin et al.13. have recently reported that highly densified UN pellets were successfully achieved by SPS at 1600 ℃ with a theoretical density (TD) above 96%. Notable studies by Claisse et al.14 have highlighted the critical influence of fuel porosity on in-reactor behavior, specifically regarding fission gas release kinetics and irradiation-induced swelling mechanisms. During SPS processing of UN, a discernible correlation between open- and closed- pore fractions has been experimentally validated15. Despite growing interest in UN-based fuels, systematic studies on their irradiation response remain scarce1,15–19, with a particular paucity of in-depth microstructural characterization of irradiation-induced defects. Ion irradiation experiments using Kr and Xe ions have revealed that initial damage in UN manifests as nanoscale defect clusters; these clusters begin to dissociate above 727 ℃ (~ 1000 K), evolving into extended defects such as dislocation loops and line defects20. Under actual reactor service conditions, high temperature, intense neutron irradiation and thermal cycling inevitably induce abundant point defects, vacancy clusters and antisite defects in UN fuel21. These irradiation-derived defects substantially modify the structural stability, elastic mechanical response, thermal conductivity and thermal expansion characteristics of UN, and further degrade the comprehensive fuel performance and long-term in-reactor service reliability22,23. Accordingly, systematic exploration of the formation mechanism of intrinsic and irradiation-related point defects, together with their temperature-dependent thermoelastic and thermal evolution behaviors, is indispensable for revealing the microstructual evolution of UN fuel and assessing its engineering feasibility in advanced reactor systems24.

Complementary to experimental efforts, significant theoretical progress has been made in elucidating atomic - and electronic - scale properties of UN. Atta-Fynn, Ray et al.25 employed GGA + U calculations to probe the thermodynamic and magnetic properties behavior of UN while Kotomin et al.20. conducted first-principles calculations of defect formation energies - including Schottky defects and nitrogen Frenkel pairs - using density functional theory (DFT). Kuksin et al.26. revealed through ab initio calculations that U anti-site defects dominate the defect chemistry in U-rich UN1−x, whereas N-rich UN1+x exhibits high - concentration of U vacancies, establishing that both U vacancy and anti-site defects significantly enhance uranium self-diffusion kinetics. Notwithstanding these advancements in point defect thermodynamics and migration mechanisms, systematic investigations into how irradiation-induced point defects influence mechanical and thermodynamic properties remain notably scarce. Current studies have yet to establish a quantitative correlation between defect configurations and macroscopic property degradation, representing a critical knowledge gap for predictive modeling of UN fuel performance.

Methodology

All calculations were performed within the framework of DFT, and the PBEsol functional combined with the Hubbard + U correction (PBEsol + U) was employed as the core computational scheme as implemented in the MedeA™ & Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP)27–31. Scalar-relativistic PAW pseudopotentials were utilized to describe the core electrons of U (6s26p66d25f27s2) and N (2s22p3) atoms, with 14 and 5 valence electrons, respectively. To account for electron-correlation effects originating from the localized U 5f orbitals in the UN lattice, we used the DFT + U method and Ueff = 1.9 eV (+ U = 2.0 eV, and J = 0.1 eV), which was chosen to match previous DFT + U studies32–34. The magnetic ordering configurations of uranium (U) ions were systematically investigated for UN, including antiferromagnetic (AFM), ferromagnetic (FM), and non-magnetic (NM) orderings, with detailed implementation settings as follows: for the AFM ordering, the localized spins on U ions were modeled with a layer alignment, which is consistent with the experimental configuration of UN observed below 53 K35,36. For the FM ordering, the localized spins on all U ions were set to be parallel-aligned, maintaining the same spin direction to simulate the ferromagnetic coupling effect. For the non-magnetic (NM) ordering, spin polarization was completely turned off in the calculations, and the electron spin degree of freedom were ignored to simulate the non-magnetic state of the system. A 5 × 5 × 5 Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh37 was employed for the sampling of the Brillouin zone, and an energy cutoff of 500 eV was set for the plane-wave basis set. Point defects simulations employed supercells constructed by expanding the unit cell translation vectors: 2 × 2 × 2 and 4 × 4 × 2 for full structure optimization, corresponding to supercells containing 64 and 216 atoms, respectively. The convergence criteria were strictly defined as follows: the total energy convergence threshold was set to 10− 6 eV, and the atomic force convergence threshold was set to 10− 3 eV/Å, which effectively guaranteed the reliability of the simulation results. To investigate the stable structural configurations of UN, five distinct exchange-correlation functionals were employed for comparative analysis to validate the robustness of the calculated results. These functionals encompass the Local Density Approximation (LDA)38; conventional Generalized Gradient Approximation (GGA) variants including the Perdew-Berke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional39, the recently proposed PBEsol functional40, and the revised Perdew-Berke-Ernzerhof (rPBE) functional41; as well as the Armiento-Mattsson 2005 (AM05) functional, which is well-suited for a wide range of crystalline solid systems42. To enhance both computational efficiency and numerical accuracy, spin-orbit coupling and non-collinear magnetic effects were excluded from all simulations.

Results

Properties of uranium nitride bulk crystal

Stoichiometric uranium nitride crystallizes in a face-centered cubic (fcc) rock-salt structure (space group Fm3m), analogous to NaCl. In this lattice configuration, uranium atoms occupy fcc lattice sites, with nitrogen atoms residing in octahedral interstices. For defect simulations, a 2 × 2 × 2 supercell (64 atoms: 32 U and 32 N) with dimensions 9.6 × 9.6 × 9.6 Å³ was used to minimize finite-size effects while ensuring computational efficiency. Crystallographic representations of the UN rock-salt structure, including unit and supercell configurations, are shown in Fig. 1.

We first compared the lattice parameters (Table 1) of structures relaxed using LDA, PBE, PBEsol, rPBE and AM05 functionals. Notably, the structures remained cubic even when FM or FM + U methods were employed for configuration optimization: the PBEsol + FM and PBEsol + FM+U schemes yielded lattice parameters of 4.852 Å and 4.854 Å, respectively. Under the PBE functional, the lattice constants are overestimated with FM, FM + U, or standalone + U treatments, yielding values of 4.928 Å, 4.911 Å and 4.912 Å, respectively. All these values are larger than the experimental data (4.88 Å)9,13,43,44 and previously reported theoretical values (4.86 for FM and 4.904 for FM + U)45. Similarly, the lattice constants calculated using the rPBE functional, for example, obtained 5.012 Å (rPBE + FM), 4.938 Å (rPBE + FM+U), and 4.938 Å (rPBE + U) were also larger than both the experimental data9,13,43,44 and theoretical values45. In contrast, the LDA and AM05 functionals underestimated the lattice constants when compared to the same experimental data9,13,43,44 and theoretical values45. For non-magnetic (NM) simulations, the benchmarked lattice constants for each functional are summarized as follows: LDA (4.785 Å), PBE (4.850 Å), PBEsol (4.803 Å), rPBE (4.887 Å), and AM05 (4.794 Å). Among these functionals, rPBE functional yielded a lattice constant consistent with experimental data9,13,43,44, while all other functionals (LDA, PBE, PBEsol, and AM05) underestimated the lattice constants. These NM models were prioritized to reflect room-temperature operational conditions while avoiding spurious symmetry-breaking effects, with all configurations maintaining cubic symmetry. When Hubbard + U correlations were applied, the lattice constants of UN (Table 1) shifted to the following values for each functional: LDA (4.835 Å), PBE (4.912 Å), PBEsol (4.854 Å), rPBE (4.938 Å), and AM05 (4.845 Å). Compared with the experimental lattice constant of UN (~ 4.88 Å)9,13,43,44, the LDA, PBEsol, and AM05 functionals underestimated the lattice constants, whereas the PBE and rPBE functionals overestimated this parameter. In contrast, when antiferromagnetic (AFM) or AFM + U methods were used for configuration optimization, all final geometric configurations transformed from cubic to tetragonal. This observation is consistent with the findings of Claisse et al.46., who reported that FM UN maintains a fully cubic structure, while AFM UN is characterized by a tetragonal distortion. Furthermore, Kocevski et al.34. demonstrated that UN exhibits imaginary phonon frequencies when the AFM + U scheme is employed, indicating a dynamically unstable crystal structure. To ensure high computational accuracy while reducing total calculation time, we selected the PBEsol + U functional to perform calculations of the lattice constants and point defect formation energies for UN, with no consideration of magnetism.

The point defects in UN crystal structures

The point defects and formation energies

We performed systematic first-principles calculations to determine formation energies of intrinsic point defects in UN, focusing on five representative defect categories: (i) Frenkel pairs (FPU: U Frenkel pair; FPN: N Frenkel pair); (ii) Schottky defects (Scho.U: U Schottky; Scho.N: N Schottky); (iii) Antisite defects (UN: U on N site; NU: N on U site); (iv) Di-vacancies (VU+VU: double U vacancy; VN+VN: double N vacancy; VU+VN: U-N divacancy) and (v) Bound Schottky defects (2VU+VN: two U vacancies and one N vacancy; VU+2VN: one U vacancy and two N vacancies). Using the optimized UN unit cells as a baseline, we constructed a 2 × 2 × 2 supercell (64 atoms) with periodic boundary conditions for defect simulations. As Di-vacancies and Bound Schottky defects are considered, the 4 × 4 × 2 supercell (256 atoms) is built to avoid the interaction between nearing defects. Figure 2 provides schematic illustrations of these defects alongside their notation conventions. Frenkel pair configurations involve displacing a lattice atom to an interstitial site (FPU: U atom migration; FPN: N atom migration). Monovacancies are modeled by removing single atom from lattice sites, denoted as Schottky defects (Scho.U: U removal; Scho.N: N removal). Anti-site defects are created via atomic substitution (UN: U occupying N site; NU: N occupying U site). Di-vacancies and bound Schottky defects are constructed by coordinately removing multiple atoms (including U and N atoms). Specifically, an inter-defect spacing greater than 10 Å is adopted to avoid direct interactions between di-vacancies defects, while a spacing smaller than 6 Å is used to simulate vacancy clusters, as illustrated in Fig. 2 (g) and Fig. 2 (h).

The elastic property of UN with point defects

Figure 3 illustrates the elastic moduli of UN calculated using diverse exchange correlation functionals, incorporating U 5f electron correlations and magnetic ordering effects. Comparative analysis reveals that calculations employing the PBEsol funtional exhibit remarkable consistency with experimental data and previous theoretical findings. In stark contrast, bulk moduli show significant overestimation when using PBEsol + U, PBEsol + FM+U and PBEsol + AFM+U methods. For shear moduli, moderate underestimation is observed in PBEsol + U and PBEsol + FM+U calculations, whereas PBEsol + AFM+U leads to overestimated shear moduli. The elastic moduli are obtained using PBEsol functional which are excellently agreement with experimental26 and previous theoretical data54. Given these systematic discrepancies, the PBEsol functional was therefore selected as the optimal approach for calculating the elastic moduli and associated thermochemical properties in the present work.

The bulk modulus characterizes a material’s resistance to fracture, while the shear modulus quantifies its opposition to plastic deformation. Elastic constants of crystals serve as a bridge between mechanical behaviors and dynamic properties. Based on the elastic stiffness constants of defected UN, the derived B, G, E, σ and κ are presented in Fig. 4. Notably, Schottky defects (Scho.U and Scho.N) and anti-site defect (NU) exhibit pronounced effects on the elastic moduli. For instance, a perfect UN bulk shows B = 247 GPa, G = 73 GPa, and E = 200 GPa. However, introducing Scho.U and Scho.N defects reduce B to 243 GPa and 238 GPa, diminish G to 50 GPa and 49 GPa, and decreases E to 141 GPa and 137 GPa, respectively. Additionally, for Scho. U, Scho. N, and NU point defects, σ values are 0.40, 0.40, 0.39, and κ values are 4.77, 4.48, 4.33 - all higher than the pristine UN bulk (σ = 0.36, κ = 3.37). As shown in Fig. 4, aside from Schottky and anti-site defects, other point defects exert negligible effects on elastic moduli, Poisson’s ratio and Pugh’s ratio.

Elastic anisotropy is a fundamental physical property with dual significance in materials science - researchers leverage it to tailor enhanced performance while often seeking to mitigate its adverse effects. When investigating material mechanical properties, microcrack initiation and lattice distortion are critical considerations, with elastic anisotropy serving as a primary driving force for these phenomena. Thus, studying elastic anisotropy is pivotal for improving material mechanical durability. Discussion

The equilibrium lattice parameter of pristine UN at 0 K is a₀= 4.8 Å for the primitive unit cell (2 atoms), in good agreement with neutron diffraction measurements86–89. The results show that the lattice parameters are good with the experimental data9,44,45 and prior theoretical values13,46 using the PBEsol + U functional. While previous studies have suggested that AFM ordering calculations without or with + U correlations might induce structural transformation from cubic to tetrahedral phases90,91 the present work aims to offer a comprehensive comparison of exchange-correlation effects including AFM, FM and NM functionals with + U values or without + Uvalues. Goncharov et al90. experimentally validated local atomic structure distortions in UN via extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS), while Zhang et al91. reported a slight cubic to tetragonal phase transition (c/a = 0.978) under AFM ordering. Motivated by these findings, we excluded FM/AFM ordering from all calculations involving perfect or defected UN systems, ensuring focus on defect physics independent of magnetic effects.

The calculated elastic constants and moduli show excellent agreement with prior theoretical benchmarks20,26,53,92, though they exhibit slight discrepancies with experimental6,9,45,54, confirming methodological consistency. The Pugh ratio (κ = B/G = 3.18) substantially exceeds the ductility threshold of 1.7551, unequivocally designating UN as an intrinsically ductile material. This mechanical characteristic - exceptional among ceramic nuclear fuels-implies that UN can significantly improve resistance to brittle fracture under thermal cycling conditions, a critical advantage for advanced reactor fuel applications. The calculated melting point is overestimated stemming from two critical factors: (i) calculations assume defect-free single crystal systems, where grain boundaries and impurities in real materials inherently lower \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}_{\mathrm{m}}$$\end{document}; (ii) the linear correlation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}_{\mathrm{m}}-{C}_{11}$$\end{document}) model neglects temperature-dependent phonon softening and anharmonic effects, which are known to influence phase transition kinetics in ceramics.

Comparative analysis shows that U and N Schottky defects exhibit substantially lower formation energies than other defects, highlighting their thermodynamic preference in UN. The formation energy of Frenkel pairs are great consistent with prior theoretical reports92,93. The formation energies of Schottky defects in UN are smaller indicating that it is easier to be formed during irradiation-induced conditions. For the anti-site defects, N atom prefers to occupy the U position. This disparity arises from the smaller atomic radius of N allows greater space accommodation in the U lattice coordination. The sum of monovacancy (VU) formation energy and monovacancy (VN) is 4.23 eV in U-rich chemical environment which is lower than that of di-vacancy (VU+VN), indicating that di-vacancy is less favorable than monovacancy under irradiation free thermal conditions. Defect formation propensity highlights the pivotal role of chemical environment in defect evolution during irradiation. Wu et al93. reported analogous phenomena in AlNbTiZr refractory high entropy alloys (RHEAs), demonstrating that vacancy formation energy is governed by the atomic configurations of first-nearest-neighbor (1NN) Al/Nb atoms - specially, vacancies exhibit a thermodynamic preference for Al-rich, Nb-depleted microenvironments. The elastic moduli (e.g., B, E and G) decrease in UN defects, which reveals that irradiation-induced defects weaken both fracture resistance and deformation capability. The σ not only describes radial strain under tensile loading but also reflects bonding characteristics: low σ values arise from directional covalent bonds in crystals56. Meanwhile, plastic deformation resistance depends on crystal chemical environment: material with short covalent bonds suppress dislocation activation/mobility, enhancing hardness, hence, covalent materials are generally harder than ionic or metallic ones94,95. For example, typical covalent materials have σ ≈ 0.1, ionic materials σ ≈ 0.3, and diamond σ ≈ 0.0796. By comparison, defect - irradiated UN predominantly exhibits ionic bonding. According to Pugh’s criterion: materials with κ < 1.75 are brittle, otherwise ductile. Notably, irradiation-induced point defects do not alter the κ, based ductility/brittleness characterization. Charatsidou et al.97, further confirmed that post-irradiation nanoindentation testing of UN and (U, Zr) composite fuels shows irradiation-induced hardening alongside a decrease in Young’s modulus relative to unirradiated regions. Based on the seven empirical hardness formulas, the calculated hardness decreases in UN defects. The Scho.U and anti-site defect (NU) exert a more pronounced effect on hardness than other defect types. Khanolkar et al98. reported that diborides exhibit an initial increase in hardness following irradiation with energetic Au ions with a subsequent decrease in hardness following further irradiation. The hardening effects of high-temperature irradiation was much lower than that of room-temperature irradiation due to the recovery of matrix defects99. For the low dose irradiation, the microstrain included by vacancy-type defects like He-vacancy clusters and helium bubbles is slight, which would not result in a large increase of hardness99. Thus, the generation of irradiation-induced defects to some extent diminishes the hardness of UN. Additionally, the calculated KIC values present a clear trend: neutron irradiation induces a decline in fracture toughness, primarily attributed to irradiation embrittlement. The mechanism involves reduced ductility and enhanced susceptibility to stress-induced fracture. This reduction in KIC is a critical consideration for the structural design and safety assessment of nuclear power plant components, as increasing neutron dose exacerbates embrittlement, leading to a sharp drop in fracture toughness during irradiation. The calculated melting point (Tm) clearly demonstrates that irradiation-induced defects cause a significant reduction in Tmof UN. Gao et al100., have pointed out that interstitial hydrogen impurities can induce lower melting point and formation of radiation defect-hydrogen complexes can also induce a lower melting point. As neutron irradiation dose accumulates, the melting point decreases systematically, attributed to neutron-induced atomic displacement from lattice sites།this leads to material degradation and altered mechanical properties. Specifically, higher neutron doses exacerbate irradiation embrittlement (e.g., reduced fracture toughness), which correlates with the observed Tm decline101. Moreover, the minimum thermal conductivity decrease based on Clark’s and Cahill’s models, respectively, in UN defects. This conclusion aligns with Yang et al13., who used Frequency Domain Thermal Reflection (FDTR) technique matched the data from Laser Flash Analyzer (LFA) to show that irradiated UN exhibit a 20% thermal conductivity reduction to unirradiated samples. The primary mechanisms include dislocation loop formation and Ar bubbles precipitation, which enhance phonon scattering and thereby suppress heat transport.

The results are presented for the Gibbs free energy (g), which align with literature: Mattucci et al102. observed temperature-dependent defect stability. Lower irradiation temperatures (100 ℃) produce similar defect structures (Frank Loops, Prefect Loops and SFTs) without the ability to thermally stabilize voids. Additionally, the higher irradiation temperatures (360 ℃) results in larger irradiation induced hardening. Ma et al103. reported void density peaks in tungsten at 800–1000 K. The increase in temperature induces an initial increase in number of density of voids, and then followed by subsequent decreases, corresponding to the peak density at 800–1000 K in tungsten. The first annealing stage is found to occur between 523 K and 573 K in tungsten104. Migration of monovacancies leads to their disappearing, essentially by the formation of larger vacancy like defects, and the resulting defects depth profile is drastically altered in tungsten104. Cheng et al105. emphasized that three different methods to estimate Gibbs free energy-namely, a minimum-potential energy calculation, a harmonic free-energy estimate and the fully anharmonic. They point out that the thermodynamic integration method gives access to the full Gibbs free energy within the statistical errors, and can be considered as the ground truth. Although vacancy formation energy depends on the temperature, the vacancy diffusion does not change the values of enthalpy change at constant pressure and temperature - marked as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{<\delta\:H>}_{P,T}$$\end{document} at high temperature, compared with the case as the vacancy is fixed at one site, due to the translational symmetry of the lattice106. As above mentioned in Sect. 3.2, the di-vacancy and bound-vacancy defects are more difficult to be formed than monovacancy at 0 K, but as the they are generated at high temperature, thermal stability is profound. Furthermore, the CTE decreases slightly in UN defects. Comparing the calculated CTE from 0 K to 3000 K with experimental and literature potentials, it is evident that our calculations are reliable. Hossain et al106. observed that in 4 H-SiC, vacancy defects exhibit negative CTE above 9% defect density (independent of temperature), interstitial defects maintain positive CTE regardless of the defect density, and Frenkel pairs transition from positive to negative CTE at densities > 8%. This work reveals that increasing vacancy defect concentrations slightly decrease CTE in UN, while temperature exerts negligible effects on CTE for systems containing Frenkel pairs, Schottky defects, and anti-site defects. These findings underscore the critical role of irradiation-induced defects in modulating thermal expansion, with direct implications for UN applications in nuclear irradiation environments. Nevertheless, several limitations still exist in this work. First, the current DFT simulations focus on static lattice calculations without considering dynamic lattice vibration effects and high-temperature irradiation cascade behaviors. Second, the present theoretical predictions are not quantitatively compared with in-situ high-temperature irradiation experimental data due to the current scarcity of systematic experimental measurements for defective UN. Subsequent studies will incorporate temperature-dependent dynamic simulations and multi-defect coupling models to further enrich the understanding of radiation damage behavior of UN nuclear fuel.

Conclusions

Density functional theory (DFT) was utilized to elucidate the influence of typical point defects on the mechanical behavior of uranium nitride (UN). Comparative analysis of lattice parameters calculated via various functionals (LDA, PBE, PBEsol, rPBE, AM05) under different magnetic configurations revealed that the PBEsol + U functional was selected for subsequent calculations of formation energies of point defects due to its balance of computational accuracy and efficiency. Considering the stoichiometric constraint-dictated chemical potential ranges of U and N (U-rich, N-rich, near-stoichiometric), defect formation energy analyses revealed Scho.N as the most thermodynamically favorable defect in U-rich and near-stoichiometric environments. Scho.U is preferred under N-rich conditions, while Scho.N tends to form in a U-rich chemical environment. Notably, the bound Schottky defect (2VU+VN) is the most unfavorable across all tested environments. The PBEsol functional was adopted as optimal for elastic modulus calculations. Based on defected UN’s elastic stiffness constants, Schottky defects (Scho.U and Scho.N) and anti-site defect (NU) exert the most pronounced negative effects on elastic moduli (B, G, E). All point defects decrease Vickers hardness (HV) and fracture toughness (KIC) of UN. Additionally, point defects enhance the elastic anisotropy of UN. The minimum thermal conductivity (κmin) of defected UN decreased consistently with the introduction of irradiation-induced defects. Gibbs free energy (g) analysis of defect UN reveals two distinct temperature zones: below 200 K, defects are thermodynamically unstable relative to pristine UN, while they stabilize at 200–3000 K. The coefficient of linear thermal expansion (CTE, αL) of UN increases dramatically below 250 K and stabilizes near a constant value up to 3000 K. This work demonstrates that a microscopic DFT-based approach accelerates understanding of irradiation-induced damage mechanisms in UN fuel, particularly when experimental data are scare.
