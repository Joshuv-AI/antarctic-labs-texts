Antioxidant and antiarthritic potential of berberine: In vitro and in vivo studies
Authors: Shweta Jain, Shalini Tripathi, Pushpendra Kumar Tripathi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo extract and isolate berberine from Berberis aristata (Berberidaceae). Isolated berberine was characterised using spectroscopy and its antioxidant and antiarthritic activity was analyzed.MethodsThe berberine was isolated from B. aristata using microwave-assisted extraction (MAE) and characterised by a spectroscopic technique. The isolated berberine was evaluated for its antioxidant activity in DPPH, nitric oxide, and superoxide scavenging assays, while antiarthritic activity was evaluated in the complete freund’s adjuvant (CFA)-induced arthritis rat model.ResultsThe antioxidant activity of berberine revealed potent antioxidant activity in DPPH, nitric oxide, and superoxide scavenging assays. The in vivo antiarthritic activity of berberine in the CFA-induced arthritis rat model showed a significant reduction in paw diameter, arthritic score, and an increase in body weight. Furthermore, a concentration-dependent ameliorating action of berberine on haematological parameters was noticed. Proinflammatory biomarkers, including IL-6, IL-10, and TGF-b in serum were reported, and histopathology examination revealed that berberine decreased pannus formation, synovial hyperplasia, and bone erosion. Radiographic investigation showed soft tissue inflammation, bone resorption and erosion, joint gap reduction, and substantial connective tissue expansion after treatment with berberine.ConclusionThe ameliorating action on haematological parameters and proinflammatory biomarkers of berberine makes them a suitable remedy for the treatment of arthritis.

Introduction

Arthritis is the most common disease globally, and around 350 million cases have been reported. Recently, every one out of four adults in the USA has been diagnosed with arthritis with severe joint pain (Scheel et al., 2006). Arthritis has been resulted from cartilage damage which protects the joints. Arthritis produces hyperplasia of synovial cells in addition to an inflammatory riposte. Subsequently, additional deposition of synovial fluid in the joints produces inflammation at joint sites. Articular cartilage damage and alkalosis of the joints have been observed in the pathology of the disease process. Ankylosing spondylitis, rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, septic arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, reactive arthritis, and gout are varieties of arthritis (Jain, Vaidya, Gupta, Rosenholm, & Bansal, 2021, Swathi, Jayaram, Sugumar, & Rymbai, 2021).

As yet little was known of the causes of the disease. However, increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines, metabolising enzymes, and decreased levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines have been reported. Furthermore, nuclear factor (NF)-κB, which affects numerous immune responses of cell proliferation, has also seen an upsurge in rheumatic arthritis (RA). Free radicals are imperative in the evolution of disease (Afonso et al., 2007, Miller et al., 2010).

Herbal products have gained popularity in traditional medicine for the last decade. Numerous plants and plant-based products have been reported for their medicinal values. The unique diversity of chemical compositions and biological activities of herbal products allow them to be used in an assortment of diseases. Nowadays, herbal products have become an imperative resource for discovering new lead compounds for treating human diseases. Numerous plant isolates possess antiarthritic activities, categorised into alkaloids, glycosides, terpenoids, flavonoids, etc. These isolated compounds showed anti-inflammatory mediated antiarthritic properties and, either used solely or in their extracts, have been used for arthritis treatment (Jain et al., 2014).

Berberine is a cationic alkaloid, first isolated in 1917 from Goldenseal (Hydrastis canadensis L.), of the buttercup family. Berberine is isolated from numerous plant families and genera, including Annonaceae (Annickia, Rollinia, and Xylopia), Berberidaceae (Berberis, Caulophyllum, Mahonia, and Nandina), Menispermaceae (Tinospora), Papaveraceae (Argemone, Corydalis, Eschscholzia, Hunnemannia, Papaver, and Sanguinaria), Ranunculaceae (Coptis, and Xanthorhiza), and Rutaceae (Evodia, and Zanthoxyllum). The genus Berberis is well-known as the most widely distributed natural source of berberine (Arayne, Sultana, & Bahadur, 2007). Berberine is a prominent constituent of a number of medicinal plants that have potential pharmacological activities and are used in a variety of therapeutic applications (Chen et al., 2022, Kamal, Singh, Tamboli, Parveen, & Ahmad, 2011, Potdar, Hirwani, & Dhulap, 2012). Neag et al. (2018) summarized the botanical occurrence, traditional uses, extraction methods, and pharmacological effects of berberine and berberine-containing plants. Semwal, Shah, Chauhan, Badhe, & Divakar, 2007 reported the antidiabetic activity of the stem bark of Berberis aristata DC in alloxan-induced diabetic rats. An in vivo result showed that berberine successfully suppresses inflammatory responses (Joshi, Shirkhedkar, Prakash, & Maheshwari, 2011).

The aim of the present study is to extract and isolate berberine from B. aristata (Berberidaceae). The isolated berberine was characterised by spectroscopic techniques and its antioxidant and antiarthritic activity was evaluated.

Materials and methods

Plant collection

Fresh roots of the plant B. aristata were collected from the local area of Malera, District Almora (Uttarakhand), India. Identification and confirmation were done by Department of Botany, Dr. H.S. Gour, Central University, Sagar (M. P.) India, where voucher specimens were deposited with the herbarium No. Bot./H/112/196119.

Chemicals and reagents

All the chemicals for study, including ethanol, methanol, 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), acetonitrile, ascorbic acid, Griess reagent, sodium nitroprusside, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), nitro blue tetrazolium chloride (NBT), paraformaldehyde, phosphate buffer pH 7.4, and complete freund’s adjuvant (CFA), were purchased from commercial suppliers (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). All HPLC solvents used were of analytical or HPLC grade.

Physicochemical and phytochemical analysis

The moisture content, water-soluble extractive, alcohol-soluble extractive, total ash, acid-insoluble ash, and water-soluble ash values were determined according to the procedure reported by Iqbal, Salim, & Lim, 2015. In the B. aristata root extract, the presence of alkaloids, glycosides, flavonoids, tannins, phenols, fats, and carbohydrates were determined using preliminary phytochemical screening methods reported by Namdeo (2021).

Plant extraction

Extraction of B. aristata roots was done using microwave-assisted extraction (MAE), or simply the microwave extraction technique, which has numerous advantages over the traditional extraction method. For the extraction of B. aristata roots, Belwal, Pandey, Bhatt, & Rawal, 2020 used a Multiwave-3000 microwave reaction system, which included the Plackett-Burman design (PBD). Approximately 2 g of the powdered B. aristata roots was dissolved in 100 mL of 70% ethanol at pH 6.0 and placed inside the extracting chamber of the microwave reaction system (IFB-China, 23BC4). A microwave power of 300 W for 5 min was applied, filtered the extract and stored it at − 20 °C in a dark place. In the extract, berberine was measured via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Younglin, Acme 9000) equipped with a C18 reverse phase column, a binary pump, and a diode-array detection (DAD-MZOA) unit. The sample was run through an HPLC system equipped with a semi-prep gradient pump and controlled by Autochro-3000 software. A dilute stock solution of extract was prepared by dissolving 1 mg of dry extract in 1 mL of ethanol. A total of 10 µL of stock extract solution was injected and run the mobile phase, i.e., a mixture of 50:50 of acetonitrile and potassium dihydrogen phosphate (0.13% of pH 2.5) solution with a 1.0 mL/min flow rate for 20 min of total run time. During the whole process, the temperature was maintained at (25 ± 1) °C. The peak of berberine was identified on the basis of retention time by comparing with the standard. Berberine concentration was determined at 266 nm, λmax for berberine, from peak areas using the regression equation obtained from the calibration plot. All analyses were performed in triplicate (n = 3).

Isolation of berberine

Isolation of berberine was done with a concentrated HCl solution. In the dilute B. aristata root extract, add drop-wise concentrated HCl solution and bring the pH to about 5.8. Refrigerate the mixture overnight after cooling it in an ice bath. The crystals of berberine were collected on filter paper and washed with water (Manikyam, Ramesh, Poluri, & Assad, 2017). The structure of isolated berberine was confirmed by spectroscopic analysis. The proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectra and 13C NMR spectra of the berberine were recorded on the Bruker Avance II 400 NMR spectrometer (Bruker, Switerzland) as a solution in CDCl3, with δ units as chemical shift values. The infrared (IR) spectra of the berberine was recorded on an IR spectrophotometer (Jasco, FT/IR-4100 type A) in the KBr phase. The Fast atom bombardment (FAB) mass spectrometer (MS; Jeol SX102-FAB) was utilized for mass spectra of the isolated berberine.

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant activity of isolated berberine was performed as reported by Shirwaikar, Shirwaikar, Rajendran, & Punitha, 2006. The berberine stock solution was prepared at a concentration of 1000 µg/mL in methanol. Different concentrations, viz. 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, 320, and 640 µg/mL were prepared in methanol. Similarly, ascorbic acid standard solution was also prepared of 1000 µg/mL concentration in distilled water and, furthermore, different concentrations, viz. 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, 320, and 640 µg/mL were prepared in water.

DPPH radical scavenging

In 1 mL of DPPH (0.1 mmol/L) solution, different concentrations of berberine and ascorbic acid solution were added. Methanol and DPPH solutions served as controls. After 20 min of incubation in the dark, absorbance was recorded at 517 nm via a UV-spectrophotometer (Biogenix-90014, New Delhi, India). The percentage scavenging of berberine and ascorbic acid was calculated in triplicate.

Nitric oxide radical scavenging

Added 0.3 mL of sodium nitroprusside (5 mmol/L, pH 7.4) to 1 mL of several concentrations of berberine and ascorbic acid solutions and placed them for incubation at 25 °C for 5 h. After incubation, 0.5 mL of Griess reagent was added and the absorbance of the chromophore formed was read at 546 nm.

Superoxide scavenging

To 0.5 mL of different concentrations of berberine and ascorbic acid solution, 1 mL of alkaline dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and 0.2 mL of nitro blue tetrazolium chloride (NBT) 20 mmol/L in phosphate buffer pH 7.4 solutions were added and the absorbance measured at 560 nm.

Assessment of in vivo antiarthritic activity through complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA)

All animal studies protocols were duly approved by the institutional Animal Ethical Committee, under vide letter No. IAEC/PhD/2020–21 of Rameshwaram Institute of Technology and Management, Lucknow, Uttar Pradesh, India. Albino rats (150–––200 g) were procured from Rameshwaram Institute of Technology and Management, Lucknow, India. All animals were housed and maintained under standard conditions of temperature (25 ± 5) °C, relative humidity (55 ± 10)%, and 12/12 h light/dark cycle. Animals were fed with commercial pellet diet and water ad libitum freely throughout the study.

Rheumatoid arthritis was induced on Day 0 by subplantar injection of complete freund’s adjuvant (CFA) of 0.1 mL to the left foot pad of rats, except for vehicle control animals and the treatment was given from Days 14 to 35.

The animals were divided into five groups of six animals each. The first group of animals received distilled water and served as vehicle control or normal (without CFA). The second group served as disease control and received the only CFA on Day 0. The third group of animals received CFA on Day 0 and the standard drug diclofenac sodium (5 mg/(kg·d); oral) from Days 14–35. The fourth and fifth groups of animals received CFA on Day 0 and isolated berberine at doses of 100 and 200 mg/(kg·d) for three weeks from Days 14 to 35. The rats in the vehicle control group and the CFA disease control group were administered with an equal volume of normal saline from Days 14–35 on the same schedule.

The paw diameter of injected and non-injected paws, were measured using a digital vernier calliper, before and after CFA administration from Day 0 to Days 7th, 14th, 21st, 28th, and 35th. Body weight was also measured daily during the experimental period, using a digital weighing balance after adjuvant injection. The arthritic score represents the severity of arthritis and is measured by the procedure as described by Laird, Carter, Grauert, & Cervero, 2001.

Haematological analysis

Animals of all groups were anaesthetized with chloroform and blood samples were collected after cardiac puncture to analyze haematological parameters including haemoglobin, white blood cells, red blood cells, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and platelet count. Biochemical parameters including alanine aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, aspartate aminotransferase, C-reactive protein (CRP), rheumatoid factor, and urea were also measured.

Proinflammatory biomarkers

At Day 35, proinflammatory biomarkers such as interleukin (e.g. IL-6, IL-10), and tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α) (as picograms per millilitre) in serum were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) according to routine laboratory methods. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm on a microplate reader.

Radiological analysis

On Day 35, animals were anaesthetized with ketamine (200 mg/kg). Radiographs of the normal and CFA injected hind paws were taken with an X-ray instrument (Allengers-325 India) for determination of radiographic changes. Finally, all the animals were sacrificed with an overdose of ketamine for histopathological examination.

Histopathological examination of ankle joints

The left ankle joint of rats was removed, fixed (in 4% paraformaldehyde), decalcified (in 10% EDTA), washed (with xylene), embedded (in paraffin), cut (to 5 mm thickness), stained (with hematoxylin-eosin) and observed by light microscopy.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed by One-way ANOVA followed by Dunnetts’s multiple comparison test using Graphpad 5.0 software. The values were expressed as mean ± SEM. P < 0.05, P < 0.01 and P < 0.001 were considered to be statistically significant.

Extraction of roots of B. aristata

A microwave power of 300 W for 5 min gave a sufficient quantity of plant extract. HPLC analysis of B. aristata at 266 nm (λmax) showed 1.23% (mass ratio) of berberine in the ethanolic extract of B. aristata roots.

Isolation of berberine

Berberine was successfully isolated from an ethanolic root extract of B. aristata. The isolation of berberine was confirmed by a spectroscopic technique using IR, 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and MS spectroscopy. The results of spectroscopic data confirmed that the isolated compound was berberine. Antioxidant activity

DPPH radical scavenging assay

The results showed that when the concentration of berberine (up-to 320 µg/mL) and ascorbic acid (up-to 160 µg/mL) was increased, scavenging activity also increased up to a limit, and when the concentration was further increased, no significant DPPH radical scavenging activity was increased. The IC50 values for both berberine and ascorbic acid were reported as 178.27 µg/mL and 118.78 µg/mL, respectively (Fig. 1A).Fig. 1Antioxidant activity of berberine and ascorbic acid. Antioxidant activity of berberine and ascorbic acid. DPPH radical scavenging activity (A); Nitric oxide radical scavenging activity (B); and NBT mediated superoxide scavenging (C).

Nitric oxide radical scavenging assay

The NO radical scavenging showed increased scavenging activity to 320 μg/mL for berberine (IC50 = 123.0 μg/mL) and 160 μg/mL of ascorbic acid (IC50 = 117.6 μg/mL) with an inhibition of 93.1% and 98.2% respectively (Fig. 1B).

Superoxide scavenging assay

The maximum % scavenging was observed at 320 μg/mL concentration for both with IC50 values of 145.6 μg/mL (for berberine) and 96.4 μg/mL (for ascorbic acid) (Fig. 1C).

Effect on body weight

The highest percentage of body weight was gained at the end of day 35 in the standard drug treated group (7.43%), followed by a berberine treated groups (5.17% and 4.72%) in different doses (i.e., 200 and 100 mg/kg respectively) (Fig. 2B).

Effect on arthritic score

The disease control rats showed the highest arthritic score (7.2 ± 0.3) at the end of study, which was reinstated by diclofenac sodium at 5 mg/kg (2.4 ± 0.2), followed with berberine at 200 mg/kg (2.6 ± 0.3), and least by berberine at 100 mg/kg (3.1 ± 0.3) treatments (Fig. 2C).

Effect on radiologic analysis

Diclofenac sodium and berberine (200 mg/kg) treatment resulted in normalised bone erosion, connective tissue resorption, joint deformity, soft tissue inflammation, and joint gap in rats. Conclusion

The berberine was successfully extracted and isolated from B. aristata and characterised by a spectroscopic technique. The antioxidant activity of isolated berberine was compared with ascorbic acid, while its antiarthritic activity was compared with diclofenac sodium. Berberine showed ameliorating action on haematological parameters and proinflammatory biomarkers. Furthermore, histopathology and radiological analysis in the CFA-induced arthritis rat model confirmed the joint protective action of berberine in a dose-dependent manner.
