CSF-1 receptor blockade in pulmonary arterial hypertension—keeping macrophages in check?
Authors: Marija Gredic, Norbert Weissmann
Published: 2026

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a progressive disease driven by inward pulmonary vascular remodeling, often leading to right heart failure and death.1 While past research on the underlying causes of vascular remodeling in PAH has often focused on the intrinsic properties of pulmonary vascular cells, more recent studies highlight the importance of perivascular inflammation, particularly the role of its most abundant cellular constituent—macrophages. A large body of evidence implicates macrophages as the orchestrators, or at least potent propagators, of pulmonary vascular cell proliferation, migration, and dysfunction. Conversely, cancer-like hyperproliferative, apoptosis-resistant, and proinflammatory pulmonary vascular cells create a microenvironment that shapes macrophage phenotype(s), thus perpetuating a vicious circle and fostering chronic tissue-remodeling conditions.2,3 Recent studies have identified a multitude of mediators involved in pathogenic crosstalk between macrophages and pulmonary vascular cells, including chemokines, growth factors, and lipid mediators. Moreover, interventions that reduce accumulation of macrophages or prevent a shift from M1 toward M2-like polarization that favors cell proliferation and tissue remodeling have shown promise in relevant preclinical PAH/pulmonary hypertension (PH) models.3–5 Despite these advances, such strategies have not yet been successfully translated into approved clinical therapies and the optimal approach to target macrophage accumulation and M2-like polarization in PAH remains to be determined.

Recognizing this need, Nishiura and colleagues investigate whether the blockade of colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF1, also known as macrophage colony-stimulating factor) receptor signaling can reduce perivascular inflammation and ameliorate PH in a sugen/hypoxia mouse model and in rats challenged with monocrotaline.6 The CSF1 receptor (CSF1R) is a tyrosine kinase receptor activated by binding of homodimeric ligands CSF1 and interleukin 34 (IL-34), an event that promotes survival, proliferation, differentiation, and chemotaxis of myeloid cells.7 After observing the accumulation of CSF1R-positive macrophages around remodeled pulmonary vessels in PAH lungs, and detecting increased circulating levels of its ligands, CSF1 and IL-34, the authors delineate the relevance of this pathway for PH in vivo. This extensive work encompasses investigation of 3 different strategies for interference with CSF1R: knockdown using antisense oligonucleotides, pharmacological inhibition with pexidartanib, and a neutralizing antibody. All 3 strategies markedly lower pulmonary artery pressures, pulmonary vascular and right ventricular remodeling, and diminish perivascular presence of M2-like macrophages compared to animals receiving vehicle only. However, further studies are needed to determine whether these promising treatment strategies halt the progression of the disease or can even promote its reversal.

Intriguingly, a fourth approach for CSF1R inhibition is currently in the phase 3 trial for the treatment of PAH: Seralutinib targets platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR), mast or stem cell growth factor receptor kit (c-KIT), and CSF1R.1 While a plethora of evidence supports inhibition of PDGFR in PAH, the study by Nishiura and colleagues is the first in an animal model to confirm what was previously an educated guess: that interfering CSF1R signaling improves pulmonary hemodynamics and preserves right ventricular function in PAH.8

Mechanistically, the authors attribute beneficial effects to reduced macrophage proliferation and migration, and to diminished downstream c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) activation and CCL2 production by these cells (Figure 1). In vitro, IL-34 and CSF1 stimulate CCL2 transcription and secretion in a CSF1R- and JNK-dependent manner. They report that enhanced proliferation of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) in response to conditioned medium from M2-like macrophages is abrogated either by addition of CCL2-neutralizing antibodies or by CSF1R inhibition in macrophages. A similar pathogenic mechanism involving CSF1 stimulation of CCL2 expression has recently been implicated in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, although the effects on pulmonary vasculature have not been investigated.9 Moreover, the influence of CCL2 on proliferation of PASMCs described here by Nishiura and colleagues is in accordance with previous studies in PAH, reporting amelioration of the disease by CCR2 inhibition and knockout.3,10

Key findings of the study by Nishiura and colleagues.6 CSF1R blockade reduces recruitment of monocytes, their proliferation, and differentiation into an M2-like phenotype. Mechanistically, binding of the ligands CSF1 or IL-34 to CSF1R induces JNK phosphorylation and transcriptional upregulation of multiple inflammatory mediators, including CCL2. Nonetheless, one should be cautious when drawing parallels between blocking CCR2, a receptor capable of binding multiple ligands upregulated in PAH, and reducing CCL2 production in macrophages. This is especially true since pulmonary endothelial and smooth muscle cells are capable of producing this chemokine and do so abundantly in PAH.11 Indeed, targeting CCL2 itself seems to be a complex issue often yielding contradicting results, at least in hypoxia mouse PH models: While knockout of the chemokine had no effect on development of pulmonary hypertension, application of neutralizing antibodies seems to achieve the goal.12,13 One possible explanation for such discrepancies is congenital effects of the CCL2/CCR2 axis,14 which have been insufficiently investigated, but it is more likely that the comprehensive work of Nishiura and colleagues provides just one piece of the complex pathogenic puzzle. Namely, beneficial effects of CSFR1 blockade may be exerted through additional signaling pathways affected by reducing perivascular abundance of M2-like macrophages and altering their secretome. The authors noted a decrease of CCL5, CCL22, and IL-6 expression in response to CSF1R blockade in the current study; however, the list is likely still incomplete. In addition, even without direct influence on mitogen expression and secretion, CSF1R blockade could be beneficial in vivo by mitigating perivascular accumulation of CSF1R-positive macrophages. Along these lines, it has been reported that CSF1R-positive macrophages substantially contribute to PDGF-B production in the vicinity of distal pulmonary arterioles and by doing so, promote their muscularization.8 Finally, for complete unraveling of this pathological cascade, cell type and signals triggering abnormal production of CSF1 and IL-34 in PAH need to be determined.

Therefore, additional preclinical and mechanistic studies are needed to better understand CSF1R signaling in the context of PAH, including the cellular sources of its ligands and its pleiotropic effects on lung macrophages, especially as clinical studies are already ongoing. In this respect, the work by Nishiura and colleagues provides important evidence for the involvement of M2-like macrophages in PAH development, offers a valuable description of the role of CSF1R signaling in this process, and supports targeting this pathway as a future promising treatment strategy.
