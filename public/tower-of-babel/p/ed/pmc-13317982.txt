Multiomics analyses of mutants for Marchantia polymorpha FERONIA and MARIS reveal a link between cell wall integrity and abscisic acid responses
Authors: Timothy Owen Jobe, Celso Gaspar Litholdo, Sara Christina Stolze, Lisa Stephan, Jens Westermann, Anne Harzen, Martin Hülskamp, Hirofumi Nakagami, Aurélien Boisson-Dernier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAt the core of cell wall integrity mechanisms that enable plant cells to coordinate their growth with their cell wall status lies the transmembrane malectin-like receptor kinase FERONIA (FER) and its family members. FER itself controls a myriad of plant developmental processes, including sexual reproduction, cell growth, and morphogenesis, often intersecting with phytohormone-dependent pathways such as abscisic acid (ABA) signaling or plant immunity. Interestingly, FER, together with its downstream receptor-like cytoplasmic kinase MARIS (MRI), has been shown to similarly control root hair and rhizoid integrity in the vascular angiosperm Arabidopsis thaliana and the early diverging bryophyte Marchantia polymorpha, respectively. It remains uncertain however whether FER and MRI cooperate beyond tip growth in Marchantia, and which of their functions in Arabidopsis originate from mechanisms established early in land plant evolution. Here, we conducted comparative transcriptomic and proteomic analyses on the M. polymorpha mutant plants, Mpfer-1 and Mpmri-1, alongside their corresponding wild-type accessions. We observed large and significant overlaps between differentially expressed genes and abundant proteins between both mutants. Our multiomics approach revealed that MpFER and MpMRI largely cooperate to repress transcriptional networks, particularly those associated with plant defense and ABA responses. Nonetheless, our phenotypic analyses showed that MpFER and MpMRI exert distinct functions in response to abiotic stresses such as ABA and salt treatment. Specifically, Mpfer-1, but not Mpmri-1, plants exhibited hypersensitivity to ABA-dependent growth inhibition, indicating that FER’s role in negatively regulating ABA-mediated growth responses is conserved between bryophytes and vascular plants.Keywords: abscisic acid, cell wall integrity, FERONIA, Marchantia polymorpha, malectin-like receptor kinases, MARIS

Introduction

In the last 30 years, studies on the plant cell wall (CW)—the extracellular matrix that surrounds plant cells and prevents them from bursting—have revealed that this extracellular compartment is much more than a rigid frame. Rather, the CW is an extremely dynamic and complex biochemical barrier that adapts and communicates actively with the cell protoplast it shelters (Cosgrove 2024, Delmer et al. 2024). Part of this communication is arbitrated by plasma membrane–localized receptor-like proteins and receptor-like kinases whose extracellular domains perceive and bind signals within the CW matrix to regulate intracellular activities (Wolf 2022). One of these receptor classes is the malectin-like receptor-kinase (MLR) family, also known as the “CrRLK1L family,” with 17 members in the flowering plant model Arabidopsis thaliana (Franck et al. 2018, Zhu et al. 2021). MLRs contain an intracellular Ser/Thr kinase domain, a transmembrane domain, and an extracellular domain with two malectin-like domains that can bind pectins of the CW and small secreted peptides called RALFs (rapid alkalinization factors). FERONIA (FER) is by far the most studied of the MLRs: its gene is expressed widely throughout the plant body—except in pollen, where its pollen-expressed sister genes ANXUR1 and 2 and BUDDHA’S PAPER SEAL1 and 2 are taking over (Boisson-Dernier et al. 2009, Miyazaki et al. 2009, Ge et al. 2017)—and is involved in a myriad of biological processes and signaling pathways (Malivert and Hamant 2023, Cheung 2024). FER was originally discovered to function in the female gametophyte as a key component for the dialogue between male and female gametophytes that results in sperm release in the synergids and hence was named after the Etruscan goddess of fertility (Huck et al. 2003, Rotman et al. 2003). Since then, FER, together with its coreceptor LLG1 (LORELEI-LIKE glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored protein) and their peptide ligand RALFs, has been shown to orchestrate many developmental processes, including root (Haruta et al. 2014) and root hair growth (Duan et al. 2010) as well as pavement cell morphogenesis (Li et al. 2015, Lin et al. 2022). The involvement of FER in such processes operates at the crossroads with many phytohormone signaling pathways such as brassinosteroids (Guo et al. 2009), ethylene (Deslauriers and Larsen 2010), and abscisic acid (ABA; Yu et al. 2012, Chen et al. 2016) and in conjunction with external stress perception, such as high salinity (Feng et al. 2018) and pathogen invasions (Kessler et al. 2010, Masachis et al. 2016, Stegmann et al. 2017). Roots of FER and LLG1 mutant plants carry root hairs that burst prematurely during growth (Duan et al. 2010, Li et al. 2015). Similarly, pollen tubes of double mutants for ANX1/ANX2 and their coreceptors LLG2/LLG3 burst in vitro and in vivo (Boisson-Dernier et al. 2009, Miyazaki et al. 2009, Ge et al. 2017, Feng et al. 2019). These studies demonstrate that MLRs and their coreceptors are required to maintain cell wall integrity (CWI) during tip growth. Interestingly, a receptor-like cytoplasmic kinase from the RLCK-VIII subfamily named MARIS (MRI), after the Etruscan god of fertility and agriculture, was identified as a common downstream regulator of these signaling receptor complexes (Boisson-Dernier et al. 2015). MRI is preferentially expressed in both pollen tubes and root hairs of Arabidopsis and mri mutants also display pollen and root hair bursting (Boisson-Dernier et al. 2015, Liao et al. 2016). Moreover, expression of an overactive variant of MRI, MRIR240C, is sufficient to rescue the bursting phenotype of fer root hairs and anx1 anx2 pollen tubes, positioning MRI downstream of the MLRs (Boisson-Dernier et al. 2015). The R240C mutation in the kinase activation loop of MRI is likely protecting MRI from the regulatory inhibition of a yet unknown protein phosphatase 2C as it was reported for the Nicotiana benthamiana MRI homolog Pti1b (Pto-interacting 1b; Giska and Martin 2019). While FER and MRI have not yet been reported to interact or to phosphorylate each other, MRI was recently shown to be phosphorylated in vitro by BUPS1, while ANX1 failed to do so (Gao et al. 2023b). In addition, both MRI and MRIR240C were found to interact with pollen-expressed MILDEW LOCUS O1 (MLO1), but only overactive MRIR240C could activate the calcium channel activity of AtMLO1, 5, 9, and 15 (Gao et al. 2023b). Altogether, these studies indicate that members of the RALF/LLG/MLR/MRI/MLO families constitute a signaling module that regulates CWI during tip growth.

Excitingly, this signaling module active in pollen tubes and root hairs of the flowering plant Arabidopsis was also found to be functional in the rhizoids of the bryophyte Marchantia polymorpha (hereafter referred to as Marchantia; Westermann et al. 2019). Rhizoids are tip-growing cells that participate in anchoring the plant body to the substrate and absorbing water and nutrients, and, as such, are considered analogous structures of root hairs (Menand et al. 2007). Transferred DNA (T-DNA) insertional knockdown mutants for MpFER and MpMRI, the unique homologs of the MLR and RLCK-VIII families in Marchantia, respectively, were found in a large genetic screen for Marchantia mutants with short rhizoids (Honkanen et al. 2016). In detailed phenotypic analyses, these T-DNA insertional Mpfer-1 and Mpmri-1 mutants as well as the CRISPR-edited Mpfer-2 and Mpfer-3 all displayed bursting rhizoids while expression of MpMRIR240C was sufficient to partially rescue Mpfer-1 bursting rhizoids (Westermann et al. 2019, Mecchia et al. 2022). Thus, the MLR/MRI signaling module that controls CWI in tip-growing cells has been conserved throughout land plant evolution from the rhizoids of bryophytes to the root hairs and pollen tubes of seed plants. Besides rhizoids, MpFER and MpMRI also play roles during gemmae and thalli growth. For example, MpFER is expressed in most cells of the thalli, and Mpfer-2 mutant thalli are smaller with dead cells and display a lower apparent stiffness than wild-type (WT) thalli (Mecchia et al. 2022). Mpmri-1 thalli are also smaller than WT ones and both MpMRI and MpFER somehow regulate gemmae growth (Westermann et al. 2019, Muller et al. 2025). In addition, trans-species complementation assays showed that MpMRI and AtMRI (64% identity at the amino acid level) can functionally replace each other in the control of tip growth (Westermann et al. 2019), while this is not true for MpFER and AtFER (43.3% identity at the amino acid level; Mecchia et al. 2022). Thus, it remains uncertain which of the known MLR and MRI functions in Arabidopsis originate from mechanisms established early during land plant evolution and which ones have arisen in the last 450 million years.

Here, we exploit the knockdown mutants of the CWI module in the liverwort Marchantia by carrying out comparative transcriptomic and proteomic analyses of Mpfer-1 and Mpmri-1 compared to the WT accessions Tak-1 and Tak-2. Our results show that MpFER and MpMRI largely cooperate to negatively regulate transcriptional networks, in particular those related to plant defense and abscisic acid (ABA) responses. However, phenotypic analyses reveal that MpFER and MpMRI have distinct functions in response to abiotic stresses. For example, in contrast to Mpmri-1, Mpfer-1 displays hypersensitivity to ABA-induced thalli growth inhibition, indicating that the negative regulation of ABA signaling by MpFER and AtFER is conserved and active in the common ancestor of land plants.

Results

The transcriptome and proteome of CWI-related mutants Mpfer-1 and Mpmri-1 overlap significantly

To reveal pathways that could be regulated by the ancestral FER/MRI module or alternative compensatory ones that are deregulated in the absence of the module, comparative transcriptomic (RNA-seq) and proteomic liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analyses of Mpfer-1 (male) and Mpmri-1 (female) lines and their respective WT male (Tak-1) and female (Tak-2) strains were carried out. For this purpose, 3-week-old Marchantia thalli grown on cellophane membranes were collected and RNA and proteins were extracted (Fig. 1a).

To simplify our complex data sets while preserving their most important structures, principal component analyses (PCAs) were performed (Fig. 1). These PCAs showed robust clustering indicating reproducibility between our independent biological samples for the transcriptomes (n = 3, Fig. 1b) and the proteomes (n = 4, Fig. 1c), as well as a clear separation between both mutants and the WT strains.

While the PCA indicated some similarity between Tak-1 and Tak-2 transcriptomes, we decided to compare the female Tak-2 to the male Tak-1 thalli transcriptomes. As expected, this analysis identified mostly, but not exclusively, transcripts of genes located on the female U and male V chromosomes among the top differentially expressed genes (DEGs; Supplementary Fig. S1, see how U and V chromosome–located genes are on both ends of the volcano plot). First, this confirms that sex chromosome–located genes are also expressed in vegetative tissues, maybe as relics of the ancestral autosome from which the sex chromosomes evolved (Bowman et al. 2017). Secondly, it indicates that a subset of genes located on autosomes are under the transcriptional control of sex chromosome–encoded genes even in vegetative tissues. For instance, this is the case for the feminizer gene BASIC PENTACYSTEINE ON THE U (BPCU, MpUg00370, 24th most upregulated gene in Tak-2 vs Tak-1), which regulates the autosomal transcription factor FEMALE GAMETOPHYTE MYB (FGMYB, Mp1g17210, 40th most downregulated gene of Tak-2 versus Tak-1), which in turn promotes female sexual differentiation (Iwasaki et al. 2021).

Concerning the CWI mutants, transcriptome profiling identified 2293 and 2038 genes that are differentially expressed (log2 fold change (FC) ≥ 1.5; P-value ≤ 0.05) in Mpmri-1 versus Tak-2 and Mpfer-1 versus Tak-1, respectively (Fig. 2). Out of roughly 5200 quantified proteins, our proteomic analysis determined that 775 and 1549 proteins have different abundance [log2 (FC) > 1.5; P-value < 0.05] in Mpmri-1 versus Tak-2 and Mpfer-1 versus Tak-1, respectively (Fig. 3). For both mutants, there were more upregulated than downregulated genes (1572 and 1127 up- vs 721 and 911 downregulated in Mpmri-1 and Mpfer-1, respectively), and more enriched proteins than depleted ones (526 and 955 enriched vs 249 and 594 depleted in Mpmri-1 and Mpfer-1, respectively). This indicates that, globally, the MpFER/MpMRI module negatively regulates transcriptional networks.

The transcriptome and proteome data of Mpfer-1 statistically significantly overlapped (Supplementary Fig. S2a and b), which was true for Mpmri-1 as well, although to a lesser extent (Supplementary Fig. S2c and d). More interestingly, statistically significant overlaps were observed between DEGs in Mpfer-1 and Mpmri-1 (Fig. 2c and d), as well as between differentially abundant proteins in Mpfer-1 and Mpmri-1 (Fig. 3c and d), suggesting that MpFER and MpMRI cooperation is not solely limited to the maintenance of CWI in rhizoids (Westermann et al. 2019).

Transcriptome and proteome comparisons indicate that the MpFER/MpMRI module regulates large gene families related to ABA and defense responses

We performed gene ontology (GO) analysis to identify overrepresented biological processes (BPs) and molecular functions (MFs) on both DEGs and differentially accumulated proteins (DAPs) using the MarpolBase Expression MBEX functional enrichment tool (https://mbex.marchantia.info/enrichment). It revealed some clear overlaps in categories found in the transcriptomic and proteomic data sets of both Mpfer-1 and Mpmri-1 (Supplementary Fig. S3), some related to chitin [chitin catabolic process (GO:0006032), chitinase activity (GO:0004568), and chitin binding (GO:0008061)], ABA [response to ABA (GO:0009737), ABA-activated signaling pathway (GO:0009738), and ABA binding (GO:0010427)], ATP [ATP binding (GO:0005524)], nucleic acid binding [(GO:0003676)], DNA binding (GO:0003677), RNA binding (GO:0003723), and oxidoreductase activity (GO:0016491).

Interestingly, while GO analyses of the transcriptomic datasets of both mutants were quite similar, the ones for their proteomic datasets displayed several opposite regulations. For example, defense response (GO:0006952), ABA-activated signaling pathway (GO:0009738), protein phosphatase inhibitor activity (GO:0004864), ABA binding (GO:0010427), and signaling receptor activity (GO:0038023) are overrepresented in depleted Mpfer-1 proteins but overrepresented in enriched Mpmri-1 proteins. This could pinpoint some specific distinct roles for MpFER and MpMRI.

In addition, when we compared our list of GO terms for Mpfer-1 with those from an integrated omics study on the Arabidopsis Atfer-4 mutant (Wang et al. 2022), we found many common enriched terms in genes and proteins, such as response to ABA (GO:0009737), plant-type cell wall organization (GO:0009664), response to oxidative stress (GO:0006979), nucleic acid binding (GO:0003676), DNA binding (GO:0003677), RNA binding (GO:0003723), defense response (GO:0006952), water transport (GO:0006833), peroxidase activity (GO:0004601), oxidoreductase activity (GO:0016491), heme binding (GO:0020037), transmembrane transport (GO:0055085), transmembrane transporter activity (GO:0022857), and intracellular protein transport (GO:0006886). This supports that at least some BPs and MFs regulated by FER are conserved in the land plant lineage (Mecchia et al. 2022).

Next, we performed GenFam enrichment analysis on all the DEGs and DAPs identified in the CWI mutant versus WT strains (Fig. 4a and b). AQP (aquaporin), LecRLK (lectin-RLK), and expansins were significantly enriched in downregulated genes of both mutants, while LEAs (late embryogenesis abundant, ABA-responsive genes), CAPs (cysteine-rich secretory proteins, e.g. pathogenesis-related 1, PR1), LHC (light-harvesting chlorophyll binding), CSS (chalcone and stilbene synthesis), dirigent (DIR), PALs (phenylalanine ammonia lyases, enzymes that eliminate ammonia from phenylalanine to form cinnamate, a precursor of lignins/lignans and flavonoids, and thus anthocyanins), NOX (NADPH oxidase), cytochromes P450 (Cy P450), GH (glycosyl hydrolase), and CCH (copper chaperone) were enriched in upregulated genes of both mutants (Fig. 4a). Interestingly, LEA, LHC, DIR, and GH were also enriched in more abundant proteins of Mpfer-1 (Fig. 4b), while DIR was enriched in upregulated genes and more abundant proteins of both Mpfer-1 and Mpmri-1. GHs are carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) that can selectively cleave various carbohydrates and glycoconjugates with functions such as CW remodeling, reserve mobilization, and pathogen defense (Kfoury et al. 2024). DIR-encoding genes are inducible by different types of abiotic and biotic stress factors, including wounding, dehydration, ABA, and NaCl (Paniagua et al. 2017) or following pathogen infection (Ponce De León et al. 2012, Reboledo et al. 2015, Carella et al. 2019). They function in secondary metabolism, defense responses, and the formation and attachment of the Casparian strip in Arabidopsis (Gao et al. 2023a). In Marchantia, approximately half of the DIRs were found in the CW proteome (Kolkas et al. 2021), suggesting that the CWs of Mpfer-1 and Mpmri-1 are likely quite different from those of WT plants. LEAs are a large and diverse group of small hydrophilic disordered proteins involved in protecting plant tissues and cells from abiotic stresses and low water availability. They are present from algae to angiosperms and their expression levels are upregulated by salt, cold, heat, drought, and ABA treatments (Hsiao 2024).

Based on the GenFam enrichment analysis, we therefore hypothesized that gene families for PRs, DIRs, CAPs, PALs, chitinases, anthocyanin biosynthesis, as well as ABA-related genes may be globally misregulated in the Marchantia CWI mutants. Indeed, when taking a closer look in our RNA-seq data at the expression levels of these toolbox genes across all our biological replicates, they did show globally higher expression levels in Mpfer-1 and Mpmri-1 mutant samples compared to WT ones (Supplementary Fig. S4–S7). Moreover, this was independently confirmed by picking representative genes for real time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analyses that showed significantly higher expression levels for PR1, 3, 4, 5, 9, PAL/CHS, and LEA1 in the mutants compared to WT plants (Fig. 4c).

Altogether, our results indicate that MpFER and MpMRI globally exert a direct or indirect negative influence on the regulation of defense-responsive genes and ABA responses similar to that observed for Arabidopsis AtFER (Wang et al. 2022).

Mpfer-1 but not Mpmri-1 plants exhibit priming and hypersensitivity to ABA exposure

Since our multiomics data pointed to a role for MpFER and MpMRI in regulating ABA responses (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. S7b), we compared our transcriptomes of Mpfer-1 and Mpmri-1 with that of Tak-1 plants treated with 1 μM ABA for 6 h (Jahan et al. 2019). Interestingly, while there was little overlap in downregulated genes (Supplementary Fig. S8a and b), there was a highly statistically significant overlap between upregulated genes in Mpfer-1/Mpmri-1 versus upregulated genes in ABA-treated Tak-1 (Supplementary Fig. S8c and d). This indicates that a significant pool of genes that are upregulated by ABA treatment in WT Tak-1 is globally already upregulated in CWI-related mutant plants without ABA treatment, as confirmed for MpLEA1 (Fig. 4c) and other ABA-related genes (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. S7b).

Because Arabidopsis Atfer-4 plants were shown to be hypersensitive to ABA in seed germination, stomatal, and root growth assays (Yu et al. 2012, Chen et al. 2016), we next tested Marchantia Mpfer-1 and Mpmri-1 mutant plant responses to ABA. Analyses of plant growth responses showed that surprisingly, among the four genotypes, Tak-1 was the least affected. Indeed, for all the genotypes but Tak-1, ABA-induced growth inhibition was already significant at 1 μM ABA and became more pronounced at 10 μM ABA (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S9 for an independent assay). Tak-1 plants only showed a mild response at 10 μM ABA. In clear contrast, Mpfer-1 growth inhibition was the highest among all genotypes at both 1 and 10 μM ABA (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S9). These results indicate that Mpfer-1 plants are hypersensitive to ABA treatment, while Mpmri-1 behaves like Tak-2. Interestingly, the observed ABA hypersensitivity for Mpfer-1 plants was also observed for plants of the independent CRISPR-edited Mpfer-2 and Mpfer-3 alleles (Fig. 5d; Mecchia et al. 2022).

To further understand the link between MpFER and ABA, we quantified by RT-qPCR transcript levels for ABA-related genes, including ABA biosynthesis genes (MpABA1, MpNCED), catabolism genes (MpCYP707A), receptor and early signaling genes (MpPYL1, MpSnRK2A), transcription factors (MpABI3A, MpABI5A), responsive genes (MpLEA1L, MpDEHYDRIN), and regulator genes (MpMFT), in thalli grown for 14 days on 10 μM ABA. The genes encoding the type-A protein phosphatase 2C (PP2Cs) MpABI1A (MpVg00730) and MpABI1B (MpUg00130), the main regulators of ABA signaling, are located on the sexual chromosomes and thus were not tested here. Apart from Tak-1, Mpfer-1, Tak-2, and Mpmri-1, we included two additional lines, JW26-3WT and JW26-13fer-1. These lines are progenies segregating from a backcross of Mpfer-1 (male) with Tak-2 (female) and are both females. JW26-3WT plants do not harbor the T-DNA insertion of Mpfer-1, display wild-type (WT)-like thalli sizes, and carried intact rhizoids. In contrast, JW26-13fer-1 plants still carry the T-DNA insertion in MpFER and are much smaller, with burst rhizoids, similar to the original Mpfer-1 plants (Supplementary Fig. S10).

Apart from MpCYP707A, whose levels of expression were moderately upregulated in Mpmri-1 compared to WT strains (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. S7b), the T-DNA insertion in MpMRI did not appear to influence ABA-related gene levels during this 14-day treatment with 10 μM ABA (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. S11). This correlates with the normal responses to ABA treatment observed in Mpmri-1 (Fig. 5) and the globally lower expression levels of the ABA toolbox in Mpmri-1 samples compared to Mpfer-1 ones (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. S7b).

Concerning Mpfer-1, first and as expected, across all the experiments, the fer-1 female mutant progeny JW26-13fer-1 behaved very similarly to the original male Mpfer-1 (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. S11), indicating that the sexual chromosomes do not influence the MpFER-dependent ABA responses. Secondly, for Mpfer-1 and its progeny JW26-13fer-1, ABA-related genes could be grouped into three categories. The first one includes genes whose levels were not impacted by the genotypes of our mutant lines (e.g. MpABA1 and MpABI5A; Supplementary Fig. S11a and e). The second one includes genes that are slightly upregulated when MpFER is disrupted in mock conditions and do not increase upon the 14-day ABA treatment (MpNCED, MpPYL1, MpSnRK2A; Supplementary Fig. S11b and d). The third one is the most prominent and includes genes that are upregulated when MpFER is disrupted (in Mpfer-1, male, and JW26-13fer-1, female) in mock conditions and whose levels in all other genotypes are increased by the 14-day ABA treatment to roughly the levels found in Mpfer-1/JW26-13fer-1 (Fig. 6). This group comprises MpCYP707A, MpABI3A, MpLEA1L, MpDEHYDRIN, and MpMFT and likely represents the targets of MpFER-dependent inhibition. Their higher transcript levels in unchallenged Mpfer-1 plants likely reflect an induced ABA state, explaining why Mpfer-1 plants respond more strongly even to low exogenous ABA concentrations (Fig. 5).

Because ABA plays a central role in abiotic stress responses, the responses of CWI mutants’ gemmae to additional environmental cues—such as growth-inhibiting salt and osmotic stresses—were tested by a 7-day treatment with various NaCl (0, 25, 50, and 100 mM) or mannitol (0, 100, and 200 mM) concentrations. Interestingly, in contrast to Arabidopsis, where the ABA-hypersensitive Atfer-4 seedlings also display salt hypersensitivity (Feng et al. 2018, Zhao et al. 2021, Liu et al. 2023, Jiang et al. 2024), the ABA-hypersensitive Mpfer-1 gemmae showed a weak but significant hyposensitivity to 25 mM NaCl compared to Tak-1 (Supplementary Fig. S12a, left panel). This hyposensitivity was also observed for JW26-13fer-1 gemmae compared to JW26-3WT (Supplementary Fig. S12a, right panel, and S12b), indicating that maybe FER’s role in mediating salt stress response has changed during land plant evolution or that ABA signaling and salt stress perception are differently coordinated in Marchantia and Arabidopsis. Furthermore, Mpmri-1 gemmae were more sensitive than any of the other genotypes at 25 mM NaCl (Supplementary Fig. S12a, left panel), highlighting again the distinct roles of MpFER and MpMRI in abiotic stress responses. Concerning the response to the growth-inhibiting mannitol treatment, no significative differences were observed between the Mpfer-1, JW23–13fer-1, and Mpmri-1 mutant gemmae and their respective controls (Supplementary Fig. S12c).

Discussion

In this study, comparative transcriptomic and proteomic analyses of CWI mutant lines versus WT strains showed that MpFER and MpMRI are global negative regulators of transcription networks, with roughly 50% more upregulated genes than downregulated genes in the mutants (Fig. 2). Furthermore, our study reveals that more than half of the DEGs and DAPs identified in Mpmri-1 mutant plants are also differentially expressed/abundant in Mpfer-1 plants, indicating that MpFER and MpMRI largely cooperate, but also most likely play specific roles independently of each other.

MpFER and MpMRI have been shown to genetically interact for the maintenance of CWI during rhizoid growth (Westermann et al. 2019, Mecchia et al. 2022), just as it was shown for AtFER and AtMRI during root hair growth and AtANXUR1/2 and AtMRI during pollen tube growth (Boisson-Dernier et al. 2015). In Arabidopsis, AtFER’s role is far from being limited to tip-growing cells as it is crucial for almost all aspects of plant biology and growth in an ever-changing environment, including ABA and defense responses (e.g. Franck et al. 2018, Cheung 2024). In contrast, AtMRI has only been shown to be involved in regulating root hair and pollen tube growth so far (Boisson-Dernier et al. 2015, Liao et al. 2016). AtMRI belongs to the RLCK-VIII (also known as “CARK” for cytosolic ABA receptor kinase) subfamily with 11 members in Arabidopsis, which have been reported to form heterodimers and homodimers with each other in planta (Li et al. 2022). On the one hand, MAZZA (CARK5) appeared to play a positive role in CLE peptide signaling in roots and possibly in stomata spacing (Blümke et al. 2021). On the other hand, CARK1, 3, and 6 have been shown to function as positive regulators of ABA-dependent physiological responses (Zhang et al. 2018, Wang et al. 2019, 2020, 2024a), phosphorylating ABA receptors and inducing their de-dimerization (Li et al. 2022). Recently, AtFER was shown to phosphorylate and interact with AtCARK1 to positively regulate ABA signaling during the early seed germination stage, suggesting that the regulatory role of AtFER during ABA signaling varies at different developmental stages (Wang et al. 2024a). In this study, interaction of AtFER with AtCARK2, 10, and 11 was also reported but not tested with AtMRI (AtCARK4). However, AtMRI can interact with and phosphorylate ABA receptors (Li et al. 2022), but no ABA-related phenotype has yet been described for AtMRI-related mutants. This could mean that either AtMRI plays no role during ABA signaling or that its ABA-related function is hidden by other closely related CARKs and the right multiple cark mutant combination has not yet been investigated. In Marchantia, MpMRI regulates rhizoid (Westermann et al. 2019) and gemmae growth (Muller et al. 2025), while a broader involvement of MpFER at both vegetative (rhizoid, gemmae cup, gemmae, thallus) and reproductive stages of development was documented (Mecchia et al. 2022, Muller et al. 2025).

The large overlap between DEGs and DAPs observed in Mpfer-1 and Mpmri-1 displays a significant enrichment for gene families involved in responses to biotic and abiotic stresses, in particular the LEA, DIR, PR, GH, PAL, CHS, and CSS gene families (Fig. 4a). Those enrichments can be visualized by looking at the heatmap of specific gene toolboxes across our samples (e.g. PRs/PAL/CHS, Supplementary Figs. S4–S7), and were further confirmed by RT-qPCR analyses (Fig. 4b).

Several gene families found to be upregulated in the CWI mutants are related to plant defense responses, indicating either that the MpFER/MpMRI module negatively regulates immunity or that the disruption of CWI and its impact on the CW physico-chemical properties activate autoimmune responses. In flowering plants such as Arabidopsis, soybean, rice, and tomato, FER has been shown to play contrasting roles in responses to pathogens. On the one hand, it is a susceptibility factor for the obligate biotrophic fungi Erysiphe orontii, the agent of powdery mildew (Kessler et al. 2010), the hemibiotrophic fungi Fusarium oxysporum (Masachis et al. 2016, Fan et al. 2024) and Fusarium graminearum (Wang et al. 2024b), the hemibiotrophic fungus Magnaporthe oryzae (Yang et al. 2020) as well as for the parasitic nematode Meloidogyne incognita (Zhang et al. 2020, 2021). In the case of Fusarium and Meloidogyne, the resistance observed in the fer-related mutant was linked to RALF-like peptides secreted by the pathogens to manipulate FER-dependent pathways (Masachis et al. 2016, Zhang et al. 2020). On the other hand, FER has also been shown to play a positive role in immune responses to the bacterium Pseudomonas syringae, acting at the plasma membrane as a scaffold component regulating the formation of the immune receptor complex (Stegmann et al. 2017) and its mobility (Gronnier et al. 2022) and in the cytosol by destabilizing MYC2, a major positive regulator of jasmonic acid and coronatine responses (Guo et al. 2018). Excitingly, Chen et al. recently discovered that Arabidopsis infection by P. syringae or RALF23 treatment leads to cleavage of the cytoplasmic domain of FER and its translocation to the nucleus, thereby likely promoting immune responses (Chen et al. 2024). In bryophytes, the role of MpFER during plant–pathogen interactions has not yet been characterized, but natural fungal pathogens have been isolated for Marchantia (Matsui et al. 2020) and pathosystems have been established with the oomycete Phytophthora palmivora (Carella et al. 2019), the bacteria P. syringae (Gimenez-Ibanez et al. 2019, Matsumoto et al. 2022), the fungus F. oxysporum (Redkar et al. 2022), and the tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) (Ros-Moner et al. 2024). Therefore, it will be possible to investigate whether the ancestral role of FER was to be a susceptibility factor, a positive regulator of immune responses, or both depending on the pathogens.

Besides plant defense responses, the drought stress hormone ABA is also a common denominator known to regulate the expression of the gene families found upregulated in Mpfer-1 and Mpmri-1. In agreement, ~80% of upregulated genes in WT Marchantia thalli treated with 1 μM ABA (Jahan et al. 2019) were upregulated in untreated CWI-related mutants (Supplementary Fig. S8). This suggests that the MpFER/MpMRI module negatively regulates ABA signaling.

However, despite the increased transcript levels of MpLEA1 (Fig. 4c) and MpCYP707A (Fig. 6a), Mpmri-1 plants displayed WT-like responses to ABA and mannitol for growth inhibition assays (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Figs. S9 and S12b) and a weak hypersensitivity to mild salt stress (Supplementary Fig. S12a). This indicates that MpMRI is not involved in ABA-dependent thalli growth inhibition and that the misregulation of ABA-related genes in Mpmri-1 may be linked to a different, yet to be revealed, ABA function. In contrast, Mpfer-1 exhibited enhanced expression of ABA-related genes compared to WT plants, including those coding for ABA biosynthesis (MpNCED), an ABA receptor (MpPYL1), key positive regulators of ABA signaling (MpSnRK2A, MpABI3, MpMFT), and ABA-responsive genes MpLEA1, MpLEA1L, and MpDEHYDRIN (Figs. 4c and 6 and Supplementary Fig. S11). Interestingly, the genes of this group are also downregulated by ectopic expression of the A-type protein phosphatase 2C PP2C MpABI1A (Eklund et al. 2018), which suggests that MpFER and PP2C MpABI1A function together to negatively regulate ABA signaling. Consistently, we found that Mpfer plants were hypersensitive to ABA in thalli growth inhibition assays (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S9) but slightly hyposensitive to mild salt stress (Supplementary Fig. S12a).

In Arabidopsis, AtFER also inhibits ABA signaling, with Atfer-4 plants displaying hypersensitivity to ABA in seed germination, cotyledon greening, stomatal, and root growth assays (Yu et al. 2012, Chen et al. 2016, Wang et al. 2022). In root growth assays, AtRALF1 also negatively impacted ABA response (Chen et al. 2016). AtFER interacts with and activates the PP2C AtABI2, a key negative regulator of ABA. Conversely, AtABI2 dephosphorylates AtFER and produces a negative feedback on ABA-induced activation of AtFER (Chen et al. 2016). In agreement, integrated omics on Atfer-4 mutant plants showed that the GO term “response to ABA” (GO:0009737) was significantly enriched among genes and proteins with increased levels in Atfer-4 (Wang et al. 2022). Moreover, FER was reported to phosphorylate and destabilize ABI5, a transcription factor and positive regulator of ABA inhibition of cotyledon greening in seedlings (Wang et al. 2022).

Altogether, our findings indicate that FER’s function of negatively regulating ABA signaling, likely in cooperation with A-type PP2Cs, is conserved between bryophytes and flowering plants and probably was present in the common ancestor of land plants. Nonetheless, it remains possible that instead of a direct molecular action of FER on an ABA signaling component, cell wall defects in Arabidopsis and Marchantia feronia mutants somehow constitutively unlock ABA signaling, making the plants more prompt to respond to ABA. A detailed phenotyping of the cell wall chemical and physical properties of the Arabidopsis and Marchantia feronia mutants and their corresponding WT lines would most likely be very informative in this regard. Of interest, a recent study reports that xyloglucan content or accessibility in the cell wall was reduced in Mpfer-2 gemmae compared to WT ones (Muller et al. 2025). Muller and coworkers hypothesize that the MpFER/MpMRI pathway could regulate cell wall extensibility through xyloglucan levels and xyloglucan remodeling. In agreement, GO:0016762, related to xyloglucan:xyloglucosyl transferase activity, is significantly enriched among the downregulated genes of both Mpfer-1 and Mpmri-1 mutants (Supplementary Fig. S3a). Finally, while MpFER and MpMRI function together in maintaining CWI during rhizoid growth (Westermann et al. 2019) and cell wall extensibility (Muller et al. 2025), our results establish that they also have distinct functions in responses to abiotic stresses. It would thus be of interest to investigate in more detail the molecular signaling pathways or regulatory networks that differentiate MpFER and MpMRI functions. For example, in Arabidopsis, besides MRI, FER has many other known downstream regulators, such as some guanine exchange factors (GEFs), the ErbB3-binding protein 1 (EBP1), the transcription factor MYC2, and the RPM1-induced protein kinase (RIPK), (recently reviewed in Cheung 2024, Tang and Guo 2025) the orthologues of which could mediate MRI-independent FER responses in Marchantia. Finally, there are three RALFs in Marchantia that belong to the AtRALF1 clade of the RALF family and two LLGs that could also constitute MpFER coreceptors (Mecchia et al. 2022). Assessing whether they could also play a negative role in ABA signaling would provide fundamental insights into the molecular mechanisms linking ABA signaling and CWI.

Materials and Methods

Marchantia material and growth conditions

The M. polymorpha Tak-1 and Tak-2 ecotypes as well as the Mpfer-1 and Mpmri-1 T-DNA insertional mutant lines (Honkanen et al. 2016, Westermann et al. 2019) or the CRISPR-edited Mpfer-2 and Mpfer-3 mutant alleles (Mecchia et al. 2022) were cultivated as described previously (Westermann et al. 2019).

Transcriptomic analyses

Total RNA for RNA-seq analysis was extracted from four biological replicates of 3-week-old Tak-1, Mpfer-1, Tak-2, and Mpmri-1 thalli grown on cellophane membranes to preserve the rhizoids using TRI reagent (Ambion Life Technologies). Two micrograms of DNase I–treated RNA (RNA integrity number RIN > 7, OD260/280 = 1.8–2.1, OD260/230 > 1.5) for three of the four biological replicates was sent to the Cologne Center for Genomics (CCG) for paired-end 100 bp short-read sequencing. Raw sequencing data were imported into the Galaxy web server (Galaxy), and the read quality was assessed using the FastQC package. Adapter sequences were trimmed, and the reads were quality-filtered using the fastp package with default settings and enabling overrepresentation analysis. The transcript count was then determined from the resulting preprocessed FASTQ files using the KallistoQuant package with default settings and sequence-based bias correction (42 bootstraps). Finally, DEGs were determined using the DESeq2 package with default settings. An adjusted P-value ≤ 0.05 and log2(fold change) ≥ 1.5 or ≤−1.5 were set as the cutoff values to identify DEGs. All the transcripts were mapped to the M. polymorpha genome (MpTakv6.1) obtained from the MarpolBase website (https://marchantia.info) (Tanizawa et al. 2025). The RNA-seq data from this study are available at the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository under accession number GSE282327.

To visualize the robustness of our experimental design and assess the quality of the biological replicates used in our RNA-seq experiment, several analyses were performed. First, PCA plots were generated from the transcript counts of each individual replicate using the plotPCA package in Galaxy (Ramírez et al. 2016). Next, a list of DEGs and the corresponding transcript abundances from each replicate were extracted and corresponding heatmaps (Tak-2 vs Tak-1, Mpfer-1 vs Tak-1, and Mpmri-1 vs Tak-2) were generated using RStudio (RStudio Desktop – Posit) with the ggplot2 and pheatmap packages. From the DEG list, the log2(fold change) versus the −log10(P-value) was plotted with R studio and the ggplot2 package to generate volcano plots showing the magnitude and significance of DEGs. Next, we determined the number of common and unique transcripts by comparing the list of DEGs in Mpfer-1 versus Tak-1 to those in Mpmri-1 versus Tak-2. The significance of overlapping transcriptomic datasets was determined using the hypergeometric test from the R phyper function and the overlaps were visualized using area-proportional Euler diagrams with the EulerR package.

Among DEGs, gene families that were significantly enriched (false discovery rate, FDR < 0.01) were identified using the GenFam online tool (https://www.mandadilab.com/genfam/; Bedre and Mandadi 2019) using a Fisher’s exact test with Benjamini–Hochberg multiple testing correction.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed to identify overrepresented BPs and MFs, within our gene sets (e.g. DEGs). Prior to analysis, all the gene IDs were converted to version 7.1 from version 6.1 using the dedicated gene ID converter tool available on the marchantia.info platform (https://marchantia.info/utils/id_converter/). Functional enrichment analysis was then conducted using the MarpolBase Expression MBEX functional enrichment tool (Kawamura et al. 2022), accessible through the marchantia.info website (https://mbex.marchantia.info/enrichment) based on the recent MpTak_v6.1 genome version. For each gene set, MBEX was utilized to identify significantly enriched GO terms. The analysis was performed against the latest available M. polymorpha GO annotation database provided by the platform. Fisher’s exact test was employed to determine statistical significance, and P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the FDR. GO terms with an adjusted P-value (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Real-time quantitative PCR

One microgram of total RNA was reverse-transcribed (RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific). RT-qPCRs were carried out in a QuantStudio 5 System (ABI/Life Technologies) using 96-well plates (0.2 mL) and cover foil (Opti-Seal Optical Disposable Adhesive) from BIOplastics following the work of Stephan et al. (2019). Reaction mixtures of 10 μL were composed of 5 μL GoTaq RT-qPCR Master Mix (Promega), 0.2 μL of each primer (10 μM), 1 μL diluted cDNA, and 3.6 μL ddH2O. Amplification was carried out with the standard settings of the QuantStudio 5 System (50°C for 2 min, 95°C for 10 s, 40 cycles at 95°C for 15 s, and 60°C for 1 min, followed by 95°C for 15 s and a final dissociation curve from 60°C to 95°C). Analysis was performed using QuantStudio TM Design, Analysis Software version 1.4.1, and Excel 2019.

Sequences for primer design were taken from MarpolBase (Genome Database for M. polymorpha, https://marchantia.info/), and in silico sequence analysis was carried out with CLC DNA Workbench version 5.6.1. The primers were designed using GenScript Real-time PCR Primer Design (www.genscript.com) at an optimum Tm of 60 ± 2°C. All the amplicons showed a single band of the expected size in gel electrophoresis and a single peak in the melting curve. Average Cq values (y) and log10 values of three biological and three technical replicates of six serial dilutions of 1:5, 1:10, 1:20, 1:40, 1:80, and 1:160 (x) were used to calculate the slope of a regression line using the formula . The efficiency of primers was then calculated with . The correlation of the Cq values (R2) was calculated with the formula . Primers for the reference genes MpACTIN7 (MpACT7) and MpADENINE PHOSPHORIBOSYL TRANSFERASE 3 (MpAPT3) were described before Saint-Marcoux et al. 2015. Novel primers for genes of interest were accepted with an efficiency of 80%–120% and a correlation between −1 and −0.99 (Supplementary Table S1).

All the transcript quantities of the genes of interest represent the average of four biological and three technical replicates from RT-qPCRs of 1:5 diluted cDNA samples. Outliers in the raw data were removed using a two-sided Grubbs test at a significance level of 0.05. Normalization against the two reference genes MpACT7 and MpAPT3, was carried out using normalization factors, according to the geNorm manual (Vandesompele et al. 2002).

Proteomic analyses

For protein extraction and fractionation, four biological replicates of frozen thalli with their rhizoids (three thalli per replicate for Tak-1 and Tak-2 controls and five to six thalli for Mpfer-1 and Mpmri-1 smaller mutant plants) were disrupted in a Retsch Mixer Mill MM 400 with stainless steel beads for 5 min at 30 Hz. To the finely ground plant material 300 μL urea/DTT extraction buffer (8 M urea/5 mM DTT in 100 mM Tris, pH 8.5/protease inhibitor cocktail 2 and 3, Sigma, 20 μL/mL) was added, and samples were mixed and incubated for 30 min at room temperature (RT). After centrifugation for 10 min (15 000 × g) at room temperature, the supernatant was removed and transferred to a fresh tube; this procedure was repeated up to three times, until no visible pellet was retained. The protein concentration was determined using the Pierce 660 nm protein assay.

An aliquot of 50 μL of total protein extract was subjected to a chloroform–methanol precipitation (Wessel and Flügge 1984). In brief, 200 μL of methanol was added, and samples were vortexed. Next, 50 μL of chloroform was added and the samples were vortexed again. Finally, 150 μL of H2O was added and, after vortexing, the samples were centrifuged for 1 min at 14 000 × g. The upper layer was then removed, without disturbing the layer of precipitated protein. Two hundred microliters of methanol was added, and the samples were vortexed and centrifuged for 10 min at 18 000 × g to pellet the precipitate. After removal of the organic layer, the pellet was air-dried for 10 min and subjected to an in-solution digest.

After precipitation, samples were taken up in 25 μL 8 M urea/5 mM DTT (pH 8.5, 0.1 M Tris–HCl) and subjected to in-solution digestion using 0.5 μg trypsin (Marques et al. 2021). After overnight digestion, samples were acidified with 5 μL 50% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and split into BoxCar and library samples (Phookaew et al. 2024). For the library samples, 20 μL (5 μg) of each condition per replicate was mixed and submitted to strong cation exchange (SCX) fractionation as previously described (Phookaew et al. 2024). Samples in buffer A* (2% acetonitrile ACN, 0.1% TFA) were diluted to 0.1 μg/μL for measurement. The first fraction was discarded, and samples were measured in reverse order of elution.

For the BoxCar analysis, the remaining samples were desalted according to the StageTip protocol (Rappsilber et al. 2003) and eluted peptides were dried and then taken up in 10 μL A* buffer. The peptide concentration was determined by using the Nanodrop program “Protein A280,” which showed some unexpected variance especially for the mutant samples. The samples were then diluted 1:10 rather than to a specific concentration. Inspection of the resulting chromatograms showed similar profiles and intensities across samples, validating the approach.

For LC–MS/MS data acquisition, library samples were analyzed using an EASY-nLC 1200 (Thermo Fisher) coupled to a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher). Peptides were separated on 16 cm frit-less silica emitters (Nikkyo Technos Co., Ltd, 75 μm inner diameter), packed in-house with reversed-phase ReproSil-Pur C18 AQ 1.9 μm resin (Dr. Maisch). Instruments settings and gradients were used as described before (Phookaew et al. 2024). BoxCar samples were analyzed using the above-described setup and previously reported methods for BoxCar acquisition (Phookaew et al. 2024).

The MS data are available at the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD058089 (Perez-Riverol et al. 2022). Raw data were processed using MaxQuant software (version 1.6.3.4, MaxQuant) (Cox and Mann 2008) with label-free quantification (LFQ) and iBAQ enabled (Tyanova et al. 2016) using the settings recently described for BoxCar analysis (Marques et al. 2021).

MS/MS spectra were searched by the Andromeda search engine (MaxQuant) against a combined database containing the sequences from Marchantia Tak-1 and Tak-2 genotypes (MpTakv6.1) and sequences of 248 common contaminant proteins and decoy sequences. Note that of the n = 4233 proteins identified in the four biological replicates of the Tak-1 and Tak-2 strains, over 97% (4111) are present in both strains most likely because Tak-1 and Tak-2 genomes are very close (Tanizawa et al. 2025). Other settings were set to default or as previously described (Marques et al. 2021).

Statistical analysis of the MaxLFQ values was carried out using Perseus (version 1.5.8.5, MaxQuant) as described previously (Pruitt et al. 2021). Quantified proteins were filtered for reverse hits and hits “only identified by site” and MaxLFQ values were log2-transformed. After grouping samples by condition, only those proteins that had been identified at least three times in at least one of the conditions were retained for the subsequent analysis. Two-sample t-tests were performed using a permutation-based FDR of 5%. Volcano plots were generated in RStudio with the ggplot2 package (Supplementary Fig. S1b and Figs. 2b and 3b).

ABA-, NaCl-, and mannitol-related responses in Marchantia

For growth quantification, images of at least 20 individual thalli grown for 7 or 14 days on square Petri dishes containing agar-solidified ½ strength Gamborg’s B5 medium, pH 5.7, supplemented with 0, 1, or 10 μM ABA (A1049; Sigma-Aldrich), 0, 25, 50, and 100 mM of NaCl (31434; Sigma-Aldrich), or 0, 100, and 200 mM d-mannitol (M9647; Sigma-Aldrich) were collected using a Scanner Epson Perfection V850 Pro. The thallus surface area was determined using classical segmentation by color thresholding with the Fiji-ImageJ v1.46 program (Schindelin et al. 2012). Pixel areas were calibrated to square centimeters using a scale bar to obtain the total surface area per individual thallus, and identical segmentation parameters were applied to all the images. The mean thallus area of mock-treated plants was set to 100% and the relative area was calculated as the percentage of the mean thallus area of treated plants to that of mock-treated plants. Data are presented as (i) violin plots, showing individual thallus areas (cm2) and (ii) bar charts, depicting the relative area to mock (%). Graph design and statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Prism Software Inc.). Statistically significant differences were determined by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s method with a significance level of P ≤ 0.05.

Concerning ABA-related RT-qPCR, total RNA extraction and reverse transcription were made according to (Giordano et al. 2022). The primers used for PCR experiments are listed in Supplementary Table S1. All the data were from three independent biological replicates, each involving three technical replicates. Reference genes were MpACT7 and MpAPT3, as indicated in the figure legends. Data from the AriaMx v1.0.8 real-time PCR machine (Agilent Technologies) were quantified with the comparative delta-Ct method, using the individual primer efficiency–corrected calculation (Rao et al. 2013). Statistically significant differences for RT-qPCR data were determined by two-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s method with a significance level of P ≤ 0.05.
