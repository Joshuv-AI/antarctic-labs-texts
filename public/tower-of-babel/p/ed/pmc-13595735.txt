Rethinking qualitative inquiry in implementation science: clarifying purpose, design, and rigor
Authors: Xuejun Yin, Hongling Chu, Pengpeng Ye, Hueiming Liu, Maoyi Tian, Ruitai Shao, Lijing Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Qualitative methods are now central to implementation science, but they are used to do different kinds of work that are not always clearly distinguished. Some studies are designed to develop contextual or conceptual understanding, while others are expected to inform near-term decisions about rollout, adaptation, or implementation strategies. When these differences remain implicit, qualitative studies may be designed and evaluated against expectations they were never intended to meet, particularly around the role of theory, openness to unanticipated findings, and what counts as rigor. In this debate paper, we argue for a more explicit way of thinking about qualitative inquiry in implementation science. Rather than treating qualitative methods as a single approach, we suggest they are being configured in different ways in response to study purpose, time and resource constraints, the state of knowledge about the phenomenon or context, and stakeholder expectations. To make these differences visible, we propose a positioning framework that locates qualitative inquiry along a continuum of three orientations: Generative, Pragmatic, and Action-oriented. Generative inquiry prioritizes contextual depth and conceptual development; Action-oriented inquiry is organized to produce timely, decision-relevant findings; Pragmatic inquiry occupies the space between these poles. We then introduce a positioning guide and an accompanying table to show how these orientations shape key aspects of qualitative design, including question formulation, the role of theory, sampling, data collection, analysis, and reporting. The contribution of this paper is a framework for describing what qualitative studies in implementation science are trying to produce and how they should be assessed. Qualitative rigor cannot be reduced to a single standard when studies are making different kinds of claims. Making methodological positioning more explicit may help reduce mismatched expectations in study design and peer review, and support more consistent judgments about the contribution of qualitative work in implementation science.

Background

Qualitative research encompasses a range of methodological traditions, many of which have been associated with inductive, interpretive, and constructivist approaches to understanding how people make sense of experience within social, organizational, and cultural contexts [1, 2]. Across these traditions, knowledge is often treated not as fixed and fully discoverable, but as situated and shaped through interaction among participants, researchers, and settings [1, 3]. Influential qualitative approaches have therefore tended to emphasize contextual sensitivity, reflexivity, openness to unanticipated findings, and close attention to meaning, process, and complexity [4]. These commitments have made qualitative inquiry especially valuable for examining phenomena that are difficult to reduce to predefined variables or standardized measures [2, 5].

This orientation is reflected in well-established qualitative traditions such as phenomenology, ethnography, grounded theory, and other interpretive approaches [1, 4]. Although these methodologies differ in epistemological assumptions and approaches to analysis, many share an interest in emergence, iterative data collection and analysis, and close engagement with context [3]. Qualitative rigor within these traditions has often been linked to methodological coherence, depth of engagement, reflexive awareness, and transparent interpretation rather than standardization alone [6]. Such approaches have been used to generate important insights into lived experience, organizational life, social processes, and the mechanisms through which practices acquire meaning in context [5]. They remain an important reference point for thinking about what qualitative inquiry can contribute, even if they do not define the only legitimate use of qualitative methods.

In implementation science, qualitative methods have become increasingly central because they can address questions that quantitative indicators often leave unresolved [7, 8]. They can help explain why an intervention was acceptable but not adopted, why fidelity varied across sites, how frontline actors interpreted and adapted a strategy, or why implementation gains were not sustained over time [7, 9]. Qualitative inquiry can also illuminate context, organizational dynamics, stakeholder priorities, and the social mechanisms through which implementation unfolds [7, 10]. It is one of the field’s main ways of making implementation visible.

At the same time, qualitative methods in implementation science are often mobilized for purposes that differ from more exploratory or theory-generating forms of inquiry. Implementation studies are frequently conducted under conditions shaped by policy timelines, funding constraints, health system priorities, pre-existing theoretical frameworks, and the need to generate findings that are useful for decision-making [11]. Within these conditions, qualitative methods may be used to identify barriers and facilitators, examine the fit of an intervention within a local context, assess the applicability of a framework, explain heterogeneity in implementation outcomes, support rapid-cycle refinement of interventions or implementation strategies, or complement quantitative findings in mixed methods designs [9, 12]. These are legitimate uses of qualitative research. But they are not neutral ones. They rely on different assumptions about sampling, design flexibility, openness to unexpected findings, the role of theory, and what counts as a sufficient qualitative contribution [13, 14]. In implementation science, frameworks such as CFIR or TDF can be used productively to organize inquiry. They can also become templates. When that happens, interview guides become overly comprehensive, coding becomes an exercise in fitting data to pre-existing constructs, and findings are fragmented into lists of barriers and facilitators that are easier to categorize than to interpret. For example, a study may produce a comprehensive inventory of determinants across framework domains while leaving less room to examine how those factors interact, which contradictions matter, or why implementation unfolds differently across sites. This is not an argument against framework-guided qualitative research. It is a caution against treating frameworks as substitutes for inquiry.

That distinction matters because qualitative methods in implementation science are now being used for multiple purposes, but those purposes are not always well articulated or consistently assessed [7]. Some studies remain closely aligned with more exploratory and interpretive traditions, prioritizing contextual depth, emergence, and conceptual development [15, 16]. Others seek to balance interpretive sensitivity with time-bounded, theory-informed, or decision-oriented aims [17]. Still others use highly structured and framework-guided qualitative designs to answer focused implementation questions under substantial practical constraints [18]. The evaluative problem begins when all three are discussed as though they were trying to produce the same kind of knowledge.

This has direct consequences for how studies are reviewed and funded [19]. A rapid CFIR-guided study may be criticized for lacking inductive depth even when theory generation was never its purpose. An exploratory study may be pushed toward framework use simply because implementation science reviewers expect to see one. In both cases, the weakness lies less in the methods themselves than in the mismatch between what the study is trying to do and the standards being applied to it [6, 20]. We think this mismatch has become common enough in implementation science to require a more disciplined language for describing qualitative purpose, design, and contribution.

This debate paper argues for a more pluralist way of thinking about qualitative inquiry in implementation science. Our aim is not to defend inductive work against deductive or framework-guided approaches, nor to legitimize superficial qualitative work in the name of pragmatism. It is to show that different forms of qualitative inquiry are doing different kinds of work and should be designed and assessed accordingly. We therefore propose a three-part positioning framework: Generative, Pragmatic, and Action-oriented, together with a positioning guide linking qualitative design choices to study purpose, time and resource constraints, stakeholder expectations, and the state of knowledge about the phenomenon or context under study. The contribution is modest but practical: a clearer way to describe what a qualitative study in implementation science is trying to produce, and how it should be evaluated.

A positioning framework for qualitative inquiry in implementation science

We propose a framework that locates qualitative inquiry in implementation science along a continuum of three broad orientations: Generative, Pragmatic, and Action-oriented. These orientations are not fixed categories, and they are not ranked in methodological value. They are best understood as different ways of configuring qualitative inquiry in relation to what a study is trying to produce, how quickly findings are needed, how well the context is already understood, and what kinds of demands surround the work [7].

At one end of the continuum, Generative inquiry is oriented toward exploration, contextual understanding, and conceptual or theoretical development. At the other end, Action-oriented inquiry is organized to produce focused, timely, decision-relevant findings in settings where implementation questions are already bounded by operational needs. Pragmatic inquiry occupies the space between these poles. It seeks to preserve interpretive value while also producing findings that can inform implementation decisions, often through some combination of framework-guided structure and openness to emergent insight.

What distinguishes these orientations is not the use of wholly different methods, but how familiar qualitative methods are configured across the research process. The same interview, observation, or coding strategy can serve different ends depending on whether the study is trying to generate conceptual insight, inform rollout and local strategy refinement, or do some combination of both [21]. This is the point at which many implementation studies become difficult to assess: methods are described, but the qualitative work those methods are meant to do is not always clearly stated [19].

The framework is intended to support prospective methodological positioning, not retrospective justification. It does not tell researchers which approach is “correct.” It offers a clearer vocabulary for describing how a qualitative study is being configured and what kind of contribution it is positioned to make. We pair the framework with a positioning guide organized around four dimensions: the primary purpose of the study, including the intended use of findings; time and resource constraints; the state of knowledge about the phenomenon or context under study; and stakeholder expectations and demands. The sections that follow elaborate the three orientations before turning to that positioning guide.

The three orientations of qualitative inquiry in implementation science

Generative orientation

The Generative orientation is primarily concerned with developing in-depth, contextually grounded understanding and, in some cases, generating new conceptual or theoretical insight into implementation processes. It is most useful when the phenomenon under study is still poorly understood, when local realities are likely to exceed what existing implementation frameworks can capture, or when the study aims to examine how meaning, practice, and context interact in a complex setting. Qualitative studies of healthcare practice and implementation have shown how knowledge, decision-making, and change are shaped through social interaction, tacit understanding, and local context rather than through formal frameworks alone [22]. Such work has also challenged simplified framings of implementation as a set of discrete “barriers” and “facilitators,” instead highlighting the dynamic, interpretive, and context-dependent nature of change processes [23].

In this orientation, qualitative inquiry is configured to preserve openness to unexpected findings. Research questions are often broad and may shift as understanding develops. Sampling is typically purposive and adaptive, with recruitment shaped by emerging insights, outlying cases, and variation across settings or participants. In studies of organizational and system-level change, researchers have used sampling strategies that deliberately compare sites with different implementation trajectories in order to understand how context shapes practice. For example, qualitative studies conducted across multiple countries or health systems have used purposive sampling to capture variation in implementation experiences and identify both shared and context-specific influences on care delivery [24]. Data collection may involve repeated interviews, observation, or more sustained engagement with implementation settings [25]. Analysis is predominantly inductive, sometimes abductive, and may draw on techniques such as coding, memo-writing, or iterative comparison to trace processes, identify mechanisms, and develop concepts [26]. Theory may be generated, extended, or critically reworked through this analytic process, whether in relation to implementation frameworks, broader social theory, or concepts grounded in the data themselves.

The main strength of this orientation is its capacity to reveal aspects of implementation that are not easily visible through predefined constructs: how staff make sense of new practices, how routines shift during rollout, why the same strategy is taken up differently across sites, or how local histories shape response to change. Its limitation is practical. Generative inquiry is harder to sustain when timelines are compressed, when the qualitative component is expected to feed immediate adaptation of interventions or implementation strategies, or when the primary demand is for concise answers to already bounded implementation questions.

Pragmatic orientation

The Pragmatic orientation is designed to balance interpretive depth with applied usefulness. It is especially valuable when qualitative inquiry is expected both to inform implementation decisions and to contribute to broader understanding, often under moderate constraints on time, scope, or resources. Qualitative implementation studies commonly operate in this space, where the aim is to generate findings that are both analytically meaningful and directly useful for improving practice across real-world settings.

In this orientation, qualitative methods are configured to combine structure with flexibility. Research questions are more focused than in generative work, but they still allow room for unexpected issues to emerge. For example, qualitative studies examining implementation of vaccination or service delivery programs often ask how known barriers and facilitators manifest in specific contexts while remaining open to locally emergent influences on uptake and delivery [27]. Studies often draw on implementation frameworks such as CFIR, TDF, or NPT to shape interview guides, coding, or reporting, while retaining space for findings that fall outside those constructs [27–29]. Sampling is purposive but usually bounded by project timelines, sites, or predefined stakeholder groups [27]. Data collection is often time-limited, though it may still include multiple data sources or modest triangulation [30]. Analysis commonly combines deductive and inductive strategies, allowing the study to speak both to existing implementation concepts and to context-specific findings. For instance, studies of vaccination program implementation have used framework-guided coding to organize data while also identifying emergent themes that inform locally tailored implementation strategies [27]. This combination allows qualitative findings to both engage with established implementation constructs and remain responsive to context.

This orientation is often where implementation teams do their most useful qualitative work. It can produce findings that matter to practice without reducing the study to a rapid checklist exercise. Its vulnerability is drift. Frameworks can help organize inquiry, but they can also start to organize it too tightly. Empirical studies have noted that extensive framework constructs can lead to overly structured interview guides and fragmented coding if not applied reflexively [14]. Interview guides may become overfull, coding becomes fragmented across dozens of constructs, and the analysis loses a clear account of what actually matters in the setting. Unless authors state clearly how framework-guided and inductive elements are being combined, the study can become difficult to read and difficult to assess.

Action-oriented orientation

The Action-oriented orientation is designed to produce focused, timely, decision-relevant findings that can directly inform implementation practice, policy, or program adaptation. It is most appropriate when qualitative input is needed quickly, when the implementation context is already reasonably well understood, or when the study is intended to answer a clearly bounded operational question. This form of qualitative inquiry is increasingly common in implementation research conducted alongside programs, trials, or policy initiatives where findings are expected to inform ongoing decision-making in real time [13, 18, 31].

In this orientation, qualitative methods are configured for efficiency and immediate use. Research questions are narrowly defined and closely linked to bounded implementation problems, such as identifying barriers to uptake, informing local strategy refinement, or explaining short-term variation in delivery across sites. For example, qualitative studies conducted to support national or program-level interventions often focus on stakeholder perspectives to inform implementation strategies and scale-up decisions within a defined policy context [32]. Study designs are often organized around existing frameworks to support clarity, comparability, and rapid synthesis. For example, rapid assessment studies evaluating implementation of complex interventions have used framework-informed field guides and structured analytic tools to generate comparable, site-level findings within constrained timelines [33]. Sampling is typically predefined and targeted toward stakeholder groups most relevant to the decision at hand. In applied implementation studies, this often involves selecting key informants such as program implementers, policymakers, or frontline providers who can directly inform specific operational questions. Data collection relies on efficient methods such as rapid interviews, focused site visits, or structured observations. In rapid assessment studies of implementation in clinical settings, data have been collected through brief, intensive site visits combined with participatory engagement and rapid synthesis across multiple data sources [33]. For instance, rapid qualitative implementation studies have employed team-based analytic processes using structured charts derived from implementation frameworks to organize data, triangulate findings, and produce actionable summaries within short timeframes [32, 33].

The strength of this orientation is obvious: it can generate findings quickly enough to inform real implementation decisions. It is often the right choice when teams need to understand why fidelity is dropping, why one site is lagging behind another, or how to adapt a strategy before the next phase of rollout. Its limitation is reduced capacity to pursue unexpected findings. Complexity may be narrowed, contradictions may receive less attention, and opportunities for conceptual development may be limited. Action-oriented work is legitimate qualitative inquiry, but it is at its strongest when the scope of its claims matches the speed and boundedness of its design.

Illustrative examples of qualitative orientations in implementation science

1. Generative orientation

Armstrong et al.’s multi-country study of hip fracture care across five low- and middle-income countries illustrates a more generative orientation [24]. The study prioritized understanding how implementation processes were shaped by diverse health system, cultural, and organizational contexts, with less emphasis on producing immediate operational recommendations.

Key features:

• Evolving study focus: Research questions were refined iteratively as insights developed across settings.

• Contextually grounded sampling: Data collection spanned multiple sites to capture variation in implementation experiences.

• Inductive analytic approach: Analysis emphasized identifying patterns, contrasts, and underlying mechanisms across contexts.

• Comparative, interpretive reporting: Findings preserved differences and tensions across settings rather than reducing them to standardized categories.

2. Pragmatic orientation

Yan et al.’s study of school-located influenza vaccination in Beijing reflects a pragmatic orientation [27]. The study aimed to inform an upcoming vaccination program while also examining how implementation challenges unfolded in a specific context. The design combined framework-based structure with attention to context-specific insights.

Key features:

• Framework-informed but flexible design: CFIR guided data collection and analysis, while allowing findings that extended beyond predefined domains.

• Purposive and bounded sampling: Recruitment targeted relevant stakeholders within practical constraints, while still capturing variation in perspectives.

• Combined analytic approach: Deductive coding using CFIR was complemented by inductive analysis to identify context-specific themes.

• Dual-purpose reporting: Results were presented both in relation to implementation constructs and as narrative accounts to preserve contextual detail.

3. Action-oriented orientation

Sanuade et al.’s evaluation of Nigeria’s sodium reduction program illustrates an action-oriented use of qualitative methods [32]. The study was conducted in a policy context where stakeholders required timely, decision-relevant input to support program scale-up. The design therefore emphasized clarity, feasibility, and rapid synthesis.

Key features:

• Framework-informed design: Research questions and data collection were informed by CFIR to support alignment with implementation planning needs.

• Targeted sampling: Participants were selected from key stakeholder groups (e.g., regulators, industry, healthcare providers) most relevant to the policy questions.

• Structured analytic approach: Analysis combined framework-guided coding with focused synthesis to identify actionable priorities.

• Decision-oriented reporting: Findings were organized to inform implementation strategies, linking identified barriers and enablers to practical recommendations.

Using the framework as a positioning guide

The orientations described above do not determine design choices on their own. In practice, qualitative studies in implementation science are shaped by a set of recurring pressures that influence where they sit along the continuum [21]. The framework is meant to support prospective methodological positioning, not retrospective justification. Its purpose is not to sort studies into fixed types or prescribe a single correct design, but to help researchers, reviewers, and editors describe more clearly what a study is trying to do and whether its design matches that task.

One of the strongest influences is what the study is being asked to produce. Some qualitative work is designed to develop conceptual or theoretical understanding [34]. Other studies are commissioned to inform near-term decisions: how to adjust a strategy, why uptake is uneven across sites, or how to refine an intervention during rollout [35]. Many studies attempt to do both [36]. These differences shape not only the questions asked, but also how theory or frameworks are used and how much room remains for unexpected findings or reinterpretation.

A second influence is time and resource constraints. When timelines are more flexible, qualitative inquiry can unfold iteratively, with sampling, data collection, and analysis informing one another over time [37]. Under tighter constraints, studies often move toward predefined sampling frames, more structured interview guides, and faster analytic approaches [38]. The issue here is not whether one approach is more rigorous than the other, but what kind of contribution can credibly be produced within the available conditions. Considerations of sample adequacy and analytic completeness also need to be understood in those terms. While saturation is often invoked as a general marker of rigor, recent work in qualitative health research has argued for data adequacy as a more appropriate framing, with adequacy depending on study aims, context, data richness, and practical constraints (e.g., the Q-FORS framework for operationalizing respondent sampling) [39].

Another influence is how much is already known about the phenomenon or setting. When implementation processes remain unclear, when local dynamics are likely to exceed what current frameworks can capture, or when key assumptions are still unstable, more open-ended inquiry becomes necessary [22]. When the task is narrower, for example, identifying barriers, explaining fidelity variation, or supporting adaptation of an intervention or implementation strategy, more structured approaches may be justified [36]. A study exploring how staff make sense of a new practice is doing different work from one examining why a program stalled at a particular phase of implementation.

Stakeholder expectations also shape qualitative design, often more than is acknowledged in published methods sections. Some projects allow time for exploratory work and can tolerate ambiguity in the findings [22]. Others require concise, decision-ready outputs within a limited window, particularly in implementation settings where rapid qualitative approaches inform ongoing program decisions [33]. These expectations affect how interview guides are built, how findings are summarized, and how much detail and nuance are retained in reporting. In our view, particularly in time-sensitive and policy-oriented implementation contexts, qualitative studies are often effectively positioned during study design and early planning, before data collection is even complete.

These factors do not operate independently, and they do not lead to a single correct methodological choice. A study may be conceptually ambitious but time-limited, or highly decision-oriented while still retaining some inductive openness. The value of the positioning guide is that it makes these constraints visible early enough to inform design, rather than leaving them to surface later as problems in analysis or peer review. Seen this way, the framework is less a classification scheme than a language for articulation. It provides a way to describe what a qualitative study is trying to produce, what kind of contribution it can reasonably make, and how it should be assessed. That matters not only for study design, but also for review, editorial judgment, and funding decisions, where expectations about qualitative work are often left implicit. Many disagreements about qualitative rigor in implementation science are, at their core, disagreements about these questions [19].

Conclusion

Qualitative methods are now central to implementation science, but they are being used to do different kinds of work that are not always clearly distinguished. The challenge is not a lack of methodological guidance, nor a divide between qualitative traditions, but a lack of clarity about how qualitative inquiry is positioned in relation to study purpose, constraints, and expected contributions. We have proposed a framework that situates qualitative inquiry along a continuum of generative, pragmatic, and action-oriented orientations, together with a positioning guide highlighting four recurring pressures that shape design: study purpose, time and resource constraints, the state of knowledge about the phenomenon or context, and stakeholder expectations. The aim is not to prescribe a preferred model of qualitative research, but to offer a clearer way of describing differences that are already present in practice.

A central implication is that qualitative rigor cannot be understood as adherence to a single standard. Studies designed to develop conceptual understanding, those intended to inform rollout and adaptation decisions, and those that attempt to do both are not making the same kind of claim. Evaluating qualitative work in implementation science therefore requires attention to how methods are aligned with what the study is trying to produce. When this positioning remains implicit, studies are more likely to be misunderstood, assessed against inappropriate expectations, or pushed toward methodological choices that weaken the inquiry. Greater clarity about positioning can support better study design and more consistent evaluation. Implementation science does not need to resolve the tensions between different forms of qualitative inquiry, but it does need to work with them more deliberately and assess them on their own terms.
