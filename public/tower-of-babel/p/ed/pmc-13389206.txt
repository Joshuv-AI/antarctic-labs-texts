High altitude limits the biogeography of common rats but not house mice
Authors: Christian Arias-Reyes, Favio Carvajal-Rodriguez, Jorge Soliz, Vincent Joseph
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Common rats (Rattus rattus and Rattus norvegicus) and the house mouse (Mus musculus) are globally distributed synanthropic rodents, yet their tolerance to high altitude has never been assessed at a global scale. We combined worldwide occurrence records with climatic and elevational data to compare their observed and potential altitudinal ranges. Using more than 200,000 curated Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) records, we assigned elevation to each occurrence and built MaxEnt species distribution models incorporating elevation and four bioclimatic variables. Rats and mice overlapped broadly at low and mid elevations; however, both rat species showed a sharp and consistent decline in occurrence above ~ 2,500 m. Only 2.3–2.7% of rat records occurred above this threshold, compared with ~ 10% for M. musculus. Species distribution models corroborated this pattern, predicting high-altitude regions, including the Andean and Tibetan plateaus, and major mountain ranges in North America and Asia, as largely unsuitable for rats but suitable for mice. Minimum and maximum temperature were the strongest predictors of habitat suitability for all species, yet elevation exerted a markedly stronger negative effect on rats than on mice. These findings identify elevation as a major global constraint on the biogeography of common rats but not house mice, likely reflecting species-specific physiological limits related to hypoxia tolerance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53527-y.

Introduction

Common rats (Rattus rattus and Rattus norvegicus) and the house mouse (Mus musculus) are the most widespread mammals on Earth due to their long-standing commensal relationship with humans. Their global dispersion has been facilitated by agriculture, trade, and urbanization, which have allowed these rodents to cross otherwise prohibitive barriers1–13. Yet even broadly adaptable, synanthropes are ultimately constrained by the fundamental ecological conditions of the regions they colonize. Among these conditions, climatic factors such as temperature regime, precipitation patterns, seasonal variability, and geographic relief play dominant roles in determining mammalian distribution, as they shape resource availability, energetic demands, and reproductive success14. For small mammals, whose metabolic rates and thermal budgets are tightly constrained by environmental conditions, large-scale climatic factors can impose strong limits on population persistence15,16. Seasonal variation in temperature and precipitation shapes vegetation structure, food availability, and habitat stability, while extreme or unpredictable conditions may hinder the establishment of new populations14,17. High-altitude environments combine several of these constraints simultaneously, i.e., lower temperatures, drier air, and a diminished oxygen availability due to the decreased pO215, creating challenging settings in which only species with sufficient physiological and ecological plasticity can persist. Although altitude often correlates with multiple climatic variables, making its effects difficult to isolate, patterns of occurrence in high-altitude regions can reveal where environmental pressures surpass the limits of altitude tolerance. Previous ecological reports and scattered field observations have hinted that common rats may be less prevalent at higher elevations compared to house mice, but such suggestions have remained largely speculative and geographically restricted19,20. Interestingly, observations from our own physiological research provide independent clues pointing in the same direction. Laboratory studies have shown that rats and mice exhibit distinct responses when exposed to environmental hypoxia, with rats often developing energetically costly or maladaptive traits and mice displaying more beneficial adjustments19,21–27. While these controlled experiments do not directly predict distributional outcomes in nature, they raise the possibility that climatic and ecological stresses encountered at high altitudes might differentially challenge these species. Importantly, rats and mice are the most commonly used mammal models in biomedical research, yet growing evidence shows they often respond differently to identical environmental, pharmacological, or genetic manipulations. Such species-specific variability can lead to divergent experimental outcomes, with important consequences for data interpretation and translational relevance28. Such physiological discrepancies motivated us to evaluate, at a global scale, whether patterns of distribution are consistent with these underlying differences. Here, we test the hypothesis that high altitude constitutes a major limiting factor for the dispersal of common rats but not for house mice. To do so, we combine extensive global occurrence records with climatic and elevational data to (1) quantify the observed global altitudinal range of R. rattus, R. norvegicus, and M. musculus; and (2) generate species distribution models (SDMs) to estimate the potential suitability of high-altitude environments for each species. By integrating these approaches, we aim to clarify the role of elevation in shaping the biogeography of these globally important synanthropic rodents and to reassess the widely held assumption that both rats and mice accompany humans into all inhabited environments.

Results

Common rats are rare at high altitudes compared to the house mouse

We retrieved geographical coordinates data of worldwide reported occurrences of common rats (Rattus rattus and Rattus norvegicus) and house mice (Mus musculus) from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF). After depurating registers with incorrect (e.g., corresponding to water bodies) or missing geographical information, we consolidated a final database of 40,376 occurrence points for R. rattus, 65,045 for R. norvegicus, and 100,353 for M. musculus. Using these data, we created world maps showing the current confirmed presence of each of these three species (Fig. 1). Confirmed-presence datapoints in the five continents proved the cosmopolitan distribution of common rats and house mice. Noticeably, a greater density of records in North America and Europe was evident, likely due to the larger input of data to GBIF from scientific collections in these countries. Also, large wild ecosystems like deserts, rainforests, and frozen lands exhibited virtually no occurrence records for any of the studied species. Initial analysis revealed that the distributions of these three species differ from one another in specific regions. R. rattus spreads more extensively than R. norvegicus, as its presence has been reported regularly in the tropical region (i.e., Central and Southern Africa, Central and South America, and Southern Asia) and Australia (Fig. 1A), while R. norvegicus is scarcely registered below the equator (Fig. 1B). Contrastingly, house mice are commonly reported in central-eastern Asia, the mid and southwest of the United States, and central and western Australia, where either R. rattus (present in the Midwest of the United States), R. norvegicus (present in central-eastern Asia), or both rat species are absent. Furthermore, a noticeably greater presence of house mice compared to common rats is manifest in the surroundings of multiple high mountain ranges across the world. As shown in Fig. 1C, M. musculus occurrence points densely populate the vicinities of the Drakensberg mountains, Ethiopian highlands, and Atlas mountains in Africa; the Andes, the Central American and Sierra Madre systems, the Pacific and Rocky Mountains, and Appalachians in America; the Caucasus, Hejaz, Himalayas, Hindukush, Karakoram, Khingan, Kolyma, Kunlun, Sulaiman, Tianshan, Yablonoi, and Zagros mountains in Asia; the Great Dividing Range in Australia; and the Alps, Apennine, Carpathians, Pyrenees, and Scandinavian mountains in Europe.

To directly test the hypothesis that common rats are less present than house mice in mountainous settings, we sought to determine whether that is reflected in narrower altitudinal niches for both species of rats compared to M. musculus. We did this by crossing the geographical data with WORLDCLIM’s digital elevation model29 to obtain the geographical elevation (altitude) for each record of occurrence. This new altitude information served to determine the confirmed presence global altitudinal range for rats and mice. We calculated an altitude range of ~ 4,400 m for R. rattus based on reports of this species at altitudes as low as -414 m (Qumran National Park, Israel) and up to 4,030 m (Orizaba peak, Mexico), while R. norvegicus scatters from sea level up to 3,141 m (San Miguel Ajusco, Mexico). On the other hand, the presence of house mice extends along a larger altitudinal span, ranging from − 400 m at Qumran National Park in Israel up to 5,500 m in Sagarmatha National Park, Nepal, suggesting an altitudinal niche of roughly 6,000 m for this species. Noteworthy, 2.7% (1,090 records) of R. rattus occurrence points and 2.3% (1,496 records) of R. norvegicus were placed above 2,500 m, a well-accepted threshold defining high altitude, while around 10% (10,035 records) M. musculus occurrences were registered above this elevation (Fig. 2).

Species distribution modelling supports a limited presence of common rats at high altitudes

Occurrence data can only confirm the presence of a certain species in a determined place; however, it does not reflect its full geographical distribution and may underestimate its actual environmental tolerance. As we were interested in investigating the potential distribution of common rats and house mice at a global scale, we used species distribution modelling (SDM) to combine the geographical data of occurrence with bioclimatic information to generate relative habitat suitability maps that predict global distribution for R. rattus, R. norvegicus, and M. musculus. We selected altitude and five bioclimatic variables (maximum temperature of the warmest month, minimum temperature of the coldest month, precipitation of the wettest month, precipitation of the driest month, and precipitation seasonality) as input variables and ran our models using the MaxEnt algorithm. Our three models scored high AUC values (R. rattus = 0.92, R. norvegicus = 0.95, M. musculus = 0.91), indicating good fit between collection data and predicted suitability. In agreement with the occurrence data, our SDM maps predict high overlap in the distribution of rats and mice in the east and west coasts of North America, south-eastern South America, southern Africa, the southeast regions of Asia and Australia, and all across Europe. Opposingly, the most northern lands in America and Asia are expected to be poorly suitable or unsuitable habitats for these rodents. The models also support different distributions for both species of rats. R. rattus is expectedly well spread in the global tropics and in the southern hemisphere, but unlikely to be present in the most northern parts of North America and north of the Himalayas in Asia (Fig. 3A). On the other hand, R. norvegicus appears to be limited to subtropical and temperate latitudes (Fig. 3B). For both rat species, the deserts seem to represent mainly a limiting factor. And, similarly, apart from the European ranges, they are predicted to be scarce if not absent in highlands. In contrast to common rats, high presence of house mice is forecasted in northern Africa, the Middle North America, the Middle East, and Central Asia. Moreover, our model anticipates a significant probability of presence of M. musculus in elevated areas like the African Central, Andean, Colorado, and Tibetan (moderately) plateaus, and the Iranian highlands, as well as in the surroundings of the highest mountain ranges, including the Andes, Rocky Mountains, Ethiopian highlands, Himalayas, Caucasus mountains, and Alps (Fig. 3C).

In-depth analysis of the contribution made by each of the input variables to the model maps shows that more than 70% of the potential distribution of the three species is explained by extreme (minimum and maximum) temperatures. Precipitation of the wettest month, precipitation seasonality, and precipitation of the driest month, in that order, represent the remaining environmental variability. The contribution of altitude to the modeled distribution is less than 10% in all cases; however, it was 1.3-fold and 2.2-fold more important for the distribution of R. rattus and R. norvegicus, respectively, than for M. musculus, insinuating a more important restrictive impact on rats than mice (Table 1).

Discussion

Results of this work show that common rats are largely absent from high-altitude environments, whereas house mice regularly occupy them. Despite broad global overlap at lower elevations, the two rat species show a sharp decline in presence above ~ 2,500 m, a threshold at which M. musculus continues to appear frequently. This elevational asymmetry is evident across continents (spanning the Andes, Tibetan Plateau, Central African Highlands, and major mountain systems in North America, Asia, and Europe) and is consistently supported by both global occurrence records and species distribution models. Although extreme temperatures remain the dominant climatic drivers for all three species, only rats exhibit a negative association with elevation, highlighting altitude as a major biogeographic barrier for R. rattus and R. norvegicus but not for M. musculus.

Physical and ecological constraints for distribution

Our results show that the distributions of R. rattus, R. norvegicus, and M. musculus are primarily limited by extremes in low and high temperatures. This is unsurprising, as these climatic parameters govern environmental stability and appear to constrain small rodent dispersal more strongly than extreme or season variations in precipitation or altitude alone30,31. Such an effect is easily anticipated for wildlife, but it also concerns human commensals such as common rats and the house mouse. As yearly fluctuations in temperature and precipitation determine the productivity and structure of habitats utilized by humans, these rodents face abrupt alterations in resource availability in both agricultural and urban environments that ultimately threaten their ability to establish stable populations. For instance, crops undergo seasonal variation between superabundance and complete scarcity. While in cities, food waste has irregular patterns of obtainability and can be unpredictably and rapidly depleted at a local level14. Common rats and house mice are synanthropic and not anthropodependent, meaning that they are not exclusively bound to human settlements but also live in natural habitats. Thus, they can profit from both anthropogenic and wild resources, although they are also liable to threats from both environments14. Despite such ecological flexibility, rats and mice greatly benefit from human food and shelter and have generally dispersed by following us along our colonization pathways2,3,12,32,33. Interestingly, and contrary to classical assumptions2,12, the present data confirm anecdotal evidence that rats struggle to establish themselves together with humans at high altitudes, whereas mice have done it successfully. In contrast to sharp or periodic seasonal changes, altitude acts as both a constant geographic and physiological barrier, imposing a greater biogeographic constraint. In mountainous habitats, elevational gradients generate temperature and humidity declines and create challenging transitional ecosystems. It has been estimated that per 100 m gain in elevation, the mean atmospheric temperature decreases by ~ 0.3 and ~ 0.65 °C and the annual mean precipitation drops by ~ 75 and ~ 50 mm in tropical and temperate zones, respectively18,34,35. On top of that, as elevation increases, oxygen partial pressure falls, yielding colder, drier, and hypoxic environments that should restrict colonization by less plastic species. Our SDM analysis considers the effects of temperature and precipitation, but it does not include pO2 as a standalone variable, and we assume it as a component of elevation. Since these models revealed that, besides the effects of temperature and precipitation, elevation itself is significant for the distributions of these three species, it seems plausible to postulate that pO2 plays a role in shaping habitat suitability for rats and mice. Furthermore, the fact that the confirmed presence data show six-times fewer records of common rats than house mice above 2,500 m, and that elevation is 1.3–2.2-times more influential in the modeled distribution of rats than mice, suggests that this role is preponderant for rats. These observations are in agreement with previous exploratory work from our lab and recent findings elsewhere reporting that R. rattus and R. norvegicus are restricted to altitudes below 3,000 m, while M. musculus, as an elevational generalist, can be found from sea level up to 4,000 m19. Here, we suggest that the elevational range for this species may be even larger, as our data confirms the presence of the house mouse up to 5,500 m.

To what extent does the physiology of rats and mice shape their biogeography?

Environmental and climatic factors alone cannot explain a species’ biogeography. Realized distributions also depend on biotic determinants such as physiological and behavioral plasticity and ecological interactions, which can modulate or even override abiotic influences. A classic and relevant example of this abiotic–biotic interplay is Janzen’s “seasonality hypothesis”, which proposed that mountain passes act as strong dispersal barriers, especially for species with limited plasticity38. Modern interpretations of Janzen’s idea emphasize that environmental gradients function primarily as physiological rather than strictly physical barriers17,39,40, a perspective consistent with our findings. In the case of elevational gradients, physiological responses to the combined stressors of both cold and hypoxia are the main determinants of altitude tolerance for endothermic vertebrates living there16. Our analyses show that although common rats are largely absent from high elevations, they are present in the northern cold lands of Europe. Contrastingly, the smaller house mouse can be commonly found in both northern and high landscapes, notwithstanding, and as confirmed by our models, cold poses even stronger physiological challenges to small-bodied species due to their high surface-area-to-volume ratio15,16. This implies that cold tolerance is not the primary factor influencing these species’ ability to colonize the highlands. Although in high altitudes, cold effects may be aggravated by an increased metabolic oxygen demand because thermogenesis in eutherian mammals relies on aerobic metabolism41, exacerbating the effects of environmental hypoxia. Still, sharing refuge with humans should buffer extreme cold in temperate areas, and temperature rarely drops below 0 °C in tropical regions18, suggesting that hypoxia, rather than cold alone, is the dominant factor limiting altitudinal distributions in these two rat species. Previous work from our group offers supporting evidence to this hypothesis. During the last fifteen years, we have consistently shown that Sprague Dawley rats and FVB mice, which are laboratory strains originally derived from R. norvegicus and M. musculus, respectively42,43, exhibit divergent physiological responses to high altitude 23–27 and long-term normobaric hypoxia19,21,22. In both species, these responses aim to enhance the capture, transport, and utilization of oxygen with distinct outcomes. Rats have impaired respiratory control, excessive levels of hematocrit and hemoglobin (thickening the blood and stressing the heart), signs of pulmonary hypertension, and depressed whole-body metabolism. In contrast, mice have enhanced ventilation, tidal volume, alveolar surface area, lung volume, and metabolic rate, with only moderately augmented hematocrit and hemoglobin. Also, rats and mice show different profiles of hepatic and cerebral mitochondrial bioenergetics during development and adulthood21,26. Overall, rats develop a detrimental phenotype for living under environmental hypoxia, while mice exhibit beneficial responses. Importantly, these contrasting physiological profiles challenge the common assumption that Rattus and Mus are interchangeable models for physiological and cellular hypoxia and high-altitude research. Because these two rodents dominate biomedical studies, results from one species are frequently generalized to the other in many labs, including ours28. Yet the ecological and physiological evidence presented here clearly shows that their hypoxia responses differ fundamentally, cautioning against such extrapolation. Finally, this body of work has also shown that rats can survive and reproduce at high altitudes in laboratory conditions44–46, where environmentalchallenges and ecologically relevant tasks, such as foraging, mating, nest building, and predator avoidance, are forgone. However, in natural high-altitude habitats, the aerobic performance of rats may be impaired by their poorly plastic ventilation and metabolism, while brain function may be torpid47 by detrimental brain mitochondrial plasticity. In this context, it seems sensible to imagine that such detrimental physiological responses will negatively affect “lethargic” rats, reduce their biological fitness, and ultimately impair their ability to establish permanent populations at high altitude19,36.

Did altitude shape the early biogeography of these species?

Common rats and house mice have very different phylogeographic histories predating their human-assisted dispersion, which might explain their current divergent distributions in high altitudes (Fig. 5). R. rattus originated in the tropical lowlands of Southern and Eastern India, from where they dispersed between 0.6 and 1.4 million years ago. During an early phase, expansion was confined to warm riverine and coastal ecosystems along southern Asia and escaped mountain ranges and highlands 2. A second phase began when sedentary settlements, long-distance trade, and stored grains enabled commensal dependence on humans ~ 2,000–1,500 BC, particularly through Indian Ocean maritime networks, which carried black rats west into Mesopotamia and later the Mediterranean and Europe12. R. norvegicus followed a similar two-stage history, but much later and from either lowland southern East or Central Asia. Natural dispersal before ~ 50,000–200,000 years ago remained ecologically limited to temperate and subtropical lowlands 12,13. Its global expansion coincided with early modern commercial growth ~ 1,500–1,800 AD, moving through ports and dense urban centers in Europe and Africa11,12. On the other hand, the biogeographic origins of Mus musculus have been identified in the surroundings of the Himalayan region, comprising the temperate steppes at the southern feet of the mountain range, the north of the Indo-Pakistani subcontinent, and the territories of Afghanistan and Iran up to the Levant in the Middle East, also known as the Iranian highlands 6,33. This region is geographically disparate and contains mountain barriers, high mountain passes, and intermediate valleys, all of which allegedly affected the early evolution of the original populations of M. musculus around 1.7 million years ago48. Indeed, even nowadays, subspecific populations of M. musculus are geographically split, but not genetically isolated, by major mountain barriers in this region such as the Zagros, the mountain chains of the Hindu Kush, and the Himalayas, implying they have been able to cross these barriers of up to 5,000 m, and overcome selective pressure that may have ultimately shaped their tolerance to high altitude 33,49. From this area, ancient populations of this species spread eastwards, reaching the Arabo-Persian Gulf and Asia Minor, where the first contacts with humans occurred 12,000 years ago 6,8, and then followed us to Asia, Europe, and Africa4,6,7,33,50. Both common rats and the house mouse only arrived in the Americas and Australia in recent times, as a consequence of the well-recorded events of anthropogenic colonization conducted by Europeans during the 15th and 16th centuries6,9,10,37,51, and have had opposing success in establishing at high altitudes 19,36. Based on these distinct phylogeographic origins, in the past19,22, we suggested that, contrary to common rats, the early exposure of M. musculus to high altitudes has conferred this species with its hypoxia-adaptive phenotype and the ability to successfully colonize the highlands across the Earth, a process known as “preadaptation” and defined as “a genotypic capacity to invade a new ecological niche as a consequence of previously acquired appropriate structural and/or functional changes”52. The results we present here further support this hypothesis.

We conclude that geographical elevation is a significantly limiting factor in the biogeography of R. rattus and R. norvegicus, and only to a lesser extent in that of M. musculus. Although both common rats and house mice are human commensals and are commonly thought to be ubiquitous in human settlements, here we show that rats are largely scarce, if not absent, at high-altitude settings where mice thrive.

Limitations of this study

Despite combining extensive global occurrence data with bioclimatic modelling, several limitations should be acknowledged. We used GBIF records, which vary in quality and may include misidentifications. The 1-km resolution of WorldClim inevitably smooths the steep environmental heterogeneity of mountain systems, potentially masking microhabitats that could support small, patchy populations. Ecological niche models (ENM) estimate potential rather than realized distributions and therefore cannot account for dispersal barriers, interspecific interactions, predation, or human-driven habitat disturbance that may prevent species from occupying climatically suitable areas. Additionally, key ecological factors such as water availability, vegetation structure, primary productivity, and explicit measures of human settlement were not included. Together, these constraints warrant caution when interpreting fine-scale elevational patterns, particularly in topographically complex regions.

Methods

Data sources

Occurrence

Data on occurrence were retrieved from the public database of the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF). For house mice, the global data corresponding to “Mus musculus (Linnaeus, 1758)” were obtained (GBIF.org [26th June 2019] GBIF Occurrence Download 10.15468/dl.huv4qe). For common rats, global data for “Rattus rattus (Linnaeus, 1758)” (GBIF.org [26th June 2019] GBIF Occurrence Download 10.15468/dl.9xuu9o) and “Rattus norvegicus (Berkenhout, 1769)” GBIF.org [26th June 2019] GBIF Occurrence Download 10.15468/dl.2hfnsx) were acquired.

Altitude and bioclimatic variables

Data of geographic elevation (altitude) and bioclimatic variables: maximum temperature of the warmest month, minimum temperature of the coldest month, precipitation of the wettest month, precipitation of the driest month, temperature seasonality, and precipitation seasonality; were acquired from the WORLDCLIM platform29.

Data processing and analysis

First, the occurrence dataset for each species was processed and cleaned using MS Excel 2019. Occurrence records with missing geographic coordinates data were discarded. Secondly, the coordinate data were cleaned by discarding those points falling on water bodies and polar regions using the “select by location” tool in QGIS v3.32.1. Finally, altitude data and geographic coordinates data for each occurrence point were paired with the “Sample Raster Values” tool in QGIS v3.32.1.

Species distribution modeling (SDM)

Relevant bioclimatic variables were selected by following these two criteria: (1) Variables of ecological relevance for rodent distribution were favored following recommendations on the importance of using environmentally interpretable predictors in global studies53,54. (2) Only uncorrelated variables (Pearson’s r < 0.7) were taken to avoid information redundancy and overfitting problems (Supplementary Fig. S1). The potential distribution of both species of common rats and house mice was estimated using the maximum entropy method (MaxEnt). MaxEnt considers the most uniform distribution as the most probable one55, and calculates a continuous probability value (0 to 100), as an indicator of the relative suitability of habitat for the studied species. This calculation is based on the principle of maximum entropy, and it is constrained by the occurrence data input.

To characterize the available environmental space, we generated 10,000 background points within the cropped extent while excluding known presences. Random background sampling (pseudo-absence) is appropriate for global analyses and for broadly distributed, human-associated species such as the rodents examined here53,56. Environmental predictors consisted of 1 km2 bioclimatic variables, a standard resolution for capturing broad climatic gradients. Occurrence records were spatially thinned to a 10 km minimum distance to reduce autocorrelation and sampling clustering, as suggested previously57. The filtered dataset was partitioned into 70% training and 30% testing points, providing a balanced design for model calibration and evaluation. Model performance was assessed using the “evaluate” function in DISMO. Following best practices for global-extent SDMs53, we focused on overall model fit and broad spatial patterns rather than fine-scale predictive accuracy, as the goal was to characterize large-scale environmental gradients rather than delineate local distributional limits. The response curves obtained for the models are presented in Supplementary figure S2.
