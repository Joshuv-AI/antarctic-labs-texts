How are chronic diseases related to depressive symptoms in older adults? The role of life satisfaction and multidimensional intergenerational relationships
Authors: Yuanyuan Fu, Xiachun Ke, Nan Hu, Xiaoyue Sun, Yuebin Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigated the association between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms among older adults while also examining the mediating role of life satisfaction and the moderating role of multidimensional intergenerational relationships in this association.MethodsData were gathered from 3917 community-dwelling older adults in China. Chronic diseases were measured by summing the total number of diagnosed conditions self-reported from a checklist of 16 chronic illnesses included in the questionnaire. Depressive symptoms were measured by the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale. Life satisfaction was measured by the Chinese version of the Satisfaction with Life Scale. Intergenerational relationships were assessed by the Intergenerational Relationship Quality Scale for Aging Chinese Parents, which includes four dimensions: consensual–normative solidarity, structural–associational solidarity, affectual closeness, and intergenerational conflict. We employed the PROCESS macro to test the hypothesized pathways. Specifically, Model 4 was used to examine the mediating effect of life satisfaction on the association between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms. Model 8 was then conducted to simultaneously assess the mediating role of life satisfaction and the moderating effects of different dimensions of intergenerational relationships.ResultsMore chronic diseases were associated with more depressive symptoms. Life satisfaction mediated the association between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms. Among the four dimensions of intergenerational relationships, structural–associational solidarity was found to moderate both the direct pathway between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms and the indirect pathway through life satisfaction.ConclusionsThese findings enhance our understanding of the association between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms and provide important insights that can guide policy-makers and practitioners in addressing the psychological challenges faced by older adults with chronic diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06513-4.Keywords: Physical health, Subjective well-being, Intergenerational relations, Moderated mediation effect

Introduction

The issue of poor psychological health has become increasingly prominent on a globally, particularly in relation to the increasing prevalence of major depressive disorders among the elderly population. As depression has emerged as the leading psychological problems affecting older adults, there has been an increased focus in recent years not only on diagnostic efforts but also on addressing depressive symptoms. Data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Survey indicates that 36.2% of older adults experience symptoms of depression [1]. It is crucial to examine the factors associated with depressive symptoms in older adults and to investigate the underlying mechanisms of these associations.

Chronic diseases and depressive symptoms: a stress process perspective

The prevalence of multimorbidity increases with age, introducing a notable stressor to the lives of older adults [2]. In China, multimorbidity affects 48.6% of the older population [3] , and the overall prevalence of chronic diseases is reported to be 81.1% (95% CI: [80.9, 81.2]) [4]. These figures underscore that the accumulation of chronic conditions has become a central challenge to healthy aging, exerting profound burdens that heighten the risk of depressive symptoms.The stress sensitivity hypothesis suggests that chronic stress may increase vulnerability to depression, a concern that is particularly relevant for older adults, who are known for their lower stress tolerance and high likelihood of experiencing loneliness and discomfort [5]. Empirical evidence from cross-sectional analyses [3, 6], and longitudinal research [7, 8] consistently corroborates the robust link between chronic diseases and the risk of depression among older adults. To better understand how chronic diseases contribute to depressive symptoms, the stress process model serves as a valuable framework for analyzing the underlying psychosocial pathways. The stress process model which was originally developed to explain psychological outcomes in caregiving contexts [9], has since been extended to a broader range of life circumstances [10, 11], particularly in the context of older adults [12–14]. Drawing on the stress process model, chronic diseases are conceptualized as primary stressors that may influence psychological outcomes—such as depressive symptoms—either directly or indirectly through secondary stressors, including intrapsychic strains [15]. Within this model, moderating resources—for example various forms of social support—are posited to mitigate the detrimental effects of stress exposure, thereby weakening the pathways from stressors to psychological outcomes [16].

Life satisfaction as a mediator between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms

Chronic diseases are known not only for their influence on depressive symptoms but also for their broader impact on psychological well-being, including life satisfaction. According to the stress process model [15, 16], diminished life satisfaction can be conceptualized as an intrapsychic strain—a key type of secondary stressor that mediates the psychological consequences of primary stressors, such as chronic diseases. Empirical research has consistently documented the individual links within this pathway: most older adults tend to experience illness and a decline in physical functioning, which negatively affect their life satisfaction [12, 17, 18]; concurrently, a consistent body of research underscores a negative association between life satisfaction and depressive symptoms [19–24]. In addition to these individual links, several studies have investigated the mediating mechanism through which life satisfaction mediates the relationship between chronic diseases and depressive states [25–27]. However, most studies have focused on single chronic conditions or have included mixed-age samples, leaving the mediating role of life satisfaction underexamined in the context of multimorbidity among older adults. To address this research gap, this study examines the mediating role of life satisfaction in the association between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms among older adults.

Moderating role of intergenerational relationships in the pathways from chronic diseases to depressive symptoms

In accordance with the stress process model, social support is considered to serve as a protective barrier, potentially delaying, averting, or diminishing the progression and consequences of stress-related disorders [16]. Empirical research has also highlighted the moderating effect of social support on the associations between physical health conditions and both depressive symptoms and life satisfaction. For example, family solidarity, encompassing both instrumental support and emotional support, can buffer the negative effects of functional disability on depression [28] and reduce the stress of physical health problems on depression [29]. Affective closeness has been identified as a moderator of the association between disability and life satisfaction in older adults [30]; similarly, the interaction between health and family support has been found to significantly affect life satisfaction among older adults [31].

Within the realm of social support, intergenerational relationships stand as an indispensable social resource in the Chinese culture context. Filial piety, a foundational principle of family life and intergenerational relationships in Chinese culture [32, 33], has been conceptualized to encompass two key dimensions: authoritative obligation, reflecting deference and duty to parents, and reciprocity, which emphasizes mutual affection and support between generations [32, 34–37]. In this value system, older adults are expected to assume meaningful roles within both the family and the broader community, thereby contributing to the pursuit of harmony—an essential cultural value that extends across interpersonal, societal, and even natural domains [38]. As such, intergenerational relationships rooted in filial norms serve as critical resources for older adults [39]. Informed by the value of children (VOC) theoretical framework, prior research in China—a developing collectivist society—has consistently shown that parents attach value to their children in multiple ways, including economic/utilitarian, psychological/emotional, and social/normative dimensions [6, 40, 41]. These perceived values reinforce expectations around intergenerational exchanges and obligations, thereby shaping both the structure and the function of intergenerational ties in later life. While previous studies underscore social support as a key moderating resource that can shape how individuals respond to physical health conditions and, in turn, influence both depressive symptoms and life satisfaction, relatively few have specifically examined the role of intergenerational relationships as a distinct and multidimensional form of social support [42, 43]. Even fewer studies have simultaneously tested whether intergenerational relationships moderate both the direct effect of chronic diseases on depressive symptoms and the indirect pathway through life satisfaction within an integrated framework. Recent work by Bai et al. (2018) has identified four distinct dimensions of intergenerational relationships in the Chinese context: consensual–normative solidarity, structural–associational solidarity, affectual closeness, and intergenerational conflict [44]. Building on this, the present study examines the extent to which these various dimensions of intergenerational relationships differentially moderate the stress pathway from chronic diseases to depressive symptoms, both directly and indirectly through life satisfaction.

The present study

Despite the theoretical clarity of the stress process model in explaining how chronic diseases affect depressive symptoms via life satisfaction and moderated by intergenerational relationships, empirical studies that systematically test these mechanisms in older adults are still relatively limited. In particular, prior research has often focused on single chronic conditions or included mixed-age samples, leaving the mediating role of life satisfaction in the association between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms among older adults underexplored—despite multimorbidity being increasingly prevalent in later life. Furthermore, while social support has been widely recognized as a buffering resource, limited attention has been given to intergenerational relationships as a unique and multidimensional source of such support. Few studies have examined whether intergenerational relationships simultaneously moderate both the direct effect of chronic diseases on depressive symptoms and the indirect pathway via life satisfaction within a single integrated framework.To address this gap in the literature, the present study aims to investigate the association between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms among older adults, while also examining the mediating role of life satisfaction in this association and the moderating role of multidimensional intergenerational relationships in the direct and indirect (via life satisfaction) pathways. Methods

Sampling

This study utilized survey data collected from Yibin city, Sichuan Province, China. Yibin had a population of 5.523 million in 2018, with 11.73% of the population aged 65 years or above [45, 46], a proportion comparable to the national average population aging at that time [47]. The city’s per capita disposable income reached 23,665 RMB in 2018, representing a mid-level economic status within Sichuan Province [47, 48]. This study employed a face-to-face questionnaire survey targeting community-dwelling older adults aged 65 and above. A multistep sampling method was used to recruit participants. Initially, three districts (Cuiping, Nanxi, and Xuzhou) in Yibin were purposively selected on the basis of the varied economic development levels of the districts. Subsequently, the probability proportionate to size (PPS) method guided the selection of urban communities and rural villages, with specific quotas allocated to each. Lists of the names of the potential communities and villages were acquired from local civil affairs bureaus. Within each location, the sampling quota was evenly distributed across five age strata: 65–69, 70–74, 75–79, 80–84, and 85+. Systematic sampling was then applied to recruit participants. In total, 230 trained interviewers conducted face-to-face interviews, supervised by a research team comprising one assistant professor, one doctoral student, and 18 master’s students in social work. A total of 4,433 older adults completed face-to-face questionnaire interviews, for a response rate of 83%. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) surveys completed by proxy respondents were excluded; (2) individuals without children were excluded, as intergenerational relationship measures required at least one child; and (3) cases with missing data on key variables were also excluded. Following these exclusions, the final sample size was refined to 3,917 participants. A comparison of sociodemographic characteristics between the included and excluded samples was conducted, and the results are presented in Table A1 in the supplementary file. Each interview lasted approximately 50 min. Ethical approval was obtained from the human subject ethics committee.

Measurement

Chronic diseases

Chronic diseases were quantified through a self-reported multiple-choice question: ‘How many chronic diseases have you been diagnosed with?’ Participants were provided with a list of 16 diseases to select from, which included hypertension, arthritis or rheumatism, cervical or lumbar spondylosis, heart disease, diabetes, glaucoma or cataract, osteoporosis, chronic pulmonary conditions (bronchitis, pulmonary emphysema, pneumonia, the asthma), stroke or cerebrovascular disease, kidney disease, gastrointestinal ulcers, hepatic or gallbladder disorders, fractures, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and cancer. The total number of reported diseases generated a composite score, with a higher count indicating a greater burden of chronic conditions.

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were measured using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10) [49], which has demonstrated good validity and reliability among older Chinese adults [50, 51]. Scores for each item range from 0 (rarely or never) to 3 (all of the time). The total score of the CESD-10 can range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater severity of depressive symptoms. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the CESD-10 was 0.836.

Life satisfaction

Life satisfaction was measured using the Chinese version of the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) translated by Shek (1998), originally developed by Diener et al. (1985) [52, 53]. Prior research has demonstrated that the Chinese version of the SWLS has good reliability and construct validity [54]. This scale consists of five items rated on a seven-point scale (ranging from 1 = completely disagree to 7 = completely agree). The total score can range from 5 to 35, with higher scores indicating higher levels of life satisfaction. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.931.

Intergenerational relationships

Intergenerational relationships were gauged using the Intergenerational Relationship Quality Scale for Aging Chinese Parents (IRQS-AP), a 13-item measure developed by Bai [55]. Designed to reflect theoretical constructs of solidarity, conflict, and ambivalence, this scale evaluates the multidimensional relationships between older Chinese adults and their adult children. It includes four dimensions: consensual–normative solidarity, structural–associational solidarity, affectual closeness, and intergenerational conflict. For consistency of interpretation, the intergenerational conflict items were reverse-coded so that higher scores on all four dimensions uniformly indicate stronger and more positive intergenerational ties. For analytical purposes, mean scores were calculated across all the adult children to gauge the overall quality of the parental–child relationship. This study considered both the total scores for the intergenerational relationships and the total scores for each dimension. The internal consistency of the scale was acceptable in our sample, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.689.

Covariates

On the basis of previous findings regarding the determinants of depressive symptoms [39, 56–59], this study controlled for gender, age, marital status, educational level, region, number of children, self-rated financial condition, friend networks, and community support. Age and the number of children were treated as continuous variables, with age represented in chronological years. Gender (female = 0, male = 1), marital status (divorced/widowed/single = 0, currently married = 1), educational level (middle school or above = 0, primary school or below = 1), and region (urban = 0, rural = 1) were coded as binary variables. Self-rated financial condition was self-assessed with a single question: “Over the past 12 months, has your household income been sufficient for your daily needs?” The responses were scaled from 1 (poor) to 3 (rich). Friend networks were assessed using three friend-related items from the Lubben Social Network Scale [60]. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.778. A higher score indicated greater friend networks. Community support was measured by the following question: “Are you satisfied or dissatisfied with the overall environment of the neighborhood in which you live?” The responses ranged from 1 (very dissatisfied) to 5 (very satisfied), with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction with the community support.

Data analysis

Initially, descriptive analyses were conducted to report the characteristics of the samples. Subsequently, intervariable relationships were assessed using Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlation analyses to detect any strong correlations. Next, we conducted linear regressions to test the associations between chronic diseases, life satisfaction, and depressive symptoms, controlling for covariates and the total score of intergenerational relationships. To examine mediation and moderated mediation, we used Hayes’ PROCESS macro. The tool was chosen over manual calculations or alternative packages because of its robust bootstrap estimation framework and suitability for conditional process analysis. If a significant relationship was observed between chronic diseases and life satisfaction, the mediating effect of life satisfaction on the association between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms was tested using Model 4 via the PROCESS macro. If the mediating role of life satisfaction was confirmed, Model 8 via the PROCESS macro was further utilized to concurrently assess the mediating effect of life satisfaction and the moderating effect of different dimensions of intergenerational relationships in the direct and indirect pathways between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms. The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) that did not encompass zero were indicative of a significant effect. The conditional effects of chronic diseases on depressive symptoms and life satisfaction at different values (M-SD, M, and M + SD) of intergenerational relationships were calculated. Bootstrapping with 5000 bootstrap resamples was performed to approximate the sampling distribution. All of the data analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 26.0 software and PROCESS v3.3.

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 1 shows the profile of the sample. More than half of the participants were female (53.5%). The age of the participants ranged from 65 to 103 years, with a mean age of 76.2 (SD = 7.2) years. Those who were currently married composed 61.3% of the sample. In terms of education, a majority (71.6%) had attained an educational level of primary school or below, indicating a generally low level of education among the participants. A total of 57.7% were from urban areas. The mean number of children was 3.1 (SD = 1.4). Financially, 32.6% of the participants considered their financial condition to be poor, 60.9% rated it as general, and 6.5% perceived it as rich. The mean scores for friend networks and community support were 9.0 (SD = 4.3) and 3.6 (SD = 1.0), respectively. The mean scores for the four dimensions of intergenerational relationships—consensual–normative solidarity, structural–associational solidarity, affectual closeness, and intergenerational conflict— were 10.4 (SD = 2.1), 11.8 (SD = 3.2), 10.7 (SD = 2.2), and 13.5 (SD = 2.1), respectively. The mean number of chronic diseases was 2.8 (SD = 2.2). The mean score for depressive symptoms was 6.7 (SD = 5.7), and the mean score for life satisfaction was 26.0 (SD = 6.1).

Moderating effects of multidimensional intergenerational relationships

We further employed Model 8 via the PROCESS macro to simultaneously examine the mediating effect of life satisfaction and the moderating effect of each dimension of intergenerational relationships on the association between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms while controlling for covariates and other dimensions of intergenerational relationships. As shown in Table 5, structural–associational solidarity moderated the first half of the mediation path (namely, path a) (coefficient = 0.038, p = 0.002) and direct path c (coefficient = −0.031, p = 0.002). For individuals with higher levels of structural–associational solidarity, the presence of more chronic diseases was associated with a less pronounced negative impact on life satisfaction and a weaker positive impact on depressive symptoms. As shown in Table 6, structural–associational solidarity significantly moderates both the direct and indirect pathways between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms. With respect to the conditional direct effect, when structural–associational solidarity is low (M-SD), the direct effect of chronic diseases on depressive symptoms is the strongest (0.625), with a 95% confidence interval ranging from 0.538 to 0.712, indicating a significant effect; as structural–associational solidarity increases, the direct effect decreases, with a moderate effect observed at the mean level (M) (0.530, 95% CI: [0.468, 0.592]) and the weakest effect at the higher level (M+SD) (0.435, 95% CI: [0.348, 0.521]). With respect to the conditional indirect effect through life satisfaction, when structural-associational solidarity is low (M-SD), the indirect effect is strongest (0.162, 95% CI: [0.122, 0.205]). This mediating effect becomes weaker as structural–associational solidarity increases, with the effect being less pronounced at the mean level (M) (0.122, 95% CI: [0.093, 0.151]) and even weaker at the higher level (M+SD) (0.081, 95% CI: [0.045, 0.119]).

Robustness analysis

To assess the robustness of the findings, we conducted a supplementary analysis using a randomly selected approximately 50% of the full sample. As shown in Table A2, Table A3, Table A4 and Table A5 in the supplementary file, the main findings remained consistent with those from the full sample.

Discussion

This study provides nuanced insights into how chronic diseases affect depressive symptoms, highlighting the specific roles of life satisfaction and different dimensions of intergenerational relationships (consensual–normative solidarity, structural–associational solidarity, affectual closeness, and intergenerational conflict) in the stress mechanism. The results reveal that chronic diseases influence depressive symptoms in older adults through their impact on life satisfaction. Moreover, structural–associational solidarity buffers the direct association between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms, as well as the indirect effect mediated by life satisfaction. However, other dimensions of intergenerational relationships do not moderate this mechanism. These findings have valuable implications for policies and practices aimed at enhancing the psychological well-being of older adults.

The direct effect of chronic diseases on depressive symptoms among older adults

The results of the current study support Hypothesis 1, which posits that an increased number of chronic diseases is positively associated with greater depressive symptoms in older adults. This finding is consistent with those previous research [6–8] and supports the stress sensitivity hypothesis, suggesting that the chronic stress associated with diseases may heighten susceptibility to depressive symptoms. Stress stemming from disease-related factors, such as persistent pain and physical discomfort, may hinder older adults’ social interactions and daily activities. Reduced social engagement can, in turn, lead to increased depressive symptoms in older adults [61]. Furthermore, the functional limitations in daily living resulting from chronic diseases can impose substantial economic and caregiving burdens on families. The long-term presence of these burdens can exacerbate the psychological distress experienced by older adults with chronic diseases [56].

The mediating role of life satisfaction in the association between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms

Although previous studies have established a direct association between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms, the extent to which life satisfaction mediates the association between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms remains less explored among older adults. This study reveals that the increased distress stemming from chronic diseases may lead to lower ratings of life satisfaction among older adults, which in turn contributes to increased depressive symptoms, thereby confirming Hypothesis 2. As chronic diseases affect both the physical and functional aspects of older adults’ daily lives, an increase in these conditions is negatively associated with a decrease in life satisfaction. Consequently, the perception of decreased life satisfaction exacerbates depressive symptoms.

The moderating role of structural-associational solidarity

This study empirically investigates the moderating effects of specific dimensions of intergenerational relationships on the association between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms, as well as on the indirect pathway through life satisfaction. Our findings partially support Hypothesis 3 and confirm Hypothesis 4. Intergenerational relationships hold particular importance in Chinese society and are closely related to the psychological well-being of older adults [62, 63]. However, the impacts of different dimensions within these relationships vary. Among the four dimensions considered, only structural–associational solidarity emerges as a moderator, influencing both the indirect path (mediated by life satisfaction) and the direct path from chronic diseases to depressive symptoms. This finding provides support for the stress process model, which posits that intergenerational relationships serve as a moderating resource expected to impede, prevent, or alleviate the progression of the stress process and its subsequent outcomes [16]. For individuals with higher levels of structural–associational solidarity, the exacerbating effect of a higher number of chronic diseases on depressive symptoms is weaker; moreover, the detrimental effect of a higher number of chronic diseases on life satisfaction is alleviated.When older adults live closer to their children and maintain frequent contact, they are more likely to receive instrumental and emotional support, which plays a critical role in mitigating the psychological burdens associated with chronic diseases.

Implications

This study provides valuable insights for policy-makers and practitioners in understanding and addressing the psychological challenges faced by older adults with chronic diseases. First, this study highlights the negative effect of chronic diseases on the psychological well-being of older adults. We advocate for a comprehensive approach that includes promoting healthy lifestyles, implementing health promotion programs, enhancing disease management through early detection and consistent interventions, optimizing treatment outcomes with innovative strategies, and empowering patients with comprehensive education. Second, the findings reveal that the effect of chronic diseases on depressive symptoms is partially mediated by life satisfaction, suggesting the need for additional intervention. Improving the psychological health of older adults involves not only effectively preventing and managing the onset and progression of chronic diseases to alleviate depressive symptoms but also promoting life satisfaction among older adults to reduce the likelihood of depressive symptoms. In daily practice, identifying older adults with life dissatisfaction is imperative, as it signals the need for interventions to prevent depressive mood [64] and mitigate the impact of chronic diseases on depressive symptoms. It is also essential to explore the specific aspects of older adults’ lives that cause dissatisfaction, allowing tailored interventions to enhance overall life satisfaction [26]. Furthermore, creating age-friendly environments is a key strategy for policy-makers and practitioners to increase the life satisfaction of older adults, thereby reducing the impact of chronic diseases on their depressive mood. Third, this study reveals that structural–associational solidarity is the only dimension among the four intergenerational relationship domains that significantly moderates both the direct and indirect pathways linking chronic diseases and depressive symptoms. In terms of practical implications, practitioners should assess the quality of intergenerational relationships—particularly structural–associational solidarity—among older adults in poor health, as those with lower levels may be more vulnerable to depression and thus require targeted psychological interventions. Additionally, practitioners can encourage older adults and their adult children to live near one another and can work with families to explore practical solutions for coresidence or geographic proximity. Practitioners may also consider to offering family communication training or family counseling to further strengthen structural-associational solidarity. Furthermore, practitioners should facilitate activities that involve both older adults and their children, creating opportunities to enhance structural–associational solidarity. In terms of policy implications, it is advisable to implement family-friendly policies, such as workplace flexibility, compensatory time off, and housing subsidies, to enable adult children to spend more quality time with their aging parents, both in person and virtually. These policies would facilitate coresidence or closer proximity between generations, strengthening structural–associational solidarity and thus supporting the psychological well-being of older adults.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, its reliance on cross-sectional data requires cautious interpretation, necessitating future longitudinal designs. Second, this study is based on data from Yibin. The city’s demographic structure and level of population aging are broadly comparable to national averages, and its economic status reflects a mid-level position within the province. These characteristics suggest that Yibin is reasonably representative of many mid-sized Chinese cities. Nonetheless, the generalizability of the findings should be interpreted with caution. The key variables in this study—chronic diseases, life satisfaction, depressive symptoms, and intergenerational relationships—may vary across regions because of differences in health care infrastructure, and socioeconomic development. To enhance the applicability of future research, studies based on multisite or nationally representative samples are warranted, particularly to assess potential regional heterogeneity in the observed pathways. Third, this study focused solely on the influence of the number of chronic diseases on depressive symptoms and did not account for the heterogeneity of specific chronic conditions, combinations, or subjective ratings. Future research could benefit from exploring these aspects more comprehensively for a more differentiated understanding of the relationship between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms. Fourth, this study employed a subjective measurement of financial condition. This subjective measurement may be shaped by individual expectations and social norms while also being susceptible to social desirability bias, potentially reducing comparability and leading to the underreporting of financial hardship. Furthermore, the measure does not account for household size, accumulated wealth, or in-kind support and may fail to capture recent fluctuations in financial status. Future studies could incorporate more objective or multidimensional economic indicators to enhance the measurement of financial conditions. Fifth, although this study adopted the stress process model as a guiding framework, not all the components of the model were incorporated into the analysis. In particular, coping resources were not measured because of limitations in questionnaire length. Future research is encouraged to incorporate these components to enable a more comprehensive application of the stress process model in understanding the psychological health of older adults.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the pathways connecting chronic diseases and depressive symptoms in older adults by examining the mediating effect of life satisfaction and the moderating effects of multidimensional intergenerational relationships. The findings demonstrate a positive association between the number of chronic diseases and depressive symptoms, with life satisfaction acting as a mediator in this relationship. Moreover, among the four dimensions of intergenerational relationships, structural–associational solidarity was identified as a key moderator, influencing both the direct relationship between chronic diseases and depressive symptoms and the indirect pathway through life satisfaction. The findings of this study provide crucial implications for policy-makers and practitioners, aiding in the development of approaches to mitigate the psychological challenges faced by older adults with chronic diseases and thus promote healthy aging.
