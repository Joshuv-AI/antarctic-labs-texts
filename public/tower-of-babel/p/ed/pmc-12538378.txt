Distinct NK Cell Genetic Variants Are Associated With HSV‐2 Versus VZV Infection of the CNS
Authors: Marianne Graninger, Laura Maria Kühner, Sarah Maria Berger, Hannes Vietzen, Elisabeth Puchhammer‐Stöckl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman alphaherpesvirus infections may cause life‐threatening CNS disease. As NK cell responses are crucial for herpesvirus control, we assessed if specific human genetic variants with influence on host NK cell functions are associated with HSV‐2 or VZV CNS disease development. We included patients with confirmed CNS disease due to HSV‐2 (n = 25) and VZV infection (n = 39), and compared them to 400 population controls. The NK cell‐related human genetic variants HLA‐E*0101/0103, rs9916629 C/T, NKG2C wt/del, FcγRIIIa 158‐F/V were assessed in all cohorts. HLA‐E‐binding peptide sequences were assessed in the HSV‐2 and VZV reference sequences by NCBI BLAST and peptide prediction. In patients with HSV‐2‐related CNS disease, we observed a significantly increased frequency of the rs9916629 CC variant (32%) and the NKG2C del/del genotype (12%) compared to controls (8% and 1%; p = < 0.0001 and p = 0.0006, respectively), and these variants were identified as independent risk factors for HSV‐2 CNS disease. In contrast, VZV CNS patients showed a higher prevalence of the FcγRIIIa 158‐F/F variant (59%) than controls (34%; p = 0.006). Our results indicate that distinct NK cell pathways are affected in HSV‐2 and VZV CNS disease, respectively, and hint towards differences in genetic predispositions to HSV‐2 versus VZV CNS infection.Keywords: FcγRIIIa, herpes simplex virus 2, NK cell, NKG2C, rs9916629, varicella zoster virus

Introduction

Human alphaherpesviruses, that is, herpes simplex virus 1 and 2 (HSV‐1 and HSV‐2) and varicella zoster virus (VZV), are neurotropic viruses which may cause life‐threatening infections of the central nervous system (CNS) [1, 2]. While HSV‐1 infection of the CNS typically results in HSV‐1 encephalitis (HSE), HSV‐2 more often causes meningitis in immunocompetent adults [1]. VZV can cause a variety of CNS manifestations [2]. Although CNS affection by alphaherpesviruses is rare, the course of infection might be fulminant, and it is essential to identify individuals at risk for severe CNS disease to enable early detection and sufficient treatment.

Host antiviral defense mechanisms are crucial to limit viral replication in the brain. Studies on HSE patients have revealed associations between HSE development and distinct human genetic variations involved in local innate immune responses in the brain [3]. Contributing to innate immunity, natural killer (NK) cells exhibit potent antiviral defense against herpesviruses, and NK cell deficiencies have been associated with HSV‐1 and VZV encephalitis [4]. In addition, we have recently identified two human genetic variants, that is, HLA‐E*0101/0101 and rs9916629 CC, which influence the individual host NK cell response and which were associated with the emergence of HSE [5]. The two HLA‐E alleles *0101 and *0103 differ in that HLA‐E*0101 exhibits lower surface stabilization upon peptide binding and decreased NK cell interaction compared to HLA‐E*0103 [6]. The rs9916629 C allele has previously been associated with alterations in the host NK cell repertoire, including increased CD56bright cell counts and elevated NKG2A expression on CD56dim cells, but also with strong NK cell cytotoxicity and decreased cytokine release compared to rs9916629 T [7]. A number of other human genetic variants further influence the individual host′s NK cell functions. These include a deletion of the NKG2C gene, leading to a gene‐dose dependent loss of the activating NKG2C receptor and impaired proliferation and function of cytotoxic NKG2C+ cells [8]. Also, the F158V dimorphism of the FCGR3A‐encoded NK cell Fcγ receptor IIIa (FcγRIIIa; CD16A) influences the functionality of antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) upon IgG binding, with the F allotype showing decreased IgG binding capacity and reduced ADCC functions [9].

The role of these NK cell‐related human genetic polymorphisms has so far only been analyzed in patients with HSE. In this study, we thus aimed to assess whether patients with CNS complications due to HSV‐2 or VZV infections display specific genetic variants influencing the host NK cell response.

Methods

Patient Cohorts

We included 25 patients with HSV‐2‐ and 39 patients with VZV‐associated CNS disease. HSV‐2 and VZV CNS disease were diagnosed by detection of HSV‐2 and VZV DNA by PCR in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [10], respectively, and by clinical signs of viral CNS affection. For comparison, we tested a cohort of 400 population controls including healthy blood donors as well as individuals whose blood had been sent to the Center for Virology for vaccine titer control. All material had been stored at −20°C in the biobank of the Center for Virology, Medical University of Vienna. Genotypes were assessed in CSF or blood samples from all patients and controls.

Genotyping

Genotyping was performed for HLA‐E*0101/0103, rs9916629 C/T, FcγRIIIa 158‐F/V and NKG2C wt/del after DNA extraction via the NUCLISENS® easyMAG® extractor (BioMérieux) or the TANBead® Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit. Genotypes were assessed by Taqman assays (HLA‐E, rs9916629, FCGR3A) or by touchdown PCR (NKG2C), as previously described [11, 12, 13].

Peptide Prediction and Sequence Analysis

To assess potential HSV‐2‐ and VZV‐specific HLA‐E‐binding peptides, we analyzed the complete protein sequences encoded by the following HSV‐2 and VZV reference genomes: HSV2 strain HG52 (NCBI Refseq: NC_001798.2) and VZV strain Dumas (NCBI Refseq: NC_001348.1). Prediction of HLA‐E‐binding HSV‐2 and VZV nonamere peptides was performed using the Epitope Prediction and Analysis Tools by IEDB Analysis Resource, applying the MHC class I binding prediction algorithm based on NetMHCpan − 4.1 EL. Comparison of previously published HSV‐1‐specific HLA‐E‐binding peptide sequences and HSV‐2 and VZV reference protein sequences was performed by NCBI BLAST.

Statistical Analysis

Age and sex distribution between cohorts was assessed by Kruskal–Wallis and χ 2 test, respectively. Genotype distributions were compared between cohorts by Fisher's exact or χ 2 test. The significance level α was set at 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Version 9.2.0.

Genetic Variants in HSV‐2 CNS Disease

As shown in Figure 1B,C, patients suffering from HSV‐2 CNS disease showed differences in genotype distributions of rs9916629 (p = < 0.0001) and NKG2C (p = 0.0006) compared to controls. Carriage of a rs9916629 C allele was significantly more common in HSV‐2 patients (76%) compared to controls (44%; p = 0.003, OR 4.07, 95% CI 1.70–9.93; Figure 1B). Notably, the homozygous rs9916629 C/C variant occurred in 32% of HSV‐2 patients but in only 8% of controls (p = 0.001, OR 5.23, 95% CI 2.14–12.88; Figure 1B). The homozygous NKG2C del/del variant occurred significantly more frequently amongst HSV‐2 patients (12%) compared to controls (1%; p = 0.008, OR 10.77, 95% CI 2.68–44.63). The overall frequency of the NKG2C del allele did not significantly differ between the two cohorts (40% and 33%; respectively; p = 0.514; Figure 1C).

Based on these observations, we defined the rs9916629 CC and the NKG2C del/del variants as risk genotypes for HSV‐2 CNS disease and assessed the associations between different genotype combinations and disease development (Table 2). The “maximum risk” genotype combination rs9916629 CC + NKG2C del/del occurred in only one HSV‐2 patient and none of the controls. Analysis of the other genotype combinations, including one predefined risk factor, indicated that rs9916629 CC and NKG2C del/del represent independent risk factors for HSV‐2 CNS disease (Table 2).

We did not observe significant differences in HLA‐E or FcγRIIIa variant distributions between HSV‐2 patients and controls (Figure 1A,D).

Genetic Variants in VZV CNS Disease

Analysis of patients with VZV‐associated CNS disease revealed that in this cohort, in contrast to HSV‐2 patients, FcγRIIIa variants were distributed differently compared to controls (p = 0.006; Figure 1D), while this was not observed for HLA‐E, rs9916629 or NKG2C genotypes (Figure 1A–C). VZV patients showed an increased frequency of the FcγRIIIa 158‐F/F variant (59%) compared to controls (34%; p = 0.003, OR 2.85, 95% CI 1.43–5.48); overall presence of the F allotype was not overrepresented in VZV patients (Figure 1D).

HLA‐E Binding Prediction

As it was previously shown that distinct HSV‐1 peptides can bind to HLA‐E, we assessed if the two HSV‐1‐specific HLA‐E‐binding peptide sequences described before (i.e. SSPSHILTL and VLPRPTITM) also occurred in the HSV‐2 and VZV reference protein sequences. Comparison of the peptide nonamere sequences to the complete protein sequences encoded by HSV‐2 and VZV revealed that the VLPRPTITM peptide also appears in the HSV‐2 protein sequence as part of glycoprotein C (UL44), but not in the VZV protein sequence. The SSPSHILTL peptide was not encoded by either HSV‐2 or VZV. These findings were confirmed by an online tool predicting VLPRPTITM as a HSV‐2‐specific, low‐affinity HLA‐E‐binding peptide.

Discussion

In the current study, we provide evidence that there are associations between the development of HSV‐2‐ and VZV‐associated CNS disease, respectively, and specific human genetic variants with an impact on the individual host NK cell response. Interestingly, the identified risk factors differed between HSV‐2 and VZV patient cohorts, implicating that different NK cell pathways are involved in the protection against CNS complications mediated by these two alphaherpesviruses.

Our current analysis revealed an increased prevalence of the rs9916629 CC variant in HSV‐2 patients (32%) compared to population controls (8%). This is similar to earlier data from HSV‐1 encephalitis (HSE) patients, where an increased frequency of the rs9916629 CC genotype was also observed [5]. Carriage of the rs9916629 C allele has previously been associated with higher numbers of circulating CD56bright cells, higher NKG2A expression on CD56dim cells, increased cytotoxicity and a trend towards decreased cytokine release upon NK cell stimulation [7]. It is unclear whether the effect of these alterations on HSV‐1 and HSV‐2 CNS disease is similar or differs depending on the underlying pathogen. Although closely related, HSV‐1 and HSV‐2 show different pathogenesis within the CNS. In HSE, which is characterized by the destruction of brain tissue by lytic HSV‐1 replication as well as cytotoxic effector functions by invading immune cells, the rs9916629 CC genotype might result in impaired functions of cytokine releasing resident CD56bright cells in the CNS, and/or in increased cytotoxicity upon influx of CD56dim cells [14]. In contrast to HSV‐1, HSV‐2 rather causes meningitis in immunocompetent adults, which is not believed to involve viral replication in the brain parenchyma [1]. Rather, it has been suggested that HSV‐2 is released from spinal ganglia into the surrounding CSF upon impaired peripheral HSV‐2 control, which eventually results in inflammatory responses of the meninges [15]. Following this hypothesis, an impact of the rs9916629 C allele on CD56bright, rather than CD56dim, cells in HSV‐2‐infected peripheral ganglia with diminished local cytokine release and uncontrolled HSV‐2 replication seems more likely as a pathomechanism contributing to HSV‐2 CNS disease. This potentially also affects peripheral HSV‐2 reactivation, which deserves further investigation.

In contrast to HSE patients analyzed previously, we did not detect differences in the HLA‐E genotype distribution between HSV‐2 patients and controls. Interestingly, however, the HLA‐E‐binding VLPRPTITM peptide previously shown to be expressed by HSV‐1 is also encoded by HSV‐2. Thus, HLA‐E surface stabilization may also occur in HSV‐2‐infected cells, but the individual HLA‐E expression profile does not seem to significantly impact the risk for developing HSV‐2 CNS disease.

We also observed an association between HSV‐2 CNS disease and the presence of the NKG2C del/del variant. Homozygous deletion of the NKG2C gene results in the loss of the activating NKG2C receptor and is rare in the general population, with a frequency of approximately 4% [8]. NKG2C+ cells show high cytotoxic functionality and strong cytokine release upon stimulation [16]. A decrease in highly potent NKG2C+ cells following NKG2C deletion has been associated with an increased risk for various viral diseases, for example, severe COVID‐19 or Puumalavirus infection [8, 17]. Only one previous study has specifically investigated the relevance of the NKG2C deletion in HSV‐1 infections, and the authors did not identify any association between the individual NKG2C genotype and the clinical course of peripheral HSV‐1 infection [18]. However, they showed that NKG2Cbright cells mediate increased ADCC activity against HSV‐1 infected cells compared to NKG2Cdim/‐ cells [19]. In this regard, an impaired HSV control and a higher risk for HSV‐related diseases in NKG2C‐deleted individuals seems plausible.

Interestingly, in patients with VZV‐related CNS disease, we observed a different pattern of NK cell‐associated genotype distribution, with an increased frequency of the homozygous FcγRIIIa 158‐F/F variant (59%) in comparison to controls (34%). This is in contrast to the HSV‐2 affected patients included here and HSE patients analyzed earlier, suggesting that different NK cell pathways are involved in the defense against HSV‐2‐, HSV‐1‐ and VZV‐driven CNS complications. Our data support earlier findings showing that the NK cell FcγRIIIa plays an important role in VZV control. FcγRIIIa initiates ADCC upon binding of IgG antibodies, and ADCC directed against VZV‐infected cells has previously been confirmed as an efficient mechanism by which antibodies may confer protection against VZV replication [20]. The FcγRIIIa 158‐F allotype was previously associated with lower IgG affinity and ADCC potency than the 158‐V allotype [9]. In line with this, the higher frequency of the FcγRIIIa 158‐F/F variant observed in our VZV patient cohort implicates decreased ADCC potency and impaired VZV control in individuals with VZV CNS infection compared to controls.

Conclusion

In the present study, we show that specific genetic variants affecting NK cell responses in the human host are associated with alphaherpesviruses‐related CNS disease. The present data suggest a different impact of specific NK cell pathways on the development of HSV‐2 versus VZV CNS disease. If functionally confirmed, our observations highlight that analysis of the patient's individual genetic risk profile may improve individual treatment and prevention strategies of alphaherpesvirus CNS disease.
