Veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation as a bridge to heart transplantation for end-stage heart failure: a single-centre retrospective analysis
Authors: Chuan Yuan, SiJing Huang, YanHeng Wang, Kejun Liu, Yingmeng Wu, Hongyu Ye, Yi Liang, BinFei Li, Weizhao Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesHeart transplantation is a key treatment for end-stage heart failure. However, few donors and long waits for transplant availability mean that patients may not receive the procedure in time. This study analysed the suitability of veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) as a bridge to heart transplantation.DesignThis study was retrospective.SettingThis single-centre study took place in cardiac center of a tertiary hospital.ParticipantsThe participants comprised 15 patients with end-stage heart failure.InterventionsAll 15 participants underwent heart transplantation under VA-ECMO–assisted circulation; six participants received extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR).Measurements and main resultsThe mean age was 41.80 ± 16.00 years, and the mean Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score was 23.20 ± 5.80. The median preoperative ECMO support duration was 168.00 h (interquartile range [IQR], 132.00–216.00 h). All patients required preoperative mechanical ventilation. The mean surgical duration was 432.13 ± 65.43 min. Postoperative ECMO was maintained in seven cases, with a median duration of 72.00 h (IQR, 24.00–120.00 h). Three in-hospital deaths occurred, resulting in 12 successful discharges. The median postoperative intensive care unit stay was 11.00 d (IQR, 4.50–14.50 d), and the median postoperative hospital stay was 35.00 d (IQR, 20.50–55.50 d). The postoperative 30-day mortality rate was 20% (3/15). Patients who underwent ECPR had a higher 30-day mortality rate (33.3%, 2/6) than those who did not (11.1%, 1/9), though the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.527). The cumulative 1-year survival rate for all 15 patients was 60%, while for the 12 discharged patients, it was 75%.ConclusionsVA-ECMO can effectively stabilize patients with end-stage heart failure, serving as a viable short-term bridge to heart transplantation for select individuals. Appropriate patient selection via risk factor stratification is necessary to improve short-term outcomes. Direct bridging to heart transplantation following ECPR is not an ideal therapeutic strategy and requires careful consideration of intermediate risk-mitigation steps.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-03981-6.

Introduction

Heart transplantation is currently the best treatment for end-stage heart failure [1], but donor shortages cause many patients to deteriorate or even die while waiting for an available transplant [2]. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is an emergency mechanical circulatory support technology widely used as a heart transplantation bridging strategy, and patients receiving ECMO are often given priority for donor heart allocation [3]. In patients with acute heart failure or cardiogenic shock, veno-arterial ECMO (VA-ECMO) effectively maintains haemodynamic stability, prolonging survival until transplantation is possible [4]. However, the therapeutic effect and value of VA-ECMO as a bridge to heart transplantation needs to be further explored. Therefore, the aim of this study was to describe the comprehensive management pathway for VA-ECMO as a transitional therapy for heart transplantation (from ECMO initiation to post-transplant outcomes), with a focus on analysing patient selection criteria, complication management, and short-term survival rates. This endeavour seeks to address the evidence gap concerning standardised single-centre ECMO bridging protocols for transplantation.

Methods

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Supplementary Material 1).

Participants

Between April 2014 and September 2023, our centre performed a total of 78 heart transplantations. Fifteen patients received VA-ECMO support preoperatively, representing a bridging rate of approximately 19%. Inclusion criteria for this retrospective study were as follows: (1) meeting the diagnostic criteria for end-stage heart failure and New York Heart Association (NYHA) cardiac function class IV; (2) meeting cardiac transplant criteria following multidisciplinary team assessment and having been placed on the cardiac transplant waiting list prior to ECMO initiation; and (3) age ≥ 12 years. Exclusion criteria included (1) presence of other severe systemic diseases, such as malignant tumours, irreversible liver damage, and renal failure precluding transplantation; (2) use of ECMO support for less than 24 h; and (3) presence of irreversible pulmonary hypertension or other contraindications to heart transplantation.

Data collection and observational indicators

We recorded baseline characteristics such as age, sex, aetiology of heart failure, NYHA class, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), APACHE II score, brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) concentration, troponin concentration, lactate concentration, creatinine concentration, and ventilatory support status. Application of the SCAI (Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions) classification for cardiogenic shock was used to supplement preoperative clinical stratification. The main outcome indicators included duration of ECMO support, operative time, duration of postoperative mechanical ventilation, length of intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital stay, and in-hospital mortality. We also recorded postoperative complications such as infection, bleeding, renal insufficiency requiring continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), acute rejection, liver impairment, acute cerebral infarction, arrhythmia, multiple organ failure, and primary graft dysfunction (PGD). Simultaneously, we collected key donor cardiac characteristics, including cold ischaemia time and functional status, to enhance the clinical dataset.

Neurological function assessment

Neurological screening is pivotal in preventing futile transplantation, particularly for patients supported with ECPR. Rigorous preoperative neurological evaluations were conducted to rule out irreversible neurological damage and ensure that transplant candidates possessed sufficient potential for neurological recovery.

Specific assessment components included:

Clinical neurological status assessment: Monitoring consciousness levels using the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), supplemented by ongoing dynamic evaluation of neurological signs and reflex activity. Patients were generally required to have a GCS score ≥ 8, demonstrate adequate spontaneous breathing capacity, and show signs of arousal before being considered for active transplantation.

Neuroimaging examinations: Routine head CT scans were performed to exclude extensive cerebral haemorrhage, significant cerebral infarction, and severe edema. Where necessary, brain MRI was employed to refine the assessment of brain tissue damage.

Electroencephalogram (EEG) monitoring: Continuous EEG monitoring was implemented in select patients to exclude non-convulsive status epilepticus or low-voltage, non-reactive states, aiding in the assessment of brain functional recovery potential.

Multidisciplinary team (MDT) assessment: A dedicated MDT, comprising experts from Cardiothoracic Surgery, Critical Care Medicine, and Neurology, jointly evaluated all imaging, clinical, and electrophysiological data to holistically determine the presence of irreversible brain injury and dynamically adjust management strategies. This process ensured that the potential for neurological recovery was maximized.

Application and withdrawal of VA-ECMO

Application of implanted ECMO

All patients underwent percutaneous cannulation of the right femoral artery and vein under surgical guidance. A longitudinal incision was made over the groin to expose the femoral vessels. The femoral artery and vein were then punctured, a guidewire was inserted, and the arterial and venous cannulae were advanced over the wire. In adults, the venous cannula was typically inserted 40–50 cm, with the tip positioned near the right atrial junction, and the arterial cannula was inserted 15–20 cm. Cannula depth in adolescents was adjusted based on height. Prior to cannulation, 0.5 mg/kg of heparin was administered intravenously. A MAQUET or Medtronic ECMO circuit with a Rotaflow centrifugal pump was used. An ECMO loop was primed with 1000 mL of colloid solution containing heparin. The initial ECMO flow rate was set at 4.0–5.0 L/min and adjusted to maintain a target flow of 2–3 L/min and an activated coagulation time (ACT) of approximately 160 s with intermittent heparin administration. Flow rates were further adjusted based on hemodynamics and oxygenation indices. To ensure adequate oxygen delivery during ECMO support, hemoglobin levels were maintained above 80 g/L. If distal limb ischemia was observed, a reperfusion catheter was placed.

Postoperative ECMO withdrawal

Withdrawal of the device was considered if the patient’s vital signs and haemodynamics remained stable. Stable haemodynamics were defined as a mean arterial pressure (MAP) > 50 mmHg in children and > 60 mmHg in adults, lactate < 2 mmol/L, LVEF > 40%, dobutamine ≤ 5 µg/(kg-min) or norepinephrine ≤ 0.05 µg/(kg-min), central venous oxygen saturation (SvO2) > 60%, oxygenation index > 200 mmHg (1 mmHg = 0.133 kPa), and ECMO-assisted flow rate < 1500 mL/min, maintained for 2–3 h. The cannula and tubing are left in place for 1 h after ECMO withdrawal, the pump is stopped, and anticoagulation is continued. Blood flow within the cannula is maintained by the patient’s voluntary circulation (arterial end by arterial pressure, venous end by venous return pressure). If the haemodynamics remained stable for at least 1 h after withdrawal of the ECMO device, the corresponding pipeline was withdrawn; otherwise, the machine was re-transferred, and withdrawal was attempted again after 1–3 h of haemodynamic stabilisation. These scenarios were regarded as a successful withdrawal of the machine. Withdrawal was considered a failure if the patient’s haemodynamics did not meet the criteria for withdrawal and the ECMO device was forced to be withdrawn anyway, if the patient died even though the device was not withdrawn, or if the patient’s condition deteriorated after the withdrawal of the device and the duration of survival was less than 48 h.

Statistical analyses

SPSS 22.0 software was used for data analysis. Categorical data were expressed as counts and percentages. Normally distributed continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed data were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR, 25th to 75th percentile). Comparisons between two groups were performed using the independent t-test for normally distributed data and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline information

The baseline characteristics of the 15 patients are detailed in Table 1. The cohort was predominantly male (13/15, 86.7%), with a mean age of 41.80 ± 16.00 years. The primary etiologies of heart failure were ischemic cardiomyopathy (7/15, 46.7%) and dilated cardiomyopathy (5/15, 33.3%). All patients were in NYHA functional class IV.

Preoperatively, patients exhibited severe cardiac dysfunction, with a mean LVEF of 28.27% ± 15.60%. All patients were in profound cardiogenic shock and were classified as SCAI Stage D or E. Biomarkers indicated significant myocardial injury and systemic hypoperfusion, with a median BNP of 10,350.50 pg/mL (IQR, 5659.25–14212.50) and a median lactate of 6.90 mmol/L (IQR, 3.90–11.50). The cohort presented with significant multi-organ dysfunction, reflected by a mean APACHE II score of 23.20 ± 5.80, corresponding to a predicted mortality of 41.70% ± 18.80%. Four patients (26.7%) had a serious infection, and two (13.3%) had pre-existing liver failure prior to ECMO initiation.

Donor hearts were procured from standard criteria donors. The median donor heart cold ischemia time was 3.0 h (IQR, 2.5–3.8 h), and all donor hearts had a documented LVEF > 55%.

ECMO support

Perioperative details and outcomes are summarized in Table 2. All 15 patients received VA-ECMO support for cardiogenic shock. Six patients (40%) required ECPR due to refractory cardiac arrest; the mean duration from cardiac arrest to ECPR initiation for this subgroup was 50 ± 8.43 min. No immediate, severe neurological injuries were detected post-ECPR initiation following our institutional assessment protocol. Preoperative ECMO was combined with an intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) in six cases.

The median duration of preoperative ECMO support was 168.00 h (IQR, 132.00–216.00). Following successful transplantation, seven patients required continued postoperative ECMO support, primarily for primary graft dysfunction (PGD) (n = 3) or severe pulmonary hypertension with right heart failure (n = 3). The median duration of postoperative ECMO support for these patients was 72.00 h (IQR, 24.00–120.00).

Postoperative clinical effects

Postoperative complications are detailed in Table 3. The most common significant complications included infection (n = 3, 20.0%), arrhythmia (n = 3, 20.0%), and the need for CRRT (n = 5, 33.3%).

There were three in-hospital deaths, resulting in a 30-day mortality rate of 20% (3/15). Causes of death included progressive PGD (n = 1), sudden cardiac arrest due to ventricular fibrillation (n = 1), and complications following re-thoracotomy for bleeding (n = 1). The remaining 12 patients (80%) were successfully discharged. Post-transplant cardiac function improved dramatically in survivors, with a mean LVEF of 65.85% ± 5.51% at one week. The median length of ICU stay was 11.00 days (IQR, 4.50–14.50), and the median postoperative hospital stay was 35.00 days (IQR, 20.50–55.50).

The cumulative 1-year survival rate for the entire cohort of 15 patients was 60% (9/15). For the 12 patients who survived to hospital discharge, the 1-year survival rate was 75% (9/12). As shown in Table 4, the 30-day mortality rate was higher in the ECPR group (33.3%, 2/6) compared to the non-ECPR group (11.1%, 1/9), but this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.527), likely due to the small sample size.

Discussion

In recent years, as the number of patients with end-stage cardiac disease has increased and the waiting list for heart transplantation has lengthened, the proportion of patients requiring preoperative mechanical circulatory assistance has risen significantly. While durable left ventricular assist devices are the predominant long-term bridging strategy internationally, VA-ECMO remains a critical life-saving tool for patients in profound cardiogenic shock or with acute biventricular failure who are not immediate candidates for other devices [5, 6]. Our study presents a single-center experience, demonstrating that despite the high-risk nature of this cohort, VA-ECMO can serve as an effective short-term bridge to transplantation for carefully selected patients.

Our observed 30-day postoperative mortality of 20% is consistent with rates reported in larger international registries, which typically range from 20% to 35% [7–9]. Furthermore, our 1-year survival rate of 60% for the entire cohort, and 75% for hospital survivors, suggests a promising long-term prognosis for those who successfully navigate the perilous early postoperative period. This finding aligns with a retrospective study from the French National Registry [6] which, while showing lower overall survival in ECMO-bridged patients compared to non-bridged patients, supports the notion that early survivors can achieve favorable long-term outcomes. This suggests that while the initial risk is high, successful bridging can translate into durable survival.

Patient selection is paramount to achieving these outcomes. Our cohort’s actual mortality was slightly lower than the APACHE II predicted mortality, suggesting that rigorous patient screening using risk stratification tools may help identify candidates who can benefit most from this aggressive strategy. International studies have identified various risk factors, including advanced age and severe multi-organ failure, that negatively impact outcomes [10]. The prognostic value of scoring systems like the APACHE score has been confirmed as a significant predictor of survival in this population [8, 11]. As there is no significant difference in the clinical application of APACHE II and APACHE IV scores [12], our use of APACHE II provides a valid assessment. Our satisfactory results may be attributable to our cohort’s younger mean age and stringent selection criteria, demonstrating that risk stratification is key.

Previous studies have established that the use of ECMO and other short-term mechanical assist devices are independent risk factors for poorer post-transplant outcomes [13]. Our findings highlight that the highest-risk subset within this group consists of patients requiring ECPR. The 30-day mortality in our ECPR group was 33.3%, threefold higher than in the non-ECPR group. Though not statistically significant due to the small sample size, this trend is clinically important. In cases of refractory cardiac arrest, ECPR is an essential, life-saving intervention. Early ECPR initiation can shorten the duration of systemic hypoperfusion, increase coronary perfusion pressure, and improve rates of ROSC and successful defibrillation [14, 15]. A randomized controlled trial by Yannopoulos et al. demonstrated that an advanced reperfusion strategy including ECPR improved outcomes in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest [16], and preclinical studies have shown it can reduce the risk of irreversible ischemic injury [17]. However, the profound physiological insult of cardiac arrest and resuscitation predisposes these patients to severe post-transplant complications. Given this elevated mortality, direct bridging from ECPR to heart transplantation is not an ideal therapeutic strategy, as it may lead to the wastage of scarce donor organs. We contend that an intermediate period of stabilization and assessment is necessary to mitigate risk and evaluate for neurological and end-organ recovery before proceeding with transplantation.

Combined circulatory support strategies were utilized in our cohort. Six patients received concomitant IABP support. In cardiogenic shock, an IABP can augment coronary perfusion and reduce left ventricular afterload, which is particularly beneficial as VA-ECMO can increase afterload [18]. The combination of IABP with peripheral VA-ECMO has been shown to reduce mortality and improve weaning rates from mechanical support [19]. Additionally, eight patients required CRRT at some point during their admission. The need for renal support is common in these critically ill patients due to pre-existing renal dysfunction, hemodynamic instability, or systemic inflammation from the ECMO circuit. While the inflammatory response and endothelial injury are contributing factors, there is no clear evidence that the combination of ECMO and CRRT independently increases mortality; rather, it is a marker of the patient’s underlying severity of illness [20].

Complications remain a major challenge. Bleeding and infection were the most significant adverse events, consistent with the known risks of ECMO and the profound immunosuppression following transplantation [21, 22]. Prolonged ECMO duration is a well-established risk factor for bloodstream infections [23–25], emphasizing the need for transplantation as expeditiously as possible once a suitable donor is available. Our center’s short median waiting time of 7 days, driven by high-urgency allocation policies for ECMO-supported patients, may have helped to mitigate some of these time-dependent complications.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, its single-center, retrospective design and small sample size (N = 15) limit the generalisability of our findings and expose the results to potential selection bias. Second, the small sample size precludes any meaningful multivariate risk factor analysis and renders the study underpowered to detect statistically significant differences between subgroups, such as the ECPR and non-ECPR cohorts. Our findings should therefore be considered descriptive and hypothesis-generating. Third, this study did not include an analysis of patients who were placed on ECMO but died before receiving a transplant. This “waitlist mortality” cohort is essential for calculating the true success rate of the bridging strategy, and future research should incorporate these patients to build a comprehensive risk prediction model. Finally, our analysis of donor factors was limited; a more detailed investigation of donor-recipient matching could provide further insights.

Conclusion

VA-ECMO can effectively prolong survival in patients with end-stage heart failure and refractory cardiogenic shock, serving as a viable short-term bridge to heart transplantation in appropriately selected patients. Rigorous patient selection based on risk factor stratification is essential to optimize short-term outcomes. For patients requiring ECPR, the postoperative mortality risk is substantial. Therefore, direct bridging to transplantation following ECPR is not an ideal therapeutic strategy; an intermediate phase of therapy and risk management should be prioritized to ensure the best possible use of donor organs and improve patient survival.
