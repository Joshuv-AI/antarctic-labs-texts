A step-by-step guide for isolating soluble and insoluble chromatin followed by next-generation sequencing library preparation
Authors: Shan Hua, Miraz A Sadi, Brandon J Park, Patrick J Murphy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMapping epigenetic features is essential for investigating chromatin regulation and gene expression control. We recently found that crosslinking- and sonication-based techniques preferentially exclude insoluble chromatin, restricting the investigation of large portions of eukaryotic genomes. Here, we present a protocol for overcoming this issue. We describe steps for chromatin fractionation, DNA extraction, and preparation of next-generation sequencing (NGS) libraries. This strategy can identify underrepresented heterochromatic regions, including repetitive elements and centromeres, permitting functional investigation of regions that are otherwise undetectable.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Park et al.1Subject areas: Sequencing, ChIPseq, Chromatin immunoprecipitation, ChIP

Before you begin

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) has been used to generate epigenetics landscapes and map chromatin features since its development in the mid 2000s.2 However, certain genomic regions have been found to be over-represented or under-represented in ChIP-seq samples, largely due to methods of extraction. Indeed, separate studies have demonstrated that genomic regions harboring active histone modifications, such as tri-methylation at lysine 4 of histone H3 (H3K4me3),1,3,4 tend to be artificially enriched when applying ChIP-Seq methods, while sub-telomeric regions tend to be depleted.5 Those sub-telomeric regions, which are resistant to sonication, are known to possess repressive histone features, including tri-methylation at lysine 9 and 27 of histone H3 (H3K9me3 and H3K27me3, respectively).4 We and others have found such regions (enriched for heterochromatic features) to be retained in the insoluble pellet which is often discarded when performing ChIP-based methods. To better study these features, we have developed a method to recover soluble and insoluble chromatin fractions, which can then be used for investigation of DNA or bound proteins.

The protocol presented here provides detailed steps for isolation of soluble and insoluble chromatin fractions, resulting from crosslinking and sonication samples, followed by DNA extraction and preparation of genomic libraries for next-generation sequencing (NGS).

For the sake of simplicity, we applied this protocol to mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs), but this method could easily be modified to accommodate other cell types or tissues. When applying to other cell types, the sonication cycles, crosslinking, and Tn5 transposase tagmentation time may need to be adjusted.

CRITICAL: All the steps need to be performed in DNase-free conditions.

Innovation

This protocol provides an efficient and straightforward method to extract DNA from insoluble chromatin after performing crosslinked nuclear fractionation, followed by the generation of NGS libraries, using Tn5 transposase tagmentation of naked DNA. For decades, biases of ChIP-Seq-based methods have restricted analysis of insoluble chromatin regions, and our method provides a means to overcome this deficiency. Our strategy allows investigators to selectively purify genomic regions that would otherwise be discarded, enabling investigation of proteins or genomics regions that are undetectable using standard ChIP-Seq strategies.

Chromatin sonication and fractionation

Timing: 1 h

This section describes the procedure for sonicating chromatin and separating the cell lysate into cell pellet and supernatant fractions.

1.Cells sonication.a.Equally aliquot 500 μL of resuspended lysed cells into 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes from 2 mL non-crosslinked cell lysates and 2 mL crosslinked cell lysates, respectively.i.Label each tube corresponding with sonication cycle numbers as 0, 1, 2, and 3.b.Sonicate each sample for 0, 1, 2, or 3 cycles using EpiShear Cooled Sonication Platform, respectively.Note: A cycle setting is defined as one 10 s pulse, followed by 20 s rest, and Amplitude 30%. There should be 5 min in total of sonication ON per cycle.c.Keep tubes on wet ice between cycles to prevent over heating of lysates during sonication.

DNA extraction

Timing: 12–16 h

This section describes the procedures for extracting DNA from supernatant and cell pellet fractions.

3.DNA isolation from supernatant and pellet samples.a.Add 5 μL Proteinase K (20mg/mL) and 20 μL 0.5M EDTA pH8.0 to each supernatant sample. Vortex to mix for 2 s.b.To each pellet sample, resuspend the pellet in 500 μL SDS Lysis Buffer, and continue to add 5 μL Proteinase K (20mg/mL), and 20 μL 0.5M EDTA pH8.0.c.Break up pellet samples using a 20-gauge needle combined with a 1-3 mL syringe. Using syringe plunger action to mix the lysed cells.i.Vortex to mix for 5 – 10 s to ensure pellets are effectively broken apart.ii.Continue this step until visible clumps of lysed cells become homogenized.d.Incubate both supernatant and pellet samples at 50°C for 1 hour.e.After incubation, add 50 μL 10% SDS to each sample.f.Continue breaking up pellet samples using the 20-gauge needle/syringe, as in step 3c.g.Incubate all samples for 12 – 16 h (overnight) at 65°C.

Expected outcomes

This protocol describes the steps for cell harvesting with or without crosslinking, fractionating chromatin, DNA extraction from both supernatant and pellet chromatin samples, and generating the NGS DNA libraries.

Gel electrophoresis can help us confirm DNA size fragmentation from both supernatant and pellet samples after performing sonication (Figure 1). The supernatant portion tends to have accessible regions, so the chromatin is easily sheered into small fragments during sonication. Higher sonication cycle numbers result in more small DNA fragments in both soluble and insoluble fractions. The DNA fragment size is much larger when extracted from insoluble pellet samples when minimal sonication (1 cycle) is applied, and DNA fragments becomes smaller after increasing the number of sonication cycles, indicating that the chromatin regions resistant to minimal sonication cycles possess higher molecular weight DNA fragments, which tend to include heterochromatic regions in the genome.1

Subsequent analysis of samples cross-linked and sonicated to varying degrees, such as supernatant or pellet samples sonicated with either 1 or 3 cycles, can allow researchers to assess the genomic contexts for each chromatin fraction (GSE288759) (Figure 2). Using the strategy we outline here, subsequent studies investigating cell state transitions, such as those occurring during developmental differentiation or metastasis, may allow researchers to investigate how particular genome region or DNA binding proteins transition between soluble and insoluble portions of chromatin. Such studies could shed light on how separate nuclear compartments influence gene expression patterns or cellular programming events. Altogether, this protocol provides an easy and straightforward method for isolation of DNA from both the insoluble and soluble nuclear fraction following cross-linking and sonication, permitting studies of chromatin regions that were previously discarded when applying ChIP-based methods.

Limitations

There are several limitations in this protocol. Firstly, we prepared cells samples with paraformaldehyde crosslinking, and the amount of paraformaldehyde needs to be adjusted to ensure that chromatin is not over- or under-crosslinked. Over-crosslinking would cause chromatin to become more rigid and remain trapped in the pellet. Thus, it would be hard to shear during sonication. Under-crosslinking would cause unstable DNA-protein interaction, which might be disrupted during sonication.

In addition, sonication cycle number also needs to be optimized. If the chromatin is over-sonicated, it would disrupt DNA-protein interaction and DNA fragments would be over-sheared. If the chromatin is under-sonicated, the DNA size fragment would be larger and potentially stay in the pellet, resulting in a low yield of DNA from the supernatant samples. Including negative control samples, such as non-cross-linked samples, can enable researchers to compare DNA fragment sizes before and after sonication, mitigating potential issues with cross-linking or sonication effectiveness.
