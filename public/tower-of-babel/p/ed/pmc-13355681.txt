Response to the correspondence regarding “age-inappropriate white matter injury reveals hidden cerebral small vessel disease burden and mortality risk”
Authors: Pei-Lin Lee, Wen-Yu Ou Yang, Kun-Hsien Chou, Wei-Ju Lee, Chen-Yuan Kuo, Shing-Huei Lin, Liang-Kung Chen, Ching-Po Lin, Chih-Ping Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Normative WMH-based evaluation framework improves early detection and risk stratification of covert CSVD.•Age-inappropriate WMH burden identifies advanced CSVD independent of chronological age.•Higher WMH deviation associates with adverse vascular profiles despite similar or younger age.•WMH deviation shows graded associations with multidomain cognitive impairment and gray matter atrophy.•Severe WMH deviation predicts increased long-term all-cause mortality (HR ∼1.9) independent of age.Keywords: White matter hyperintensities, Covert cerebral small vessel disease, Normative model, Brain chart, Hierarchical bayesian regression

Dear Editor,

We thank Tribhuvan and Phatke for their thoughtful comments on our article, “Age-Inappropriate White Matter Injury Reveals Hidden Cerebral Small Vessel Disease Burden and Mortality Risk.” We appreciate their recognition of the potential value of age- and sex-referenced normative modeling for individualized assessment of white matter hyperintensity (WMH) burden. Their correspondence raises important issues regarding biological specificity, prognostic utility, and network-level interpretation, and provides an opportunity to clarify the conceptual framework and intended scope of our study.

First, the authors suggest that the WMH deviation metric may partly reflect cumulative vascular risk exposure because hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and metabolic dysfunction were not included in the normative reference model. We agree that vascular risk factors are deeply involved in the pathophysiology of WMH accumulation. However, this is precisely why we did not adjust them away in the construction of the normative framework. WMH in the context of cerebral small vessel disease is not intended to be interpreted as a vascular-risk-free imaging phenotype. Rather, vascular risk factors are part of the biological pathway through which clinically relevant ischemic white matter injury develops. If hypertension, diabetes, and related cardiometabolic exposures were incorporated into the reference model as expected covariates, the resulting deviation score would estimate WMH burden beyond vascular risk exposure, but it would no longer represent the vascular-related ischemic WMH burden that is clinically meaningful in CSVD. In our view, such adjustment would risk normalizing disease-related injury among individuals with long-standing vascular risk factors and thereby reduce the ability to identify those whose WMH burden is pathologically excessive relative to age- and sex-expected norms. Our goal was not to isolate a risk-factor-independent residual, but to identify disproportionate WMH accumulation after accounting for the dominant demographic determinants of WMH—age and sex—while preserving vascular-risk-related disease expression.

Second, we agree that future studies may evaluate whether normative WMH grading improves formal risk prediction beyond conventional vascular risk factors, absolute WMH volume, and existing clinical risk scores. However, our study was not designed as a comparative prediction-model study, and we did not claim that WMH deviation outperforms absolute WMH burden or established risk markers. The main contribution was conceptual and translational: to provide a framework for interpreting WMH burden in relation to what would be expected for a person’s age and sex. Absolute WMH volume remains important, but it does not answer whether the observed lesion burden is proportionate or disproportionate to normative aging. Our illustrative cases showed that individuals with similar absolute WMH ratios could be assigned different normative severity categories, with distinct clinical profiles, depending on age- and sex-referenced expectations. Thus, the value of the framework lies not only in statistical prediction, but also in clinical interpretation: a modest WMH burden in a younger individual may represent more advanced disease than a similar burden in an older individual. We agree that c-statistics, net reclassification improvement, calibration, and decision-curve analyses would be useful in future implementation studies, particularly if the goal is to build a clinical decision-support tool. Nevertheless, absence of these analyses does not negate the present finding that age- and sex-referenced WMH deviation captures clinically relevant information associated with vascular profiles, cognition, gray matter volume, and mortality.

Third, the authors correctly note that CSVD is increasingly understood as a network disorder rather than a disease of isolated lesions or selected regions. We fully agree with this perspective. In the present study, we selected global gray matter volume and predefined hippocampal and thalamic regions to provide an initial, biologically interpretable assessment of gray matter vulnerability associated with disproportionate WMH burden. These analyses were not intended to provide a complete neuroanatomical map of CSVD-related brain injury. Rather, they served as an initial validation that age-inappropriate WMH accumulation is accompanied by structural brain vulnerability beyond visible white matter lesions. We consider whole-brain morphometry, cortical thickness mapping, structural covariance analysis, and connectome-based approaches to be important next steps. Indeed, one of the major goals of our normative WMH grading framework is to enable future studies to elucidate CSVD mechanisms at the brain-network level, including how disproportionate WMH burden relates to gray matter degeneration, disconnection, cognitive-domain vulnerability, and longitudinal brain aging trajectories.

In summary, we appreciate the opportunity to clarify that our normative WMH deviation metric was not intended to remove vascular biology from WMH interpretation, nor to claim definitive superiority over conventional prediction models. Instead, it was developed to address a fundamental clinical problem: the strong age-dependence of WMH makes absolute lesion burden difficult to interpret across individuals. By estimating the degree to which WMH burden deviates from age- and sex-expected norms, this framework provides a complementary approach for identifying clinically meaningful, disproportionate white matter injury in covert CSVD. Future work should extend this method to multimodal CSVD markers, formal risk-prediction metrics, diverse populations, and network-level neuroanatomical analyses. We believe such studies will further define the role of normative WMH grading as a tool for individualized risk stratification and mechanistic investigation in cerebral small vessel disease.

Sincerely,

Chih-Ping Chung, MD, PhD
