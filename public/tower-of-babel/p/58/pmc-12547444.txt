Missed inguinoscrotal bladder hernia following herniorrhaphy: Diagnostic pitfalls and lessons from a rare case
Authors: Renandha Septaryan Yustira, Sean Peter Haruman, Robertus Bebet Prasetyo, Cosmas Gora Triaswhoro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Inguinoscrotal bladder hernia is a rare entity, accounting for 1–3 % of inguinal hernias, and often misdiagnosed as typical intestinal hernia. We report a 60-year-old man with prior herniorrhaphy who presented with persistent right inguinoscrotal swelling, which decreased after voiding. CT revealed bladder herniation with catheter balloon in the scrotum. The patient underwent testicular exploration, partial cystectomy, and hernia repair, with a favorable postoperative outcome. This case underscores the diagnostic challenges of bladder hernia and highlights the importance of preoperative imaging when inguinoscrotal swelling fluctuates with urination.

Introduction

Inguinoscrotal bladder hernia, also known as scrotal cystocele is a rare finding, reported in only 1–3 % of inguinal hernia cases.1 Due to nonspecific symptoms, diagnosis is often delayed, with many cases detected intraoperatively. Early recognition is crucial to prevent complications such as bladder injury during herniorrhaphy. Herein, we report a case of a large inguinoscrotal bladder hernia in an adult male who initially underwent inguinal herniorrhaphy but developed persistent swelling and urinary symptoms postoperatively. This case highlights the diagnostic pitfalls of inguinoscrotal bladder hernia and emphasizes the importance of early recognition and appropriate surgical management to prevent complications.

Case presentation

A 60-year-old male was referred to our urology clinic after undergoing surgery for a right inguinal hernia at another hospital. He reported a mass extending from the right inguinal area into the right scrotum for the past six months, which temporarily decreased after voiding each morning but enlarged again during the day. The mass was painless, unrelated to physical activity, and not associated with any reproductive complaints. He had no significant comorbidities or prior surgical history, but initially sought alternative therapy and postponed medical consultation until symptoms became bothersome.

At the referring hospital, the patient underwent herniorrhaphy under the impression of a lateral inguinal hernia containing small intestine or fat. Within hours post-surgery, however, he experienced voiding difficulty accompanied by acute scrotal pain and swelling. A second scrotal incision was performed for suspected hematoma, but intraoperatively urine leakage was encountered, leading to the diagnosis of a vesical hernia. Because of limited facilities and expertise, the wound was closed and the patient was managed with a urinary catheter before being referred to our center.

On physical examination, a right inguinoscrotal mass was observed, along with two prior surgical scars (inguinal and scrotal) and an indwelling urinary catheter. The right testis was palpable and normal in consistency (Fig. 1). Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated herniation of the urinary bladder into the scrotum, with the catheter balloon located inside the herniated segment, and mild bilateral hydronephrosis-hydroureter (Fig. 2). Testicular exploration and hernia repair were performed in collaboration with a digestive surgeon. Dense adhesions from the previous operations and a narrow hernia neck made safe reduction of the bladder difficult. Therefore, a partial cystectomy of the irreducible segment (measuring 7 × 4.5 × 3 cm) was performed, followed by hernia repair. Postoperatively, the patient remained in good condition and was discharged on postoperative day three with a catheter in place. At one-week follow-up, ultrasound evaluation showed normal bladder contour, minimal scrotal edema, and no hydronephrosis. The catheter was removed, and uroflowmetry performed at one week and one month after operation demonstrated satisfactory voiding results.

Discussion

This case involved a large right-sided inguinoscrotal hernia with a history of inguinal herniorrhaphy. Bladder involvement in inguinal hernia is uncommon and most often occurs on the right side, typically in elderly in elderly men with risk factors such as obesity, chronic bladder outlet obstruction, reduce bladder tone, and weakness of pelvic floor muscles.2, 3, 4, 5 However, the differential diagnoses of an inguinoscrotal mass are extensive, including hernias, cystic lesions, infectious or inflammatory processes, vascular abnormalities, neoplasms, and other less common causes.6

The clinical presentation is highly variable. Some patients remain entirely asymptomatic, particularly in cases of small hernias, whereas others develop inguinoscrotal swelling with or without pain, or lower urinary tract symptoms, such as dysuria, nocturia, increased frequency, urgency, or urinary retention.7,8 A classic but not universal feature is Mery's sign, in which scrotal swelling reduces after voiding or patients require manual compression of the scrotum to complete micturition.2,5 Because of this nonspecific spectrum, bladder hernia is often mistaken for a more typical inguinal hernia containing bowel or fat.

Preoperative diagnosis of bladder hernia remains challenging due to its nonspecific symptoms. Studies suggest that only a minority of cases (around 7 %) are diagnosed before surgery, while most are discovered intraoperatively (77 %) or postoperatively (16 %) when complications occur.2,4 Therefore, imaging is essential whenever clinical suspicion exists. Ultrasound may detect a hypoechoic extension of the bladder into the scrotum and assess for hydronephrosis, whereas voiding cystourethrography has traditionally been considered the gold standard, demonstrating the characteristic “dog-ear” or “dumbbell” bladder contour.6,9 In our case, CT scan provided sufficient detail to establish the diagnosis and guide surgical planning. Compared with voiding cystourethrography and ultrasound, CT scan offers a superior ability to identify structures within the hernia sac and helps exclude differential diagnoses.

Surgery remains the treatment of choice for symptomatic or complicated cases. Standard management consists of careful reduction of the bladder followed by hernia repair, with or without mesh reinforcement. Partial cystectomy is rarely required and is reserved for situations in which reduction is impossible or unsafe, such as bladder wall necrosis, a herniated bladder diverticulum, bladder injury during hernioplasty, a constricted hernia neck, or a bladder tumor.10, 11, 12, 13 In some patients, especially those who are asymptomatic or have mild symptoms, a conservative approach involving watchful waiting or intermittent self-catheterization may be appropriate.14 In our patient, a tight hernia neck and dense adhesions from previous surgery prevented safe reduction, thereby necessitating partial cystectomy.

Conclusion

Inguinoscrotal bladder hernia, though uncommon, should be considered in the differential diagnosis of men presenting with inguinoscrotal swelling, especially when the size of the mass changes with micturition. Early recognition through careful clinical assessment and appropriate imaging is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and intraoperative complications. Surgical repair remains the cornerstone of management, with partial cystectomy reserved for cases where reduction is not feasible, as demonstrated in our patient with an excellent postoperative outcome.
