Characteristics of nursing profession self-efficacy and associated factors: a multiple regression analysis
Authors: Yoko Takeuchi, Koji Tanaka, Takako Wada, Mayu Seto, Kaede Fujimura, Mayumi Kato, Takako Isihara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEnhancing nursing expertise contributes to the advancement of healthcare as a whole. Therefore, it is crucial that nurses be able to perform specialized tasks. However, the factors related to nursing profession self-efficacy are insufficiently understood. This study aimed to theoretically and comprehensively examine the factors associated with nursing profession self-efficacy.MethodsThis cross-sectional study involved 325 nurses from seven facilities in the Chubu region of Japan. Based on prior research, factors influencing nursing profession self-efficacy were established as professional autonomy, communication skills with patients, work motivation, self‑education, perceived stress, and basic attributes. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, nonparametric tests, and multiple regression analysis.ResultsNursing profession self-efficacy was positively associated with work motivation (β = 0.384), self‑education (β = 0.246), professional autonomy (β = 0.236), and communication skills with patients (β = 0.157) (all p <.001). In contrast, perceived stress and basic attributes were not statistically significant in the final model. Model fit indices (R =.756, R2 = 0.571, adjusted R2 = 0.557; Durbin–Watson = 1.988) indicated no issues with residual independence or autocorrelation.ConclusionThis study identified work motivation, self‑education, professional autonomy, and communication skills with patients as factors associated with nursing profession self-efficacy. These findings suggest that workplace environments that support professional meaning in daily work and promote continuous learning may help strengthen nursing profession self-efficacy among nurses. Organizational cultures that encourage autonomous decision-making and educational programs that incorporate practical communication training may also be useful for supporting nursing profession self-efficacy.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Self-efficacy, Motivation, Autonomy, Self‑education, Communication, Professional, Multiple regression analysis

Background

Nurses are core professionals in healthcare settings, and it can be said that enhancing their professionalism contributes to improving the overall quality of healthcare. In contrast, a decline in professionalism is said to lead to increased turnover and decreased productivity, and thus enhancing nursing professionalism is crucial [1]. However, nursing professionalism is diverse, and the duties of nurses are complex. Nurses must engage in multifaceted and complex professional practices, including advanced clinical judgment, interdisciplinary collaboration, communication with patients, educational roles and the self-improvement that accompanies them, and ethical decision-making. To perform these complex roles, in addition to knowledge and skills, we consider that nurses must be confident that they can act appropriately as professionals, a concept known as nursing profession self-efficacy (NPSE) [2].

Based on previous studies [2–7] as well as Bandura’s social cognitive theory [8] and the concept of self-efficacy [9–11], the present study defines NPSE as a belief that nurses can accomplish professional tasks in situations specific to nursing practice. Self-efficacy in nursing practice directly contributes to improved quality of care, including nurses’ clinical judgment and responses to patients, and we consider it to be a central psychological foundation for nurses to demonstrate their professionalism.

Previous studies have reported that NPSE has a positive effect on the quality of family nursing in the field of pediatric nursing [12]. It has also been shown that the involvement of nurses with high NPSE is a predictor of parents’ recognition that family-centered care is being practiced [13]. Furthermore, in the fields of intensive care and emergency care, it has been confirmed that NPSE has a positive effect on patient safety and the quality of care [14]. Moreover, correlations with comprehensive nursing practice competence and associations with mental health literacy have been suggested [15], and relationships with job satisfaction have also been reported [16]. From these findings, it has been shown that NPSE plays an important role in clinical practice.

In addition, the development of the Nursing Profession Self-Efficacy Scale (NPSS) [2], a short version of the NPSS [17], and an increasing number of translated versions suggest the importance of this concept and the high level of need for it. The translated versions include the Albanian version [3] and its short version [18], the Persian version [4], two Korean versions [5, 6], and the Japanese version [7], with international dissemination progressing in recent years. However, few studies have examined, in a multifaceted manner, the associated factors influencing NPSE. To enhance NPSE, it is important to identify associated factors, and we believe that theoretical and comprehensive research is necessary to effectively develop organizational capacity and educational programs. In addition, regarding the associated factors, a multifaceted structure is assumed with multiple interrelated aspects, including judgment, behavior, interpersonal relationships, and psychological states in nursing practice. Thus, clarifying these relationships may lead to suggestions for beneficial organizational support systems for nurses.

Against this backdrop, this study aims to comprehensively clarify the characteristics and associated factors of NPSE. The significance of this study lies in its potential to contribute insights for examining support strategies to systematically enhance NPSE as well as to provide suggestions for effective organizational strategies for nurses. Furthermore, knowledge that contributes to the enhancement of NPSE will contribute to enhanced nursing professionalism, thereby improving the overall quality of healthcare, and by improving the quality of nursing practice, will lead to improved patient care.

Methods

Research design

This study adopted a cross-sectional approach.

Hypothesized conceptual framework

In this study, factors considered to be associated with NPSE were organized theoretically and comprehensively based on previous research. The main related domains were autonomy, motivation, interpersonal competence, learning behavior, and stress‑coping factors. These factors are important elements for nurses and may be interrelated; however, whereas NPSE represents a cognitive concept indicating the belief that one can “engage in nursing practice,” each factor has its own distinct characteristics.

Autonomy reflects the sense of discretion that nurses have to make judgments based on their own judgment. Motivation represents the psychological drive that provides the energy to act through meaning-making. Interpersonal competence refers to the actual communication skills with patients, and learning behavior indicates self‑educational efforts. Stress coping is an independent construct that reflects the extent to which stressful situations are perceived as burdensome. Basic attributes were organized as background factors, and based on these elements, a hypothetical conceptual framework was constructed. This framework is presented in Fig. 1.

Rationale for selected variables

The main concepts and basic attributes were selected based on previous findings on general self-efficacy, Bandura’s social cognitive theory [8], and the concept of self-efficacy [9–11].

Autonomy is a professional competency in which nurses perceive that they can make clinical decisions based on their own judgment. Given its reported association with self-efficacy [19], we assumed a positive association. Motivation has been reported to be related to self-efficacy [20], and we considered it a factor that may enhance NPSE; therefore, we assumed a positive association. Interpersonal competence, particularly communication skills with patients, is important in nursing practice [21], and it has been shown to contribute to stress coping and improvement in self-efficacy [22]; therefore, we assumed a positive association. Learning behavior was assumed to have a positive association based on the theoretical rationale that mastery experiences are a primary source of self-efficacy [9, 10] and reports of improvement in general self-efficacy following educational interventions [23]. In contrast, because many studies have shown that stress reduces self-efficacy [24–29], we assumed a negative association for stress appraisal. Basic attributes (gender, age, years of clinical experience, employment status, and educational level) were included as background factors, based on prior research demonstrating their association with general self-efficacy [9, 10, 23, 30–32].

Sample size

The sample size for this study was calculated based on an a priori power analysis for multiple regression, with reference to Kang [33]. The power analysis was performed using G*Power ver. 3.1 [34, 35]. The predictors entered into the multiple regression analysis consisted of a total of ten variables: five main independent variables (nurses’ professional autonomy, work motivation, communication skills with patients, self- education, and perceived stress), and five basic attributes (gender, age, years of clinical experience, employment status, and educational level). Setting the effect size to medium (Cohen’s f2 = 0.15), the significance level to 5%, and the statistical power to 80%, the required sample size for multiple regression analysis was estimated to be 118 participants. Referring to previous studies [36], the number of distributed questionnaires was calculated assuming a mail survey response rate of 24%–30%. Taking into account the theoretically required minimum sample size, as well as actual response rates, missing data, and potential response bias, the target sample size was set at 300 participants in order to ensure sufficient statistical power and stability of estimates.

Participant selection

The participants were nurses employed at seven general hospitals located in the Chubu region of Japan. Nurses from hospitals that provided research consent were included, with the selection criterion being nurses engaged in clinical nursing duties. The exclusion criterion was nurses not engaged in clinical duties, including nursing directors and assistant nursing directors, who served in managerial roles overseeing all nurses.

Data collection

The survey was conducted using an anonymous, self-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire covered basic attributes (gender, age, years of clinical experience, employment status, and educational level), the NPSS, nurses’ professional autonomy, work motivation, communication skills with patients, self‑education, and perceived stress. First, the purpose, objectives, methods, and ethical considerations of the study were explained to the directors of nursing at the hospitals in both written and oral form, and written consent was obtained. Thereafter, the head nurses of each department were asked to distribute the questionnaires to the participants. The request letter to potential participants clearly stated that they could freely choose whether or not to participate in the survey based on their own free will. Returning the completed questionnaire by mail was regarded as providing consent to participate in the study. The data collection period was from March to July 2025.

Measures

Dependent variable: nursing profession self-efficacy

NPSE was measured using the Japanese version of the NPSS (NPSS-J) [7]. This scale is a translated version of the 19‑item NPSS developed by Caruso et al. [2]. The reliability coefficient of the scale is Cronbach’s α = 0.925. Responses were rated on a 5‑point Likert scale (1 = cannot do at all, 2 = can do poorly, 3 = can do moderately, 4 = can do well, 5 = can do very well), with higher scores indicating higher levels of NPSE.

Independent variables

Professional autonomy

Professional autonomy was measured using the Professional Autonomy in Nursing Scale developed by Kikuchi and Harada [37]. Responses were rated on a 5‑point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), and the scale includes reverse‑scored items. Higher scores indicate higher levels of professional autonomy in the domains of situational awareness, judgment, and practice. The reliability coefficients (Cronbach’s α) for the five factors were 0.93, 0.93, 0.88, 0.87, and 0.79, respectively.

Work motivation

Work motivation was measured using the Work Motivation Scale for Nurses developed by Nishimura et al. [38]. Responses were rated on a 4‑point scale assessing the degree of interest (0 = not interested at all, 3 = very interested), with higher scores indicating higher levels of work motivation. The reliability coefficient was McDonald’s ω = 0.829.

Communication skills with patients

Communication skills with patients were measured using the Patients–Nurse Communication Skill Scale developed by Ueno [39]. Responses were rated on a 5‑point scale (1 = not applicable, 5 = applicable), and the scale includes reverse-scored items. Higher scores indicating higher levels of nurses’ communication skills with patients. The reliability coefficient of the scale was Cronbach’s α = 0.874.

Self‑education

Self‑education was measured using the Self‑Education Scale for Nurses developed by Noyori and Shimizu [40]. Responses were rated on a 6‑point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 6 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating higher levels of self‑education as a nurse. The reliability coefficient of the scale was Cronbach’s α = 0.945.

Perceived stress

Perceived stress was measured using the Japanese version of the Perceived Stress Scale developed by Sumi [41]. Responses were rated on a 5‑point scale assessing the frequency of experiences during the past month (0 = never, 4 = very often), and the scaleincludes reverse-scored items. Higher scores indicate higher levels of perceived stress. The reliability coefficient was Cronbach’s α = 0.71.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 31, with the significance level set at p < .05. Descriptive statistics were calculated for basic attributes and each scale; categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, depending on their distribution. Missing values were handled using the listwise deletion method, and all analyses were performed using only valid data. The normality of each scale was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test; only perceived stress showed a normal distribution, whereas the other scales were non‑normally distributed. Therefore, correlations among the scales were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients. Characteristics of nursing profession self‑efficacy were identified based on the distribution of the total score of the NPSS and the differences among items, while item‑level comparisons were conducted using the Friedman test.

After confirming multicollinearity using variance inflation factors (VIF), hierarchical multiple regression analysis was performed to examine factors associated with NPSE. The dependent variable was the total NPSS score, and the independent variables were basic attributes (gender, age, years of clinical experience, employment status, and educational level) and the total scores of the five related scales (professional autonomy, work motivation, communication skills, self‑education, and perceived stress). Basic attributes were included in the model as background factors in order to adjust for confounding. Considering the theoretical framework of this study, hierarchical multiple regression analysis was performed by entering the background factors in Step 1 and the related domains in Step 2. Because dichotomization of continuous variables has been shown to cause loss of information and reduce estimation accuracy [42], scale scores were treated as continuous variables.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted after undergoing ethical review and approval by the Ethics Review Committee of Gifu Shotoku Gakuen University (approval number: 2024-20).

Results

Of the 555 questionnaires distributed, 332 were returned (response rate, 59.8%). Valid responses were defined as those without missing values on the NPSS-J. There were 325 valid responses (valid response rate, 97.8%). The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the NPSS-J in this study was 0.923, and those for each scale were as follows: 0.959 for professional autonomy, 0.951 for work motivation, 0.749 for communication skills with patients, 0.929 for self‑education, and 0.784 for perceived stress. All scales satisfied the commonly accepted criterion (α ≥ 0.70), confirming acceptable internal consistency.

In the final model, work motivation (β = 0.384, p <.001), self‑education (β = 0.246, p <.001), professional autonomy (β = 0.236, p <.001), and communication skills with patients (β = 0.157, p <.001) were all significant positive predictors of professional self‑efficacy. These standardized coefficients indicate that a one‑standard‑deviation increase in each factor corresponds to increases of 0.38, 0.25, 0.24, and 0.16 standard deviations, respectively, in professional self‑efficacy. Notably, none of the background factors remained significant in Step 2, indicating that professional self‑efficacy was more strongly explained by the main domains.

Discussion

This study aimed to comprehensively clarify the characteristics of NPSE and its related factors. The characteristics of NPSE, based on scale scores, as well as the related factors are described below.

Characteristics based on the evaluation scores of nursing profession self‑efficacy

When examining the item‑level evaluation of the nursing profession self‑efficacy scale, the item with the highest mean score rank was “Item 8: As a professional, I respect confidentiality and thoroughly uphold it.”, indicating that self‑efficacy related to ethical practice was the highest. Confidentiality is explicitly stated as a fundamental responsibility in the Code of Ethics for Nurses [43] and is an ethical principle repeatedly emphasized throughout educational curricula and daily practice. Therefore, it is reasonable that nurses exhibit high self‑efficacy in this domain. This suggests that the establishment of ethical awareness contributes to professional self‑efficacy.

In contrast, the item with the lowest rank was “Item 11: I make use of research findings (knowledge) in professional practice.” In clinical settings, factors such as heavy workloads, the difficulty of conducting research, and limited educational opportunities to connect research with practice have been identified as barriers to research utilization [44], and these factors may contribute to lower self‑efficacy in this area.

Based on the demographic overview, the characteristics of the participants in this study were generally consistent with the trends observed in the population of nurses in Japan [45], indicating that the distribution was similar to that of the national nursing workforce overall. Therefore, educational support to develop the ability to apply research findings in clinical practice, as well as a workplace culture that facilitates the sharing and utilization of evidence, emerged as issues in Japan. Examination in other country contexts is also warranted.

Factors related to nursing profession self‑efficacy

In this study, a theoretical and hypothetical conceptual framework was established to identify factors that might be associated with NPSE. Therefore, the finding that many variables showed associations with NPSE in the correlation analysis can be considered to support the validity of the conceptual framework. However, in the hierarchical regression analysis, after controlling for interrelationships among variables, work motivation, self-education, professional autonomy, and communication skills with patients showed independent positive associations. Contrary to the hypothesis, perceived stress showed a negative correlation in the bivariate analysis but did not show an independent association in the multivariable model, and background factors were not statistically significant in the final model. Among the background factors, only employment status was significant in Step 1, but all factors became non-significant after the major domains were entered in Step 2. Given that this study employed a cross-sectional design and thus cannot determine causal relationships, the following section examines these associations under this premise.

The factor most strongly associated with NPSE was work motivation (β = 0.384). A high level of motivation, including finding meaning in daily practice and work tasks, was suggested to be linked to the belief that one is able to perform one’s professional duties. The association between motivation and self-efficacy has also been reported in previous studies [20, 46], and the present study supports this finding in the domain of professional self-efficacy. In addition, motivation was associated with self-education (r =.381) and autonomy (r =.307), suggesting that it may occupy a meaningful position among the psychological factors associated with NPSE.

Next, self-education (β = 0.246) and autonomy (β = 0.236) both showed moderate associations with NPSE. Self-education showed the strongest association in the bivariate analysis (r =.573), indicating that nurses who engage more actively in learning tend to report higher levels of professional self‑efficacy. However, the result that self-education was lower than motivation in the multivariable analysis suggests that self-education may be associated not only with NPSE but also with other psychological factors such as autonomy (r =.551) and motivation (r =.381). These findings suggest that learning orientation and autonomy may be interrelated and simultaneously associated with NPSE. This is consistent with previous research showing that learning orientation and autonomy are associated with self-efficacy [23, 47, 48].

Communication skills with patients showed a smaller association with NPSE than did the other factors (β = 0.157), but it demonstrated a moderate association in the bivariate analysis (r =.426). This suggests that the interpersonal skills involved in understanding patients and building trust relationships have a certain association with the belief that one can perform one’s professional duties [22]. In contrast, the relatively small standardized coefficient indicates the possibility that communication ability may be a peripheral factor within the broader set of psychological characteristics associated with NPSE, rather than as a central component of NPSE. In fact, communication ability was also strongly associated with autonomy (r =.504) and self-education (r =.523), and it is considered that its independent contribution became smaller as the overlapping portions with these factors were adjusted.

Meanwhile, perceived stress and background factors did not show significant associations in the final model. These results suggest that NPSE is a characteristic that is more closely associated with modifiable psychological factors such as motivation, self-education, and autonomy rather than fixed attributes.

Taken together, the order of associations observed in this study suggests that NPSE is related to multiple psychological factors. The multiple regression model had a relatively high adjusted R2 of 0.571, sufficiently explaining the variance in NPSE. In addition, the Durbin–Watson ratio was close to 2 (d = 1.988), indicating no problem with the independence of residuals, and the VIF values ranged from 1.04 to 4.54, indicating that multicollinearity was not problematic. These results indicate the statistical validity of the model used in this study.

However, the participants in this study were limited to one region of Japan, and the sample composition may not adequately reflect the broader nursing population or international contexts. Factors such as professional autonomy, work motivation, communication style, and self-directed learning may be influenced by cultural and organizational characteristics specific to the nursing practice and educational system in each country. Therefore, careful interpretation is required when applying these findings to different healthcare environments or cultural backgrounds.

Implications and challenges for nursing practice and nursing management

The results of this study suggest that multifaceted support addressing both individual and organizational factors is required to enhance NPSE. First, given that work motivation showed the strongest association, it is important to develop a workplace environment in which nurses can find value and a sense of accomplishment in their work. Specifically, initiatives that support professional motivation, including clarifying roles, providing positive feedback, and presenting career paths, may be considered. In addition, because autonomy was associated with NPSE, it is critical to foster an organizational culture that enables nurses to make autonomous judgments. Expanding opportunities for participation in decision-making, establishing systems that support ethical judgment, and granting appropriate discretion may be related to fostering greater confidence in professional practice. Furthermore, the association between self-education and NPSE indicates the importance of continuous learning support. In addition to providing training opportunities, it is necessary to develop an organizational culture that promotes learning and to establish systems that support self-directed learning. Moreover, because communication ability with patients was associated with NPSE, it may be useful for practical training to include educational programs aimed at improving interpersonal skills.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, because it employed a cross‑sectional design, the observed associations do not indicate causal relationships. Accordingly, verification through longitudinal studies or intervention studies is necessary. Second, the data in this study were collected using a self‑administered questionnaire, which may have been influenced by social desirability bias or the respondents’ subjective judgments. Third, the participants were limited to nurses working in medical institutions in one region, and facility characteristics or regional factors may have influenced the results. Therefore, caution is required when generalizing the findings of this study to nurses as a whole. Fourth, although the predictors were conceptually distinguished from NPSE, some degree of conceptual overlap may still exist between NPSE and related constructs such as professional autonomy, self‑education, communication competence, and work motivation. Because the discriminant validity among these constructs was not formally examined, this potential conceptual overlap should be acknowledged as a limitation. Finally, factors that are difficult to quantify—including the quality of clinical experience or the nature of successful experiences—may also contribute to the development of professional self‑efficacy. Because this study did not address these qualitative aspects, future research should incorporate qualitative approaches or mixed‑methods designs for more comprehensive examination.

Conclusions

This study was conducted with the aim of comprehensively clarifying the characteristics of NPSE and its related factors. Based on the item-level scores, it was found that nurses in Japan demonstrated high self-efficacy in ethical practice but tended to show lower professional self-efficacy in utilizing research findings. The findings suggest that to further advance professional practice in nursing, it is important not only to maintain ethical practice but also to provide support that enhances the ability to integrate research findings into clinical judgment and nursing interventions.

Factors related to NPSE were identified as work motivation, self-educational behavior, professional autonomy, and communication skills with patients. These findings suggest that a work environment that supports professional meaning in daily practice and promotes continuous learning may help to enhance nurses’ NPSE. An organizational culture that encourages autonomous decision-making and educational programs that incorporate practical communication training with patients are also considered effective in supporting NPSE.
