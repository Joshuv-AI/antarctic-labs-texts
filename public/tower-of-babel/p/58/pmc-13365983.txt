Smc5/6 uses its head-NSE module to preferentially associate with ssDNA gaps and ss-dsDNA junctions
Authors: Elizabeth M Irvin, Sophia T Park, Victoria Miller-Browne, Rebecca Lever, Shibai Li, Grace Waterman, Parminder Kaur, Xiaolan Zhao, Hong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStructural Maintenance of Chromosomes (SMC) complexes play critical roles in genome maintenance. While canonical SMCs, such as cohesin and condensin, organize the genome into 3D structures, Smc5/6 directly promotes genome replication and repair. Insights into Smc5/6 function have emerged from recent fluorescence-based single-molecule studies showing its binding to structures resembling replication and repair intermediates. To provide a higher-resolution view of this facet of Smc5/6′s function, we employed atomic force microscopy (AFM) to visualize the budding yeast Smc5/6 at nanometer-scale resolution and investigate how it interacts with short ssDNA gaps that frequently form during genome maintenance. We report that the free or DNA-bound Smc5/6 adopts a ‘bead-on-stick’ shape, with its head-NSE module separated from its hinge domain by a non-bending arm region. Smc5/6 predominantly uses its head-NSE module to engage with dsDNA, and mutating DNA-binding residues within this structural module shifts the complex toward a hinge-mediated DNA-binding mode. Importantly, Smc5/6 shows an intrinsic association preference for ssDNA gap regions and ss-dsDNA junction sites over the flanking dsDNA regions, predominantly through the head-NSE module. AFM visualization of Smc5/6 and its structural elements in preferential gap and junction DNA association provides a framework for our continued understanding of Smc5/6′s roles in genome maintenance.

Introduction

The Structural Maintenance of Chromosomes (SMC) family of protein complexes has diverse roles in chromosome organization and genome maintenance [1, 2]. In eukaryotes, the cohesin and condensin SMCs drive chromosome compaction and 3D genome organization through their DNA loop extrusion and tethering activities [1, 2]. Distinct from cohesin and condensin, the relevance of DNA loop extrusion by Smc5/6 in genome maintenance remains unclear, as genome organization is largely unaffected by the loss of Smc5/6 [3]. Rather, Smc5/6 is thought to represent a non-canonical SMC member with a critical role in ensuring timely processing of DNA replication and repair intermediates [4–7]. While recent studies have begun to reveal Smc5/6 interactions with intermediate DNA structures formed during genome replication and repair, higher-resolution characterization of this activity is required to advance our understanding of how Smc5/6 performs its genome maintenance functions.

Mechanistic examination of Smc5/6 has mostly been conducted on the budding yeast complex due to its amenability to purification and genetic analysis. The budding yeast Smc5/6 contains eight subunits, which are conserved among eukaryotes [4–7]. These include the large Smc5 and Smc6 subunits and six smaller non-SMC elements (NSEs), namely Nse1-6 (Fig. 1A). Recent cryo-electron microscopy (EM) data have revealed that the budding yeast Smc5/6 adopts an elongated shape due to the Smc5 and Smc6 subunits [8, 9]. Both Smc5 and Smc6’s globular head domains, which possess DNA binding and ATPase activities, are connected to their hinge domains via long coiled-coil arm regions (Fig. 1A) [8, 9]. These head domains are hubs for NSE interactions, contacting the Nse1-3-4 subcomplex (composed of Nse1, Nse3, and Nse4) and the Nse5-6 subcomplex (composed of Nse5 and Nse6) [8–15]. The Nse2 subunit, which has conserved SUMO E3 ligase activity, is bound to the mid-arm region of Smc5 and Smc6 [8, 9, 16–20]. As such, three principal structural modules of Smc5/6 include: the SMC head regions attached to the two NSE subcomplexes, the filamentous SMC arm regions, the associated Nse2, and the SMC hinge regions located most distal to the head regions (Fig. 1A).

A fundamental aspect of Smc5/6 function is its association with DNA. Biochemical data have shown that the Smc5-Smc6 heterodimer and the Nse1-3-4 subcomplex can bind to dsDNA and ssDNA fragments, while Nse5-6 and Nse2 lack this activity [13, 21–25]. Fluorescence-based DNA curtain and optical trap studies further examined kilobase-long and more complex DNA substrates. The yeast Smc5/6 has been shown to slide along dsDNA, which is a shared behavior of SMCs [26–29]. Strikingly, Smc5/6 can stably bind to DNA regions where ssDNA sequences juxtapose dsDNA sequences (referred to as ss-dsDNA junctions) [26–28]. In cells, ss-dsDNA junctions often flank ssDNA gap regions, which are intermediate structures formed upon disruption of DNA replication or after DNA lesion excision. These intermediates require proper management to prevent DNA breakage and enable sequence restoration. In cells, Smc5/6 deficiencies lead to increased sensitivity to ssDNA modifications [30], thus connecting it to ssDNA gap metabolism. A better determination of how Smc5/6 associates with ssDNA gap regions and ss-dsDNA junctions will advance our understanding of its cellular functions.

While fluorescence-based single-molecule studies have provided important insights into the dynamic association of Smc5/6 with dsDNA and its stable binding to ss-dsDNA junction regions, a few limitations remain. First, ssDNA gap sizes in these studies were mostly in the kilo-nucleotide range. While large-gapped DNA structures exist in cells, small gaps frequently form as intermediates during DNA replication and repair [31, 32]. It is thus also important to characterize the association of Smc5/6 with small ssDNA gap substrates. Second, DNA molecules examined in optical-trap studies were under certain levels of mechanical tension, which may not reflect their physical state in cells. Third, the resolution of fluorescence-based visualization is insufficient to discern which structural modules of Smc5/6 contact the ssDNA gap or junction, precluding structural insights into how Smc5/6 targets these DNA substrates.

To address the knowledge gaps in our understanding of Smc5/6 interactions with DNA structures generated during genome maintenance, we used AFM imaging, which provides nanometer-scale resolution at the single-molecule level. While AFM studies have recently revealed how other SMCs associate with DNA [33–36], no systematic examination of Smc5/6 or its interactions with DNA using this method has yet been reported. Here, we use AFM to visualize Smc5/6 configurations and investigate how its structural modules contribute to its interaction with 15-nt or 37-nt ssDNA gaps flanked by dsDNA regions or a ss-dsDNA junction site. Our AFM data reveal that the yeast Smc5/6 adopts a ‘bead-on-stick’ shape without arm bending and primarily uses its head-NSE module to contact the ssDNA gap. Importantly, Smc5/6 preferentially binds to the short ssDNA gap region and the ss-dsDNA junction site over dsDNA regions. Our data examining several Smc5/6 variants suggest that DNA-binding sites in its head-NSE module enable optimal gap binding, and that mutating these sites shifts Smc5/6 toward a hinge DNA-binding mode. Collectively, our data establish that Smc5/6 has an intrinsic ability to preferentially bind ssDNA gaps via multi-subunit collaboration and suggest that this gap binding preference is due to its preferential association with ss-dsDNA junctions.

Materials and methods

Purification of Smc5/6 complexes

Purification of different variants of Smc5/6 complexes used in this work follows the methods described previously [13, 15, 37]. All complexes, except the Smc5/66mer, were purified from yeast strains containing the overexpression construct for each of the subunits of the S. cerevisiae Smc5/6 complex, such that their expression can be induced by the addition of galactose. Yeast cells were first grown at 30°C in YP media containing 2% glycerol and 2% lactic acid until the late log phase. Then, galactose was added to a final concentration of 2% to induce protein expression, and cells were grown for another 4 h before collection. Cell pellets were frozen dropwise in liquid nitrogen in buffer E (45 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.6, 10% glycerol, and 0.02% NP40) supplemented with 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 1x protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma), and 1x cOmpleteTM ULTRA EDTA-free protease inhibitor (Roche). A freezer mill (SPEX CertiPrep 6850 Freezer/Mill) was used to break cells via seven cycles of 2 min at a rate of 15 impacts per second. Cell powder was then resuspended in 1 volume of buffer E supplemented with 300 mM NaCl and 1 mM DTT, and the extracts were cleared by centrifugation at 177 469 × g for 30 min at 4°C. The cleared protein extract was mixed with 2 mM CaCl2 and calmodulin affinity resin for 2 h at 4°C to pull down CBP-tagged Smc5 and other Smc5/6 subunits. After washing the resins with 10 column volumes of buffer E supplemented with 300 mM NaCl, 2 mM CaCl2, and 1 mM DTT, the bound proteins were eluted with the same buffer, supplemented with 1 mM EDTA and 2 mM EGTA, and without CaCl2. Peak fractions were applied to a Superose 6 Increase column in a buffer containing 45 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.6, 10% glycerol, 200 mM NaCl, 0.02% NP40, and 1 mM DTT. Proteins in the peak fractions were concentrated, then snap-frozen for storage.

The wild-type and mutant Smc5/66mer complexes were purified from bacterial cells as described previously [37]. The pET expression plasmid contained the S. cerevisiae genes encoding Smc5, Smc6 fused with 3C-Twin-Strep, and Nse1-4. The 4DNAm Smc5/66mer expression construct contains the DNA-binding sites shown in Fig. 6A, mutated to alanine residues except those located on Nse4, which were mutated to negatively charged residues, as described previously [37]. E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells containing either the wild-type or 4DNAm expression plasmid were grown in TB medium at 37°C before the addition of 0.4 mM IPTG to induce Smc5/66mer protein expression at 22°C for 16 h. All the following steps were carried out at 4°C. Cell pellets were first resuspended in of lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, and 25 mM imidazole) containing 5 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, and 150 U Benzonase (EMD Millipore). Cells were broken via sonication at 70% Amplitude for 6 min with pulsing of 1 s on and 2 s off cycle. After centrifugation at 40 000 × rpm for 40 min, the cleared cell lysates were filtered through a 0.45 µm filter and then loaded onto a Strep-tactin HP column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated with lysis buffer or Strep-tactin XT column (Cytiva) equilibrated with binding buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8). Same buffer was used to wash the corresponding column, and then to elute proteins upon supplementing with 5 mM desthiobiotin (Strep-tactin HP ) or 50 mM biotin (Strep-tactin XT). Eluates were then loaded onto a 5 mL HiTrap Heparin column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated with 10 mL of lysis buffer supplemented with 2 mM DTT. After washing the column with 25 mL of the same buffer, bound proteins were eluted with 20 mL elution buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 1 M NaCl, and 2 mM DTT). Peak protein fractions were concentrated using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter units (30 kDa cutoff) and then loaded onto a 24 mL Superose6 10/300 GL size-exclusion chromatography column (Cytiva). Gel filtration and storage buffers contain 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 250 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP. Peak fractions containing Smc5/66mer were concentrated, then snap-frozen with 15% glycerol in aliquots.

ATPase activity assay for Smc5/6

The analyses were conducted as previously described [27]. Briefly, Smc5/6 complexes at 250 nM were incubated with 1 mM ATP and 80 nCi [α-32P]-ATP in 10 μl buffer containing 25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 2 mM MgCl2, 50 mM NaCl, 2% glycerol, and 1 mM DTT. Reactions were conducted at 30°C, and samples at 10-, 30-, 60-, and 90-min time points were analyzed by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) followed by phosphor imaging. The activity was calculated by comparing ADP and ATP levels at each of the four time points to determine the amount of ATP hydrolyzed per minute per Smc5/6 complex.

Generation of gap DNAs, dsDNA, and Poly-T overhang substrates

Linear DNA containing a 37 or 15-nt ssDNA gap at a defined position (15-nt or 37-nt gap DNA) was produced from the circular pUC19-derived pSCW01 plasmid (head-to-tail duplication of 2030 bp, a gift from Peggy Hsieh at NIDDK) [38]. pSCW01 contains two regions of four closely spaced recognition sites for the Nt.BstNBI nickase enzyme. To generate the 37-nt long gap site, nicking reactions (40 µL total, 5 µg pSCW01 DNA, and 4 µL Nt.BstNBI at 10 U/µL) were carried out for 4 h total at 55°C in 1x NEBuffer r3.1 Buffer (100 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.9, 10 mM MgCl2, and 100 µg/ml Recombinant Albumin). To generate the 15-nt long gap site, two of the Nt.BstNBI nickase recognition sites were mutated on the pSCW01 plasmid prior to the nicking reaction. For both 15-nt and 37-nt gap DNA substrates, nicking was confirmed using gel electrophoresis (0.8% agarose, 1X TAE, 100 V, 60 min), in which the band for nicked pSCW01 is shifted upwards compared to unnicked pSCW01 due to the relaxation of plasmid supercoiling. Gapping reactions (100 µL total) were then initiated by incubating nicked pSCW01 (5 µg) with a 30-fold excess of oligos complementary to the nicked strand (1.3 µM, IDT DNA) for 30 min at 65°C in a water bath. Gapping reactions were cooled to room temperature, then loaded onto Amicon Ultra 0.5 ml 50 K centrifugal filters and centrifuged at 14 000 x g for 10 min at room temperature to remove annealed oligo-nicked fragments and excess oligos. pSCW01 after gapping was collected from the filter by inverting the filter and centrifuging for 2 min at 1000 x g at room temperature. Gapping reactions were repeated two additional times to ensure high gapping efficiency.

Gapping validation reactions (30 µL total, 300 ng gapped pSCW01, 0.75 µL BamH1 at 20 U/µL, 0.75 µL Pst1 at 20 U/µL, and 0.75 µL NcoI-HF at 20 U/µL) were incubated for 1 h at 37°C in 1X NEBuffer r3.1 Buffer, then loaded onto an agarose gel (0.8% agarose, 1X TAE, 100 V, 45 min). If gapping was successful, gapped sites resist cleavage by BamH1, Pst1, and NcoI-HF, preventing linearization and producing no band shift relative to untreated gapped pSCW01. Gapped pSCW01 was then linearized by incubating the DNA with ScaI-HF (30 µL total, 5 µg gapped pSCW01, and 2 µL ScaI-HF at 20 U/µL) in 1x NEB rCutSmart Buffer (50 mM CH3CO2K, 20 mM Tris–CH3COO, 10 mM Mg(CH3COO)2, and 100 µg/ml Recombinant Albumin) for 4 h at 37°C. ScaI-HF linearization positioned the gap site at 470 nt (23%) from the nearest end of the DNA molecule [39]. Control dsDNA matched for sequence and length was generated by directly linearizing the pSCW01 plasmid with ScaI-HF. Both linear gap DNA and dsDNA were then purified using a DNA Clean and Concentrator PCR Purification Kit (Zymo Research) before being imaged with AFM. The contour lengths of the gap DNA and dsDNA were validated using AFM imaging in air. The gap site position was previously validated by incubation of the gap DNA with the mitochondrial single-stranded DNA-binding protein and AFM imaging [40].

To generate the poly-T overhang substrate, we annealed two ssDNA molecules (550 and 720 nt, Genewiz). The ssDNAs were dissolved in 10 mM Tris–HCl, then combined in equimolar ratios in 1x Annealing Buffer (50 µL total, 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, and 50 mM NaCl). The sample was heated for 5 min at 95°C, then slowly cooled to room temperature over approximately 2 h. The resulting DNA molecule consists of a 550-bp dsDNA region with a 5′ 170 nt Poly-T ssDNA overhang, thus generating a 3′ ss-ds DNA junction site.

AFM imaging acquisition

All Smc5/6 protein variants (WT Smc5/68mer, EQ-Smc5/68mer, Smc5/66mer, Smc5/65mer, and 4DNAm Smc5/66mer, 50 nM) were incubated with purified linear gap DNAs, or dsDNA, or poly-T overhang DNA (5 nM) in SMC Reaction Buffer (40 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 50 mM NaCl, and 2 mM MgCl2) for 20 min at room temperature. For the WT Smc5/68mer (+ATP) and EQ-Smc5/68mer conditions, reactions were supplemented with 2.5 mM ATP. The reactions were diluted 10- to 16-fold in AFM Imaging Buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.6, 100 mM NaCl, and 10 mM Mg(CH3COO)2) and immediately deposited onto a freshly cleaved mica surface (SPI Supply). The samples were rinsed with DI water and dried under a stream of nitrogen gas. Samples were then imaged using an MFP-3D-Bio AFM (Asylum Research) and Pointprobe PPP-FMR probes (Nanosensors, spring constants ∼2.8 Nm−1). All images were collected at a scan size of 3 µm x 3 µm, a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels, and a scan rate of 1–2 Hz.

AFM image analysis

Smc5/6 binding positions along linear 37- and 15-nt gap DNAs and dsDNA were measured with Asylum Research software using a tracing tool to measure DNA contour length. Protein binding positions were fitted with a Gaussian distribution. The Asylum Research tracing tool was used to measure Smc5/6 dimensions and to distinguish head-NSE from hinge-binding events based on their AFM heights. To measure Smc5/6 length dimensions, a single trace was made from the furthest end of the head-NSE domain to the furthest point on the SMC arms, and the transition from the head-NSE domain to the SMC arms was marked based on the decrease in AFM height. DNA-binding events were assigned as head-NSE-mediated or hinge-mediated by using the tracing tool to determine the height of the part of Smc5/6 bound to DNA. The calibration of molecular weights versus AFM volumes was reported previously [41]. Briefly, we used Asylum Research software to measure AFM volumes of TRF1 monomer (51 kDa), UvrB monomer (75 kDa), TRF1 dimer (102 kDa), Histone octamer (130 kDa), Taq MutS dimer (181 kDa), Taq MutS tetramer (362 kDa), and Taq MutS hexamer (543 kDa). The plot of molecular weight vs. AFM volume was fit with a linear equation: V (nm3) = 1.45 MW – 21.59. Meanwhile, for the poly-T DNA overhang substrate, the determination of Smc5/6 binding to dsDNA, ssDNA, and junction sites was based on the AFM heights of DNA on either side of bound Smc5/6, since the AFM height of ssDNA is approximately half that of dsDNA. AFM Data were compiled from a minimum of three technical repeats per condition and included two separately purified batches of the Smc5/68mer.

AFM simulation

AFM simulation images were generated using BioAFMviewer software [42]. Cryo-EM structures of Smc5/68mer (PDB: 7YQH), Smc5/66mer (PDB: 8I13), and Smc5/65mer (PDB: 8T8E) were obtained from RCSB Protein Data Bank. The BioAFMviewer line tool was used to quantify protein lengths (total length, head-NSE length, and SMC arm length) and arm width. To account for variation in protein orientation on the mica surface, the cryo-EM structure was rotated at several angles during measurements. BioAFMviewer settings included AFM probe characteristics matching those used in AFM experiments (a 4 nm tip radius and a 10° cone angle), and a 0.5 nm AFM scan step.

Statistical analysis

Proportions of Smc5/6 binding events within the 20–26% position of gap DNA versus dsDNA were compared using an unpaired two-sided Student’s t-test with P-values indicated on the graphs. This method was also used to compare the binding positions of Smc5/6 variants on gap DNA with those of Smc5/68mer and to assess the frequency of head-NSE- versus hinge-mediated DNA-binding events. All statistical analyses were performed using Origin.

Results

AFM imaging reveals Smc5/6 adopts a ‘bead-on-stick’ shape without arm bending

To study DNA binding by Smc5/6 at the single-molecule level, we purified the budding yeast Smc5/6 containing all eight subunits (Smc5/68mer) close to homogeneity following an established procedure (Fig. 1B) [13]. The purified Smc5/68mer was biochemically active, as it exhibited ATPase activity at a level comparable to previous measurements (Supplementary Fig. S1A) [27]. We first examined free Smc5/68mer using AFM imaging after depositing the protein samples onto freshly peeled mica surfaces. A collection of protein particles ranging in size was observed, suggesting a combination of full Smc5/68mer complexes and subcomplexes (Supplementary Fig. S1B). The presence of subcomplexes was expected, as protein samples were diluted to a low nanomolar range for AFM studies, which can favor subunit dissociation [43]. Leveraging the single-molecule resolution afforded by AFM imaging, we identified Smc5/68mer amongst the particles based on a linear correlation between protein AFM volume (nm3) and molecular weight (kDa) [44, 45]. As a single Smc5/68mer has a molecular weight of 520 kDa, its AFM volume is expected to be approximately 600 nm3. Among protein particles with at least 400 nm3 AFM volumes, the mean AFM volume was 580 nm3, with a standard deviation of 196 nm3 (Supplementary Fig. S1C), suggesting that these species represent monomeric Smc5/68mer.

Consistent with negative stain EM and cryo-EM data, Smc5/68mer particles in AFM images showed ‘bead-on-stick’ shapes (Fig. 1C) (8–14]). Based on the cryo-EM structures, we inferred that the ‘bead’ element corresponds to the head-NSE module, which includes the SMC head regions and the associated Nse1-3-4 and the Nse5-6 subcomplexes (Fig. 1A; 1C). The filamentous SMC arms were denoted as the arm module, and the arm tip furthest from the head-NSE was denoted as the hinge module (Fig. 1C). Measuring protein particle length through tracing and sectional analysis (Supplementary Fig. S2A; Materials and Methods) showed that Smc5/68mer was, on average, approximately 47.9 nm (total length, Supplementary Fig. S2B), in agreement with cryo-EM data. In addition, we measured the length of the head-NSE module and the length and width of the SMC arms, which averaged 17.8 nm, 29.0 nm, and 14.6 nm, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S2B). It is worth noting that the Smc5/6 arm length distribution fits with a single Gaussian distribution. If Smc5/6 arms display both bent and unbent conformations, we expect to observe two distinct arm lengths (hinge-to-head distances) in AFM images, corresponding to unbent and bent/collapsed arms, as shown previously for condensin [46]. Furthermore, all length measurements were consistent with predictions from AFM image simulations of Smc5/6, based on cryo-EM data showing no arm bending of the complex (Fig. 1D; S2B) [47, 48]. These results collectively indicate the absence of an arm-bending configuration in Smc5/6. This feature differs from the arm-bent cohesin and condensin structures, wherein the filamentous arms fold back at the mid-arm regions towards the head domains [49–51]. Importantly, the ‘bead-on-stick’ shape enabled us to distinguish the SMC head, arm, and hinge regions, and to determine the Smc5/6 structural regions that are associated with DNA.

Generating a DNA substrate containing a 37-nt ssDNA gap flanked by dsDNA regions

To examine how Smc5/6 associates with ssDNA gaps that mimic intermediates produced during DNA replication and repair, we constructed a dsDNA substrate (2-kb) embedded with a single 37-nt ssDNA gap at a defined location [38]. The ssDNA region is sufficiently long for binding Smc5/6 based on previous biochemical studies and is flanked by two ss-dsDNA junctions without any ssDNA flaps [23, 24]. We note that longer ssDNA gaps are prone to breakage during the preparation procedure (below) [38], and thus were not examined here. The 37-nt gap DNA substrate was generated from a plasmid composed of two identical 2030-bp regions, each of which contains four closely spaced sites of the Nt.BstNBI nickase (Fig. 2A).

Smc5/68mer preferentially localizes to a 37-nt ssDNA gap region. (A). A workflow for generating linear gap DNA and control dsDNA substrates used in this study. See text for details of the five-step procedure. The final linear gap DNA substrate (2.0 kb) contained a 37-nt ssDNA gap located 23% down the length of the DNA from the nearest end. The control linear dsDNA matched the gap DNA in sequence and length. (B). Representative AFM images of Smc5/68mer binding to the gap DNA (left) and dsDNA (right) in the absence of ATP. The middle panel shows zoomed-in images of Smc5/68mer–DNA complexes. (C). Histograms of Smc5/68mer binding positions along the linear gap DNA and dsDNA substrates, measured from the nearest DNA end. Dotted lines highlight the gap region located at 20–26% from the nearest DNA end (left) and the corresponding region on dsDNA (right). The percentage of Smc5/68mer binding events occurring within the 20–26% regions for each DNA substrate type is shown above the histograms. The Gaussian fit (solid line) to gap DNA-binding positions is centered at 24.2% (±4.3%; R2 > 0.98). Data were compiled from a minimum of three independent experiments in the absence of ATP. An unpaired two-sided Student’s t-test was used to derive the P-value.

We streamlined the procedure in five steps (Fig. 2A). First, the nickase was used to generate two clusters of DNA nicks on the plasmid. Second, the nicked plasmid was mixed with a 30-fold excess of three ssDNA oligos (each 9-nt long) that contain sequences complementary to regions of the nicked strand. Third, base pairing between the oligos and the short ssDNA strands released from the nicked plasmid was enabled by three cycles of heating and cooling of the DNA mixture. This led to the removal of nicked regions and formation of the ssDNA gap. Fourth, short dsDNA fragments and remaining oligos were separated from the circular gap DNA by filtration. Fifth, the gapped circular DNA was linearized with the ScaI restriction enzyme, producing two identical 2-kb linear DNA fragments. Each DNA fragment contains a 37-nt ssDNA gap, located at 23% from the nearest DNA end, out of the total DNA length. We refer to this substrate as the 37-nt gap DNA hereafter. In addition, we generated a dsDNA control by linearizing the plasmid with ScaI digestion without nicking.

After purification, we used AFM imaging to confirm that the resultant 37-nt gap DNA (0.61 µm ± 0.05 µm) and the control dsDNA (0.62 µm ± 0.03 µm) showed the expected DNA contour lengths (Supplementary Fig. S3A-B). Furthermore, we validated gapping efficiency by assessing resistance to a cocktail of three restriction enzymes with recognition sequences within the ssDNA gap region. The gapped plasmid, but not the original and nicked plasmids [39], was resistant to linearization by the enzyme cocktail, since ssDNA is refractory to restriction enzyme digestion (Supplementary Fig. S3C). High efficiencies of ssDNA gapping were obtained for all gap substrates used in this work (90.5% ± 4.8%, N = 15 preparations).

Smc5/6 preferentially binds to ssDNA gap regions compared to dsDNA regions

To study how Smc5/6 interacts with ssDNA gap versus dsDNA regions, we incubated purified Smc5/68mer (50 nM) with either the linear 37-nt gap DNA or the control dsDNA (5 nM) without ATP (Supplementary Fig. S3D). After depositing the Smc5/68mer-DNA mixtures onto freshly cleaved mica, hundreds of AFM images were collected, and binding events by full Smc5/68mer complexes (AFM volumes ≥ 400 nm3) were further analyzed (Fig. 2B). DNA-bound Smc5/68mer was easily distinguished from dsDNA, as the two exhibited distinct AFM heights (1.84 nm ± 1.62 nm vs. 0.33 nm ± 0.04 nm; p < 0.05). We further measured the binding position of Smc5/68mer on DNA as the percentage of its distance to the nearest DNA end over the total DNA length (2 kb), with binding to the DNA end at 0% and the middle of DNA at 50% (Fig. 2C). Accounting for slight variation in DNA length measurements (±3%) (Supplementary Fig. S3B), we defined binding events within the 20–26% range from the nearest gap DNA end as corresponding to Smc5/68mer interacting with the ssDNA gap region. Because the small gap size precluded distinguishing the ssDNA region from the ss-dsDNA junctions flanking it, the combined region is referred to as the “ssDNA gap region”. Our analyses showed that the Smc5/68mer binding position distribution centered at 24.2% (±4.3%), with the majority (64%) of Smc5/68mer binding events observed at the ssDNA gap region (Fig. 2C). In stark contrast, Smc5/68mer bound randomly along the control dsDNA substrate, with only 9% of the protein bound at the 20–26% region of the DNA (Fig. 2C). Thus, Smc5/68mer showed a 7-fold higher binding preference for the ssDNA gap region compared to dsDNA regions in our experimental setup.

To investigate the oligomeric states of Smc5/6, we measured the AFM volume of DNA-bound Smc5/6. On gap DNA and control dsDNA, 54% and 68% of DNA-bound Smc5/68mer appeared to be monomeric with AFM volumes at 649 nm3 (± 61 nm3) and 626 nm3 (± 89 nm3), respectively. The remaining Smc5/68mer-DNA complexes appeared in higher-order states, with AFM volumes of 2603 nm3 (± 842 nm3) and 2920 nm3 (± 1239 nm3) on gap and control DNA, respectively. These results suggest that a single monomeric Smc5/68mer is sufficient to engage DNA, while multiple Smc5/68mer complexes can localize in proximity on DNA. The potential biological relevance of this finding requires further investigation. In summary, our AFM imaging results indicate that Smc5/68mer binds randomly along dsDNA but preferentially localizes to a ssDNA gap region without flanking ssDNA flaps.

Smc5/68mer binds to ssDNA gap regions and dsDNA primarily via its head-NSE module

A cryo-EM structure of Smc5/6 revealed that its head-NSE module binds dsDNA [37], while gel-based assays suggest that the Smc5 and Smc6 hinge regions preferentially bind to ssDNA [23, 24, 52]. As such, it remains unclear whether the head-NSE and/or the hinge module of Smc5/68mer is responsible for the observed binding to ssDNA gap regions. With the nanometer resolution afforded by AFM, the globular head-NSE region containing the SMC heads and the Nse1-3-4 and Nse5-6 subcomplexes can be distinguished from the hinge region located at the opposite end of the filamentous SMC arms (Fig. 1C).

Among DNA-bound Smc5/68mer complexes, head-NSE versus hinge DNA binding events were distinguished based on their distinct AFM heights. As illustrated by the example height traces (Fig. 3A–B), the DNA-bound head-NSE module (1.32 nm ± 0.50 nm) appeared taller than the DNA-bound hinge module (0.44 nm ± 0.08 nm), since the former is significantly larger than the latter. In the absence of ATP, a large majority of Smc5/68mer complexes used their head-NSE to engage with the gap or control dsDNA (Fig. 3C). The percentages of Smc5/68mer–DNA complexes bound via the head-NSE were 9.0 and 3.0 times greater than those bound via the hinge, for gap and control dsDNA, respectively. Furthermore, the arm lengths of Smc5/68mer bound to DNA were similar to those of free Smc5/6, suggesting that DNA binding did not induce arm-bending (Supplementary Fig. S3E). ATP addition did not change the dominance of the head-NSE module over hinge in Smc5/68mer DNA binding (Fig. 3C). In the presence or absence of ATP, the head-NSE module of Smc5/68mer preferentially bound at the gap region (Fig. 3D). The low frequency of hinge-binding events precluded further analyses of its gap-binding preference and suggests that hinge DNA binding may represent a transient state during Smc5/6 interaction with DNA. We note that DNA-binding events involving the SMC arm region were not observed in our datasets. Taken together, these results suggest that the head-NSE module harbors the major DNA-binding sites responsible for dsDNA binding and gap region targeting by Smc5/68mer.

Smc5/6 associates with ssDNA gap regions independently of ATP or ATP hydrolysis

Our data thus far show that Smc5/68mer primarily uses its head-NSE region to bind gap DNA regions, regardless of ATP. This agrees with optical-trapping and DNA-curtain data wherein Smc5/6 was found to bind to ss-dsDNA junction areas independently of ATP [26, 27]. This notion was also supported by similar frequencies of Smc5/68mer binding events at the gap region in the presence of ATP (average 63%; Fig. 4A, B, left) and absence of ATP (average 64%; Fig. 2C). Moreover, AFM volumes of gap-bound Smc5/68mer in the presence of ATP showed a monomeric population at 690 (± 45) nm3 and a higher-order population at 2802 (± 376) nm3, similar to those observed without ATP. Thus, the ability of Smc5/6 to target the ssDNA gap region in vitro does not require ATP.

We next tested whether perturbing ATP hydrolysis by Smc5/6 affects its preference for DNA binding, since this change could alter Smc5/6 configuration [53]. A Smc5/68mer variant mutated at two key residues required for ATP hydrolysis (Smc5-E1015Q, Smc6-E1048Q, or EQ-Smc5/68mer, Fig. 4C) was examined [10, 37]. As expected, the EQ-Smc5/68mer showed greatly reduced ATPase activity compared to the wild-type Smc5/68mer (Supplementary Fig. S1A). In AFM images, EQ-Smc5/68mer maintained the ‘bead-on-stick’ shape, and its dimensions were similar to those of the wild-type complex (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Also like the wild-type Smc5/68mer, the free EQ-Smc5/68mer was present in the monomeric form with AFM volumes of 573 (±173) nm3 (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Further, EQ-Smc5/68mer bound randomly along the control dsDNA, as observed for the wild-type complex (Supplementary Fig. S4B). In addition, there was no statistically significant difference in the percentage of complexes bound at the gap region between the wild-type (63%) and EQ-Smc5/68mer (58%, Fig. 4B). Finally, EQ-Smc5/68mer also primarily used the head-NSE module to engage with the linear gap and control DNA (Supplementary Fig. S4C left). Importantly, EQ-Smc5/68mer specifically bound to the gap region via the head NSE module (Supplementary Fig. S4C, right). Close to two-thirds of the EQ-Smc5/68mer complexes (64%) bound to gap DNA possessed AFM volumes consistent with monomers, with the rest appearing in higher-order states. These observations suggest that the ATP hydrolysis defect does not detectably change the overall shape of Smc5/68mer imaged by AFM, nor its preference for ssDNA gap regions and its reliance on the head-NSE module for DNA binding.

NSE subcomplexes are required for optimal Smc5/6 binding at the ssDNA gap region

Our data suggest that the head-NSE module of Smc5/6, including SMC-head regions and two NSE subcomplexes (Nse1-3-4 and Nse5-6), is mainly responsible for mediating Smc5/6 binding to the ssDNA gap region. We next asked whether these two subcomplexes could directly contribute to this interaction. To address this, we purified two Smc5/6 variants lacking either Nse5-6 (Smc5/66mer, Fig. 5A, left) or Nse1-3-4 (Smc5/65mer, Fig. 5A, right) [27]. Consistent with previous reports, Smc5/66mer but not Smc5/65mer was capable of ATP hydrolysis (Supplementary Fig. S5A) [9, 27].

We first assessed the AFM volumes and shapes of Smc5/66mer and Smc5/65mer in their free forms. The AFM volumes of Smc5/66mer (586 ± 242 nm3) and Smc5/65mer (586 ± 188 nm3) suggested that both were in monomeric forms (Supplementary Fig. S5B), like Smc5/68mer (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Further, Smc5/66mer and Smc5/65mer adopted the ‘bead-on-stick’ shape with dimensions like those observed for the Smc5/68mer (Supplementary Fig. S2D, E). The total, head-NSE module, and SMC arm lengths of Smc5/66mer were averaged at 49.3 nm, 19.6 nm, and 28.9 nm, respectively, while these values for the Smc5/65mer were 48.1 nm, 19.9 nm, and 28.0 nm, respectively. Like Smc5/68mer, these dimensions match those predicted by AFM simulations based on their cryo-EM structures (Supplementary Fig. S2D, E). These results suggest that the absence of either the Nse1-3-4 or Nse5-6 subcomplexes does not significantly affect the overall shape or dimensions of Smc5/6 in AFM images.

Next, we assessed Smc5/65mer and Smc5/66mer DNA binding after incubation with either the control dsDNA or the gap DNA. The incubation was performed without ATP, as ATP did not affect the preferential binding of ssDNA gap regions by Smc5/68mer (Fig. 4). As observed for Smc5/68mer (Fig. 2C), Smc5/66mer and Smc5/65mer were randomly distributed along the control dsDNA (Supplementary Fig. S5C). While both complexes retained preferential binding to the gap region, gap binding frequencies were significantly reduced for Smc5/66mer (43%) and Smc5/65mer (36%) (Fig. 5D) compared with Smc5/68mer (64%; Fig. 2C). These results suggest that both subcomplexes contribute to optimal gap region association by Smc5/6. Despite the defect described above, the head-NSE module of Smc5/65mer or Smc5/66mer remained dominant over the hinge for DNA binding (Supplementary Fig. S5D) and preferentially associated with the gap region (Supplementary Fig. S5E). AFM volume measurements suggest that gap DNA-bound Smc5/66mer complexes were a mixture of monomers (40% of species: 739 ± 48 nm3) and higher-order species (60% of species: 2698 ± 510 nm3). Gap DNA-bound Smc5/65mer showed 83% of protein particles with monomeric volume (586 ± 67 nm3) and 17% with larger volumes (1905 ± 505 nm3). Compared to Smc5/68mer, the frequency of monomeric complexes was significantly higher (p = 0.001) for Smc5/65mer, but not for Smc5/66mer. This result suggests that the absence of Nse1-3-4 may reduce Smc5/6′s ability to form multi-complex entities at gapped DNA regions. Collectively, these results suggest that while loss of Nse1-3-4 or Nse5-6 does not alter Smc5/6′s dependence on the head-NSE module for DNA binding or preference for gap DNA regions, these subcomplexes contribute to optimal Smc5/6 association with the ssDNA gap region.

Head-NSE module’s DNA binding sites contribute to Smc5/6 gap region association

It has been shown that mutating DNA-binding sites in the head-NSE module of Smc5/6 identified in the cryo-EM structure sensitizes cells to the ssDNA-specific cytidine deaminase, connecting these sites with ssDNA metabolism in cells [30]. Building on these findings, we investigated whether these DNA-binding sites contribute to Smc5/6′s targeting of ssDNA gap regions. To this end, we mutated the DNA-binding sites mapped on Smc5, Smc6, Nse3, and Nse4 and purified the resultant mutant Smc5/66mer complex (4DNAm, Fig. 6A–B). While 4DNAm Smc5/66mer still interacted with dsDNA or 37-nt gap DNA (Fig. 6C), it displayed a significant reduction in preference for the ssDNA gap region compared to the wild-type Smc5/66mer (Fig. 6D). For the control dsDNA substrate, 4DNAm Smc5/66mer remained randomly distributed (Fig. 6D). Strikingly, while DNA binding through the head-NSE by 4DNAm Smc5/66mer was still dominant, hinge-DNA binding events were significantly increased for 4DNAm Smc5/66mer compared to the wild-type Smc5/66mer on both gapped DNA (p = 0.036) and dsDNA (p = 0.023) (Fig. 6E). The shift to the hinge binding by 4DNAm Smc5/66mer was more pronounced on dsDNA than on gap DNA (Fig. 6E). Together, these results demonstrate that DNA binding sites on the head-NSE module directly contribute to ssDNA gap region association, and that without these sites, Smc5/6 relies more on the hinge module to associate with DNA.

Smc5/68mer gap association is influenced by ssDNA length and junction site preference

To gain further insight into Smc5/6′s preferential association with ssDNA gap regions, we asked whether this association is influenced by the ssDNA length. We generated a DNA substrate containing a 15-nt ssDNA gap using the same protocol as described above, except that the starting plasmid lacks two of the four nickase sites (Fig. 2A). As such, the resultant 15-nt ssDNA gap site is located at the same position as the 37-nt gap on the linear DNA substrate (23% from the nearest DNA end). Under the same experimental conditions, we found that Smc5/68mer maintained its preferential association with the gap region, though the relative occupancies were reduced compared with those at the 37-nt gap region (Supplementary Fig. S6A, B).

The above data suggest that Smc5/68mer binding to the ssDNA gap region can be partly influenced by ssDNA length. However, it could not discern whether Smc5/6 binds preferentially to the ss-dsDNA junction site. As described above, the gapped DNA substrates could not be used to distinguish the junction site from the ssDNA region using AFM. We thus generated a substrate with a 550-bp dsDNA region followed by a 5′ 170-nt poly-T overhang, thus containing a single ss-dsDNA junction site (Fig. 7A). In AFM images, dsDNA and ssDNA on the poly-T tail DNA display distinct heights of 0.38 nm (± 0.07 nm) and 0.17 nm (± 0.04 nm), respectively. This AFM height difference allowed us to unambiguously identify the dsDNA and ssDNA regions, with the poly-T overhang appearing with lower AFM heights than dsDNA (Fig. 7A). As such, the transition of these two regions represented the ss-dsDNA junction site. We classified Smc5/6 binding positions as being at the ss-dsDNA junction, along the ssDNA, or along the dsDNA, based on whether the DNA on either side of bound Smc5/6 was double- or single-stranded. Among DNA-bound Smc5/68mer, 58% of the complexes bound at the ss-dsDNA junction, compared to 19% along the ssDNA and 23% at the dsDNA (Fig. 7B–D). We note that, in line with our finding that Smc5/6 has similar binding preferences for ssDNA and dsDNA at the single-molecule level, bulk biochemical results also demonstrate that Smc5/6 binds to both types of DNA with comparable affinities [10]. In summary, our results show that, on the poly-T overhang DNA, Smc5/6 exhibits about 3-fold higher binding frequencies at ss-dsDNA junction sites than at ssDNA or dsDNA. Considering that there is only one ss-dsDNA junction site, but 170 nt of ssDNA, the relative Smc5/6 binding specificity for ss-dsDNA junction compared to ssDNA is expected to be significantly higher. These data, using poly-T overhang DNA, suggest that ss-dsDNA junction binding is the major factor contributing to Smc5/6 targeting of ssDNA gap regions.

Discussion

While Smc5/6 has multiple roles in genome maintenance processes, our understanding of how it interacts with the various forms of DNA generated during these processes remains limited [4, 5, 7]. Recent fluorescence-based single-molecule analyses have revealed that Smc5/6 can recognize DNA structures resembling DNA replication and repair intermediates, thereby providing a molecular framework for some of its biological functions [26–28]. However, a higher-resolution understanding of how Smc5/6 interacts with DNA structures remains lacking. To address this knowledge gap, AFM imaging was used to examine Smc5/6 interactions with small ssDNA gap structures, which frequently form during genome replication and repair. Previous studies investigated Smc5/6 binding to longer ssDNA gaps on the order of kilo-nucleotides [27, 28]. In this study, we focused on gap DNA substrates containing 37-nt or 15-nt ssDNA regions flanked by dsDNA regions, which can mimic subsets of endogenous DNA gaps. AFM results show that Smc5/6 preferentially binds to the ssDNA gap regions over dsDNA regions in an ATP and partially ssDNA gap size-dependent manner (Fig. 8). Using an overhang DNA substrate that allows visualization of ss-dsDNA junction sites, we show that Smc5/6 prefers ss-dsDNA junction sites over ssDNA and dsDNA regions, providing one explanation for its selective association with gap regions. Moreover, studies of several Smc5/6 variants inform our understanding of its DNA-binding mechanisms. We show that Smc5/6 associates with gap regions predominantly via its head-NSE module. Within the head-NSE module, two NSE subcomplexes and multiple DNA-binding residues facilitate optimal association with the gap region (Fig. 8). Importantly, mutating DNA-binding sites on its head-NSE module shifts Smc5/6 toward a hinge DNA-binding mode (Fig. 8). Collectively, our data provide insights into how Smc5/6 associates with ssDNA gap regions and ss-dsDNA junction sites, which inform its roles in genome maintenance.

Summary of key findings and a mechanistic model of Smc5/6 association with ssDNA gap regions. Smc5/6 displays a ‘bead-on-stick’ shape consistent with a globular head-NSE module connected to SMC arm and hinge regions. Smc5/6 preferentially engages a ssDNA gap region over surrounding dsDNA through its head-NSE module, and this is partly mediated by predominant association with the ss-dsDNA junction site (boxed region). Gap region association is facilitated by the Nse1-3-4 and Nse5-6 subcomplexes, as the Smc5/66mer and Smc5/65mer variants are recruited to the gap region less efficiently than the Smc5/68mer. DNA-binding residues within the head-NSE module are essential for its engagement with the gap region, and without them, Smc5/6 shifts DNA binding to the hinge module.

Smc5/6 lacks arm-bending, and the implication of this unique feature

Our AFM data and image simulation results show that Smc5/6 and its variants examined here adopt ‘bead-on-stick’ shapes without arm bending (Fig. 1C–D S2). The dimensions calculated based on AFM data for the entire Smc5/6, its arm module, and its head-NSE module agree with those derived from cryo-EM and negative stain EM data (Supplementary Fig. S2B) [8–13, 15, 37]. All three types of techniques failed to detect the arm-bending feature reported for cohesin and condensin, which entails arm folding back at an almost 180-degree angle based on both cryo-EM and AFM studies [46, 50, 51, 54]. This conclusion is also supported by data from two recent pre-prints: a HS-AFM study of the yeast Smc5/66mer in solution and a cryo-EM study of human Smc5/66mer [53, 55]. Both studies found no evidence of arm-bending [53, 55]. A structural explanation for this distinguishing attribute of Smc5/6 among eukaryotic SMCs is that the coiled coils of the Smc5 and Smc6 arm regions lack a simultaneous helix break, which allows arm bending in cohesin and condensin [9]. Smc5/66mer, like cohesin and condensin, can perform DNA loop extrusion in vitro [29, 56–59]. Its lack of arm-bending suggests that either this structural feature is unnecessary for SMC-based DNA loop extrusion or Smc5/6 uses a distinct mechanism to perform DNA loop extrusion. In either case, the no-arm-bending feature needs to be considered when devising models of Smc5/6-mediated DNA manipulations and genome activities.

Smc5/6 preferentially binds to ssDNA gap regions and the underlying mechanisms

Our AFM analyses found that Smc5/68mer preferentially binds ssDNA gap regions (37- or 15-nt) over dsDNA regions (Figs. 2C; S6B). This result is consistent with findings from orthogonal optical trap methods [26–28]. AFM and optical trap studies complement each other, reporting Smc5/6 behaviors toward short and long ssDNA gaps, respectively. The combined results suggest that Smc5/6 targets both short and long ssDNA gaps, indicating that it may help manage ssDNA gaps of different lengths in cells. Due to limited resolution, optical trap methods cannot directly test if Smc5/6 binds to a ss-dsDNA junction site. We addressed this issue by AFM-based examination of a substrate containing a dsDNA region tailed by poly-T ssDNA (Fig. 7). We found that Smc5/68mer showed preferential binding to the ss-dsDNA junction site over ss- or ds-DNA regions (Fig. 7). This represents the first single-molecule visualization of Smc5/6 contacting a ss-dsDNA junction site at nanometer resolution and provides an explanation for its predominant association with ssDNA gap regions (Fig. 2C; Supplementary S6B). When comparing a 37-nt ssDNA gap substrate with a 15-nt ssDNA gap substrate, we found that the former allowed more frequent engagement of the gap region by Smc5/6 (Fig. 2C; Supplementary S6B). A possible interpretation of this result is that Smc5/6 association with the ssDNA gap region requires a minimum ssDNA length within the gap.

AFM images showed that monomeric Smc5/6 is sufficient to support its association with either the ssDNA gap region or the dsDNA regions. In addition, multiple Smc5/6 complexes were observed bound in close proximity on DNA. While the resolution offered by AFM could not discern if the assembly represents oligomeric states or multiple Smc5/6 complexes adjacent to each other, the near absence of these species in the DNA-free pool of Smc5/6 suggests this is a DNA-mediated assembly (Supplementary Fig. S1C, S4A, S5B). Previous studies found that Smc5/6 complexes labeled with two differently colored quantum dots are colocalized on DNA and that a small fraction of Smc5/66mer could assemble into dimeric forms [26, 29]. Currently, the in vivo relevance of the higher-order states of Smc5/6 is unclear, and future exploration is needed to clarify whether Smc5/6 complexes engage cooperatively in DNA association.

The head-NSE module and its DNA binding sites contribute to Smc5/6 gap association

For all complexes examined here, Smc5/6 was found to be predominantly engaged with DNA through the head-NSE module (Figs 3C, D, 6E, Supplementary S4C, S5D). This conclusion could not be derived from optical trap investigation due to its limited spatial resolution, but is consistent with the cryo-EM finding that more than two dozen DNA-binding residues are located in the head-NSE module [37]. Using AFM, we further defined which components and biochemical activities of the head-NSE module contribute to DNA association by testing Smc5/6 variants lacking individual NSE subcomplexes or carrying ATPase site mutations on Smc5 and Smc6. We found that optimal ssDNA gap-region binding by Smc5/6 requires both Nse1-3-4 and Nse5-6 but is independent of ATP or ATP hydrolysis by the SMC subunits (Figs 4B and 8). Our finding of ATP-independent gap region association is consistent with conclusions from fluorescence-based single-molecule studies and warrants further testing for its biological consequences [26–28].

We also examined a Smc5/6 variant for which DNA-binding residues within the head-NSE module, as mapped by a cryo-EM structure, were mutated [37]. Our data suggest that these DNA-binding sites located on Smc5-head, Smc6-head, Nse3, and Nse4 contribute to Smc5/6 association with ssDNA gap regions (Fig. 6D). Differing from these subunits, Nse5-6 does not have DNA-binding ability [13, 21–25]. However, given that Nse5-6 can alter Smc5/6 conformations [8, 9], we envision that this regulatory role may underlie its contribution to efficient Smc5/6 binding to gap DNA regions. These findings may provide an explanation for the in vivo observations that mutating not only the Nse3-4 or Smc5-6 DNA binding sites but also the Nse5-6 binding sites on Smc5-6 severely impair genome maintenance processes or lead to cell death [9, 15, 37]. In summary, our data support a model in which Smc5/6 uses its head-NSE and the DNA-binding sites within this structural module to preferentially associate with ssDNA gaps and ss-dsDNA junctions. This association is promoted by a non-DNA-binding NSE subcomplex and does not require the arm-bending feature seen in cohesin and condensin nor ATP hydrolysis (Fig. 8). These unique features can provide a molecular basis for the markedly different roles of Smc5/6 compared with cohesin and condensin in cells.

Smc5/6 hinge may play a transient role in DNA association

Our AFM data also revealed a low frequency of events in which the hinge region of Smc5/68mer engaged with DNA (Fig. 3C). This finding is consistent with the ability of the hinge domain to interact with DNA [23, 24, 52, 53]. We further showed that mutating DNA-binding sites in the head-NSE module increased the frequency of hinge-DNA binding (Figs 6E, and 8). These data suggest that Smc5/6′s hinge domain may play a transient role in DNA association. This visualization of hinge DNA binding in the holo-Smc5/6 complex provides an explanation for the finding that hinge DNA-binding mutations in fission yeast lead to slow growth and genotoxin sensitivity [24]. The dominance of Smc5/6′s head-NSE module over its hinge region in DNA binding is likely because the former contains 25 positively charged residues, while the latter contains only 2–3 positively charged potential DNA binding residues [24, 37, 52]. AFM studies and biochemical examinations have shown that hinge DNA binding in cohesin can serve several roles, such as transferring DNA to SMC head regions and establishing sister-chromatid cohesion [34, 60], whereas this activity in condensin can facilitate DNA loop growth [61]. For both cohesin and condensin, hinge-mediated functions are thought to be facilitated by dynamic arm bending, which brings the hinge and head regions in proximity. The lack of arm bending on Smc5/6 suggests that its hinge may contribute to complex activity in mechanisms distinct from other SMCs. Currently, little is known about the biological functions of Smc5/6 hinge DNA binding in specific genome maintenance processes, warranting further study. As a general limitation of in-air AFM imaging, DNA-binding steps and transient conformational states are not fully resolved. Despite these limitations, our studies reveal the intrinsic ability of Smc5/6 to use its head-NSE module to preferentially target a simple ssDNA gap region. This study provides a framework for subsequent studies to elucidate the structure and dynamics of Smc5/6 interactions with DNA intermediates generated during DNA replication and repair.
