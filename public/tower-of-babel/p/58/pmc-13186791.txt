Passive cooling garments for outdoor thermal adaptation: a field experiment
Authors: Yasuhiro Shimazaki, Naoto Tominaga, Jihui Yuan, Masayoshi Adachi, Nabila Putra, Masaki Tajima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thermal adaptation and mitigation measures have been actively undertaken owing to warming climates, focusing on human-centered designs. Because clothing affects human energy balance namely thermal comfort, a suitable choice of clothing can be thermal adaptation. Similar to typical urban mitigations, two passive cooling techniques, which are ventilation and solar reflection were applied to a conventional garment. Accordingly, four types of garments (non-reflective loose-fitting, non-reflective tight-fitting, solar-reflective loose-fitting, and solar-reflective tight-fitting) were prepared and investigated by participants in terms of physical thermal load and subjective perceptions. Field measurements during walking were conducted in summer outdoors. Overall human thermal loads for clothed participants were smaller than undressed condition, indicating wearing clothing fundamentally acts as a buffer against environments suggesting comfort. Because the main heat-gain mechanism outdoors is irradiation, significant difference was observed in radiative heat gain between reflective loose and non-reflective loose, as well as reflective tight and non-reflective tight wearers. Despite the main cooling mechanism is convective heat loss, no significant difference in convective heat loss between loose and tight fitting garments were observed. Observed relationships among thermal sensation, thermal comfort, and human thermal load further suggest the validity of the experiments. Ideally, garments with both reflective and ventilation can improve thermal exposure significantly compared with the conventional garment. Our small scheme for heat adaptation in summer outdoor was confirmed in human energy balance; however, it did not work satisfactorily in perceptions, and thus more effective measures accompanying with detailed understanding of clothing effects are expected even under varying climate conditions.

Introduction

Higher urban temperature relative to the surrounding areas, so-called Urban Heat Island phenomenon (UHI), coupled with global climate change, negatively effects on humans living there, as reported by Heaviside et al. (2017). Since the replacement of natural urban surfaces to their darker and less vegetated artificial surfaces is a cause of UHI (Mohajerani et al. 2017), urban design strategies play a significant role in mitigating UHI depending on regions. Utilizing ‘cool materials’ with higher solar reflectance potentially contribute to reducing UHI (Akbari et al. 2001; Synnefa et al. 2008). It is also considered that natural ventilation in urban area called ‘urban ventilation’ could also potentially reduce UHI effect and improve outdoor thermal comfort in various location (Di Bernardino et al. 2023; He et al. 2020).

Efforts to reduce UHI effects are increasing, nevertheless, there is a growing recognition that these efforts will be insufficient to resolve this issue instantly. Therefore, with the progress of the UHI, thermal adaptation measures as well as thermal mitigation have been actively undertaken (Akbari and Kolokotsa 2016). Various adaptations have been devised to deal with this issue. Since intense summer heat can lead to uncomfortable conditions or heat-related illnesses for residents, workers and even visitors, particularly human-centered design concepts are crucial. To reflect human sensibility in the design of better living environments, it is desirable to focus on human thermal comfort or sensation as criteria (Jamei et al. 2016).

Thermal comfort can be influenced by six major factors: human elements, such as human activity level and clothing, and climatic elements such as air temperature, humidity, air flow, and radiation. Based on Fanger’s comfort equation (Fanger 1970), the human energy balance, including the above-mentioned influential factors, namely, the heat transfer between humans and the ambient environment, is a good indicator of the human thermal state of comfort.

Regarding human elements, because clothing affects human energy balance (Havenith et al. 2012) and is the only way to improve outdoor passively, the better choice of clothing can be considered an adaptation. Clothing adjustments can reflect personal preferences for thermal comfort; moreover, they can work quickly and inexpensively, depending on the situation. The effects of clothing on human thermal comfort are commonly understood simply by insulation characteristics of clo-value under a constant indoor condition (ISO 9920:2007). However, in actual environments, particularly outdoors, this is complicated depending on the air that is trapped in and on the clothing and the properties of the fabric, and there is a lack of a specific method for designing and evaluating its wearers.

As heat transfer loss from human is normally enhanced with increasing air movement in vicinity of fabrics, forced cooling garments are frequently used in industrial applications such as using fan (Butts et al. 2017; Chan et al. 2017), and other active applications are use of textile temperature regulation by such as a semiconductor refrigeration (Zhu and Feng 2021); however, mechanical cooling clothing is not always suitable for any wear form. Ultimately, fundamentally understanding the formation of heat and ventilation inside clothing microclimate is important, therefore, quantification of ventilation has been attempted (Ando, Shimazaki, and Kodama 2021).

The effects of color, namely the radiative properties related to heat absorption in clothing, are well understood under sunlight (Hodder and Parsons 2007). Even in low indoor solar radiation, clothing color can also affect human thermal comfort. (Ji et al. 2024). Temperature elevations in the clothing microclimates under the solar radiation were reported to tend to follow the order of absorptance (Shimazaki et al. 2017). Despite the importance of radiation properties on humans outdoors (Ichinose et al. 2024), only a few researchers focus on outdoor heat gain through outdoor garments in addition to conventional insulation characteristics of clo-value.

Similar to UHI strategies, ventilation and radiative passive modifications using vicinity of human-body are promising strategies for mitigating outdoor heat. Because the main cooling mechanism in the human energy balance equation outdoor is convective heat loss (both sensible and latent), there are several ways to enhance airflow, including the selection of fabric, clothing microclimate space, and material morphology, such as thinness and porosity. The air gap volume influenced by microclimate thickness was reported to be attributed to the onset of convection in the microclimate space (Li et al. 2024). On the contrary, because the main heat-gain mechanism outdoors is irradiation, shielding or reflection from irradiation is an effective way to reduce radiation heat gain.

Previous studies mainly utilized clo-values alone and did not include radiative effects and mainly focused on active strategies for outdoor wearers. Thus, considering the importance of outdoor space on human health and well-being, we attempted to improve the outdoor human thermal environment by applying novel functional cool garments to the outdoor stayers. In response to comfort with energy saving solutions, we experimentally examined outdoor thermal environmental improvements using two passive cooling techniques, namely ventilation and solar reflection, implemented through four novel garments designed for ventilation and reflective solar shielding. A methodology for understanding human-biometeorology for specific passive cooling garments was proposed to clarify how these passive garments affect the quantitative human thermal load, and subjective perception evaluations were also conducted simultaneously to collect user experiences. The findings of this study can provide a theoretical basis for a future UHI adaptation in terms of improving human’s thermal experience.

Field measurement

An outdoor field experiment was designed to evaluate the differences in the thermal environments of cool garments. The same short white trousers and shoes were worn in each shirt condition as shown in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Experimental situation

Experimental situation

Participants

Nine healthy Japanese male university students aged 22.6 ± 0.9 year voluntarily participated in this study (mean ± S.D.). Their mean height was 169.1 ± 5.4 cm and their mean weight was 64.1 ± 11.3 kg, consequently their Body Mass Index (BMI) was 22.3 ± 3.4 and fit with a Japanese standard body range. The participants were expected to adapt to the local climate. The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Toyohashi University of Technology.

To identify the degree of clothing fit for individuals, a 3D whole-body scanner (VITUS; Vitronic, Wiesbaden, Germany) was utilized for measurement of human shapes. The scans were conducted vertically for 500 mm every 5 mm, from the neck to the abdomen. The shapes of both the naked and clothed human bodies were digitalized at each height as shown in Fig. 3 (a), and the gap area and volume were calculated for all participants. The degree of fitting differs among individuals; a preliminary measurement summary of the fitting conditions is presented in Fig. 3 (b).Fig. 3Quantifying clothing gaps

Quantifying clothing gaps

The mean gap volume and the standard deviation was 1782 ± 843 cm3 for the tight fitting and 9173 ± 1873 cm3 for the loose fitting. Lee et al. (2007) previously reported that the gap volume when wearing shirts was approximately 6000 cm3, and our measurements showed a similar extent. Assuming the human body to be approximately cylindrical shape, the average air gap width can be calculated as the difference between diameters for clothed and naked body, which is 2.4 cm for loose fit and 0.5 cm for tight fit, respectively.

Human thermal perceptions

A questionnaire survey was also conducted to subjectively quantify human experiences. The air current and radiation perceptions, as well as typical thermal sensations, and thermal comfort were obtained. Perceptual questionnaires on thermal sensation (7 point scale from − 3: cold to + 3: hot), thermal comfort (5 point scale from − 2: uncomfortable to + 2: comfortable) (ASHRAE 2013), draft sensation (4 point scale from 0: imperceptible to 3: very noticeable) (S. Lee et al. 2014), and thermal radiation sensation (4 point scale from 0: imperceptible to 3: very noticeable) (McIntyre 1977)were conducted. The expressions were translated from their original language into Japanese. Notably, because the purpose is to evaluate difference in clothing microclimate, the draft sensation was not asked for undressed conditions.

Experimental procedures

The participant experiments were performed using the above-mentioned four garments: tight fitting and reflective, loose fitting and reflective, tight fitting and non-reflective, and loose fitting and non-reflective. For comparison, the undressed naked conditions were also investigated. In this experiment, based on a within-subjects design, all nine participants were tested under all garment conditions. The experiments were performed on an outdoor paved road around a schoolyard at Toyohashi University of Technology campus during the summer. The climate in Toyohashi is mild, and summer (July to September) is hot and humid like other cities in Japan. Field experiments were conducted in the middle of summer within a short period of time, from August 24th to September 1 st in 2020. Each participant participated in only one session per day (single session) and their garments were randomly provided. Subjects with different garments were sometimes simultaneously exposed to the outdoor environment for 50 min at the same activity level, including 10 min of rest before and after 30 min of walking at a velocity of 81 m/min (equivalent to 3.3 Mets).

Comparative analysis

Based on a within-subjects experimental design, repeated measures multiple comparisons analysis was used to assess differences among all garment conditions. Paired-samples t-test was also conducted to determine whether there is a difference between the existences of cooling mechanisms. Human thermal load for each garment conditions

As the participants and their activities were unified, human metabolism and mechanical work were the same among the conditions for individuals. The overall human thermal load for the undressed condition had the highest value, which was significantly different from the overall human thermal load of reflective loose, reflective tight, non-reflective tight and non-reflective loose wearers from multiple comparisons. Basically, undressed participants have larger solar gain due to direct insolation and at the same time and relatively larger convective heat and evaporative losses due to direct wind, compared to dressed participants. Except for this undressed condition, the values among the dressed conditions were relatively the same for the convective heat loss. Totally, the overall human thermal loads during the experimental period of 30 min are 63 ± 33 W/m2 for reflective loose garment, 65 ± 26 W/m2 for reflective tight garment, 86 ± 32 W/m2 for non-reflective loose garment, 114 ± 43 W/m2 for non-reflective tight garment, and 148 ± 47 W/m2 for undressed conditions, respectively.

Overall human thermal load for reflective-loose is significantly smaller compared with conventional non-reflective tight wearers due to the combined effects of solar shielding and ventilation. The amount of radiation gain (net radiation) for reflective loose, reflective tight, non-reflective tight and non-reflective loose garments was also significantly smaller than that for undressed humans based on multiple comparisons. For example, the difference in net radiation between reflective loose and undressed conditions was approximately 100 W/m2. When we compare the amount of net radiation, significant differences were observed between reflective loose and non-reflective loose, and also reflective tight and non-reflective tight wearers based on t-test.

When we evaluate the effects of ventilation garments, it is hard to find significant difference in convective heat loss between loose and tight fitting garments. Instead, we observed the largest convective heat loss when undressed.

Discussion

The experiments were conducted under summer heat, and thus thermal experiences leaned towards hotter and uncomfortable side. To utilize the findings for heat adaptation in terms of an evidence-based approach, the primary outcome was a quantitative indicator of human thermal stress. Preferably, improvements are also perceivable. As we can see from the human thermal load, fundamentally, wearing clothing is a good choice to maintain thermal states better under heat because it reduces solar heat gain through the shading effect. Although undressed humans can achieve convective heat loss owing to the direct wind without garments, they receive more direct solar heat. The amount of convective heat releases is limited compared to the amount of outdoor radiative heat gains, and it is important to consider the optical properties of materials in the clothing microclimate.

In fact, the novel reflective and loose-fitting garment can improve overall thermal load compared to conventional non-reflective and tight-fitting garments due to combined effects of solar shielding and ventilation. This was confirmed in terms of the energy balance. Radiation shielding is effective; in particular, reflective garments exhibit smaller radiative gains. Light-color garments basically tend to absorb less solar radiation than dark-colored materials (Ichinose et al. 2024; Shimazaki et al. 2017) and white colored shirts used in the experiment basically lead to comfort by reducing solar gain. Use of different colored garments may deliver a good outcome. Despite radiative gain significantly differ between reflective and non-reflective garments and convective heat loss does not significantly differ between loose and tight fitting garment in energy analysis, the difference is significantly perceived between loose and tight fitting garments and cannot be perceived between reflective and non-reflective garments. Since participants were still exposed to a certain level of heat and felt enough warm, the radiative heat reduction may be insufficient for improving perceptions and any positive effect for improvements may induce cooler perception. The experiments were conducted during walking. For example, if participants stay calm or sit down with relatively lower metabolic heat gain consequently smaller overall human thermal comfort, they possibly experience cooler and more comfort considering the relative contribution of amount of reduction in thermal load.

Limitations

As we attempted, it is important to include clothing effects rather than conventional insulation treatment for individual comfort. The formation of clothing ventilation and radiative heat-flow in a real environment is a complex and unpredictable mechanism and only few researchers have attempted to reflect detailed mechanisms of such clothing effects on conventional human energy balance calculation. Therefore, a detailed understanding of the formation and application of air and heat flow under garments, particularly outdoors, is required in future studies.

This study proposes a design methodology for determining ‘which clothing strategy cools the human body better outdoors’ in terms of physical energy calculations. Understanding the fundamental mechanism of clothing is important; however, for realistic garment geometries and dynamic human movement, uniform gap assumptions and pure natural conventions may not reflect real-life geometry.

The experimental conditions were influenced by mainly insolation rather than wind. The results would be sensitive to given conditions. Considering outdoors, although downward radiation is undoubtedly an influential factor, solar reflection affects human heat gain in some cases, such as in light-colored pavements. Normal asphalt pavements have an albedo of approximately 0.1 (Li et al. 2013); however, pavements with high reflectance are in some case utilized under realistic conditions (Kousis and Pisello 2023). Reflective garments are more effective in improving thermal comfort under reflective urban conditions. Therefore, understanding the relative importance of urban albedo and clothing reflectivity is also beneficial for adapting to outdoor summer heat.

Conclusions

Because clothing affects human biometeorology, the choice of suitable clothing can be considered an adaptation to heat. Similar to typical UHI mitigations, we prepared novel ventilation and solar reflective techniques for passive cool garments from a conventional T-shirt and experimentally examined human thermal environments of wearers during walking in summer outdoors.

The effects of clothing on human thermal comfort are commonly understood based on the insulation characteristics of the clo value. We successfully provide a way to understand the physical state of wearers and their perceptions simultaneously for specific garment characteristics other than the conventional clo value in actual outdoor applications. We can represent an example in which the human state can be changed even if the fundamental insulation properties are the same. Wearing clothing is a good choice for maintaining a better thermal state under heat because it basically acts as a buffer against outer environments; thus, garment conditions experience a reduction in both radiative heat gain and convection heat loss, suggesting comfort. The outdoor radiative heat gain can be significantly improved by wearing reflective cool garments. Despite personal ventilation of clothing having little effect in human thermal load, draft sensation can be slightly improved if wearing loosely. Our novel reflective and loose-fitting garment was confirmed to improve the overall thermal load compared to conventional non-reflective and tight-fitting garments owing to the combined effects of solar shielding and ventilation in terms of energy balance.

Ideally, garments with a combination of solar reflection and ventilation were expected to significantly improve human thermal environments compared to the conventional garment. Although, our small scheme for heat adaptations in summer garments did not work satisfactory for perceivable improvement of overall human’s thermal environments, it provides valuable insights into the role of clothing design parameters, such as ventilation and solar reflectivity, in influencing human thermal load under outdoor conditions. Along with a detailed understanding of the formation and application of air and heat flow under garments, more effective measures or modification of properties are required even under varying climatic conditions.
