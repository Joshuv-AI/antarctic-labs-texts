Anatomical characterization and visualization of the left dominant inferior mesenteric ganglionated plexus for nerve sparing colorectal surgery
Authors: Miki Yaguchi, Tomokazu Kawashima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The current Japanese colorectal surgical guideline excludes preservation of the inferior mesenteric plexus (IMP) from the category “preservation of all autonomic nerves (AN4).” However, clinical and experimental evidence indicates that the IMP surrounding the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) is essential for maintaining postoperative bowel, urinary, and sexual function. In clinical practice, the inferior mesenteric ganglia (IMGs) within the IMP are frequently overlooked because they are small, inconspicuous, and difficult to identify during colorectal surgery. To clarify their surgical relevance, we analyzed the anatomy of the IMP and IMGs using physical and virtual dissection of 17 formalin-fixed human cadavers. Left-dominant IMGs and one to four ganglion clusters were consistently identified on IMA’s left side. Importantly, the location and size of the IMGs showed significant correlations with the branching patterns of the IMA. These findings support the concept of a “left-dominant inferior mesenteric ganglionated plexus,” which is rarely visible in standard anterior or right-oblique laparoscopic views. From a surgical perspective, our results indicate that preoperative assessment of IMA branching patterns may allow prediction of the size and extent of the IMGs, thereby facilitating nerve-sparing colorectal surgery. When identification is challenging, intraoperative confirmation using lymphatic or nerve markers may further improve preservation of autonomic function.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-39814-8.

Introduction

The inferior mesenteric plexus (IMP) plays a crucial role in regulating the lower colon and rectum, which are the distribution areas of the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA). In addition to its primary function depending on the accompanying artery, animal experiments have revealed its involvement in regulating the surrounding pelvic organs, such as the bladder and reproductive organs1. Postoperative defecation and urogenital dysfunction due to IMP damage have been reported2,3. Furthermore, in terms of the ligation site of the IMA with the IMP during rectal cancer surgery, better continence, fewer obstructive urinary symptoms, and improved sexual function were achieved for a low-ligation group than for a high-ligation group at 9 months4. There appears to be debate regarding the relationship between the IMA ligation height and postoperative function. However, a systematic review and meta-analysis of colorectal cancer surgeries clearly showed that low ligation reduces genitourinary dysfunction compared to high ligation5. Therefore, considering factors, such as cancer stage and the presence or absence of gross nerve invasion, it is necessary to preserve the autonomic nerves, including the IMP, to the greatest extent possible without compromising the potential for cure3,6. Furthermore, procedures, such as IMP blocks for pain relief in chronic lower abdominal pain and cancer pain, are also being performed7,8, necessitating the reaffirmation and reorganization of anatomical knowledge regarding human IMP.

Within the IMP, the ganglion associated with the IMA root is known as the inferior mesenteric ganglion (IMG). This IMG is consistently present and well-developed in experimental mammals9,10. Its neurological basis has been elucidated through research, and the mammalian IMG plays a central role in signal relay, integration and modulation, regulation of involuntary functions, and neurotransmitters for neurons within complex nerve fiber circuits projecting to the pelvic organs1,11,12. The clinical outcomes of postoperative functional disorders suggest that the human IMG plays a central role in the experimental mammalian IMP3.

However, although the IMG is defined in human anatomy textbooks and numerous illustrations exist, explanations are limited. The human IMG is a small ganglion compared with other structures, such as the celiac ganglion. Many illustrations inappropriately depict the IMG within the ambiguous flat mesh preaortic plexus. In general, the human IMG is depicted as an unpaired single ganglion in the midline region of the IMA root13–16. Those located above the IMA root are commonly observed14,15,17. However, some lesions are located below the root13 or the bilateral ganglia18, indicating that the location is inconsistent.

Even the few classical anatomical studies focusing on the human IMG have merely classified its position as either right or left relative to the IMA root19. A recent meta-analysis of anatomical variations of the IMA branch20 reported no findings regarding the three-dimensional interaction and visualization of the IMA and IMG. Failure to consider its interactions with surrounding structures may have merely resulted in an emphasis on its diversity and complications in the anatomy of the human IMG. For these reasons, particularly in the clinical setting, the autonomic nerves surrounding the IMA have been uniformly described as the IMP, without distinguishing the unclear human IMG2,3,6. It also remains unclear whether the human IMG exists constantly, as in experimental mammals, as well as its number (single or paired ganglion), location, and characteristics.

This study aimed to (1) re-evaluate anatomical and histological findings of the human IMP and its contained IMGs in relation to their surroundings, (2) characterize anatomical variabilities of the abdominal preaortic ganglia contained in the IMP, and (3) provide visualized anatomical evidence of the IMP and its forgotten IMG for anatomical basis and nerve-sparing colorectal surgery.

Results

Preaortic plexus contributing to the inferior mesenteric plexus

Rather than being a flat, complex mesh-like structure in front of the abdominal aorta, the preaortic intermesenteric plexus, located between the superior and inferior mesenteric arteries, was a thin plexus connecting two thick main trunks, the intermesenteric trunks (IMT). The IMT descended alongside the aorta, receiving the lumbar splanchnic nerves (LSNs) along the way. In the lower portion, the left and right branches of the IMT joined to form the superior hypogastric plexus (SHP) (Fig. 1). Approximately 1–3 developed ganglia were observed on each side of the IMT. The ganglia identified macroscopically as being on the IMT or IMG (Fig. 1A) were histologically confirmed to be clusters of large ganglion cells (Fig. 1B, C). Small nerve ganglia scattered along the IMA (Fig. 1D) showed poorly transilluminated dense areas (Fig. 1E), which were histologically confirmed to be populations of ganglion cells (Figs. 1F–H). In contrast, areas that transilluminated well, despite ganglion-like swelling (indicated by yellow arrowheads in Figs. 1D and 1E), only contained nerve fibers.

Course of the inferior mesenteric plexus and its contained ganglia

The fine descending branches, which originated from the left and right IMTs, converged into the IMA, forming a complex IMP around the IMA (Fig. 2). Owing to fibrous replacement by bifurcation and communication of branches, these courses became increasingly distally indistinct. However, examples were observed where the stratification was relatively well-preserved, even distally (Fig. 2A). Others were observed in which stratification was only preserved proximal to the region near the three main branches of the IMA (Fig. 2B). In general, the right preaortic plexus branches derived from the right IMT contributed to the anterior plexus of the descending and sigmoid mesocolon, whereas the left preaortic plexus branches (left IMT) contributed to the posterior plexus. The IMGs were scattered within the IMP, forming an inferior mesenteric ganglionated plexus. Regarding the positional relationship of the IMG relative to the IMA, the IMG was located on the right side of the IMA in 5/17 cases (29.4%) (Figs. 2A, B), but on the left side or left posteriorly in 100.0% of the cases (Fig. 2B). Right IMGs were observed in the right or right anterior aspect of the IMA root, ranging from zero to one ganglion (average, 0.29). Their average volume was small, ranging from 1.00 to 18.85 mm3 (average, 12.14 mm3).

The left IMGs formed along the IMA on the left or left posterior side, ranging from one to four ganglia (average, 2.47). Their volume also significantly varied, ranging from 4.19 to 904.78 mm3 (average, 74.83 mm3), with considerable variation in location and number among individuals.

These results demonstrate that although small IMGs occasionally appear on the right side of the IMA root, they generally constitute a series of ganglia located to the left or posterolateral to the main trunk of the IMA within the IMP. When the IMA was short (predominantly type A), the first IMG was extremely large. Conversely, when the IMA was long (atypical types A, B, and C), the IMGs were small and appeared in multiple consecutive clusters.

Discussion

The peripheral autonomic pathways are difficult to anatomically characterize because of their complex morphology and significant individual variation. This study demonstrated that the inferior mesenteric ganglionated plexus was undeveloped compared with the corresponding structure in experimental mammals but was consistently present in humans. The IMG was associated with the length and branching pattern of the IMA. In addition to this comprehensive anatomical characterization, physical and virtual visualizations provided realistic images for future nerve-sparing colorectal surgery.

Localization of the undeveloped human IMG

Compared with the well-developed and consistent mammalian IMG, the human IMG is underdeveloped, has an unclear location, and contains much unknown anatomical information. Nevertheless, the IMG is important in the IMP because it controls nerve fibers and serves as a transfer site1,11,12, which makes nerve preservation desirable for function-preserving surgery3,6. However, in many human anatomy textbooks, illustrations depict the IMA root with an IMG, but explanations are scarce13–16,18. Slightly older human autonomic nerve studies contain descriptions and schematic diagrams of the IMG. Mitchell, publishing in 1953, stated that the IMG might exist just above the root of the IMA. However, minute, discrete ganglia are more often found above and below the vessel or scattered in the proximal part of the plexus21. Southam, publishing in 1959, investigated the presence of IMGs in 22 stillborn infants19. He found an IMG in each specimen: eight cases had a single ganglion anterior to the IMA; seven cases had a ganglion along the left border of the artery; and four cases had a ganglion along the right border of the artery. In the remaining three cases, the IMG score doubled. Furthermore, recent studies of the abdominal autonomic ganglia have reported that the IMG may be absent or present on either the right or left side or bilaterally22. The IMG may also be located at the midline or distal to the root. In other words, descriptions of the IMG vary among studies and are inconsistent.

In our analysis, human IMGs were (1) undeveloped but persistently present. Furthermore, (2) they appeared more frequently on the left than on the right side of the IMA, forming a cluster of ganglia that extended along the left or posterior left side of the IMA. Therefore, although the developmental arrangement of the IMG, whether a pair of ganglia or a single central IMG, remains unclear, it is evident that the final morphology comprises a small cluster of ganglia consistently appearing on the left side of the IMA, albeit with individual variations. In vivo identification is difficult because of the generally small size of the IMG embedded in the connective tissue. However, it should be recognized as the inferior mesenteric ganglionated plexus, not merely as a simple IMP.

Anatomical characteristics of the IMG and IMP

The presence of small IMGs, predominantly found on the left side of the IMA, provides useful information for nerve-sparing surgery. We examined their relationship with the IMA branches to understand the reasons for IMG diversity.

Our results showed that, particularly when the LCA branches form a proximal point alone (type A), the IMA trunk is short and the first IMG is larger in volume. In types B and C, the IMA trunk was relatively long with small ganglia scattered along it, and the ganglia were comparatively well-developed at the last vascular bifurcation. Blood vessels are essential for nerve differentiation, aggregation, and progression23–25. Therefore, the lack of branching in the accompanying blood vessels and their increased lengths may result in longer migration distances for the ganglia, potentially leading to the formation of small scattered ganglia. Furthermore, the relationship between the ganglion position and presence of large arterial branches crossing nearby is in line with our previous findings26–28 and those of previously mentioned developmental studies23–25, consistent with the tendency of the last ganglion at the arterial branching site to be the largest IMG in types B and C.

Notably, one patient with atypical type A had an unusually long IMA trunk, measuring 62 mm, resembling the characteristics of types B and C. Furthermore, in this variant, the first IMG was small, whereas the last IMG was the largest. This is similar to the characteristics of types B and C. In other words, although this variant had arterial branching typical of type A, the length of IMA’s main trunk resembled that of types B and C. Consequently, these variants shared characteristics with types B and C, such as ganglion arrangement and size. These findings suggest that although the arterial branching morphology is a key factor in ganglion arrangement and size, the IMA main trunk length is also an important factor affecting these characteristics.

Furthermore, the stratified course of the IMP was intriguing. The nerves from the right IMT primarily contributed to the anterior mesenteric plexus, whereas those from the left IMT contributed to the posterior plexus. The reasons for the stratification of the IMP and the left-dominant appearance of the IMGs could not be definitively determined in this study. However, it has been speculated that developmental positional changes in the lower digestive tract may also contribute. Developmental biology texts clearly demonstrate the relationship between the course of autonomic nerves and effects of esophageal development29. Owing to the influence of gastric rotation, a similar rotation occurs in the lower esophagus. Consequently, the vagus nerves located bilaterally in the upper esophagus transform into the anterior and posterior esophageal plexuses in the lower esophagus. Although considerable individual variation in vagus nerve anastomoses and branches is known, detailed anatomical data support this variation30.

Consequently, although developmental positional changes in the gastrointestinal tract and corresponding course of the surrounding autonomic nerves are often discussed in the esophagus29, this phenomenon has not been described in the colorectal region. Evolutionary and comparative anatomy of the abdominal autonomic nerves

The evolutionary transition of the IMG was understood with the aid of foundational data for IMG neural projection, physiological experimental analysis, and comparative anatomical data. This included studies in cats31, dogs32, rabbits33, guinea pigs9, and rats10.

Although there are some differences in these experimental mammals, the shared morphology shows that the structure and spatial relationship of the IMG significantly differ from those in humans. The morphology of the preaortic plexus is relatively simple. A single thick intermesenteric nerve descends from the celiac and SMG to the IMG root along the aorta. The IMG does not form at the IMA root. Instead, the intermesenteric nerve runs along the IMA for a considerable distance before forming an IMG near the intestine. This results in the IMG being relatively free of retroperitoneum. Although multiple IMGs may form, in such cases, they develop craniorostally rather than bilaterally from the IMA. Additionally, the right and left hypogastric nerves branch directly from the floating IMG, and an SHP does not form. In contrast, monkeys and apes exhibit a series of comparative anatomical changes. The structures surrounding the IMG become more complex and differentiated, the IMG is positioned at the root of the IMA, and the SHP forms in front of the aortic bifurcation into the iliac arteries34. Based on evolutionary changes in the autonomic nervous system of the abdomen, a single unilateral ganglion is considered the prototype in mammals. In humans, the surrounding structures are presumed to have become more complex and have developed within the IMP. Although small ganglia may appear around the IMA owing to developmental changes in the digestive tract, the primary IMG is believed to be located on the left or posterior left side of the IMA. Because morphology significantly differs between animals and humans, proving this theory requires developmental studies using human embryos, making such investigations extremely difficult.

Implication for nerve-sparing colorectal surgery

To preserve the pelvic function during colorectal surgery, particular attention must be paid to “the left LSN,” which passes posteriorly to the IMA and is connected to the SHP. Ensuring the preservation of this nerve along with the pelvic autonomic nerves is crucial35–39. This heightened focus is also related to the finding that the central fiber group, which is primarily composed of descending nerves from the celiac plexus, innervates the left half of the colon. The lateral fiber group, which is formed by the LSNs, innervates the pelvic organs40,41. Furthermore, the latest Japanese surgical guidelines (Japanese Classification of Colorectal, Appendiceal, and Anal Carcinoma)42,43 state that preservation of all autonomic nerves (AN4) includes nerves from the LSNs, which constitute the SHP to branches from the pelvic plexus. Exclusion of the IMA plexus (ganglion) is related to this42,43. However, because a considerable number of nerves originating from the IMP connect to the left LSNs and SHP, it should be referred to as the left IMT itself or the left prehypogastric nerve16 rather than the left LSNs (Figs. 1 and 2). Retrograde neuron tracing studies have confirmed that neurons from the bladder in pigs and humans project not only to the LSNs but also to the IMG44. Notably, dissecting the IM ganglionated plexus along with the lymph nodes can impair urinary, sexual, and bowel function, even when preserving the nerve bundles considered part of the hypogastric nerves. Recent studies of nerve-sparing surgery around the IMA have shown that the nerve and lymphatic layers are distinct. This allows selective lymph node dissection2. Zheng et al. also reported a possible IMP preservation technique for the high ligation of the IMA in colorectal cancer surgery6. Identifying and preserving the IMG is particularly important, as it plays a central role in the IMP. This allows D3 lymph node dissection and total autonomic nerve preservation, including the inferior mesenteric ganglionated plexus, during colorectal surgery. This combination was previously considered difficult to achieve. This study demonstrates the importance of predicting the location of the IMGs based on their characteristic positioning along the left and posterior sides of the IMA. These left-dominant IMGs correlate with IMA branching, i.e., preoperative confirmation of IMA branching allows prediction of the number and size of the IMGs. This is useful for nerve-sparing colorectal surgery, especially compared to situations where the IMGs are buried within intra-mesenteric adipose tissue and no anatomical information is available.

Furthermore, nerves contributing from the IMP to the SHP were consistently identified as were fibers converging from the distal sigmoid mesenteric plexus to the SHP and the hypogastric nerve and fibers retrograding from the SHP and hypogastric nerves (Fig. 6 green arrowheads). These complex neural networks among the IMP, SHP, and HN have been known for a long time19,45. Preserving nerves during rectal or sigmoid colon cancer surgery requires careful attention to the dissection of the ureterohypogastric and/or prehypogastric fascia, which contain nerves distributed to the pelvis46–51.

Finally, considering the functional importance of the IMGs, we summarize the precautions for preserving the IMGs contained within the IMP. The IMGs are located posterior or left posterior to the IMA, placing them in positions that are difficult to recognize. Even when segmented and colored differently, as shown in Fig. 5, the IMGs remain difficult to identify. In patients with cancer, lymph nodes are enlarged, making it difficult to distinguish between lymph node #253 and the IMG. In our analysis, the LCA formed a large solitary ganglion when it branched proximal to the IMA. During D3 lymph node dissection for lower colon cancer, careful dissection of the swollen #253 lymph node group is important. Furthermore, relatively large ganglia formed at the branching points of the main branches of the internal mammary ganglion (IMG), specifically the LCA, superior common artery, and SRA. The sizes of these ganglia suggest that their functions are important. One solution is to use indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence as a lymphatic vessel marker52,53 carbon nanoparticle staining as a lymph node maker54, and methylene blue staining as a neural marker55,56. However, it is also important to have preliminary knowledge that the ganglia tend to develop at the arterial bifurcation points.

Conclusion

The present study provides visual evidence of the anatomical characteristics of the ambiguous inferior intermesenteric ganglionated plexuses, especially those containing forgotten ganglia. The anatomical characteristics of the localization and size of the IMGs, which are strongly related to the arterial branching pattern and length of the IMA, significantly contribute to the anatomical and clinical identification of the IMGs. This evidence would also be beneficial for distinguishing the IMGs from the #253 lymph nodes in D3 dissections of lower colon and rectal cancer. However, it is advisable to actively use markers such as ICG fluorescence, carbon nanoparticle staining, and methylene blue staining, as it is challenging to distinguish IMGs buried within mesenteric fat from lymph nodes. Future studies on how to better visualize and preserve these structures in clinical practice, including technological advancements, are essential.

Limitations

All samples used in this study exclusively originated from donated elderly cadavers. Therefore, we must note that age-related and degenerative changes may influence the interpretation of our results, as proposed in a modern framework for the structured reporting of anatomical variation57. Further verification in younger adults is necessary for deeper understanding, as is the application of additional immunohistochemical techniques, such as immunofluorescence, to better visualize the organization and types of nerve fibers within the plexus. Furthermore, microdissection of the delicate autonomic plexus is a skilled and time-consuming process, which makes analyzing numerous cases difficult. However, obtaining more precise results requires analysis of a larger sample size owing to its potential influence on plexus morphology.

Methods

Specimens

This study used 17 donated adult cadavers (four of men and 13 of women; age, 72–103 years; mean age, 89.8 ± 8.9 years) with no medical history of colon or rectal disease. Seventeen formalin-fixed cadavers were randomly selected from those provided to the Toho University School of Medicine for educational and research purposes. This sample size was deemed appropriate for analysis once multiple examples of each major IMA branching pattern20,58 were included and anatomical features of the IMG were identified for each of these IMA branching patterns. The cadavers were fixed with 7% formalin solution via the radial artery, and their donors provided informed consent to their use in anatomical research. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Toho University Faculty of Medicine (approval number: A24015-A21069). All study steps were in line with the provisions of the 1995 Declaration of Helsinki (revised in Edinburgh 2000).

Dissection

Abdominal autonomic nerves below the celiac plexus to the hypogastric nerves and their surrounding arterial branches were macroscopically and histologically analyzed in 17 formalin-fixed human cadavers. First, a regional abdominopelvic specimen was prepared by removing most of the peritoneal organs along with the celiac and superior mesenteric arteries while leaving the digestive tract distal to the descending colon and pelvic viscera with the IMA intact. Next, the autonomic nerves distributed to the retroperitoneal organs were dissected using a binocular microscope (SZ60; Olympus) and/or a stand loupe (SL-30; Engineer Inc.). The findings were recorded using a digital camera (Pentax WG-90; Ricoh Imaging Co. LTD, Tokyo, Japan).

Macroscopic and histological identification of the IMG

We performed macroscopic identification of IMG candidates using previously published methods26. IMG candidates were defined as slightly bulging structures with a grayish hue compared with the surrounding white nerves. They appear darker during transillumination. Subsequently, clusters of large ganglion cells within these histological structures were definitively classified as IMG after Masson’s trichrome staining. Histological findings were recorded using a digital microscope imaging system (BX51 microscope with a DP71 digital capture system; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Morphometric measurements

The lengths of the slightly meandering IMA from the aortic base to the branching portion into the left subclavian artery, SCA, and SRA passing through each central point were measured using a nylon thread, and the straight-line length was measured using a caliper. The maximum length (a), width (b), and thickness (c) were measured using calipers. Three-dimensional reconstruction model

To understand the topographical arrangement of the IMGs, a three-dimensional anatomical model of the typical abdominal autonomic nervous plexus, preaortic ganglia, and arterial system was constructed using contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT). After removing most of the peritoneal organs, excluding the descending and sigmoid colon, a specimen containing the retroperitoneal and pelvic organs was prepared. The specimen was subsequently immersed in a 10% Isodine® solution for 2 weeks. It was imaged using an industrial CT scanner (NAOMI CT 3D-L; RF, Nagano, Japan). The imaging conditions were 5 mA, 100 kV, 0.2 mm thickness, and a 1040 × 1040 matrix size. Subsequently, manual segmentation was performed using the Inovitec Pro software (version 2.5.1; Inovitec Software FZ-LLC, Dubai, UAE), followed by image processing and modification using the Amira imaging software (version 2024.2; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).
