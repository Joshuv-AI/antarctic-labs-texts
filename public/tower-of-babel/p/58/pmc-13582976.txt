Conformational flexibility of talin enables force-free sampling of activation-competent states
Authors: Bright Shi, Gilbert Reyes, Tsutomu Matsui, Thomas M. Weiss, David J. E. Callaway, Zimei Bu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Talin serves as the central mechanotransduction hub in integrin–extracellular matrix adhesion, orchestrating the assembly of focal adhesions—multi-protein complexes that link integrins to the actin cytoskeleton. While cryo-EM revealed compact, autoinhibited architectures, talin’s behavior in solution remains unknown. Here, we integrate SEC–SAXS with Monte Carlo modeling (SASSIE), using AlphaFold predictions as the initiating template to determine the conformational landscape of full-length talin in solution. We show that talin does not adopt a single compact structure but instead populates a broad, flexible conformational ensemble characterized by R3 repositioning and partial F3-R9 disengagement. Critically, this ensemble intrinsically samples activation-prone conformations without mechanical force, which establishes a dynamic conformational equilibrium that lowers the energetic barrier for integrin engagement, vinculin recruitment, and actin association. This ensemble framework unifies structural, biochemical, and mechanobiological models of talin activation and suggests that intrinsic flexibility plays a central role in adhesion initiation and force transmission.

Introduction

In integrin-mediated adhesion to the extracellular matrix, focal adhesions serve as dynamic multiprotein assemblies that integrate mechanical and biochemical signals, enabling cells to sense, transmit, and respond to forces across the plasma membrane. These macromolecular complexes coordinate integrin signaling with cytoskeletal architecture, thereby regulating essential cellular processes, such as migration, morphogenesis, and differentiation1–5. Central to focal adhesion assembly and maturation is talin—a large, mechanosensitive scaffold that links integrins to the actin cytoskeleton and orchestrates adhesion initiation, stabilization, and force transmission1,6,7. Dysregulation of talin function has been implicated in a range of pathologies, including bleeding disorders, cardiovascular disease, and cancer metastasis4,8–10. Elucidating the structural mechanisms underlying talin-mediated mechanochemical signaling is critical for understanding how cells adapt to mechanical cues.

Talin’s mechanochemical versatility arises from its multi-domain and modular architecture: an N-terminal FERM domain (F0–F3) that binds integrins, FAK, and other signaling partners, and a rod domain (R1–R13 followed by a C-terminal dimerization domain (DD)) containing actin-binding sites, RIAM binding site, and numerous cryptic vinculin-binding sites (VBSs) (Fig. 1A)3,4,11,12. In the cytosol, talin adopts a compact, autoinhibited conformation that masks these key interaction surfaces, yet retaining low-affinity binding capacity13–16. This autoinhibition is enforced through intramolecular contacts that conceal key functional motifs: the F3 subdomain binds the R9 bundle to mask the integrin-binding site; R1–R2 and R7–R8 helices sequester cryptic VBSs; and the intrinsically unstable R3 domain, whose unfolding regulates vinculin or RIAM binding11,13,17–23. Despite these structural insights, the conformational behavior of full-length talin in solution remains unknown. The available cryo-EM structures were obtained under sample preparation conditions that may limit conformational sampling. For example, the earlier cryo-EM structure of autoinhibited talin (PDB 6R9T) employed GraFix stabilization, which uses sucrose gradient (10–30%) with glutaraldehyde gradient (0–0.2%) to chemically cross-link proximal residues and stabilize weak or transient interactions prior to vitrification24. While effective for preserving particle integrity, such cross-linking can restrain large-scale domain motions and preferentially stabilize conformations in which interacting domains are already in close proximity, thereby enriching compact autoinhibited states. Cryoprotectants, such as glycerol, may further favor compact conformations by increasing solution viscosity and stabilizing intramolecular contacts through preferential hydration effects25. A more recent cryo-EM study (PDB: 8VDO, 8VDP, 8VDR, and 8VDQ) minimized these potential artifacts by omitting cross-linkers and cryoprotectants and consequently revealed greater conformational heterogeneity in full-length talin17. Nevertheless, structural bias may still arise during vitrification, as proteins can adsorb to the air–water interface of the thin aqueous film prior to freezing, a process that may promote preferred particle orientations, such as more compacted conformations to minimize exposed hydrophobic surfaces26. These factors raise the possibility that the compact talin conformations observed by cryo-EM represent only a subset of the conformational ensemble populated under physiological solution conditions.

This limitation is critical in light of emerging evidence that talin operates through both force-dependent and force-independent mechanisms. For instance, while mechanical stretching of the rod domain exposes cryptic VBSs to recruit vinculin5,6,27, talin can also engage partners in the absence of force—such as during early adhesion formation14,28. Therefore, talin’s function cannot be fully explained by a simple binary switch between static autoinhibited and force-unfolded states. Instead, we propose that talin samples a dynamic conformational landscape that facilitates rapid transitions between autoinhibited and activation-prone states.

These observations motivate a solution-state analysis capable of capturing talin’s intrinsic structural heterogeneity. Size-exclusion chromatography coupled with in-line small-angle X-ray scattering (SEC-SAXS) is uniquely suited for this purpose, providing ensemble-averaged information on macromolecular size, shape, and flexibility29. By integrating SEC-SAXS with Monte Carlo modeling—using the full-length AlphaFold prediction as a starting template—we determined the dynamic conformational ensemble of full-length talin. We show that talin in solution occupies a broad spectrum of partially compact to extended states, with reorientation of the R3 domain and intermittent weakening of the F3–R9 interaction. These conformations are significantly more open than the available cryo-EM structures, revealing a continuum of pre-activated states that emerge spontaneously without force.

Our findings support a unified model in which talin’s activation arises not from a binary mechanical switch but from a finely tuned conformational equilibrium that integrates mechanical tension, local ionic environment, and binding partner engagement. This intrinsic structural plasticity provides a mechanistic basis for talin’s ability to mediate both force-dependent and force-independent adhesion assembly, providing new insight into how talin integrates mechanical and biochemical cues to regulate cell adhesion.

Results

Establishing monomeric talin conditions for solution-state structural analysis

Full-length talin undergoes a concentration-dependent monomer ⇌ dimer equilibrium mediated by the C-terminal DD, with autoinhibited dimer becoming appreciable at 2 mg/mL (7.4 µM) in 50 mM NaCl buffer12,13. Similarly, activated talin mutants that disrupt the intramolecular F3–R9 autoinhibitory interaction exhibit dimerization with a dissociation constant of ~1.82 µM13. These observations highlight the importance of defining solution conditions that preserve the monomeric state for structural analysis.

To characterize the intrinsic conformational ensemble of full-length talin, we first optimized biochemical conditions that maintain talin in a monomeric state. Purification by SEC in buffer containing 25 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, and 0.5 mM TCEP yielded a single, symmetric elution peak (Fig. 2A), and subsequent SDS–PAGE analysis confirmed the high purity of the eluted talin (Fig. 2B). We then performed SEC coupled with in-line multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS). Analysis of a 10 µg talin injection into Superose 6 Increase 3.2/300 GL column in buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.5 mM TCEP yielded a monodisperse species with a measured molecular weight of 270.0 kDa ± 0.4%, consistent with monomeric talin (Fig. 2C).Fig. 2SEC-MALS characterization of full-length talin.A SEC elution profile of purified talin in buffer (25 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, and 0.5 mM TCEP) showing a single, symmetric peak. A SEC-MALS concentration series (5–75 µg; Supplementary Fig. S1) further demonstrated that the measured molecular weight converges to ~270 kDa at lower injection amounts (5–17.5 µg), indicating minimal interparticle effects under these conditions, while higher injections show deviations consistent with concentration-dependent self-association (see Supplementary Note 1 for detailed analysis).

Because talin autoinhibition and conformational equilibria are sensitive to ionic strength, we further examined the effect of salt concentration on talin behavior by performing SEC-MALS measurements at 100, 150, and 500 mM NaCl (Supplementary Fig. S2). Increasing NaCl concentration to 500 mM shifted the elution peak to a lower volume compared with 150 mM, indicating an increase in hydrodynamic size; however, the measured molecular weight remains ~270 kDa, demonstrating that talin remains monomeric while adopting a more expanded conformation at higher ionic strength.

Together, these analyses demonstrate that under the optimized SEC conditions used for SEC-MALS and SEC-SAXS measurements, talin remains monomeric while sampling salt-dependent conformational changes in solution. These conditions, therefore, provide a reliable experimental baseline for probing talin’s molecular conformations in solution.

Monte Carlo modeling of SAXS data using an AlphaFold template

To interpret the SAXS profile in structural terms, we performed Monte Carlo simulations using the SASSIE suite31, beginning with the AlphaFold-predicted32 full-length talin model as the initial template (Fig. 5A). Flexibility was introduced at all known interdomain linker segments, including those between FERM subdomains (F0–F1, F1–F2, and F2–F3), between F3 and R1, and between each rod subdomain (R1–R13) and the DD. Each conformation was evaluated by calculating the theoretical SAXS profile and scoring the fit to the experimental data using reduced χ² values. The resulting χ²-Rg distribution has a pool of 612 generated structures (Fig. 5B), from which 42 conformations with χ² ≤ 1.25 were selected as being consistent with the experimental scattering (Fig. 1B, C). RMSD analyses comparing the SAXS-consistent ensemble, the AlphaFold model, and the cryo-EM structures to the best-fit SAXS model are provided in Supplementary Fig. S10.

Collectively, the selected ensemble captures the intrinsic conformational heterogeneity and flexibility of talin in solution, with notable mobility in both the N-terminal head and C-terminal tail. From this ensemble, the best-fitting structure yields a theoretical SAXS curve that closely matches the experimental data (Figs. 5C and S11). Its conformation (χ² = 1.102; Rg = 52.1 Å) preserves the general FERM-rod topology of AlphaFold but features a distinctly more extended C-terminal tail (Fig. 5D). This result indicates that reproducing the SAXS scattering profile requires conformations more expanded than the AlphaFold prediction and supports the presence of substantial interdomain mobility in solution. By integrating high-confidence atomic detail from AlphaFold structure, our modeling results define an experimentally constrained ensemble representing talin’s intrinsic conformational heterogeneity.

AlphaFold predicts the core architecture but underestimates talin’s conformation flexibility

The best-fit SAXS model (χ² = 1.102) retained the overall organization of the FERM (F0–F3) and rod (R1–R10) domains, showing only minor deviations from the AlphaFold prediction. (Unless otherwise stated, references to the “SAXS structure” below refer to this best-fit model). Confidence metrics for the AlphaFold prediction, including the predicted local distance difference test score and the predicted aligned error matrix, are presented in Supplementary Fig. S12.

To quantitatively compare the SAXS-derived model with the AlphaFold prediction, we calculated theoretical SAXS curves for the AlphaFold structure using the SasCalc module of SASSIE-web33. Both Guinier analysis and P(r) distributions revealed that the AlphaFold model is considerably more compact than the SAXS-derived ensemble (Fig. 4). Specifically, the AlphaFold model exhibits an Rg of 49.64 ± 0.32 Å and a Dmax of 157 Å, whereas the SAXS-refined ensemble displays an Rg of 54.24 ± 0.93 Å and a Dmax of 199 Å (Figs. 4B and S13). Because conformational sampling in SASSIE is restricted to flexible linker regions, the increased Rg and Dmax observed for the SAXS-refined ensemble arise from intrinsic conformational flexibility of talin rather than talin dimerization.

Structural superposition of our SAXS model with the AlphaFold-predicted structure in PyMol yielded an overall RMSD of 1.385 Å over 13,328 aligned atoms, indicating broad architectural similarity but with pronounced deviations in flexible domains. The greatest divergence occurred in the R11–DD tail region, with additional deviations observed in the F0–F3 head, consistent with earlier reports of conformational plasticity within the FERM domain and the C-terminal tail17,34–37. Collectively, our analysis indicates that while AlphaFold accurately recapitulates the core talin architecture, it does not fully capture the extent of its inherent conformational flexibility in solution.

SAXS uncovers talin’s enhanced domain flexibility limited in the cryo-EM models

We next compared our solution-derived model with recently reported high-resolution cryo-EM structures of autoinhibited talin (PDB: 8VDO, 2.7 Å; 8VDP, 3.4 Å; 8VDR, 3.7 Å; and 8VDQ, 5.5 Å)17. The F0–F1 and the DD domains, however, were not resolved in these structures due to inherent flexibility. We therefore selected 8VDQ (spanning F2–R13) for subsequent P(r) analysis, as it provides the most complete architectural context, encompassing a greater number of talin domains than the highest-resolution structure, 8VDO (F3–R11). The P(r) function of 8VDQ exhibited a Rg of 44.42 ± 0.23 Å and a Dmax of 133 Å, substantially smaller than both the AlphaFold (157 Å) and SAXS-refined (199 Å) models, indicating a more compact conformation (Fig. 4B). Although the missing F0–F1 subdomains in the 8VDQ contribute to this size difference, the substantial reduction in Dmax relative to the SAXS-refined model still points to a markedly more compact structure.

Direct structural superposition further validated this finding. Superposition of our SAXS refined structural model with the 8VDO structure, aligning 11,799 atoms, gave an RMSD of 1.158 Å over 8542 atoms, signifying a high degree of global structural similarity. Deviations were primarily confined to the F3 subdomain and, more prominently, the R3 and R11 domains. In contrast, alignment with the more complete 8VDQ structure (15,491 atoms) produced a higher RMSD of 3.156 Å over 10,530 atoms. Given the shared compact core between 8VDQ and 8VDO, this increased RMSD arises mainly from conformational differences in the additional regions present in 8VDQ—notably the F2 domain and, most significantly, the flexible R12–R13 domains, which adopt a more extended conformation in solution.

Collectively, these analyses indicate that talin in solution samples more expanded and flexible conformational ensembles than those observed in the cryo-EM structures. This increased domain mobility under nonrestrictive solution conditions underscores the dynamic nature of full-length talin. Thus, our SAXS data captures a broader spectrum of functional states than static cryo-EM snapshots, providing a critical link between molecular architecture and talin’s biochemical activity.

Discussion

To fully delineate talin’s pivotal function as a central mechanosensitive hub, it is essential to characterize the dynamic conformational ensemble it samples in solution. Talin’s intrinsic equilibrium fluctuations underpin its functional readiness and ability to integrate diverse activation cues. Despite recent cryo-EM structures17 providing unprecedented detail into the architecture of the autoinhibited state, they offer only limited insight into the inherent dynamic behavior of the full-length talin, particularly concerning the N-terminal F0–F2 subdomains and the C-terminal R12–R13–DD region. Critically, these static structures cannot fully reconcile talin’s rapid and versatile responsiveness across both force-dependent and force-independent signaling pathways.

Here, leveraging SEC-SAXS and Monte Carlo ensemble modeling, we establish the solution-state conformational ensemble of full-length talin, complementing both the high-resolution cryo-EM structures and the predictive insights from AlphaFold32. Our data fundamentally challenge the view of talin as being constrained to the fully compact autoinhibited state. Instead, we reveal that talin in solution populates a continuum of partially extended structures characterized by intrinsic domain mobility that is far more pervasive than previously appreciated, extending beyond the F0–F2 and R12–DD regions. These findings fill a critical gap by defining talin’s basal conformational ensemble from which mechanical, biochemical, and membrane-associated cues act.

Building on this ensemble-level perspective, our SAXS-constrained model reveals several mechanistic features that directly substantiate talin’s intrinsic dynamism. Foremost among these is a major conformational departure from the compact autoinhibited structure: a distinct rearrangement where the R3 domain adopts a nearly perpendicular orientation relative to the cryo-EM structure (8VDO) (Fig. 6). This reorientation also present in the AlphaFold predicted model, increases the distance and alters the spatial alignment between R3 and R10–R11, disrupting the critical hydrogen-bonding network that stabilizes the autoinhibited core17. Consequently, the R3 domain becomes markedly more solvent-exposed, with solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) calculations in UCSF ChimeraX of 5969 Å² (our SAXS model) and 5793 Å² (AlphaFold) compared to 4699 Å² in 8VDO. The greater solvent exposure in our SAXS model’s R3, compared to the AlphaFold prediction, results from a more extended conformation of the R13–DD domains, which positions R13 further from the R3 domain (Fig. 7). This dynamic movement of R3 confers substantial flexibility upon the R10–R11 domains, complementing the known mobility of the R12–R13 segment17. Structurally, this F3–R9 disengagement is coupled to a global rearrangement that positions the FERM domains closer to the R12–R13 segment, a configuration consistent with cryo-EM observations of dynamic F2–R12 interactions and minor populations of R13 engaging with F2–F317. We propose that these interdomain associations do not arise coincidentally but instead stabilize the transient F3–R9 disengagement. As the F3–R9 interface is a central clasp of autoinhibition, its spontaneous disruption—together with the observed mobility of R3—weakens key autoinhibitory interfaces and positions talin closer to activation-competent conformations prior to full mechanical unfolding.

The role of ionic strength provides a critical rationale for the more extended conformations we observe in solution. Talin’s conformational equilibrium is strongly salt-dependent, shifting progressively from a fully compact state at 75 mM salt to a fully extended one at 500 mM salt, with approximately 50% of the population elongated at 234 mM24. Our measurements were conducted in 150 mM NaCl, HEPES pH 7.4—conditions that provides a significantly higher ionic environment than those used to stabilize the compact 8VDO/8VDQ structure (50 mM KCl, Tris pH 8.0). These findings indicate that physiological salt conditions weaken electrostatic contributions to autoinhibitory interfaces, shifting the basal equilibrium toward more open states. This ionic modulation of talin’s energetic landscape occurs even in the absence of mechanical tension, implying that local ionic microenvironments—at membranes or adhesion sites—could serve as an independent, tension-independent regulator of talin activity.

Integrating these findings with AlphaFold predictions and cryo-EM structures allows us to refine prevailing models of talin activation. Earlier structural and single-molecule studies established a force-driven mechanical switch paradigm, in which talin transitions from a compact autoinhibited conformation to an unfolded, binding-competent state under tensile load13,17,24,38. Within this framework, intermediate configurations were largely interpreted as short-lived transition states along a cooperative unfolding pathway, and full-length talin was implicitly assumed to be predominantly compact in the absence of force. While this model has been foundational for understanding mechanotransduction, it did not directly address how intact talin behaves in solution under physiological ionic conditions, nor whether activation-prone conformations might already be thermodynamically accessible prior to mechanical input. Indeed, domain-level analyses have shown that key rod domains, such as R3, are intrinsically poised between folded and partially unfolded conformations in a 50:50 equilibrium under native conditions, suggesting that force may bias a pre-existing equilibrium rather than generate a new state de novo39.

Within this broader context, our SAXS-derived ensemble indicates that full-length talin intrinsically samples a distribution of partially extended states, with mobility concentrated in the N-terminal FERM region and the C-terminal R11–R13–DD segment, alongside internal rearrangements involving R3 and F3. The cryo-EM structures capture strongly compact, low-entropy states accessible under stabilizing conditions, whereas the AlphaFold prediction reflects a moderately compact architecture. Although these models define one region of talin’s conformational spectrum, neither alone encompasses the full range of states observed in solution. The partial release of autoinhibitory interfaces within the solution ensemble indicates that such conformations are thermodynamically accessible components of talin’s basal ensemble rather than exclusively force-induced intermediates. In this framework, mechanical stretch, membrane association, and partner binding act to shift the distribution within an existing energy landscape rather than function as binary triggers. Thus, talin is more accurately described not as a rigid switch, but as a dynamic ensemble whose intrinsic fluctuations lower the energetic threshold for activation.

Our findings establish that talin’s function as a central regulatory hub in adhesion complexes stems from its intrinsic conformational plasticity, governed by a dynamic equilibrium tuned by ionic conditions, molecular crowding, and partner interactions. This equilibrium integrates environmental cues into coordinated structural responses, wherein the reorientation of the R3 domain, disengagement of the F3–R9 interface, and C-terminal flexibility form an interconnected network that continuously modulates talin’s functional responsiveness.

Under physiological ionic strength, this equilibrium shifts talin toward an ensemble of partially open conformations. Crucially, the ability to sample these activation-prone states without force provides a structural basis for early adhesion events, enabling initial integrin engagement, vinculin recruitment, and RIAM exchange when mechanical load is minimal14,28. This pre-configuration becomes biologically decisive at the high local concentrations within focal adhesions12,40, where these transiently exposed sites are rapidly captured and stabilized. Specifically, partial F3–R9 release promotes integrin clustering and primes them for ligand engagement12,41, while increased R3 mobility may facilitate vinculin pre-binding or RIAM exchange, thereby shaping the composition of nascent adhesions. Furthermore, the extended conformations position the C-terminal actin-binding domain for early, weak cytoskeletal linkages that subsequently mature under contractility42–44. This cooperative stabilization drives downstream adhesion processes—including talin dimerization, integrin activation, and vinculin recruitment—thereby directly coupling the cellular microenvironment to adhesion assembly. Thus, talin’s conformational fluidity constitutes the fundamental mechanistic foundation for its role as a mechanochemical integrator, linking nanoscale rearrangements to macroscopic adhesion dynamics.

In summary, our solution-based analysis reveals that full-length talin exists as a dynamic ensemble that spontaneously samples partially open states, rather than a single compact autoinhibited structure. By combining SEC-SAXS with ensemble modeling, we show that physiological solution conditions promote mobility in both the FERM and rod regions and destabilize key autoinhibitory interfaces involving R3 and F3. These intrinsic flexibilities lower the activation energy for integrin engagement, vinculin binding, and cytoskeletal linkage, situating talin to respond rapidly to mechanical or biochemical cues. Together with recent cryo-EM structures17 and AlphaFold predictions32, our results establish a continuum model of activation, wherein force-independent conformational sampling primes talin for rapid transitions into force-bearing states.

This ensemble-based framework parallels our previous structural and dynamic studies of adherens junction complexes, in which cadherin-catenin and vinculin-bound cadherin-catenin assemblies exhibited progressively increased conformational flexibility and dynamics upon higher-order complex formation, underscoring the intrinsically dynamic nature of adherens junctions45–49. By analogy, our findings suggest that talin dynamics may be further amplified within focal adhesion assemblies, motivating future studies of its dynamic behavior in increasingly complex, force-bearing environments.

Materials and methods

Protein expression and purification

The cDNA open reading frame encoding full-length talin was subcloned into a pET-32a vector for bacterial expression, generating a construct with an N-terminal thioredoxin (Trx) tag followed by a tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease cleavage site and a C-terminal 6×His tag.

The plasmid was transformed into E. coli Rosetta 2 (DE3) cells. Cells were grown at 37 °C in terrific broth (TB) supplemented with 0.1 mg/mL ampicillin and 35 μg/mL chloramphenicol. Protein expression was induced at an OD₆₀₀ of 0.6 by adding 0.065 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), followed by incubation shaking for 5 h at 20 °C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5200 × g for 20 min, and pellets were stored at −80 °C.

For purification, cell pellets were resuspended in binding buffer (1× PBS, pH 7.4, 300 mM NaCl, 30 mM imidazole, 0.1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride) and lysed by sonication on ice. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 20 min. The supernatant was loaded onto a HiTrap Chelating HP Ni-affinity column. The column was washed with 15–20 column volumes of high-salt binding buffer (500 mM NaCl), and the protein was eluted with elution buffer (1× PBS, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole). The eluted protein was then incubated with TEV protease to cleave the N-terminal Trx tag.

The TEV-treated sample was further purified by SEC using a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 pg column (Cytiva) with a void volume of ~48 mL. The peak fractions corresponding to talin were pooled and loaded onto a HiTrap Q HP anion-exchange column (Cytiva). The column was washed with 10 column volumes of starting buffer (150 mM NaCl, 25 mM Tris pH 8.0, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.20 mM TCEP), and the protein was eluted with a 50-column-volume linear gradient to elution buffer containing 1.5 M NaCl, 25 mM Tris pH 8.0, 0.1 mM EDTA, and 0.20 mM TCEP. A final purification step was performed using SEC on either a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL or a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva), both with a void volume of ~8.0 mL.

Size exclusion chromatography with multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS)

SEC-MALS analysis was performed using a Thermo Fisher Scientific UltiMate 3000 UHPLC system. Talin was prepared at 11.77, 5.00, and 2.50 mg/mL in the running buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM TCEP). Samples of 5, 10, 12.5, 17.5, 25, 35, 37.5, and 75 µg were injected onto a Superose 6 Increase 3.2/300 GL column maintained at 25 °C, with a flow rate of 0.05 mL/min.

The column effluent was directed into a Waters/Wyatt microDAWN MALS detector (λ = 659.4 nm) equipped with a Wyatt QELS+ module for dynamic light scattering. The system was equilibrated, and data were collected and processed using Wyatt ASTRA 8 software. The weight-average molar mass of 270.0 kDa ± 0.4% was determined using the Zimm model with a dn/dc value of 0.185 mL/g.

SEC-SAXS data collection

SEC-SAXS experiments were conducted at the Stanford Synchrotron Radiation Lightsource Bio-SAXS Beamline 4-2, as previously described50. Detailed parameters for data collection and analysis are provided in Table S1.

Data acquisitions were performed using the SECAXS tab of BL4-2 BluIce51, a total of 500 X-ray images were collected with a 1-s exposure time every 5 s at a flow rate of 0.05 mL/min. After the first 100 images, which served as a blank, the X-ray shutter was closed until just before the sample eluted to prevent radiation-induced debris accumulation in the sample cell. An additional cell wash was performed during this shutter closure period. Data reduction and initial analysis were performed using the BL4-2 automated SEC-SAXS pipeline, SECPipe (https://www-ssrl.slac.stanford.edu/smb-saxs/node/1860)50, which utilizes the programs SASTOOL (https://www-ssrl.slac.stanford.edu/smb-saxs/node/1914) and ATSAS AUTORG52. Scattering data are presented as I(q) versus q, where q = 4πsin(θ)/λ, 2θ is the scattering angle, and λ is the X-ray wavelength. After manual inspection of the data, five frames were selected and averaged to generate the final scattering profile for further analysis.

SAXS data analysis and structural modeling

Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) data were analyzed to derive real-space structural parameters and construct molecular models of talin. The scattering intensity profiles, I(q), were processed using GNOM53 to compute the pair-distance distribution function, P(r), and determine the radius of gyration Rg and the maximum particle dimension Dmax. For P(r) calculations, data within the range 0.0076 Å−1 ≤ q ≤ 0.20 Å−1 were used. A range of Dmax values was tested, and the optimal value was selected based on the minimum α value and the highest total quality score reported by GNOM.

An ab initio molecular envelope of talin was reconstructed from the SAXS data using GASBOR (with the reciprocal space data fitting mode) within the ATSAS 3.0 package30. To further explore conformational flexibility, computational modeling was performed using the SASSIE program suite31 with the AlphaFold2-predicted full-length talin structure32 serving as the initial model. Multiple simulation rounds were conducted while treating the interdomain linkers as flexible regions. Each simulation round consisted of 5000–10,000 trial conformations. The resulting models were compared to the experimental SAXS data, and the best-fitting conformations were selected based on the lowest χ² values.

Theoretical SEC-SAXS scattering profiles for the AlphaFold2 model and the cryo-EM structure (PDB 8VDQ) were calculated using the SasCalc module implemented in SASSIE-web33. Corresponding P(r) functions were then derived as described above to facilitate direct comparison with experimental data.
