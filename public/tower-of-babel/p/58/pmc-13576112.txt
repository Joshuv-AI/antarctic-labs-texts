Plasma cyclophilin a as a novel predictor of major adverse cardiac events in patients with acute myocardial injury cohort study
Authors: Chaopu Zhang, Wei Zhong, Xiang Tang, Xia Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCyclophilin A (CypA) is a multifaceted immunophilin implicated in cardiovascular disease; however, its predictive value for Major Adverse Cardiac Events (MACEs) after acute myocardial injury remains poorly defined.PurposeThe study aimed to analyze whether admission Plasma CyPA independently predicts MACEs and to assess its predictive feasibility beyond recognized clinical risk factors.MethodIn this cohort study, 217 adults with an acute myocardial injury diagnosis exhibiting symptom onset within 24 hours and high cardiac troponin 1 were included. Plasma CyPA concentrations were evaluated using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Participants were closely followed up for 6 months to determine MACEs, defined as all-cause mortality, ischemic stroke, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or unexpected heart failure hospitalization. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis, multivariable Cox proportional hazard models, Kaplan-Meier survival curves, and calibration analysis were conducted with adjusted standard covariates.Result30% of participants experienced MACEs, with significantly higher CyPA concentrations among those with events than among those without (median 1.942 vs. 1.026 ng/mL; p < 0.001). Introduction

Acute myocardial injury is one of the most clinically significant manifestations in cardiovascular medicine. Nevertheless, despite major advances in reperfusion strategies, pharmacological management, and supportive care, a high number of patients admitted with acute myocardial injury still experience adverse outcomes, such as recurrent myocardial infarction, stroke, progressive heart failure, and cardiovascular death [1]. Early identification of patients at the greatest risk of such events has been a pressing, unmet need, helping focus efforts on enhancing therapeutic measures.

The existing prognostic systems for acute myocardial injury are largely based on traditional variables, including left ventricular ejection fraction, B-type natriuretic peptide, cardiac troponins, and clinical risk scores [2]. Although useful as prognostic markers, these variables are increasingly being supplemented by inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers, which may reflect pathophysiological aspects of cardiovascular susceptibility not captured by traditional parameters [2].

CyPA (or Peptidyl-Prolyl Isomerase A, PPIA) is a protein of the immunophilin family, initially described as an intracellular receptor for the immunosuppressant cyclosporine A [3]. In addition to its peptidyl prolyl cis-trans isomerase activity, CyPA is secreted in response to vascular cell exposure to reactive oxygen species, inflammatory cytokines, and mechanical stress [4]. Extracellular CyPA binds the CD147 receptor (also known as Basigin/EMMPRIN), triggering downstream inflammatory pathways, including NF-kB activation, matrix metalloproteinase secretion, and leukocyte chemotaxis [5]. These pathways are highly involved in cardiomyocyte injury, atherosclerotic plaque, and pathological myocardial remodeling [6].

Elevated circulating CyPA concentration levels have been reported in numerous cardiovascular conditions like hypertension, artery disease, aortic aneurysm, and heart failure [7, 8]. Etiologically, CyPA induces cardiomyocyte apoptosis and adverse ventricular remodeling, consequently intensifying heart injury. Despite significant biological plausibility, clinical evidence supporting CyPA as a diagnostic biomarker in patients with acute myocardial injury remains limited, with anticipated research evaluating its relationship with major adverse cardiac events (MACEs) [9].

This study aims to prospectively measure plasma CyPA levels in patients admitted to the hospital with acute myocardial injury to: (1) determine the predictive accuracy of plasma CyPA to identify patients who will ultimately develop MACEs; (2) establish its independent prognostic value after adjustment for known clinical risk factors; and (3) quantify the added value it brings to existing risk prediction models.

Methods

Study definitions

Acute myocardial injury

In this study, an increase and/or decrease in cardiac troponin I levels exceeding the 99th percentile upper reference limit, in the context of an acute clinical presentation consistent with myocardial ischemia or injury, was considered acute myocardial injury in line with the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction [1].

MACEs

The main composite endpoint, Major Adverse Cardiac Events (MACEs), was defined as the event of any of the following during six months of follow-up: (1) all-cause mortality; (2) non-fatal myocardial infarction; (3) non-fatal ischemic stroke; or (4) unplanned hospitalization due to worsening heart failure. All endpoint adjudication was performed.

Study design

A prospective single-center observational cohort study was conducted at the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University, Zhenjiang, Jiangsu Province, China. Of the 217 patients, 168 (77.4%) presented with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) - including 89 STEMI and 79 NSTEMI, while 49 (22.6%) presented with acute decompensated heart failure with myocardial injury. Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) was performed in 142 (84.5%) patients with ACS. Standard dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin + clopidogrel or ticagrelor) and anticoagulation were used according to current guidelines. Maximum CK and CK-MB values were recorded.

Study population, inclusion and exclusion criteria

Adult patients (aged 18 years and above) were enrolled consecutively, and a clinical diagnosis of acute myocardial injury was required. Inclusion criteria were: (1) they had to present within 24 hours of developing symptoms; and (2) they must have been willing to sign an informed written consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) the presence of severe chronic kidney disease. Glomerular filtration rate less than 15 ml/min/1.73 m2. Alternatively, ongoing dialysis, (2) inability to follow up, and (3) no remaining serum to perform the CyPA assay.

Clinical data collection

Demographic information, medical history, and vitals were measured at the time of admission. Hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, CHD, atrial fibrillation, smoking history, alcohol consumption, and family history of premature cardiovascular disease were systematically ascertained in the medical history. The values of the parameters of echocardiography, such as the left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVDd) and left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVSd), with the modified Simpson biplane method, were measured during the first 48 hours of admission. The admitting cardiologist recorded the New York Heart Association functional class. BNP concentrations were measured from EDTA-anticoagulated plasma using a chemiluminescence immunoassay. Patients were managed per guideline medical therapy, which included angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers/angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ACEi/ARB/ARNI), beta-blockers, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA), and soluble guanylate cyclase stimulators (sGC), as clinically indicated.

Plasma CyPA measurement

Venous blood samples were collected at admission (within 24 hours of hospital presentation), prior to any therapeutic interventions. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 10 minutes at 4 °C and stored at −80 °C until batch analysis. Plasma CyPA concentration was quantified using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit with a lower detection limit (0.1 ng/mL). Intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation were below 8% and 12%, respectively. Patients were stratified into two groups based on the optimal plasma CyPA cut-off determined by Youden's index from ROC analysis: low CyPA (Group 1, CyPA at or below 1.651 ng/mL, n = 162) and high CyPA (Group 2, CyPA above 1.651 ng/mL, n = 55).

Plasma CyPA was quantified by a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Catalog No. EK0974, MyBioSource, San Diego, USA). We used the 99th-percentile upper reference limit (0.04 ng/mL) for the cardiac troponin I assay at our hospital to define elevated levels.

Follow-up procedures

All patients were followed up at 1, 3, and 6 months after discharge through outpatient clinic visits and telephone interviews. Follow-up echocardiography and BNP measurement were performed at 6 months. An independent committee performed MACE adjudication without knowledge of CyPA values.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R, including data import (readxl package), data manipulation (dplyr package), data baseline description (tableone package), Cox regression (survival package), survival visualization (survminer package), ROC (pROC package), model validation (rms package), and figure generation (ggplot2 package).

Continuous variables are reported as the mean ± standard deviation. Owing to the categorical nature of the variables, counts and percentages are reported. T-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests were applied for between-group comparisons on continuous variables, as appropriate, and chi-squared or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables.

ROC analysis was used to assess the predictive accuracy of plasma CyPA for MACEs. The Youden index was used to establish the best cut-off. The Kaplan-Meier estimator produced MACE-free survival curves, compared with the log-rank test. Prognostic predictors were assessed using univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression; variables with p < 0.10 in the univariable analysis were included in the multivariable model. The incremental value of CyPA was evaluated using Cox models (base vs. full) with respect to C-index, AUC (DeLong method), likelihood ratio test, and AIC. The significance level was two-tailed, p < 0.05.

The proportional hazards assumption of the Cox model was assessed with a non-significant global p-value supporting model validity. Fisher's z-transformation was used to compute confidence intervals for Spearman correlations (ρ < 0.20 = negligible, 0.20–0.39 = weak, 0.40–0.59 = moderate, 0.60–0.79 = strong, and ρ≥ 0.80 = very strong). Highly skewed variables, including cTnI, were log-transformed prior to Cox regression analysis to improve model stability.

Model Calibration was conducted for the full multivariable Cox model incorporating plasma CyPA and was assessed using predicted 6-month MACE risk following the framework described by [10].

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 217 patients with acute myocardial injury were enrolled. The mean age was 63.8 ± 13.7 years, and 151 (69.6%) were male. Median admission BNP was 896 pg/mL (IQR: 324–1,850 pg/mL), mean LVEF was 32.5 ± 10.1%, and 70.0% of patients were in NYHA class II. The most prevalent comorbidities were hypertension (39.6%), type 2 diabetes mellitus (20.7%), coronary heart disease (23.5%), and chronic kidney disease (7.4%). Almost all patients received guideline-directed medical therapy at discharge. Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of patients with and without MACEs. The composite MACEs consisted of 18 all-cause deaths, 21 non-fatal myocardial infarctions, 9 ischemic strokes, and 17 unplanned heart failure hospitalizations.

Median plasma CyPA at admission was 1.12 ng/mL (IQR: 0.93–1.92 ng/mL). Using the Youden-optimal cut-off of 1.651 ng/mL, 162 patients (74.7%) were classified as low CyPA and 55 (25.3%) as high CyPA. Compared with the low-CyPA group, the high-CyPA group had a higher prevalence of a family history of premature cardiovascular disease (p = 0.001) and received mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists less frequently (p = 0.012). MACEs occurred in 61.8% of the high-CyPA group versus 19.1% of the low-CyPA group (p < 0.001).

The distribution of ACS and AHF as shown in Table 1 did not differ significantly between CyPA groups (p = 0.08). Because the diagnostic subtype (ACS vs AHF) may be clinically relevant, a predefined sensitivity multivariable Cox analysis incorporating diagnosis subtype was performed.

Predictive performance: ROC analysis of plasma CyPA

Plasma CyPA showed good discriminatory ability for predicting 6-month MACEs, with an area under the curve of 0.755 (95% CI: 0.680–0.830) (Figure 1). The optimal cut-off, determined by Youden’s index, was 1.651 ng/mL, with sensitivity 58.5%, specificity 83.6%, positive predictive value 60.3%, and negative predictive value 82.5%.

ROC curve of Plasma CyPA Level for predicting MACEs

Admission plasma CyPA levels were significantly higher in patients who experienced MACEs than in event-free patients (median 1.942 ng/mL [IQR 1.127–4.208] vs 1.026 ng/mL [IQR 0.919–1.283]; p < 0.001). Although all patients fulfilled the inclusion criterion of elevated cTnI, many showed only modest elevations, consistent with type 2 myocardial injury or acute heart failure.

Univariable Cox regression analysis identified log-transformed cTnI, BNP, LVSd, LVEF, family history of premature cardiovascular disease, and plasma CyPA as significant predictors of 6-month MACEs (Table 2). Plasma CyPA demonstrated one of the strongest associations with adverse outcomes (HR 1.64, 95% CI 1.434–1.875; p < 0.001).

Prognostic findings

Kaplan–meier survival analysis

MACEs occurred in 65 patients (30.0%) during the six-month follow-up. Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrated clear separation between the low- and high-CyPA groups (Figure 2). At 6 months, MACE-free survival was 80.8% (95% CI: 74.9–87.1%) in the low-CyPA group versus 38.2% (95% CI: 27.3–53.4%) in the high-CyPA group.

Kaplan- Meier Curves: MACE-Free Survival by Plasma CyPA Level

Multivariable cox - proportional hazards regression

In the multivariable Cox model adjusted for age, sex, NYHA class, BNP, LVEF, log-transformed cTnI, hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease (Table 3, Figure 3), plasma CyPA remained a strong independent predictor of MACEs (HR 1.698 per 1 ng/mL increase; 95% CI: 1.462–1.973; p < 0.001). BNP was not independently associated with MACEs after multivariable adjustment (p = 0.7834).

LVEF was the only other independently significant covariate (HR 0.967; 95% CI: 0.938–0.996; p = 0.0265). Log-transformed cTnI was not independently associated with MACEs after adjustment (HR = 1.347; 95% CI: 0.759–2.388; p = 0.3084). Given the isolated nature of this finding and the non-significant global test, the overall proportional hazards assumption was considered acceptable.

Incremental value of plasma CyPA

The addition of plasma CyPA to the baseline clinical model significantly improved risk prediction. The AUC was statistically significant (DeLong test p = 0.0001). The likelihood ratio test (χ2 = 39.65, p < 0.001) and the reduction in AIC (−37.65) confirmed the added prognostic value of CyPA. C-index improved from 0.632 to 0.770 after addition of Plasma CyPA. AUC increased from 0.663 (95% CI: 0.582–0.744) to 0.805 (95% CI: 0.736–0.874), demonstrating improved discrimination

Correlation analysis

Spearman correlation analysis showed weak positive associations between plasma CyPA and systolic blood pressure (ρ = 0.174, p = 0.010) and cTnI (ρ = 0.176, p = 0.0095). No significant correlations were observed with BNP, LVEF, or other conventional markers. All correlations were indicated as negligible or weak (Table 6, Figure 4).

Follow-up outcomes and cardiac remodeling

At 6-month follow-up, the high-CyPA group had significantly higher BNP levels and lower LVEF compared with the low-CyPA group (Figure 6). Delta changes showed less favorable ventricular remodeling in the high-CyPA group (both p < 0.0001). Higher admission CyPA was associated with a smaller reduction in BNP (ρ = 0.326, p < 0.0001) and a smaller improvement in LVEF (ρ = −0.318, p < 0.0001).

Subgroup analysis

The increased risk associated with high versus low CyPA was consistent across major clinical subgroups (Figure 8), with hazard ratios ranging from 2.85 to 5.24 (all interaction p > 0.21). Additional stratified analyses were conducted by diagnostic subtype (ACS vs AHF). In ACS patients (n=168), high CyPA was associated with significantly increased MACE risk (HR: 4.21; 95% CI: 2.331–7.67; p<0.001). A significant association was observed in AHF patients with myocardial injury (n=49; HR: 3.86, 95% CI: 1.14–13.07; p=0.030), although the confidence interval is wider due to a smaller sample size. These findings suggest that the prognostic association of CyPA is consistent across ACS and AHF presentations within the spectrum of acute myocardial injury.

Subgroup Analysis: CyPA Effect on MACE Across Clinical Subgroups

Comparative predictive performance

Plasma CyPA had greater discriminative ability (AUC 0.755) than cTnI alone (AUC 0.531; DeLong p < 0.0001). As shown in Figure 9, the combination provided only minimal additional benefit (AUC 0.764). However, because cTnI is primarily intended for diagnosis rather than long-term prognostication, this comparison should be interpreted with caution.

Model calibration results

Full multivariable Cox model calibration incorporating plasma CyPA was assessed by predicted 6-month MACE risk. The model showed acceptable agreement between predicted and observed event risk across predicted-risk quartiles (Table 7 and Figure 10). The calibration intercept was 0.113, and the calibration slope was 1.113, indicating no major systematic miscalibration and only mild underestimation of risk in the highest-risk quartile. The overall Brier score was 0.158, supporting acceptable accuracy of absolute risk prediction.

Discussion

This prospective cohort study of 217 patients with acute myocardial injury demonstrates that plasma Cyclophilin A (CyPA) is a potent, independent predictor of major adverse cardiac events. The principal findings are as follows: (1) admission plasma CyPA effectively discriminates between high- and low-risk patients for 6-month MACEs, with good predictive performance (AUC = 0.755); (2) elevated CyPA levels (above the optimal cut-off of 1.651 ng/mL) are associated with a markedly reduced six-month MACE-free survival, approximately halving event-free survival compared with lower levels; and (3) CyPA remains a strong independent predictor of MACEs after multivariable adjustment, with a hazard ratio of 1.698 per 1 ng/mL increase (95% CI: 1.462–1.973; p < 0.001).

Furthermore, integrating CyPA into standard clinical risk models markedly improved predictive performance, raising the AUC from 0.663 to 0.805 and the C-index from 0.632 to 0.770. These findings highlight that CyPA is involved in major pathophysiological mechanisms, specifically oxidative stress and inflammation, that are inadequately captured by established cardiovascular biomarkers such as cTnl, B-type natriuretic peptide, and Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (LVEF) [11].

Mechanistic rationale

The biological plausibility of Cyclophilin A (CyPA) in acute myocardial injury is well-known. A key feature of myocardial injury in response to oxidative stress is the active release of intracellular CyPA into the extracellular space. Once released, extracellular CyPA interacts with its receptor CD147/EMMPRIN on immune and vascular cells, initiating pro-inflammatory, profibrotic, and pro-oxidative signaling pathways [6, 12]. Moreover, CyPA regulates leukocyte recruitment and platelet activation at sites of vascular injury. Using these mechanisms, it lies at the intersection of oxidative stress, inflammation, fibrosis, and thrombosis, which are pivotal pathophysiological mechanisms that stimulate adverse cardiovascular events [7, 13, 14].

Predictive performance

The predictive performance of plasma CyPA for MACEs, with an AUC of 0.755, is comparable to established emerging cardiac biomarkers. For instance, soluble ST2 has exhibited AUC values of approximately 0.70–0.75 in acute heart failure populations, while galectin-3 has shown AUCs of approximately 0.68–0.72 in post-myocardial infarction cohorts [9, 15]. At an optimal cut-off value of 1.651 ng/mL, identified by the Youden index, CyPA showed good specificity (83.6%) and moderate sensitivity (58.5%). Although a negative CyPA result may help identify lower-risk patients, the 82.5% negative predictive value is not sufficient to rule out MACEs in clinical settings. Furthermore, median plasma CyPA concentrations reported in this study were lower than prior estimates, potentially due to variation across ELISA assays, inclusion of patients with different ethnic backgrounds, or milder myocardial injury severity. Overall, these results still remain for hypothesis generation [16].

Independence and prognostic magnitude

The adjusted hazard ratio of 1.698 per ng/mL increase depicts a clinically meaningful effect size. A similar relationship has been reported for standard biomarkers such as high-sensitivity CRP (about 1.3–1.7 per unit increase) and NT-proBNP (about 1.5–2.0 per doubling) in cardiovascular outcome studies [17]. Significantly, CyPA remained an independent predictor after adjustment for BNP, LVEF, and cTnl, which are pivotal indicators of cardiac function and injury. The consistency of significance indicates that CyPA has distinct pathophysiological pathways that are not yet captured by standard biomarkers.

Notably, BNP and log-transformed cTnI did not emerge as independent predictors of MACEs in the fully adjusted multivariable model (log cTnI: HR = 1.35, p = 0.308). Although log cTnI demonstrated a significant association with MACEs in univariate analysis (HR = 2.009; 95% CI: 1.212–3.333; p = 0.0069), this relationship was attenuated and lost statistical significance after adjustment for clinical covariates, whereas CyPA consistently retained strong and independent prognostic value.

The multivariate Cox regression satisfied the assumption of proportional hazards (global p = 0.2454 based on Schoenfeld residuals), supporting the robustness of the predictive hazard ratios. At the individual covariate level, only type-2 diabetes mellitus exhibited borderline evidence of non-proportionality, which did not materially impact overall model validation.

Incremental value

The addition of CyPA improved model discrimination, increasing the C-index from 0.632 to 0.770; furthermore, CyPA was associated with improved statistical measures of model performance within the derivation cohort. A C-index of 0.632 for the conventional model reflects only modest discrimination. The full model incorporating CyPA achieves 0.770, a range generally considered to represent good discriminative accuracy. The AUC increased from 0.663 to 0.805 further confirming this, with the DeLong test (p = 0.0001) and likelihood ratio test (chi-squared = 39.65, p < 0.001) ruling out chance as an explanation. A reduction in AIC of 37.65 confirms that the full model provides a substantially better fit to the data. While these findings support the potential prognostic value of CyPA, further validation is required before routine clinical application can be considered.

Both discrimination and calibration should be considered when evaluating prognostic models because accurate risk prediction requires not only separation of high- and low-risk patients but also agreement between predicted and observed outcomes [10].

These results are exploratory and hypothesis-generating. CyPA should be evaluated in larger, multicenter studies before being considered for general clinical use. The value of incorporating CyPA into established clinical risk stratification tools such as the GRACE score should be investigated in future studies to determine whether it provides incremental prognostic information beyond existing validated models [18].

Because the study population included both ACS and AHF patients, additional sensitivity and subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate whether diagnosis subtype affected the predictive value of CyPA. Diagnosis subtype was not independently related to MACEs after multivariable adjustment (HR 1.384; 95% CI 0.758–2.526; p = 0.290). Furthermore, improved CyPA sustained the relationship with increased risk in both ACS and AHF subgroups. These findings reinstated the robustness of CyPA as a predictive biomarker across different manifestations of acute myocardial injury.

Clinical implications

A single plasma CyPA measurement at hospital admission - during routine blood sampling - could stratify patients into high and low-risk groups, potentially guiding intensity of monitoring, pharmacological therapy, and follow-up scheduling. Whether CyPA-guided clinical management can improve outcomes remains unknown and warrants evaluation in prospective validation studies. Furthermore, the identification of CyPA as an independent predictor opens avenues for evaluating CyPA-directed therapeutic strategies, including CyPA inhibitors and anti-CD147 agents, which have shown promise in experimental cardiovascular models [6]. While previous studies, such as Ohtsuki et al., showed the prognostic value of CyPA in patients with stable coronary artery disease, the present study extends these findings by prospectively evaluating plasma CyPA levels in patients with acute myocardial injury and assessing its association with hard clinical outcomes (MACEs) over 06 months [2].

Limitations

This study has some limitations. It was a single-center study with a small sample size (n=217, 65 events), which may limit generalizability because some subgroup analyses may be underpowered and should be interpreted with caution. There is no independent cohort available during the study period. Future multicenter studies are required to validate these findings. The cohort study population included patients with both Acute Coronary Syndrome (77.4%) and Acute Heart Failure with myocardial injury (22.6%), indicating a broad clinical spectrum of acute myocardial injury. Although diagnostic subtype was not independently related to MACEs in the sensitivity analysis (HR 1.384; p=0.290), the disproportionate sample sizes between the AHF (n=49) and ACS (n=168) subgroups, particularly the AHF subgroup (n = 49), limited the statistical robustness of subgroup-specific AHF estimates. Larger studies retaining dedicated or well-characterized AHF cohorts will be well-positioned to highlight the definitive role of CyPA in each disease entity. Although adjustments were made for several variables, residual confounding by unmeasured factors (such as Killip class and detailed hemodynamic parameters) cannot be ruled out. Lastly, CyPA was measured only at admission; obtaining serial measurements would provide more data. The effects of individual medications on CyPA levels were not compared. Although model calibration demonstrated acceptable agreement between predicted and observed risks, both discrimination and calibration may be optimistic when assessed within a single derivation cohort. Therefore, external validation of the model and the proposed CyPA cut-off (1.651 ng/mL) is required before clinical implementation [10].

Conclusion

This prospective cohort study provides evidence that plasma Cyclophilin A (CyPA) is a promising, independent predictor of MACEs in patients with acute myocardial injury. Patients with admission CyPA levels above 1.651 ng/mL exhibited significantly worse six-month MACE-free survival, with an independently adjusted hazard ratio of 1.698 (95% CI: 1.462–1.973; p < 0.001) after multivariable adjustment for LVEF, BNP, cTnI, and other clinical covariates. Incorporation of plasma CyPA into the conventional risk model resulted in clinically meaningful improvements in predictive performance (ΔC-index = +0.138; ΔAUC = +0.142), both statistically significant by the DeLong test and likelihood ratio test.

The multivariable Cox model met the proportional hazards assumption (global Schoenfeld test, p = 0.2940), supporting the validity of the estimated hazard ratios. Overall, these outcomes support the association between high CyPA levels and amplified cardiovascular risk in acute myocardial injury. The predictive relevance of CyPA may highlight biological mechanisms underlying oxidative stress and inflammation that are not fully captured by established predictive biomarkers. Extended external validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is imperative for further mechanistic research and for prospective interventional and etiological studies, to facilitate better clinical translation into practice.
