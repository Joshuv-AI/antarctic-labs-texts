Battling with HIV infection in children in a resource-poor setting: an analysis of the coping strategies of the caregivers
Authors: Ikechukwu Frank Ogbonna, Kazeem Abimbola Uthman, Onyinye Hope Chime
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe devastating effects of HIV infection are more worrisome in resource-poor settings. In children, it runs a more chronic course, resulting in worsening morbidity that necessitates appropriate care. Caregivers, who may also be HIV-infected, are saddled with the responsibility of providing lifelong care and assistance to themselves, their children infected with HIV, and other members of the family. This is a battle that portends grave consequences, including a predisposition to psychological distress. However, not all caregivers present with psychological distress, signifying some levels of coping in them. This is this research’s thrust, which aims to determine how caregivers in resource-poor settings cope with HIV infection in their children. It also determined coping predictors among the caregivers.MethodsThis cross-sectional analytical research enrolled 158 participants according to the inclusion criteria. The caregivers’ sociodemographic variables were obtained, and the Brief-COPE was used to determine their coping strategies. Inferential statistics was applied where necessary, to draw logical conclusions.ResultsAvoidant coping was the most utilized strategy among the participants (Median = 2.63, IQR = 1.25). Although a significant relationship existed between avoidant coping and caregivers’ sex, HIV status, marital status, socioeconomic class, and presence of other HIV-infected family members; a multivariate linear regression showed that only marital status (β = 0.17; t = 2.16; P = 0.03) and socioeconomic class (β = 0.21; t = 2.54; P = 0.01) independently predicted the use of avoidant coping among the participants. This showed a positive relationship as divorced participants and those of the lower socioeconomic class were more likely to use avoidant coping than participants in other categories.ConclusionCaregivers of children infected with HIV largely deploy avoidant coping as a means to avert stressful life events. Being divorced and belonging to the lower socioeconomic class were the independent predictors of the use of avoidant coping.Keywords: HIV infection, Children, Resource-poor setting, Coping strategies

Background

A resource-poor setting is a locale with reduced capacity to provide quality healthcare due to a lack of necessary resources [1]. It is predominant in low-income countries such as Nigeria. In these locales, the negative impact of diseases is enormous, resulting in substantial morbidity and mortality [2]. One of such diseases is HIV infection, which despite its pandemic nature, has had its untoward effect domiciled in resource-poor settings, including those in Nigeria [3, 4]. In children, HIV infection has continued to pose a significant threat [5], resulting in physical, emotional, and psychological harm [6, 7]. This is due mainly to its physical effects, chronic medications, stigma, missed school days, and social disengagement [8]. Although giant strides in HIV treatment have been achieved, infection remission is still a far cry [5, 9], particularly in resource-poor settings. This culminates in a more chronic process in children and is burdensome [5, 9]. Unfortunately, the burden is more on the caregivers who may also be HIV-infected and require chronic medication use despite other daunting challenges from the infection. Also, the caregivers may struggle with the physical effects and other HIV complications, thereby compounding their burden.

Besides fending for themselves and other non-infected family members, the caregivers of Children Infected with HIV (CIWH) are also saddled with the responsibility of providing lifelong care and other forms of assistance to their CIWH. These are life stressors that may precipitate psychological distress in these caregivers [10]. This distress arises from mental health impairment and may negatively affect the level and quality of care, particularly for those of the CIWH [10, 11]. However, the ability of these life stressors (and psychological distress) to negatively influence the level of care and support of the CIWH is dependent on the coping strategies of the caregivers [12, 13].

Coping strategies are a set of cognitive and behavioral responses applied by individuals through thoughts, emotions, behaviors, and actions (or inactions) geared towards avoiding burnout from life stressors [14, 15]. They are categorized into problem-focused, emotion-focused, and avoidant coping [16], and their outcomes could be effective or ineffective [16, 17]. Effective coping promotes physical, social, and psychological well-being, whereas ineffective coping has the opposite effect [17]. Problem-focused and emotion-focused coping have effective outcomes and are referred to as adaptive coping strategies because they effectively ameliorate the negative effects of life stressors [16]. Greater use of problem-focused coping indicates a strong psychological and physical strength aimed at battling with and changing stressful situations [16, 18]. Also, greater use of emotion-focused coping indicates a strong quest to avert the emotional complications of stressful situations [16]. Conversely, avoidant coping has ineffective outcomes, is referred to as a maladaptive coping strategy, and indicates poor psychological strength [16, 19]. They are physical or cognitive attempts to disengage from the stressors, and thus, they have negative effects on individuals [16].

There is a relationship between stressful life events and coping as reported by Moreno et al. [20] in 2021 in Colombia. Also, there have been documented studies on the coping strategies of caregivers of CIWH in resource-poor settings in South Africa [17], Uganda [13], and Nigeria [7]. However, these studies [7, 13, 17] had some drawbacks, as they did not specify the type of coping deployed by the caregivers. Specifying the deployed coping strategy is necessary because it could be effective, signifying adaptive and helpful approaches in mitigating the stressors, or ineffective, signifying maladaptive and destructive approaches. Thus, these studies [7, 13, 17] did not state if the deployed strategies were effective or ineffective, to enable interventional action plans and policies geared towards redirecting the ineffective coping and encouraging the effective ones. Our study addressed these gaps by determining and analyzing the patterns of coping (problem-focused, emotion-focused, or avoidant) deployed by caregivers of CIWH in Umuahia, a resource-poor setting in Nigeria. We, in addition, determined the predictive factors that encouraged their use. Exploring and specifying caregivers’ coping strategies could help formulate interventional policies that may improve the health and well-being of caregivers of CIWH. This also informed the need for this study.

Methods

Research design and population

This was a cross-sectional and analytical research of primary caregivers of Children Infected with HIV (CIWH) aged 2–15 years who attend the Pediatric HIV Clinic (PHC) of the University of Nigeria Teaching Hospital (UNTH), Enugu, Nigeria. These caregivers were defined as individuals with the utmost responsibility for providing unpaid care and assistance to CIWH [21] and were referred to as “participants” in this study.

Only the consenting participants of the CIWH receiving care in the PHC for at least six months were enrolled. Those whose children’s HIV status was unknown and who had children with other chronic disorders (asthma, sickle cell anemia, epilepsy, or cerebral palsy) under their care were excluded.

Sample size determination

The sample size for this study was 158, obtained using the statistical formula for means estimation in a cross-sectional survey [22]. It was based on the standard normal variate of 1.96 at 5% error, a standard deviation of 7.3 obtained from a coping score of caregivers of CIWH [23], and a precision of the measurement of 1.2. Also, a drop-out rate of 10% was considered in the calculation.

The recruitment process

The participants underwent HIV counseling and testing according to the Nigerian algorithm to determine their HIV status. Their sociodemographic data was obtained using a proforma, and Socioeconomic Class (SEC) was determined using Oyedeji’s classification [24], which utilizes the average scores of the participants’ educational and occupational status. These scores were graded from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating lower SEC and vice versa. A score of 1 or 2 signified upper SEC, 3 represented middle, and 4 or 5 represented lower SEC.

The participants’ coping strategies were determined using the abridged version of the Coping Orientation to Problems Experienced (Brief-COPE) inventory developed by Carver [19]. It is a 28-item self-administered questionnaire that is configured to detect effective (adaptive) and ineffective (maladaptive) coping with life stressors [16, 19]. It is recommended for use in adult populations, has good psychometric properties [19, 25], and has been used by researchers to determine the coping strategies of individuals with stressful life events [26, 27]. The Brief-COPE assesses coping using three subscales: problem-focused, emotion-focused, and avoidant coping [16]. Problem-focused and avoidant coping strategies contain eight items each, while emotion-focused coping contains 12 items [16]. Each item on each of these subscales is rated on a four-point Likert scale with scores ranging from 1 to 4 [16, 25]. These scores are presented as average scores for each subscale [16]. To generate an average score, the cumulative score for each subscale is divided by the total number of items on the subscale [16]. A higher average score on any subscale indicates a higher use of the particular subscale [16, 26].

Pre-testing of the questionnaires

Before commencing the study and to detect any ambiguity, the questionnaires were pre-tested among some participants, who were told to omit confusing items (or questions) on the questionnaires. At the end of the exercise, none of the items (or questions) were omitted, and the selected participants answered the questions correctly, indicating that the terminologies were well understood. These participants were excluded from the main study.

Operational definitions

Primary caregivers

Individuals with the utmost responsibility of providing unpaid care and assistance to children infected with HIV [21].

Participants

Primary caregivers of children infected with HIV who attend the Pediatric HIV Clinic of the University of Nigeria Teaching Hospital, Enugu.

Coping strategies

A set of responses that are applied by the participants to avoid burnout from life stressors.

Brief-COPE

A psychometric tool consisting of three subscales, deployed in this study to assess the caregivers’ coping strategies. The highest mean score on any subscale indicates greater use of the particular subscale as a coping strategy [16, 26].

Data handling and analysis

Data was obtained using hard copies of the questionnaires and was handled with maximal caution. The obtained data was later converted to an electronic version and transferred into a Microsoft Excel sheet. To ensure confidentiality, questionnaires were de-identified using serial numbers rather than names, and the electronic version of the obtained data was preserved in a secured and password-protected personal computer.

The electronic data was exported, cleaned, coded, and analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21. Continuous variables, including the coping strategies scores, assumed a non-normal distribution pattern and were described using median and Interquartile Ranges (IQR). Frequencies and percentages were used to describe categorical variables. Mann-Whitney U test was used to determine the relationship between the coping strategies scores and independent variables that had two groups, while Kruskal-Wallis’ test was used for the same purpose if the independent variables had more than two groups. A multivariate linear regression analysis was done for significant outcome variables to predict the factors responsible for the deployed coping strategies. P < 0.05 was considered to be significant and was reported as two-sided. The findings were presented in prose, tables, and/or figures.

A multivariate linear regression analysis was done on all the significant sociodemographic characteristics to independently predict the sociodemographic characteristics with a significant relationship with the deployed coping strategies. The independent predictors of the use of avoidant coping were marital status and SEC (P < 0.05). A positive relationship existed between avoidant coping, marital status, and SEC, as divorced participants (β = 0.17; t = 2.16; P = 0.03) and those belonging to the lower SEC (β = 0.21; t = 2.54; P = 0.01) were more likely to use avoidant coping strategies than those in the other categories. Discussion

Our study revealed that the caregivers of the Children Infected with HIV (CIWH) adopted mostly avoidant coping as their strategy for handling life stressors. This finding was expected and likely due to HIV-induced stigma and discrimination. It explained the fact that the caregivers averted the social consequences of HIV by engaging in denial, self-distraction, behavioral disengagement, and substance abuse. These are features of maladaptive coping and imply that these caregivers coped ineffectively [16, 27, 28], which may negatively affect their overall quality of life [18, 27, 28]. By extension, it may affect the care and support of the CIWH, including the non-infected. This finding from our study was in keeping with those of Musangali et al. [29] in 2016 in Kenya, who noted the dominant use of maladaptive coping among the caregivers. However, our finding is at variance with those of Oyeyimika et al. [7] in 2020 in Lagos, Nigeria, and Atanuriba et al. [30] in 2023 in Ghana, who independently reported religion as the dominant coping style of the caregivers. The variation may arise from the differences in the deployed assessment tools (methodology). Our study deployed Brief-COPE, a structured, standardized, and reliable tool that assesses and categorizes coping (problem-focused, emotion-focused, and avoidant coping), and enables its stratification into adaptive and maladaptive coping. Thus, it helped to determine if the coping was efficient or inefficient. In this standardized method, religion is only seen as one of the items on the subscales and does not stand alone as an independent determinant of coping. In contrast, Oyeyimika et al. [7] and Atanuriba et al. [30] deployed an unstructured and non-standardized coping tool that ignores the categorization of coping. Each coping item from this tool was viewed as an independent determinant of coping. Thus, religion, which had the highest proportion among their responders, was adjudged to be the deployed coping mechanism. It is important to state that, aside from religion, there are other factors that determine coping in individuals. This is evident by the fact that religion is just an item among the 12 items on emotion-focused coping on the Brief-COPE [16], and may not individually provide a desired outcome if allowed to stand alone as a coping strategy. Thus, it may not independently imply a greater use of emotion-focused coping. In addition, and depending on the mode of use, religion has been observed to have both positive and negative coping outcomes [31, 32]. Though the negative outcome may result in maladaptive and ineffective coping [31, 32], the flexibility of religion as an independent coping assessment tool may result in conflicting findings. These observations may have been responsible for the variations in findings between our study and the aforementioned studies.

In our study, avoidant coping had a significant relationship with the caregivers’ sex, SEC, marital status, HIV status, and the presence of other HIV-infected family members. Contrary to expectations, only marital status and SEC independently predicted the use of avoidant coping by the caregivers, as a positive relationship existed between avoidant coping, caregivers’ marital status, and SEC. There was greater use of avoidant coping by divorced caregivers and those in the lower SEC. Divorce, particularly in resource-poor settings, results in a lack of previously available spouse support, which provides succor during stressful life situations [33]. In these settings, it is greeted with stigma as a social consequence [33, 34]. Additionally, the spouses may no longer be alternating care and support for their CIWH and other children [33]. Furthermore, belonging to a lower SEC is complicated by a lack of basic amenities and challenges in societal integration [35]. These are stressors that may aggravate previously existing stressful events in the caregivers and may be responsible for the increased use of avoidant coping by the divorced caregivers and those in the lower SEC.

Despite a reduced use of adaptive coping (problem-solving and emotion-focused), which are effective means of coping [16], our study showed that problem-focused coping had a significant relationship with the type of caregiver, CIWH’s age, CIWH’s sex, SEC, and place of residence. Similarly, our study showed that emotion-focused coping had a significant relationship with the caregivers’ age, CIWH’s age, CIWH’s sex, SEC, and marital status. Surprisingly, there were no independent predictors for problem-focused coping. However, the independent predictors for the use of emotion-focused coping among the caregivers were the CIWH’s sex and SEC. This showed that emotion-focused coping has a negative relationship with CIWH’s sex and SEC, as the caregivers with male CIWH under their care and those in the higher SEC used less emotion-focused coping. Males tend to deploy more problem-focused coping and less emotion-focused, and the reverse is the case for females, as reported by Smriti and Latha [36] in 2018 in Karnataka. Though no independent predictor of problem-focused coping was noted in our study, it is possible that this inverse relationship between males and females based on problem-focused and emotion-focused coping may have been responsible for the negative relationship between emotion-focused coping and the caregivers who had male CIWH under their care. Also, based on context, attributes, and outcomes, higher and lower SECs have inverse relationships. Thus, as documented by Caplan in 2007 [37], lower SEC may result in greater use of emotion-focused coping and a lesser use of problem-focused coping, implying that higher SEC may result in a lesser use of emotion-focused coping. This may have been responsible for the negative relationship between emotion-focused coping and higher SEC.

Contrary to expectations, we observed a dearth of literature regarding the objective assessment of the coping strategies of the caregivers of CIWH and the factors that encourage the deployment of any of the coping strategies. Most of the literature searched generalized coping based on individual item scores and did not categorize coping into adaptive (effective) or maladaptive (ineffective), to enable appropriate comparison with our study. Despite the dearth of literature, some studies also analyzed the factors that encourage coping in caregivers of CIWH. For instance, Mathew [38] in 2013 in India noted that avoidant coping was predominant among older caregivers, males, and breadwinners. This study individualized coping as individual item scores and ignored the categorization of the coping strategies. Thus, it is unclear if the coping strategies that had this significant relationship were the adaptive or the maladaptive ones. Also, their findings were merely descriptive, as they did not ascertain if their observations had statistical significance or had occurred by chance. Also, Mujjuzi et al. [13] in 2021 in Uganda documented a positive relationship between the caregivers’ coping strategies and marital status, and a negative relationship with occupation. This study, just like the aforementioned, did not categorize the coping strategies to enable appropriate comparison with our study. Despite the lack of categorization and not being specific to the coping subscales used in our study, these studies [13, 38] also showed that certain factors influence the decision to deploy a particular coping strategy. However, there is a need for further research to provide more insight into the subject matter. This may help to provide counselling tactics on the use of effective coping strategies by the caregivers of CIWH.

Conclusions

Caregivers of CIWH deploy adaptive coping as their strategies to avert the stressful life events arising from the HIV infection in their children, and the factors that determine the use of this strategy were their marital status and socioeconomic class.

Limitations of the study

Our study had some limitations, including the fact that it was hospital-based. It would have had a far-reaching effect if it had been conducted in the community where most of the children infected with HIV reside. Also, our study was cross-sectional in design rather than longitudinal. As a longitudinal study, it would have enabled the follow-up of these caregivers to determine if those with adaptive coping maintained their adaptive states or regressed to maladaptive, and vice versa. However, it is important to state that these limitations are less likely to negatively impart the outcome and beneficial effects of our study.
