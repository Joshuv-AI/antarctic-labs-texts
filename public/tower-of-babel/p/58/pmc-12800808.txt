Spectrum of DNA Variants in Southwestern Ontario Patients with Familial Hypercholesterolemia
Authors: Sanaz Lordfard, Jian Wang, Adam D. McIntyre, Brooke A. Kennedy, Robert A. Hegele
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HeFH) is the most prevalent inherited dyslipidemia, and it predisposes individuals to premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. Genetic testing can provide a definitive diagnosis. The spectrum of causal DNA variants in Ontario patients with hypercholesterolemia is not fully defined.MethodsIn Southwestern Ontario patients with a clinical diagnosis of HeFH, we performed targeted next-generation DNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis to determine the qualitative and quantitative spectrum of pathogenic and likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants.ResultsWe observed 101 unique P/LP variants in 254 patients, of which 6 were novel LDLR pathogenic variants. We observed 23 variants of uncertain significance among 30 patients. Phenotypic severity followed a descending biochemical gradient for the LDLR P/LP variant, the APOB variant, and the variants of uncertain significance subgroups. The 3 most commonly observed P/LP variants were APOB p.R3527Q, LDLR 15.8 kb French Canadian deletion, and LDLR p.C681X Middle Eastern variant, seen in 15.4%, 7.1%, and 3.1% of patients, respectively. About three-quarters of variants originated in Europe, with others from Asia, Africa, and the Middle East.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive overview of the clinical and genetic spectrum of HeFH in Southwestern Ontario. The P/LP variant diversity reflects historical colonization and later migration patterns both from across the world and interprovincially from Quebec.

Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is an autosomal semi-dominant disorder characterized by markedly elevated plasma levels of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol and premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD).1 An estimated 1 in 250-300 people globally, and in Canada, are affected with heterozygous FH (HeFH), making it among the most prevalent of genetic disorders.1 Inherited pathogenic DNA variants, primarily in the LDLR gene encoding the LDL receptor, are found in 85%-90% of HeFH patients. In 10%-15% of patients, pathogenic DNA variants are found in secondary genes, including APOB encoding apolipoprotein (apo) B, the ligand by which the LDL particle binds to the receptor.1 Causal DNA variants in FH patients undermine clearance of LDL particles from the blood, causing lifelong elevated untreated LDL cholesterol > 5.0 mmol/L.1 Left untreated, HeFH patients face a 20-fold increased risk of coronary artery disease by age 50 years,1 and homozygous patients may develop cardiovascular events in childhood or adolescence.2 HeFH is clinically diagnosed using a combination of LDL cholesterol levels, patient and family history, and physical findings; genetic testing can confirm a definite diagnosis and identify causal variants.3

The genetic landscape of FH exhibits substantial heterogeneity, with > 3000 documented pathogenic variants in LDLR archived in databases such as ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/).4 More than 20 years ago, in 60 Ontario HeFH patients, we reported 25 distinct LDLR pathogenic variants, including exon deletions, missense mutations, and splice-site alterations.5 This diversity of variants was considered to reflect Ontario's ethnic heterogeneity and growing immigrant population, possibly contrasting with other regions, such as Quebec, where at the time, a limited number of founder variants dominated the FH landscape.6 Here, we report an update of the spectrum of pathogenic DNA variants in Ontario HeFH patients that emphasizes the province’s ethnic diversity.

Methods

Study subjects

Following our established procedures, data were collected from charts of patients at the Lipid Genetics Clinic, London, Ontario,7 which is a referral centre for ∼2.7 million people living in Southwestern Ontario. Inclusion criteria were a Dutch Lipid Clinic Network score > 5.1 The project was approved by the Research Ethics Board of Western University (protocol number 0379), and all participants provided informed consent.

Biochemical, clinical, and demographic information

Baseline plasma lipid profiles were determined after 8-12 hours of fasting at the Lipid Genetics Clinic.7, 8, 9 Clinical and demographic data were collected at the initial visit. Historical untreated fasting plasma lipid profiles were collected from referral notes or by imputation.10

DNA preparation and sequencing

Genomic DNA isolation from whole blood and gene-sequencing protocols have been described previously.7, 8, 9 Sequencing was performed at the London Regional Genomics Centre on a MiSeq personal sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, CA).

Genetic analysis

Our standard bioinformatic processing and annotation pipeline was used to call variants. Paired FASTQ files were generated for each sample after sequencing and imported into CLC Bio Genomics Workbench (CLC Bio, Aarhus, Denmark) for bioinformatic processing. Sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19/GRCh37) and underwent local realignment to improve alignment quality. From the assembled reads, variants were called if discrepancies between the reference genome and the sample’s sequence were present. Following this process, variant call format (VCF) and Binary Alignment Map (BAM) files were created for each sample; these files contain information on the genomic position and zygosity of identified variants, as well as the depth of coverage for each sequencing read.9 Variant annotation and copy number variation analysis were performed as described using VarSeq (Golden Helix, Bozeman, MT).9 Pathogenicity of variants was determined according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics pathogenicity classification; factors such as population frequency, published functional data, co-segregation with phenotype, and in silico modelling are used by the college to ascribe pathogenicity to variants.11 Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen) criteria for classifying variants were applied as described.4

Statistical analysis

The χ2 test was used to compare categorical data between genotypic classes. Unpaired student t-tests were used to compare continuous variables between genotypic classes. All tests were 2-sided, with nominal P-values < 0.05 deemed to be statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed on Prism 10 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA).

The second most common pathogenic variant is the well known 15.8 kb deletion involving the 5’-untranslated region of the LDLR gene.6,14 This variant was seen in 18 patients (7.1% of the cohort), and its high prevalence likely reflects 18th- and 19th-century immigration patterns from Quebec to Ontario. Regions with a relatively high density of Franco-Ontarians include several communities near the Windsor-Detroit border.

The third most common P/LP variant is the LDLR p.C681X or so-called “Lebanese variant,” which originated in the Middle East.15 This variant was seen in 8 patients (3.1% of the cohort), and its prevalence likely reflects more-recent 20th- and 21st-century migration to Ontario.

Four of the remaining 8 commonest LDLR pathogenic variants observed in a total of 22 patients (8.7% of the cohort) come exclusively from Europe or the United Kingdom, whereas the other 4 recurring LDLR pathogenic variants observed in a total of 20 patients (7.9% of the cohort) have geographic origins reported in both Asia and Europe.4

LDLR variants of uncertain significance

A total of 23 LDLR variants (18 singletons) observed in 30 patients were classified as VUSs; these are listed in Supplemental Table S2.

Between-group comparisons of phenotypes

Table 1 shows clinical and biochemical features according to genetic subgroups as follows: individuals with LDLR P/LP variants; those with the APOB p.R3527Q variant; and those with LDLR VUSs. Between-genotype comparisons are shown. Groups were similar with respect to mean age, percent female, and body mass index, as well as plasma concentrations of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, triglycerides, and lipoprotein (a). However, LDLR P/LP variant carriers had significantly higher total, LDL, and non-HDL cholesterol than did APOB P/LP carriers, who in turn had higher levels of these variables, compared to VUS carriers. Apo B levels were significantly higher in LDLR P/LP carriers, compared to both APOB and VUS carriers, whereas apo B levels did not differ significantly between APOB and VUS carriers.

A notable finding is that, in the VUS group, mean untreated plasma levels of total, LDL, and non-HDL cholesterol were high, relative to the population means (eg, LDL cholesterol of 5.53 ± 1.79 mmol/L). However, the fact that these levels were significantly lower than those for individuals with both P/LP variants in LDLR and the APOB p.R3527Q variant suggests that some portion of the VUS is actually benign and thus does not affect levels of LDL and non-HDL cholesterol or apo B.

Discussion

The principal findings of this study of FH patients from Southwestern Ontario are as follows: (i) a notable diversity of P/LP variants, with 101 different variants in 254 individuals, including 6 novel LDLR variants first seen in this cohort; (ii) half of LDLR P/LP variants were missense variants, and 43% of variants were seen in ≥ 2 patients; (iii) relatively few VUSs were present—23 unique variants among 30 patients—that were associated with less-severe biochemical phenotypes than LDLR and APOB P/LP variants; (iv) the 3 most commonly observed P/LP variants were APOB p.R3527Q, the LDLR 15.8-kb French Canadian deletion, and the LDLR p.C681X Middle Eastern variant, seen in 15.4%, 7.1%, and 3.1% of patients, respectively; (v) the geographic origins were mainly European (∼75%), but with a significant minority from Asia, Africa, and the Middle East.

The concentration of missense variants within exon 4 of LDLR (Fig. 1) aligns with observations in international databases, reflecting the functional importance of this region in ligand binding.4 The predominance of singleton P/LP variants alongside recurrent variants underscores the complexity of FH genetics and the importance of local founder effects and population-specific variation. The presence of novel variants also highlights the continued need for functional characterization and robust clinical annotation to better understand their pathogenicity.

Among recurrent variants, the high prevalence of APOB p.R3527Q and the LDLR 15.8-kb deletion is particularly striking. The findings suggest founder effects driven by historical European and Quebecois patterns of immigration to Southwestern Ontario. The frequency of the APOB p.R3527Q variant, which accounts for 15.4% of FH cases in this cohort, is among the highest ever reported worldwide.13 This frequency is likely due to repeated introduction from Northern European settlers, emphasizing the role of regional ancestry in FH variant distribution. Similarly, the high prevalence of the large LDLR 15.8-kb deletion variant appears consistent with a French-Canadian founder effect crossing provincial boundaries over centuries of internal migration.

The identification of the LDLR p.C681X originating in the Middle East (also known as the “Lebanese variant”) in 3.1% of subjects provides further evidence of more recent migratory influences on the genetic makeup of the region’s FH population. The fact that this variant appears in multiple unrelated individuals suggests either a founder effect or multiple introductions from the Middle East during recent immigration waves. This finding underlines the importance of considering current demographic shifts when interpreting FH variant patterns and constructing population screening strategies.16 Several other recurrent variants with European, Asian, and mixed origins further support the heterogeneous ancestry of Southwestern Ontario’s population,16 which appears to be reflected in the observed FH genotype diversity.

The stepwise gradient in biochemical severity from LDLR P/LP to APOB p.R3527Q to LDLR VUS subgroups supports the longstanding clinical observation that LDLR mutations generally result in 10%-15% more severe hypercholesterolemia than do APOB mutations.13 Furthermore, VUS carriers likely comprise a heterogeneous group with a mixture of truly pathogenic and some benign or partially penetrant alleles.4 Characterization of LDLR VUSs remains challenging. Some VUSs may be reclassified eventually as P/LP, whereas others may represent rare benign polymorphisms. Systematic efforts, including co-segregation studies in affected families and expanded in vitro assays, might clarify the clinical impact of these VUSs.

Documenting Ontario's FH variant profile carries clinical and public health implications. Ontario is among the world’s most diverse jurisdictions, with 30% of its residents being born outside Canada (although ultimately, all residents except for Indigenous People can theoretically trace their ancestry outside Canada).17 In addition, the ancestors of some Ontarians immigrated internally from Quebec. Both Quebec and Ontario bear traces of their shared history as the Province of Canada (1841-1867). Insight into mutation distribution across ancestral groups could guide tailored genetic screening strategies. Precise molecular characterization may improve diagnostic accuracy in borderline lipid cases and inform cascade screening, as endorsed by Ontario Health. In addition, drug eligibility for PCSK9 inhibitors via third-party payers is facilitated by a definite diagnosis of FH, which is provided by genetic testing. Characterization of HeFH in Ontario’s multiethnic population ultimately enhances early intervention, reduces cardiovascular burden, and may help mitigate healthcare disparities in FH management.

Conclusion

Our study provides a regional snapshot of the HeFH genetic landscape, emphasizing both the diversity and historical underpinnings of disease-associated variants in Southwestern Ontario. The discovery of novel variants and the unresolved status of several VUSs support the continued need for comprehensive sequencing and variant curation. Future work should include functional validation of novel and uncertain variants, evaluation of polygenic contributions to lipid phenotypes,18 and assessment of genotype-specific responses to lipid-lowering therapies. In addition, the findings could allow for cross-national or cross-continental comparisons of carriers of the identical variant as a means to study the potential impact of cultural, environmental, and geographic factors of countries of origin on the phenotype. Collectively, these efforts will help refine HeFH diagnosis, enable personalized treatment strategies, and ultimately reduce the burden of premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.
