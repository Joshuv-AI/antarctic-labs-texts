Embryo quality control via lineage-specific aneuploid cell elimination in embryos and stem cell-derived embryo models
Authors: Lisa K. Iwamoto-Stohl, Laura Amaya, Pallavi Panda, Yuqi Wang, Ninadini Sharma, Catherine King, Magdalena Zernicka-Goetz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aneuploidy is frequent in human pre-implantation embryos and a leading cause of early pregnancy loss, yet is rarely observed at birth, implying robust embryonic surveillance. Here we define how this surveillance operates after implantation using an integrated, three-lineage stem cell–derived embryo model that recapitulates the epiblast (EPI), visceral endoderm (VE), and extraembryonic ectoderm (ExE). Seeding of aneuploid cells into each lineage independently reveals lineage-specific fates: aneuploid cells are selectively depleted from EPI and VE but persist within ExE. Live imaging captures their removal by apoptosis or physical extrusion. Single-cell RNA sequencing shows p53 activation and Myc repression in aneuploid cells, and pathway perturbations modulate their clearance, confirming causality. Together, these findings demonstrate a post-implantation, lineage-restricted quality-control program that eliminates aneuploid cells from the embryo proper while permitting extraembryonic tolerance. They also establish integrated embryo models as a tractable platform to dissect the molecular logic of developmental quality control.

Introduction

Up to 70% of human pregnancies fail before birth, with most losses occurring during pre-gastrulation development1,2. A major contributing factor is the extraordinarily high incidence of aneuploidy in human embryos: studies from in vitro fertilization (IVF) and pre-implantation genetic diagnosis (PGD) report rates ranging from 20% to over 90%, with more than half of embryos being mosaics composed of both diploid and aneuploid cells3,4. These chromosomal abnormalities are a major cause of developmental failure2,5–7. Yet, aneuploidy is rare in live births (around 0.6%)8, suggesting that embryos have mechanisms to manage abnormal cells4. For example, a study reported a 20% live birth rate in a cohort where only 9% of sampled embryos were fully diploid, indicating that the embryo may have a mechanism to cope with aneuploidy4. Aneuploid cell clearance mechanisms have also been reported in the pre- and peri-implantation human embryo and in stem-cell micropatterns9. These mechanisms might include: (1) selective elimination of aneuploid cells10–13, (2) their preferential segregation to extraembryonic lineages, such as the trophectoderm (TE)14, and (3) self-correction within affected cells15. However, the relative contribution of each of these mechanisms and the timing of aneuploid cell elimination in relation to key morphogenetic events remain poorly understood.

Due to ethical and technical constraints in studying human embryos, mouse models are commonly used to understand mechanisms behind human embryogenesis16–21. Mouse embryos are reported to have much lower aneuploidy rates (1–4%)22. Therefore, to induce aneuploidy, we previously treated embryos with reversine, a reversible inhibitor of the spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC) kinase MPS123,24, and created chimeric embryos composed of diploid (normal) and aneuploid (abnormal) cells side by side. These studies allowed us to show that aneuploid cells are selectively eliminated from the EPI in a p53-dependent process involving both autophagy and apoptosis25. Aneuploid cells also exhibit reduced contribution to the VE but, remarkably, persist in the TE and can be found in the ExE derivatives, specifically the placenta of mosaic aneuploid chimearas23,25. While these studies defined the mechanisms of aneuploid cell elimination during pre-implantation development, what happens after implantation, when the embryo undergoes rapid morphogenesis, lineage segregation, and gastrulation, remains largely unknown. Understanding this stage is crucial, as these processes establish the body plan and determine the developmental potential of each lineage, providing a new context in which aneuploid cells may still be selectively eliminated.

Recently, stem-cell-derived embryo models have emerged as powerful in vitro systems that recapitulate key stages of early human and mouse development, providing complementary platforms to investigate processes inaccessible in vivo26–28. To investigate lineage-specific mechanisms of aneuploidy surveillance at post-implantation stages, we have developed an integrated post-implantation mouse embryoid model (ETiX) comprising embryonic stem cells (ESCs), iGata4-ESCs (with doxycycline-inducible Gata4 expression), and trophoblast stem cells (TSCs), which self-organize into EPI-, VE-, and ExE-like tissues, respectively25,26. These embryoids recapitulate key post-implantation events, including pro-amniotic cavity formation, anterior–posterior patterning, and gastrulation leading to early organogenesis26–29. By introducing labeled aneuploid cells into each lineage independently, we uncovered a post-implantation quality-control system that safeguards the embryo proper while permitting extraembryonic tolerance. Distinct p53- and Myc-dependent responses underlie this surveillance, revealing lineage-specific thresholds of cellular fitness that shape early development.

Results

Aneuploidy impairs ESC survival in chimeric post-implantation mouse embryos

To understand the lineage-specific consequences of aneuploidy after the embryo implants, we first induced aneuploidy in stem cells representing all three early lineages: ESCs representing EPI29, iGata4-ESCs representing VE30,31, and TSCs representing ExE32. To this end, cells were synchronized in G2/M by RO-3306 treatment and then transiently treated with the Mps1 inhibitor reversine or vehicle (DMSO) (Fig. S1A–D). Aneuploidy was induced by Reversine treatment across all lines examined, achieving a ~75–80% aneuploid rate by metaphase spread analysis (Fig. S1D). For simplicity, we refer to reversine-treated and DMSO-treated cells as ‘aneuploid’ and ‘diploid’, respectively, throughout the remainder of this manuscript. To assess the developmental potential of these three stem-cell types, we aggregated aneuploid or diploid GFP-labeled stem cells with 8-cell-stage mouse embryos, cultured them in vitro, and monitored their contribution to chimeras at the blastocyst and post-implantation stages. Embryos were assessed for the presence or absence of GFP-positive cells, and post-implantation development was assessed based on: the presence of an Oct4-positive EPI, the presence of a lumen in the EPI, and the presence of Gata4- positive VE around the EPI or a Cdx2-positive domain adjacent to the EPI.

While ESCs contributed to pre-implantation blastocysts regardless of ploidy, aneuploid ESCs showed significantly reduced contribution to the EPI at the blastocyst stage compared to controls, and chimeras with aneuploid ESCs were less likely to successfully develop to the post-implantation stages in vitro (Fig. S2A–D). iGata4-ESCs contributed to blastocysts at similar frequency and proportions regardless of ploidy (Fig. S2E–G), but the post-implantation chimeras developed poorly (Fig. S2F, H). Aneuploid TSCs modestly reduced the frequency of GFP+ blastocysts, but the proportion of GFP+ cells per embryo was similar (Fig. S2I–K). However, at post-implantation stages, GFP+ TSCs were rarely detected, and their presence correlated with abnormal development, regardless of ploidy status (Fig. S2J–L)33.

These results indicate that while chimaera formation potential appears to be unaffected by ploidy status for VE-like iGata4-ESCs and TSCs, induced aneuploidy compromises the persistence and survival of ESCs specifically in chimeric embryos. Embryos containing aneuploid ESCs also display limited post-implantation potential. Decreased efficiency of in vitro development was seen for iGata4-ESCs and TSCs regardless of ploidy status, limiting the ability to assess differential behavior of aneuploid and diploid cells. This variability in chimaera formation prompted us to find more stable ways to study the long-term, lineage-specific impacts of aneuploidy.

First, we explored the effects of aneuploidy in the ESC compartment of ETiX embryoids. We pre-treated differentially labeled ESCs (membrane-GFP “green” or membrane-tdTomato “red”) with reversine to induce aneuploidy or DMSO for diploid controls. ESCs were mixed at 1:1 to create diploid-diploid (DD), diploid-aneuploid (DA or AD), and aneuploid-aneuploid (AA) conditions and aggregated with iGata4-ESCs and TSCs to form embryoids (Fig. 1A). We quantified the proportion of egg-cylinder-like structures, with a single EPI domain, a central pro-amniotic cavity, and a VE overlying the EPI and ExE compartments. In each condition, DA and AA ETiX showed reduced formation of organized embryoids compared to DD controls (Fig. 1B, C). We then quantified the fluorescence composition of the EPI-like compartment in the embryoids (all red, all green, mixed, and not fluorescent) (Fig. 1D, E). In DD conditions, 24% of structures displayed mixed green and red EPI, 30% contained only red cells, 20% were all green, and 26% lacked any fluorescence. In DA or AD conditions, embryoids with a fully or partially aneuploid EPI were rare (i.e., fully green EPIs for DA, fully red EPIs for AD, or mixed EPIs in either condition), while the proportions of embryoids with diploid EPIs (i.e., red for DA, green for AD) increased. AA ETiXs displayed an increase in ‘no fluorescence’ structures compared to DD (Fig. 1E, F). These findings confirm selective pressure against aneuploid ESCs in the EPI.

Next, we generated VE-mosaic ETiXs using non-fluorescent or mCherry-labeled iGata4-ESCs (diploid or aneuploid) and assessed the efficiency of development and VE contribution in ETiXs (Fig. 1G–L). Similar to our observation in EPI-mosaic ETiX, DA and AD models showed reduced developmental efficiency and contribution of aneuploid cells, while AA models frequently lacked a VE layer altogether. These trends were consistent with selective exclusion in the VE-like compartment of the ETiX embryoid, too.

In contrast, the presence of aneuploid cells in the TSC-derived ExE lineage had minimal impact. ETiX embryoids with mosaic ExE compartments (diploid vs. aneuploid TSCs, labeled with YFP or tdTomato) formed organized structures at similar frequencies in DD, DA, and AD conditions, with no significant differences in lineage contribution (Fig. 1M–R). Only when all TSCs were aneuploid (AA ETiXs) was efficiency reduced, indicating that TSC-derived lineages are relatively insensitive to aneuploid mosaicism during early ETiX embryoid morphogenesis.

Overall, AA ETiXs showed reduced developmental efficiency for all lineages, but whilst DA or AD ETiXs with a mosaic EPI or VE also showed a reduction in developmental efficiency and aneuploid cell contribution. Mosaicism of the ExE did not significantly impact developmental efficiency, indicating the differential impact of mosaicism in each of these lineages in ETiX embryoids. To further validate these findings, we dissociated all pooled ETiX embryoids from an aggrewell at day 4 and analyzed lineage-specific contributions by flow cytometry (Fig. S3). Diploid cells were consistently enriched over aneuploid cells in mosaic (DA or AD) epiblast and VE compartments (Fig. S3A–D), although this enrichment did not reach statistical significance for the VE. No differences were observed in mosaic ExE compartments (Fig. S3E, F), supporting a relative tolerance to aneuploidy in this lineage.

We also evaluated the diploid-aneuploid cell composition within individual ETiX on day 4. Organized egg-cylinder-like ETiXs were selected, and the ratio of differentially labeled cells in the lineage of interest was determined with normalization to the DD condition. This analysis revealed that diploid cells contributed a higher proportion of ESC-derived cells to the EPI (Fig. 2A, B) and of iGata4-ESC-derived cells to the VE compared to aneuploid cells (Fig. 2C, D) in DA and AD ETiX embryoids. In contrast, diploid and aneuploid cells had similar contributions in the ExE for DA and AD ETiX embryoids (Fig. 2E, F). To examine how diploid and aneuploid cells are spatially organized within mosaic ETiX embryoids, we developed a connectivity score, defined as the total number of cells divided by the number of cell clusters. This metric reflects the extent of clustering versus intermixing (Fig. 2G). We hypothesized that if communication between aneuploid and diploid cells promotes spatial segregation34,35 in this system, distinct aneuploid and diploid cell clusters would form, leading to a higher connectivity score in DA and AD ETiXs but not in DD or AA (Fig. 2G). Indeed, we observed higher connectivity scores in the EPI (Fig. 2H) and VE (Fig. 2I) of DA and AD ETiXs than in DD and AA ETiXs. However, for the ExE, there was no significant difference in connectivity scores between DA, AD, DD, and AA ETiXs (Fig. 2J). Together, these results reveal a lineage-dependent response to aneuploidy during post-implantation development of the ETiX embryoid: the EPI exhibits the strongest selective pressure, the VE displays intermediate sensitivity, and the ExE is comparatively permissive.

Dynamics of cell death in response to aneuploid mosaicism

To understand how differences in cell survival and proliferation contribute to the reduced contribution of aneuploid cells, we performed immunostaining for cleaved caspase-3 (Casp3, a marker of apoptosis) and phospho-histone H3 (pHH3, a marker of proliferation) in individual day 4 ETiX embryoids. Casp3+ and pHH3+ cells were rare across all three lineages and did not show significant differences between groups (Fig. S4). We hypothesized that this may be due to the aneuploid cells being eliminated prior to day 4, leading to (1) biased detection of cell death in the remaining diploid cells which may reflect normal levels of cell death at this stage and (2) selection of aneuploid cells which may harbor less deleterious aneuploidies or have failed to undergo chromosome mis-segregation with reversine treatment, which may then be less likely to undergo cell death or reduced proliferation.

Therefore, in order to better understand the dynamics of reduced aneuploid cell contribution to ETiX, we performed a time course of DD and DA ETiX embryoids across the three lineages. We assessed the presence of all three lineages at day 2, the formation of distinct EPI and ExE compartments with overlying VE at day 3, and organization into an egg-cylinder-like structure at day 426 (Fig. S5A–F). DD and DA ETiX embryoids showed similar organization efficiency at days 2 and 3 (Fig. S5B, F, G). By day 4, DA ETiX with a mosaic EPI showed significantly reduced organization efficiency compared to DD controls (Fig. S5B). A similar trend was observed for ETiX with a mosaic VE, although this did not reach statistical significance (Fig. S5D). ETiX with DD and DA ExE compartments did not show significant differences in organization efficiency from day 2 to 4 (Fig. S5F).

We also classified pooled ETiXs based on fluorescent labels in each lineage. For the EPI, we observed an increasing proportion of ‘no fluorescence’ ETiXs from day 2 to 4 for DA but not DD, being significantly higher for DA than DD by day 4 (Fig. S5G, H). We also observed that the proportion of structures with ‘green’ aneuploid EPIs was similar between DD and DA ETiXs until day 4, when these declined in DA ETiX (Fig. S5G, H), reflecting a decrease in ETiXs with a homogeneous aneuploid EPI. For the VE, we observed the proportion of ETiXs classified as ‘no VE’ was higher for DA than DD throughout, but there was a significant increase in the proportion of homogenous ‘red’ diploid VE among DA ETiXs compared to DD at days 3 and 4 (Fig. S5I, J). In parallel, the proportion of ‘non-red’ aneuploid ETiXs was significantly lower for DA than DD (Fig. S5I, J). Finally, for the ExE, we observed an increasing fraction of ‘no ExE’ ETiXs over time for DA compared to DD (Fig. S5K, L). However, DA ETiXs also exhibited an increased frequency of ‘green’ aneuploid ExE (Fig. S5K, L). Finally, we also picked structures on days 2 and 3 and quantified the diploid-aneuploid cell composition within individual ETiX. Structures were picked based on having formed a consolidated mass of cells (day 2) and having an inner and outer layer (day 3). This analysis revealed that there was no significant shift in diploid cell contribution in DA structures relative to DD, as we had observed for day 4 structures in the EPI and VE (Fig. S5M–O). Overall, our results showed that aneuploid contribution decreased mainly between days 3 and 4.

Thus, we performed live imaging of ETiX embryoids over this period to track differentially fluorescently labeled cells in individual ETiX. We used Sytox to stain dying cells25,36. ETiXs with AA EPI exhibited the highest number of Sytox+ EPI cells over the 24 h, followed by ETiXs with DA and AD EPI, and ETiXs with DD EPI having little to no dying cells (Fig. 3A–C), suggesting that aneuploid cells in the EPI were selectively eliminated. Time-lapse imaging revealed Sytox+ cells in the pro-amniotic cavity (Fig. 3D) and extrusion of a green aneuploid EPI cell from surrounding red diploid EPI cells (Fig. 3E), suggesting clearance of aneuploid cells from the EPI in mosaic DA ETiX. In contrast, ETiX with mosaic VE did not show a significant difference in the number or fluorescent labeling of dying Sytox+ cells across the genotypes, and overall cell death remained low (Fig. 3F–H). In some DA VE-ETiX embryoids, aneuploid VE cells formed protrusions and were extruded from the ETiX structure (Fig. 3I, J), suggesting that the VE may not show increased aneuploid cell death and instead may utilize alternative mechanisms for cell elimination. Finally, for the ExE, total Sytox+ cell numbers were similar across DD, DA, AD, and AA ETiX (Fig. 3K, L). Overall, we infer that aneuploid cell elimination occurs primarily in the EPI between days 3 and 4, with some examples of cells being extruded into the developing pre-amniotic cavity. Whilst the EPI exhibited the most robust and rapid elimination mechanisms, the ExE remained more tolerant of aneuploid cells. The VE did not show trends of increased aneuploid cell death, but extrusion of aneuploid cells was observed, which may lead to their reduced contribution to ETiXs.

While scRNA-seq detected some fluorescent reporter transcripts, not all could be reliably recovered at the single-cell level, so the red and green labels were only partly captured. Nevertheless, the overall trends were consistent with our imaging results. Green (aneuploid) cells were significantly reduced in mosaic DA EPI (Fig. S6E, top), in agreement with our microscopy analysis (Fig. 1E). A similar but weaker reduction of aneuploid cells (non-red) was detected in the VE proportion of iGata4-ESC-mosaic DA ETiXs (Fig. S6E, middle). Interestingly, we detected an increase of green (aneuploid) cells in mosaic DA ExE (Fig. S6E, bottom). The quantification of fluorescent reporter expression also demonstrated that most cells contributed to their expected lineages: ESC-derived cells predominantly contributed to the EPI, iGata4+ cells to the VE, and TSCs exclusively to the ExE (Fig. S6E).

To assess chromosomal status, we applied inferCNV37 to predict copy number variations (CNVs) across single cells. Cells with >0.9 altered chromosomal fractions were classified as aneuploid. Aneuploidy frequencies differed sharply by lineage: 54.7% of ExE, 19.2% of VE, and only 3.5% of EPI cells were aneuploid (Fig. 4D–F), revealing strong selection against aneuploidy in the embryonic compartment of ETiX embryoids. Among aneuploid EPI cells, 11% expressed markers consistent with ExE identity, and 20% of aneuploid VE cells clustered with the ExE lineage, indicating possible cell state transitions of aneuploid cells into the ExE lineage. In contrast, the diploid population remained well distributed across lineages. Notably, all of the ExE cells maintained their ExE identity, regardless of ploidy status (Fig. 4D–F). Extending this analysis to DD and AA conditions confirmed the lineage-specific hierarchy of aneuploidy tolerance. In DD controls, predicted aneuploidy rates were low in the EPI (~6%) and ExE (~19%), with a moderate baseline in the VE (~26%) (Fig. S7A). In AA ETiX structures, aneuploid cells persisted at higher rates in the EPI (~41%) (Fig. S7B), far exceeding the ~3.5% observed in DA structures. While the reduction from ~75% post-reversion to ~41% in AA indicates that some cell-autonomous elimination occurs, the near-complete clearance in DA suggests that cell competition with diploid neighbors substantially enhances aneuploid cell elimination from the epiblast38,39.

Mapping CNV profiles revealed both shared and distinct alterations across lineages (Fig. S7C). Recurrent duplications included chromosomes 8, 11, and 12. In the EPI, duplications of chromosomes 11 and 8 predominated (Fig. S7D), whereas genomic DNA from reversine-treated ESCs prior to ETiX formation showed early duplications of chromosomes 6, 8, 13, and 19, and losses of 1, 12, 13, and 18 (Fig. S7D). The persistence of chromosome 8 trisomy in the EPI might suggest that it confers a growth advantage, as was reported in mouse ESCs40. The VE displayed duplications of chromosomes 11, 8, 19, 12, 16, 13, 18, and 10, while ExE exhibited a broader spectrum of gains (chr 11, 18, 2, 1, 12, 3) and losses (chr 15, 16, 18) (Fig. S7D). Together, these data reveal that the EPI enforces strict genome surveillance, while extraembryonic lineages tolerate more extensive chromosomal imbalance.

To contextualize these findings, we compared ETiX transcriptomes with in vivo post-implantation (E5.5) mouse embryos. Pseudotime analysis revealed that, within the EPI, aneuploid cells in the DA condition were more developmentally advanced than their diploid counterparts. In the VE lineage, pseudotime distributions were comparable between mosaic types, suggesting similar differentiation states. In the ExE lineage, aneuploid cells also appeared more developmentally advanced than diploid cells (Fig. 4G). In agreement with this, diploid cells in the DA EPI expressed higher levels of naïve pluripotency markers such as Pou5f1 (Oct4), Nanog, and Fgf5, consistent with maintenance of an earlier, less differentiated state, whilst aneuploid EPI cells displayed elevated levels of Sox241 and Otx242–44, transcription factors that mark the transition from naïve to primed pluripotency and promote lineage commitment. Sox2, while essential for maintaining pluripotency, also cooperates with Otx2 during the exit from the naïve state to initiate epiblast maturation41,42,44. In the VE, diploid cells expressed higher levels of early VE specification markers Sox1745 and Amn, which are essential for establishing endodermal identity and epithelial organization, respectively. In contrast, aneuploid cells upregulated Gata4, Gata6, and Hnf4a, transcription factors that act downstream of Hnf1b and are key regulators of terminal VE differentiation and function46. HNF4A, in particular, controls genes involved in epithelial polarity and cell–cell adhesion47. Similarly, in the ExE, aneuploid cells consistently showed increased expression of lineage-specific transcription factors48 such as Cdx2, Elf5, Tfap2c, Ascl2, Hand1, Esx1, and Eomes compared to diploid cells (Fig. 4H, Source Data Table 3). These data indicate that aneuploid cells progress comparably, or in some cases more rapidly than diploid cells, arguing against developmental arrest as a major factor in aneuploid cell persistence. Notably, “unknown” cells that did not cluster into defined lineages were enriched in the AA condition and over twofold in AA ESC-derived ETiX, suggesting significant developmental perturbations (Fig. S8A).

To explore the molecular basis of this accelerated differentiation and differential selection, we examined apoptosis and proliferation pathways, focusing on p53 signaling. At the global level, components of the p53 pathway demonstrated lineage- and mosaic-dependent expression patterns. The negative regulators Mdm249(which promotes p53 degradation) and Cdkn1a (p21)50 (a p53 transcriptional target that enforces cell-cycle arrest) showed the highest expression in iGata4-AA ETiX, whereas Casp3 expression was highest in TSC-DD ETiX. Interestingly, Trp53 itself was lowest in iGata4-AA and ESC-AA ETiX, suggesting lineage-specific mechanisms of p53 regulation or feedback inhibition (Fig. S8B). When looking specifically within each lineage, EPI cells from ESC-AA ETiX had the highest Trp53 expression, followed by the EPI of ESC-DA ETiX. In the VE, iGata4-AA ETiX showed reduced expression of Bak151 and Bcl252 (pro- and anti-apoptotic members of the BCL2 family, respectively) but elevated Mdm2 and Cdkn1a, highlighting a potential shift towards cell-cycle arrest rather than apoptosis. Trp53 levels in the VE were relatively similar between diploid and aneuploid cells in the DA ETiX. In contrast, the ExE lineage displayed the highest Cdkn1a and Bak1 expression in AA ETiX, but low Trp53 and Bax, suggesting a distinct mode of aneuploid cell handling (Fig. S8C, Source Data Table 4).

Proliferation-associated gene expression also varied with lineage and mosaic composition. We examined canonical S-phase markers (Mcm2, Mcm4, Mcm653, Pcna54, Tyms55) and G2/M-phase regulators (Mki6756, Top2a57, Cdk158, Kif1159, Kif20a60) to evaluate cell-cycle activity. These genes are essential for DNA replication and mitotic progression, and their downregulation often reflects p53-mediated checkpoint activation. Consistent with this, TSC-DD ETiX showed reduced expression of Top2a, Kif11, and Mki67, whereas ESC-DA ETiX exhibited lower levels of Tyms, Pcna, and Cdk1, suggesting impaired cell-cycle progression (Fig. S8D, Source Data Table 5). Within the EPI, Mki67 expression was highest in DA mosaics relative to DD, with diploid cells consistently showing the greatest proliferation, perhaps indicating compensatory proliferation in response to aneuploid cell loss. VE aneuploid cells exhibited similar levels of Mki67 levels between DD and DA ETiX, whereas in the ExE, proliferation marker expression was highest in diploid cells of the DA ETiX (Fig. S8E, Source Data Table 6). These findings indicate that aneuploid cells in the EPI and VE activate p53-dependent stress responses that promote either cell-cycle arrest or apoptosis, while ExE cells tolerate genomic instability through reduced p53 activity.

To assess how aneuploidy affects the global transcriptional landscape of ETiX embryos, we examined the combined effects of chromosomal imbalance on both aneuploid and diploid cells. Using previously published WT ETiX30 diploid cells as a reference, we identified distinct lineage-specific responses to aneuploidy. Differential gene expression analysis further revealed that aneuploidy activates overlapping stress pathways across lineages. Compared to diploid cells, aneuploid EPI cells upregulated p53 signaling, DNA repair, interferon responses, and MYC target repression, indicating a balance between damage response and growth inhibition (Fig. 4I, Source Data Table 1). The VE displayed dampened PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling61. and oxidative phosphorylation, suggestive of adaptive survival (Fig. 4J). The ExE exhibited upregulation of glycolysis, hypoxia, and p53 pathways, alongside suppression of the unfolded protein response (UPR) and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 4K). The repression of the UPR is particularly notable, as it may impair the ExE’s ability to resolve ER stress, potentially influencing cell fate decisions, which has been shown in pre-implantation aneuploid embryos25. Together, these data highlight shared stress signatures, activation of p53, repression of MYC, and mitochondrial metabolism overlaid with lineage-specific adaptations.

Remarkably, diploid cells in mosaic embryos also exhibited pronounced transcriptional reprogramming in response to neighboring aneuploid cells. Diploid EPI cells upregulated p53, interferon, and apoptotic pathways, while repressing MYC, E2F, and mTORC1 signaling, consistent with activation of surveillance and competitive elimination programs (Fig. S9A, Source Data Table 2). Diploid VE cells showed induction of protein secretion and inflammatory responses, while diploid ExE cells activated glycolysis, hypoxia, and MYC target pathways but suppressed oxidative phosphorylation and UPR (Fig. S9B, C, Source Data Table 2). These findings reveal that diploid cells actively sense and respond to aneuploid neighbors, triggering non–cell-autonomous stress programs that may facilitate clearance or adaptation.

Together, these analyses establish a unified model in which aneuploidy elicits a shared p53–MYC–metabolism stress axis across all lineages. The embryonic EPI enforces stringent genome surveillance through p53-mediated arrest and elimination, while the VE and ExE adopt compensatory metabolic strategies (suppressing mitochondrial metabolism or enhancing glycolysis) to preserve viability. Diploid cells contribute to this balance by activating immune and stress pathways, orchestrating an embryo-wide adaptive response that integrates cell fate decisions with selective elimination of genomically unstable cells.

To test whether p53 expression is required for aneuploid cell elimination in mosaic EPI, we generated a stable p53 knockdown (KD) ESC line using anti-p53 siRNA (Fig. 5H) and generated four ETiX combinations: WT/WT DD, WT/WT AD, p53 KD/WT DD, and p53 KD/WT AD (Fig. 5I). At day 4 WT/WT AD ETiX showed the expected depletion of tdTomato+ aneuploid cells, consistent with aneuploid cell loss (Fig. 5J, K, top right). However, p53 KD/WT AD ETiXs showed enhanced persistence of tdTomato+ aneuploid cells (Fig. 5J, K, bottom right). Flow cytometry of dissociated pooled ETiXs confirmed these results: in the DD condition, WT/WT EPI displayed comparable levels of GFP⁺ and tdTomato⁺ cells, reflecting balanced contributions from each cell population (Fig. 5L), with this distribution being shifted in p53 KD/WT DD EPI, where loss of p53 increased the representation of tdTomato+ cells relative to GFP+ cells. In the WT/WT AD condition, there was a striking reduction in tdTomato⁺ cells, consistent with a loss of aneuploid cells (Fig. 5L); however, tdTomato+ aneuploid cells were able to persist in p53 KD/WT AD ETiX. Finally, we also examined how p53 KD affected the proportion of green, red, and mixed green/red EPI in DD and AD p53 KD/WT ETiX. We found that p53 KD in tdTomato+ aneuploid cells increased the proportion of all red and mixed EPIs, whereas the frequency of green EPIs was reduced, suggesting that p53 promotes the elimination of aneuploid cells in mosaic ETiX EPI (Fig. 5M).

Our scRNA-seq data also highlighted differential regulation of Myc, another known key player in cell competition38,62. Specifically, Myc was downregulated in aneuploid EPI cells compared to diploid EPI cells, along with several of its downstream targets, such as cell-cycle related genes (Cdc20, Krt17) and metabolic genes (Eif4e, Ldha) (Fig. S10A). To explore how differential Myc expression affects aneuploid:diploid cell dynamics, we generated an ESC line with stable Myc overexpression (OE) (Fig. S10B). As above, we generated four ETiX combinations with DMSO-treated GFP+ diploid and tdTomato+ DMSO- or reversine-treated ESCs: (1) WT/WT DD, (2) WT/WT AD, (3) Myc OE/WT DD, and (4) Myc OE/WT AD (Fig. S10C). We calculated the tdTomato and GFP fluorescence ratios for day 4 ETiXs (Fig. S10D) and observed a marked depletion of tdTomato fluorescence in WT/WT AD ETiX compared to W/WT DD ETiX, as expected. However, found that WT/WT DD EPI displayed comparable proportions of GFP⁺ and tdTomato⁺ cells, while Myc OE/WT DD EPIs showed a shift towards higher representation of tdTomato⁺ cells due to Myc overexpression favoring their expansion (Fig. S10F). In the WT/WT AD condition, tdTomato⁺ cells were markedly reduced; however, in the Myc OE/WT condition, the tdTomato+ cells persisted in AD ETiX (Fig. S10F). Our bulk measurements of EPI fluorescence composition also showed that Myc OE in aneuploid cells increased the proportion of all-red (aneuploid) and mixed EPI in AD ETiXs, and the frequency of all-green (diploid) EPI was slightly reduced (Fig. S10G). We infer that Myc overexpression in aneuploid ESCs increases their contribution to a mosaic EPI in ETiX.

These findings underscore the critical role of p53 in promoting, and of Myc in counteracting, the selective clearance of aneuploid cells in the EPI in ETiXs. Modulation of these pathways can partially rescue the aneuploid cell elimination.

Discussion

Although the EPI is crucial as the lineage that gives rise to the somatic cells of the newborn mouse or human, it cannot develop without the extraembryonic lineages. These lineages are descended from the same early blastomeres and therefore share inherited chromosome segregation errors. Although there is evidence that different lineages of the embryo have differential tolerance for aneuploidy23, a characterization of aneuploid cell behavior in all three lineages remains missing. Here, we utilized the ETiX stem-cell embryo model to examine each lineage independently but in the context of the overall embryonic structure. In agreement with previous studies focusing on the EPI23,25, we find that aneuploid cells continue to be eliminated from the EPI in a model of the post-implantation stage mouse embryo, as well as in the VE lineage. In contrast, the ExE does not show a marked reduction in aneuploid cells. These differences are also reflected in the efficiency of development, with the presence of aneuploid cells in the EPI and VE reducing the developmental efficiency of ETiXs. Such lineage-dependent differential sensitivity to aneuploidy has been previously observed in human ESC micropatterns9.

The differential tolerance to aneuploidy in the three lineages may have implications for the interpretation of pre-implantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A) or chorionic villous sampling (CVS). Such testing often relies on biopsies of extraembryonic trophectoderm or chorionic villous cells. The phenomenon of healthy diploid live births following implantation of embryos diagnosed as mosaic aneuploid by PGT-A6,63 or discordance between trophectoderm and embryonic biopsies64 is well documented, and our findings would suggest that aneuploid cells may persist in the placenta while possibly being cleared from the embryonic lineage. In support of this, confined placental mosaicism is thought to occur in approximately 4% of pregnancies14.

Mechanisms driving aneuploid cell apoptosis have been previously studied within the pre-implantation EPI. Aneuploid cells were found to be under increased proteotoxic stress25 downstream of putative changes in protein stoichiometries resulting from abnormal copy numbers of transcripts65–67. In response to this stress, an upregulation of autophagy and eventual cell death was observed25,68,69. p53 has also been found to be upregulated in aneuploid cells within the embryo25. In agreement with this, we find genes involved in p53 activity and the UPR to be differentially expressed in aneuploid cells.

We found several key pathways, such as Myc expression, p53 and mTOR signaling, and altered metabolism, which have been previously found to play a role in cell competition during the early stages of embryonic development38,39,70,71. Modulating these pathways by knockdown or overexpression was able to partially rescue aneuploid cell elimination, promoting their survival in mosaic aneuploid ETiXs. In the future, these pathways could be targeted to instead induce aneuploid cell depletion and promote euploid embryo development. However, aneuploid cells may also undergo elimination due to intrinsic mechanisms, rather than being influenced by the presence of diploid cells. Indeed, we observe that AA ETiX show reduced development, supporting this hypothesis.

For the mosaic aneuploid embryo to successfully develop, it must combine aneuploid cell death with compensatory diploid cell proliferation. Compensatory proliferation of the remaining diploid cells may allow for tissue size regulation and for the embryo to attain the necessary cell number and size for further successful development. The decrease in aneuploid cell proportion could also be at least partly driven by diploid cell proliferation. This has been observed in the mouse embryo, although the molecular drivers are not known25. We found some evidence of compensatory proliferation with aneuploid and diploid cells showing differences in their expression of cell-cycle markers and regulators. This could be further followed up to determine whether pathways such as JAK/STAT and Hippo signaling upstream of mTOR signaling, which have been implicated in cell proliferation in Drosophila and mouse, respectively, may drive this compensation62,72,73.

Although reversine treatment has been used in a variety of models to induce aneuploidy9,23, a recent study compared reversine- treated human embryos to aneuploid and euploid human embryos and found only 10% of differentially expressed genes overlapped between reversine-treated or aneuploid embryos when compared to euploid embryos74. However, the upregulated pathways in both classes of aneuploid embryos were conserved, and p53 was again highlighted as a key upregulated pathway. Nonetheless, findings from mouse embryos or stem-cell models may need to be validated in the natural human embryo or human stem-cell-based embryo models in the future, to examine to what extent the response to aneuploidy is conserved for each lineage75,76. As human embryos show higher intrinsic rates of aneuploidy, they may also show differential sensitivity and mechanisms for aneuploid cell elimination.

Within our analysis, we found that although reversine treatment induced a wide range of aneuploidies, many of these were completely eliminated by day 4 of ETiX development, with this effect being more noticeable in the EPI. However, some specific aneuploidies remained in day 4 ETiXs. Such differences between specific aneuploidies have been observed before in mouse ESCs77,78, human ESCs and embryos64,79,80. In the future, ESCs harboring specific aneuploidies could be utilized within stem-cell-based embryo models to systematically characterize their different behaviors.

Reversine was used to induce aneuploidy in mouse stem cells in this study. Reversine treatment generates complex mosaic aneuploidy, wherein each cell may be affected by different chromosomal abnormalities, resembling patterns seen in human embryos4,5. However, reversine has limitations, including off-target effects on differentiation (previously documented in myogenic progenitors and sheep fibroblasts81,82), and inhibition of Aurora B kinase at higher concentrations83. To minimize such effects, we used a low concentration (0.5 μM) and brief exposure. In future studies, alternative methods could be used to induce aneuploidy, such as AZ3146, another MPS1 kinase inhibitor84,85. Other methods, such as knocking down the SAC component Mad2, can also induce chaotic aneuploidy, albeit with lower efficiency25,86. These strategies induce random karyotypic changes across cells, allowing the identification of a generalized response to aneuploidy. Ultimately, induction of specific aneuploidies may help determine whether a common surveillance mechanism applies across specific chromosomal imbalances.

Overall, this study utilizes a recently developed stem-cell based model of the post-implantation embryo to systematically examine the consequences of aneuploid mosaicism within each of the three lineages that normally build an embryo. Our results reveal clear lineage-specific effects, and scRNA sequencing highlights key pathways that could be modulated to improve the survival and development of mosaic embryos by selectively targeting aneuploid cells.

To induce aneuploidy, cells were first treated with 10 μM RO-3306 (Sigma-Aldrich, SML0569-5MG), a CDK1 inhibitor, to induce G2/M arrest and synchronize the cell cycle for 12 h, before being treated with 0.5 μM reversine (Cayman Chemicals, 10004412), a MPS1 inhibitor, to induce chromosome mis-segregation or an equivalent concentration of DMSO (1:2000, Sigma-Aldrich D2650) as a control, for 8 h.

TSCs were cultured on mitomycin C (Sigma-Aldrich, M4287) treated MEFs (Insight Biotechnology, ASF-1201) in TSC medium which contained RPMI medium (Gibco, 21870076), 20% heart inactivated fetal bovine serum (iFBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 35-010-CV), 2 mM GlutaMAX, 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, 11360039) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. TSC medium was supplemented with 25 ng/ml FGF4 (R&D Systems 7486-F4-025) and 1 μg/ml heparin (Sigma-Aldrich, H3149-25KU).

Mouse ESCs and TSCs were routinely passaged at 70% confluency following trypsinization (Trypsin-EDTA 0.05%, Life Technologies, 25300054) for 4 min at 37 °C. Feeder cell medium was added to terminate the trypsinization, and cells were dissociated by gentle pipetting and centrifuged for 4 min at 1000 rpm, before being re-plated at a 1:10 or 1:20 dilution. Feeder cell medium contained Dulbecco’s modified essential medium (DMEM, Gibco, 41966052), 15% iFBS, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 2 mM GlutaMAX, 1% MEM non-essential amino acids (Gibco, 11140035), 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Chimeras

4–6-week-old F1 females (C57BI6 x CBA, Charles River), 6–52-week-old F1 males (C57BI6 x CBA, Charles River) (616, Jackson Laboratories) were maintained in the animal house facilities at the Gurdon Institute or the Combined Animal Facility at the Department of Physiology, Development and Neuroscience on a 12:12 h light-dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. Females were placed with males overnight, and plug checks were performed in the morning.

Pre-compacted 8-cell-stage mouse embryos were recovered from oviducts and uterine horns by flushing in M2 medium (Cat# M7167-100ML), and the zona pellucida was removed prior by brief treatment with Acidic Tyrode’s solution (Cat# T1788-100ML), followed by washes in M2 medium. In parallel, cells were prepared, after which embryos and cells were placed in indentations and cultured for a further 48 h to the late blastocyst stage at 5% CO2 and 37 °C.

CAG-GFP/tetO-H2B-mCherry (CAG-GFP) ESCs: Following reversine treatment to induce aneuploidy, or DMSO treatment as a control, cells were washed twice with PBS and then dissociated by trypsinization for 4 min at 5% CO2 and 37 °C. Cells were spun down for 4 min at 1000 rpm and then resuspended in N2B27 2iLIF medium. 3–4 cells were picked and washed through pre-equilibrated KSOM medium (Cat# MR-101-D) before being placed in indentations with a single 8-cell stage embryo.

CAG-GFP/tetO-H2B-mCherry/tetO-Gata4 (iGata4) cells: Cells were treated with reversine or DMSO, with 1 μg/mL of doxycycline (Sigma-Aldrich, D9891) also added 6 h prior to chimaera generation to induce Gata4 expression. Cells were dissociated, and 3–4 cells aggregated as above.

Cdx2-GFP TSCs: TSCs were reversine- or DMSO-treated and dissociated by trypsinization. Following resuspension in TSC medium, cells were added to a gelatinized plate for MEF depletion for 20 min at 37 °C, after which cells were collected and 6–8 TSCs (1–2 small clumps of cells) were aggregated with a single 8-cell-stage embryo. Chimaeras were cultured in TSC medium for the first 24 h and then washed and moved to KSOM medium. Control embryos, without TSCs, were cultured in TSC medium or KSOM and showed no difference in blastocyst formation.

For in vitro culture of blastocysts to early post-implantation stages, the protocol from Ma et al.33 was used. Briefly, blastocysts were transferred to 8-well μ-slide tissue culture plates (Ibidi, 80826) coated in 100% Matrigel (Corning, 354230) and cultured in CMRL 1066 medium (Gibco, 11530037), supplemented with 2 mM GlutaMAX (Thermo Scientific, 35050-038), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, 11360039), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific,15140122), 0.5% N2 (in-house) and 1% B27 (Gibco, 10889038). Serum was added in increasing concentrations of 10% inactivated fetal bovine serum (iFBS) on days 1–2, 20% iFBS on day 3, and 30% knockout serum replacement (KSR, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10828010) on day 4. Up to 10 embryos were cultured in each well at 5% CO2 and 37 °C. Embryos were fixed on day 5 and assessed for the presence of lineages, egg-cylinder morphogenesis, and chimeric contribution.

ETiX embryoids

Mouse post-implantation stem-cell-based embryo models - the ETiXs- were generated following a previously published protocol30. ESCs, iGata4-ESCs, and TSCs were treated with reversine or DMSO as a control, as described above. iGata4-ESCs were similarly treated with doxycycline for 6 h to induce Gata4 expression for use in chimaeras or for ETX generation. Following drug treatments, ESCs, TSCs, and iGata4 cells were washed and dissociated by Trypsin-EDTA exposure as for passaging, and the TSCs were plated on gelatin-coated wells for MEF depletion for 20 min.

Aggrewell 400 24-well plates (STEMCELL Technologies, 34415) were precoated with anti- adherence rinsing solution (STEMCELL Technologies, 07010). Plates were centrifuged at 2000 × g for 5 min and then left to incubate for 20 min at room temperature with 500 μl of feeder cell medium added after incubation and washes. All three cell types were counted, and mixtures were generated such that there would be approximately 5 ESCs, 5 iGata4 cells, and 16 TSCs per microwell in the aggrewell (38,400 TSCs, 12,000 ESCs, and 12,000 iGata4 cells in total). In order to generate mosaic ETiXs, DMSO or reversine-treated cells were combined in a 1:1 ratio: for ESCs, 6000 + 6000 cells, for iGata4-ESCs, 6000 + 6000 cells, and for TSCs, 19,200 + 19,200 cells.

Cell suspensions were pelleted at 1000 rpm for 4 min and resuspended in 1 ml of feeder cell medium with 7.5 nM Y27632, before being plated in a dropwise manner to the Aggrewell. The aggrewell plate was then spun down at 100 × g for 3 min and cultured at 37 °C, 20% O2, and 5% CO2. After 24 h (day 1), the media was changed twice, replacing 1 ml of media with fresh feeder cell medium without Y27632. On day 2, 1 ml of media was replaced with 1 ml of fresh feeder cell medium once, and on day 3, 1.2 ml of media was removed, and 1.5 ml of mIVC1 medium was added. mIVC1 medium was equilibrated for 30 min in the incubator at 37 °C, 20% O2, and 5% CO2 and contained: advanced DMEM/F-12 (Gibco, 21331020), with 20% iFBS, 2 mM GlutaMax, 1% v/v penicillin–streptomycin, 1× ITS-X (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 51500-056), 8 nM β-estradiol (Sigma-Aldrich, E8875), 200 ng/ml progesterone and 25 μM N-acetyl-L-cysteine (Sigma-Aldrich, A7250)91. ETiXs were collected at the indicated timepoints.

Cell sorting

Aggregates were collected after 72 h of culture for dissociation and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). Aggregates were then resuspended in Trypsin- EDTA for mouse and incubated for 10 min at 37 °C. To aid dissociation, aggregates were also pipetted up and down every 2 min. After 10 min, feeder cell medium was added, and the cells were pelleted at 1000 rpm for 4 min. Finally, cells were resuspended in N2B27 2iLIF. The dissociated cells were filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer (Merck, CLS431750) and sorted with a Sony Sy3200 Cell sorter based on fluorescence (RFP and GFP fluorescence).

Metaphase spreads

To induce metaphase arrest, mouse and human ESCs were cultured in 0.1 μg/ml colcemid (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15212012) for 12 h, after which they were dissociated as described above for passaging and resuspended in 5 ml of warm hypotonic solution (1% sodium citrate (Sigma-Aldrich, W302600) in water) by dropwise addition and gentle inversion to resuspend cells. Cells were incubated in hypotonic solution for 10 min and then spun down at 500 rpm for 5 min and gently resuspended again in 5 ml of Carnoy’s fixative (75% methanol (Sigma-Aldrich, 322415) and 25% glacial acetic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, ARK2183)). Cells were washed in Carnoy’s fixative three times (10 min each) and finally resuspended in 0.5 ml of Carnoy’s fixative. 200 μl of the cell suspension was dropped onto clean Superfrost Plus adhesion glass slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific, J1800AMNT) from approximately 30 cm height. Slides were allowed to air dry and then mounted with Vectashield Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories, H1200). Metaphase spreads were imaged with an SP5 or SP8 scanning confocal microscope (Leica) using the 63× oil objective, with a Z-step size of 0.5 μm.

Immunofluorescence and confocal imaging

Embryos and ETiXs were fixed in 4% PFA for 30 min at room temperature and then washed through PBST. Samples were permeabilized in 0.3% Triton X-100 and 0.1 mM glycine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, BP381-1) in PBS for 30 min at room temperature and washed through PBST again, before being transferred to blocking buffer (5% bovine serum albumin in PBS) for 3 h at 4 °C.

Following blocking, samples were incubated with primary antibody (Table S2) mixes (diluted in blocking buffer) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, samples were washed through PBST and incubated in secondary antibody (Table S3) mixes (diluted in blocking buffer) with DAPI (1:500, Life Technologies, D3571) for 2 h at room temperature.

Following incubation with secondary antibodies, samples were washed through PBST and M2 medium, and mounted on glass bottomed petri dishes in drops of M2 covered in mineral oil or imaged in Ibidi dishes (for adherent samples).

Imaging was carried out on a SP8 scanning confocal microscope (Leica) using the 40X oil objective or 25X water objective. Z stacks were taken at 2–4 μm for fixed samples, 5–7 μm for tile scans, or 5 μm for live imaging.

Live imaging

Live imaging was performed with an SP8 scanning confocal microscope (Leica) using the 40× oil objective or a Zeiss LSM 980 using a 20× air objective. Picked day 3 ETiXs were imaged on glass-bottom dishes (MatTek, P35G-1.5-14-C) within a nylon mesh (Plastok) in mIVC1 media with Sytox (Invitrogen, S34859, 1:1000) under mineral oil and were kept in a humidified chamber at 5% CO2 and 37 °C throughout imaging. Images were captured every 20 min with a z-step size of 5 μm. Time-lapse recordings were processed with Fiji software to assess cell death (Sytox-positive cell number)

Image analysis and statistics

Images were processed with Fiji software (Schindelin et al.92) (https://imagej.net/software/fiji/) to assess cell or ETiX number, classification, and efficiency, as well as lineage allocation and fluorescent marker cell counts. Cell numbers or ETiXs were counted manually, using the multi-point counter function. Efficiency of ETiX formation and fluorescent marker ratios were normalized to the average of all DD ETiXs/replicates for each experiment to account for variability between experiments.

p53 Immunostaining quantification

The intensities of p53 in red versus green cells were quantified using CellProfiler93. Individual nuclei were identified as objects using the DAPI channel, and cells with cytoplasm were inferred by expanding the borders of DAPI objects. Cells were filtered by red and green channel intensities to identify GFP+ cells and tdTomato+ cells. The p53 and DAPI intensities were then measured for all GFP+ and tdTomato+ cells. The p53 intensity of each cell was normalized to the DAPI intensity within each cell, and then the normalized p53 intensity of every cell for GFP+ or tdTomato+ cells was averaged per ETiX. The per ETiX-average p53 intensity of each colored cell line was then normalized to the average of that cell line (GFP+ and tdTomato + ) in all DD ETiXs, and the differences between DD and DA/AD were assessed with a two-tailed t-test.

Bulk analysis

All ETiXs within an aggrewell were collected and processed for imaging using the tilescan function. Maximum intensity projections of each tilescan (following stitching) were created. The intensities of the GFP and tdTomato+ signals per ETiX were quantified in CellProfiler. Following intensity adjustments, each ETiX was identified as an object using the DAPI channel. The average intensity of GFP and tdTomato+ was quantified for each ETiX. The intensity values of each channel were normalized to the average intensity of the channel in the DD WT condition.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were chosen based on previous experience. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized, as the specific cell lineages and treatments required targeted allocation. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment, given the visual nature of the experimental setups and the distinct fluorescence markers (mT, mG, mCherry) used to track lineages.

All statistical details, including the exact value of n, can be found in the corresponding figure legends. In all cases, the two-tailed version of the test was used. Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Statistical analysis was performed using Prism software (GraphPad Software v9.2.0, https://www.graphpad.com/scientific-software/prism/). For comparing two groups, P-values were determined using Student’s t-tests (two-tailed). For comparing more than two groups, one-way ANOVAs followed by Tukey’s test were applied. Differences between groups were considered significant for P-values < 0.05.

Statistical significance between groups in Fig. 4H was determined using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. For comparisons across multiple groups in Fig. S8B–E (Source Data Tables 3–6), we employed the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test with p-values adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. Statistical significance was defined as adjusted p-value < 0.05. All analyses were performed in R using the rstatix package. Significance annotations are indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Single-cell RNA-seq

ETiXs were generated as described above and cultured until day 3. ETiXs containing all three lineages and with a visible cavity formation were selected. Approximately 200 ETiXs per condition (DD or DA for each lineage) were collected.

ETiXs were washed with PBS and dissociated using TrypLE for 5 min at 37 °C. After dissociation, cells were counted and resuspended in PBS supplemented with 0.04% BSA to a final concentration of 10,000 cells/μL. Single-cell capture and library preparation were performed using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ Kit with Chip G (10x Genomics), following the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of eight libraries were generated.

Paired-end sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq X Plus platform. Samples underwent quality control and demultiplexing using the Cell Ranger software (10x Genomics). The mm10 mouse genome was used as the reference for alignment and analysis.

scRNA-seq data were processed using Seurat (v3.0) with the following filtering criteria: nFeature_RNA between 500 and 5000, percent.mito <0.25, and nCount_RNA < 50,000. After filtering, the data were clustered and analyzed following the standard dataset integration and analysis workflow in Seurat.

Samples were merged into a single Seurat object, and gene expression counts were normalized and scaled. Graph-based clustering was conducted using the top 20 principal components at a resolution of 0.5. Clusters were annotated based on the expression of canonical marker genes representing the three distinct embryonic lineages: epiblast, visceral endoderm, and trophectoderm. Annotations were performed using the scType R package.

Aneuploidy prediction and differential expression analysis

scRNA-seq data from WT ETiX embryoids (Amadei et al.; GEO: GSE161947) were downloaded and aggregated with each DA condition per lineage.

For the inferCNV karyotype analysis, we used WT ETiX scRNA-seq data as the euploid reference baseline. To avoid lineage-specific transcriptional signatures being misinterpreted as copy number alterations (a known limitation of inferCNV), we performed the analysis in a lineage-matched manner: WT epiblast cells served as the reference for epiblast, WT VE cells for VE, and WT ExE cells for ExE. Each DA condition and the WT ETiX control were clustered, followed by sub-clustering for each lineage, before running the inferCNV algorithm. The inferCNV R package was applied with a cutoff of 0.1, clustering by groups, and using the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) for aneuploidy prediction. Cells with a CNV proportion greater than 0.9 for any chromosome were classified as aneuploid. We further validated this approach by running the same lineage-matched inferCNV analysis on DD ETiX cells, allowing us to identify and account for any baseline karyotypic variation or technical artifacts not attributable to reversine treatment.

For the differential gene expression analysis, we compared aneuploid or diploid cells from DA ETiX structures against their corresponding lineage in WT ETiX structures using Seurat’s FindMarkers() function. To ensure that the resulting signatures reflect aneuploidy-driven transcriptional changes rather than batch effects, culture conditions, or passage-related differences, we also performed a DGE comparison between WT ETiX and DD ETiX cells. Genes that were differentially expressed between WT and DD were removed from the final DA-derived gene lists. Remaining genes were filtered with an adjusted p-value < 0.001 and an average log2 fold change >1.5. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the GSEA pipeline with hallmark gene sets to identify enriched pathways.

Functional studies with stable p53 knockdown (p53 KD) and Myc overexpression (Myc OE) mTmG mESCs

mTmG ESCs were used to generate a stable cell line with p53 knockdown. To achieve this, mTmG ESCs were transduced with a lentivirus expressing p53-targeting siRNA and a puromycin resistance cassette. After transduction, cells were selected with 1 μg/mL puromycin for 5–7 days to enrich for successfully transduced cells.

Similarly, the Myc OE line was generated by transducing mTmG ESCs with lentiviral particles containing the mouse Myc ORF cDNA, driven by a CMV promoter, and fused to a C-terminal V5 tag and a GFP reporter for sorting.

Following selection, individual colonies were manually picked and expanded. RNA was extracted from each colony, and p53 and Myc expression levels were quantified by reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) using primers specific for p53 and Myc (Table S6). Colonies showing the highest p53 knockdown and Myc overexpression compared to control ESCs were selected for further experiments.

The stable p53 KD and Myc OE mTmG ESC lines were subsequently used to generate mosaic ETiX embryos as described above. We found that RO-3306 treatment was detrimental to p53 KD ESCs, and so we eliminated this step, instead treating ESCs with DMSO or Reversine for 8 h.

We generated four combinations of ETiXs structures with p53 KD. Briefly, we combined TSCs and iGata4-ESCs with DMSO-treated GFP+ diploid ESCs mixed with (1) DMSO-treated tdTomato+ wild-type (WT) ESCs (WT DD), (2) reversine-treated tdTomato+ WT ESCs (WT AD), (3) DMSO-treated tdTomato+ p53 KD ESCs (p53 KD DD), and (4) reversine-treated tdTomato+ p53 KD ESCs (p53 KD AD) (Fig. 5i).

We generated four combinations of ETiX for Myc OE, combining TSCs and iGata4-ESCs with a mixture of DMSO-treated GFP+ diploid ESCs and (1) DMSO-treated tdTomato+ WT ESCs (WT DD), (2) reversine-treated tdTomato+ WT ESCs (WT AD), (3) DMSO-treated tdTomato+ Myc OE ESCs and (Myc OE DD), and (4) reversine-treated tdTomato+ Myc OE ESCs (Myc OE AD) (Fig. S10c).
