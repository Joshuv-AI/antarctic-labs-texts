Identification of lncRNAs Indicating Immune Pathway Activity for Predicting Prognosis and Immunotherapy Response in Pan‐Cancer
Authors: Mengyu Xin, Danni He, Yifan Dai, Qian Liu, Rui Sun, Houxing Li, Yujie Zhang, Jiatong Li, Shangwei Ning, Qiuyan Guo, Yue Gao, Peng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe critical role of long non‐coding RNAs (lncRNAs) in tumor immunity has garnered increasing attention, and immune pathways are key regulatory factors in tumor immune mechanisms. However, current research on the association between lncRNAs and immune pathways remains limited. Therefore, quantifying this association may provide new insights for predicting prognosis and immunotherapy response.MethodsA method was developed to classify candidate immune‐related lncRNAs into complex (CIlncRNAs), moderate (MIlncRNAs), and specific (SIlncRNAs) types based on the breadth of their associations with immune pathway activity. The features of these lncRNA types were then compared from multiple aspects.ResultsThe genomic variations and correlations with DNA methylation of three types of immune‐related lncRNAs were significantly different in multiple cancers. CIlncRNAs were more strongly associated with immune infiltration than MIlncRNAs and SIlncRNAs. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) patients were classified into two subtypes based on CIlncRNAs, with one subtype exhibiting better prognosis and higher immune cell infiltration. High expression of CIlncRNAs in immune cells and their role in CD8+ T cell differentiation in LUAD were validated using single‐cell data. Furthermore, a CIlncRNA signature (CIsig) was established for each cancer, and significant differences in overall survival (OS) were observed across risk stratifications in most cancers. Lastly, CIlncRNA was validated in independent datasets for its ability to predict immunotherapy response and enhance the predictive performance of established biomarkers.ConclusionsOverall, our analysis innovatively explores the breadth of associations between lncRNAs and immune pathway activity. We identified CIlncRNAs as potential novel biomarkers for predicting patient prognosis and immunotherapy response, offering valuable insights for clinical practice.

Introduction

The ongoing proliferation of cancer cells relies heavily on immune evasion. Immunotherapy, unlike standard treatments, can produce durable responses in patients and has transformed the therapeutic outlook for various solid tumors [1]. Accumulated evidence from prior studies has firmly established the robust efficacy of immunotherapy in oncology. This modality has now emerged as a cornerstone approach in the therapeutic armamentarium against metastatic or recurrent solid tumors. Therapies targeting immune checkpoint proteins (ICPs), such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1), programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1), and cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte‐associated protein 4 (CTLA‐4), have demonstrated substantial efficacy in cancer treatment by targeting the immune evasion mechanisms employed by cancer cells [2, 3]. Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy provides enduring benefits, but the need for biomarkers to predict its efficacy before treatment initiation remains unmet. Current evidence supports the use of immune checkpoint gene expression, microsatellite instability (MSI) status, and tumor mutation burden (TMB) as prognostic predictors for individuals undergoing immunotherapy for cancer [4, 5, 6]. However, high PD‐L1 expression, TMB, or MSI is not always a reliable predictor of immunotherapy response, as patients without PD‐L1 expression or with low TMB and microsatellite stability can still benefit. Therefore, further research on new markers to predict immunotherapy response will be essential.

Long non‐coding RNAs, a type of non‐coding RNAs longer than 200 nucleotides, are known to play roles in various biological processes [7, 8]. LncRNA dysregulation is a common feature of tumorigenesis, potentially influencing both tumor initiation and progression [9]. Research into cancer transcriptomes has revealed a multitude of lncRNAs associated with diverse cancer types [10]. The tumor microenvironment profoundly impacts cancer development and progression, with immune system dysregulation being a key contributor to tumorigenesis [11]. Recent studies have highlighted the growing significance of lncRNAs as key regulatory components within the immune system, playing crucial roles in regulating immune function and preserving immune stability [12, 13, 14]. Considering the importance of lncRNAs in immune responses, conducting detailed research on them and their immunomodulatory functions is key to finding new targets for cancer immunotherapy.

Immune‐related pathways are crucial in cancer development. One example is endogenous antigen processing and presentation, a key pathway that allows epitope peptides from the intracellular proteome to be displayed on major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC‐I) proteins in jawed vertebrates [15]. It has been reported that antigen processing and presentation pathways are key elements of an effective response to immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy [16]. Chemokines have gained recognition over the last 20 years as vital regulators of cell migration in processes such as immune surveillance and cancer progression. T‐cell receptor signaling plays a crucial role in the development and activation of T‐cells [17]. Pathway‐related signatures have been employed in numerous studies to predict cancer prognosis, while few studies have integrated these immune‐related pathways with lncRNAs. Therefore, it is meaningful to investigate the associations between lncRNAs and immune pathway activity to obtain lncRNA signatures that predict patient prognosis and response to immunotherapy.

We integrated transcriptomic data, cancer cell lines, and immune cell lines to develop a computational method that classified candidate immune‐related lncRNAs into three types based on the number of immune‐related pathways significantly associated with each lncRNA. The three types of immune‐related lncRNAs had different degrees of genomic variation and correlation with methylation. We noted that CIlncRNAs were more strongly associated with immune infiltration. CIlncRNAs showed higher expression levels in immune cells and exhibited a robust association with immune cell activation. CIlncRNAs have great potential in predicting patient prognostic outcome and immunotherapy response. Taken together, these results may contribute to the identification of novel biomarkers for guiding cancer immunotherapy.

Materials and Methods

Data Collection and Preprocessing of Pan‐Cancer Cohorts, Immune, and Tumor Cell Lines

We sourced the raw data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) for 115 purified cell lines from 19 immune cell types (Table S1). Next, raw data were processed using the R package “affy”, and batch effects were corrected with ComBat across datasets within each cell type. Sequence comparison was performed using Seqmap to reannotate Affymetrix HG‐U133_Plus 2.0 microarray probes, thereby obtaining lncRNA expression profiles. Transcriptional data for pan‐cancer cell lines were retrieved from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia Project (CCLE, https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ccle) [18].

RNA‐seq data for 19 cancer types, which overlap between The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and CCLE and contain normal samples, were sourced from the University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC) Xena platform (http://xena.ucsc.edu/, accessed November 9, 2022) [19]. Table S1 lists all datasets employed in this study.

Collection of lncRNAs With Increased Expression in Immune Cells

For each immune cell line, the top 5% of lncRNAs by expression level were obtained [20]. To identify lncRNAs specifically expressed in immune cells rather than tumor cells, differential expression analysis was conducted using the R package “limma”. Candidate immune‐related lncRNAs were defined as those significantly upregulated in immune cells and downregulated in tumor cells (FDR < 0.05, logFC > 1). These lncRNAs showed selective expression in immune cells and were selected for further analysis due to their potential involvement in immune regulation.

Calculation of Association Scores and p‐Values Between lncRNAs and Immune Pathway Activity

A total of 1811 human immune‐related genes across 17 immune‐associated pathways were obtained from the ImmPort project [21]. These gene sets capture genes involved in key immune regulatory activities and have been widely used in immune‐related analyses. During the calculation of contribution weights for genes within each pathway, the interferon receptor pathway was excluded because it contained only three marker genes, which prevented the computation of enrichment scores. As a result, 16 pathways were retained for the classification analyses.

To quantify the association between lncRNAs and immune pathway activity, we developed a method to calculate association scores and p‐values. Classification of Candidate Immune‐Related lncRNAs

The number of lncRNA‐pathway pairs with p‐values less than 0.05 was calculated and represented as N. Thresholds for classification were determined based on the distribution of N across all pan‐cancer samples. Accordingly, lncRNAs were classified into three types: specific immune‐related lncRNA (SIlncRNA, N ≤ 6), moderate immune‐related lncRNA (MIlncRNA, 6 < N < 11), and complex immune‐related lncRNA (CIlncRNA, N ≥ 11). To assess the robustness of the classification, the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) was calculated using the R package “mclust” for classifications obtained under different threshold settings. The ARI provided a measure of consistency with the original classification. To further verify the reproducibility of the classification, a 10‐fold cross‐validation was performed. In each iteration, nine‐tenths of the samples were used to reclassify the lncRNAs, and the resulting ARI values were calculated to assess the impact of sample perturbation on the classification outcomes.

Validation of Immune‐Related lncRNAs in Independent Datasets

We downloaded an RNA‐seq dataset for prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD)‐CA from the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC) and an RNA‐seq dataset for LUAD from the GEO database (accession number: GSE40419). These two independent datasets were used to reclassify lncRNA types. The overlap with the results from TCGA was then analyzed, and its significance was evaluated using hypergeometric test.

To investigate whether candidate immune‐related lncRNAs are associated with immunity, we queried PubMed to determine whether each lncRNA co‐occurred with the term “immune” in the literature using the R package “RISmed” (https://rdrr.io/cran/RISmed/). The proportions of candidate immune‐related lncRNAs and other lncRNAs co‐occurring with the “immune” were calculated, and significance was assessed using the chi‐square test.

Analysis of Genomic Mutations and Methylation

Copy number variation (CNV) and somatic mutation data were sourced from the R package “TCGAbiolinks” [22]. Then, the frequencies of amplification (Segment_Mean > 0.2) and deletion (Segment_Mean < −0.2) of the three types of lncRNAs were counted as F. LncRNAs with F > 0.3 were defined as amplified or deleted and recorded as 1, while those with F ≤ 0.3 were recorded as 0. The numbers of 0 and 1 were compared between the three types of lncRNAs, and significance was assessed using the chi‐square test. Similarly, lncRNAs with F > 0.02 were considered somatically mutated and compared across the three lncRNA categories.

We downloaded methylation profiles from the UCSC Xena platform. Pearson's correlation between methylation data and expression data was then calculated for each lncRNA. The absolute correlation values for the three types of lncRNAs were compared, and significance was assessed using a Kruskal–Wallis test. In addition, we calculated the TMB using the R package “mafTools”.

Cancer Similarity Scores Based on Immune‐Related lncRNA Classification

The Jaccard index was applied to quantify the similarity of immune‐related lncRNA classifications between cancer types, as follows: Jaccard indexij=LncTypei∩LncTypejLncTypei∪LncTypej where LncTypei and LncTypej are the classifications of immune‐related lncRNAs in cancers i and j, respectively.

Evaluation of Anti‐Tumor Immunoreactivity in Pan‐Cancer Cohorts

The ImmuneScore, StromalScore, ESTIMATEScore, and TumorPurity were calculated for each patient across 19 cancer types using the R package “estimate” [23]. Immune infiltration data for all TCGA samples were obtained from the Tumor Immune Estimation Resource 2.0 (TIMER 2.0) (http://timer.cistrome.org/) database [24]. We calculated Spearman's correlation coefficients between lncRNA expression and immune cell infiltration. The immunophenotype score (IPS) was obtained from The Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA, https://tcia.at/home) [25].

The MHC score reflects antigen presentation essential for T cell recognition and subsequent T cell–mediated tumor cell elimination. It was calculated based on the mean expression of core MHC‐I genes, including HLA‐A, HLA‐B, HLA‐C, TAP1, TAP2, NLRC5, PSMB9, PSMB8, and B2M [26]. The CYT score, reflecting immune cell‐mediated tumor clearance, is calculated based on the geometric average of GZMA and PRF1 expression levels [27].

Association Between CIlncRNAs and Intratumoral Microbial Heterogeneity

Microbiome data at the genus level for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), and rectum adenocarcinoma (READ) were retrieved from the Cancer Microbiome Atlas (TCMA) database [28]. Shannon diversity indices for each sample were calculated using the R package “vegan”. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationship between CIlncRNA expression levels and microbial alpha diversity.

Expression and Functional Analysis of Immune‐Related lncRNAs in a Pan‐Cancer Single‐Cell Dataset

We downloaded a single‐cell RNA sequencing dataset including multiple cancer types (GSE210347) and selected tumor samples from cancers overlapping with our study for further analysis. The R packages “Seurat” and “Harmony” were used for quality control, cell screening, removal of batch effects, and cell type identification. The marker genes for the different cell types were as follows: endothelial cells (CD34, CLDN5, PECAM1, and VWF), epithelial cells (CDH1, KRT18, CLDN4, and KRT8), fibroblast (DCN, COL1A1, and FAP), lymphocyte (CD3D, CD3E, and CD2), myeloid (CD68 and CD14), and plasma cells (IGHA1, IGHG1, and JCHAIN). The area under the curve (AUC) values for the three types of immune‐related lncRNAs were calculated for each cell using the R package “AUCell”. All comparisons of AUCs were assessed for significance by the Kruskal–Wallis test.

The keyword “immune activation” was searched in the Gene Ontology (GO) database, and the gene sets were downloaded. Enrichment scores were calculated by single‐sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) and subsequently correlated with the AUC values of immune‐related lncRNAs via Pearson correlation analysis.

Expression and Functional Analysis of Immune‐Related lncRNAs in Immune Cells of LUAD Patients

We collected three independent non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) single‐cell RNA‐seq datasets, including LUAD (GSE171145) and two additional NSCLC datasets (GSE127465 and GSE179373). All datasets were processed using the R package “Seurat” for quality control, cell filtering, and cell type annotation. For the LUAD dataset (GSE171145), cell types were identified using the following marker genes: mast cells (MS4A2, CPA3, TPSB2), neutrophils (S100A9, CSF3R, FCGR3B), T cells (CD3D, CD3E, CD8A), B cells (CD19, CD79A, MS4A1), plasma cells (IGHG1, MZB1, SDC1, JCHAIN), and NK cells (FGFBP2, CX3CR1). For the additional NSCLC datasets (GSE127465 and GSE179373), cell type annotations were directly obtained from the Tumor Immune Single‐cell Hub (TISCH) database (http://tisch.comp‐genomics.org/). Enrichment scores for each cell were calculated using ssGSEA in both the GSE171145 and GSE127465 datasets. For comparisons among the three types of lncRNAs, significance was assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. For comparisons between two groups, the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was applied.

For further classification of CD8+ T cells across both the GSE171145 and GSE179373 datasets, the following marker genes were used: Tn (CCR7, LEF1, SELL, TCF7), TCM (GPR183), TRM (CD6, ZNF683), TEM (GZMK), and Tex (LAYN). The R package “monocle” was used to infer the pseudotime trajectories of CD8+ T cells [29]. The relationship between CIlncRNAs differentially expressed along pseudotime and all mRNAs was evaluated using Pearson correlation analysis. Functional enrichment analysis was performed for the correlated mRNAs (r > 0.7 and p < 0.01) via the R package “clusterProfiler”. Pearson correlation analysis was performed between established CD8+ T cell activation markers and the CIlncRNAs showing pseudotime‐dependent expression changes shared across the two single‐cell datasets.

Calculation and Validation of the CIlncRNA Signature

We first performed univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses to identify CIlncRNAs associated with OS in 19 cancers. Next, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and stepwise multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression were used to construct the prognostic model. CIsig was constructed by weighting the expression of these CIlncRNAs with their corresponding multivariate Cox regression coefficients. Survival differences between the two groups divided according to the median CIsig were evaluated using log‐rank test. Five independent datasets from different regions (GSE31210, GSE20685, GSE48390, GSE58812, and GSE41613) were obtained. The corresponding cancer‐specific models were applied to calculate CIsig scores for each sample. Optimal cut‐off values were determined using the “surv_cutpoint” function, and patients were stratified into high‐ and low‐risk groups. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis with the log‐rank test was then performed to evaluate differences in OS between the risk groups. We constructed nomograms using the R package “rms” and evaluated the predictive ability of the nomograms by calibration curves.

Prediction of Immunotherapy Response by CIlncRNAs

We used the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE, http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/) website to predict immunotherapy response in the TCGA‐LUAD cohort [30]. LASSO regression was used to select more important OS‐related CIlncRNAs when the number of CIlncRNAs was excessive. Patients were randomly divided into training and test sets. Logistic regression and support vector machine (SVM) models were then constructed using the training set to predict immunotherapy response. The SVM model was trained using the R package “e1071”.

We further evaluated the predictive performance of the SVM model using OS‐related CIlncRNAs in an independent NSCLC cohort (GSE135222) and anESCA cohort (GSE165252) treated with ICB.

Results

Identification and Multi‐Level Validation of Immune‐Related lncRNAs

We reannotated the 19 types of immune cell lines to obtain the expression profiles of 5556 lncRNAs and then extracted the expression profiles of the top 5% most highly expressed lncRNAs for each immune cell line. LncRNAs upregulated in immune cell lines but downregulated in 19 types of cancer cell lines from CCLE (FDR < 0.05, logFC > 1) were identified as candidate immune‐related lncRNAs. The candidate immune‐related lncRNAs identified across the 19 cancer types are listed in Table S2. Next, the association scores and p‐values between candidate immune‐related lncRNAs and immune pathway activity for each of the 19 cancer types were calculated using TCGA data. The number of immune pathways significantly associated with each lncRNA was calculated, with a peak observed between 6 and 11 pathways, corresponding to the middle range of the observed values. Based on this observation, thresholds of 6 and 11 were selected to classify lncRNAs into three types—SIlncRNAs (≤ 6), MIlncRNAs (6–11), and CIlncRNAs (≥ 11)—in each cancer type (Figure 1A, Figure S1A). The similarity between classifications obtained by changing the thresholds and the original classification was assessed (Figure S1B). When the thresholds were increased or decreased by one, the classification results changed minimally (ARI > 0.8), indicating the stability of the chosen thresholds, while larger adjustments led to more pronounced changes, highlighting the influence of threshold selection on lncRNA categorization (Figure S1C). The 10‐fold cross‐validation results, in which nearly all ARI values exceeded 0.5, further confirmed the reproducibility of our approach under sample perturbations (Figure S1D).

As a preliminary validation, we tested whether the classification of lncRNAs was consistent across different datasets of the same cancer type. The association scores and p‐values between candidate immune‐related lncRNAs and immune pathway activity were recalculated in two independent datasets, LUAD and PRAD, and the lncRNAs were reclassified. For each cancer type, lncRNA types showed significant overlap across datasets (Figure 1B,C, hypergeometric test, p < 0.001 for LUAD and PRAD). Next, we calculated the proportion of candidate immune‐related lncRNAs and remaining lncRNAs co‐occurring with “immune” in PubMed. We found a significantly greater proportion of candidate immune‐related lncRNAs (Figure 1D, chi‐square test, p < 0.001). Similarly, there was a significantly greater proportion of candidate immune‐related lncRNAs in the Lnc2Cancer (chi‐square test, p = 0.0417) and LncRNADisease (chi‐square test, p = 0.013) databases (Figure 1E,F). The results confirm that candidate immune‐related lncRNAs are accurately classified and are closely associated with immunity and disease.

We compared copy number gains and losses, somatic mutations, and correlations with methylation for the three types of lncRNAs across 19 cancer types. The analysis revealed significant variations in these genomic and epigenetic features across the lncRNA types in several cancers (Figure 1G). In COAD, copy number gains, somatic mutations, and methylation correlations were significantly different (Figure 1H–K). Overall, the three types of immune‐related lncRNAs differed significantly in terms of genomic variation and methylation, suggesting that they may differ in the extent of their influence on disease progression.

Diverse Immunomodulation of Immune‐Related lncRNAs Among Cancers

To compare immune‐associated lncRNAs between cancers, we focused on the different classifications of each lncRNA in the 19 cancers and found that there was diversity (Figure 2A). For each lncRNA, we counted the number of cancers in which it was classified into each type and the number in which it showed significant associations with each immune‐related pathway. We also calculated the difference (maximum‐median‐minimum) in the number of cancers for the three lncRNA types. A higher diff indicates that the lncRNA is more uniformly categorized across the 19 cancers. We focused on the top 20% of lncRNAs, of which more than half were classified as CIlncRNAs across various cancers, and these lncRNAs were significantly associated with immune‐related pathways in more cancer types (Figure S2).

Specific Immune Cell Infiltration Patterns of CIlncRNAs

To identify which type of immune‐related lncRNA plays a more significant role in immune regulation, we assessed the correlation between the expression of all lncRNAs and immune cell infiltration using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (|r| > 0.3, p < 0.05). Immune cell infiltration data were obtained from TIMER 2.0 (TIMER and CIBERSORT algorithms) and ESTIMATE. Among all lncRNAs associated with immune infiltration (TIMER), CIlncRNAs were more numerous across most cancers. Their proportion associated with immune infiltration was significantly higher among the six immune cell types (Figure 3A, Figure S3, Kruskal–Wallis test). In addition, we focused on the six immune cell types that overlapped according to the TIMER and CIBERSORT algorithms and found that the proportion of immune‐related lncRNAs associated with these immune cell infiltrations was higher in most cancers (Figure 3B, chi‐square test). Similarly, CIlncRNAs exhibited the strongest associations with ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and TumorPurity (Figure 3C–E, Kruskal–Wallis test). In five cancer types (HNSC, ESCA, STAD, COAD, and READ), the Shannon diversity indices of tumor‐infiltrating microbiota were calculated and correlated with CIlncRNA expression. Several CIlncRNAs showed significant associations with microbial alpha diversity, indicating a potential association between CIlncRNAs and the heterogeneity of intratumoral microbiota (Figure S4). These findings indicate that CIlncRNAs exhibit a stronger correlation with immune cell infiltration compared to other types of immune‐related lncRNAs and are significantly associated with intratumoral microbial heterogeneity, highlighting their distinct immune‐related profiles and potential significance within the tumor immune microenvironment.

Next, we performed consensus clustering for TCGA‐LUAD patients by CIlncRNAs. Based on CIlncRNA expression, we categorized LUAD patients into two subgroups (C1 and C2), and C1 had better OS (Figure 3F,G). Using the TIMER, CIBERSORT, and ESTIMATE algorithms, we found that C1 tumors exhibited increased immune cell infiltration (Figure 3H, Figure S5). The expression of immune checkpoint genes, which influence immune responses and promote tumor immune evasion, is significantly correlated with the therapeutic response to ICB. Our analysis revealed that C1 showed significantly high expression of ICI genes—PDCD1 (PD‐1), CD274 (PD‐L1), CTLA4, and LAG3—compared to C2, and this group also had higher IPS, MHC, and CYT scores (Figure 3I). This suggested that C1 might have a better outcome after receiving ICB therapy. In summary, the subtypes identified based on CIlncRNAs exhibited distinct survival outcomes and immune checkpoint gene expression, highlighting their potential in predicting prognosis and immunotherapy response.

Expression of Three Types of Immune‐Related lncRNAs Within the Pan‐Cancer Tumor Microenvironment and Their Regulation of Immune Cell Activation

To investigate the expression of immune‐related lncRNAs in the pan‐cancer tumor microenvironment, we used three types of immune‐related lncRNAs as gene sets to calculate the enrichment scores for six types of cells via AUCell (Figure 4A,B). Immune‐related lncRNAs exhibited higher AUC values in immune cells (lymphocyte, myeloid, plasma cells) compared to non‐immune cells (endothelial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblast), and CIlncRNAs showed the highest AUC values among the three lncRNA types in immune cells (Figure 4C–F). When analyzed separately for each cancer type, similar trends were observed in most cancers, with CIlncRNAs exhibiting higher AUC values in immune cells (Figure 4G). These findings highlight the potential importance of CIlncRNAs in the immune microenvironment.

The activation of immune cells is critical for initiating and maintaining effective immune responses against tumors. To investigate the relationships between the three types of immune‐related lncRNAs and immune cell activation, we collected gene sets related to immune cell activation from the GO database. The AUC values of immune‐related lncRNAs were positively correlated with the ssGSEA score of GO:0002276 (basophil activation involved in immune response) and negatively correlated with GO:0002275 (myeloid cell activation involved in immune response) and GO:0002366 (leukocyte activation involved in immune response) (Figure 4H). These findings highlight the potential regulatory role of immune‐related lncRNAs in the activation of specific immune cell types, with CIlncRNAs exhibiting the strongest association. In conclusion, the expression pattern of CIlncRNAs in the tumor immune microenvironment highlights their crucial role in modulating immune responses, particularly in the activation of immune cells.

CIlncRNAs Were Associated With CD8 + T Cell Activation Within the LUAD Tumor Microenvironment

Building on the pan‐cancer analysis, we focused on LUAD to further explore the expression and regulation of immune‐related lncRNAs in its tumor immune microenvironment. To this end, ssGSEA was employed to calculate enrichment scores for the three types of lncRNAs in immune cells based on an independent LUAD single‐cell dataset, providing additional validation through a different analytical approach and dataset (Figure 5A–D). We found that the ssGSEA score was significantly higher for CIlncRNAs compared to MIlncRNAs or SIlncRNAs (Figure 5E, Kruskal–Wallis test, p < 0.001). CIlncRNAs exhibited relatively high expression levels, indicating their prominent presence within the LUAD tumor immune microenvironment, consistent with observations from the pan‐cancer analysis. In addition, the high enrichment scores of CIlncRNAs in immune cells within the GSE127465 dataset further corroborated their elevated expression in immune cell populations (Figure S6A–D).

Cytotoxic CD8+ T cells are central to immune surveillance, effectively targeting and eliminating intracellular pathogens and tumor cells. Based on the correlation between CIlncRNAs and immune cell activation revealed by the pan‐cancer analysis, we further investigated the regulation of CD8+ T cell activation by CIlncRNAs in LUAD. CD8+ T cells were extracted and further annotated (Figure S6G, Figure 5F). The pseudotime analysis revealed a trajectory that was consistent with the known differentiation pathway of CD8+ T cells (Figure 5G,H). CIlncRNAs with significant differences in expression among pseudotimes were clustered into three types, each highly expressed at different CD8+ T cell differentiation stages. (Figure 5I). Notably, HCP5 and MIR155HG, which were highly expressed during the late stages of differentiation, also showed relatively high expression in exhausted CD8+ T cells in the GSE127465 dataset (Figure S6E,F). The mRNAs associated with these differential CIlncRNAs (correlation > 0.7, p < 0.01) were mainly involved in biological functions related to T cell activation, including regulation of T cell activation, T cell receptor signaling, and Th1/Th2 cell differentiation (Figure 5J,K). In addition, two CIlncRNAs were reported to be closely associated with CD8+ T cells. The lncRNA SNHG1 promotes the establishment of memory CD8+ T cells, and the XIST/miR‐34a‐5p/PD‐L1 axis contributes to lung cancer progression and influences CD8+ T cell immune function [31, 32]. To further validate the expression patterns observed in GSE171145, pseudotime analysis was subsequently performed on CD8+ T cells from the independent dataset GSE179373. Several CIlncRNAs exhibited expression changes along pseudotime during CD8+ T cell differentiation, consistent with the patterns observed in GSE171145 (Figure S6H–K). Notably, MIR155HG and TRG–AS1 showed significant positive correlations with established markers of CD8+ T cell activation, providing additional evidence for the potential role of specific CIlncRNAs in CD8+ T cell activation (Figure S7). In summary, the dynamic expression patterns of CIlncRNAs during CD8+ T cell differentiation in LUAD reflect their linkage to T cell developmental processes. Their association with biological processes related to T cell activation further supports their functional relevance and further validates their potential as reliable markers of immune activity.

CIlncRNAs Could Be Used to Predict Patient Prognosis

To investigate whether CIlncRNAs could be used as prognostic markers, we performed univariate Cox regression to assess the relationship between OS and the expression of immune‐related lncRNAs across 19 cancer types. Analysis indicated that the number of CIlncRNAs correlated with OS was the highest among these cancers (Figure 6A). Kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) had the largest number of immune‐related lncRNAs associated with OS (Figure 6B). In addition, we summarized the number of lncRNAs associated with OS across different numbers of cancer types and found that the number of OS‐related lncRNAs in three cancer types was the highest (Figure 6C).

We then calculated CIsig score for each cancer based on CIlncRNAs and further divided patients into high‐ and low‐risk groups according to the median value (Table S3). This revealed significant differences in OS between the groups across 14 cancer types (Figure 6D). In addition, across five independent cohorts from other regions, patients in the low‐risk groups consistently exhibited significantly longer OS than those in the high‐risk groups, further demonstrating the robustness and generalizability of the CIsig (Figure S8). We performed further analysis for LUAD, and 10 CIlncRNAs were included in the CIsig, of which LINC00324 was associated with OS in eight cancers (Figure 6E,F). Patients belonging to the low‐risk group experienced longer OS compared with the high‐risk group (Figure 6G, log‐rank test, p < 0.001). The AUC values for predicting survival at 1, 3, and 5 years postoperatively were 0.717, 0.686, and 0.684, respectively (Figure 6H). Based on CIsig and clinical variables including age, sex, stage, and TNM, nomograms were established to predict OS probabilities at 1, 3, and 5 years in LUAD patients (Figure 6I). The calibration curves at 1, 3, and 5 years showed that the predicted survival probability of the nomogram deviated little from the actual survival probability (Figure 6J,K, Figure S9). We also found that the low‐risk group had higher expression of ICI genes and higher IPS and CYT scores (Figure 6L). In addition, we compared the expression of ICI genes across 19 cancer types and found that the low‐risk group generally exhibited higher ICI gene expression (Figure S10). Overall, our results confirm the strong prognostic value of CIlncRNAs across cancers.

CIlncRNAs Could Be Used to Predict Immunotherapy Response

To investigate whether CIlncRNAs could predict immunotherapy response, we applied LASSO regression to select 29 CIlncRNAs associated with immunotherapy response from OS‐related CIlncRNAs in the TCGA‐LUAD cohort (Figure 7A,B). Among the models constructed based on these CIlncRNAs, SVM demonstrated higher AUC values than logistic regression on the test set and was thus selected as the final model (Figure 7C). We found that CIlncRNAs exhibited a higher AUC compared to ICIs and TMB (Figure 7D). The AUC values of the ICI gene and TMB improved after the addition of CIlncRNAs, and the combination of CIlncRNAs with these factors produced the greatest predictive effect (Figure 7E–G). The patients who were predicted to respond to immunotherapy based on CIlncRNAs had better OS (Figure 7H, log‐rank test, p = 0.00069). We further validated the predictive value of CIlncRNAs in two ICB‐treated cohorts with NSCLC (GSE135222) and ESCA (GSE165252). In these two datasets, 39 and 6 OS‐related CIlncRNAs were identified and subsequently used to construct SVM models, respectively. In the GSE135222 dataset, CIlncRNAs showed the best predictive effect and enhanced the prediction ability of PDCD1 and CD274 (Figure 7I). In the GSE165252 dataset, although CIlncRNAs had lower AUC values than PDCD1, they still improved the AUC values of both PDCD1 and CD274 (Figure 7J). These results highlight the potential of CIlncRNAs as powerful predictors of immunotherapy response, enhancing the predictive accuracy of established biomarkers.

Discussion

Growing evidence points to lncRNAs as crucial regulators of immune processes in the tumor microenvironment, highlighting their potential impact on tumor immunology. Meanwhile, immune pathways actively regulate essential biological processes within the immune system. In this study, an algorithm was developed to classify candidate immune‐related lncRNAs into different types according to the breadth of their associations with immune‐related pathways. The results suggested that there were significant differences in the genomic variation of the three types of immune‐related lncRNAs and their correlation with methylation. CIlncRNAs were more strongly correlated with immune infiltration and were more highly expressed in immune cells. CIlncRNAs are involved in the differentiation of CD8+ T cells in LUAD and serve as potential predictors of patient prognosis and response to immunotherapy.

Genomic variants have been widely recognized as key contributors to cancer development. For example, the accumulation of somatic mutations can lead to cancer, and CNV can promote cancer initiation, progression and therapeutic resistance [33, 34, 35]. Methylation is a crucial form of epigenetic regulation, involving the transfer of a methyl group to specific targets, a process catalyzed by methyltransferases. This modification does not alter the underlying DNA sequence, but it can significantly affect gene expression and cellular function [36]. Methylation deregulation is involved in many diseases, including human cancers [37]. CNVs, somatic mutations and correlations with methylation of three types of immune‐related lncRNAs were compared in pan‐cancer. Among them, there were significant differences in CNVs, somatic mutations and correlations with methylation in COAD patients. CIlncRNAs exhibited the highest proportion of copy number gain, and their expression levels were strongly associated with DNA methylation levels. In addition, all three types of immune‐related lncRNAs had a greater proportion of somatic mutations, among which the proportion of MIlncRNAs was the highest. This suggested that the three types of immune‐related lncRNAs may differ in their extent of influence on disease progression, with CIlncRNAs possibly having the most significant effects.

The relationship between the immune system and tumors is currently one of the most studied topics in the field of cancer. Immune cells within the tumor microenvironment play diverse roles in regulating tumor progression. Although immune cells can normally recognize and eliminate nascent tumor cells through a process known as cancer immunosurveillance, their function can be altered by various factors in the presence of the tumor, a phenomenon referred to as cancer immunoediting. Immune cells can be both anti‐tumor immune and pro‐tumor immune [38]. Moreover, intratumoral microbiota play an important role in shaping the tumor microenvironment by modulating immune and metabolic programs, thereby influencing tumor progression [39, 40]. LncRNAs are increasingly recognized as pivotal regulators of immune responses, playing an integral role in the initiation, progression, and immune evasion of tumors. Although immune‐related lncRNAs have great potential, only a limited number have been shown to influence cancer. We compared the correlations between three types of lncRNAs and immune cell infiltration, finding that CIlncRNAs were more strongly associated, highlighting their key role in immune regulation and cancer progression. Notably, CIlncRNAs were highly expressed in immune cells and exhibited potential involvement in CD8+ T cell activation, suggesting a possible functional link to T‐cell‐mediated anti‐tumor immunity. Moreover, the observed associations between certain CIlncRNAs and microbial alpha diversity in five cancer types highlight the potential interplay between CIlncRNAs and tumor‐infiltrating microbiota. Subgroup C1, based on consistent clustering of CIlncRNAs, had greater immune cell infiltration, expression of ICI genes, IPS, MHC, and CYT scores and a better prognosis. Together, these findings underscore the significant association of CIlncRNAs with the tumor immune microenvironment and microbial heterogeneity, supporting their potential utility as biomarkers for predicting patient prognosis and immunotherapy response.

The prognosis of patients has long been a major focus in cancer treatment, and multi‐omics advances have led to the identification of numerous biomarkers with promising clinical utility. Recent studies have highlighted lncRNAs as essential components of genomic regulatory networks, with growing evidence showing their significant involvement in the development and activation of immune cells. Accumulating studies over the last decade have highlighted the crucial roles of lncRNAs in cancer diagnosis and therapeutic development. Predicting the prognosis of patients based on lncRNAs will contribute to rapid diagnosis and accurate screening of cancer patients. In our study, the CISig, which was constructed based on CIlncRNAs, had a good ability to predict prognosis in pan‐cancer. The CISig constructed in LUAD contained 10 CIlncRNAs, with LINC00324 associated with OS in 8 cancers. LINC00324 has been reported to be associated with prognosis in various cancers and is involved in the construction of several prognostic markers for LUAD. The low‐risk group had better prognosis and higher ICI gene expression, IPS, and CYT scores, which are associated with enhanced T cell activity and a stronger immune response. Higher ICI gene expression and IPS scores reflect increased T cell activation and immune system responsiveness, while elevated CYT scores indicate stronger cytotoxic T cell activity. These factors collectively suggest that the tumor microenvironment in the low‐risk group is more favorable for immune‐mediated tumor control, potentially contributing to improved overall prognosis. These results highlight the significant prognostic value of CIlncRNAs, emphasizing their potential as robust biomarkers for predicting patient prognosis.

During cancer development and progression, tumors continuously evolve and employ diverse strategies to escape immune surveillance and suppress anti‐tumor immune responses [41]. A key mechanism by which tumors evade immune surveillance is through the engagement of immune checkpoint pathways. Blocking ICPs is widely recognized as an effective approach for reactivating anti‐tumor immunity [42]. Among ICB therapies, antibodies targeting CTLA‐4, PD‐1, and PD‐L1 represent the most extensively explored and clinically validated approaches in cancer immunotherapy [43]. However, only a subset of patients benefits from these treatments, making the identification of reliable biomarkers to predict immunotherapy response a critical need in cancer treatment. LncRNAs have been increasingly recognized as promising biomarkers for predicting immunotherapy response due to their regulatory roles in immune processes and cancer progression. Although detecting lncRNAs requires relatively high sequencing depth to ensure sufficient sensitivity, advances in high‐throughput sequencing have significantly reduced the cost per sample. In addition, lncRNA sequencing can simultaneously capture mRNA information, providing a more comprehensive molecular profile from the same dataset. We validated the potential of CIlncRNAs in predicting immunotherapy response based on the TCGA‐LUAD cohort and two datasets from patients treated with ICB. CIlncRNAs had good performance in all three datasets and were able to improve the predictive efficacy of well‐known markers. The core advantage of CIlncRNAs lies not only in surpassing existing biomarkers but also in their use alongside them to provide more accurate predictions. Traditional markers such as PD‐L1 expression and TMB often offer valuable but imperfect predictive information, and CIlncRNAs effectively complement these limitations. These results suggest that CIlncRNAs with broader associations with immune pathway activity have the potential to serve as reliable biomarkers for predicting patients' responses to immunotherapy, thereby facilitating more personalized and effective treatment strategies.

A key limitation of this study is the lack of experimental validation. Although our analyses provide valuable insights into the potential roles of long non‐coding RNAs in immune cell regulation and CD8+ T cell differentiation, they do not extend to functional or mechanistic confirmation, which requires further experimental investigation. Looking forward, CIlncRNAs merit further investigation in multiple areas. Among these, immune‐related adverse events represent a major clinical challenge accompanying immune checkpoint blockade, substantially affecting treatment decisions [44]. Given their associations with immune pathways and potential role in predicting immunotherapy response, CIlncRNAs warrant future study as indicators for patients at high risk of immune‐related adverse events. Beyond immunotherapy, drug discovery is increasingly driven by multi‐omics technologies, including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics [45, 46]. Integrating these approaches with CIlncRNA‐based models could further refine predictive frameworks and accelerate the identification and validation of novel drug targets and combination therapies in cancer treatment.

In summary, we classified immune‐related lncRNAs across cancers based on the breadth of their associations with immune pathway activity. We compared the three types of immune‐related lncRNAs from multiple aspects and ultimately identified a unique group of lncRNAs–CIlncRNAs. CIlncRNAs are broadly associated with immune pathway activity, exhibiting strong correlations with immune regulation and robust predictive potential for patient prognosis and immunotherapy response. By focusing on the associations between lncRNAs and immune pathway activity, our study offers new perspectives for identifying potential signatures predictive of patient prognosis and immunotherapy response.
