ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveStatus epilepticus (SE) treatment is more effective when benzodiazepines (BZDs) are given soon after SE diagnosis. The Quality Improvement in Time to Treat Status Epilepticus (QuITT‐SE) trial is a multicenter, randomized, stepped‐wedge effectiveness‐implementation hybrid study aimed at improving time to SE treatment. We report the QuITT‐SE baseline cohort and test the hypothesis that acute seizure emergency episodes treated promptly differ in clinical and demographic features from those with delayed treatment.MethodsData were summarized from 371 consecutive acute seizures episodes treated with a BZD in the inpatient, non‐intensive care unit (ICU) setting from 207 individual patients across eight centers per the QuITT‐SE protocol. To test our hypothesis, we utilized a multivariable negative binomial mixed effects model with random intercepts to estimate the incidence rate ratios for variables of interest for time to BZD treatment.ResultsThe median age was 7 years (IQR = 3–12). Nonelective ICU transfers occurred with 26% of episodes, including 13% within 6 h of the episode. The median time from seizure onset to BZD treatment was 6 min (IQR = 4–10), with 74% of all episodes and 63% of SE treated within 10 min. BZD administration was faster when nurses made the treatment decision, when the intranasal (IN) route was used, when the patient received prior rescue medication within 24 h, or with certain elective admission types. Seizures were shorter when BZD administration was faster or when treatment was with IN midazolam versus intravenous lorazepam (median = 8 min [IQR = 6–15] vs. 10 min [IQR = 7–17], p = .04). Patients were less likely to be transferred to the ICU if they received IN midazolam (odds ratio = .3, p = .007).SignificanceDelayed BZD administration occurs in one third of SE treatment. Prolonged seizures and ICU transfers are associated with modifiable risk factors. Baseline drivers of delayed acute seizure treatment may be addressed using the QuITT‐SE interventions, the study of which is currently ongoing.

INTRODUCTION

Treatment for status epilepticus (SE), or life‐threatening prolonged seizures, is more effective when benzodiazepines (BZDs) are given rapidly upon diagnosis of SE. 1 Children with refractory SE treated >10 min after seizure onset are 11 times more likely to die during their hospitalization than patients with earlier treatment. 1 Despite specialty guideline recommendations recommending rapid treatment, 2 treatment often remains delayed. 3 , 4

The ideal path to improving BZD administration times is unclear. Merely disseminating SE protocols does not appear to improve overall seizure times but may improve time to second‐line treatment. 5 Results from single center studies of specialized response teams are mixed, with some showing faster treatment with second‐ or third‐line medications, 6 , 7 whereas another demonstrated improved BZD treatment times when the bedside nurse treated without a specialized team. 8 Route of administration may have a more definitive effect on treatment times. In a prehospital randomized clinical trial, intramuscular midazolam was faster to administer than intravenous (IV) lorazepam and was noninferior in time to seizure cessation despite a more prolonged onset of action. 9 A single‐center quality improvement study utilized several interventions together and decreased BZD treatment times, need for second‐line therapies, intensive care unit (ICU) transfers, and hospital charges. 8

The Quality Improvement in Time to Treat Status Epilepticus (QuITT‐SE) multicenter, randomized, stepped‐wedge clinical trial is an effectiveness‐implementation hybrid study developed to study methods to improve time to SE treatment with BZD across multiple centers (NCT06194747). 10 It utilizes a bundle of pragmatic process changes across eight US hospitals to improve the speed with which a BZD can be administered in the hospital for acute seizure emergencies, including SE. 8 Here, we describe the characteristics of the QuITT‐SE baseline cohort and test the hypothesis that acute seizure episodes treated more rapidly will have different characteristics—including the role of the person making the decision to treat, age, previous treatments, and route of BZD administration—compared to those treated more slowly prior to implementation of trial interventions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

In this ambispective continuous cohort study, data were extracted from the electronic health record (EHR). This study was a multicenter baseline observational cohort study performed at eight sites prior to the intervention step in the parent QuITT‐SE clinical trial that was ongoing at the time of submission. Implementation research and quality improvement science often rely on measurement and feedback to improve adoption and implementation of interventions. Therefore, baseline data from trial sites were evaluated in this cohort study to examine current practice across sites and provide data for local quality improvement teams. This study followed STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines and was approved by the Nationwide Children’s Hospital Institutional Review Board.

Participants

We defined the cohort of interest to include children and adolescents >30 days and <19 years old admitted to a non‐ICU floor who received a BZD for focal or generalized clinical seizures of any duration, excluding children with infantile spasms or those with electrographic‐only seizures without clinical signs other than encephalopathy. Episodes consisting solely of clusters of brief seizures were excluded if consciousness returned to baseline between seizures. We defined acute seizure emergency episodes broadly to include both seizures lasting ≥5 min (SE) as well as seizures of any duration treated emergently with a BZD. This intention‐to‐treat population was necessary to study the implementation of interventions aimed at decreasing time to treatment.

Procedures

Patients were screened if they received a BZD as an inpatient in the defined hospital areas (e.g., inpatient units); episodes occurring in the prehospital setting or in the emergency department were excluded. Acute seizure emergency episodes occurred between July 1, 2023 and the random date between April and November 2024 on which the individual site dissemination visit occurred per the stepped‐wedge design. 10 The primary site did not have an implementation visit, as it served as the control for QuITT‐SE. Thus, episodes from the primary site were included if they occurred between July 1, 2023, and July 13, 2024, which represented the median number of days (377) in the baseline periods for the other seven sites.

Outcomes and statistical methods

The primary outcome was the time (in minutes) from seizure onset to treatment with the first BZD following seizure diagnosis. The secondary outcomes were time from seizure onset to seizure cessation and rate of transfer to the ICU. Cost of hospitalization, change in Pediatric Cerebral Performance Category (PCPC) score, and social drivers of health were exploratory variables. 11 Patient and episode features were summarized with frequency (percentage) for categorical variables and median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables.

The cost of hospitalization was calculated utilizing gross‐costing methods based on Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality guidelines as previously described. 10 Increased cost per day is associated with SE type as follows: (1) nonrefractory SE = $1227.86, (2) refractory SE (RSE) = $2157.96, and (3) superrefractory SE (SRSE) = $2848.95. 12 The total estimated cost of hospitalization was determined by multiplying the estimated cost per day for the type of SE by the length of stay. RSE was defined as SE that continued despite the patient receiving two antiseizure medications (BZD plus one dose of a non‐BZD), and SRSE was defined as continuous or recurrent seizures lasting ≥24 h following anesthetic medication initiation. 13

To test our primary hypothesis, we utilized a multivariable negative binomial mixed effects model with random intercepts by site to estimate the incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for variables of interest for time to BZD treatment. We utilized only those cases with exact times available for the primary analysis, but we also performed sensitivity analyses with the whole cohort as well as with only seizures ≥5 min and exact times. Independent variables included role making the decision to treat (reference: floor nurse), age at admission (years), timeframe of prior rescue medication (reference: 0 to <8 h), reason for admission (reference: acute unplanned), deviation from institution SE pathway (reference: no), route of BZD (reference: IV), onset location (reference: epilepsy monitoring unit [EMU] or neuroscience unit). IRRs > 1 represent slower treatment, and IRRs < 1 represent faster treatment with first BZD.

Spearman correlation was computed between time to treatment with first BZD and seizure duration and visualized via scatterplot. The Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was used to compare two measures of social drivers of health, Childhood Opportunity Index (COI) 14 and Social Vulnerability Index (SVI), 15 by reason for admission, seizure duration by route of first BZD administration, and time to treatment with first BZD by ICU transfer within 6 h of the episode or at any point during the admission. Change in PCPC score was analyzed descriptively. A univariable mixed effects logistic regression model with random intercept by site was fit for any ICU transfer during the admission by route of administration of the first BZD, with estimates presented as odds ratio (OR) with 95% CI. Probability values < .05 were considered statistically significant.

All analyses were performed in R version 4.3 (R Core Team) with reproducible programming in R Markdown.

RESULTS

This continuous cohort study included data from 371 acute seizure emergency episodes treated with a BZD in the inpatient, non‐ICU setting from 207 individuals across eight centers. Forty‐nine percent of patients were female, and 57% were publicly insured. Eighty‐four percent of patients had a preexisting diagnosis of epilepsy, with 56% having focal epilepsy.

Episode and patient characteristics are detailed in Tables 1 and 2. The median patient age at episode was 7 years (IQR = 3–12). Time from seizure start to first BZD administration was a median of 6 min (IQR = 4–10; n = 353), with 26% of all episodes and 37% of SE treated after 10 min. The median time from seizure diagnosis to end was 10 min (IQR = 6–15; n = 315), and relatively few episodes evolved into RSE (5.7%) or SRSE (1.4%). Seizure types were predominantly those with motor signs (n = 325, 87.6%) and also included nonmotor (n = 23, 6.2%), unknown (n = 15, 4.0%), and psychogenic nonepileptic events (PNEEs; n = 8, 2.2%). Thirty‐four percent of SE episodes were preceded by SE within the previous 24 h. The majority (53%) had electroencephalography performed during the episode or started within 12 h.

Nearly half (48%) of episodes occurred in patients with an admission PCPC score of 4 (severe disability). Overall discharge PCPC scores were unchanged but included two deaths. The median cost of hospitalization from prolonged or acute unplanned admissions was $10 605 (IQR = $5807–$21 083). Of note, those admitted electively had higher socioeconomic status compared to those admitted acutely (Table S1). The median COI score was 68 (IQR = 51–79) for elective admissions and 53 (IQR = 25–79) for acute unplanned admissions (p = .001). The median SVI score was .29 (IQR = .15–.51) for elective admissions and .56 (IQR = .38–.71) for acute unplanned admissions (p < .001).

On multivariable modeling from episodes with exact times (n = 183), time to BZD was faster when associated with several factors, such as when the floor nurse made the decision to treat instead of a physician (physician trainee vs. nurse: IRR = 1.64, 95% CI = 1.18–2.29, p = .003; attending physician vs. nurse: IRR = 1.79, 95% CI = 1.24–2.59, p = .002). Treatment was also faster when the patient had received rescue medication within the past 24 h (no rescue medication or unknown vs. 0 to <8 h prior to current episode: IRR = 1.42, 95% CI = 1.08–1.89, p = .013), with certain types of elective admissions (elective epilepsy surgery or other vs. acute unplanned: IRR = .71, 95% CI = .53–.95, p = .023), or when the intranasal (IN) route was used instead of the IV route (IN vs. IV: IRR = .69, 95% CI = .53–.91, p = .007; Table 3). Significant variables were similar to those from the full cohort model (n = 353; Table S2) and to a sensitivity analysis including only cases with a seizure lasting ≥5 min and exact seizures onset times while excluding PNEEs (n = 146; Table S3).

Seizures were shorter when time to BZD was faster (r = .55, p < .001; Figure 1). When comparing median seizure duration between routes, IN medications were associated with shorter seizures (n = 108, 8 min [IQR = 6–15]) compared to IV (n = 235, 10 min [IQR = 7–17]; p = .04). Forty‐seven (13%) episodes were followed by ICU transfer within 6 h, and 96 (26%) episodes were associated with ICU transfers occurring at some point during the admission.

The likelihood of transfer within 6 h was not associated with shorter time to BZD (ICU transfer: median = 7 min [IQR = 4–14]; no ICU transfer: median = 6 min [IQR = 4–10]; p = .9). However, median time to BZD was faster for those episodes during admissions without an ICU transfer at any point (6 min [IQR = 4–9]) compared to those with an ICU transfer (7 min [IQR = 5–12], p = .032). Furthermore, patients were less likely to be transferred to the ICU if they received IN BZD (OR = .3, 95% CI = .13–.72, p = .007) instead of IV BZD.

DISCUSSION

Time to BZD was delayed in one third of all SE episodes, and nearly one in eight episodes resulted in transfer to the ICU within 6 h among 371 consecutive cases from the QuITT‐SE baseline phase. The primary aim of QuITT‐SE is to reduce the interval between seizure diagnosis and BZD administration, and these findings describe the current state and identify generalizable targets for improvement. Several variables were associated with faster treatment or shorter seizure duration.

Shorter seizure duration was strongly correlated with faster BZD administration, similar to previous studies. 1 , 16 In this cohort, time to BZD administration was faster and seizures were shorter through the IN route compared to the IV route. The mechanism for more effective IN treatment may be related to speed of administration or pharmacology‐related mechanisms, such as differences between midazolam and lorazepam pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics, as both medications represent the majority of IN or IV administrations, respectively. Notably, all inpatient medication applications were generic formulations except rectal diazepam gel (Diastat).

When the decision to treat was made by the bedside nurses, treatment was faster. Pretreatment activities and team members vary across US epilepsy centers, and one quarter of center SE protocols require calling a code or specialized team to the bedside for treatment. 17 Although treatment with second‐ or third‐line medications may improve with specialized response teams, 6 , 7 BZD administration is fastest when bedside caregivers are empowered to begin acute seizure treatment before others arrive.

Inpatient BZD treatment times were also faster for specific elective admissions to the EMU or if the patient had received rescue BZD in the previous 24 h. On the contrary, outpatient treatment times do not seem to improve with repetitive episodes. 18 Repetitive episodes in the hospital setting are potentially treated faster due to improved situational awareness of care teams and deployment of acute seizure rescue plans. Electively admitted patients receive faster treatment and have better socioeconomic measures. However, elective admissions at study hospitals often had tailored rescue medication orders for administration 2 min into the seizure after medication weans for phase 1 presurgical testing, for instance. Although tailored orders and staffing differences may play a role in more rapid treatment, potential sociodemographic influences warrant further study.

Only 7% of all episodes (17% of seizures with exact start times lasting ≥5 min) developed into RSE or SRSE, compared to previous reports of 10%–30%. 12 , 19 The lower rate in our cohort is likely secondary to a lower severity of illness at seizure onset due to exclusion of ICU or ambulatory onset and rapid availability of rescue treatment in the hospital. This lower rate may also be explained by care improvements over time or lower selection bias and broader enrollment and capture rate in our cohort, which included seizures treated within 5 min. Therefore, it is also possible that the low incidence of RSE or SRSE is due to more rapid treatment, as the percentage of episodes treated after 10 min was 26% in our baseline cohort compared to historical rates of up to 60%. 3

The ICU transfer rate was 26% for any point during the hospitalization and 13% within 6 h of the episode. This rate is lower than that reported in a recent survey of 341 US epilepsy center directors, which reported that 43% of SE episodes resulted in transfer to the ICU. 20 Interestingly, only 7% of episodes were RSE or SRSE and 1.9% in our baseline cohort required intubation. This implies the reasons for transfer extended beyond respiratory failure and need for infusion therapy. We anticipate data from the prospective QuITT‐SE cohort will be informative as to the drivers for ICU transfer.

The primary limitations of this multicenter, continuous cohort study are related to the use of the EHR as a primary data source. The ambispective data were primarily collected prospectively, although several months of data were retrospectively collected. Importantly, rare missing data were supplemented by reviewing cases directly with the primary clinical caregivers and enrollment bias was mitigated by using a continuous cohort of all eligible cases. Another limitation is that nearly half of times were estimated but were within a few minutes of treatment. However, significant variables from models including either the full dataset or only exact times were nearly identical.

SE treatment remains delayed in one third of cases with inpatient onset of SE, resulting in additional morbidity and cost. Notably, baseline data highlight several modifiable factors that contribute to delayed treatment. These factors are directly targeted interventions in the QuITT‐SE study, which was designed to rigorously measure the impact of these changes and establish a framework for broader implementation across health care centers. 10
