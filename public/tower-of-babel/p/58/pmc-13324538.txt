Simvastatin Protects the Dopaminergic Neurons from MPTP-Mediated Injury by Inducing BDNF via PPARα Activation
Authors: Ganesh Bohara, Nikesh Rimal, Shristi Khanal, Pil-Hoon Park, Dong-Young Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disease characterized by preferential loss of the dopaminergic neurons in the nigrostriatal pathway. The specific molecular mechanisms for the neuronal loss remain unclear, and no cure currently exists. BDNF is a neurotrophic factor that restores the dopaminergic neurons in variant PD models. Emerging evidence suggests that simvastatin could upregulate BDNF in the brain. This study aimed to assess the neuroprotective effect of simvastatin and delineate its action mechanism. We found that simvastatin rescued the SH-SY5Y cells from MPP+ toxicity at 10 nM by upregulating BDNF and activating its downstream signaling pathways, and the protective action was significantly counteracted by BDNF siRNA or TrkB antagonist (K252a). Importantly, simvastatin upregulated BDNF via peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα) activation, as silencing the PPARα gene decreased the simvastatin-mediated BDNF upregulation and the cell viability upon exposure to MPP+. Next, we evaluated its neuroprotective effects in the PD animal model mediated by MPTP. Pretreatment with simvastatin for 2 weeks attenuated the MPTP-caused loss of the nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons as determined by immunohistochemical staining for tyrosine hydroxylase. In parallel with the results, simvastatin ameliorated the decrease in the striatal dopamine level and behavioral impairment elicited by the neurotoxicant. Consistent with the in vitro study, simvastatin upregulated BDNF expression and restored the BDNF level in the MPTP-treated striatonigral regions. These findings suggest that simvastatin’s neuroprotection may be linked to the upregulation of BDNF via activation of PPARα, and it could be a possible therapeutic intervention for the treatment of PD.Keywords: Parkinson’s disease, Simvastatin, MPTP, BDNF, PPARα

INTRODUCTION

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disease after Alzheimer’s disease. Dopaminergic neuronal loss inside the substantia nigra and the development of a Lewy body, an intracytoplasmic, and proteinaceous inclusion are cardinal neuropathological characteristics of PD (Shulman et al., 2011). The disease involves typical motor symptoms such as resting tremors, rigidity, bradykinesia, and postural instability (Harris, 2012), and non-motor symptoms such as constipation, mood changes, and sleep problems. Currently, no medication is approved to stop or slow the progression of neurodegeneration in patients with PD. The treatment for PD focuses on relieving symptoms including movement-related and non-movement-related such as tremors, stiffness, slow movement, constipation and mood changes. This is because the degeneration and dysfunction of dopamine-producing neurons in the brain are the main causes of most symptoms in PD (Aminoff, 1994; Poewe et al., 2017). To address this, researchers are actively pursuing the development of comprehensive neuroprotective therapies aimed at preserving brain function and integrity (Henchcliffe and Severt, 2011; Rimal et al., 2025).

Research on neurotrophic factors as potential therapies for neurodegenerative illnesses is vast (Kelly et al., 2015). Neurotrophic factors bind to tropomyosin-related kinase receptor type (Trk) receptors a group of secreted peptides that have an indispensable role in neuronal growth, proliferation, survival, and protection in both central and peripheral neuronal cells (Olanow et al., 2003). Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) promotes neuronal development and survival by binding to TrkB and p75NTR receptors (Nagahara and Tuszynski, 2011). TrkB/MAPK/ERK1/2, PI3K/AKT pathway through BDNF activation results in neuroprotective effect, mitigating apoptosis, glutamate, and nitric oxide neurotoxicity as well as cell damage arising from oxidative stress which is elevated in PD and other neurological disorders (Andero et al., 2014; Numakawa et al., 2010).

Local infusions of BDNF in the striatum increased spontaneous electrical activity of midbrain DA neurons, increased DA turnover, and increased activity-dependent DA release. It prevents neuron loss and holds promise for treating PD, showing significant potential in safeguarding and rejuvenating dopamine neurons in an animal (Sullivan and O’Keeffe, 2016). However, the oral intake of BDNF is hindered by its limited bioavailability, posing difficulty in reaching the specific brain region (Wu et al., 2014).

Simvastatin, a 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitor, reduces cholesterol levels and is commonly prescribed medications for people with hypercholesterolemia (Pedersen et al., 1996). In several studies, simvastatin exhibited neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory properties (Segatto et al., 2014). The drug inhibits p21ras activation, suppresses NF-kB activation, and lowers the expression of pro-inflammatory markers in an acute MPTP model of PD (Ghosh et al., 2009). It exhibited neuroprotective effects by enhancing cerebrovascular injury following ischemia-reperfusion by reducing apoptosis through the MyD88/TRIF signaling pathway (Chen et al., 2018). In addition, it suppresses neuronal cell death and enhances locomotor recovery via activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway following spinal cord damage (Gao et al., 2016). The drug mitigates spatial memory decline by inhibiting both microgliosis and apoptotic cell death, providing protection against ethanol-induced neurotoxicity in the developing rat hippocampus (Jafari et al., 2021). Further, simvastatin as a lipophilic drug, readily crosses the BBB and shows several effects on the central nervous system by various mechanisms (Vuu et al., 2023).

Recent research has suggested that statins may interact with nuclear transcription factors like peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα) to provide neuroprotective effects through their anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties (Bedi et al., 2016). This has been demonstrated in various research models and has been supported by clinical trials and in vitro investigations, which have shown that both statins and PPARα agonists can regulate inflammatory response genes (Esposito et al., 2012). PPARα plays a role in the regulation of genes that produce proteins involved in maintaining the balance of glutamate and in cholinergic/dopaminergic transmission. In neurodegenerative and neurodevelopmental conditions, specific activators of PPARα may be able to improve brain cell metabolism and cognitive function. Moreover, statins binding to PPARα have been found to boost neurotrophic factors and memory in an animal model of Alzheimer’s disease (Roy et al., 2015).

Here, we examined the neuroprotective efficacy of simvastatin against the Parkinsonian neurotoxin 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1, 2, 3, 6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP) induced neuronal injury. We demonstrated that simvastatin protects the nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons from MPTP-induced toxicity by upregulating BDNF in the brain.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

SH-SY5Y cell culture

SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells were kindly provided by Professor Jeong (College of Pharmacy, Keimyung University, Korea). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s media (#SH30243.01, Hyclone Laboratories, South Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (#SH30084.03, Queensland, Australia) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (#SV30010, Hyclone Laboratories, South Logan, UT, USA) on culture dishes and kept at 37°C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2. When the cells reached a confluence of 80-90%, they were either subcultured after trypsinization with 0.25% trypsin (#SH40003.01, Hyclone Laboratories, South Logan, UT, USA) or plated at specific densities for different treatments. The cells were passaged every 2-3 days.

Drugs and treatment

Simvastatin (#S0509, TCI chemicals, Tokyo, Japan), MPP+ Iodide (#16958, Cayman chemicals, MI, USA), and K252a a potent TrkB receptor inhibitor (#SF23700, Jerusalem, Israel) were used. Simvastatin and K252a were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and made a final concentration of 0.1% DMSO in the plain media. MPP+ was dissolved in autoclaved distilled water. Freshly prepared drugs were used while experimenting.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability assay was determined by using cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo Laboratories, Japan). SH-SY5Y cells were properly seeded at the density of 10,000 cells per well in a 96-well plate overnight. The cells were treated with drugs and after 24 hr of treatment, the 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added and incubated for 1 hr. The absorbance was measured at 450 nM in a Microplate reader (Multiskan Go, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Ratastie, Finland) to obtain the viability of the cells. The results were expressed as a percentage of the control group.

Western blot analysis

Cells and brain tissue were homogenized by using RIPA lysis buffer and a 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (#1861281, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA). The tissue homogenate was centrifuged at 4°C for 20 min at 13,000 rpm and the supernatant was collected in a fresh tube without touching the pellet. Similarly, the nuclear protein was separated using NE-PERTM nuclear and cytoplasmic extraction reagent (#78833, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The BCA protein assay kit (#23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA) was used to calculate the protein concentration. Proteins were loaded in equal amounts in a 12% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred to a PVDF membrane (#IPVH00010, Millipore Corporation, Temecula, CA, USA). Membranes were blocked for 1 h using 5% skim milk in Tris-Buffered Saline with 0.1% Tween 20. Primary antibodies to BDNF (1:1,000; Abcam, #AB108319, Waltham, MA, USA), p-CREB (1:2,000; Cell Signaling Technology, #9198S, Danvers, MA, USA), CREB (1:2,500; Invitrogen, #PA1-850, Rockford, IL, USA), PPARα (1:2,000; Invitrogen, #PA1-822A, Rockford, IL, USA), GAPDH (1:5,000; Ab Frontier, #LF-PA0212, Seoul, Korea), Lamin-B1 (1:5,000; Cell Signaling Technology, #12586, Danvers, MA, USA) were incubated at 4°C overnight. After that, the membrane was washed with TBST 3 times and incubated with a secondary antibody (1:5,000; Ab Frontier, #LF-SA8002, Seoul, Korea) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, employing enhanced chemiluminescence reagents, the membrane was exposed to a luminescence image analyzer (Fusion Solo, VilberLourmat, France) to detect the immunoreactive complex. GelQuant.Net was used to determine the density of each blot.

siRNA transfection of SH-SY5Y cells

Using HiPerfect transfection reagent (#301704, Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA), SH-SY5Y cells were transfected transiently with siRNA against specific target genes (BDNF and PPARα) or non-specific genes (scramble siRNA) (Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea) (Table 1). In 35-mm culture dishes, 2.5×105 cells were plated and incubated overnight. 1X PBS was used to wash the cells before replacing them with the new plain medium. The transfection mixture was then gently applied to each well. After 6 hr, the cells were given an equal volume of complete medium and cultured for another 24 h. After that, the cells were washed in 1X PBS and treated as described in the figure legends. After 36 h, the efficacy of gene silencing was determined using western blot analysis.

Animals and drug treatment

Adult male C57BL/6 mice (21-25 g, 8 weeks old, Japan SLC, Shizuoka, Japan) were housed in micro isolator cages with free access to food and water on a 12 h light/dark cycle. The mice were separated into distinct groups after one week of acclimatization. Before the study began, the IACUC of Yeungnam University authorized the protocol for animal studies (approval number: 2022-024). To optimize the dose of simvastatin, animals were randomly assigned to four groups (3 mice/group) receiving different dose (0, 1, 5, or 10 mg/kg). Based on the experimental results, a dose of 5 mg/kg was selected for subsequent experiments, as it induced the highest expression levels of BDNF and phosphorylated CREB (p-CREB) in the striatum. For the behavioral and other in vivo experiments, the animals were divided into 4 groups containing 5 mice in each group. Group 1 was treated with vehicle (5% DMSO+50% PEG+45% DW) for 14 days intraperitoneally. Group 2 was also treated with the vehicle solution for 14 days, and on day 15, they received intraperitoneal injection of MPTP (15 mg/kg×4, 1.5 h interval). Group 3 was treated with simvastatin (i.p., 5 mg/kg) dissolved in vehicle (5% DMSO+50% PEG+45% DW) for 14 days and then saline on day 15. Group 4 was given simvastatin for 14 days and then MPTP on day 15. All drugs were freshly prepared and administered according to the body weight of the mice.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

HPLC (1260 Infinity, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was performed for the measurement of monoamines with an electrochemical detector (Coulochem III, Thermo Scientific). Each striatal tissue was dissected, weighed, and homogenized in 50 µL of 0.1 N perchloric acid using sonicator. The samples were centrifuged at 13,000 g at 4°C for 20 min, and the supernatants were collected. A series of DA and DOPAC standards solutions were made fresh by diluting the stock solution. 10 µL of prepared sample or standard solution was injected into the mobile phase (75 mM sodium phosphate monobasic, 1.7 mM 1-octane sulfonic acid, 25 µM EDTA, 100 µL triethylamine, 15% acetonitrile) with a flow rate of 0.6 ml/min. The concentrations of monoamines were calculated in ng/g of wet tissue.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

On the last day of the experiment, all the mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation, the right hemisphere of the brain was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and then transferred into 30% sucrose. Coronal brain sections with thickness of 30 µM were made using freezing sliding microtome (Microm HM450, Thermo Scientific) and the tissue sections were collected in cryoprotectant solution in 24 well plates. Immunostainings for tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)-positive cells were performed using free-floating brain sections. Endogenous peroxidase activity of brain sections was quenched by incubation in 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) for 20 min. The sections were then incubated at 4°C overnight. The sections were treated with biotinylated secondary antibody (1:1000, Vector Laboratories Inc, #BA-1000, Burlingame, CA, USA) diluted in KPBS containing 0.4% Triton X-100. Following washes in KPBS, sections were immersed in avidin-biotin-peroxidase complex (#PK-4000, Vector Laboratories Inc., Newark, CA, USA). The immunocomplex was visualized using a diaminobenzidine solution (Sigma-Aldrich St. Louis, MO, USA). The sections were mounted onto gelatin-coated glass slides and observed under the light microscope.

Behavioral tests

To determine the effects of simvastatin on MPTP-induced dysfunctions of sensory-motor function in mice, a beam test, and challenge beam test were performed on day 15. To adapt to the behavioral testing conditions, the mice underwent training several times before the actual experiment day.

Beam walking test: The beam walking test is a method to evaluate motor coordination. In this test, a wooden beam 100 cm long with four sections of decreasing width (starting from 3.5 cm and ending at 0.5 cm) is placed above the floor, with the narrow end placed to the home cage. The mouse is placed on the start point of the beam (wider end) and trained to ambulate along it toward the home cage. The mice were trained 3 to 5 times per day for one week before the actual experiment (Shadfar et al., 2022). To encourage mice to reach the home cage through the narrow beam, they were gently oriented or touched on their backside during the initial training phase. The time it takes for the mice to walk from the wider end of the beam to the home cage was measured using a stopwatch and documented as the mean of three trials per animal.

Challenging beam test: On the day of the behavioral test, a mesh grid (1 cm2) of width equivalent to the beam size was placed over the beam surface. Subsequently, the animals were permitted to traverse this grid, and the stopwatch was used to record the duration it took for them to reach their habitat. The trials were repeated thrice for each mouse.

Statistical analysis

The results were provided as mean ± SEM of three independent experiments. To determine statistical differences between multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was used, followed by the Student-Newman-Keuls comparison procedure (Graph Pad Prism 8.0, San Diego, CA, USA). The significance criterion was set at a p-value of less than 0.05.

RESULTS

Simvastatin protects the dopaminergic neurons from MPTP-induced neurotoxicity

We treated the SH-SY5Y cells with different doses of simvastatin ranging from 10 nM-1 µM to check if the drug was toxic to the cells. Simvastatin did not show any significant cytotoxic effects up to 1 µM (Fig. 1A). After that, the cells were treated with different concentrations of MPP+ to select the appropriate dose of the neurotoxicant. MPP+ ranging from 1 mM to 3 mM caused significant cell death in a dose-dependent pattern and 3 mM of MPP+ was chosen for the next experiments (Fig. 1B). The cells were pretreated with different concentrations of simvastatin (0.01, 0.1, and 1 µM) for 24 h, followed by treatment with MPP+ for an additional 24 h. The results showed that 0.01 and 0.1 µM of simvastatin were able to significantly reverse the neurotoxic effects of MPP+ (Fig. 1C), while 1 µM simvastatin did not protect the neuroblastoma significantly. We selected 0.01 µM of simvastatin for further study because the dose appeared to be the best among the three doses. Next, we conducted the western blot analysis to see the time-dependent effect of simvastatin on BDNF expression in SH-SY5Y cells. The BDNF expression was increased in a time-dependent manner and the most pronounced rise of BDNF expression was seen at 24 hr post to treatment with the drug (Fig. 1D).

Simvastatin attenuates the decrease of BDNF and phosphorylated CREB caused by MPP+ in SH-SY5Y cells

To prove that simvastatin’s protective effect against MPP+-induced toxicity is involved in upregulation of BDNF and activation of its downstream signaling pathway, we evaluated levels of BDNF and phosphorylated CREB after treatment of simvastatin and/or MPP+ with SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells. The western blot analysis clearly showed MPP+ significantly decreased BDNF and phosphorylated CREB in comparison to the control (Fig. 2A, 2B). Importantly, the results showed that simvastatin alone significantly increased BDNF and phosphorylated CREB compared to the control. Simvastatin mitigated the decrease in BDNF (Fig. 2A) and phosphorylated CREB (Fig. 2B) caused by MPP+, suggesting that BDNF plays a significant role in the protective effect of simvastatin against neurotoxicity induced by MPP+.

Simvastatin protects SH-SY5Y cells against MPP+ toxicity via BDNF induction and activation of TrkB receptor signaling

To investigate the role of BDNF and its TrkB receptor in simvastatin’s cytoprotection, SH-SY5Y cells were pretreated with the TrkB receptor inhibitor K252a (100 nM) for 1 h, and then treated with 10 nM simvastatin for 24 h, followed by MPP+ treatment. The cell viability assay showed that simvastatin attenuated MPP+-induced cell loss, and this was counteracted by the treatment of K252a (Fig. 3A). From western blot analysis, it was evident that simvastatin alleviated MPP+-induced downregulation of BDNF and K252a significantly attenuated simvastatin-induced restoration of BDNF (Fig. 3B) and p-CREB (Fig. 3C).

To further verify the association of BDNF with simvastatin’s cytoprotective effects, SH-SY5Y cells were exposed to siRNA against BDNF. Importantly, siRNA against BDNF diminished the cytoprotection of simvastatin from MPP+ neurotoxicity as determined by the cell viability assay (Fig. 3D). The western blot analyses showed that transfection with BDNF siRNA itself reduced BDNF expression in the cells, which was exaggerated when it was combined with MPP+ treatment (Fig. 3E). These results suggest that simvastatin’s neuroprotection against MPP+ is linked to BDNF induction and the TrkB signaling pathway.

BDNF induction by simvastatin is related to the activation of PPARα

PPARα is a transcription factor that regulates the gene of neurotrophic factors, including BDNF and its nuclear translocation is required for its activation (Roy et al., 2013). Thus, we examined the effect of simvastatin on the level of PPARα in the cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions of SH-SY5Y cells. The cells were pretreated with 10 nM simvastatin for 24 h followed by MPP+ treatment for 24 h. There were no significant effects of MPP+ or simvastatin treatment on the level of PPARα in the cytoplasmic fraction. However, the level of PPARα in the nuclear fraction was significantly decreased by MPP+ and vice versa in the case of simvastatin treatment (Fig. 4A, 4B). Remarkably, the decreased PPARα in the nuclear fraction due to MPP+ was restored by pretreatment with simvastatin (Fig. 4B).

To explore the involvement of the PPARα in BDNF induction by simvastatin, we transfected cells with siRNA against PPARα and performed Western blot analyses to evaluate the efficacy of the siRNA. The results indicated that the siRNA alone significantly reduced the level of BDNF. Moreover, the siRNA attenuated simvastatin’s restorative effects on BDNF upon the presence of MPP+ (Fig. 4C). Consistent with the results from Western blot analyses, the siRNA alone reduced the cell viability in comparison to the control. Notably, siRNA targeting PPARα also reduced the cytoprotective effects of simvastatin against MPP+ neurotoxicity, as assessed by the cell viability assay (Fig. 4D). These results suggest that PPARα might play a substantial role in simvastatin’s BDNF induction and its subsequent protective effect against MPP+ neurotoxicity.

Simvastatin induces BDNF expression and activates its downstream signaling pathway in the substantia nigra and striatum

To validate the aforementioned findings, we conducted an in vivo study using C57BL/6 mice to see whether simvastatin induces neurotrophic factor BDNF and its downstream signaling pathway in the nigrostriatal axis. To determine the optimal dose of simvastatin for the study, the drug was injected into C57BL/6 mice at a different dose (1 mg/kg, 5 mg/kg, and 10 mg/kg) over 14 days, and the expression level of BDNF and p-CREB was analyzed. 5 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg doses significantly upregulated the expression of BDNF (Fig. 5A) and p-CREB (Fig. 5B) in the striatum. Specifically, there was a significant difference between the efficacy of 5 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg, and these effects were higher than that of 1 mg/kg. Therefore, we selected a dose of 5 mg/kg for the next experiments. We further evaluated the neuroprotective effect of simvastatin, focusing on its ability to mitigate MPTP-driven neurotrophic deficits in the substantia nigra and striatum. The results showed that MPTP significantly reduced the expression of BDNF in the striatum (Fig. 5C), which was abolished by pretreatment with simvastatin, suggesting that BDNF plays a significant role in simvastatin’s neuroprotective effects against MPTP. Consistent with this restoration of neurotrophic factor, MPTP-induced suppression of CREB phosphorylation was significantly rescued by simvastatin (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, simvastatin not only restores BDNF levels but also stimulates downstream signaling pathways in the substantia nigra as well (Fig. 5E, 5F).

Amelioration of MPTP-induced motor deficit by Simvastatin in C57BL/6 mice

The beam walking test and challenge beam test were performed following pretreatment with simvastatin (5 mg/kg for 14 days) to evaluate its potential to alleviate motor deficits caused by MPTP treatment. The analysis revealed that MPTP injection significantly increased the time for traversing the beam in both tests compared to the normal vehicle-treated group (Fig. 6A, 6B). Notably, the motor deficit induced by MPTP was remarkably mitigated upon pretreatment with simvastatin (Fig. 6A, 6B), suggesting the potential of simvastatin to counteract the motor deficit prompted by MPTP exposure.

Simvastatin attenuates the nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurodegeneration from MPTP-induced neurotoxicity

IHC targeting TH was conducted to visualize the nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons and assess the neuroprotective effects of simvastatin. We did not observe that simvastatin treatment alone significantly affect the number of dopaminergic neurons in the nigrostriatal pathway. However, MPTP injection led to a substantial loss of TH-positive neurons (~65%) and fibers (~62%) in the substantia nigra and the striatum respectively (Fig. 7A, 7B). Importantly, treatment with simvastatin significantly mitigated the MPTP-mediated deletion of the TH-positive dopaminergic neurons (Fig. 7A) and striatal TH-positive fibers (Fig. 7B).

HPLC analysis was conducted to analyze the level of dopamine and its metabolite DOPAC in the striatum. The results showed that simvastatin alone did not significantly change neurochemical concentration within the striatum. Conversely, MPTP injection led to a substantial reduction in dopamine (~88%) and DOPAC (~77%), while simvastatin treatment attenuated loss of dopamine (Fig. 7C) and DOPAC (Fig. 7D) in the striatum.

DISCUSSION

The prevalence of PD continues to increase in parallel with the aging population. Current pharmacological treatments primarily aim to alleviate motor symptoms but do not slow or halt the progression of neurodegeneration. In particular, prolonged use of L-DOPA often leads to uncontrollable side effects such as dyskinesia.

Although substantial progress has been made in understanding the pathophysiology of PD and exploring novel therapeutic strategies, no treatment has yet been developed that can fundamentally modify disease progression. To overcome this limitation, disease-modifying approaches such as gene therapy aimed at enhancing neurotrophic factor expression and astroglial transplantation have been explored, with some promising outcomes reported (Baloh et al., 2022; Roberts et al., 2024). However, neurotrophic factors like BDNF cannot effectively cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), necessitating direct intracerebral administration to reach target brain regions. These invasive delivery methods carry surgical risks and have shown limited clinical success (Bondarenko and Saarma, 2021). As an alternative, pharmacologically inducing endogenous BDNF expression is gaining attention as a less invasive and potentially more effective therapeutic strategy. Indeed, numerous studies have demonstrated that drug-induced BDNF upregulation provides neuroprotection against various neurotoxic insults (Bhurtel et al., 2018; Mohamed et al., 2023).

Our current study demonstrated that simvastatin protected the dopaminergic neurons from MPP+ and MPTP-induced toxicity by upregulating BDNF and activating its downstream signaling pathways. Notably, the involvement of PPARα appeared essential for the induction of BDNF, as silencing PPARα abolished the neuroprotective actions of simvastatin. Supporting our findings, clinical studies have reported that the use of lipophilic statins, including simvastatin, is associated with a reduced risk of developing PD (Lee et al., 2013). Likewise, a 12-year longitudinal study by Gao et al. found that regular statin use significantly lowered the incidence of PD (Gao et al., 2012). Our data suggest a previously underexplored pharmacological dimension of simvastatin, particularly its potential to harness the PPARα-mediated BDNF signaling pathway to mitigate nigrostriatal degeneration. Such insights provide a compelling framework for the development of targeted neuroprotective interventions addressing current challenges in PD drug development; however, the underlying signaling mechanisms require further validation in vivo.

BDNF has been known to promote neuronal development, differentiation, survival, and synaptic plasticity by binding to TrkB receptors, which in turn activate intracellular pro-survival pathways such as PI3K/AKT and CREB signaling (Benarroch, 2015). In a model of traumatic brain injury, it was reported that simvastatin significantly upregulated BDNF expression via activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway contributing to therapeutic improvement (Wu et al., 2008). Similarly, Han et al. demonstrated that simvastatin enhanced functional recovery following experimental spinal cord injury by increasing BDNF levels in the brain (Han et al., 2011). In line with the previous findings, our study showed that simvastatin treatment increased BDNF expression both in vitro and in vivo. The upregulation of BDNF was accompanied by the activation of CREB, a critical transcription factor involved in promoting neuronal survival and proliferation (Sakamoto et al., 2011). Previous studies have shown that simvastatin-induced BDNF induction was directly linked to TrkB activation. In the neuronal model of Aβ-induced neurotoxicity, simvastatin increased both BDNF and phosphorylated TrkB levels, indicating that the BDNF/TrkB pathway was involved in simvastatin-mediated neuroprotection (Fang et al., 2024). Similar observations have been reported in independent studies (Ahmed and Kamel, 2020; Cui et al., 2012). Given this established mechanistic relationship, the primary aim of our study was to ascertain if BDNF-TrkB signaling is necessary for simvastatin’s neuroprotective effects rather than to validate simvastatin–mediated TrkB phosphorylation, which has already been shown, and we used the TrkB receptor inhibitor K252a to answer this question. Furthermore, inhibition of BDNF receptor using K252a significantly reduced BDNF expression (Fig. 3B) and viability of SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 3A), indicating that disruption of BDNF-TrkB axis impairs BDNF production itself and the BDNF-TrkB-CREB signaling pathway is essential for the neuroprotective effect of Simvastatin.

In a previous study, Roy et al. demonstrated that simvastatin’s interaction with PPARα was associated with enhanced neurotrophin expression and improved cognitive function in a mouse model of Alzheimer’s disease (Roy et al., 2015). The research group reported that simvastatin exerted its memory-enhancing effects through the PPARα–BDNF–CREB signaling pathway. Consistent with these findings, our study exhibited that the PPARα–BDNF–TrkB–CREB signaling cascade played a central role in simvastatin’s neuroprotective action against MPP⁺ and MPTP-induced dopaminergic toxicity. While both studies emphasize the significance of PPARα-mediated BDNF signaling, our study provided additional mechanistic insights. First, we demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of TrkB with K252a significantly reduced BDNF and p-CREB expression in SH-SY5Y cells. This result indicates that TrkB-mediated signaling is necessary for upregulation of BDNF and p-CREB expression and consequent neuroprotective effects of simvastatin.

Second, we provided direct evidence for the role of BDNF in simvastatin-mediated neuroprotection by employing BDNF-targeted siRNA, which abolished the protective effects of simvastatin. Third, we showed that nuclear translocation of PPARα was essential for the upregulation of BDNF and the resultant neuroprotective outcomes in MPP⁺-challenged neuronal cells, which was not addressed in Roy et al.’s study (Roy et al., 2015). These distinctions highlight the novel contributions of our study in elucidating the precise molecular events underlying simvastatin’s neuroprotective effects in parkinsonian models.

We propose that simvastatin could be a promising therapeutic candidate for PD based on several compelling lines of evidence. First, as a lipophilic compound, simvastatin can readily cross the BBB and activate the PPARα signaling pathway in the brain (Fig. 8). Second, simvastatin interacts directly with the PPARα receptor contributing to a range of neuroprotective effects. Notably, simvastatin’s anti-inflammatory (Esposito et al., 2012) and anti-atherogenic (Yano et al., 2007) properties have been attributed to PPARα activation.

Schematic representation illustrating the mechanism of neuroprotection by simvastatin. Simvastatin crosses the blood–brain barrier and activates PPARα, leading to the upregulation of BDNF. Increased BDNF subsequently binds to TrkB and activates the CREB signaling pathway, thereby promoting the survival of the dopaminergic neurons. BDNF: Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor, CREB: cAMP response element-binding protein, PPAR: Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor, TrkB: Tropomyosin receptor kinase B.

PPARα is a ligand-activated transcription factor expressed in the brain and peripheral organs, and it regulates key processes including oxidative stress responses, energy metabolism, mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation, neuroinflammation, glutamatergic signaling, and dopaminergic/cholinergic neurotransmission (De Felice et al., 2019; Wojtowicz et al., 2020). Previous studies have also shown that PPARα agonists enhanced BDNF expression by interacting with PPAR response elements in the BDNF promoter, leading to transcriptional activation via CREB signaling (Jiang et al., 2017; Roy et al., 2013). Moreover, PPARα has been implicated in the modulation of microglial activation (Roy et al., 2015), and its activation by statins has been shown to mediate the neuroprotection (Benedetti et al., 2018). Earlier studies have shown that the number of transcriptional factors, such as CREB (Ser133), c-jun, c-fos, and MeCP2 (Ser421), is known regulators of BDNF transcription. However, we focused on PPARα in this study, because prior investigations demonstrated that PPARα activation was specifically linked to BDNF induction under neurotoxic and metabolic stress conditions, which are highly relevant to MPP⁺- or MPTP- induced dopaminergic neurodegeneration. Unlike CREB (Ser133), c-jun, c-fos, MeCP2 (Ser421), which are broadly activity-dependent transcription factors activated by variety of stimuli like calcium influx, neuronal depolarization (Loebrich and Nedivi, 2009; Lonze and Ginty, 2002), PPARα reacts specifically to lipid metabolism-related signaling, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress, which are usually involved in MPP⁺- or MPTP neurotoxicity. Notably, Roy et al. demonstrated that PPARα is required for statin-induced BDNF upregulation, as inhibition or deletion of PPARα abolished both CREB activation and BDNF induction (Roy et al., 2015). Thus, PPARα functions as the main upstream regulator of the CREB-BDNF pathway under statin treatment, providing a strong rationale for its selection as the primary mediator of simvastatin-induced BDNF upregulation in this study.

Even though our in vitro results strongly imply that BDNF/TrkB signaling or PPARα activation are linked to simvastatin’s neuroprotective effects, several limitations should be acknowledged. We did not directly evaluate the in vivo dependency of these effects through pharmacological or genetic inhibition of PPARα or TrkB. Further research using PPARα knockout models, selective pharmacological inhibitors, and TrkB targeted approaches will be critical to validate their roles in mediating simvastatin-induced neuroprotection against dopaminergic neurodegeneration.

In conclusion, our investigation demonstrated a novel mechanism by which the dopaminergic neurons were protected by simvastatin against MPTP-induced neurotoxicity. Thus, these results raise the possibility that simvastatin could be an effective therapeutic intervention for PD. Further studies are warranted to determine whether the neuroprotective effects of simvastatin could be recapitulated in various PD models and clinical studies with PD patients.
