Formation mechanism of grinding wheel oxide layer in the ultrasonic-assisted ELID grinding of silicon carbide ceramics
Authors: Xiaofeng Jia, Chongyang Zhao, Yang Cao, Zhicheng Xu, Yanan Bao, Sen Yin, Bo Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs a substrate material for lightweight optical mirrors, silicon carbide (SiC) ceramics are extensively employed in deep space exploration applications. Nevertheless, conventional grinding/milling and polishing processes are prone to induce subsurface damage while exhibiting inherently low processing efficiency. Therefore, this study proposes the combination of ultrasonic vibration and online electrolytic sharpening, firstly employing ultrasonic-assisted ELID (Electrolytic In-process Dressing) (U-ELID) grinding to address these machining challenges associated with SiC ceramics. Theoretical analysis and multi-physics field simulations reveal that the formation mechanism of the grinding wheel oxide layer under high-frequency ultrasonic vibration is the critical factor governing the surface integrity of the machined workpiece. Subsequently, through the integration of multi-physics field simulation, pre-sharpening experiments, and grinding trials, this research establishes a thickness growth model for the oxide layer formed during U-ELID pre-sharpening. It further investigates the formation characteristics of the oxide layer during the pre-sharpening stage in U-ELID grinding of SiC ceramics, revealing the underlying mechanisms and inherent laws governing the influence of ultrasonic vibration on the uniformity and compactness of the oxide layer. The results demonstrate that the synergistic effects of ultrasonic high-frequency mechanical action, ultrasonic cavitation, and ultrasonic acoustic streaming act upon the oxide layer. Compared to conventional ELID grinding, the oxide layer generated during U-ELID grinding exhibits significantly higher compactness and superior grinding performance. Specifically, under the same power supply voltage, the porosity of the oxide layer formed during U-ELID pre-sharpening is reduced by 51%, with narrower cracks and fewer pits induced by abrasive grain detachment. As the grinding depth decreases from 5 μm to 2 μm, the arithmetic mean surface roughness (Sa) of the ELID ground surface decreases by 35%, whereas that of the U-ELID ground surface decreases by 44.2%. Compared with ELID grinding, the reduction rate of Sa for the U-ELID ground surface is increased by 9.2%. Additionally, the surface scratches are finer and more uniform. This study, for the first time, unveils the mechanism by which the introduction of ultrasonic vibration influences oxide layer formation on grinding wheels during U-ELID grinding. The identified synergistic mechanism offers a novel pathway for achieving ductile-mode grinding of SiC ceramics, holding significant promise for advancing the machining precision of optical components to the nanoscale and supporting the demands of national major engineering projects such as deep space exploration.Keywords: Ultrasonic-assisted machining, ELID grinding, Oxide layer, Silicon carbide ceramics, Density and uniformity

Introduction

Silicon carbide (SiC) ceramics exhibit exceptional resistance to elevated temperatures and corrosive environments, characterized by a low coefficient of thermal expansion, high specific stiffness, and significant hardness. Compared to traditional optical glass, these properties render SiC ceramics more suitable as substrates for lightweight optical reflectors, enabling them to endure extreme service conditions, including alternating high and low temperatures, intense vibrational shocks, and high-intensity radiation [1], [2]. However, these stringent service conditions impose exceptionally high precision requirements on SiC ceramic components: specifically, ultra-high surface form accuracy and ultra-low surface and subsurface damage, essential to ensure optimal optical performance and service life. SiC ceramics possess a Mohs hardness of 9.3, approaching that of diamond, while exhibiting relatively low fracture toughness, approximately 3–4 MPa·m½ [3], [4]. Consequently, their precision and ultra-precision machining typically employs a traditional mechanical processing sequence combining milling, grinding [5], [6], and precise polishing. Nevertheless, during milling and grinding operations, the material is highly susceptible to brittle fracture, resulting in micro-cracks, edge breakage, and other forms of damage. Critically, cracks initiated in preceding processing stages propagate under subsequent machining loads, inducing more severe damage and significantly degrading the component's imaging quality and operational lifespan. Furthermore, the extremely low material removal rate inherent to precision polishing[7] severely constrains overall machining efficiency. This limitation significantly impedes the broader application of SiC ceramics within industrial and high-technology sectors.

Extensive research efforts have been directed toward addressing the machinability challenges of SiC ceramic materials through energy field-assisted machining techniques, such as ultrasonic-assisted machining, magnetorheological polishing, abrasive water jet machining, and laser machining [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]. However, each of these technologies exhibits distinct advantages and inherent limitations. Although ultrasonic-assisted machining can effectively reduce cutting force and thermal load, and has high surface quality and grinding efficiency[14], high-frequency vibration impact will accelerate tool wear. Magnetorheological polishing enables nanometer-level precision on complex surfaces, yet faces challenges in controlling the stability and response time of the magnetorheological fluid. Abrasive water jet machining eliminates thermal effects during processing, but its cutting resolution (>0.5 mm) is insufficient for precision machining requirements. Laser processing, owing to its high thermal energy input, frequently induces surface layer damage to the workpiece. Consequently, single energy field-assisted machining approaches are inadequate for meeting the stringent demands of precision and ultra-precision machining of SiC ceramics, necessitating the development of efficient, high-quality machining methodologies.

Multi-energy field assistance, achieved through the synergistic superposition of two or more energy fields, consistently produces a “1 + 1 > 2” effect and plays a critical role in the high-performance manufacturing of difficult-to-machine materials [15], [16]. Among these approaches, electrolytic in-process dressing (ELID) grinding is a hybrid machining method that integrates mechanical grinding, electrolytic action, and electrochemical passivation behavior. Through the synergistic effect of multiple physical fields, ELID grinding enables online self-sharpening of the grinding wheel, constant cutting edge protrusion height control, and surface quality optimization during processing, making it particularly suitable for the ultra-precision machining of hard and brittle materials such as ceramics, glass [17], [18]. ELID grinding exhibits advantages including stable processing, high abrasive utilization efficiency, and excellent surface integrity. However, issues such as low machining efficiency and insufficient grinding wheel dressing remain pressing challenges to be addressed [19], [20], [21], [22]. Consequently, the ultrasonic-assisted ELID (U-ELID) grinding technology has been developed, which allows for in-process electrolytic dressing of metal-bonded superabrasive grinding wheels. This technology maintains a constant protrusion height of abrasive grains, mitigates grinding wheel dulling, and enables efficient achievement of nanoscale machined surfaces [23], [24]. Xiang et al. [25] validated the advantages of U-ELID grinding in enhancing the surface quality and inducing residual compressive stress in hardened 12Cr2Ni4A steel. Jia et al. [26] investigated the critical grinding depth corresponding to the brittle-plastic transition during U-ELID grinding of nanocomposite ceramics. Compared to conventional ELID grinding, U-ELID grinding exhibited an expansion in the processing range of the plastic deformation zone. Fu et al.[27] explored the evolution of surface properties in nanocomposite ceramics under U-ELID grinding conditions. Relative to ELID grinding, U-ELID grinding yielded a more uniform microstructure distribution, with surface roughness (Ra) reduced by 14.5%. Li et al. [28] demonstrated enhanced grinding wheel dressing under ultrasonic vibration in U-ELID grinding: the average abrasive grain protrusion height increased by 14.2%, while the average inter-abrasive spacing decreased by 21.2%. Ji et al. [29] studied the correlation between oxide layer thickness and mechanical properties during U-ELID grinding, finding that both the average friction coefficient of the oxide layer and the grinding wheel wear rate increased with increasing oxide layer thickness. Jie et al. [30] conducted U-ELID grinding experiments, revealing that the oxide layer formed following ultrasonic vibration application to the grinding wheel exhibited superior density, uniformity, and hardness-properties conducive to improving workpiece surface quality. Lei et al. [31] developed a mathematical model for the surface residual stress of 12Cr2Ni4A quenched and tempered steel during U-ELID grinding. It was found that the governing laws of how different parameters affect the workpiece surface residual stress in UVAEG are as follows: the residual compressive stress on the ground surface increases with the increase of ultrasonic amplitude and duty cycle, while it decreases as the grinding depth increases. Wu et al. [32] employed UA-ELID grinding to machine M50 aerospace bearing steel and developed a solid–liquid hybrid normal force calculation model that incorporates the coupling effects of three dynamic contact modes: micro-cutting by diamond abrasive grains, sliding of oxide film protrusions, and electrolyte film contact. By analyzing the synergistic relationships between contact modes and their governing laws on normal force under varying process parameters, the precise quantification of mechanical contact characteristics of the ground surface and optimization of the machining process were successfully achieved. In conclusion, the synchronous implementation of high-frequency grinding wheel vibration and in-process electrolytic dressing in U-ELID grinding technology offers a viable solution for mitigating damage issues such as microcracks and edge chipping during SiC ceramic machining, while simultaneously enhancing processing efficiency. It thus presents a promising technical approach for achieving high-efficiency, low-damage machining of SiC ceramics. However, to date, no studies have been reported on the application of U-ELID grinding technology to SiC ceramics.

Notably, current research on U-ELID grinding has predominantly focused on grinding force, thermal effects, and surface quality prediction. However, in U-ELID machining, the oxide layer − an insulating film formed on the grinding wheel surface-exerts a critical influence on the machining process. First, its self-regulating capability maintains a constant abrasive protrusion height and chip pocket volume, facilitating continuous and stable grinding. Second, the oxide layer partially encapsulates the abrasive grains participating in grinding; its thickness and hardness directly govern the distribution of grinding forces and dictate the material removal mechanism. Furthermore, the adsorbed and lattice water contained within the oxide layer effectively reduces grinding zone temperatures through evaporative cooling. Therefore, clarifying the formation mechanism of the oxide layer is a key factor in determining the surface integrity of the machined workpiece. Nevertheless, the mechanism by which the process quantities (processing parameters, force, heat, etc.) of U-ELID grinding affect the formation characteristics of the oxide film still lacks systematic research. For instance, Chen et al. [33] performed U-ELID grinding on zirconia-toughened alumina ceramics, proposed an internal stress model for oxide layer damage, and revealed the effect of thermomechanical loading on oxide layer properties. However, the mechanism by which ultrasonic vibration influences the formation characteristics of the oxide layer has yet to be investigated. This lack of theoretical understanding consequently impedes the optimization of U-ELID process and restricts its broader application in SiC ceramic machining.

Thus, this study developed a U-ELID machining experimental platform, hypothesizing that ultrasonic vibration can regulate the formation characteristics of the oxide layer on the grinding wheel surface during U-ELID grinding, thereby enabling the synergistic optimization of surface quality, grinding wheel wear, and machining efficiency in SiC ceramic processing. For the first time, U-ELID grinding technology was introduced into the ultra-precision machining of SiC ceramics. The formation mechanism of the oxide layer on the grinding wheel surface during U-ELID machining was investigated, and a model for oxide layer thickness growth was established. This model was subsequently refined through experimental validation, clarifying the mechanisms and governing laws by which ultrasonic vibration influences the thickness and compactness of the oxide layer in U-ELID machining. These findings provide a theoretical foundation and technical support for optimizing the high-quality, high-efficiency U-ELID grinding process for SiC ceramics.The core innovations of this study relative to existing research are summarized as follows: (1) U-ELID grinding technology was, for the first time, applied to the field of ultra-precision machining of SiC ceramics, overcoming the limitations of traditional grinding technologies in processing this material and providing a novel approach for the precision machining of hard-brittle materials. (2) Beyond establishing an oxide layer thickness growth model, the influence mechanism of ultrasonic vibration on oxide layer properties was explicitly elucidated through experimental validation, revealing the intrinsic microscale mechanisms underlying the improvement in machining quality. (3) The proposed machining method enables synergistic optimization of machined surface quality, grinding wheel wear control, and machining efficiency, providing technical support for the high-performance machining of large-scale lightweight SiC ceramic optical reflectors. Additionally, the research findings offer critical references for extending U-ELID technology to the machining of other hard-brittle and difficult-to-machine materials, and provide guiding insights for the design of specialized grinding wheels and the innovation of wear-reduction processes.

Mechanism of ultrasonic vibration on oxide layer formation

Role of oxide layers in U-ELID grinding

The operational principle of U-ELID machining is depicted in Fig. 1 a). A cast iron-bonded diamond grinding wheel functions as the anode, while the tool electrode serves as the cathode. An electrolytic grinding fluid is continuously delivered into the inter-electrode gap. As shown in Fig. 1 b), Under the influence of a pulsed power supply, the metallic binder on the grinding wheel surface undergoes electrolysis, forming an oxide layer. Concurrently, high-frequency axial vibration induced by ultrasonic excitation is imparted to the grinding wheel. This vibration transports the oxide layer to act in concert on the workpiece surface.

Schematic diagram of the U-ELID processing principle and the periodic change of the grinding wheel surface.

Within U-ELID grinding process, the oxide layer serves as a critical interface mediating both grinding wheel dressing and workpiece machining. Its formation characteristics directly dictate the material removal mechanism and the evolution of the ground surface. The U-ELID pre-sharpening stage was selected as the research focus, as no direct contact occurs between the grinding wheel and workpiece during this stage; thereby permitting the exclusion of interference from the workpiece’s reaction force on the oxide layer.

Effect of High-Frequency mechanical action on oxide layers

The newly formed oxide layer through electrolysis is in a moist, loose and porous structure, with a large amount of electrolyte adsorbed in its pores. The electrochemical reaction process on the surface of the grinding wheel is as follows: Fe → Fe2++2e-, Fe2+→Fe3++e-, H2O → H++OH–, Fe2++2OH → Fe(OH)2, Fe3++3OH–→Fe(OH)3, firstly, the physical evaporation process of adsorbed water occurs, and then as the grinding temperature continues to rise, Fe(OH)2 and Fe(OH)3 decompose under the effect of grinding heat to form γ-Fe2O3 and α-Fe2O3 [34]. Therefore, in the U-ELID pre-sharpening process, the main components of the oxide layer formed by electrochemical action are Fe(OH)2, Fe(OH)3 and hydroxyl iron oxides (FeOOH) with different degrees of dehydration [35]. Under the applied electric field, internal stress develops within this oxide layer on the anode surface of the cast iron-bonded diamond grinding wheel. Concurrently, the progression of the redox reaction induces variations in this internal stress corresponding to changes in oxide layer thickness. The Pilling-Bedworth Ratio (PBR) is a key parameter in materials science used to assess whether metals or alloys can form protective oxide films in high-temperature oxidation environments. Its core function is to predict whether the oxide film is dense and continuous, thereby determining the material's oxidation resistance [36]. Influence of ultrasonic cavitation on oxide layers

Cavitation occurs when a liquid undergoes rapid flow through a constriction, resulting in increased flow velocity and a concomitant reduction in local pressure. When this pressure falls below the saturated vapor pressure of the liquid, vaporization ensues, leading to the formation of cavitation bubbles [41]. During the U-ELID pre-sharpening process, the utilized electrolyte contains entrapped gaseous impurities (e.g., air and insoluble gases) as well as solid particles derived from the detached oxide layer. These constituents can generate mobile structural imperfections within the electrolyte medium. Furthermore, electrolyte penetration into microcracks present on the oxide layer surface can induce additional “random” structural defects. Collectively, these structural defects are termed cavitation nuclei. Under conditions of reduced external environmental pressure, dissolved gases within the liquid accumulate at these cavitation nuclei to form bubbles [42].

With the periodic filtration cycle of the electrolyte, the electrolyte is in a supersaturated state, as shown in Fig. 3 d). Due to the existence of pressure difference, the supersaturated gas in the electrolyte diffuses into the micro-cracks, and the gas pressure Pg2 inside the micro-cracks gradually increases. The micro-cracks on the surface of the oxide layer crisscross and interconnect. During the U-ELID pre-sharpening process, some cavitation bubbles directly escape from the micro-cracks. The grinding wheel moves at high frequency in the electrolyte in a reciprocating manner. Due to inertia, the electrolyte cannot follow the high-frequency vibration of the grinding wheel in time, resulting in a “desorption” phenomenon, and the local pressure drops sharply below the saturation vapor pressure. Under the action of the ultrasonic negative pressure phase, the internal pressure of the electrolyte decreases. When Pg> P0, the cavitation nuclei within the microcracks gradually grow, and the system tends to reach the state of minimum free energy. The gas–liquid interface inside the microcracks becomes planar, as illustrated in Fig. 3 e). When the gas pressure inside the microcrack continues to increase, the surface tension direction of the gas–liquid interface points to the gas side, and the gas surface presents a convex surface, as shown in Fig. 3 f). At this time, the surface tension additional pressure Pσ will partially offset the decrease in the local pressure of the liquid, and the pressure inside the cavitation nucleus is Pg=P0+Pσ. When the gas pressure inside the cavitation nucleus continues to rise, as illustrated in Fig. 3g), the gas–liquid interface further protrudes outward. The relatively small radius of curvature of the liquid leads to a continuous increase in the surface tension-induced additional pressure Pσ, which further counteracts the decrease in liquid pressure. When Pσ increases to a certain threshold, the liquid pressure will reach a critical value. At this point, if the liquid pressure continues to decrease, the gas–liquid interface will be pulled to the edge of the crack, causing the cavitation nuclei to escape from the microcracks and form free cavitation bubbles, as illustrated in Fig. 3 h). During the escape of free cavitation bubbles from microcracks, they also interact with cavitation bubbles in adjacent microcracks, leading to the formation of two or even multiple cavitation bubbles, as illustrated in Fig. 3 i) and 3 j).

From the above analysis, it can be concluded that the “mobile” structural defects in the electrolyte and the “random” structural defects in the oxide layer store a large number of cavitation nuclei. When a negative pressure region forms in the electrolyte, the gas–liquid interface ruptures, leading to the escape of a large number of cavitation bubbles [43]. Cavitation induced by ultrasonic vibration depends on whether the negative pressure amplitude of the acoustic wave exceeds the cavitation threshold of the electrolyte.

For larger cavitation bubbles in the electrolyte, their cavitation threshold is primarily determined by the static pressure of the electrolyte. For smaller cavitation bubbles in the grinding fluid, their cavitation threshold is primarily determined by the surface tension of the grinding fluid [44]. Cavitation bubbles migrate to the surface of the oxide layer, where they undergo rapid collapse or implosion, releasing intense shock waves and micro-jets. On one hand, these dynamic effects induce erosion and damage to the oxide layer surface, resulting in cavitation erosion of the oxide layer. On the other hand, the low-pressure environment generated by the negative pressure phase facilitates the penetration of the electrolyte into the microcracks on the oxide layer surface. Simultaneously, the shock waves from cavitation collapse agitate the detached oxide layer particles suspended in the electrolyte, thereby enhancing cooling efficiency and abrasive renewal rate, as well as mitigating grinding wheel clogging and thermal damage.

Influence of ultrasonic streaming on oxide layer

This study investigated the electrolyte flow field, current density distribution, and ion concentration distribution within the electrolytic zones during both ELID pre-sharpening and U-ELID pre-sharpening processes. Experimental methods

The parameters for electro-discharge dressing of the grinding wheel are as follows: grinding wheel rotational speed of 100 r/min, dressing wheel rotational speed of 60 r/min, interelectrode gap of 0.2 mm, and electrolyte flow rate of 10 L/min. The HDMY type ELID concentrate (specifically for hard and brittle materials) developed by Harbin Institute of Technology was used to prepare the electrolyte with a dilution ratio of 1:50 (concentrate: deionized water). The pH value is between 9.0 and 9.5, and the temperature is controlled at 25 ± 2℃. A dial indicator gauge was employed to measure radial runout values at designated points on the grinding wheel’s outer circumference until a roundness error below 3 μm was achieved, signifying dressing completion. As illustrated in the Fig. 10, three square pieces of high-temperature-resistant insulating tape were randomly and uniformly affixed to the circumferential surface of the grinding wheel, serving as control regions that were excluded from electrolysis. Upon completion of pre-dressing, the tapes were removed, and the observation areas at the interfaces between the taped and non-taped regions were examined using a VHX-2000 ultra-depth-of-field microscope. Five measurement points were randomly selected from each observation area, and the average value of the height differences across the fifteen measurement points was defined as the oxide film thickness of the grinding wheel. During the pre-sharpening process, a non-dense oxide layer with pores forms on the grinding wheel surface. According to Darcy’s Law [50], [51], [52], within the porous oxide layer, electrolyte can only diffuse and migrate through the pores; thus, porosity directly governs the connectivity and efficacy of ion transport pathways. As per Fick’s diffusion law[53], ion flux is inversely proportional to the diffusion layer thickness: an increase in diffusion layer thickness leads to a corresponding reduction in ion flux, whereas a decrease in diffusion layer thickness results in enhanced ion flux. From an electrochemical mechanism perspective, the oxide layer acts as a high-resistance insulating layer, and its inhibitory effect on the circuit current is primarily determined by film thickness – film thickness directly influences the length of ion migration paths and diffusion resistance. In accordance with Ohm’s Law, under constant voltage, current decreases significantly as resistance increases[54]. By contrast, the degree of densification affects local electrical conductivity mainly by reducing porosity and blocking electrolyte penetration channels, and thus functions as a regulatory factor. Consequently, the current value in the electrolytic circuit can serve as an indirect indicator of oxide layer thickness (the greater the thickness, the lower the current), while porosity directly reflects the densification degree of the oxide layer (the lower the porosity, the higher the densification) and acts as an indirect indicator of the oxide layer state. The experimental scheme for U-ELID pre-sharpening and grinding trials is detailed in Table 4. Each group of tests adopted 6 stratified feedings, with 4 axial reciprocating grinding operations performed after each feeding (i.e., 4 S per layer), totaling 24 unidirectional grinding strokes. Workpiece surface roughness was assessed at six uniformly distributed locations using a Taylor Surtronic S128 handheld roughness tester, with the reported value representing the arithmetic mean. Grinding wheel surface morphology was characterized via a VHX-2000 ultra-depth-of-field microscope, while 3D topographic of the workpiece surface was acquired using a NANOVEA ST400 3D profilometer.

Correction of the oxide layer thickness generation model

To determine the comprehensive influence coefficient kc of ultrasonic vibration during the U-ELID pre-sharpening stage, U-ELID pre-sharpening experiments were conducted on W40 metal-bonded grinding wheels employing the parameters specified in Table 4. The experimentally measured oxide layer thicknesses and their corresponding theoretically calculated values are summarized in Table 4.

a) The variation of theoretical and experimental values of oxide layer thickness over time; b), c), d), e) Comparison of oxide layer morphology of ELID pre-sharpening and U-ELID pre-sharpening at different pre-sharpening times.

As depicted in Fig. 11(a), post-model correction, both theoretical and measured oxide layer thickness values demonstrate an increasing trend with prolonged U-ELID pre-sharpening duration, ultimately reaching a plateau. This behavior originates from the initial oxidation phase exhibiting a relatively rapid chemical reaction rate, enabling swift film formation. Subsequently, as thickness stabilizes, the resultant oxide layer becomes non-conductive and self-sealing, thereby rendering it electrically insulating. This property elevates electrical resistance, causing a reduction in current density. Consequently, the electrochemical reaction rate decreases, and further significant increases in oxide layer thickness cease. The maximum discrepancy between theoretical predictions and experimental measurements remains within 12.1%, demonstrating the modified oxide layer thickness growth model's efficacy in accurately forecasting actual oxide layer thickness.

Oxide layers formed under ELID and U-ELID pre-sharpening conditions at durations of 30 s and 120 s were selected for comparison. As illustrated in Fig. 11 b)-e), U-ELID process yields a substantial reduction in surface pores and cracks on the grinding wheel relative to conventional ELID pre-sharpening, albeit accompanied by a slight reduction in oxide layer thickness. This phenomenon arises from ultrasonic vibration-induced electrolyte agitation and the generation of ultrasonic cavitation bubbles. Upon bubble collapse, the liberated energy generates localized high-pressure micro-jets and shock waves that directly impinge upon the grinding wheel surface. This impact disrupts the oxide layer, compromises its interfacial bonding with the substrate, and consequently promotes accelerated oxide layer detachment. Although the resultant oxide layer exhibits reduced thickness, the introduction of ultrasonic vibration concurrently enhances its structural uniformity and density.

Oxide layer quality generated by U-ELID pre-sharpening

The U-ELID pre-sharpening test was conducted on the 200# metal-bonded grinding wheels using the parameters in Table 4. Fig. 12 a) shows the trend of the sharpening current over time. Within 0––10 s, the loop current rapidly rises to its maximum value. The larger the loop voltage, the more sensitive the current response. This indicates that the voltage directly affects the intensity of the loop current. The higher the voltage, the faster the initial rising rate of the loop current, thereby shortening the time for the current to reach a steady state. It can increase the current density, thereby enhancing the dissolution rate of the metal bond. Under the same voltage, the initial rate of increase of the circuit current during U-ELID pre-sharpening process is not as fast as that during the ELID pre-sharpening process at the beginning. Subsequently, the rate of increase rises, and the peak current of U-ELID pre-sharpening is lower than that of ELID pre-sharpening. The cavitation effect and acoustic streaming produced when ultrasonic waves propagate in a liquid can effectively enhance the convection and diffusion of the solution, thereby accelerating the transfer rate of reactants to the electrode surface. However, a certain reaction time is required. Within the time frame of 10–120 s, the loop current exhibits an overall trend of fluctuation and decline, with the decreasing rate decelerating over time. This phenomenon indicates that the oxide layer formed on the surface of the anode grinding wheel increases the circuit resistance and induces electrode polarization, leading to a deviation of the actual current from the equilibrium potential. Consequently, the current presents a nonlinear decreasing trend. At a power supply voltage of 20 V, the loop current of U-ELID pre-sharpening system exhibits a slow declining rate. The low voltage leads to a small inter-electrode current, which results in a low dissolution rate of the metal bond and a slow formation rate of the oxide layer. Consequently, the impact on the current is relatively minor, thereby retarding the sharpening speed of the grinding wheel. When the power supply voltage is 60 V, the current decline rate of U-ELID pre-sharpening circuit significantly increases, the dissolution rate of the metal bond accelerates, and the generation rate of the oxide layer rises. At a power supply voltage of 40 V, the declining rate of the loop current in U-ELID pre-sharpening system falls between those observed at 20 V and 60 V. Under the 40 V condition, the oxide layer formed is relatively dense, leading to a rapid increase in the equivalent resistance between the electrodes, which quickly attains a stable state. Compared with ELID pre-sharpening, the loop current of U-ELID pre-sharpening decreases at a faster rate, indicating that the ultrasonic streaming effect increases the concentration of reactants on the electrode surface, and the oxide layer forms rapidly on the grinding wheel surface. The micro-jet and shock wave generated by the ultrasonic cavitation effect enhance the density of the oxide layer, making it less likely to fall off, and the resistance in the loop increases rapidly, resulting in an increased rate of decrease in the loop current. Ultimately, after 120 s, the loop current tends to stabilize. In comparison with ELID pre-sharpening, the loop current of U-ELID pre-sharpening is higher after reaching a steady state. This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact that the high-frequency ultrasonic vibration removes the weakly adherent oxide layer, leading to the formation of a thinner oxide layer with lower resistance, which in turn results in a higher loop current value in the steady state.

Grinding performance of oxide layers with different sharpening methods

Conventional grinding experiments were conducted on SiC ceramics using grinding wheels subjected to different pre-sharpening electrolytic voltages and durations. Pre-sharpening electrolytic parameters corresponding to superior workpiece surface roughness were screened out. Fig. 13 illustrates the influence of oxide layers formed under varying U-ELID pre-sharpening voltage and time conditions on workpiece surface roughness under conventional grinding conditions.

The variation trend of surface roughness of workpieces ground by conventional grinding with time under different U-ELID pre-sharpening voltages.

As depicted in Fig. 13, the surface roughness of the workpiece exhibits an overall decreasing trend with the extension of U-ELID pre-sharpening time. This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact that, as electrolysis proceeds on the grinding wheel surface, the dissolution of the metallic bond increases, which enhances the protrusion height and sharpness of abrasive grains while improving the grinding performance of the oxide layer, thereby reducing the workpiece surface roughness. In the initial stage of U-ELID pre-sharpening (0–60 s), the dissolution of the metallic bond on the grinding wheel surface is limited, and the ultrasonic high-frequency mechanical effect is not pronounced. The uneven protrusion height of abrasive grains leads to a relatively high surface roughness of the ground workpiece. During the intermediate stage (60–120 s), electrolysis continues, and both the ultrasonic high-frequency mechanical effect and acoustic streaming effect are intensified. With the dynamic dissolution of the metallic bond, the protrusion height of abrasive grains gradually becomes uniform, resulting in a significant reduction in surface roughness and a corresponding enhancement in the grinding performance of the oxide layer. In the stable stage (120–180 s), prolonged electrolysis causes the oxide layer to thicken continuously and cover the bond surface, leading to an increase in the equivalent resistance of the grinding wheel and a decrease in conductivity. Consequently, the electrolytic current gradually diminishes, and the dressing rate slows down. When the passivation film reaches a critical thickness, the electrolysis process is significantly inhibited. Even with sustained application of electrolytic voltage and ultrasonic high-frequency vibration, the dressing amount of the grinding wheel barely increases further, indicating the attainment of a “sharpen saturation” state. Grinding with the wheel pre-dressed in this stage results in stable workpiece surface roughness, with negligible changes in the grinding performance of the oxide layer.

When the electrolytic voltage is 20 V, the electrolytic current density is insufficient, which causes the dissolution of the metal binder on the grinding wheel surface to be slow, and the protrusion height of the abrasive grains is not enough, making it difficult to form a complete film layer. The grinding performance of the oxide layer is poor, resulting in a relatively large surface roughness value of the ground workpiece. When the electrolytic voltage is 60 V, a complete film layer is easily formed, and the grinding performance of the oxide layer is better than that at 20 V, with the surface roughness value of the workpiece reduced. However, the current density is too high, which is prone to local over-corrosion, and the generated oxide layer is relatively loose, with the abrasive grains retained by the oxide layer prone to fall off. When the electrolytic voltage is 40 V, the current density is moderate, the oxide layer is uniform and dense, the abrasive grains have a certain protrusion height and chip space, and the surface roughness of the workpiece has decreased by 38.8%, and can be as low as 0.30 μm, reaching the state of “effective sharpen but not over-saturation”. Therefore, “electrolytic voltage 40 V, pre-sharpening time 120 s” is selected as the optimal parameters for U-ELID pre-sharpening, providing favorable grinding conditions for the subsequent high-efficiency and precision grinding experiments of silicon carbide ceramics.

ELID grinding and U-ELID grinding experiments

The selected parameters were used to conduct the U-ELID pre-sharpening of the grinding wheel. Then, ELID grinding and U-ELID grinding tests were carried out. The stability of the online electrolytic dressing process was judged by observing the uniformity and compactness changes of the oxide layer. As shown in Fig. 14, the surface morphology of the grinding wheel after ELID grinding and U-ELID grinding at different grinding depths can be observed. It can be seen that the oxide layer on the surface of the ELID grinding wheel has pores and cracks, as well as pits left by the detachment of abrasive grains. Moreover, as the grinding depth increases, the number of abrasive grains worn decreases, while the number of abrasive grains broken and detached gradually increases, and the cracks become wider and more numerous. This is because as the grinding depth increases, the material removal amount of the workpiece increases, and the grinding force becomes larger, resulting in an increase in the number of abrasive grains broken and detached. Compared with ELID grinding, the surface contour of the oxide layer after U-ELID grinding is relatively flat. The oxide layer surface has both pits caused by the detachment of old abrasive grains and newly exposed unprocessed abrasive grains. There are only narrow and few microcracks on the oxide layer surface. This indicates that during U-ELID grinding, the combined effect of ultrasonic vibration can still make the oxide layer more compact, which is conducive to maintaining a stable grinding state. As the grinding depth increases, the number of abrasive grains in the wear state decreases, and the number of abrasive grains broken increases, but the number of abrasive grains broken is less than that in ELID grinding. This is because the periodic separation characteristic of the ultrasonic high-frequency mechanical action significantly reduces the continuous load borne by the abrasive grains, thereby reducing the friction force and grinding force.

However, in contrast to conventional ELID grinding, U-ELID grinding leverages the synergistic effect of ultrasonic vibration to substantially enhance electrolyte penetration and abrasive grain sharpening. This effectively suppresses detrimental phenomena such as workpiece burns and microcracks. As the grinding depth is reduced, the abrasive scratching marks become finer and more uniformly distributed, while the overall surface flatness approaches the high-quality standard achieved by ELID grinding under equivalent conditions. Owing to the auxiliary action of ultrasonic vibration, U-ELID grinding further diminishes grinding forces and abrasive wear, thereby ensuring enhanced stability in workpiece surface quality.

Fig. 15 i) shows the comparison of surface roughness between ELID grinding and U-ELID grinding under different grinding depths. As the grinding depth decreases, the reduction in Arithmetic Mean Surface Roughness (Sa) of the ELID grinding surface is 35%, while that of the U-ELID grinding surface is 44.2%. Compared with ELID grinding, the reduction in Sa of the U-ELID grinding surface increases by 9.2%. This result confirms that the introduction of ultrasonic vibration enables surface quality comparable to ELID grinding while significantly enhancing grinding efficiency.

Discussion

SiC ceramics have extremely high hardness and significant brittleness, which leads to rapid tool wear, easy cracking and chipping, and makes it difficult to control the machining accuracy. This study proposes the combination of ultrasonic vibration and online electrolytic dressing grinding to address the processing challenges of SiC ceramics. In U-ELID grinding, the oxide layer plays a pivotal role by forming a passivation layer on the grinding wheel surface through electrolytic reactions. This layer not only suppresses excessive electrolysis of the wheel but also maintains a stable protrusion height of abrasive grains and sufficient chip accommodation space. Furthermore, it enhances the metal bond’s ability to firmly anchor superhard abrasive grains, while the free-state oxide layer contributes to grinding and polishing effects on the workpiece surface, thereby significantly reducing subsurface microcracks.

The influence of ultrasonic high-frequency vibration on oxide layer formation is a complex multi-factor coupling process. As illustrated in Fig. 16, it primarily acts through the synergistic interplay of the following three mechanisms:

(1)High-frequency mechanical action: Intense mechanical vibrations induced by ultrasonic high-frequency oscillation effectively disrupt the oxide layer formed on the workpiece surface. This exposes nascent metallic substrates, providing additional active sites for oxide layer growth. Concurrently, this action enhances interfacial electrolyte distribution uniformity and facilitates ionic transport, thereby accelerating oxide nucleation and growth kinetics. Furthermore, the resultant micro-high-frequency impact effect mitigates material embrittlement tendencies, reduces edge chipping and microcrack formation, refines chip morphology, and diminishes cutting thermal loads.

(2)Ultrasonic cavitation effect: During collapse, cavitation bubbles generate localized transient conditions including extreme temperatures (exceeding 5000 K), pressures (reaching 1000 atmospheres), and intense shock waves. These conditions markedly enhance electrolyte chemical activity, promoting electrochemical reactions essential for oxide layer development. Additionally, cavitation-induced shock waves effectively remove surface contaminants and adsorbed layers from the oxide structure, thereby improving the purity and density of the resulting oxide layer.

(3)Ultrasonic acoustic streaming effect: Directional flow induced by acoustic streaming induces agitation within the electrolyte, facilitating accelerated and homogenized transport of reactants (e.g., oxygen ions) to the workpiece surface while concurrently removing reaction products (e.g., hydrogen ions). This process sustains a stable concentration gradient, thereby promoting continuous and uniform oxide layer growth.

Mechanism of ultrasonic high-frequency vibration on oxide layer formation; a) High-frequency mechanical action; b) Cavitation effect; c) Acoustic streaming.

This triad of mechanisms operates not in isolation but as mutually coupled and synergistic processes. The high-frequency mechanical effect supplies the mechanical impetus and active sites requisite for oxide layer formation; the cavitation effect enhances reaction kinetics through the generation of extreme physicochemical conditions; and the acoustic streaming effect optimizes mass transport dynamics. Their concerted action enables accelerated formation, enhanced structural quality, and superior functional performance of the oxide layer under ultrasonic assistance.

This study revealed that as the grinding depth decreased from 5 μm to 2 μm, the surface profile of the oxide layer following U-ELID grinding exhibited enhanced flatness and density, facilitating the maintenance of a stable grinding state. Although the Sa value of U-ELID ground surface was comparable to that achieved by conventional ELID grinding, the Sa value reduction for ELID grinding was 35%, whereas U-ELID grinding demonstrated a more substantial reduction of 44.2%. These results clearly indicate that the introduction of ultrasonic vibration significantly enhanced the grinding efficiency for SiC ceramics. This observation aligns with the documented advantages of ultrasonic high-frequency vibration in SiC ceramic grinding reported in references [55] and [56], effectively demonstrating that the integration of ultrasonic vibration with ELID online electrolytic dressing not only resolves the issues of rapid grinding wheel wear and low grinding efficiency inherent in ELID grinding, but also effectively suppresses thermal damage and material defects. Furthermore, the distinctive ultra-precision and isometric cutting effect inherent to U-ELID grinding significantly improves the processing stability and yield of SiC ceramic components.

In addition, our current research primarily investigates the influence mechanism of ultrasonic vibration on oxide layer formation. However, U-ELID grinding process for SiC ceramics involves the alternating generation and removal of the oxide layer coupled with thermomechanical interactions within the grinding zone. The interaction mechanism of ELID electrical parameters (such as ELID power supply voltage, duty cycle, and inter-electrode gap) and grinding parameters (such as feed rate and cutting depth) in conjunction with ultrasonic waves remains unclear. Future research will integrate the coupling effects of the electric field (ELID parameters), ultrasonic vibration field and thermal field, establish a cross-scale theoretical framework, and reveal the influence laws of parameter interaction on the evolution of material microstructure. In response to the nonlinear response of ultrasonic vibration and electrolytic dressing, a dynamic system model will be developed to predict the critical threshold of parameter mutation on processing stability. By introducing machine learning algorithms and through real-time monitoring of surface quality feedback, a quantitative prediction model will be established to optimize the combination of process parameters and achieve adaptive closed-loop control of processing, further enhancing the stability of surface quality.This is crucial for the effective implementation and broader adoption of U-ELID grinding in SiC ceramic processing.

Conclusion

This study commences with an investigation into three principal aspects: the high-frequency mechanical effect, cavitation effect, and acoustic streaming effect of ultrasonic waves, to elucidate the underlying mechanisms through which ultrasonic vibration influences the U-ELID oxide layer. A theoretical model describing the growth kinetics of the oxide layer thickness during U-ELID pre-sharpening is established. Furthermore, the film-forming characteristics of the oxide layer during the pre-sharpening phase in U-ELID grinding of SiC ceramics are examined. Experimental investigations involving U-ELID pre-sharpening and grinding under varied parameters are systematically conducted. The principal conclusions derived are as follows:

(1)Equivalent force analysis of the micro-crack segmented oxide layer demonstrates that the “screening” effect induced by ultrasonic high-frequency mechanical action constitutes the key mechanism for enhancing the density and integrity of the U-ELID pre-sharpening oxide layer. Concurrently, this study analyzes the growth behavior of cavitation nuclei within the oxide layer micro-cracks and their conditions for escape. It was observed that ultrasonic vibration induces intense cavitation within the electrolyte, effectively enhancing cooling efficiency and abrasive renewal rate, while reducing wheel clogging and workpiece thermal damage.
