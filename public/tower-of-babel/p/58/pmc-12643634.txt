Acupuncture combined with modified Zhi Gan Cao decoction in the treatment of frequent premature ventricular contractions
Authors: Rui Liu, Wei Li, Xing Zhao, Qing Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the clinical efficacy of acupuncture combined with modified Zhi Gan Cao decoction in the treatment of frequent premature ventricular contractions (PVC) and its effect on cardiac function and analyzes the correlation between the improvement of TCM syndromes and the reduction of PVC, as well as the efficacy differences under different PVC burden levels. A retrospective cohort study was conducted to collect data of patients diagnosed with frequent PVC in our hospital from April 2023 to April 2025. After propensity score matching, 200 patients were included and divided into a treatment group (acupuncture combined with modified Zhi Gan Cao decoction) and a control group (conventional western medicine treatment), with 100 cases in each group. The treatment cycle lasted 4 weeks. Changes in 24-hour PVC frequency, PVC burden, left ventricular ejection fraction, left ventricular end-diastolic diameter, N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide, and TCM syndrome scores were evaluated before and after treatment. Efficacy differences were analyzed, and the correlation between TCM syndrome improvement and PVC reduction as well as the impact of PVC burden level on efficacy were explored. The treatment group showed superior results in reducing 24-hour PVC frequency (decrease of 62.3% vs 37.8%), PVC burden (decrease of 63.2% vs 36.5%), TCM syndrome scores (decrease of 57.0% vs 32.6%), and improving left ventricular ejection fraction (+2.6% vs +0.8%) compared with the control group (all P < .05). The total effective rate was 93.0%, with a marked effective rate of 54.0%. TCM syndrome improvement was positively correlated with PVC reduction (r = 0.812, P < .0001). Subgroup analysis showed that patients with high PVC burden (≥10%) benefited more significantly in terms of cardiac function and symptom improvement. Acupuncture combined with modified Zhi Gan Cao decoction demonstrates good efficacy in treating frequent PVC, effectively reducing arrhythmic burden, alleviating symptoms, and potentially improving left heart function. TCM syndrome improvement is closely related to PVC reduction, with high-burden patients benefiting more, suggesting a promising clinical application of this integrated approach worthy of further promotion and in-depth study.

1. Introduction

Premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) are among the most common types of arrhythmia, particularly prevalent in the elderly and in patients with cardiovascular diseases. The current consensus defines frequent PVCs as those exceeding 500 beats within 24 hours or accounting for more than 10% of total heartbeats.[1] Although most PVCs are benign, persistent high PVC burden – especially in patients with structural heart abnormalities – can trigger or exacerbate left ventricular dysfunction and may even lead to PVC-induced cardiomyopathy.[2,3] Therefore, early and effective intervention to reduce PVC burden and its related complications has become a clinical priority.

In western medicine, treatment mainly relies on antiarrhythmic drugs such as β-blockers and amiodarone, as well as high-frequency radiofrequency ablation. While pharmacologic therapy is widely used, its effectiveness varies, and some patients experience limited efficacy or intolerable adverse effects, including amiodarone-induced thyroid dysfunction, elevated liver enzymes, and QT interval prolongation.[4,5] Radiofrequency ablation, though effective, carries procedural risks, high cost, and is not suitable for all PVC patients – especially those with mild symptoms or moderate frequency. Thus, there is a pressing need for safer, more acceptable treatment options that address both subjective symptoms and objective clinical indicators.

In traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), frequent PVCs are categorized under “palpitations” (xin ji, jing ji), with pathogenesis often involving “heart qi deficiency, “yin-yang imbalance, “phlegm-damp obstruction, or “blood stasis.”[6] The classic formula Zhi Gan Cao decoction, recorded in Treatise on Cold Damage Disorders (Shang Han Lun), is traditionally used for irregular pulses and palpitations, with functions of tonifying qi, nourishing yin, warming yang, benefiting the pulse, and calming the spirit. Modern pharmacological research suggests that Zhi Gan Cao decoction and its modifications may improve calcium channel activity, stabilize electrophysiology, and modulate autonomic function in myocardial cells.[7,8] Acupuncture therapy, through regulating meridian qi and blood, coordinating the functions of the heart, kidney, spleen, and lung, promotes smooth qi flow and emotional stability. Recent studies have shown its potential influence on sympathetic-parasympathetic balance and heart rate variability, thereby aiding in arrhythmia control.[9,10]

The synergistic mechanism of acupuncture and herbal medicine likely involves multi-target, bidirectional regulation – addressing both subjective symptoms (such as palpitations, chest tightness, fatigue, and insomnia) and potentially affecting arrhythmic activity and cardiac function.[11] Although small-scale studies have indicated that acupuncture combined with herbal medicine has some therapeutic effect on PVCs, there remains a lack of large-sample, evidence-based research to systematically evaluate its multidimensional efficacy. Furthermore, clinical observations suggest that patients with higher PVC burden often exhibit more pronounced cardiac dysfunction and symptoms. However, whether such patients derive greater benefit from TCM interventions remains unclear and merits further investigation.

This study adopts a retrospective cohort design, using propensity score matching (PSM) to rigorously control for baseline differences. It systematically compares the efficacy of acupuncture combined with modified Zhi Gan Cao decoction versus conventional western treatment in alleviating symptoms, reducing PVC frequency and burden, and improving cardiac function. Additionally, it explores the correlation between improvements in TCM syndrome scores and reductions in PVC burden, as well as the heterogeneity in response to TCM treatment across different PVC burden levels. The goal is to provide evidence-based support for optimizing personalized treatment strategies and expanding integrated TCM-western approaches in clinical practice.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Anzhen Hospital (Approval No. 2023-ANZ-042). This study was a retrospective cohort study. Data were collected from inpatients and outpatients diagnosed with frequent PVCs at our hospital between April 2023 and April 2025. According to the treatment methods previously received by patients, they were divided into a treatment group (acupuncture combined with modified Zhi Gan Cao decoction) and a control group (conventional western medical treatment).

To minimize the impact of baseline differences between groups, the PSM method was employed. A 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching strategy was used, with matching variables including age, gender, disease duration, baseline PVC frequency, and comorbidities (such as hypertension and diabetes). A total of 200 patients with balanced baselines were ultimately included, with 100 patients in each group.

2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

2.2.1. Inclusion criteria

Met the diagnostic criteria for frequent PVCs (Holter monitoring showing ≥500 PVCs/24 hours); age between 18 and 80 years, regardless of gender; complete clinical data and ECG/Holter records before and after treatment; received continuous and standardized treatment for ≥4 weeks.

2.2.2. Exclusion criteria

Presence of structural heart disease (e.g., cardiomyopathy, heart failure, severe valvular disease); severe hepatic or renal insufficiency, bleeding tendency, or malignant tumors; history of using other medications that affect heart rhythm (e.g., digitalis); change in treatment regimen or poor adherence during the treatment period; incomplete data or loss to follow-up.

2.3. Group treatment methods

2.3.1. Treatment group (acupuncture combined with modified Zhi Gan Cao decoction)

Patients in the treatment group received acupuncture combined with modified Zhi Gan Cao decoction for 4 consecutive weeks. The herbal formula was based on the classical Zhi Gan Cao decoction and adjusted according to syndrome differentiation, with daily decoction taken in 2 divided doses for 28 days.

For acupuncture, treatment followed the principle of syndrome differentiation with symptom-based acupoint selection. Core acupoints included Taiyuan (LU9), Daling (PC7), Lieque (LU7), and Neiguan (PC6). Additional points were selected according to symptoms: Shanzhong, Xinshu, Yinlingquan for chest tightness/pain; Zusanli, Shenmen for palpitations; Zusanli, Qihai for shortness of breath; and Sishencong, Taixi for insomnia.

Standard needling techniques were applied with a balanced reinforcing-reducing method. After insertion and achieving deqi, needles were retained for 30 minutes with manual stimulation every 10 minutes. Treatment was administered once daily, 7 sessions per course, with a 3-day break between courses, for a total of 3 courses.

2.3.2. Control group (conventional western medical treatment)

Patients in the control group received stepwise antiarrhythmic therapy over a 4-week period. Initially, all patients were prescribed β-blocker monotherapy: either metoprolol sustained-release tablets at a dose of 47.5 mg once daily or bisoprolol at 5 mg once daily. After 2 weeks of treatment, 24-hour Holter ECG was repeated. If PVCs had decreased by <50% or symptoms persisted, the β-blocker dosage was increased (metoprolol to 95 mg/d or bisoprolol to 10 mg/d). If no clinical improvement was observed, amiodarone was added. The initial loading dose of amiodarone was 0.4 g/d, taken orally in 2 divided doses and continued until PVC reduction reached ≥50% or for a maximum of 2 weeks, after which a maintenance dose of 0.2 g/d was administered.

During the treatment period, patients underwent rigorous monitoring: weekly ECGs to evaluate QT interval, with a target QTc < 500 ms; thyroid function and liver enzyme levels were checked every 2 weeks before and during treatment; chest X-rays were performed at baseline and at the end of week 4. If heart rate dropped below 50 bpm or systolic blood pressure fell below 90 mm Hg, the β-blocker dose was halved. Amiodarone was discontinued in cases of thyroid dysfunction or QT prolongation. All patients maintained serum potassium levels at ≥4.0 mmol/L and were prohibited from using any other antiarrhythmic drugs or Chinese herbal preparations.

2.4. Data collection

All clinical data used in this study were sourced from the hospital’s electronic medical record system and ECG monitoring database. Collected information included demographic data (age, sex, disease duration), underlying diseases (such as hypertension, diabetes, and coronary artery disease), lifestyle habits (smoking and alcohol consumption), physical indicators (weight, height, body mass index), and baseline laboratory test results (including serum potassium and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide [NT-proBNP] levels).

PVC-related assessments were uniformly conducted using 24-hour Holter monitoring, recording total daily PVC count, PVC burden (percentage of total beats), and morphology (monomorphic or polymorphic). These metrics were compared between baseline and the end of week 4. Non-sustained ventricular tachycardia was also documented as a comorbidity.

Cardiac function was evaluated using echocardiography, including measurements of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD), and left atrial diameter. NT-proBNP, a biomarker of cardiac volume load and functional status, was measured via blood sampling at baseline and at the end of week 4.

Subjective symptoms and psychological status were assessed using standardized questionnaires, including palpitation and fatigue symptom scores, the generalized anxiety disorder 7-item scale, and the patient health questionnaire-9 for depression. To evaluate the efficacy of TCM differentiation and treatment, the TCM syndrome score scale was used, covering symptoms such as palpitations, chest tightness, shortness of breath, and insomnia. Scores were assigned based on severity, and changes and improvement percentages were calculated before and after treatment.

All data collection was independently completed by 2 researchers using double-entry verification to ensure accuracy and completeness. Only cases meeting criteria for complete data, standard treatment duration, and proper follow-up were included to ensure the reliability of subsequent statistical analyses.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Data collation and statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 (Armonk) and GraphPad Prism 9.0. All data were first tested for normality. Variables conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± standard deviation), and comparisons between groups were performed using independent samples t-tests. Variables not conforming to normal distribution were expressed as median [interquartile range], and between-group comparisons were conducted using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test.

Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (%) and compared between groups using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. For within-group comparisons of continuous variables before and after treatment, paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were applied depending on distribution.

Changes in indicators before and after treatment were calculated as “posttreatment value − pretreatment value,” and improvement rates were calculated as “(pretreatment value − posttreatment value)/pretreatment value × 100%.” The total effective rate was defined as the proportion of patients with a ≥30% improvement in symptom scores, while the marked effective rate was defined as a ≥70% improvement.

To enhance comparability between groups, the study employed the PSM method. Matching variables included age, gender, disease duration, 24-hour PVC count, baseline cardiac function parameters, and major comorbidities. A 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching approach was used with a caliper of 0.2 to achieve baseline balance.

Correlation analysis between the degree of improvement in TCM syndrome scores and the reduction rate of PVCs was conducted using Pearson’s correlation coefficient, with scatter plots drawn to illustrate the linear trend and degree of fit.

All statistical tests were 2-sided, and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics and TCM syndrome score comparison

A total of 200 patients with frequent PVCs were included in this study, with 100 cases in each group. The treatment group consisted of 52 males and 48 females, with a mean age of 56.8 years. The control group included 50 males and 50 females, with a mean age of 57.1 years. There were no statistically significant differences in gender or age between the 2 groups.

After PSM, no significant differences were observed between the treatment and control groups in terms of disease duration, 24-hour PVC count, PVC burden, heart rate, LVEF, LVEDD, and left atrial diameter. Similarly, baseline characteristics such as chronic comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, diabetes), smoking and alcohol history, psychological status scores, body mass index, serum potassium, and NT-proBNP levels were well balanced between the 2 groups.

The TCM syndrome scores before treatment were also comparable between the 2 groups, indicating a similar degree of subjective symptom severity and establishing a solid foundation for comparative analysis. Detailed baseline data are presented in Table 1.

3.2. Comparison of PVC improvement

Before treatment, the frequency and burden of PVCs were comparable between the 2 groups. After 4 weeks of treatment, the group receiving acupuncture combined with modified Zhi Gan Cao decoction showed significantly greater improvement in PVC-related outcomes.

Specifically, in the treatment group, the average 24-hour PVC count decreased from approximately 11,237 to 4283 beats – a reduction of over 60%. In contrast, the control group saw a decrease from 11,094 to 6874 beats, representing a reduction of <40%. The difference between the 2 groups was statistically significant (P < .001).

Regarding PVC burden, the treatment group showed a decrease from 9.8% to 3.6%, while the control group decreased from 9.6% to 6.1%. The improvement rate in the treatment group (63.2%) was markedly higher than that of the control group (36.5%), with a statistically significant difference (P < .001), as shown in Table 2.

Regarding the distribution of clinical efficacy, more than half of the patients in the treatment group (54%) achieved the “markedly effective” standard (≥70% reduction in TCM scores), compared with only 21% in the control group. These results highlight the clear advantage of acupuncture combined with Chinese herbal medicine in alleviating subjective symptoms in patients with frequent PVCs.

3.5. Correlation analysis between TCM syndrome improvement and PVC reduction

To explore whether the improvement in subjective symptoms aligns with the objective reduction in arrhythmia, we analyzed the relationship between the decrease in TCM syndrome scores and the reduction rate of 24-hour PVCs in the treatment group. The results revealed a significant positive correlation between the 2 variables (Pearson r = 0.812, P < .0001), indicating that the greater the reduction in TCM scores, the greater the decrease in PVCs.

The scatter plot shows that most data points are closely distributed around the regression trend line, suggesting a good fit and reinforcing the stability of this association. This finding implies that acupuncture combined with modified Zhi Gan Cao decoction not only effectively alleviates clinical symptoms but also significantly reduces PVC burden. 4. Discussion

This study demonstrates that acupuncture combined with modified Zhi Gan Cao decoction yields significant therapeutic effects in the treatment of frequent PVCs, outperforming conventional western medical therapy across multiple clinical dimensions.

First, in terms of PVC frequency and burden, the treatment group achieved a reduction of over 60% in both 24-hour PVC count and burden – significantly more than the control group. This suggests that acupuncture and herbal medicine may not only regulate autonomic nervous system activity but also enhance myocardial electrophysiological stability.[12] The Zhi Gan Cao decoction contains ingredients such as Zhi Gan Cao, Mai Dong, Sheng Di, and E Jiao, which are known to tonify qi and yin, restore the pulse, and calm the spirit. These components have been shown to modulate ion channels and protect against myocardial ischemia.[13] Meanwhile, acupuncture at points such as Taiyuan, Daling, and Neiguan may balance sympathetic and parasympathetic tone, improve vagal activity, and stabilize heart rhythm.[14]

In terms of symptom relief and TCM syndrome score improvement, the treatment group showed significantly greater reductions in core symptoms like palpitations, chest tightness, shortness of breath, and insomnia. This superiority likely reflects not only symptom-specific TCM modulation but also broader effects on heart rate variability and emotional regulation.[15] Acupuncture has been shown to stimulate central neurotransmitter release, such as serotonin and GABA, which may help reduce anxiety and improve sleep, indirectly alleviating symptom perception.[16]

Cardiac function also improved more markedly in the treatment group, as reflected by greater increases in LVEF, reductions in LVEDD, and declines in NT-proBNP. These findings suggest the combined treatment may not only address electrical instability but also help delay or reverse early-stage functional cardiomyopathy.[17] Ingredients such as Dang Shen, Sheng Di, and E Jiao may provide antioxidant effects, enhance microcirculation, and protect myocardial cells, while acupuncture may support myocardial contractility and reduce sympathetic overactivity – key benefits for patients with frequent PVCs.[18]

Correlation analysis further confirmed a strong positive relationship between TCM syndrome score improvement and PVC reduction, suggesting a consistent alignment between subjective symptom relief and objective electrophysiological improvement. This consistency may enhance patient compliance and satisfaction, and hints at a possible link between TCM syndrome differentiation and underlying electrophysiological mechanisms – worthy of further study.

Subgroup analysis revealed that patients with higher baseline PVC burden (≥10%) experienced more pronounced benefits, including greater improvements in cardiac function, PVC reduction, and symptom relief. This may be due to the greater reversibility of myocardial strain in high-burden patients and their higher sensitivity to intervention. It also suggests that TCM may be particularly effective in early intervention to prevent progression to PVC-induced cardiomyopathy.[19–21] These findings have clinical implications for personalized treatment, indicating that patients with high PVC burden may be especially suitable candidates for integrated TCM-western approaches.

Despite the encouraging results, this study has several limitations. It is a single-center retrospective study, and although PSM was used to control for confounding, selection and information biases cannot be ruled out. The follow-up duration was only 4 weeks, which does not allow for assessment of the long-term sustainability of treatment effects or the risk of PVC recurrence. Future studies should therefore extend the follow-up period to evaluate the persistence of efficacy and the likelihood of arrhythmia relapse. In addition, because this was a retrospective analysis, data collection was limited to routinely available clinical records. As a result, no mechanistic biomarkers – such as inflammatory factors or oxidative stress indicators – were measured, which restricts further insight into the biological pathways underlying the intervention. Treatment adherence was assessed mainly through clinical records, which may lack full objectivity. Moreover, although we observed a significant correlation between TCM symptom improvement and PVC reduction, the relatively limited sample size did not allow for more detailed subgroup analyses of different PVC burden levels after controlling for potential confounders. Larger prospective studies are warranted to address this issue.

In conclusion, acupuncture combined with modified Zhi Gan Cao decoction appears to offer significant advantages in reducing PVC burden, improving symptoms, and enhancing cardiac function – particularly in patients with high PVC burden. This integrative strategy shows strong potential for clinical application. Future multicenter, prospective, long-term randomized controlled trials are needed to further validate its efficacy, explore mechanisms, and refine intervention protocols.
