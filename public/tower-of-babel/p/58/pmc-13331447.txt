Introduction

Adults in decent employment experience greater financial security, higher social status in society, and more meaningful, purposeful lives [1]. Compared with work-disabled adults, they have better mental health [2] and lower pain [3], reduced risk of self-harm and suicide [4], lower use of health and social care resources [5], less financial distress [6], and have a 5–10 year longer life expectancy [7]. Work disability in working-aged adults is caused by various health conditions, but the leading causes are mental health conditions and musculoskeletal disorders [1]. The focus of this narrative review is on disability for work caused by musculoskeletal pain.

Musculoskeletal pain has long affected humans (e.g. back pain was described in ancient papyri) but disability from back pain has risen by 50% since 1990, increasing most rapidly in low- and middle-income countries [8]. Our aim is to re-examine work disability caused by musculoskeletal pain by comparing the extensive evidence on low back pain with that for shoulder pain and elbow/forearm pain. We consider the modes of diagnosis and treatment of pain across these sites and explore why, despite substantial knowledge of their epidemiology and risk factors, work disability, with its accompanying disadvantages, is increasing and consequently the burden of health inequalities continues to widen. We then use this evidence to highlight the need for new approaches.

Low back pain

The burden

The most recent data from the UK Health and Safety Executive (HSE) show that the 2023/24 rates of work-related musculoskeletal disorders are similar to those prior to the Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS‑CoV‑2) (COVID-19) pandemic in 2018/19 with 543 000 total cases (prevalence rate 1600/100 000 workers) [9]. They accounted for a third of all work-related ill-health and a quarter (26%) of working days lost. In total, the prevalence rate of work-related back pain was 690/100 000 workers resulting in 2 550 000 working days lost [9]. Similar patterns are seen globally [10–12]. Disability from back pain has increased by over 50% since 1990, with the greatest impact among people of working age; it is the commonest cause of medically certified sick leave and early retirement in Europe and accounts for more sick days than any other musculoskeletal condition in the USA [10, 13].

Low back pain causes greater work disability among people from socio-economically deprived circumstances with lesser educational attainment who are generally doing the least autonomous and most physically demanding jobs. These groups also have fewer opportunities for alternative employment opportunities, thereby worsening health inequalities. The prevalence of low back pain is increasing most rapidly in low- and middle-income countries [8], where it has been shown to cause similar disability for work among rural farmers, compounding its economic impacts [14].

Risk factors and causation of low back pain

Despite the substantial disability caused by back pain, a specific cause is rarely identified [10]. This is the case despite numerous recognized risk factors (see Table 1) [15–18]. Although relatively under-studied, low back pain rarely appears to be effectively prevented by occupational intervention strategies aimed at reduction of lifting, bending, heavy manual tasks, awkward postures, and performing activities while tired or distracted [19]. Prognostically, low back pain follows a chronic episodic course which relapses and remits over time with poorer outcomes best predicted by general ill-health (poor self-rated health, psychological ill-health, other chronic disease) and/or previous episodes of low back pain [10].

Treatment of low back pain

Despite multiple trials and the use of increasingly complex imaging techniques, there is no evidence that dividing most cases of non-specific low back pain into presumed diagnostic categories improves treatment outcomes. There is also clear evidence that much of the care provided for low back pain is non-evidence-based, low value, or even harmful [12]. For example, among workers with accepted compensation claims for low back pain, physical treatments have been associated with more delayed return-to-work [20]. In workplaces, a biomedical model reinforces a commonly-held belief that low back pain results from injury, implying blame or negligence, and the need for compensation.

Recent evidence suggests that promoting a biomedical model of low back pain within communities increases the associated disability [13]. At the same time, the expansion of costly diagnostic and treatment services has driven overuse of unnecessary low-value care, while more affordable, high-value care remains underused [11]. This is demonstrably the case in high-income countries but is increasingly apparent in low- and middle-income countries. Opioids, gabapentinoids, spinal fusion surgery, spinal cord stimulation, and regenerative therapies have been widely adopted over the past two decades despite limited evidence of benefit for low back pain and insufficient evaluation of harm [11].

Guidelines for managing low back pain

As early as the 1990s, evidence of the disabling impact of biomedical approaches to low back pain led to a range of evidence-based international guidelines with broadly similar recommendations [21]. These emphasized history-taking and examination to rule out rarer pathology requiring specific and/or urgent management, screening for risk factors for chronicity (including psychosocial factors and work factors); avoiding unnecessary imaging and other investigations; promoting a patient-centred biopsychosocial approach to education and maintenance of physical activity; supporting continued work participation and avoidance of prolonged sickness absence; and minimizing drug use, particularly harmful prescriptions such as strong opioids [21].

More recent publications have reinforced these principles, with greater emphasis on active self-management and reserving pharmacological therapies as second-line options [11]. Foster and colleagues have proposed some new approaches to reduce disability from low back pain, including integrating clinical care with occupational health, reforming compensation and disability policies, and promoting healthier beliefs about back pain to influence public behaviour. Only time will tell how successful these suggested approaches will be in reducing the burden of disability from low back pain.

While low back pain accounted for the largest proportion (43%) of work-related musculoskeletal disorders according to the 2023/24 UK data shown above, the next biggest cause (37%) of the burden came from disorders mainly affecting the upper limbs or neck [9]. The next sections will compare and contrast non-specific low back pain with non-specific musculoskeletal pain occurring at two upper limb sites: the distal upper limb and the shoulder.

Shoulder pain

The burden of shoulder pain

Shoulder pain is highly prevalent, affecting an estimated 16% of the population (range 0.67%–55.2%) [22]. Like back pain, it is a common cause of healthcare use and disability for work, resulting in substantial direct and indirect costs [23].

Risk factors and causation of shoulder pain

The currently established risk factors for shoulder pain are remarkably alike those identified for low back pain (Table 1). Over the past century, a wide range of patho-anatomical diagnoses for shoulder pain have been proposed, including conditions affecting different soft tissue components of the shoulder region, such as ligaments, muscles, capsule, tendons, labrum, and bursae. For rotator cuff disease, nomenclature has varied somewhat (e.g. rotator cuff tendinitis, subacromial bursitis, impingement syndrome), particularly as imaging techniques have become more sensitive. Excluding glenohumeral joint osteoarthritis, there is little evidence that separating different pathoanatomical shoulder conditions, either clinically or by imaging, improves treatment outcomes or prognosis [24]. As with non-specific low back pain, advances in imaging techniques and increased use of imaging have not translated into better outcomes (pain, disability, workability). A possible exception is the subset of people being considered for repair of full-thickness rotator cuff tears, but a systematic review of the evidence for surgical repair has failed to find convincing evidence of benefit [25].

Treatment of shoulder pain

Rotator cuff disease

Multiple systematic reviews of treatments for rotator cuff disease show little evidence beyond short-term benefit (oral NSAIDs, glucocorticoid injections, therapeutic exercise, and manual therapy) [26]. Similarly, in people with presumed rotator cuff pathology or impingement syndrome, there has been no benefit found for subacromial decompression surgery compared with sham surgery [27]. Evidence also suggests that shoulder pain persists beyond 6–12 months in at least one-third of patients [26, 28], and current treatments do not appear to reduce chronicity or relate to baseline examination findings [25].

As with low back pain, despite extensive research and numerous randomized controlled trials, shoulder pain appears to be a common disabling symptom for which, to date, diagnosis, imaging, and treatment options have not substantially reduced the risk of disability or chronicity. Unfortunately, as with back pain, there is also evidence of considerable low-value care being provided [29].

Elbow/forearm pain

The burden of elbow/forearm pain

Elbow/forearm pain is another major cause of work disability [30]. Although not separately reported in the UK HSE data, population studies have estimated a prevalence of pain lasting a day or longer in the past 7 days of 19% and 21% in working-aged men and women, respectively [31].

Risk factors/causation of elbow/forearm pain

As shown in Table 1, risk factors for pain in the elbow/forearm are similar to those for low back pain, including occupational, mechanical, and psychosocial factors, as well as mental health and wellbeing, and beliefs/perceptions [15–17]. There have also been major occupation-related epidemics of elbow/forearm pain, such as the outbreak of Repetitive Strain Injury, which occurred in the 1980s in Australia, where many workers developed severe, chronic, and disabling arm pain [32].

Treatment of elbow/forearm pain

Management of pain in the elbow/forearm typically involves a specific diagnosis of conditions such as epicondylitis based on history, examination, and imaging. Following this, arm pain attributed to epicondylitis should be treated with combinations of splints or orthoses, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, glucocorticoid injections, physical therapy, and, in resistant cases, surgery. It is interesting, therefore, to explore the evidence of the effectiveness of this approach (see Table 2) [33–38]. Evidence from systematic reviews shows only limited benefit for any of these treatment modalities. However, the meta-analysis by Ikonen et al. found that symptoms resolve in around 90% of people with untreated epicondylitis in the placebo arms of randomized controlled trials, suggesting a favourable natural history [39].

Given the lack of convincing evidence for the effectiveness of differentiating the above specific conditions and treating them, a multicentre randomized controlled trial was undertaken, which recruited anybody referred for physiotherapy with pain affecting the elbow or forearm [40]. Participants were randomized into three arms—immediate physiotherapy vs delayed (usual waiting-list), or physiotherapy providing different information during the delay. One group received conventional injury-diagnosis-treatment information consistent with the medical model and the other group received the ‘arm book’ [41], which was derived similarly to the ‘back book’ in avoiding the medical language and featured biopsychosocial messages about maintaining activities and having expectations of good recovery. After 6 months, the primary outcome was the absence of disability according to the modified Disability of the Arm Shoulder and Hand scale. Strikingly, the group who were least disabled at 6 months follow-up were those who received delayed physiotherapy but given advice to remain active (Fig. 1) [40]. Disability in the other two groups was comparable. Subsequently, the trial data were analysed for the two-thirds of participants who were in paid work when recruited [42]. Although this secondary analysis was under-powered, again the levels of disability appeared less among those with delayed physiotherapy after biopsychosocial advice, including among those who fulfilled diagnostic criteria for lateral epicondylitis at baseline (Fig. 2) [42].

Although elbow/forearm pain has been less studied than low back pain, many parallels can be drawn. Risk factor profiles are very similar (Table 1), and current treatment paradigms suggest little benefit from separating states of disease into presumed diagnostic entities. There is also emerging evidence supporting de-medicalization and avoidance of prolonged rest.

Multi-site pain and pain sensitization

Until the last two decades or so, most research on musculoskeletal pain focused on incidence, prevalence, risk factors, or treatment options for pain at a single site (most commonly the low back). However, it has become clear that, at least in the workplace, few people actually report single-site musculoskeletal pain when asked. In the Cultural and Psychosocial Influences on Disability (CUPID) study of over 8000 workers doing manual work, nursing, and office work, only a small proportion (<10%) reported single-site pain [43]. In the same study, the authors found that mechanical risk factors were most strongly associated with single-site pain, while psychosocial factors (e.g. beliefs, fear of pain, somatization, mental health, psychological distress) were more closely linked with pain in at least two anatomical locations [43].

Researchers using brain imaging techniques and functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) scans have postulated the phenomenon of peripheral and central sensitization, identifying characteristic features in people with both chronic low back pain and shoulder pain [44]. It is hypothesized that once central sensitization develops, it is less responsive to peripheral treatments, consistent with findings from some trials and systematic reviews. It is therefore unsurprising that the factors associated with poor prognosis for shoulder pain (pain duration and intensity; previous episode of shoulder pain; catastrophizing; fear-avoidance beliefs; severity of disability; comorbid mental health; current sickness absence; comorbid pain at other sites) resemble those for low back pain (pain intensity; psychological distress; comorbid pain at other sites) [10].

Discussion

This narrative review compared low back pain, a leading cause of work disability in working-aged adults, with musculoskeletal pain from two other regional sites: the shoulder and the elbow/forearm. For low back pain, a substantial evidence-base and clinical guidelines support de-medicalization, avoidance of imaging, prioritization of non-pharmacological approaches, promotion of self-management, and changes in healthcare pathways and public health messaging to reshape the public’s views about pain and disability. This has been accompanied by calls to better integrate occupational health with healthcare and broader reconsideration of workers’ compensation arrangements [11]. For shoulder pain and elbow/forearm pain, the available evidence indicates similar risk factors and diagnostic approaches that fail to separate disease/conditions requiring different treatments or with different prognosis. Moreover, much of the care provided makes minimal difference to outcomes, particularly in the long-term. Achieving sustained reductions in work disability may therefore require longer-term workplace adaptations and different healthcare approaches.

Thus, there is a pressing need to apply lessons learned from low back pain to musculoskeletal pain at other regional sites. Work disability has a major individual and societal impact, reducing national productivity, increasing healthcare costs, and placing strain on welfare systems. However, as recently highlighted by Reneman and colleagues, the emphasis on physical occupational risk factors (e.g. heavy lifting and low back pain) may reinforce the belief that it is possible to damage the back through lifting too much weight at work [45] and that back pain is a sign of tissue damage and injury. This perpetuates a biomedical model of pain—injury—blame—compensation, potentially contributing to increasing rates of disability [45]. While abandoning manual handling regulations in workplaces is unrealistic, a new approach is needed. As called for in the Lancet back pain series (2018), integrating occupational health with health care is an important step [11]. Rehabilitation for musculoskeletal pain should prioritize maintaining work participation as far as possible, focusing on problem-solving and function, not on the pain itself, with appropriate modifications to duties where needed.

It is important to reinforce the message that working is better for health than sickness absence and does not require an individual being 100% pain-free. For the future, greater emphasis may be needed on normalizing pain and managing it proactively to reduce disability, alongside more balanced messaging about the benefits of work, physical activity, and overall health. Such a paradigm shift will require reconsidering routine reporting of pain symptoms at work and placing less emphasis on physical occupational exposures, with greater focus on holistic messages about the value of decent work for health and the importance of prevention through exercise both within and outside the workplace.
