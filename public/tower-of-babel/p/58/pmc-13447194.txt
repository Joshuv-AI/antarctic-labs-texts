Ischemic Evaluation and Long-Term Mortality in New-Onset Heart Failure Across Various Age-Groups
Authors: Alan Manivannan, Morten Schou, William E Boden, Emil Fosbøl, Lars Køber, Gunnar H Gislason, Kevin Monahan, Christian Torp-Pedersen, Charlotte Andersson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesThe prognostic significance of ischemic evaluation in patients with new-onset heart failure is not well understood and may differ according to age and sex. In a real-world cohort, we analyzed the long-term mortality risk after undergoing an ischemic evaluation.MethodsAll new-diagnosed heart failure patients 2008–2018 without prior ischemic evaluation, known coronary artery disease, or acute myocardial infarction were identified from the Danish National patient registry. The association of an ischemic evaluation within 90 days of heart failure diagnosis with long-term mortality was analyzed by inverse probability weighted Cox regression models.ResultsA total of 61,475 patients were included (mean age 73.3±13.9 years, 46% women), of which 12,503 (20%) underwent an ischemic evaluation. During a mean follow-up of 4.6 years, 37% of patients died, corresponding to a mortality rate of 8.0 (7.9–8.1) per 100 person-years. The multivariable-adjusted hazard ratio of death was 0.92 (95% confidence interval, 0.90–0.95) for patients who underwent ischemic evaluation, compared with patients who did not. An ischemic evaluation was associated with a lower hazard ratio in males than females: 0.87 (0.84–0.90) vs. 0.98 (0.94–1.02), and in middle-aged vs. older or younger individuals: 0.91 (0.75–1.12) in patients ≤50 years, 0.82 (0.72–0.93) in 51–60 years, 0.83 (0.79–0.87) in 61–75 years, 0.93 (0.88–0.97) in >75–85 years, and 1.02 (0.97–1.07) in >85 years, respectively.ConclusionsObtaining an ischemic evaluation was associated with marginal to no improvement in long-term mortality for many new-diagnosed heart failure patients, including the very elderly.Keywords: Heart failure, Ischemic evaluation, Mortality risk

INTRODUCTION

The prevalence of heart failure is estimated between 1–12% and rising with advanced age, with ischemic heart disease representing a common underlying cause.1) The prognosis of heart failure is poor with 1- and 5-year mortality approximating 20% and 50%, respectively, and is comparable in ischemic and non-ischemic heart failure. Mortality increases rapidly with age and may be as high as 50% during the first year after onset in patients 85 years and older.2) Although the implementation of high-quality randomized controlled trials has led to improved survival in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (secondary to guideline directed therapy), there are still major knowledge gaps in the contemporary management of heart failure. In particular, it is not known whether routine ischemic evaluations among new-diagnosed heart failure (i.e., without unstable features such as angina, ventricular arrhythmia, or myocardial infarction) translate into improved outcomes across age-groups.

While ischemic workup is routinely undertaken in many places at the time of initial heart failure diagnosis, the evidence supporting this practice is sparse and more data are needed to understand the outcomes of this practice, especially in older individuals. Notably, the role of revascularization (the main motivation for doing an ischemic evaluation) over medical therapy is uncertain in this subgroup. In this context, the STICH trial demonstrated no difference in mortality for the first 5 years between coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery and medical therapy in patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy (median age 60 years, 12% women),3) although a late mortality benefit was present when the study follow-up was expanded to 10 years.4) Studies investigating medical management vs. percutaneous interventions (PCIs) in patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy have also yielded modest differences in outcomes. The most recent REVIVED trial showed that revascularization by PCI did not improve mortality or hospitalizations for heart failure, compared with medical therapy alone.5)

In addition to guiding decisions for revascularization, there could be additional advantages to defining the underlying coronary anatomy and ischemic burden, including optimizing antiplatelet therapy, cholesterol-lowering regimen, as well as risk-stratifying patients with regard to sudden cardiac death. In this paper, we analyzed the outcomes of undergoing (vs. not undergoing) an ischemic evaluation within 3 months after heart failure onset with subsequent long-term mortality in a nationwide observational study using the Danish National patient registry in different age-groups.

METHODS

The present study was conducted in adherence with the STROBE guidelines. Full methods for obtaining the present cohort have been described elsewhere.6) In brief, we utilized the Danish nationwide administrative databases to identify all patients who were diagnosed with heart failure (International Classification of Diseases [ICD] 8th version codes 427.09–427.11, 427.19, 428.49, 428.99 or ICD 10th version codes I50, I42, and I43) for the first time between Jan 1, 2008 and Dec 31, 2018. We excluded patients who died within 90 days or were censored due to shorter follow-up (emigration) to allow all individuals equal time and chance to undergo an ischemic evaluation. Patients presenting with myocardial infarction (ICD-10 I21) at the time of heart failure, had known ischemic heart disease (prior ICD-10 code I21–I25, or ICD-8 codes 411–413), or had prior revascularizations (CABG surgery or PCI, Nordic Medico-statistical Committee’s Classification of Surgical Procedures codes KNFA-KNFE, KNFG) were excluded.

A routine ischemic evaluation was defined as having a coronary angiogram (Nordic Medico-statistical Committee’s Classification of Surgical Procedures code UXAC85), coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA, UXCC00A), nuclear myocardial scintigraphy (WHBPDN, WHBPDO, WHBPDR, WHBPSF, WHBSS except for WHBSSJHXX), or exercise or dobutamine stress echocardiography (UXUC80A or UXUC80B) undertaken in the absence of the above defined exclusion criteria. Use of medications, including angiotensin-converting-enzyme (ACE) inhibitor/angiotensin II antagonist (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system code C09), beta blocker (C07), spironolactone (C03DA01), aspirin (B01AC06), and statin (C10AA), were defined based on a claimed prescription within 90 days prior to, to 90 days after heart failure diagnosis. The number of ischemic evaluations undertaken beyond 90 days of the heart failure diagnosis has previously been reported to be low.6)

Danish registry-based studies are exempted from Institutional Review Board review given their use of deidentified data.

Statistics

Patients were followed for all-cause mortality and were censored upon emigration or at end of follow-up (December 31, 2019). The probability of undergoing an ischemic evaluation within 90 days was calculated by logistic regression models, taking all variables from Table 1, except for statins and aspirin (which may be conditional on an ischemic evaluation), and calendar year, yielding a c statistic of 0.79. For the long-term mortality endpoint (primary endpoint), inverse probability weighted Cox proportional hazards regression models were then used to estimate the absolute risks (for the cumulative survival curves) and hazard ratios of mortality associated with ischemic evaluation (SAS proc phreg) in the overall sample and stratified by sex and age-groups (≤50, 51–60, 61–75, >75–85, and >85 years, respectively). All models were adjusted for the variables in Table 1 except for statins and aspirin use, which may be conditional on the results of an ischemic evaluation (and thereby along the causal pathway). The proportional hazards assumption was tested by visual inspection of log(-log[survival probability]) by log (survival time) plots. As a sensitivity analysis, we used a doubly robust estimation by augmented inverse probability weighting to calculate the average effect associated with an ischemic evaluation on the 1- and 5-year risk of mortality (SAS proc causaltrt). The same variables were included in the model, as for the inverse probability-weighted Cox models. As additional sensitivity analyses, we propensity-score matched patients who underwent ischemic workup with patients who did not (1:1) and conducted Cox regression models stratified by matched sets and adjusted for calendar year. Two-sided p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were undertaken in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics for the 61,475 patients with new-onset heart failure are presented in Table 1. Overall mean age was 73.3±13.9 years and 46% were women. Totally, 12,503 (20%) underwent an ischemic evaluation, of which 33% were women. Most ischemic evaluations were undertaken as anatomical testing by invasive coronary angiography (84%), or coronary computed tomography (15%).

Patients who underwent an ischemic evaluation were younger (mean age 65 vs. 75 years) and had significantly fewer comorbidities, e.g. lower prevalence of chronic kidney disease (3% vs. 6%, p<0.0001), cancer (13% vs. 20%, p<0.0001), prior stroke (8% vs. 15%, p<0.0001), and atrial fibrillation (29% vs. 42%). The proportion of females was higher among the group who did not undergo an ischemic evaluation (49%) vs. among those who did (33%, p<0.0001).

Mortality risk

During a mean follow-up of 4.6 years (p25–p75 1.9–6.9 years), 22,520 (37%) patients died, corresponding to an unadjusted mortality rate of 8.0 (7.9–8.1) per 100 person-years.

The adjusted hazard ratio for long-term mortality was 0.92 (95% confidence interval 0.90–0.95) in the overall sample for patients undergoing an ischemic evaluation, compared to patients who did not.

Sex differences

Absolute survival curves in females vs. males are shown in Figure 1. As seen, for females, survival was grossly similar between patients who did vs. did not undergo an ischemic evaluation in fully adjusted models. For males, those who underwent an ischemic evaluation had an associated greater absolute survival. The prognostic importance of an ischemic evaluation differed statistically significantly in females vs. males: adjusted hazard ratio 0.98 (0.94–1.02) in females vs. 0.87 (0.84–0.90) in males, p for interaction <0.0001.

Sensitivity analyses

The estimated 1- and 5-year average effects were 0.99 (95% confidence interval 0.97–1.00, p=0.09) and 1.00 (0.98–1.02, p=0.76) in the overall sample. Stratified by sex, ischemic evaluation was associated with 1-year average effects of 0.97 (0.95–0.98, p<0.0001) in males and 1.01 (0.98–1.04, p=0.64) in females. Corresponding 5-year estimated average effects were 0.98 (0.96–0.998, p=0.03) and 1.02 (0.98–1.05, p=0.40) in males and females, respectively.

A propensity-matched sample revealed well-matched groups (Supplementary Table 1) and overall similar results, though the hazard ratios tended to be of greater magnitude (e.g., 0.80 [0.75–0.86] in the overall sample), including among females (0.84 [0.75–0.94]) and young adults (e.g., 0.64 [0.48–0.88] for those ≤50 years; Supplementary Table 2 for full results). Crude incidence rates per 100 person-years for the various groups are also given in Supplementary Table 2.

In a subgroup of patients with presumed heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF; use of an ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker plus a beta blocker; n=30,371),7) the hazard ratio for long-term mortality associated with ischemic evaluation was 0.82 (0.78–0.85).

DISCUSSION

Our study showed that among patients with new-onset heart failure without known coronary artery disease, an ischemic evaluation within three months of heart failure onset was associated with marginal to no improvement in long-term mortality risk for many patients, including females and the elderly. For middle-aged individuals and males specifically, some mortality risk reduction associated with ischemic workup was observed, although this was not consistent across sensitivity analyses. Our sensitivity analysis of the propensity score-matched sample suggested lowered hazard ratios among those who underwent ischemic workup vs. those who did not up to age 75 years, among both females and males. These data could suggest that in selected patients, with presumed higher a priori risk of ischemic heart disease and less comorbidity, there might be a benefit of ischemic workup within the first 3 months of heart failure onset. It is important to understand the difference between the statistical analyses leading to different results. The main difference between an inverse probability weighted analysis (our main models) and a propensity-score matched analysis (our sensitivity models) relates to the weighting of the cohort and selection of which patients are included in the analyses. In the propensity-score matched analyses, patients undergoing ischemic evaluation were matched 1:1 with patients who did not undergo testing, resulting in a selected subset in which treated and untreated patients were equally represented, regardless of their prevalence in the original cohort. In our study, only a minority (20%) of all patients underwent ischemic testing, and these were generally younger and had less comorbidity than patients who did not undergo an ischemic workup. Within this pre-selected population, it is possible that an ischemic evaluation would translate into greater long-term survival. In contrast, our inverse-probability weighted models retained the entire cohort and assigned each patient a weight based on the inverse of their probability of receiving an ischemic evaluation. As a result, patients who received an ischemic evaluation contrary to expectation (e.g., frail patients who underwent testing) were upweighted, whereas more typical patients were downweighted. This approach generated a pseudo-population in which measured baseline characteristics were balanced across treatment groups, approximating the conditions of a randomized comparison of the full sample. Consequently, the inverse-probability weighting models reflected the average effect of ischemic evaluation in the population as a whole, whereas the matched analyses reflected the effect of ischemic evaluation within a selected subgroup.

While ischemic workup is often routinely performed at the time of the heart failure diagnosis, the evidence supporting such practice is overall sparse. Notably, statin therapy has not been shown to improve clinical outcomes (including mortality) in patients with heart failure.8,9) Prior randomized trials of aspirin have likewise demonstrated no benefit, and possibly harm for patients with heart failure.10,11,12) From our prior work (within the same cohort), we know that a minority of all individuals who had an ischemic evaluation ended up being revascularized (<20%) and the majority of revascularizations were performed by PCI (11%) rather than by CABG surgery (5%)—a practice that remains of unproven benefit.6) Age-stratified analyses showed that the proportion who underwent revascularization increased with age from <5% under age 50 to approximately 20% among patients aged >85 years, with PCI being more common than CABG across all age-strata (and disproportionately more often used among older patients).6) Residual confounding is a possibility among the middle-aged and male subgroups, although, notably, the prevalence of coronary disease is relatively higher in this segment than, for instance, in women and the youngest patients.

Differences in long-term outcomes between females and males with new-onset heart failure who underwent an early ischemic workup may be attributable to several factors including the pathophysiology of heart failure, access to care, age of diagnosis, prevalence of underlying coronary artery disease, and management of disease. In general, females tend to have a higher incidence of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction compared with males.13) There are several differences in the coronary macro- and microvasculature between females and males, with females often having smaller and stiffer vessels. Females also tend to have a higher burden of non-obstructive coronary artery disease or microvascular dysfunction, which may not be as effectively captured by a coronary angiogram or treatable by revascularization.14) This difference may contribute to a lower likelihood of benefit from undergoing ischemic workup. In addition, although disparities between females and males may have improved, females continue to demonstrate a higher rate of major adverse cardiac events and mortality after both PCI and CABG.15,16)

Several factors may contribute towards less associated mortality benefit for older patients with newly diagnosed heart failure undergoing ischemic workup. Elderly patients with heart failure demonstrate a significantly worse prognosis overall.2,17) Elderly patients with heart failure also demonstrate a high degree of comorbidity, including renal dysfunction.18) Elderly patients undergoing PCI for acute coronary syndrome and stable angina have also demonstrated higher degrees of comorbidities and more post-procedural complications.19) Elderly patient populations also have a higher prevalence of frailty and age-associated impairments which can adversely impact the ability to tolerate invasive procedures such as a coronary angiogram. For example, Ratcovich et al.20) showed that frail compared with robust patients aged 75 years or older undergoing PCI for non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction were almost twice as likely to experience the primary endpoint defined as all-cause mortality, unplanned revascularization, myocardial infarction, stroke, and bleeding. Certainly, the observed difference in outcomes could also be influenced by selection bias. Patients aged 85 and above who undergo a coronary angiogram may represent a select group with better overall health and functional status compared to those in the 75–85 age-group. This healthier subgroup of older patients may have a more favorable prognosis irrespective of the procedure, thus masking any potential differences in outcomes.

The REVIVED randomized controlled trial demonstrated no significant difference in the incidence of death from any cause or hospitalization for heart failure for patients with severe left ventricular dysfunction who underwent PCI on top of optimal medical therapy.5) Our study did demonstrate a significant associated decrease in mortality for middle-aged men undergoing ischemic evaluation (which may or may not be related to the revascularization). There are important differences in study design in addition to patient selection. Of note, the REVIVED trial randomized patients with known severe left ventricular dysfunction of an undetermined chronicity, whereas our observational study was specific to a cohort of patients with a recent diagnosis of heart failure (less than 90 days). In addition, patients included in the REVIVED trial had known extensive coronary artery disease. Although our study did demonstrate lower mortality associated with ischemic workup, the estimated mortality benefit varied according to patient characteristics. A similar study conducted by McGuinn et al.21) showed significantly larger all-cause mortality risk reduction associated with ischemic workup in patients admitted to the Veterans Affairs with newly diagnosed heart failure (mean age in weighted sample 68 years, >98% men, 40% underwent workup, of whom 10% were revascularized); adjusted hazard ratio for mortality 0.54, 95% confidence interval 0.47–0.61. One hypothesis is that the a priori risk of multivessel disease was greater in the Veterans Affairs samples vs. our sample overall.

In addition to those already highlighted, our study has important limitations. Firstly, as a primary Danish population with universial healthcare, we are unable to generalize our results to other regions. Second, despite adjusting for several confounding variables, this observational study is susceptible to residual confounding. As nearly 85% of all initial ischemic evaluations were performed by coronary angiograms in the present cohort,6) we assumed that individuals undergoing an ischemic evaluation were more often non-frail with better kidney function than those who did not undergo ischemic evaluations. Although we used causal inference methods to overcome such differences, residual confounding is possible. In addition, our study design limits assertions regarding cause and effect. We cannot exclude that some patients had angina, which could have affected the propensity of undergoing ischemic workup. Although angina does not appear to be associated with increased mortality risk or alter the mortality benefit of revascularizations in ischemic HFrEF, it is important to acknowledge that not all patients may have been truly asymptomatic in our sample.22) Lastly, our study does not distinguish between patients with preserved vs. impaired left ventricular function, which could impact decisions to undergo ischemic workup and the eventual outcome.

In conclusion, our study investigated the association of ischemic evaluation with long-term mortality in patients with newly diagnosed heart failure using data from the Danish National patient registry. The study included more than 60,000 patients with new-onset heart failure, of whom 20% underwent an ischemic evaluation, within the first 90 days. The results showed that patients who underwent an ischemic evaluation had a marginally lower mortality of uncertain clinical relevance compared to those who did not. Males had a lower hazard ratio associated with ischemic evaluation compared to females, while perhaps not unexpectedly older patients had higher mortality associated with ischemic evaluations than middle-aged individuals. The findings of this study call into question the use of routine ischemic evaluations but could support a more selective diagnostic approach in certain subsets of heart failure patient populations, such as middle-aged men; a premise that needs to be further evaluated, ideally in a randomized trial setting.
