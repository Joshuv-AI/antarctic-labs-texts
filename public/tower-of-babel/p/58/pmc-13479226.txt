Liver disease diagnostics and access barriers in African settings: a narrative review
Authors: Habiblah Ayomide Jagunmolu, Emmanuel Oyedeji Oyetola, Yusuff Abiola Lawal, Stella Odunayo Akinsanya, Mukhtar Abimbola Ibrahim, Toluwanimi Oladele Idowu, Samuel Oyeponle Oyelude, Muminat Adeyemi Jubreel, Dhikrat Olamide Ajibade, Taofeek Oyetola Olaniyi, AbdulFatai Arikewuyo Sheriff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn Africa, chronic liver conditions are discordantly higher due to infectious and parasitic aetiologies, especially viral hepatitis. Early diagnosis is a linchpin for successful management and improved outcomes; however, diagnostic power is extremely limited. Yet, profound gaps compromise every step of the diagnostic continuum. Histopathological evaluation through liver biopsy remains the gold standard for establishing aetiology, grading inflammation and staging fibrosis. Biopsy services in Africa are limited by limited infrastructure, a deficit of qualified professionals, substantial costs, unreliable tissue samples and limited access to advanced pathology techniques such as immunohistochemistry and molecular testing. Non-invasive diagnostic tools such as serum markers, transient elastography and imaging techniques are unequally accessible, poorly updated for African-specific aetiologies and largely inaccessible outside tertiary centres. This review explores the aetiological spectrum of chronic liver disease in Africa and evaluates the availability and performance of both histological and non-invasive diagnostic techniques. This review illustrates gaps affecting screening, staging, initiation of management and he⁠patoc⁠e‍llular carcino‍ma surveillance. Addressing these diagnostic limitations through improved infrastructure, expanded professional training and local validation of non-invasive tools is essential to reducing the burden of liver disease across Africa. In addition, it offers tiered and feasible recommendations while considering the current reality of healthcare in sub-Saharan Africa.

Methodology

A narrative review approach was used for this study, as it will enable the synthesis of various sources to obtain a holistic picture of diagnostic gaps in hepatopathology in the African context. It is particularly beneficial in this study, where the available research is fragmented and culturally diverse, as it enables the identification of critical gaps and the formulation of recommendations for policy and practice. Our study used a narrative review method to explore the diagnostic gaps in hepatopathology in Africa, with focus on aetiology and availability of histological and non-invasive tools. A detailed search was conducted of the following databases: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar and AJOL. By extension, grey literature relevant to this topic was also reviewed. Search terms included keywords like “hepatopathology”, “liver disease”, “hepatocellular carcinoma”, “cirrhosis”, “fibrosis”, “diagnostic gap”, “diagnostic challenge”, “underdiagnosis”, “Africa”. Boolean operators used to refine the search include AND & OR. Studies published in the English language that address any part of the topic were added. Studies focusing on non-African settings without contextual relevance or in other languages other than English were excluded. Title, abstract and full text of the included studies were reviewed and information was synthesised using the narrative approach. Due to the narrative nature of this review, no formal quality assessment or meta-analysis was performed. Findings were synthesised to meet the aim and objective of this manuscript.

Aetiological landscape of chronic liver disease in Africa

CLD in SSA constitutes a serious health burden, with viral infections contributing significantly and being greatly influenced by metabolic and environmental risk factors.17 The variations between Africa and other continents appear to be due to the distinct prevalence of the underlying risk factors for liver disease between the different regions. The age-standardised incidence rate (ASIR) of HCC is reported as 6.3/100 000 persons/year, 8.6/100 000 persons/year, 10.3/100 000 persons/year in North Africa, Southern SSA and Eastern SSA, respectively. Central and Western SSA show the highest incidence (16.9/100 000 persons/year) of HCC.18 About 2 billion people have been infected with HBV globally, of whom 248 million are chronic carriers, and are at increased risk of liver cirrhosis and HCC.9 The overall estimated prevalence of chronic hepatitis B (CHB) infection in the African continent is 6.1%, with SSA carrying the largest number of 78 million infections.19 20 In SSA, Central Africa has the highest CHB prevalence of 9.7% among adults followed by West Africa with an 8.3% prevalence. Eastern SSA, Southern SSA and North Africa have a 5.5%, 3.8% and 2.8% prevalence of CHB, respectively.21 HCC and cirrhosis remain part of the leading aetiology in the sub-Saharan region, and knowledge of the aetiological landscape is essential as it will help in diagnosis, monitoring and treatment of the disease.17

Chronic HBV infection remains the single most important aetiology of HCC and CLD in SSA, with HBV contributing to over 50% of HCC cases in various African regions, indicating both widespread prevalence and restricted availability of antiviral treatments or early detection initiatives.22 Africa is second only to the Western Pacific region in terms of the prevalence of chronic HBV infection (6.1% vs 6.2%); HBV is hyperendemic (> 8% prevalence of chronic carriers) in some sub-Saharan countries like Nigeria, Gabon, Cameroon and Burkina Faso; endemic to an intermediate degree (2–7.99% prevalence) in Kenya, Zambia, Ivory Coast, Liberia, Sierra Leone and Senegal; and comparatively endemic to low degree (<2% prevalence) in North African countries, such as Egypt, Tunisia, Algeria and Morocco.21 Remarkably, HCC in SSA typically occurs in younger individuals, advances swiftly and is commonly identified at advanced stages due to insufficient imaging and diagnostic pathology facilities.17 The incidence of HBV-related and HCV-related HCC varies between North Africa and SSA due to variations in the prevalence of infections of the two viruses. HBV contributes 18% and 70%, while HCV is responsible for 60% and 20% of HCC in North Africa and SSA, respectively.23 Hepatitis D virus (HDV), although not as common as HBV, also contributes greatly to the disease burden, and effects differ by region, showing greater prevalence in Central and Western Africa.22 In contrast to HBV-related illness, HCV-associated HCC generally appears in somewhat older demographics, yet death rates remain elevated due to restricted treatment alternatives in numerous environments.17 This is not surprising, as coinfections like HBV/HIV, HBV/HDV and HBV/HCV increase risk, speeding up fibrosis progression and raising HCC incidence, culminating in eventual liver failure.17 HDV requires HBV for replication; therefore, HDV burden is highest where HBV is common.16 In SSA, where the HIV burden is significant, these interactions worsen liver disease outcomes and complicate treatment, particularly in regions with restricted diagnostic capabilities.17 In addition, dietary aflatoxin exposure as an environmental cofactor also heightens HCC risk for individuals infected with HBV.24 25 Aflatoxin-B1, prevalent in improperly stored grains and nuts, acts synergistically with HBV to increase HCC risk.25 26

There is a paradigm shift away from only viral causes towards metabolic, lifestyle-related and multifactorial aetiologies in the world, and this pattern is also noticed in SSA.22 Aflatoxin exposure, endemic in many SSA agricultural settings, remains one of the world’s most potent natural hepatocarcinogens and continues to fuel HCC development, particularly in combination with HBV.17 Additionally, iron overload syndromes have been described in some parts of Africa, contributing to hepatic fibrosis and cancer risk.17

Hepatocellular carcinoma

HCC represents the combined impact of the causal factors influencing CLD in SSA. More than 80% of global patients with HCC are estimated to occur in SSA and Eastern Asia.21 The most common risk factor of HCC in SSA is chronic HBV infection, with the incidence highest in West Africa.21 Incidence of HCC in SSA is highest in West Africa, with Gambia and Guinea being the major contributors.21 Although HBV continues to be the primary factor, the epidemiology of HCC is changing. Worldwide evaluations indicate increasing percentages of HCC due to metabolic liver disease and alcohol consumption, patterns that are also being seen more frequently in SSA.27 28 However, challenges in diagnosis, monitoring and treatment persist due to late presentation, inadequate surveillance and limited access to curative therapies, subsequently leading to high morbidity and mortality secondary to diagnosis at advanced stages.28–30

Schistosomiasis and its contribution to periportal fibrosis

Schistosomiasis is a major cause of non-cirrhotic portal hypertension and periportal fibrosis, as chronic S. mansoni infection leads to deposition of eggs in the portal venous system.31 The resulting immune reaction causes granuloma formation and periportal fibrosis (Symmers’ fibrosis). Even in the absence of cirrhosis, patients develop portal hypertension, splenomegaly and varices.31

Alcohol-related liver disease

Unrecorded consumption of home-brewed and artisanal alcoholic beverages is widespread in Africa compared with the Western world, and this poses a substantial, under-recognised public health risk.32

Metabolically dysfunctional associated steatotic liver disease/ non-alcoholic fatty liver disease

In recent times, due to increased obesity prevalence, sedentary lifestyle and type 2 diabetes mellitus, metabolically dysfunctional associated steatotic liver disease/non-alcoholic fatty liver disNon Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease (MASLD/NAFLD) has become more common and widespread in SSA.27 Recent studies emphasise NAFLD as a growing factor in CLD, with an estimated prevalence of around 13%, although this percentage is probably understated, as a result of insufficient screening and dependence on hospital-centred research.27 Significantly, certain SSA groups seem to manifest metabolic liver disease at reduced body mass index (BMI) levels compared with Western populations, indicating possible genetic or epigenetic factors.27 Unfortunately, diagnostic resources in these regions are scarce, thus limiting the understanding, treatment and monitoring of disease. The disease is intimately linked to obesity, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome.33 34 NAFLD can progress to cirrhosis or HCC; however, public knowledge and diagnostic capacity for NAFLD remain low in SSA, contributing to under-reporting.34 Studies also highlight cardiometabolic consequences of NAFLD, emphasising the dual burden of liver and cardiovascular disease in affected populations.33 34

Aflatoxin exposure and hepatocellular carcinoma risk

High aflatoxin concentrations were found in foods such as nuts in Nigeria, rice grains in Ghana, peanuts in Zambia, locally brewed alcoholic drinks in Kenya and Ethiopia, and cow milk in Cameroon.21 Several studies in West Africa demonstrate that aflatoxin exposure is strongly associated with both cirrhosis and HCC, and may synergistically increase HCC risk in HBV carriers.24 25 Other important causes of liver disease, although less common than metabolic and infectious causes in SSA, include genetic conditions such as hereditary haemochromatosis, Wilson’s disease and alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency.35 These conditions often go underdiagnosed in SSA due to limited access to genetic testing and specialised diagnostic tools.35 However, they are clinically significant as they can mimic more common forms of CLD and require targeted therapy to prevent progression.

Histological assessment tools: the standard and the constraints

Liver biopsy remains the reference standard for the diagnosis of CLD, enabling aetiological identification, grading of necroinflammation, as well as fibrosis staging.36 The severity of diseases, such as viral hepatitis, schistosomiasis and other chronic liver pathologies, is evaluated by established scoring systems such as METAVIR, Ishak and Batts-Ludwig.37 The histological findings help guide therapeutic decisions, including eligibility for antiviral therapy in HBV infection, antiviral prioritisation in hepatitis C infection management and differentiation of overlapping pathologies.36 38 International guidelines recommend liver biopsy in selected clinical scenarios, including discordant non-invasive findings, suspected co-infections such as hepatitis D and cases with mixed or unclear aetiology.37 39 Across several SSA settings particularly in resource-constrained public and district-level facilities, liver biopsy remains difficult to implement routinely because of infrastructure, workforce, logistics and cost limitations.40

Global analyses, including the Lancet Commission on pathology in low-income and middle-income countries, highlight a substantial shortage of pathologists across SSA compared with high-income settings, reflecting a critical workforce gap in diagnostic service.41 Available data indicate that the density of pathologists in many African settings is approximately one per 1 000 000 population, compared with around one per 15 000–20 000 in high-income countries, representing a 50–70-fold disparity in workforce capacity. Although distribution varies considerably across regions, with pathologists concentrated disproportionately in selected hubs such as South Africa, Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya and Ethiopia.41 42 This shortage is further compounded by uneven geographic distribution, with most pathologists concentrated in a few urban and regional hubs.42 Cross-sectional assessments from Nigeria highlight gaps in infrastructure, workforce distribution and access to diagnostic services that may reflect challenges seen in parts of West Africa, though these patterns are not uniform across the continent.43

These workforce gaps compound broader laboratory system limitations across national tiered networks.40 Availability of fully functional histopathology laboratories adequate to process liver tissues is limited, and they are most often found in capital cities and some university centres.38 At a systems level, the absence of well-integrated tiered laboratory networks has been identified as a key limitation affecting diagnostic capacity across many, though not all, African health systems, particularly where tiered laboratory networks remain underdeveloped.44 Patterns also differ across tertiary referral centres, district facilities and region health systems, with much of the published evidence derived from selected West and East African settings.41 42 Ultrasound (US)-guided liver biopsy is associated with improved safety profiles, with meta-analytic evidence reporting major complication rates below 1% when performed under appropriate conditions.45 However, its safe implementation depends on the availability of functional imaging equipment and imaging equipment and trained personnel. Dependable coagulation testing may be limited outside tertiary institutions in some lower-resource settings. In some lower resource settings, blind or minimally guided approaches may still be used where imaging and procedural support are limited.46

Available data from selected African settings suggest that the cost of liver biopsy in private facilities may reach several hundred dollars, representing a substantial financial burden for many patients.47 48 Studies from centres report substantial rates of patient refusal often attributed to concerns about cost and procedural risk.49 50 The quality of the specimen is vital to diagnostic accuracy, and even after performing a biopsy, specimen quality often compromises the reliability of the diagnosis. Studies from selected African cohorts suggest that a substantial portion of biopsy samples fall below adequacy criteria, including shorter core lengths and insufficient portal tracts.51 A constellation of challenges nags at the standard of assessment. Another from this series is reused, poorly sharpened Menghini needles that lead to fragmentation, and delays in transport may contribute to autolysis and reduced diagnostic quality.52

Limited availability of specialised histochemical stains, including reticulin has been reported in some African settings, which may constrain the histological differentiation of certain liver pathologies. In addition, the interpretation of fibrosis in the context of overlapping conditions such as viral hepatitis and schistosomiasis can present diagnostic challenges, particularly in resource-limited environments.52 Summarily, evidence from multiple African settings indicates that although liver biopsy remains the diagnostic reference standard, its practical utility is constrained by limitations in access, infrastructure, workforce capacity and diagnostic supportive systems. Importantly, these constraints vary substantially across regions, countries and levels of care, underscoring the need for context-specific diagnostic approaches rather than continent-wide assumptions.41

Non-invasive assessment tools: prospects and implementation gaps

Non-invasive assessment tools are increasingly being recognised as the more scalable solution for the diagnosis of liver disease, especially in resource-limited settings like Africa with one pathologist per 1 million people.42 Among these, serum biomarkers and scoring systems such as APRI and the FIB-4 score stand out due to their cost-effectiveness and use of readily available laboratory tests.53 These scores offer a practical alternative to invasive liver biopsy and are recommended by the WHO for the assessment of fibrosis in chronic viral hepatitis, which is prevalent in Africa. APRI and FIB-4 have been validated in some African countries, including Nigeria and Ethiopia, and have demonstrated acceptable sensitivity and specificity for detecting advanced liver fibrosis and cirrhosis. The accuracy of APRI and FIB-4 can be influenced by the mixed aetiologies of liver disease commonly seen in African populations, such as coexisting infections and alcohol-related damage. These also highlight the need for region-specific cut-off adjustment to enhance diagnostic precision.54 55 While APRI, FIB-4 and elastography have some scalability advantages, these tools were predominantly developed and validated in high-income cohorts with viral hepatitis or NAFLD dominance.56–58 African populations show complex multiple aetiology disease patterns, including HBV/schistosomiasis coinfection, aflatoxin injury, unrecorded alcohol and emerging metabolic disease that alter biological correlates of fibrosis. Local SSA validation studies demonstrated variable performance which requires aetiology and population specific cut-offs: Ethiopia HBV (FIB-4 AUC 0.75 vs Western 0.84), Cameroon HIV/HCV (APRI cut-off >1.5 vs standard>1.0) and West Africa GPR superior to APRI (AUC 0.91).56–59 Thus, the most important question is not about tool availability, but rigorous local validation capturing Africa’s unique hepatopathology.

Transient elastography, for example, FibroScan and ARFI elastography, represents advanced, non-invasive method that measures liver stiffness for fibrosis staging with an accuracy that is higher than serum biomarker.55 Despite the proven utility of these tools, their availability in Africa is limited to tertiary urban centres due to cost and specialised training that it requires.60 Emerging local validations support the feasibility of their use in SSA settings: FibroScan performance has been documented in the Democratic Republic of Congo (Kinshasa cohort; cirrhosis threshold≥14 kPa; sensitivity 60%, specificity 93.3% vs APRI/FIB-4), Nigeria (UBTH Benin City and Jos studies confirming utility in MASLD and viral hepatitis) and Ethiopia (Addis Ababa HBV validation; thresholds≤7.9/>11.7 kPa),55 while ARFI reference values have been established in Cameroon (healthy median 1.10–1.15 m/s). Widespread deployment, however, remains constrained by infrastructure gaps across national laboratory networks.61–63 Efforts to expand access are ongoing, although it has been grossly hindered by infrastructural and financial constraints.56 57

Imaging modalities, including US, CT and MRI, also play an important role in detecting features of advanced liver diseases such as cirrhosis, ascites and portal hypertension.58 US is the most accessible and most used imaging technique for initial liver assessment and HCC surveillance in most healthcare settings in Africa, the sensitivity of US for early detection of HCC is limited. Advanced imaging techniques such as contrast-enhanced CT and MRI, which are crucial for accurate tumour characterisation and staging, are scarce outside urban centres, leading to significant disparities between healthcare access in urban and rural settings. Africa-specific adaptations include gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase-to-platelet ratio (GPR), superior to APRI/FIB-4 for West African HBV fibrosis (cut-off≥0.32; AUC 0.91) and point shear wave elastography (pSWE) for schistosomiasis mansoni periportal fibrosis (cut-off>1.39 m/s; specificity 86%).59 64 65 Bridging these gaps requires investment in infrastructure, training and allocation of resources.

Although these tools were primarily developed in high-income countries, African validation studies confirm reasonable performance in viral hepatitis cohorts (Ethiopia HBV AUC 0.75–0.84, Cameroon HIV/HCV APRI>1.5), there is also a need for regional adaptation of the cut-off threshold tailored to our population to improve diagnostic performance.66–68 Comparative studies done in Africa showed that APRI and FIB-4 correlate with liver stiffness measurements from transient elastography and histological findings, which support their use as preliminary screening tools to identify patients who may need further diagnostic evaluation.55 However, the limitations of APRI and FIB-4 mean that they should be complemented by other diagnostic modalities when possible. Despite this limitation, APRI and FIB-4 still remain tools for scaling liver disease diagnosis in African healthcare settings due to their affordability, ease of use and capacity to aid prioritisation of patients for treatment in resource-limited settings.58 59 Transient elastography (FibroScan) and ARFI have emerged as valuable non-invasive techniques for assessing fibrosis of the liver by quantifying liver stiffness. These modalities offer more accurate diagnostic accuracy compared with serum biomarkers and help in staging fibrosis and cirrhosis in patients with CLDs, including hepatitis B, C and NAFLD.55 FibroScan uses a low-frequency elastic shear wave to measure the stiffness of tissues, providing immediate results that correlate with liver biopsy findings. ARFI, integrated with conventional US machines, operates by generating localised tissue displacement and measuring the velocity of shear waves to assess fibrosis and cirrhosis. These methods are safe, reproducible and painless, which makes them applicable for clinical use.60

There are limited local validation studies done in Africa, but there is a good correlation between transient elastography measurements and the severity of fibrosis, which supports their applicability across diverse populations.56 57 Implementation problems also include deficits in infrastructure, a lack of maintenance capabilities and financial constraints that hinder procurement and widespread deployment. Efforts are focusing on integrating these tools into existing hospital workflow and training healthcare workers to operate them effectively.53 54 Pilot programmes have shown that with appropriate funding and coordinated strategies, elastography can improve early diagnosis and management of liver fibrosis, reducing complications like cirrhosis and hepatocellular cancer.55 Transient elastography and ARFI represent very promising non-invasive diagnostic technologies for liver fibrosis with superior accuracy and clinical relevance. Addressing their availability and local validation gaps remains essential in leveraging their full potential in resource-limited settings, particularly for scalable disease assessment and monitoring.56 Beyond advanced disease detection, imaging is also critical for HCC surveillance and diagnosis, particularly in Africa, where the major cause of HCC is viral hepatitis. Contrast-enhanced US, CT and MRI provide better characterisation of liver nodules, distinguishing benign lesions from HCC through washout patterns and arterial hyperenhancement as highlighted in the international guidelines.54 55 However, the sensitivity of US for early-stage HCC remains operator-dependent and limited by obesity or steatosis; hence, the need for advanced modalities for confirmation.60 Disparities in availability between urban and rural African settings have a significant impact on diagnostic equity. While US is widespread in urban tertiary centres, CT and MRI are still scarce outside major cities due to cost, the challenge of maintenance and power instability.57 Rural patients rely most often on US, leading to delayed diagnosis and increased mortality; studies showed up to 70% underutilisation in peripheral areas.57 59 These disparities between urban and rural areas further exacerbate late-stage presentation, underscoring the need for mobile imaging units and training programmes.57 Strategies to mitigate these gaps include leveraging point-of-care US for HCC screening in high-risk cohorts and integrating AI-enhanced imaging for improved accuracy in low-resource environments.53 Ultimately, expanding infrastructure and subsidising imaging access are important to optimise outcomes in Africa’s hepatopathology landscape.58 59

Synthesis of diagnostic gaps and clinical impact

While the burden of disease is driven by a unique African profile of infectious and parasitic cofactors, the diagnostic approach remains fragmented and largely reliant on tools ill-suited for low-resource settings.69 This failure is not simply a lack of technology but a structural disconnect between screening, initial assessment and confirmatory staging. Current evidence suggests that without accessible point-of-care tools to replace the logistical impossibility of widespread liver biopsy, the majority of patients are lost in the gap between initial viral detection and the definitive staging required for treatment initiation.8 This diagnostic bottleneck effectively renders the available therapeutic advancements moot for a significant proportion of the population. The immediate clinical consequence of this diagnostic gap is a significant barrier to treatment eligibility. Although access to DAAs for HCV and suppressive therapy for HBV has expanded across the continent, treatment guidelines often mandate evidence of significant fibrosis (≥F2) or cirrhosis before initiation. Reliance on expensive or unavailable staging tools (such as FibroScan or biopsy) creates a delay. Patients are diagnosed with viral hepatitis but are unable to proceed to treatment because they cannot undergo the requisite staging. In patients with hepatitis B/schistosomiasis coinfection, widely used biomarkers may yield discordant results due to distinct patterns of periportal fibrosis, leading to the potential undertreatment of patients who have significant liver damage but lack textbook cirrhotic markers.64 65 This inability to accurately stage fibrosis early in the disease course directly undermines HCC surveillance efforts. Effective surveillance relies on identifying the at-risk population (primarily those with cirrhosis) and entering them into regular US screening protocols.70 The data suggest that the high mortality rate of HCC in Africa is not solely a failure of cancer treatment but a failure of the antecedent diagnostic infrastructure to flag high-risk patients when curative interventions are still viable, resulting in a median survival of less than 3 months at presentation.70 71

Ultimately, these clinical disconnects are symptoms of a broader policy void. While the WHO African Region has adopted ambitious viral hepatitis elimination targets for 2030, few nations have integrated liver-specific diagnostics into their essential health packages. The critical shortage of hepatology-specific training programmes outside of a few regional hubs (eg, South Africa, Egypt), leaving the vast majority of care in the hands of general practitioners who lack access to guidelines or specialist support. This human resource crisis is compounded by a lack of harmonised pathology training, meaning that even when biopsies are performed, the histopathological interpretation may lack the standardisation required for complex cases.72 Figure 1 illustrates a proposed tiered diagnostic pathway for liver fibrosis staging in resource-limited settings, considering the African peculiarity. The algorithm prioritises cost-effective, non-invasive tests (APRI, FIB-4, GPR) at the primary care level to identify patients requiring immediate treatment (see figure 1). Specialised imaging (US) and elastography are reserved for grey-zone cases to maximise resource efficiency and reduce the diagnostic bottleneck. Emerging artificial intelligence tools represent a potentially significant contribution to the diagnostic constraints described above. AI-assisted analysis of liver histology (including automated fibrosis staging using convolutional neural networks applied to whole-slide images) has demonstrated accuracy comparable to experienced hepatopathologists in several external validation studies.73 If adapted to African biopsy workflows, such tools could partially compensate for the critical shortage of trained hepatopathologists outside regional centres and reduce the interobserver variability that currently limits histological reproducibility. AI-assisted reading of US and CT scans has shown some promise for detecting early hepatic lesions and estimating fibrosis burden where radiology expertise is thin on the ground.74 None of this is ready for immediate deployment. Meaningful use in African settings would first require local validation studies, training datasets that reflect the populations being screened, and real integration with existing health infrastructure, none of which is trivial. But as the technology matures and digital pathology becomes less of a rarity, it deserves serious consideration as part of a tiered diagnostic approach in low-resource settings.

Recommendations

Addressing the diagnostic gaps in SSA requires a shift from aspirational goals towards a practical, feasible and tiered implementation agenda while considering the current reality of healthcare in SSA. The following framework outlines prioritised recommendations categorised by health-system tier, designed to bridge the gap between initial screening and definitive staging.

Level 1: immediately actionable (primary/district level)

Standardisation of first-line algorithms

Formally integrate APRI and FIB-4 as mandatory assessments in national CLD guidelines, leveraging routine chemistry and haematology infrastructure already in place.

Formalised referral pathways

Develop specific clinical and biochemical ‘triggers‘ for equivocal results (the 'grey zone’), ensuring patients are seamlessly escalated from primary to secondary care.

Specimen ‘hygiene’ protocols

Implement standardised training for tissue fixation and transport to reduce autolysis and fragmentation, maximising the diagnostic utility of the few biopsies performed.

Surveillance training

Strengthen focused US training for healthcare workers in endemic areas to improve HCC detection in high-risk groups.

Level 2: intermediate structural goals (secondary/regional level)

Hub-and-spoke telepathology

Establish digital linkages where peripheral clinics transmit slide images to urban pathology hubs, bypassing the physical shortage of specialists in rural areas.

Strategic hardware placement

Subsidise and place elastography units (FibroScan/ARFI) in strategically selected public regional centres rather than only in private tertiary hospitals.

Specialist incentivisation

Create professional and financial ‘hardship’ packages to attract and retain diagnostic specialists (pathologists and radiologists) in underserved regions.

Level 3: long-term developmental goals (national/tertiary level)

Localised recalibration research

Fund multicentre African trials to recalibrate biomarker cut-offs specifically for indigenous aetiologies like schistosomiasis-related periportal fibrosis and HBV genotypes A and E.

Infrastructure resilience

Prioritise the procurement of ‘tropicalised‘ hardware designed with dust filtration and battery backups to withstand unstable power and high-heat environments.

Workforce expansion

Scale up specialised residency programmes to close the pathologist-to-population deficit.

Conclusion

The African disease burden is driven by a unique epidemiological profile, including chronic HBV, HCV, schistosomiasis and aflatoxin exposure. Diagnostic gaps in hepatopathology in the African population have been linked to multifactorial aetiologies, with limited density of pathologists (one per 1 000 000 population) being a major contributor. In addition to this, centres with adequate diagnostic and modern facilities are clustered in urban areas and tertiary institutions, further hampering equitable access. Most non-invasive diagnostic tools were developed in Western cohorts, and thereby require recalibration for the African cohort in order to account for the diverse hepatic disease burden. Advanced tools like FibroScan and ARFI are superior in accuracy but remain unavailable in rural settings due to high cost of procurement and maintenance, in addition to the need for specialised training. To achieve maximum benefit, non-invasive technologies (FibroScan/ARFI) should be prioritised and subsidised in district hospitals, and functional and well-equipped, accessible diagnostic centres should be established and centralised for complex diagnostics.
