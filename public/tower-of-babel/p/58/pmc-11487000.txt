Special issue overview

Despite an increased understanding of the underlying biology of the disease, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains a challenging condition with poor outcomes and a 5-year survival rate, even at an early stage, of less than 10%. This dismal prognosis is due in part to biological aggressiveness, the lack of early diagnosis, suboptimal surgical selection, and the need for effective neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapies.

However, progress is being made with significant advancements in the diagnosis, staging, and treatment of PDAC, with a focus on precision therapy, improved surgical care, and supportive care, coupled with a deeper understanding of PDAC’s biology.

This Special Issue aims to provide an overview of these advancements, summarizing recent research key findings and contributions.

Linda C. Chu and Elliot K. Fishman, from The Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine, Baltimore, Maryland, United States, in their article ‘Pancreatic Ductal Adenocarcinoma Staging: A Narrative Review of Radiologic Techniques and Advances’ reviewed the current role of radiology in diagnosing and staging PDAC (pp. 6052–6063). They noted that contemporary radiological modalities play a crucial role in diagnosing and staging PDAC, with pancreatic protocol computed tomography (CT) being the preferred imaging modality due to its wide availability, consistency in image quality, and lower cost. However, the authors noted that in selected cases and depending on local availability/expertise, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography (PET)/CT may have an important role. The authors highlighted the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, which provide a framework for determining the resectability of the tumor. However, challenges remain in accurate preoperative staging, including predicting lymph node metastases and peri-neural invasion, detecting subtle liver or peritoneal metastases, and disease restaging following neoadjuvant therapy. Drs Chu and Fishman also discussed emerging technologies in radiology, such as advanced visualization techniques and Artificial Intelligence (AI), raising the possibility that these technologies could help improve the detection of small, early-stage tumors, predict disease recurrence and assist in selecting patients most likely to benefit from surgery.

Urban Arnelo and colleagues from Umea University, Umea, Sweden, in their article ‘Endoscopic Workup in Pancreatic Cancer’ discussed the role of endoscopy in the diagnosis and management of PDAC, highlighting the importance of obtaining tissue samples for accurate diagnosis and guiding treatment decisions, especially in patients eligible for chemotherapy treatment and requiring histological confirmation (pp. 6064–6069). They highlighted emerging therapeutic techniques such as intratumoral or intraductal radiofrequency ablation, which deliver targeted energy, providing additional options for managing pancreatic tumors and slowing their progression. The authors also emphasized the important role that endoscopy has in the palliative care setting, particularly for managing metastatic pancreatic cancer, noting that it offers effective palliation of biliary and enteric strictures through minimally invasive approaches, significantly improving patients’ quality of life by relieving symptoms such as jaundice and obstruction.

Drug therapy has advanced for the treatment of PDAC, including drugs that inhibit critical molecular pathways, drugs that capitalize on homologous repair deficiencies, immunotherapeutic approaches, antimetabolic agents, and drugs that attack the extracellular matrix supporting PDAC growth. Vincent J. Picozzi from the Virginia Mason Medical Center, Seattle, Washington, United States, in the article ‘Pancreatic Cancer: New Approaches to Drug Therapy’ reported that the integration of treatments, particularly induction chemotherapy (IC) before chemoradiotherapy (CRT), has demonstrated improved survival outcomes, increased resection rates, and better local disease control (pp. 6070–6080).

The article ‘The Impact of Intensified Staging and Combined Therapies in Locally Advanced Pancreatic Cancer: A Secondary Analysis of Prospective Studies’ by Sara Ramella, Gian Marco Petrianni, and Michele Fiore from University Campus Bio-Medico di Roma, Rome, Italy, examined the impact of intensified staging and combined therapies in locally advanced pancreatic cancer (pp. 6081–6091). They suggested that the combination of staging laparoscopy and PET/CT scan can significantly improve the detection rate of hidden metastases in borderline resectable and unresectable pancreatic cancer patients. The integration of treatments, particularly induction chemotherapy (IC) before chemoradiotherapy (CRT), demonstrated improved survival outcomes, increased resection rates, and better local disease control. The authors highlighted the importance of a multidisciplinary approach to patient care and advanced imaging modalities in accurately staging pancreatic cancer and guiding treatment decisions. However, they also noted the need for further research to optimize treatment strategies for borderline and locally advanced pancreatic cancer patients.

The multicenter international study ‘Preoperative CA19.9 level predicts lymph node metastasis in resectable adenocarcinoma of the head of the pancreas: a further plea for biological resectability criteria’, discussed in the article by Tommaso Farolfi and co-workers from the University Campus Bio-Medico di Roma, Rome, Italy, investigated the relationship between preoperative levels of carbohydrate antigen 19.9 (CA 19.9) and the presence of lymph node metastasis in pancreatic cancer patients (pp. 6092–6099). The study found that CA 19.9 levels above the standard laboratory cut-off of 37 U/ml were significantly associated with a high rate of lymph node involvement. This was particularly true for patients with cT3 tumors, where CA 19.9 could be a valid tool to suspect nodal involvement. The study suggests that patients with radiologically resectable pancreatic cancer and elevated CA 19.9 values should be considered in a more biologically advanced stage. The findings could support surgeons and oncologists in identifying patients who should move from an upfront resectable stage to a biological borderline resectable stage.

Sarah Bradley Hays, Aram E. Rojas, and Melissa E. Hogg from the University of Chicago Medical Center, Chicago, Illinois, United States, in their article ‘Robotic Pancreas Surgery for Pancreatic Cancer’ reported on the use of robotic surgery in pancreatic cancer treatment, focusing on robotic distal pancreatectomy and robotic pancreatoduodenectomy (pp. 6100–6110). Robotic surgery for pancreatic cancer has seen increased adoption since the early 2000s, with studies suggesting similar or improved outcomes compared to standard open approaches. These include shorter hospital stays, faster recovery with fewer postoperative complications, and equivalent resection rates. The learning curve for robotic pancreas surgery is crucial for patient safety. Studies suggest that surgeons become proficient after performing 15–40 operations, depending on the specific procedure. Despite the benefits, the adoption of robotic surgery for pancreatic cancer has been slow due to concerns about inferior oncologic outcomes and the need for further research. Ongoing randomized control trials are required to address these concerns.

Ugo Boggi and his colleagues from the University of Pisa, Pisa, Italy, detailed their recent study on ‘Postoperative results, learning curve, and outcomes of pancreatectomy with arterial resection: a single-center retrospective cohort study on 236 procedures’ (pp. 6111–6125). This study evaluated the feasibility, learning curve, and outcomes of pancreatectomy with arterial resection (PAR) in patients with locally advanced pancreatic cancer. The authors found that PAR, including superior mesenteric artery resection (PAR-SMA), is feasible and associated with acceptable postoperative outcomes. The learning curve for PAR was steep, with mortality rates decreasing after ~100 procedures. The authors also reported that R0 resection (no cancer cells seen at the edge of the removed tissue) was achieved in 70% of PAR cases, and long-term survival was possible with appropriate patient selection. They concluded that pancreatic surgeons should be prepared to address the technical challenges of PAR.

Marc Besselink and his colleagues from Amsterdam University Medical Center, Amsterdam, The Netherlands, performed a systematic review of randomized controlled trials examining the strategies to prevent or mitigate post-pancreatectomy hemorrhage (PPH), a leading cause of surgical mortality after pancreatic surgery. The review ‘Treatment strategies to prevent or mitigate the outcome of post-pancreatectomy hemorrhage: a review of randomized trials’ found that out of 99 RCTs, five reported strategies that significantly reduced the rate of PPH (pp. 6145–6154). Three of these strategies were related to surgical technique: pancreatic anastomosis with small jejunal incision, falciform ligament wrap around the gastroduodenal artery stump, and pancreaticojejunostomy (vs. pancreaticogastrostomy). The other two strategies were related to perioperative management: perioperative pasireotide administration and algorithm-based postoperative patient management. The authors concluded that more studies should focus on treating patients with PPH, as RCTs are currently lacking.

Postoperative pancreatic fistula (POPF) remains a common and dreaded complication after pancreatic resections and is associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Alessandro Zerbi and Martina Nebbia from IRCCS Humanitas Research Hospital, Rozzano, Milan, Italy, in their article ‘Updates in the management of postoperative pancreatic fistula’, reported on contemporary management strategies for this complication, highlighting that management is often based on local expertise and driven by the patient’s clinical condition (pp. 6135–6144). A consensus on the optimal management strategy is still lacking. The article also emphasized the importance of a standardized postoperative patient monitoring protocol to detect deviations from the ordinary postoperative course early on. The authors suggested that the continuous implementation of centralized, high-volume pancreatic units with appropriate facilities and multidisciplinary experience may significantly contribute to standardizing the management and improving the outcomes of POPF.

Malnutrition is a characteristic aspect of pancreas cancer. The article ‘Preoperative low skeletal muscle mass index assessed using L3-CT as a prognostic marker of clinical outcomes in pancreatic cancer patients undergoing surgery: A systematic review and meta-analysis’ by Emanuel Rinninella from the Catholic University of the Sacred Heart: University Cattolica del Sacro Cuore, Rome, Italy, aimed to assess the association between preoperative low skeletal muscle index (SMI) measured by L3-CT and postoperative clinical outcomes in pancreatic cancer patients (pp. 6126–6134). The study found that more than half of the patients (50.9%) had low muscle mass in the preoperative phase. The study also found a significant association between low SMI and worse overall survival (OS), but no significant associations between low muscle mass and postoperative outcomes were found. The study concluded that CT-defined sarcopenia should be routinely assessed and discussed promptly by a multidisciplinary tumor board, including clinical nutrition teams, to care for more vulnerable patients, especially in the case of surgery.

Eduard Jonas from the University of Cape Town Faculty of Health Sciences Cape Town, South Africa, in the article ‘The Role of national population-based registries in pancreatic cancer surgery research’ discussed the role of National Population-Based Registries (NPBRs) in pancreatic cancer surgery research (pp. 6155–6162). He highlighted that NPBRs offer cost-effectiveness, larger sample sizes, and representation of real-world clinical practice, making them less prone to selection bias than non-NPBRs. However, the article also points out several methodological shortcomings of NPBRs, such as lack of control over data collection, lack of reporting on the quality of the source registry or database in terms of validation of coverage and data completeness and accuracy, and the risk of double publication. The article emphasized the need for guidelines for the design of pancreatic cancer registries, standardization of core parameters, and mandatory reporting of validation parameters and missing data. It also calls for awareness of the risks of double publication, especially from databases where oversight from the registry/database administration on published articles is lacking.

Finally, the article ‘Treatment of oligo-metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma to the liver: is there a role for surgery? A narrative review’ by Felice Giuliante and colleagues from the Fondazione Policlinico Universitario Agostino Gemelli IRCCS, Rome, Italy, discussed the potential benefits of surgical treatment for patients with PDAC that has metastasized to the liver (pp. 6163–6169). The authors provocatively suggested that surgical treatment for patients with liver-only oligometastases from PDAC has shown promising results in highly selected patients, particularly those with metachronous metastases after perioperative chemotherapy. They noted that the survival rate for patients with metachronous PDAC liver metastases who underwent surgical treatment varied from 11.4 to 57.6 months, suggesting that this approach may be beneficial for carefully selected patients. However, they concluded that there is no standardized definition for oligometastatic disease, and more research is needed to reliably assess the role of resection for PDAC metastatic to the liver, particularly the role of perioperative chemotherapy.

In conclusion, the diagnosis, staging, and treatment of PDAC are complex and multifaceted, requiring a multidisciplinary approach and a deep understanding of the disease’s biology. While significant advancements have been made, challenges remain, and further research is needed to optimize treatment strategies and improve patient outcomes. The findings and contributions summarized in this Special Issue highlight the importance of ongoing research in this field and the potential for future advancements to impact the management and prognosis of PDAC significantly.
