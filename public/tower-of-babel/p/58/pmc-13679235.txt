CFI-400945 enhances radiosensitivity in colorectal cancer models by promoting mitotic dysregulation and DNA damage-associated apoptosis
Authors: Jeong-Woo Hwang, Eun-Young Gong, Jae-Min Cho, Shin Kim, Sang Jun Byun, Hyowon Hong, Hye Won Lee, Sung Uk Bae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadioresistance remains a major barrier to improving radiotherapy (RT) efficacy in colorectal cancer (CRC). Since mitotic fidelity and centrosome homeostasis influence cell fate under genotoxic stress, we investigated whether CFI-400945 enhances the radiation response in CRC models and whether this effect is associated with polo-like kinase 4 (PLK4)-related mitotic dysregulation.MethodsHCT116 and HT29 CRC cells were treated with CFI-400945 and/or irradiation. Radiation response was evaluated using MTT viability and clonogenic survival assays, alongside analyses of cell-cycle distribution, centrosome-associated abnormalities, nuclear phenotypes, DNA damage-associated signaling, and apoptotic markers. PLK4 involvement was evaluated using siRNA-mediated PLK4 depletion, and Aurora kinase B (AURKB) phosphorylation was assessed to examine potential Aurora B-related effects. Combination treatment was also tested in non-malignant CCD 841 CoN colon-derived control cells and patient-derived CRC organoids.ResultsCombined CFI-400945 and RT treatment reduced short-term viability and long-term clonogenic survival in HCT116 and HT29 cells compared with either monotherapy. In CCD 841 CoN cells, CFI-400945 did not significantly enhance radiation-associated loss of clonogenic survival versus RT alone. Combination treatment increased G2/M accumulation, >4 N and sub-G1 fractions, γ-tubulin-positive foci, multinucleation, and micronucleation, consistent with centrosome-associated mitotic abnormalities and reduced survival. DNA damage-associated signaling was enhanced, with increased DNA-dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit (DNA-PKcs) and phosphorylated histone H2AX (γH2AX) levels and increased phosphorylation of ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATM) and CHK2. Apoptotic marker induction also increased, including robust cleavage of caspase-3 and PARP1. CFI-400945 did not markedly suppress AURKB phosphorylation under the experimental conditions. Importantly, siRNA-mediated PLK4 depletion reduced clonogenic survival after irradiation and increased cleaved PARP1 and cleaved caspase-3, supporting PLK4 involvement in the radiation response. In patient-derived CRC organoids, combination treatment reduced viability and increased DNA damage-associated and apoptotic markers compared with single treatments.ConclusionsCFI-400945 enhanced radiation response in CRC cell lines and patient-derived organoids, accompanied by centrosome-associated mitotic abnormalities, increased DNA damage-associated signaling, and apoptotic marker induction. siRNA-mediated PLK4 depletion partially recapitulated these effects, supporting a role for PLK4-associated mitotic regulation. However, because CFI-400945 may also target non-PLK4 mitotic kinases, these support an association --between radiosensitization and PLK4-related mitotic dysregulation rather than an exclusively PLK4-dependent mechanism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12935-026-04407-y.

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a major global health burden, with approximately 1.9 million new cases and 930,000 deaths reported worldwide in 2020, and projections indicate a continued rise in incidence and mortality over the coming decades [1]. CRC is characterized by substantial molecular and phenotypic heterogeneity that contributes to variable clinical behaviors, therapeutic responses, and patient outcomes [2]. Clinically, CRC is treated using multimodal strategies that incorporate surgery, systemic chemotherapy, and radiotherapy (RT) [3, 4]. RT is particularly integral to the management of rectal cancer, where neoadjuvant RT or chemoradiotherapy contributes to local tumor control and reduces local recurrence [5–7]. Nevertheless, a substantial proportion of patients exhibit an incomplete response to RT owing to intrinsic or acquired radioresistance, and dose escalation is often limited by toxicity to adjacent normal tissues [3, 8]. Collectively, these limitations underscore the need to elucidate the mechanistic determinants of radioresistance and develop rational radiosensitizing strategies that enhance RT efficacy while minimizing toxicity to adjacent normal tissues.

The cytotoxic effects of RT are primarily mediated by the induction of DNA lesions, including DNA double-strand breaks, that activate coordinated DNA damage response (DDR) networks [9]. DDR signaling, including ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATM)/ATM and Rad3-related (ATR) activation, CHK1/CHK2-mediated checkpoint control, and repair pathways such as homologous recombination (HR) and non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), determines whether irradiated tumor cells arrest, repair, or progress with unresolved damage [9–11]. Accordingly, tumors that retain robust DNA repair and checkpoint control may exhibit reduced radiosensitivity [9, 12]. However, radiosensitivity is not determined solely by DNA repair proficiency. Beyond repair capacity, the fate of irradiated cells is strongly influenced by how damaged genomes are transmitted through cell-cycle progression and mitosis [13, 14]. In particular, mitotic defects can convert residual or sublethal DNA lesions into chromosome segregation errors, micronuclei formation, and lethal genomic instability [13, 15]. Radioresistance often reflects efficient genome maintenance under genotoxic stress, disrupting mitotic fidelity and centrosome homeostasis, which may provide a complementary approach to increasing radiation vulnerability in CRC [16–18]. Although DDR-related mechanisms of radiation response have been extensively investigated, the contribution of mitotic regulation and centrosome-associated processes to radiosensitivity and therapeutic resistance remains unclear.

Mitosis represents a clinically relevant axis for radiosensitization because accurate chromosome segregation requires intact centrosome homeostasis and spindle organization, particularly after genotoxic stress [19, 20]. Centrosomes function as the principal microtubule-organizing centers that orchestrate mitotic spindle assembly, centriole duplication, and faithful chromosome segregation [21]. Under physiological conditions, tight control of centrosome number preserves chromosomal stability [22]. In contrast, centrosome amplification (CA), defined as an abnormal increase in centrosome number, can promote multipolar spindle formation, chromosome missegregation, lagging chromosomes, aneuploidy, and micronuclei formation, thereby contributing to chromosomal instability [23, 24]. These mitotic abnormalities can intersect with radiation-induced DNA damage by increasing the likelihood of cells entering or progressing through mitosis with unresolved genomic lesions. Such defective mitotic progression may culminate in mitotic catastrophe, a process associated with aberrant mitosis, multinucleation, and subsequent cell death [19, 25]. These observations suggest that targeting mitotic fidelity and centrosome-associated processes may increase the vulnerability of tumor cells to DNA-damaging therapies, including RT [26, 27]. However, the extent to which centrosome-associated mitotic dysregulation contributes to radiation response in CRC remains unclear [28].

Polo-like kinase 4 (PLK4) is a serine/threonine kinase that serves as a central regulator of centriole biogenesis and centrosome duplication, thereby coordinating centrosome homeostasis and cell cycle progression [21]. Dysregulated PLK4 expression or activity can disrupt centrosome number and mitotic fidelity, processes closely associated with chromosomal instability [23, 29]. In CRC, increased PLK4 expression promotes tumor progression and is associated with aggressive clinicopathological features including larger tumor size, lymph node metastasis, and advanced TNM stage [16, 30, 31]. In rectal cancer, PLK4 expression in pre-treatment specimens has also been proposed as a potential biomarker associated with response to neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy [32]. These observations provide a rationale for investigating PLK4-associated mitotic regulation as a potential modifier of radiation response in CRC.

CFI-400945 is a small-molecule inhibitor originally developed to target PLK4, and has been reported to induce dysregulated centriole duplication, mitotic defects, and cancer cell death in preclinical models [33–35]. Notably, the radiosensitizing effects of PLK4 suppression have been reported in certain tumor contexts, supporting the broader concept that perturbation of mitotic regulation can enhance radiation responses [28, 36]. However, the contribution of PLK4-associated mitotic regulation to the RT response in CRC remains unclear [32]. In addition, because CFI-400945 has been reported to affect other mitotic kinases, including Aurora B, the pharmacological findings obtained with this compound require interpretation together with genetic PLK4 validation and assessment of Aurora kinase B (AURKB)-associated signaling [37]. Therefore, it is important to determine whether CFI-400945 enhances the radiation response in CRC models through mitotic dysregulation, DNA damage accumulation, and apoptotic signaling and whether PLK4 depletion recapitulates these effects.

In this study, we investigated whether CFI-400945 enhanced radiation sensitivity in CRC models and examined the associated cellular mechanisms, focusing on mitotic dysregulation, DNA damage accumulation, and apoptotic signaling. Using CRC cell lines with distinct genetic backgrounds and patient-derived CRC organoids, we assessed the effects of CFI-400945, RT, and their combination on cell viability, clonogenic survival, cell-cycle progression, centrosome-associated abnormalities, nuclear defects, DDR activation, and apoptosis. To strengthen the target interpretation, we further evaluated PLK4 involvement using siRNA-mediated PLK4 depletion and assessed AURKB phosphorylation after CFI-400945 treatment. We also examined the effect of the combined CFI-400945 and RT treatment on non-malignant colon-derived control cells to estimate the relative impact on normal cells. Collectively, our findings provide experimental evidence that CFI-400945 enhances radiation response in CRC models and supports the involvement of PLK4-associated mitotic dysregulation in this response, while acknowledging the need to consider potential non-PLK4 mitotic kinase signaling.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

HCT116 and HT29 human CRC cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. The non-malignant human colon-derived epithelial-like cell line, CCD 841 CoN, was obtained from ATCC (CRL-1790; Manassas, VA, USA). CCD 841 CoN cells were maintained in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (EMEM; ATCC or Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Irradiation was delivered using a clinical linear accelerator (VitalBeam, Varian, USA) with 6-MV X-rays at a dose rate of 2 Gy/min, and the cells were exposed to the indicated doses. CFI-400945, a small-molecule inhibitor originally developed to target PLK4, was kindly provided by Dr. Shin Kim and dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a stock solution. For experiments involving CFI-400945 (CFI-400945 alone or in combination with irradiation), cells were treated with CFI-400945 for 2 h prior to irradiation or sham irradiation. For the siRNA-based irradiation experiments, cells were irradiated 48 h after transfection with control or PLK4-targeting siRNAs.

siRNA-mediated PLK4 knockdown

Cells were seeded in culture plates and incubated for 24 h before transfection. The cells were transfected with non-targeting control siRNA or PLK4-targeting siRNA synthesized by Bioneer (Daejeon, Republic of Korea) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For transfection, siRNA and Lipofectamine RNAiMAX were diluted in Opti-MEM reduced-serum medium (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), and control siRNA and PLK4 siRNA were used at a final concentration of 25 nM. 48 h after transfection, PLK4 knockdown was confirmed using western blot analysis. For irradiation experiments, cells were exposed to irradiation or sham irradiation 48 h after transfection and used for clonogenic survival and apoptosis-related analyses. The siRNA sequences were as follows: non-targeting control siRNA, sense 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′ and antisense 5′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′; PLK4 siRNA, sense 5′-CCUUCUAUCUUGGAGCUUUTT-3′ and antisense 5′-AAAGCUCCAAGAUAGAAGGTT-3.’

Cell viability assay

To evaluate the radiosensitizing effect of CFI-400945 in CRC cell lines, cell viability was measured using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5,000 cells per well in 200 µL of complete medium and allowed to attach for 24 h. Cells were assigned to vehicle control, CFI-400945 alone, irradiation alone, or the combination of CFI-400945 and irradiation, and subsequently incubated for either 72–96 h, as specified for each experiment. Cell line-specific concentrations of CFI-400945 were selected based on preliminary dose-response assays to achieve sublethal drug exposure, which is defined as approximately 70% cell viability after CFI-400945 treatment alone. MTT solution (50 µL; 0.5 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The culture medium was removed and the formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm using an Infinite M200 Pro or F200 Pro microplate reader (TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland).

Clonogenic assay

A clonogenic survival assay was performed to assess the effects of CFI-400945 treatment or siRNA-mediated PLK4 depletion on the radiation-associated suppression of long-term colony-forming ability in HCT116, HT29, and CCD 841 CoN cells. For CFI-400945-based experiments, cells were seeded in 12-well plates at 600 cells/well and incubated for 24 h. Cells were then assigned to vehicle control, CFI-400945 alone, irradiation alone, or a combination of CFI-400945 and irradiation groups. CFI-400945 was added 2 h before irradiation or sham irradiation. For siRNA-based experiments, cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 600 cells/well, incubated for 24 h, and transfected with non-targeting control siRNA or PLK4-targeting siRNA. The cells were assigned to control siRNA, PLK4 siRNA, control siRNA plus irradiation, or PLK4 siRNA plus irradiation groups. 48 h after transfection, cells were exposed to the indicated doses of irradiation or sham irradiation. The culture medium was gently replaced every 2 days with fresh complete medium, with CFI-400945 included only in the corresponding CFI-400945-treated groups, and the cells were incubated for 14 days. For the siRNA-based clonogenic assays, no additional siRNA transfection was performed after the initial transfection. During medium replacement, the culture medium was aspirated gently from the edge of each well, and fresh medium was added slowly along the wall of the well to minimize the disturbance of the attached cells. Cell attachment and colony growth were monitored by microscopy during the assay, and the same medium replacement procedure was used for all treatment groups. Following incubation, the colonies were rinsed twice with PBS, fixed in cold methanol for 20 min, and stained with 0.25% crystal violet solution for 30 min. Colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted as single colonies and ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used for quantification.

Cell cycle analysis

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well and incubated for 24 h. Cells were assigned to the following groups: vehicle control, CFI-400945 alone, irradiation alone, or the combination of CFI-400945 and irradiation, and then incubated for 24–48 h. After incubation, cells were detached using trypsin-EDTA, washed with PBS, and fixed in 70% cold ethanol in PBS overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, cells were washed with PBS and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with 50 µg/mL propidium iodide (PI, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 100 µg/mL RNase solution (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). Cell cycle distribution was analyzed by flow cytometry using FACSCanto II (BD Biosciences, Becton Dickinson, San Jose, CA, USA). Cell cycle distribution was quantified using Flowing Software 2 (Turku Centre for Biotechnology, Turku, Finland). PI-based DNA content histograms were used to define the sub-G1, G0/G1, S, G2/M, and >4 N populations. The same phase-gating criteria were consistently applied to all treatment groups, and representative gated histograms are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Western blot

For Western blot analysis, CRC cells and patient-derived CRC organoids were harvested 48 h after irradiation or sham irradiation following treatment with CFI-400945 or transfection with PLK4 siRNA, as indicated in each experiment. For the CFI-400945-based experiments, the cells were treated with CFI-400945 for 2 h before irradiation. For siRNA-based experiments, cells were irradiated 48 h after transfection with a non-targeting control siRNA or PLK4-targeting siRNA. For protein extraction, the cells and organoids were lysed in RIPA buffer (Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) containing 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (APExBIO, Houston, TX, USA) for 20 min on ice. Cell lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the collected supernatants were used for protein quantification with the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Protein samples were separated on 8–15% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk or BSA in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C. Subsequently, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were developed using an ECL solution (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) and visualized using a Fusion Fx7 system (Vilber Lourmat, Marne-la-Vallée, France). Immunoblot quantification was performed using FusionCapt Advance software. Information on the antibodies used is shown in Table 1.

Immunocytochemistry (ICC)

For immunocytochemistry, cells were grown on coverslips and assigned to vehicle control, CFI-400945 alone, irradiation alone, or a combination of CFI-400945 and irradiation. For the CFI-400945-treated groups, the cells were treated with CFI-400945 for 2 h prior to irradiation or sham irradiation. After 48 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. The cells were blocked for 1 h in PBS containing 3% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100. Following blocking, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in 1% BSA containing 0.1% Triton X-100. After incubation, cells were treated with Alexa Fluor 488- or 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclear staining was performed using a mounting solution containing DAPI (Vector, Burlingame, CA, USA), and the samples were mounted with coverslips. Fluorescence images were acquired using a Leica Stellaris 5 confocal microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and quantified using the ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). Detailed information on the immunocytochemistry is presented in Table 1.

Establishment of patient-derived colorectal cancer organoids

Fresh human CRC tissues were obtained from Keimyung University Dongsan Medical Center (Daegu, Korea). All patients underwent surgical resection after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy and the resected CRC tissues were immediately processed for patient-derived organoid (PDO) establishment. Fresh tissues were washed twice with PBS, cut into approximately 1 mm³ pieces, and incubated with 1 mg/mL collagenase IV (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) for 1 h at 37 °C with vortexing every 10 min with gentle shaking. After incubation, the mixture was centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended in advanced DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min; the washing step was repeated twice. Subsequently, the cells were strained through a 100 μm cell strainer (Corning, NY, USA) and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was removed, and the cells were resuspended in cold Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA), seeded into 24-well plates, and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to allow Matrigel solidification. Organoids were cultured in 1 mL advanced DMEM/F12 supplemented with 1× GlutaMax (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), HEPES (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), primocin (InvivoGen, San Diego, CA, USA), B27 (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), N-2 (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), 1.25 mM N-acetylcysteine (NAC, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), 50 ng/mL recombinant human EGF (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), 100 ng/mL Noggin (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), 10 mM nicotinamide (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), 500 nM A83-01 (Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, UK), and 10 µM SB431542 (Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, UK). Following the first subculture or seeding, to prevent apoptotic cell death, 10 µM Y-27,632 (Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, UK), a ROCK inhibitor, was included in the medium for 2–3 days.

For passaging and seeding, Matrigel was dissolved in 1 mL Cell Recovery Solution (Corning, NY, USA) on ice, and organoids were collected by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Organoids were dissociated using TrypLE Express enzyme (Gibco,

Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C for 5 min with gentle pipetting to obtain single cells or small clusters. After dissociation, cells were washed with advanced DMEM/F12, centrifuged, resuspended in cold Matrigel, and seeded into 24-well plates. Matrigel domes were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to solidify, and the organoid culture medium was then added. Patient-derived colorectal cancer organoid (PDO) viability assay

To evaluate the radiosensitizing effect of CFI-400945 in patient-derived colorectal cancer organoids (PDOs), organoid cells were embedded in Matrigel in 96-well plates (100 cells/well) in 100 µL of organoid culture medium and incubated for 24 h. PDOs were then treated with vehicle control, CFI-400945, radiation, or the combination of CFI-400945 and radiation, and incubated for 72 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. Cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo® Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). An equal volume of the reagent was added to the culture medium, followed by orbital shaking for 10 min to induce lysis. The luminescence was measured using an Infinite M200 Pro or F200 Pro microplate reader (TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland). Luminescence values were normalized to the vehicle control, which was set to 100%, and presented as a percentage of the control. Experiments were performed as biological replicates (n = 3).

Bright-field imaging of PDOs

To visually corroborate the effects of CFI-400945 and radiation on PDO growth, organoid cells were embedded in Matrigel in 12-well plates (10,000 cells/well) in 1 mL of organoid culture medium and incubated for 24 h. PDOs were treated with vehicle control, CFI-400945, radiation, or their combination and incubated for 72 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. Representative bright-field images of the organoids were acquired using an eXcope K5 microscope (DIXI Science, Daejeon, Korea) at 100× magnification. Images were captured using identical acquisition settings across the treatment groups. Representative images are shown (one field per biological replicate; n = 3).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times, and data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using the Student’s t-test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by multiple comparison analysis was used for comparisons among three or more groups. Experiments involving two independent variables–treatment concentration, incubation time, radiation dose, and treatment group–were analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by Šídák’s multiple comparison test. All statistical analyses and graphs were generated using the GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical significance was denoted by *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Results

Assessment of PLK4 expression, AURKB phosphorylation, and siRNA-mediated PLK4 depletion in CRC cells

PLK4 is a serine/threonine kinase that regulates centriole duplication and centrosome biogenesis and may contribute to centrosome-associated mitotic regulation in cancer cells. To establish the rationale for examining PLK4 in CRC models, we first compared the basal PLK4 protein expression between non-malignant colon-derived control cells and CRC cells. PLK4 expression was higher in HCT116 and HT29 cells than in CCD 841 CoN cells, providing a rationale for examining PLK4-associated mitotic regulation in CRC cells (Fig. 1A, B).

Next, we examined whether irradiation with CFI-400945 alone or a combination of both altered PLK4 protein expression in CRC cells. CFI-400945 treatment alone did not significantly change PLK4 expression in HCT116 or HT29 cells. Radiation alone slightly reduced PLK4 expression, whereas combined treatment with CFI-400945 and radiation markedly decreased PLK4 protein levels compared to either treatment alone (Fig. 1C-E). These findings indicate that PLK4 protein levels were reduced under combined CFI-400945 and radiation treatment.

Because CFI-400945 has been reported to affect mitotic kinases other than PLK4, we examined AURKB phosphorylation under the same treatment conditions. p-AURKB levels were not substantially changed by CFI-400945 treatment alone compared to those in control cells. Irradiation increased p-AURKB levels, whereas the addition of CFI-400945 did not alter p-AURKB levels compared with irradiation alone. Total AURKB expression remained largely unchanged across the treatment groups (Fig. 1F-H). These results suggested that CFI-400945 did not markedly suppress AURKB phosphorylation under the experimental conditions used in this study.

To further validate the involvement of PLK4, independent of pharmacological treatment, we performed siRNA-mediated PLK4 knockdown. PLK4 siRNA reduced PLK4 protein expression in both HCT116 and HT29 cells compared to control siRNA (Fig. 1I-K). These data confirmed efficient PLK4 depletion and provided a basis for subsequent functional analyses of the radiation response following PLK4 knockdown. Collectively, these findings provide a rationale for examining PLK4-associated mitotic regulation in CRC cells and support the use of PLK4 siRNA-mediated depletion to further evaluate the contribution of PLK4 to the radiation response. AURKB phosphorylation data further suggested that CFI-400945 did not markedly suppress AURKB phosphorylation under the experimental conditions used in this study.

CFI-400945 treatment reduced the viability of both the cell lines in a dose- and time-dependent manner, with a more pronounced reduction observed after 96 h than after 72 h. During the preliminary dose-response screening, HCT116 and HT29 cells showed different sensitivities to CFI-400945 (Fig. 2A, B). Therefore, subsequent combination experiments were performed using cell line-specific sublethal concentrations of CFI-400945, which preserved approximately 70% of cell viability after CFI-400945 treatment alone. This approach allowed the evaluation of the combined effect of CFI-400945 and irradiation without excessive cytotoxicity from CFI-400945 alone.

CFI-400945 enhances radiation-associated centrosome abnormalities and aberrant nuclear phenotypes in CRC cells

To examine whether CFI-400945 enhances radiation-associated centrosome abnormalities and nuclear defects in CRC cells, HCT116 and HT29 cells were treated with CFI-400945, RT, or the combination and incubated for 48 h. Immunocytochemistry was then performed using γ-tubulin staining to assess centrosome-associated foci. CFI-400945 and RT alone induced a modest increase in the number of γ-tubulin-positive foci. Cells with more than two γ-tubulin-positive foci were defined as having CA, and the percentage of positive cells was quantified. In HCT116 cells, combined treatment with CFI-400945 and RT increased the proportion of cells with CA compared to either monotherapy (Fig. 4A, B). Similarly, in HT29 cells, CFI-400945 or RT alone modestly increased CA, whereas the combination treatment further increased CA compared to either CFI-400945 or RT alone (Fig. 4C, D). As centrosomal abnormalities are closely associated with defective mitotic progression, we next examined whether CFI-400945 enhances radiation-associated nuclear abnormalities in CRC cells. HCT116 and HT29 cells were treated as described above, stained to visualize the cytoskeleton and nuclei, and assessed for multinucleation and micronucleation by fluorescence microscopy. In HCT116 cells, the combination treatment increased the proportion of multinucleated and micronucleated cells compared with either treatment alone (Fig. 5A–C). Similar findings were observed in HT29 cells, where CFI-400945 enhanced radiation-associated multinucleation and micronucleation compared to either monotherapy (Fig. 5D–F). These data indicate that CFI-400945 potentiates radiation-associated centrosome abnormalities and aberrant nuclear phenotypes, which is consistent with defective mitotic progression in CRC cells.

PDOs were treated with vehicle, CFI-400945, RT, or their combination for 48 h, and apoptosis-associated proteins were analyzed by Western blotting. In both PDO models, combined treatment with CFI-400945 and RT increased p53 protein levels and markedly elevated the apoptotic cleavage markers, cleaved PARP1 and cleaved caspase-3, compared to either monotherapy (Fig. 10A-D). These findings indicate that the combined treatment with CFI-400945 and RT enhances apoptotic signaling in CRC PDOs, as reflected by the increased levels of cleaved PARP1 and cleaved caspase-3, in association with enhanced DNA damage-associated signaling.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated whether CFI-400945 enhances radiation response in CRC models and whether this response is associated with PLK4-related mitotic dysregulation, DNA damage signaling, and apoptosis. In both HCT116 and HT29 cells, combined treatment with CFI-400945 and irradiation reduced clonogenic survival and increased sub-G1 and >4 N populations, γ-tubulin-positive foci, multinucleation, and micronucleation. These changes were accompanied by increased levels of DNA damage-associated markers, including phosphorylated histone H2AX (γH2AX), DNA-PKcs, phosphorylated ATM, and phosphorylated CHK2, as well as apoptotic markers, including cleaved PARP1 and cleaved caspase-3. Importantly, siRNA-mediated PLK4 depletion enhanced radiation-induced loss of clonogenic survival and increased cleaved PARP1 and cleaved caspase-3 levels, supporting the involvement of PLK4 in the radiation response. CFI-400945 did not markedly suppress AURKB phosphorylation under the conditions used in this study. These response patterns were further supported in patient-derived colorectal cancer organoids, where the combined treatment reduced viability and increased DNA damage-associated and apoptotic markers compared with single treatments.

Since total PLK4 protein abundance does not directly reflect PLK4 kinase activity, we did not interpret the reduced PLK4 levels after the combined treatment as a direct pharmacodynamic marker of PLK4 inhibition [33]. PLK4 is tightly regulated by ubiquitin–proteasome turnover via an autophosphorylation-dependent degron that promotes SCF/βTrCP-mediated degradation, providing a mechanistic framework by which genotoxic stress could alter net PLK4 abundance [38–40]. Because PLK4 abundance is regulated through ubiquitin–proteasome turnover, genotoxic stress may influence PLK4 protein stability in a context-dependent manner [40, 41]. In HCT116 and HT29 cells, total PLK4 protein abundance showed a treatment-dependent pattern, with minimal changes after CFI-400945 treatment alone, a modest reduction after irradiation, and the most pronounced decrease after combined treatment at 48 h. A similar reduction in PLK4 levels after the combined treatment was observed in patient-derived colorectal cancer organoids (Figs. 1 and 8). These results indicate that the combination of CFI-400945 and irradiation is associated with reduced total PLK4 protein abundance. However, this reduction should be interpreted as a treatment-associated change in PLK4 protein levels rather than as definitive evidence of PLK4 inhibition. The reduction in the PLK4 signal after PLK4 siRNA transfection further supported the specificity of the detected PLK4 band and enabled functional validation of PLK4 involvement in the radiation response.

Aberrant PLK4 upregulation has been reported in multiple cancers and is frequently associated with aggressive clinicopathologic features [42]. In CRC, high tumor PLK4 expression has been linked to advanced disease and poor OS [16, 30]. Similar adverse prognostic associations have also been described in other epithelial malignancies, including breast cancer [42, 43]. Beyond tumor growth, PLK4 has been implicated in therapeutic resistance; notably, PLK4 promoted radioresistance in glioblastoma models [42, 44]. Consistent with the concept that mitotic regulatory pathways can influence radiation response, CFI-400945 has been reported to enhance radiation response in non-small cell lung cancer, and PLK4 inhibition combined with radiation has shown synergistic antitumor effects in triple-negative breast cancer models [27, 28]. In line with these observations, our data showed that CFI-400945 enhanced radiation-induced suppression of cell viability and clonogenic survival in CRC models. Importantly, siRNA-mediated PLK4 depletion also reduced clonogenic survival after irradiation compared to control siRNA, supporting the involvement of PLK4 in the radiation response. In patient-derived colorectal cancer organoids, the combined CFI-400945 and irradiation treatment decreased viability and was associated with reduced organoid growth in representative bright-field images. In contrast, CFI-400945 did not significantly enhance the radiation-associated loss of clonogenic survival compared to irradiation alone in CCD 841 CoN non-malignant colon-derived control cells under the tested conditions. Together, these findings support PLK4-associated mitotic regulation as a potential contributor to the radiation response in CRC. Further validation using additional genetic tools, rescue experiments, and normal intestinal models will be required to define on-target dependency and therapeutic selectivity.

PLK4 is a key regulator of centriole duplication and centrosome homeostasis, which supports bipolar spindle assembly and faithful chromosomal segregation during mitosis [21]. Ionizing radiation can perturb centrosome homeostasis and mitotic progression, leading to CA, multipolar mitoses, chromosome missegregation, and nuclear abnormalities that are often associated with mitotic catastrophe-like outcomes [20, 45, 46]. In our CRC models, combined CFI-400945 and irradiation treatment increased G2/M and >4 N populations, γ-tubulin-positive foci, multinucleation, and micronucleation, indicating that the combination promoted centrosome-associated mitotic abnormalities under genotoxic stress. Because γ-tubulin-positive foci remained detectable and were increased after treatment, we interpret these findings as centrosome-associated abnormalities rather than centrosome loss. These phenotypes are consistent with defective mitotic progression and polyploidization, which may contribute to reduced clonogenic survival and subsequent apoptotic signaling. Given that CFI-400945 may affect mitotic kinases beyond PLK4, and that PLK4 perturbation can produce different centrosome/centriole phenotypes depending on the cellular context, these data should be interpreted as evidence of amplified mitotic vulnerability rather than a single deterministic PLK4-specific mechanism [47].

Ionizing radiation exerts its antitumor effects primarily by inducing DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), thereby activating the DNA damage response (DDR) to enforce checkpoints and coordinate repair through the NHEJ and HR pathways regulated by ATM/ATR and DNA-PK signaling [48, 49]. Mechanistically, centrosomal and mitotic dysfunction can promote chromosome missegregation and micronucleus formation. Micronuclei undergo defective or asynchronous replication, which can generate DNA breaks and fragmentation, providing a mechanistic link between mitotic errors and heightened DDR signaling under genotoxic stress [50, 51]. Consistent with this mechanistic framework, CFI-400945 treatment has been reported to increase γH2AX and activate DDR-associated signaling, including ATM/ATR pathway activation, in cancer models [52, 53]. In addition, irradiation can drive CA and multipolar mitosis, linked to mitotic catastrophe–like outcomes, reinforcing the convergence of DNA damage and mitotic failure pathways [20, 46]. In this study, combined CFI-400945 and irradiation treatment produced the strongest induction of DDR/DSB-associated markers, including DNA-PKcs, p-ATM, p-CHK2, and γH2AX, in CRC cell lines and patient-derived colorectal cancer organoids. Given that γH2AX and checkpoint phosphorylation primarily reflect DDR signaling—and that γH2AX can also arise from replication stress or apoptosis—we interpret these data conservatively as enhanced or sustained DDR signaling consistent with increased damage burden and/or delayed damage resolution, rather than definitive evidence of a specific repair pathway blockade [54–56]. To further refine this interpretation, time-resolved γH2AX/DDR foci kinetics and orthogonal DSB assays will help distinguish increased DSB induction from delayed damage resolution under the combined regimen [57].

Ionizing radiation–induced DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) can trigger apoptosis when damage is excessive or poorly resolved, commonly through DDR-linked activation of intrinsic apoptotic signaling and caspase activation [58, 59]. Although p53 can participate in this process, apoptotic responses after irradiation may also occur through p53-independent mechanisms, depending on the cellular context. Additionally, mitotic failure following genotoxic stress can converge on apoptotic execution, and mitotic catastrophe is frequently discussed as an upstream process that culminates in apoptosis in a context-dependent manner [25]. Consistent with this framework, CFI-400945 has been reported to induce apoptotic phenotypes, including caspase-3/7 activation and PARP1 cleavage in cancer models [53, 60, 61]. In this study, the combined CFI-400945 and irradiation treatment produced the most pronounced apoptotic signaling in CRC cells, with increased p53 accumulation in HCT116 cells, a pro-apoptotic shift in BCL-2 family markers, and a robust induction of cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved PARP1. In HT29 cells, p53 levels were not markedly altered, but the combination treatment still increased Bax, cleaved caspase-3, and cleaved PARP1, while reducing Bcl-2, indicating that apoptotic execution markers increased, even in a distinct p53-mutant background. Importantly, siRNA-mediated PLK4 depletion combined with irradiation also increased cleaved PARP1 and cleaved caspase-3 levels compared with control siRNA plus irradiation, further supporting the involvement of PLK4 in radiation-associated apoptotic signaling. Patient-derived colorectal cancer organoids showed increased levels of apoptotic cleavage markers under combination therapy. Because the TP53 status of the PDO models was not available, and p53 was not genetically manipulated in the present study, these data should not be interpreted as evidence of p53-dependent apoptosis. Rather, we conservatively interpreted the combination effect as enhanced apoptotic commitment associated with increased DNA damage signaling and defective mitotic progression.

However, the limitations of this study warrant further consideration. First, although CFI-400945 was originally developed to target PLK4, its cellular selectivity is not absolute and off-target effects involving other mitotic kinases, including Aurora B, have been reported [37, 60]. In the present study, CFI-400945 did not markedly suppress AURKB phosphorylation under the tested conditions, and siRNA-mediated PLK4 depletion partially recapitulated the radiosensitizing and apoptosis-associated effects of CFI-400945. However, because only one PLK4 siRNA sequence was used and no rescue experiments were performed, the contribution of PLK4 could not be fully established. Future studies using multiple independent PLK4 siRNA/shRNA sequences, rescue experiments, and more selective PLK4-targeting tools are required to further define the on-target dependency [47, 62, 63]. Second, although CFI-400945 did not significantly enhance radiation-associated loss of clonogenic survival in CCD 841 CoN non-malignant colon-derived control cells under the tested conditions, this single normal colon-derived epithelial-like cell model was insufficient to define a therapeutic window or normal tissue safety. In vivo combination studies and additional normal intestinal models, including normal colon organoids, are necessary to evaluate tumor–normal tissue selectivity. Third, although PDOs provide patient-proximal evidence, they do not fully recapitulate stromal, vascular, and immune components that shape radiation response, and the number of PDO models examined in this study was limited [64]. In addition, the TP53 status of the PDO models was not available, limiting our ability to determine whether p53 status influenced the response to CFI-400945 and RT. Finally, time-resolved DDR and apoptosis assays, together with selected causality tests, will help distinguish increased lesion induction from delayed resolution, and clarify which death pathways are functionally required for the combination phenotype [65].

In summary, our data across CRC cell lines and PDOs show that CFI-400945 enhances the radiation response, reducing short-term viability and long-term reproductive survival, while amplifying DNA damage-associated signaling and apoptosis. The observation that siRNA-mediated PLK4 depletion enhanced radiation-associated clonogenic suppression and apoptotic cleavage marker expression supports the involvement of PLK4-associated mitotic regulation in this response. These findings support a model in which perturbation of centrosome- and mitosis-associated processes lowers the threshold of radiation-induced genotoxic stress to trigger lethal outcomes. However, because CFI-400945 may affect mitotic kinases beyond PLK4, these data should be interpreted as evidence that CFI-400945-mediated radiosensitization is associated with PLK4-related mitotic dysregulation, rather than as definitive proof of an exclusively PLK4-dependent mechanism. Further studies involving additional genetic validation, rescue experiments, expanded patient-derived and normal intestinal models, and in vivo RT models are required to define the therapeutic relevance and safety boundaries of this combined approach.

Conclusions

CFI-400945 enhances the radiation response in CRC cell lines and PDOs, accompanied by centrosome-associated mitotic abnormalities, increased DNA damage-associated signaling, and induction of apoptotic markers. siRNA-mediated PLK4 depletion partially recapitulated the radiosensitizing and apoptosis-associated effects of CFI-400945, supporting the involvement of PLK4-associated mitotic regulation in the radiation response. However, given the potential activity of CFI-400945 against non-PLK4 mitotic kinases, these findings should be interpreted as evidence that CFI-400945-mediated radiosensitization is associated with PLK4-related mitotic dysregulation, rather than as definitive proof of an exclusively PLK4-dependent mechanism. Further studies are required to define on-target dependency, normal tissue selectivity, optimal treatment schedules, and in vivo efficacy before the translational relevance of this combination approach in CRC radiotherapy can be determined.
