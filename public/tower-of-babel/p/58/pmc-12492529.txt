Lipid reprogramming and ferroptosis crosstalk in clear cell renal cell carcinoma: metabolic vulnerabilities and therapeutic targeting
Authors: Qing Deng, Yu Ji, Jian Liu, Tao Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractClear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) features metabolic dysregulation, with altered lipid metabolism and ferroptosis dysregulation driving malignancy. This review examines the interplay between lipid reprogramming and ferroptosis resistance in ccRCC pathogenesis and therapy. Tumor cells exploit lipid accumulation for growth and evade ferroptosis adaptively. Preclinical studies show targeting lipid metabolism or inducing ferroptosis synergizes with anti-angiogenic/immunotherapy, improving survival. This study provides a framework for dual therapeutic strategies.Keywords: CcRCC, Lipid reprogramming, Ferroptosis, Metabolic vulnerabilities, Therapeutic targeting

Background

ccRCC, accounting for 70%−80% of all renal cancer cases, represents the most prevalent subtype of RCC. Despite recent advances in diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, ccRCC continues to exhibit high incidence and mortality rates, particularly in patients with advanced disease who demonstrate poor clinical outcomes [1–4]. A hallmark pathological feature of ccRCC is the intracellular accumulation of lipid droplets, strongly implicating dysregulated lipid metabolism in tumor initiation and progression [5–8]. Beyond serving as fundamental membrane components, lipids play multifaceted roles in energy provision, signal transduction, and cellular proliferation, making the investigation of lipid metabolic alterations in ccRCC crucial for both understanding its pathogenesis and developing novel therapeutic strategies [9–14].

Emerging research has revealed that ccRCC exhibits profound lipid metabolic abnormalities, characterized by enhanced de novo fatty acid synthesis, increased lipid uptake, and impaired fatty acid oxidation [15, 16]. These metabolic adaptations not only provide essential building blocks for energy production and membrane biogenesis but also activate oncogenic signaling pathways that drive tumor growth and metastasis. Notably, lipid metabolism demonstrates intricate interplay with ferroptosis, where polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) peroxidation and glutathione (GSH) metabolic disruption constitute core features of this iron-dependent cell death process [17, 18]. While lipid accumulation in ccRCC underscores an intrinsic Susceptibility to ferroptosis, these tumor cells evade this cell death process by activating adaptive metabolic compensatory mechanisms, Such as upregulating glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) expression or modulating acyl-CoA synthetase long chain family member 4 (ACSL4) activity [19, 20]. Additionally, recent studies have demonstrated that lipid metabolic reprogramming and ferroptosis regulation synergistically drive tumor microenvironment remodeling and immunotherapy resistance in ccRCC. By integrating multi-omics data, developing precision therapeutic strategies targeting lipid metabolism and ferroptosis pathways and combination immunotherapies has emerged as a promising new direction in ccRCC treatment.

This review provides a comprehensive analysis of lipid metabolic reprogramming in ccRCC, elucidating its molecular mechanisms, pathophysiological consequences, and potential as therapeutic targets for precision oncology. Particularly, this review delves into the intrinsic connections between lipid metabolic reprogramming and ferroptosis, and their impact on the development and progression of ccRCC. Through this comprehensive analysis, we aim to provide new perspectives and approaches for future treatment strategies in ccRCC.

Lipid Metabolic Reprogramming in ccRCC

Alterations in lipid uptake in CcRCC

Extensive experimental evidence demonstrates that ccRCC displays a characteristic metabolic Shift toward enhanced exogenous fatty acid uptake relative to adjacent normal Renal tissues. At the molecular level, this phenotypic alteration is driven by the significant upregulation of key fatty acid transport proteins, including cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36) and fatty acid transport protein family members (FATPs), which facilitate lipid accumulation in tumor cells.

CD36, a membrane-bound fatty acid translocase, facilitates long-chain fatty acids (LCFAs) uptake by binding to extracellular ligands [21]. Studies have demonstrated consistent overexpression of CD36 in human ccRCC tissues and cell lines, correlating with shorter progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) [22]. The loss of Von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) in ccRCC leads to an increase in the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF-1α) and hypoxia-inducible factor 2 alpha (HIF-2α), both of which can upregulate the expression of CD36 [23, 24]. Chemerin is a pleiotropic adipokine that modulates Lipid metabolism, immune regulation, and angiogenesis through its cognate receptors, chemokine-like receptor 1 (CMKLR1) and G protein-coupled Receptor 1 (GPR1) [25, 26]. Notably, CMKLR1 promotes CD36-mediated exogenous Lipids uptake via sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c (SREBP1-c) [27]. In ccRCC, the mitochondrial outer membrane-localized rate-limiting enzyme of fatty acid β-oxidation, carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1 A (CPT1A), exhibits significantly reduced expression and enzymatic activity compared to normal renal tissues. This CPT1A deficiency upregulates CD36 expression, driving intracellular lipid accumulation. Overexpression of CPT1A significantly downregulated CD36 expression and led to reduced tumor volume and decreased lipid deposition in mouse models [28–30].

Fatty acid transport proteins (FATPs), located in the cell membrane, mediate cellular uptake and transport of LCFAs [31]. In ccRCC, FATP4 expression is significantly higher than in normal cells, and elevated FATP4 levels are associated with shorter RFS in patients [32]. Thus, FATP4 may be a key mediator of lipid metabolic reprogramming in ccRCC and a potential prognostic biomarker for disease progression.

Scavenger receptor B class I (SCARB1) is a membrane-bound receptor protein primarily responsible for mediating the uptake of high-density lipoprotein (HDL)-associated cholesterol [33]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that SCARB1 is upregulated in ccRCC, with increased HDL cholesterol uptake through SCARB1 contributing to intracellular cholesterol accumulation in ccRCC [34, 35]. Studies have shown that ccRCC cells uptake cholesterol via SCARB1 and activate the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway to promote proliferation, with the underlying mechanism involving VHL gene mutations upregulating SCARB1 expression through the HIFα/PI3K/AKT/mTOR/SREBP signaling axis [36–38]. Circ_0003146 upregulates SCARB1 by sponging miR-1272, consequently accelerating ccRCC malignancy [39]. Clinical analyses established that SCARB1 overexpression positively correlates with larger tumor size, advanced histological grade, and distant metastasis, while inversely associating with PFS, underscoring its prognostic significance in ccRCC [40]. A recent study revealed that circular RNA circABCA1 interacts with insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 3 (IGF2BP3) to stabilize SCARB1 mRNA, thereby enhancing cholesterol uptake while suppressing its efflux, ultimately promoting ccRCC cell proliferation and migration. Furthermore, by modulating cholesterol metabolism, circABCA1 remodels the tumor microenvironment and induces M2 macrophage polarization, which in turn accelerates ccRCC progression [41].

The low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) is a cell surface receptor protein primarily responsible for the uptake of circulating low-density lipoprotein (LDL) [42]. It has been demonstrated that LDLR expression is elevated in ccRCC tissues compared to normal kidney tissue and progressively increases with RCC advancement. Moreover, LDLR knockdown has been demonstrated to suppress the proliferation, migration, and invasion of ccRCC cells [43]. VHL loss causes an increase in HIFα, which activates the PI3K/AKT/mTOR/SREBP pathway, driving LDLR transcription. This enhances cholesterol uptake in ccRCC, leading to cholesteryl ester (CE) accumulation and promoting ccRCC progression [37].

Fatty acid-binding proteins (FABPs) are small intracellular proteins that bind LCFAs and facilitate their transport from the cell membrane into cells, acting as key molecular chaperones for fatty acid transport [44]. Research has identified significant upregulation of FABP5 in ccRCC, with its expression levels demonstrating a positive correlation with tumor progression. When FABP5 is depleted, ccRCC cell proliferation, colony formation capacity, and migratory potential are markedly impaired, establishing FABP5 as a promising therapeutic target for ccRCC treatment [45].

In summary, the upregulation of these lipid transport proteins enhances exogenous lipid uptake in ccRCC, driving metabolic reprogramming and promoting lipid accumulation. Inhibiting these transporters may partially curb ccRCC progression. Exploring targeted interventions against these proteins could enable precise exploitation of ccRCC’s metabolic vulnerabilities, offering new therapeutic avenues for patients.

Alterations in lipogenic metabolism in CcRCC

Fatty acid biosynthesis

Fatty acids (FAs), as essential structural components of cell membranes, energy sources, and signaling molecules, can be acquired through either exogenous uptake or de novo synthesis. Numerous studies have demonstrated that de novo fatty acid synthesis is also critical for lipid accumulation in ccRCC.

The synthesis of FAs involves multiple enzymatic steps: First, within the mitochondria, acetyl-CoA (AcCoA) and oxaloacetate (OAA) are condensed into citrate (CIT) by citrate synthase (CS). Citrate is then transported to the cytosol via the citrate transport protein (CTP). In the cytosol, ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY) cleaves CIT back into AcCoA and OAA. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) subsequently catalyzes the carboxylation of AcCoA to form malonyl-CoA (MCoA). Next, AcCoA and MCoA undergo a series of condensation reactions mediated by the fatty acid synthase (FAS) complex via acyl carrier protein (ACP). This process involves repeated cycles of reduction, dehydration, and further reduction, each extending the fatty acid chain by two carbon units, ultimately yielding 16-carbon palmitate [46]. Current studies have demonstrated enhanced de novo fatty acid synthesis in ccRCC, characterized by upregulated expression of key enzymes including ACLY, ACC, FAS, and stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD).

ACLY, a pivotal enzyme in lipogenesis, plays a critical role in various cancers. In ccRCC tissues, ACLY protein expression is significantly upregulated compared to adjacent normal tissues, and its high expression correlates positively with tumor progression and poor clinical prognosis. Inhibition of ACLY suppresses ccRCC cell proliferation and migration while inducing apoptosis [47]. Further mechanistic studies have revealed the regulatory network of ACLY in ccRCC: HIF-2α directly binds the lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 1 (LPCAT1) promoter to enhance its transcription, whereas suppression of LPCAT1 expression triggers the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, which subsequently upregulates the expression of the E3 ubiquitin ligase FBXW7, leading to the ubiquitination and degradation of ACLY, ultimately inhibiting de novo lipogenesis [48]. Moreover, the micropeptide ACLY-BP, encoded by LINC00887, stabilizes ACLY, driving lipid accumulation and ccRCC proliferation [49]. Additionally, high ACLY expression is associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and enhanced tumor aggressiveness mediated by epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT)/mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signaling [50].

In addition to the ACLY pathway, AcCoA can also be generated through direct acetate conversion catalyzed by acyl-CoA synthetase (ACSS) [51]. In ccRCC, ACSS2 demonstrates significant overexpression that correlates with poor clinical prognosis. Notably, the ACSS2-dependent acetate metabolic pathway is markedly enhanced under hypoxic conditions. Furthermore, ACSS2 regulates HIF-2α stability, where genetic knockout or pharmacological inhibition of ACSS2 significantly reduces glycolytic flux and impairs mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation in ccRCC cells [52].

ACC, the rate-limiting enzyme converting AcCoA to MCoA, is upregulated in ccRCC and contributes to metabolic reprogramming [53]. Mechanistic investigations showed that 8-chloroadenosine (8-Cl-Ado) activates adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) to phosphorylate and inhibit ACC, attenuating lipogenesis [54]. Further research implicated Hippo signaling in ACC regulation [55] while additional analyses established significant correlations between ACC expression and the AMPK/GATA binding protein 3/short-chain enoyl-CoA hydratase (AMPK/GATA3/ECHS1) pathways [56].

FAS, the multi-enzyme complex responsible for palmitate synthesis, is significantly overexpressed in aggressive ccRCC [57–59]. Mechanistic studies reveal two distinct regulatory pathways in ccRCC: (1) Hippo pathway-mediated control of FAS expression [55] and (2) the fat mass and obesity-associated protein/oxoglutarate dehydrogenase-like/transcription factor AP-2 alpha (FTO/OGDHL/TFAP2A) axis promoting FAS transcription through m6A demethylation [60]. Pharmacological inhibition of FAS markedly increases apoptosis rates while reducing lipid droplet formation in ccRCC cells [61]. As a member of the aurora kinase family, aurora kinase B (AURKB) regulates mitosis and cytokinesis through substrate phosphorylation, playing an oncogenic role in tumorigenesis [62–64]. Notably, AURKB is overexpressed in ccRCC, and its silencing suppresses FASN expression, establishing a functional link between mitotic regulation and lipid metabolism [65].

SCD catalyzes FA desaturation to generate unsaturated FAs for membrane and lipid droplet biogenesis. At the molecular regulatory level, Krüppel-like factor 6 (KLF6) promotes SCD expression through sterol regulatory element-binding transcription factor 1/2 (SREBF1/2)-dependent transcriptional activation, thereby modulating lipid synthesis in ccRCC [66]. SCD1 is a member of the SCD family, and extensive research has demonstrated its involvement in various physiological functions in mammals [67]. SCD1 is overexpressed in ccRCC and sustains tumor survival [68]. HIF1α transactivates AHNAK nucleoprotein 2 (AHNAK2) to stabilize SCD1, with AHNAK2 knockdown reducing lipogenesis in ccRCC [69]. Reciprocal regulation between SCD1 and HIF-2α via PI3K-Akt promotes ccRCC aggressiveness [70]. Y-box binding protein 1 (YB-1) and small-molecule drug STF-62,247 targeting VHL modulate SCD1-dependent lipid storage [71, 72].

These enzymes (ACLY, ACC, FAS, SCD1), which play crucial roles in lipid metabolism, are actionable targets in ccRCC. Inhibitors targeting these enzymes have demonstrated preclinical efficacy in disrupting lipid addiction. This discovery not only provides important insights into the metabolic characteristics of tumor cells but also opens up new avenues for the development of novel anti-tumor drugs.

Triglyceride biosynthesis in CcRCC

In addition to fatty acids, triacylglycerol (TAG) represents another essential component of lipids. TAG synthesis primarily occurs in the endoplasmic reticulum through a sequential enzymatic process. Glycerol-3-phosphate (G-3-P) and fatty acyl-CoA (FA-CoA) serve as substrates for glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase (GPAT), generating lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), which is subsequently converted to phosphatidic acid (PA) by 1-acylglycerol-3-phosphate O-acyltransferase (AGPAT). PA undergoes dephosphorylation via lipin phosphatidic acid phosphatase (PAP) to form diacylglycerol (DAG), which is finally esterified with a third FA-CoA molecule by diacylglycerol acyltransferase (DGAT) to produce TAG. This pathway provides essential lipids for energy storage and membrane biogenesis [73, 74].

G-3-P originates either from glycerol phosphorylation by glycerol kinase (GK) or through reduction of dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) by glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GPD). In ccRCC, the expression of GPD1 is significantly downregulated. GPD1 can stabilize HIF1α, which directly suppresses the transcription of GPD2, thereby inhibiting mitochondrial function. Overexpression of GPD1 effectively suppresses tumor progression in ccRCC. Although overexpression of GPD1 may lead to an increase in G-3-P, providing more substrates for triglyceride synthesis, the elevated G-3-P is more likely to be channeled toward glycerophospholipid production, and the increase in G-3-P is transient [75].

LPA is a key regulator of tumor progression, acting through its receptors to modulate processes including cell proliferation, migration, survival, and inflammation [76]. LPA activates phospho-MAPK and phospho-S6 signaling pathways, increasing lipid droplet (LD) size and abundance while upregulating diacylglycerol acyltransferase 2 (DGAT2) mRNA expression to enhance TAG synthesis and cell survival [77]. Additionally, LPA activates Arf6 - a small GTPase governing plasma membrane component recycling - through G protein-coupled receptors, to facilitate mesenchymal-type invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance in ccRCC [78].

DGAT, a key rate-limiting enzyme in TAG synthesis, plays a pivotal role in LD biogenesis and ccRCC tumorigenesis. In ccRCC, the expression levels of DGAT1 and DGAT2 are markedly upregulated. Mechanistically, HIF-2α promotes the transcription of RNASET2, a conserved acidic ribonuclease belonging to the T2 ribonuclease family [79] to drive the upregulation of DGAT1 and DGAT2, leading to LD accumulation and subsequent enhancement of tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and migration [80]. Jumonji domain-containing protein 6 (JMJD6), a bifunctional enzyme with both N-methylarginine demethylase and lysyl C-5 hydroxylase activities, cooperates with RNA-binding motif protein 39 (RBM39) to occupy the DGAT1 promoter region. This interaction facilitates transcriptional activation via H3K4me3 histone modification. Both genetic and pharmacological inhibitors of DGAT1 significantly suppress LD formation and ccRCC progression [81]. Additionally, CD36 enhances cellular fatty acid uptake, thereby supplying DGAT1 with abundant substrates (fatty acyl-CoA) to further promote LD formation and lipid storage [23].

Cholesterol biosynthesis in CcRCC

Cholesterol is a critical steroid molecule that mediates diverse physiological functions, including maintenance of membrane architecture, vitamin D biosynthesis, and cellular signaling transduction [82, 83]. Cholesterol synthesis occurs primarily in the cytoplasm and endoplasmic reticulum, initiating from AcCoA. The pathway begins with the condensation of AcCoA into 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) catalyzed by HMG-CoA synthase (HMGCS). HMG-CoA reductase (HMGCR) then converts HMG-CoA into mevalonate, the rate-limiting step in cholesterol biosynthesis. Mevalonate undergoes sequential phosphorylation by mevalonate kinase (MVK) and phosphomevalonate kinase (PMVK), followed by decarboxylation by diphosphomevalonate decarboxylase (MVD) to yield isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP). IPP is isomerized to dimethylallyl pyrophosphate (DMAPP) by IPP isomerase, and subsequent reactions catalyzed by geranyl pyrophosphate synthase (GPPS) and farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase (FPPS) generate farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP). Two molecules of FPP are condensed by squalene synthase to form squalene, which is then cyclized by squalene epoxidase and oxidosqualene cyclase to produce lanosterol. Finally, lanosterol undergoes a series of modifications, including cytochrome P450-mediated demethylation, reductase-catalyzed double-bond reduction, and side-chain remodeling, ultimately yielding cholesterol [84]. Notably, HMGCR expression is significantly upregulated in ccRCC tissues [85]. Correspondingly, therapeutic intervention with statins (HMGCR inhibitors) effectively Suppresses both tumor initiation and progression in Subcutaneous 786-O-derived ccRCC xenograft models [86].

Alterations in lipid catabolism in CcRCC

Fatty acid β-oxidation

Fatty acid β-oxidation, a central pathway for cellular energy metabolism, is critically dysregulated in ccRCC pathogenesis. This process initiates with fatty acid activation by acyl-CoA synthetase (ACS) in the cytosol, forming FA-CoA. Long-chain acyl-CoAs require transport into the mitochondrial matrix via the carnitine shuttle system composed of CPT1, carnitine-acylcarnitine translocase (CACT), and CPT2. Within mitochondria, acyl-CoA undergoes four sequential reactions catalyzed by acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (ACAD), enoyl-CoA hydratase (ECH), 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (HADH), and ketoacyl-CoA thiolase (KAT) through dehydrogenation, hydration, secondary dehydrogenation, and thiolysis to generate AcCoA [87].

In ccRCC, acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 1 (ACSL1) is significantly downregulated, and its reduced expression correlates with disease progression and poor prognosis. Epigenetic analyses demonstrate that CpG island methylation is the primary mechanism underlying ACSL1 suppression [88]. Intriguingly, the role of ACSL3 in lipid accumulation appears to be paradoxical, likely attributable to its dual functionality in regulating both lipid metabolism and ferroptosis [89, 90].

In VHL-deficient ccRCC, elevated HIF1α and HIF2α inhibit CPT1A transcription, impairing mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation [30, 91]. Both CPT1A and CPT1B, members of the CPT1 family, serve as rate-limiting enzymes for fatty acid β-oxidation [92]. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα) activates CPT1A expression, and pharmacological activation of the PPARα/CPT1A axis using WY-14,643 significantly inhibits ccRCC proliferation, migration, and lipid deposition [93]. Furthermore, CPT1A sustains PPARα activity by regulating SCARB1-mediated and CD36-mediated exogenous fatty acid uptake, forming a self-reinforcing positive feedback loop [28]. Research indicates that the metal-binding protein MT1G downregulates the expression of CPT1B by inhibiting trimethylation of lysine 14 on histone H3 (H3K14me3), thereby exacerbating mitochondrial β-oxidation dysfunction and promoting lipid droplet accumulation [94]. Additionally, CPT2 expression is downregulated in ccRCC and correlates with advanced pathological stages and unfavorable prognosis. Overexpression of CPT2 significantly inhibits ccRCC cell proliferation, invasion, and migration, while concurrently enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of sorafenib against ccRCC cells [95].

Medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (ACADM), a rate-limiting enzyme in mitochondrial β-oxidation of medium-chain fatty acids, is downregulated in ccRCC and correlates with poor patient prognosis, though its regulatory mechanisms remain unclear [96]. AMPK activates the transcription factor GATA3 through the β-catenin signaling pathway, thereby enhancing the expression of short-chain enoyl-CoA hydratase 1 (ECHS1). However, in ccRCC, both the expression levels and activities of AMPK and ECHS1 are significantly reduced. Overexpression of ECHS1 markedly suppresses the proliferation, clonogenicity, and migratory capabilities of ccRCC cells [56, 97]. Similarly, the expression of HADH is reduced in ccRCC, with its deficiency linked to poor clinical outcomes. Mechanistically, HADH inhibits tumor growth by blocking NRF2 nuclear translocation and glutathione synthesis, thereby reducing lipid droplet formation [98]. Acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase 1 (ACAT1), another downregulated enzyme in ccRCC, promotes fatty acid oxidation while suppressing cell growth and inducing apoptosis when overexpressed. ACAT2 downregulation is similarly associated with tumor progression and unfavorable prognosis [99, 100].

Other lipid catabolic pathways

In addition to the β-oxidation pathway, fatty acids can also be decomposed through the α-oxidation pathway and the ω-oxidation pathway. The specialized alpha-oxidation pathway, primarily occurring in peroxisomes, degrades branched-chain fatty acids (BCFA) that cannot undergo β-oxidation. Cytosolic phytanoyl-CoA is hydroxylated at the α-carbon by phytanoyl-CoA hydroxylase (PHYH), then cleaved by 2-hydroxyphytanoyl-CoA lyase (2-HPL) to form pristanoyl-CoA. PHYH is downregulated in ccRCC, with high expression correlating with better prognosis and serving as an independent prognostic factor [101]. The cytochrome P450-mediated ω-oxidation pathway converts fatty acids to dicarboxylic acids via ω-hydroxy and ω-aldehyde intermediates, though its alterations in ccRCC require further investigation. Cholesterol can be utilized by the body to perform various physiological functions, Such as constituting cell membranes, synthesizing steroid hormones, and bile acids. Among these, the synthesis of bile acids is an important pathway for cholesterol metabolism. Cholesterol 27-hydroxylase (CYP27A1) is a key enzyme in the conversion of cholesterol to bile acids. It reduces intracellular cholesterol content by activating the liver X receptor (LXR)/ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1) pathway, thereby inhibiting the proliferation and migration of ccRCC [102] (Fig. 1 ).

Interplay between lipid metabolic reprogramming and ferroptosis dysregulation in CcRCC

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of programmed cell death characterized by the excessive accumulation of lipid peroxides and the collapse of the GSH-dependent antioxidant system. Intracellular free Fe²⁺ catalyzes lipid peroxidation via the Fenton reaction, leading to the irreversible buildup of PUFA peroxidation products, which disrupt plasma membrane structure and function. Concurrently, the loss of GPX4 activity exacerbates ferroptosis.

Excessive lipid accumulation primes CcRCC cells for ferroptosis

In ccRCC, VHL gene loss stabilizes HIF-2α, driving profound lipid metabolic reprogramming. The characteristic accumulation of lipid droplets provides abundant PUFA substrates, making ccRCC inherently sensitive to ferroptosis [103].

The ACSL family and lipoxygenases (LOXs) critically regulate ferroptosis. ACSL1, frequently downregulated in ccRCC due to CpG island methylation, correlates with poor prognosis. A multi-omics analysis shows that ACSL1 may be involved in regulating the ferroptosis process [88]. ACSL3/4 expression enhances the susceptibility of ccRCC cells to ferroptosis, suggesting a critical role in redox homeostasis [90, 104]. ACSL3 inhibition reduces ferroptosis Susceptibility in a manner dependent on exogenous fatty acids and 5-LOX activity [89]. ACSL4 determines ferroptosis sensitivity by modulating membrane PUFA composition [105]. GA-binding protein transcription factor subunit α (GABPA), downregulated in ccRCC, binds the ACSL4 promoter to enhance ferroptosis sensitivity while suppressing tumor proliferation and invasion [19]. LOXs catalyze PUFA oxidation to hydroperoxides, which act as ferroptosis executioners. Vitamin E antagonizes ferroptosis by inhibiting LOXs activity [106]. 5-lipoxygenase (LOX5) is one of the members of LOXs and is significantly highly expressed in ccRCC tumor tissues [107].

Enhancement of the reducing system in CcRCC

The cystine/glutamate antiporter system xc⁻, a heterodimeric complex composed of SLC7A11 (xCT) and SLC3A2 (4F2HC/CD98HC) [108] mediates cystine uptake in exchange for glutamate efflux [109]. And the uptake of glutamine in ccRCC is also increased to produce more glutamate in exchange for cystine [110]. Intracellular cystine is reduced to cysteine by thioredoxin reductase (TXNRD1) [111] promoting GSH biosynthesis [112]. As a critical antioxidant, GSH scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) and sustains GPX4 activity [113]. The small molecule erastin selectively inhibits system xc⁻, disrupting antioxidant defenses and inducing ferroptosis [17]. Impaired β-oxidation in ccRCC cells leads to increased lipid storage and impaired fatty acid degradation, making ccRCC cells highly dependent on cysteine uptake and GSH synthesis to prevent the accumulation of lipid peroxides and maintain cell viability [18].

Cysteine combines with glutamate under the catalytic action of glutamate-cysteine ligase (GCL) to form γ-glutamylcysteine (γ-GC). Subsequently, γ-glutamylcysteine is further combined with glycine, catalyzed by glutathione synthetase (GS), to synthesize GSH. Studies demonstrate that in ccRCC, GSH levels are elevated [5]. This elevation effectively counteracts the negative effects of ROS, thereby maintaining tumor viability and growth. Therapeutic approaches targeting GSH metabolism primarily involve inhibiting the activity of key enzymes or transporters such as SLC7A11, GLS, and GCL [114].

SLC7A11 and SLC3A2 demonstrate positive expression in ccRCC tissues, with their expression levels correlating with tumor cell proliferation and poor patient prognosis [115, 116]. Ovarian tumor deubiquitinase 3 (OTUD3) is specifically upregulated in ccRCC, where it stabilizes SLC7A11 protein through deubiquitination, thereby suppressing ferroptosis and promoting sunitinib resistance. Knockdown of OTUD3 significantly enhances susceptibility to sunitinib-induced ferroptosis [117]. Salinomycin triggers ferroptosis in ccRCC cells by inducing autophagic degradation of protein disulfide isomerase family A member 4 (PDIA4), which consequently inhibits the activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4)-SLC7A11 signaling axis [118]. Notably, enhanced cystine uptake is observed not only in tumor cells but also in immune cells within the tumor microenvironment [119] indicating that therapeutic targeting of system xc⁻ requires careful evaluation of its potential effects on normal cells. Elevated levels of kynurenine – a tryptophan metabolite with ROS-scavenging capacity – are observed in ccRCC tissues. Kynurenine is transported into tumor cells via SLC7A11, where it activates the NRF2 pathway, thereby upregulating SLC7A11 expression. Through this dual mechanism of enhanced SLC7A11 expression and ROS clearance, tumor cells not only acquire ferroptosis resistance but may also promote tumor metastasis. Notably, although kynurenine can suppress T-cell proliferation and induce apoptosis by augmenting fatty acid β-oxidation in T cells, its limited concentration increase within the tumor microenvironment results in minimal immunosuppressive effects [120–122]. Enolase 2 (ENO2), a crucial glycolytic enzyme, is upregulated in ccRCC where it potently enhances glycolytic flux to provide additional carbon precursors for fatty acid accumulation. ENO2 depletion may trigger ferroptosis in ccRCC cells through multiple mechanisms, including glycolysis suppression, mitochondrial dysfunction, and/or downregulation of SLC7A11 expression [123]. GLS2 exhibits antioxidant effects within the cell and is upregulated during ferroptosis. Its knockdown reduces cell viability, depletes GSH, and increases lipid peroxides, making cells more susceptible to oxidative stress-induced damage and subsequent ferroptosis [124].

Epigenetic modifications also regulate GSH levels in ccRCC. The deletion of the histone demethylase KDM5C enhances antioxidant capacity by promoting glycogen accumulation and pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) activation, thereby increasing NADPH and GSH levels [125].

GPX4 is a key inhibitor of ferroptosis, and its depletion triggers the accumulation of lethal lipid peroxides. Studies have found that GPX4 expression is increased in ccRCC and is associated with disease progression and poor prognosis in patients [126, 127]. ccRCC exhibits heightened sensitivity to GPX4 inhibition, and drug-resistant cancer cells rely on GPX4 for survival, making GPX4 a promising therapeutic target [113]. Krüppel-like factor family members KLF2 and KLF11 can promote ferroptosis by inhibiting ferritin, system xc⁻, and GPX4, but their expression is downregulated in ccRCC [128, 129]. Tribble pseudokinase family member TRIB3 suppresses ferroptosis in ccRCC by stabilizing the SLC7A11-GPX4 axis [130]. Circular RNA circASAP1 can stabilize GPX4 mRNA, is overexpressed in ccRCC, and is associated with poor prognosis. Depletion of circASAP1 can downregulate GPX4 and activate ferroptosis [20]. As a member of the transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) superfamily, growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF15) is specifically downregulated in ccRCC and exhibits a negative correlation with GPX4 [131]. Metallothionein-1G (MT1G) can confer resistance to ferroptosis in ccRCC cells by reducing the depletion of GSH [132]. In VHL-mutant HK-2 cells, upregulated lipocalin-2 (LCN-2) increases ROS and inhibits GPX4, thereby sensitizing cells to ferroptosis [133].

Ferroptosis Suppressor protein 1 (FSP1) inhibits ferroptosis independently of the GPX4 pathway by catalyzing the regeneration of reduced coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) [134]. Its molecular mechanism relies on utilizing NAD(P)H as a reducing equivalent to convert oxidized CoQ10 into reduced coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10H₂)—a potent antioxidant. CoQ10H₂ subsequently suppresses ferroptosis by scavenging lipid peroxyl radicals, thereby inhibiting lipid peroxidation [135]. Notably, as a key enzyme in the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid cycle, malate dehydrogenase 2 (MDH2) enhances ferroptosis sensitivity by promoting lipid peroxidation and degrading FSP1 via ubiquitination. In ccRCC, MDH2 downregulation leads to FSP1 accumulation, reducing ferroptosis susceptibility and fueling tumor growth [136].

In summary, lipid accumulation in ccRCC sensitizes tumor cells to ferroptosis while simultaneously enabling an adaptive up-regulation of antioxidant defenses that counteract this lethal stress. VHL loss–driven lipid reprogramming supplies abundant PUFA substrates, and the concomitant down-regulation of ACSL1/4 and GABPA, together with up-regulation of 5-LOX, sets the membrane-peroxidation threshold. To neutralize the ensuing oxidative stress, ccRCC cells overexpress the cystine/glutamate antiporter system xc⁻ (SLC7A11/SLC3A2), thereby enhancing cystine import and boosting glutathione (GSH) biosynthesis and GPX4 activity. This antioxidant capacity is further reinforced by epigenetic events—such as KDM5C deletion—and by metabolic circuits involving the kynurenine–NRF2 axis and the PPP–NADPH pathway. Thus, ccRCC operates at a tenuous equilibrium: lipid excess predisposes cells to ferroptosis, whereas the fortified GSH/GPX4 and FSP1–CoQ10H₂ axes confer resistance. This redox balance constitutes a rational target for precision therapies designed to selectively disrupt antioxidant homeostasis and trigger ferroptotic cell death (Fig. 2).

Targeting lipid accumulation and ferroptosis for improved CcRCC therapy

The overexpression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) mediated by HIF-α has established angiogenesis as a primary therapeutic target in ccRCC. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), such as sunitinib and pazopanib, have become the first-line standard treatment options for ccRCC by blocking VEGF receptors (VEGFRs) [137]. However, these inhibitors have limited efficacy and may cause adverse reactions such as vascular toxicity and off-target effects. Therefore, the development of new treatment methods for ccRCC is urgently needed. Emerging therapeutic strategies focus on the interaction between lipid metabolism and ferroptosis, opening up new avenues for cancer treatment. Increasing evidence suggests that targeting the crosstalk between lipid metabolic reprogramming and ferroptosis dysregulation has therapeutic potential in ccRCC.

Inhibit lipid accumulation

Lipid accumulation may be the culprit behind cancer cell resistance to treatment, and by reducing lipid accumulation, cancer cells may regain sensitivity to therapy.

HIFα plays a crucial role in lipid reprogramming in ccRCC, making it a potential therapeutic target. Across multiple clinical trials, HIF inhibitors have demonstrated prominent antitumor activity in both patients with ccRCC in VHL disease and those with advanced ccRCC [138–140]. Moreover, the combination therapy of HIF-2α antagonist with targeted immunotherapy and VEGFR TKIs demonstrates more significant efficacy compared to monotherapy [141]. Malonyl-CoA decarboxylase (MLYCD) is a key regulatory factor in fatty acid synthesis. Its expression is decreased in ccRCC, and restoring its expression can inhibit tumor progression [142].

The exploration of lipid metabolism-targeted therapies for ccRCC has yielded significant findings. The LXR agonist LXR623 effectively reduces intracellular cholesterol levels and induces tumor cell apoptosis by downregulating LDLR expression while upregulating ABCA1. Concurrently, the LXR inverse agonist SR9243 markedly decreases intracellular fatty acid content and selectively triggers apoptosis in ccRCC cells through suppression of SREBP-1c, FASN, and SCD1 expression, without exerting significant toxicity on normal renal tubular epithelial HK-2 cells [143]. In studies investigating lipid-lowering agents, simvastatin—an inhibitor of the mevalonate synthesis pathway—not only diminishes ccRCC cell viability and induces autophagy and apoptosis but also potently suppresses tumor metastasis and lipid accumulation. Its mechanism involves blockade of cholesterol synthesis and protein prenylation, particularly through inhibition of RhoA pathway activation, thereby exerting anti-metastatic effects. These outcomes are reversible upon exogenous mevalonate supplementation [144]. Salinomycin inhibits fatty acid synthesis by suppressing SREBP1, which can enhance the sensitivity of ccRCC cells to sorafenib-induced ferroptosis, thereby overcoming drug resistance [145]. Collectively, these discoveries provide a critical theoretical foundation for metabolically targeted therapies in ccRCC.

Several studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of combining lipid synthesis targeting with other therapies in various types of cancer. For instance: when combined with targeted therapy, it can inhibit the progression of non-small cell lung cancer [146]; when used in conjunction with chemotherapy, it can slow down the progression of breast cancer; when combined with radiotherapy [147], it can help control the progression of both breast and lung cancer [148]; and when used alongside immunotherapy, it can suppress the progression of melanoma and colorectal cancer [149]. Therefore, targeting lipid metabolism in ccRCC in combination with other therapies holds great therapeutic promise.

In ccRCC, lipid metabolites critically interact with immune cells: upregulated ganglioside (GM2) binds to T-cell surfaces, inducing apoptosis through caspase-3/8/9 pathway activation while suppressing IFN-γ secretion in CD4+ T cells [150]. Microenvironmental TNF-α synergistically enhances GM2 synthase expression in tumor cells, promoting GM2 accumulation and amplifying T-cell death signals [151, 152]. Concurrently, overexpressed apolipoprotein1 (APOC1) in ccRCC drives macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, reinforcing the immunosuppressive microenvironment [153]. These mechanisms collectively suggest that targeting lipid-immune interactions could enhance therapeutic efficacy in ccRCC.

Induce ferroptosis

This section will independently introduce therapies targeting ferroptosis, which primarily function by inducing cellular ferroptosis.

The upregulation of SLC7A11 and GPX4 confers ferroptosis resistance in ccRCC. Studies have shown that blocking SLC7A11 function or inhibiting GPX4 activity can promote ferroptosis [154]. For example, conventional ferroptosis inducers Such as erastin and RAS-selective lethal 3 (RSL3) act by inhibiting SLC7A11 function and GPX4 activity, respectively [155, 156]. Beyond these traditional ferroptosis inducers, a recent study has identified moscatilin—a bibenzyl compound derived from Dendrobium orchids—as a novel agent that induces ferroptosis in ccRCC cells by blocking SLC7A11 function, leading to reduced intracellular GSH levels and enhanced ferroptosis [157]. More significantly, a preclinical trial revealed that imetelstat (a telomerase inhibitor) promotes PUFA synthesis accumulation, indirectly suppresses GPX4 activity, and consequently induces ferroptosis. This process triggers lipid peroxidation and elevates oxidative stress in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cells, effectively reducing AML burden and delaying post-chemotherapy relapse [158].

Although ccRCC exhibits natural sensitivity to ferroptosis due to its lipid accumulation characteristics, it can suppress the activation of ferroptosis through various mechanisms and develop resistance to TKIs. For example, IL-6, TRIB3, and NRF2 can upregulate SLC7A11 expression to resist ferroptosis, thereby inducing TKI resistance in ccRCC [130, 159–161]. Furthermore, overexpression of absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2) leads to downregulation of ACSL4, which subsequently reduces the sensitivity of ccRCC to ferroptosis and results in resistance to sunitinib [162]. Increasing evidence indicates that ferroptosis induction can reverse tumor chemoresistance [163]. For example, chrysin, a natural bioflavonoid, can induce ferroptosis and enhance the sensitivity of pancreatic cancer to gemcitabine [164]. Saponin formosanin C induces ferroptosis in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) by promoting the formation of lipid ROS, depleting GPX4, and downregulating the expression of iron transporters and xCT in cells, making it sensitive to cisplatin [165]. Dihydroartemisinin (DHA) enhances chemosensitivity to cisplatin and gefitinib in lung cancer cells and augments sorafenib-mediated growth inhibition in HepG2 cells through ferroptosis induction, while synergizing with cisplatin to induce ferroptosis in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells [166–169]. In ccRCC, curcumin can increase the expression of the ADAMTS18 gene and restore the sensitivity of ccRCC to sunitinib [170]. Artemisinin inhibits the growth of drug-resistant ccRCC by downregulating the expression of GSH and GPX4 [171].

However, the clinical application of conventional ferroptosis inducers remains constrained by several limitations, including poor tumor targeting, unstable in vivo metabolism (characterized by a short half-life and low water solubility), and nonspecific accumulation in off-target tissues. To address this, researchers have developed delivery systems such as iron-based metal-organic framework nanoparticles (MIL-101(Fe) NPs), Fe@Fe3O4 core–shell nanoparticles and the hydrophobic RSL3 encapsulated by the targeting DSPE-PEG-iRGD liposomes (ipFFR). By precisely co-delivering Fe²⁺ and RSL3, these systems significantly enhance the ferroptosis sensitivity of ccRCC. Moreover, these nanomedicines exhibit no significant cytotoxicity toward normal cells, with negligible off-target effects [172–174]. Researchers have discovered a novel oral ferroptosis inducer, KD061, which reduces the levels of HIF-1/2α by targeting iron Sulfur cluster assembly 2 (ISCA2) and induces ferroptosis, demonstrating significant in-vivo anti-tumor effects in ccRCC while exhibiting minimal impact on normal cells at therapeutic doses [175].

Although it has been confirmed that synergizing ferroptosis can enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy drugs against tumor cells, current research is still limited to the laboratory stage, and clinical application studies on the combined use of ferroptosis and chemotherapy drugs are insufficient. In the future, further exploration of the optimal regimens for combining ferroptosis with chemotherapy drugs, as well as personalized treatment strategies for specific tumor types such as ccRCC, can be pursued to achieve better therapeutic effects in clinical practice.

Beyond enhancing chemosensitivity, ferroptosis inducers can also potentiate radiotherapy efficacy. However, their application in radiotherapy may exacerbate damage to normal tissues, potentially leading to adverse effects such as tissue fibrosis [176, 177]. Therefore, when utilizing ferroptosis inducers as radiosensitizers, their impact on normal tissues requires special consideration, and corresponding measures should be implemented to mitigate these adverse effects.

Since 2019, when Professor Wang’s team demonstrated that CD8+ T cells in the tumor microenvironment can secrete IFN-γ to downregulate SLC7A11 while upregulating ACSL4—thereby altering tumor cell lipid metabolism and promoting lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis—the interplay between ferroptosis and immune cells has become a major research focus in cancer immunotherapy [178, 179]. Current studies confirm that inducing tumor ferroptosis triggers immunogenic cell death (ICD), promoting dendritic cell maturation and T cell activation [180–186]. Novel ferroptosis inducers (e.g., dihydroartemisinin and iron oxide nanoparticles) not only enhance NK cell cytotoxicity but also synergize with PD-L1 inhibitors to suppress tumor growth [187, 188]. Notably, Treg cells in the tumor microenvironment rely on GPX4 for survival, and GPX4 inhibitors can increase Treg ferroptosis while promoting CD8+null T cell infiltration [189, 190]. However, CD8+ T cells exhibit ferroptosis vulnerability due to CD36-mediated increased lipid uptake, driving rapid advancements in nano-targeted drug delivery systems to minimize immune cell toxicity [191, 192]. Recent progress shows that combining ferroptosis inducers with immune checkpoint blockade (e.g., anti-PD-1) can reverse drug resistance [193, 194]particularly showing efficacy in “cold” tumors [195]. However, research on this strategy in ccRCC remains limited.

Targeting the crosstalk between lipid metabolism and ferroptosis

In addition to the enhancement of the reductive system, ccRCC cells can also construct a defensive system against ferroptosis through the reprogramming of lipid metabolism. Targeting the critical nodes within this system can transform it from a “protective shield” into an “Achilles’ heel.”

Lipid droplets serve as storage sites for neutral lipids (such as triglycerides and cholesteryl esters), with their primary lipid components being peroxidation-insensitive saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids. In ccRCC cells, excess PUFAs are esterified and stored within lipid droplets, thereby preventing their involvement in lipid peroxidation and reducing cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis. Targeted degradation of lipid droplets facilitates the release of stored PUFAs, increasing the “fuel” for ferroptosis and consequently enhancing cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis [196, 197].

By regulating the expression or activity of key enzymes, the intracellular lipid composition can be directly altered to modulate sensitivity to ferroptosis. ACC is the rate-limiting enzyme in de novo fatty acid synthesis. Inhibiting ACC not only cuts off the supply for lipid synthesis but, more importantly, reduces the production of protective monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), alters membrane lipid composition, and relatively increases the proportion of peroxidation-sensitive PUFAs, thereby enhancing ferroptosis sensitivity [198]. SCD is responsible for generating MUFAs, while ACSL3 activates MUFAs and incorporates them into membrane phospholipids, competitively reducing the proportion of PUFAs and suppressing ferroptosis. Inhibiting both SCD and ACSL3 can not only reduce intracellular lipid accumulation but also promote the occurrence of ferroptosis [199, 200]. Members of the ACSL family, particularly ACSL4, play a key role in activating free PUFAs, converting them into acyl-CoA derivatives for incorporation into membrane phospholipids. Upregulating the expression or activity of ACSL4 promotes the integration of more PUFAs into the cell membrane, providing abundant substrate for lipid peroxidation and thereby sensitizing cells to ferroptosis. Notably, CD8⁺ T cells release IFN-γ and activate the STAT1 signaling pathway to upregulate ACSL4—a mechanism that provides a rationale for combining immunotherapy with ferroptosis inducers [201]. Additionally, ACSL1 also plays an important role in ferroptosis: conjugated fatty acids such as α-eleostearic acid are incorporated into membrane lipids via ACSL1-mediated esterification. Their conjugated double-bond structure makes them more prone to lipid peroxidation, thereby inducing ferroptosis; studies have shown that this process can inhibit tumor growth and metastasis in mouse models of breast cancer [202]. ABHD12 is an enzyme that hydrolyzes lysophosphatidylserine (LPS) and 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG); inhibition of its activity leads to the accumulation of these lipids and significantly enhances cancer cell sensitivity to ferroptosis [203].

In addition to directly targeting key enzymes in lipid metabolism, modulating upstream signaling pathways can indirectly affect the activity and expression of downstream metabolic enzymes, ultimately altering ferroptosis sensitivity. MDM2 and MDMX, as negative regulators of p53, may—when inhibited—activate the PPARα signaling pathway to promote intracellular PUFA accumulation, thereby supplying lipid substrate for ferroptosis [204]. In targeted androgen therapy, prostate cancer cells upregulate genes related to lipid uptake and activate lipid metabolic enzymes, increasing lipid uptake and remodeling—especially elevating the content of polyunsaturated fatty acids in membrane lipids—thereby enhancing sensitivity to ferroptosis [205].

In summary, the therapeutic strategy for ccRCC is shifting from direct cytotoxicity towards exploiting its metabolic vulnerabilities for “synthetic lethality.” The ultimate common goals—whether through directly inhibiting protective enzymes like ACC and SCD, activating pro-ferroptotic enzymes like ACSL4, or modulating upstream signals like PPARα—are to decrease the MUFA/PUFA ratio in cell membranes and increase the PUFA content in membrane phospholipids. Concurrently, disrupting the lipid droplet “firewall” can further release lethal substrates to induce ferroptosis (Fig. 3).

Conclusions

The reprogramming of lipid metabolism and dysregulation of ferroptosis pathways jointly constitute the core mechanisms underlying the malignant progression of ccRCC, offering novel strategies for targeted therapy. Lipid metabolism remodeling drives tumorigenesis and development through multifaceted alterations: enhanced exogenous lipid uptake (via upregulation of CD36, FATP4, SCARB1, LDLR) synergizes with activated endogenous synthesis (overexpression of enzymes like ACLY, FAS, SCD) to promote lipid accumulation, while impaired catabolism (downregulation of CPT1A, ACSL1) further exacerbates lipid storage. This lipid accumulation inherently sensitizes ccRCC to ferroptosis; however, ccRCC employs multiple mechanisms to suppress ferroptosis. On one hand, it inhibits fatty acid catabolism, while on the other hand, it activates antioxidant systems (such as the xCT–GSH-GPX4 axis and FSP-CoQ10 system) and downregulates the expression of pro-ferroptotic proteins (such as ACSL4 and GABPA), thereby forming a protective barrier to counteract lipid peroxidation damage.

Current therapeutic strategies have expanded from traditional anti-angiogenic therapies to metabolic targeting therapies. HIF-2α inhibitors (such as Belzutifan) demonstrate clinical efficacy by blocking the core regulatory axis of lipid metabolism, while drugs directly interfering with lipid synthesis pathways, such as LXR agonists (LXR623) and SCD inhibitors (SR9243), also exhibit antitumor potential. Additionally, inducing ferroptosis has emerged as a novel approach to overcoming treatment resistance: GPX4 inhibitors (such as RSL3) can bypass traditional signaling pathway inhibition, while the combination of iron chelators and lipid peroxidation inducers (such as erastin) enhances efficacy by disrupting the redox balance. Notably, the interplay between epigenetic regulation (such as KDM5C) and metabolic reprogramming has gradually attracted attention, providing new targets for precise intervention.

The therapeutic landscape of ccRCC is evolving from broad cytotoxic strategies toward precision targeting of metabolic vulnerabilities, particularly the interplay between lipid accumulation and ferroptosis resistance. Key approaches—including inhibition of lipid-protective enzymes (e.g., ACC, SCD), activation of pro-ferroptotic drivers (e.g., ACSL4), disruption of lipid droplets, and modulation of upstream signaling pathways—converge on a common goal: reshaping membrane lipid composition to increase PUFA abundance and sensitize tumors to ferroptosis. Combining these strategies with existing modalities (e.g., immunotherapy, targeted agents, and nanotechnology-enhanced delivery) offers a promising synthetic lethality-based framework to overcome therapeutic resistance. Future efforts should prioritize clinical translation, immune-metabolic crosstalk, and personalized nanotherapeutics to maximize efficacy and minimize off-target effects.

In summary, decoding ccRCC’s metabolic vulnerabilities will accelerate the clinical translation of lipid metabolism and ferroptosis modulation, ultimately improving patient outcomes.
