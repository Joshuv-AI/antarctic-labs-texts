Bilateral ictal EEG is associated with better memory outcome after hippocampal sclerosis surgery
Authors: Humberto Castro‐Lima, Valmir Passarelli, Elyse S Ribeiro, Carla C Adda, Ana Paula P Preturlon‐Santos, Carmen L Jorge, Rosa Valério, Wen Hung Tzu, Ney Boa‐Sorte, Leonardo Zumerkorn Pipek, Luiz Henrique M Castro
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare memory outcomes after surgery for unilateral hippocampal sclerosis (HS)‐associated epilepsy in patients with unilateral and bilateral ictal electrographic involvement.MethodsWe prospectively evaluated HS patients, aged 18‐55 years and IQ ≥70. Left (L) and right (R) surgical groups underwent noninvasive video‐EEG monitoring and Wada test. We classified patients as Ipsilateral if ictal EEG was restricted to the HS side, or Bilateral, if at least one seizure onset occurred contralaterally to the HS, or if ictal discharge evolved to the opposite temporal region. Patients who declined surgery served as controls. Memory was evaluated on two occasions with Rey Auditory‐Verbal Learning Test and Rey Visual‐Design Learning Test. Baseline neuropsychological test scores were compared between groups. Pre‐ and postoperative scores were compared within each group. Reliable change index Z‐scores (RCI) were obtained using controls as references, and compared between surgical groups.ResultsWe evaluated 64 patients. Patients were classified as: L‐Ipsilateral (9), L‐Bilateral (15), L‐Control (9), R‐Ipsilateral (10), R‐Bilateral (9), and R‐Control (12). On preoperative evaluation, memory performance did not differ among surgical groups. Right HS patients did not present postoperative memory decline. L‐Ipsilateral group presented postoperative decline on immediate (P = 0.036) and delayed verbal recall (P = 0.011), while L‐Bilateral did not decline. L‐Ipsilateral had lower RCI Z‐scores, indicating delayed verbal memory decline compared to L‐Bilateral (P = 0.012).SignificanceDominant HS patients with bilateral ictal involvement presented less pronounced postoperative verbal memory decline compared to patients with exclusive ipsilateral ictal activity. Surgery was indicated in these patients regardless of memory impairment on neuropsychological testing, since resection of the left sclerotic hippocampus could result in cessation of contralateral epileptiform activity, and, therefore, improved memory function.

Key Points The study compared memory outcomes after surgery for unilateral hippocampal sclerosis (HS)‐associated epilepsy in patients with unilateral and bilateral ictal electrographic involvement.64 patients were evaluated using noninvasive video‐EEG monitoring and Wada test and were classified as Ipsilateral or Bilateral.Patients with bilateral epileptiform activity did not have an increased risk of postoperative memory decline compared to patients with exclusive ipsilateral activity.Dominant HS patients with bilateral ictal involvement presented less pronounced postoperative verbal memory decline compared to patients with exclusive ipsilateral ictal activity.

INTRODUCTION

Postoperative verbal memory decline is a well‐recognized complication of dominant anterior temporal lobectomy and may impact quality of life, especially in high functioning individuals. 1 , 2 , 3 It is less clear if patients undergoing nondominant TLE surgery are at higher risk of nonverbal memory decline. 4 Selecting appropriate surgical candidates requires evaluation of individual chances of postoperative seizure freedom and risk of cognitive decline. Overestimating this risk may lead to erroneously denying surgery to patients who could potentially benefit from the procedure. 5

Factors associated with higher risk of postoperative verbal memory decline in TLE include: better preoperative memory performance, 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 later epilepsy onset, 7 better memory capacity in the sclerotic hippocampus evaluated with Wada test, 11 and stronger left hemisphere lateralization on fMRI language paradigms. 8

Seizure onset on noninvasive video‐EEG contralateral to the MRI evidence of HS does not seem to influence postoperative seizure outcome. 5 , 12 , 13 Contralateral ictal seizure onset may represent an undetected lesion in the contralateral hippocampus or ictal activity spread to the contralateral temporal lobe (“false lateralization”). It remains unclear if bilateral epileptiform activity negatively impacts postoperative cognitive outcome.

Patients with unilateral HS and bilateral epileptiform activity present worse baseline cognitive performance compared to patients with epileptiform activity restricted to the HS side. 14 , 15 Epileptiform activity contralateral to the HS side may be reverted after successful epilepsy surgery, leading to improved function in the contralateral hippocampus.

Considering the potential reversal of contralateral epileptiform activity after surgery, we tested the hypothesis that patients with bilateral ictal involvement present less pronounced memory decline after anterior temporal lobectomy than patients with ictal EEG activity restricted to the HS side.

METHODS

Patients

We selected patients with epilepsy associated with unilateral HS from the Epilepsy Clinic of the Hospital das Clínicas at Faculdade de Medicina da Universidade de São Paulo‐Brazil, between 2009 and 2016. We included patients with pharmaco‐resistant mesial temporal lobe epilepsy, secondary to unilateral HS detected by 3‐Tesla MRI, without dual pathology, aged 18‐55 years, with eight or more education years, without significant medical or psychiatric comorbidities, and IQ ≥70. Patients who declined surgery and agreed to undergo repeated neuropsychological evaluations served as controls. Only patients in the surgical groups underwent noninvasive video‐EEG monitoring and Wada testing. Patients were excluded if they presented extratemporal discharges on Video‐EEG, if they presented right hemisphere language dominance on Wada testing or if they did not undergo postoperative neuropsychological evaluation. All patients signed the informed consent, approved by the institutional ethics committee.

Video‐EEG monitoring and ictal EEG pattern classification:

All patients underwent noninvasive video‐EEG monitoring with a 32‐channel Bio‐logic Ceegraph Vision 7:15:06 equipment (Natus Medical San Carlos, CA, USA). Electrodes were placed according to the 10‐20 system, with additional anterior temporal and subtemporal electrodes. For video‐EEG monitoring, ASM doses were reduced depending on seizure frequency, aiming to record a minimum of three seizures for each patient.

All electrographic seizures were evaluated by the same electroencephalographer (V.P.), and reviewed by at least one other experienced clinical neurophysiologist (C.L.J., H.C.L., L.H.C., and R.M.V.). Patients were classified according to HS side as right (R) or left (L) groups, which were then subdivided into two ictal electrographic pattern groups: Ipsilateral (Ip) if ictal EEG showed ipsilateral onset to the HS side and ictal discharges remained restricted to that side throughout the seizure and Bilateral (Bi) if at least one seizure onset occurred contralaterally or bilaterally to the HS side or if ictal onset occurred on the HS side, but ictal activity evolved to the opposite side. Study flow diagram showing groups divided by HS side and ictal involvement. HS (hippocampal sclerosis); L‐Surgery (left surgery group); R‐Surgery (right surgery group); L‐Ipsilateral (left HS patients with exclusive ipsilateral ictal involvement); L‐Bilateral (left HS patients with bilateral ictal involvement); R‐Ipsilateral (right HS patients with exclusive ipsilateral ictal involvement); and R‐Bilateral (right HS patients with bilateral ictal involvement).

Neuropsychological assessment

All patients underwent a comprehensive neuropsychological battery on two occasions, by an experienced neuropsychologist (C.C.A., A.P.S, and E.R.S), blinded to Video‐EEG findings. Patients in the surgical groups were tested at least 12 months after surgery. Controls were retested after at least 6 months to minimize follow‐up losses. IQ was estimated using WAIS III matrix reasoning and vocabulary test scores. Verbal memory was evaluated with the Rey Auditory‐Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) 16 and nonverbal memory was assessed with the Rey Visual‐Design Learning Test (RVDLT). 17 For both tests, we considered Immediate recall as the total number of words/items recalled after the encoding phase, and Delayed recall as the total number of words/items recalled after 30 min, postinterference. 17 , 18 , 19

Clinical and demographic data included: age, years of education, handedness, age at epilepsy onset, epilepsy duration, mean frequency of seizures with impaired awareness 3 months prior to each evaluation, total antiseizure medication (ASM) load, and sedative ASM load on each evaluation. We considered barbiturates, benzodiazepines, and topiramate as sedative drugs (sASM). AED loads were calculated with the formula: ASM1/sASM1 + ASM2/sASM2… ASMn/sASMn = ASM load. The numerator represents total daily ASM dose and the denominator represents a standard ASM dose. 20 Adopted standard ASM doses were as follows: phenobarbital 100 mg/day, phenytoin 300 mg/day, carbamazepine 600 mg/day, oxcarbazepine 900 mg/day, valproate 750 mg/day, clobazam 15 mg/day, clonazepam 2 mg/day, topiramate 200 mg/day, primidone 250 mg/day, lamotrigine 200 mg/day, and gabapentin 900 mg/day.

Wada testing

Wada test was performed on all patients in the surgical group using etomidate due to sodium amytal unavailability, according to a published institutional protocol, which is safe and effective. 21 Strength and language were tested prior to anesthetic injection. Both hemispheres were sequentially tested, starting with injection on the HS side. Naming, repetition, and language comprehension were assessed during anesthetic effect, verified by contralateral hemiplegia. Atypical language was considered as preserved language abilities during right hemiplegia following left injection.

Surgical treatment

All operated patients underwent the same resection procedure (anteromesial temporal lobectomy with minimal neocortical resection), 22 if video‐EEG monitoring showed exclusive temporal seizure onset (regardless if ipsi‐ or contralaterally to the HS side) and exclusive interictal temporal epileptiform activity regardless of Wada test results. Patients with noncongruent findings on noninvasive video‐EEG monitoring were operated without intracranial implants and recording The same experienced surgeon (W.H.T.) performed all surgeries.

Statistical analysis

Left and right HS groups were analyzed separately. Clinical data and baseline neuropsychological scores were compared among Ipsilateral, Bilateral, and Control groups with the Kruskal–Wallis test and post hoc Dunn‐Bonferroni test if P < 0.05.

Correlation among age, age at onset, epilepsy duration and total ASM load, and baseline IQ and memory scores were evaluated using Spearman correlation and stratified according to surgical groups (L‐Ipsilateral, L‐Bilateral, R‐Ipsilateral, and R‐Bilateral). Preoperative and postoperative scores within the same group were compared using the Wilcoxon test. To normalize data and to control for retest practice effect, we calculated the Reliable Change Index (RCI), using controls as references, for each HS side (left or right). 23 We obtained Z‐scores for every surgical patient on each neuropsychological test. Mean Z‐scores were compared between surgical groups (L‐Ipsilateral versus L‐Bilateral and R‐Ipsilateral versus R‐Bilateral) using the Mann‐Whitney test. Individual performance was defined as improved if Z‐score >1.645 and as declined if Z‐score <−1.645. 24 Data regarding ASM load on the second evaluation were missing in one patient of the L‐ipsilateral group and in two patients of each remaining group. Data regarding immediate nonverbal recall were missing in one patient of L‐Bilateral group. Statistical analysis was performed with IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 24 (IBM Corp.). Statistical significance was established as P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Seventy‐two patients with unilateral HS confirmed with 3‐Tesla MRI were included. Twenty‐one patients who met inclusion criteria declined surgery and were included as controls. Four patients in the L‐surgery group were excluded: two presented right hemisphere language representation, one presented only one seizure (classified as ipsilateral) during video‐EEG monitoring, and one did not undergo postoperative neuropsychological evaluation. Four patients in the R‐surgery group were excluded. Two were lost to follow‐up, one had right hemisphere language representation, and one did not undergo Wada Testing. Final analysis included 64 patients, of whom 43 underwent temporal lobe surgery. Video‐EEG monitoring duration was 5.7 ± 3.1 days. A mean of 4.9 ± 3.7 seizures per patient was recorded.

Patients were classified as L‐Ipsilateral (9), L‐Bilateral (15), L‐Control (9), R‐Ipsilateral (10), R‐Bilateral (9), and R‐Control (12) (Figure 1). Seven patients in the L‐Bilateral group presented ictal onset over the right temporal region or bilaterally, and eight presented ictal onset in left temporal region with ictal evolution to the right temporal region. In the R‐Bilateral group, seven presented left temporal or bilateral seizure onset, and two patients presented ictal onset in the right temporal region with evolution to the left temporal lobe. Two patients in the L‐Ipsilateral and two patients in R‐Ipsilateral were left‐handed. There were no differences between Ipsilateral and Bilateral groups regarding video‐EEG duration or number of recorded seizures (Table 1).

Postoperative seizure control and ASM load

Good postoperative was considered as Engel class I. Approximately 74% of the patients (32/43) remained completely seizure‐free (Engel I) on a 5.3 ± 1.2 years follow‐up. In the L‐Ipsilateral group, eight cases were classified as Engel IA, and one case was classified as Engel IIA. In the L‐Bilateral group, seven cases were classified as Engel IA, two cases as Engel IC, three cases as Engel IIA, one case as Engel IIC, one case as Engel IIIA, and one case as Engel IVB. In the R‐Ipsilateral group, seven cases were classified as Engel IA, one case as Engel IC, and two cases as Engel IIA. In the R‐Bilateral group, seven cases were classified as Engel IA, one case as Engel IIA, and one case as Engel IIIA.

We observed decreased ASM loads after surgery. Mean total ASM loads were decreased in the L‐Ipsilateral (−1.1 ± 1.5; P = 0.028) and in the L‐Bilateral groups (−0.8 ± 1.5; P = 0.045). A mean reduction in sedative ASM load was noted in R‐Bilateral group (−0.5 ± 0.9; P = 0.046). Mean ASM load reductions did not differ between Ipsilateral and Bilateral groups.

DISCUSSION

Superiority of anterior temporal lobectomy over medical treatment for temporal lobe epilepsy is well established. 25 Additionally, surgery positively impacts quality of life and probably decreases mortality rates associated with epilepsy. 26 , 27 Correct identification of the epileptogenic zone is crucial for optimal surgical outcomes. 27 , 28 Invasive electrodes are usually recommended when seizure onset zones are poorly lateralized or bilateral seizure onsets are recorded on scalp EEG monitoring and are frequently needed in nonlesional temporal lobe epilepsy. 27 , 28 When MRI discloses unilateral hippocampal sclerosis, even patients with discordant EEG findings benefit from surgery. 5 , 12 , 28

Several studies have demonstrated postoperative memory decline, particularly verbal memory, after dominant temporal lobe surgery. 3 , 4 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 29 Predicting which patients are at higher risk of memory decline is challenging. Preserved memory function in the hippocampus to be resected is associated with poorer postoperative cognitive outcomes. 30 Although several factors have been associated with worse cognitive outcomes in temporal lobectomy for unilateral mesial temporal sclerosis, 6 , 9 , 29 , 31 , 32 the role of bilateral epileptiform activity on cognitive outcome in mesial temporal sclerosis surgery has not been clearly evaluated. A small retrospective study has shown favorable prognosis for patients with bilateral epileptogenesis in temporal lobe epilepsy due to unilateral hippocampal sclerosis. 33 A large retrospective study evaluating cognitive outcomes 5 years after temporal lobe epilepsy surgery has shown that long‐term memory improvement can be achieved, especially in patients with successful seizure control. 34

When evaluating seizure outcome after epilepsy surgery, ictal EEG is usually classified as concordant or discordant according to ictal onset side. 27 We were interested in studying the functional impact of epileptiform activity on cognition. We, therefore, classified patients as bilateral EEG involvement when they presented at least one seizure with bilateral or contralateral onset or in whom seizures started ipsilateral to HS and later evolved to the opposite temporal lobe. A similar ictal EEG classification has been used for anatomo‐electroclinical phenotypic characterization of bitemporal epilepsy. 35

The same EEG classification strategy was adopted in previous studies that demonstrated that bilateral epileptiform activity negatively impacts verbal and nonverbal memory in unilateral HS patients. 14 , 15 Based on these studies, we hypothesized that patients with bilateral electrographic ictal involvement would present less pronounced postoperative memory decline when compared to patients with ictal involvement restricted to the HS side. This hypothesis is in agreement with the model that proposes that cognition in patients with epilepsy depends on a complex interaction between structural, clinical, and demographic factors, which are largely irreversible, as well as functional factors, such as ASM use and epileptiform activity, that are potentially modifiable. 2

We demonstrated that left HS patients with bilateral ictal involvement present less pronounced verbal memory decline compared to patients with ictal electrographic involvement restricted to the HS side. This finding may seem paradoxical, but this may not be the case. Patients with bilateral ictal EEG had lower baseline verbal memory scores (although not significantly, possibly due to small sample size), which may be associated with lower postoperative verbal memory decline. Alternatively, epileptiform activity has been shown to negatively impact on cognition. 2 , 36 , 37 Ictal electrographic involvement of the contralateral temporal lobe may impair normal functioning of an otherwise intact temporal lobe, affecting memory reserve in the contralateral temporal lobe. Postoperative resolution of ipsilateral and contralateral epileptiform activity surgery could theoretically remove the deleterious impact of epileptiform activity in the contralateral temporal lobe and could result in improved memory function, partially counteracting postoperative memory decline.

Scalp EEG has limitations to correctly lateralize seizure ictal onset zone. Up to 72% of patients with bilateral ictal onset registered on scalp EEG present exclusive unilateral ictal onset when monitored invasively. 28 Truly “bilateral” patients represent a minority of temporal lobe epilepsy patients. Truly “bilateral” seizure onset is associated with poorer postoperative seizure outcome. In this setting, bilateral EEG activity probably represents a marker of contralateral disease not detected by neuroimaging.

Our study has limitations. Although we included 64 patients in our final analysis, we studied left and right HS separately and these groups were further subdivided according to ictal electrographic involvement, resulting in group sizes ranging from nine to 15 patients. Some neuropsychological baseline differences, including verbal memory performance, were observed between L‐Control and L‐ Surgery groups, as well as differences regarding epilepsy duration and interval test‐retest between R‐Control and R‐Surgery. These differences may be explained by the fact that certain clinical, demographic, and neuropsychological features may influence patient's decision to undergo or not a surgical intervention. Cognition after temporal surgery is dynamic and varies between subjects. The small sample size in each patient subgroup hindered multivariate analysis. Future studies should also analyze baseline and postoperative EEG recordings. Nevertheless, we prospectively evaluated a relatively homogeneous group of patients with unilateral temporal sclerosis confirmed with 3‐Tesla MRI. Patients in the surgical groups underwent a standardized anterior temporal lobectomy, performed by the same surgeon. Clinical and demographic features, mean postoperative ASM load changes, and seizure outcomes did not differ among surgical groups, making it unlikely that our findings could be attributed to these confounding factors.

Our study can easily be replicated since noninvasive video‐EEG is mandatory in every epilepsy surgery center. Additional studies including advanced neuroimaging techniques should enhance the ability to predict cognitive change and expand the understanding of the complex structural and functional impacts of epilepsy surgery.

CONCLUSIONS

Detailed analysis of electrographic ictal patterns may help predict cognitive outcome after temporal lobe epilepsy surgery. Right HS patients, regardless of ictal EEG patterns, did not present memory decline. Left HS patients with ictal EEG involvement restricted to the HS side presented significant postoperative verbal memory decline. Left HS patients with bilateral ictal involvement presented less pronounced verbal memory decline compared to patients with exclusive ipsilateral ictal activity. Bilateral epileptiform activity is not associated with increased risk of postoperative memory decline. Surgery should, therefore, not be denied to these patients, and memory outcome should not be regarded as less favorable than in cases with ictal activity restricted to the HS side.
