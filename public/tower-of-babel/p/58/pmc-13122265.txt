Service Evaluation of a “Straight to Scan” Pathway for Neck Lumps Referred as Urgent Suspected Head and Neck Cancer
Authors: Adan Chew, Isaac Costa, Ishita Sen, Aurora Egalla Dewage, Abdelrahman Ibrahim, Christopher Metcalfe, James Philip McMorran, Ajith George
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThe number of referrals to the urgent suspected head and neck cancer pathway is increasing exponentially with the relatively slower increase in cancer incidence. Tertiary services may find it challenging to cope with this increasing demand. We have therefore analyzed data from a novel “straight‐to‐scan” pathway for patients referred under the Urgent Suspected Cancer (USC) pathway in our unit. This study aims to assess the impact on the referral pathway.MethodsData pertaining to all USC referrals for patients with a neck lump to our regional tertiary head and neck cancer unit, between January and December 2021, were collected retrospectively. All patients referred with a neck lump had a neck ultrasound prior to review. Demographic data, referral metrics, and clinical information including final diagnosis were recorded and later used for analysis of the clinical course and patient outcomes.Results1808 USC referrals were screened, of which a total of 563 referrals were for patients referred with a neck lump as the only presenting symptom. The cancer conversion rate was 10% (57 cancers). Two received a diagnosis following a subsequent referral after the initial ultrasound scan was graded as indeterminate. 56% of cancers were primary head and neck cancers, while 33% of cancers were hematological.ConclusionOur data showcases the robustness of ultrasound in diagnosing neck lumps. This highlights the potential for the development of a straight‐to‐scan (STS) pathway which can further streamline the referral process and ensure effective resource allocation and workforce planning.Level of EvidenceN/A.Keywords: head and neck cancer, pathway, service evaluation, ultrasound

Introduction

The number of referrals to the urgent suspected head and neck cancer pathway is increasing exponentially and out of proportion with the relatively slower increase in cancer diagnoses [1, 2]. Coupled with workforce pressures across the National Health Service (NHS), these pathways are under unprecedented pressure and unsustainable strain as a result [1]. Efficient diagnostics, radical restructuring of pathways and efficient triage have therefore been emerging issues in this field [2].

A neck lump remains the strongest symptomatic predictor for head and neck cancer, particularly in the context of the rising incidence of Human Papillomavirus (HPV)‐positive oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinomas (SCC) [3, 4]. As such, this is an essential inclusion criterion for referral on the urgent suspected head and neck cancer pathway form [5]. There are numerous differentials for a perceived palpable lump in the neck ranging from normal anatomical structures to pathological neck nodes leading to a high number of false positive referrals [2, 3, 4].

The ultrasound scan is a frequently utilized mechanism to identify lesions requiring further investigation. One stop clinics have been in use across tertiary units for decades but require on‐site radiology and pathology specialists, which may lead to a significant cost [2]. Furthermore, many cases referred to these clinics often do not require a biopsy, which highlights poor utilization of resources. The use of a pre‐biopsy ultrasound may therefore be useful as a validating investigation for patients referred under strict criteria. This allows identification of patients referred with normal anatomy and downgrading of referrals for patients with radiologically evident benign pathology such as British Thyroid Association (BTA) graded U2 thyroid nodules, skin cysts, lipomas, thyroglossal duct cysts and reactive lymph nodes [6].

Over the past 5 years, our unit has undergone a change in the management of Urgent Suspected Cancer (USC) referrals with the introduction of a novel telescopic pathway [7, 8]. This has efficiently managed low‐risk patients with dysphonia, demonstrating a NPV of 95% with over 2200 patients managed. The current study analyses data for those patients managed via a “straight to scan (STS)” pathway. Of all cancer sites, the patients more likely to return within 2 years with a positive diagnosis following cancer exclusion are those with head and neck pathology [3]. We therefore felt it was important to perform an analysis of the STS pathway with up to a 3 year follow‐up, explaining our 2021 cohort for analysis.

Materials and Methods

This retrospective service evaluation included all patients referred via the Ear, Nose and Throat (ENT) 28‐day Faster Diagnostic Standard (FDS) pathway at a UK tertiary head and neck unit between January 2021 and December 2021.

Pathway and Population Characteristics

Patients were eligible for inclusion in this study if they had been referred under the 28‐day FDS pathway with an unexplained neck lump. The STS pathway offers a patient an ultrasound appointment by a trained radiographer within 48 h of referral triage at the tertiary unit ultrasound suite, eliminating the need for all to have an immediate ENT face‐to‐face appointment.

The following exclusion criteria were applied:

Referrals without the presence of a neck lump as the primary concern such as hoarse voice, dysphagia, feeling of a lump in the throat.

These criteria were designed to isolate a cohort for whom neck ultrasound (USS) was used as the sole initial investigation, with no prior or concurrent imaging modalities, forming the basis for what is hereafter referred to as the Straight‐to‐Scan (STS) pathway. Thyroid nodules were classified as per the UK British Thyroid Association guidelines [6].

Data Analysis

Data analysis was conducted quantitatively using Microsoft Excel and the R software.

Subgroup Analyses of Re‐Referrals

A re‐referral was defined as any patient who was re‐referred to the ENT/Head and Neck service within 12 months of their original referral date.

To minimize inter‐observer bias in this specific group of patients, all ultrasound reports were independently reviewed by two authors (AC and IC) to categorize them into three interpretive groups. In instances of disagreement between the two reviewers, a third independent reviewer (IS) was consulted. The final categorization was determined by majority consensus.

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted as a retrospective service evaluation with prior institutional approval and involved no direct patient contact or deviation from standard care. As such, formal ethical approval was not required in accordance with local institutional guidelines on service evaluations.

Results

1808 referrals through the ENT USC pathway service from January 2021 to December 2021 were evaluated. After removing 25 duplicate referrals, 717 referrals were for patients with neck lumps, of which 563 referrals were patients who presented with a neck lump as the only presenting symptom and had a single neck ultrasound as the initial work‐up investigation as per the inclusion criteria for analysis (Figure 1).

Identification of all referrals.

We also observed that patients who reported only a globus sensation in the throat were also included in this study as on our FDS pathway referral form, the box was ticked as a “neck lump.” Patients who were referred with a globus sensation in the throat were thus excluded from the study, as they concurrently also received a nasendoscopy examination on the Telescopic Pathway [7] alongside an ultrasound scan following triage, so as not to positively bias the positive outcome of a completely normal scan.

Patients had a mean age of 53.5 (range = 16–88, SD = 17.8), comprising 72.1% females and 27.9% males.

98.5% referrals were from General Practitioners (555), while a small minority were from acute portals such as Emergency Medicine (0.5%, 3) or Acute Medicine (0.3%, 2) (Table 1). A small remainder (0.5%, 3) was from allied specialties where patients with pathology were identified either during a course of admission or referrals regarding symptomology of soft tissue in the head and neck region.

The median time from referral to triage was 2 days (range 0–23 days). 91% (511) of the referrals were triaged within 6 days corresponding to the Best Times Head and Neck Pathway set by NHS England [2].

The median time from referral to the initial ultrasound scan was 10 days (range 0–31 days). The median time from triage to initial ultrasound was 7 days (range 1–27 days). 80% of ultrasound scans were completed within 13 days.

Conversion Rates

A total of 57 malignancies were identified, with 55 diagnosed following the initial ultrasound (Figure 2). Two were diagnosed following a re‐referral, with the initial scan being classed as intermediate. Figure 2 summarizes the types of malignancies diagnosed. 32 (56%) primary head and neck cancers were diagnosed, of which 13 were of a thyroid origin, 16 were primary SCCs of the head and neck, and three were parotid malignancies. 19 (33%) of the cancers identified were hematological malignancies. There was a small remainder of three lung malignancies that had metastasized to the neck lymphatics, a malignant melanoma, breast cancer, and a nerve sheath tumor.

Cancer incidence (n = 57).

There were 130 referrals that received a biopsy, and 53 cancers were diagnosed as a result of the biopsy. Out of the remaining, two cases were planned for a biopsy, but the patients received cross‐sectional imaging that revealed a malignancy, giving a total of 55 cancers diagnosed through the pathway.

A true positive was defined as the scan raising suspicion with histological confirmation of cancer or with cross‐sectional imaging stating the presence of malignancy. The sensitivity of this pathway was 96%, the specificity was 85%. The negative predictive value was 99.5%, and the positive predictive value was 42%.

Subsequent Management

Following the initial triage scan, patients were either discharged from the USC pathway or deemed to require further investigations depending on the results of the ultrasound report (Figure 3). If an initial neck ultrasound was normal or benign, patients would be discharged (pathway 1) using a digital patient notification communication service. If there was intermediate suspicion (pathway 2), patients would undergo further imaging, nasendoscopy, or interval surveillance scans for their pathology. This consultation was also done digitally via two‐way messaging. Occasionally patients would request a face‐to‐face discussion. If the degree of suspicion was high for a malignant diagnosis (pathway 3), patients would either proceed straight to a diagnostic or therapeutic surgical operation, cross‐sectional imaging, nasendoscopy, or be referred for discussion for the head and neck multi‐disciplinary team (MDT). All these patients were seen face to face to discuss their management. All decisions and communications were made by a head and neck ENT Consultant.

Pathways following initial triage ultrasound scan.

From our analysis, the majority of patients (67.1%, 378) were reassured and discharged following the initial ultrasound scan appointment.

There were a small proportion of patients (3.5%, 20) who were re‐referred within 1 year.

Subgroup Analyses of the Re‐Referrals

Out of the 20 re‐referrals received within a year, 54.5% (12) of them had no lump identified at the time of their initial ultrasound.

Six of the patients had an ultrasound as a result of the re‐referral, four had a face‐to‐face appointment with endoscopic evaluation, with another one having both an ultrasound and endoscopy evaluation. One proceeded straight to cross‐sectional imaging, and they were later found to have metastatic breast cancer. Nine of the patients were subsequently discharged with no follow‐up. The remaining two patients were confirmed benign and referred onwards for ENT and speech and language therapy input.

When grading the initial ultrasound reports of the re‐referral cohort, initial inter‐observer agreement was observed in 60% of referrals. The majority of reports (85%) were graded as low risk (likely benign). Both reports that led to a malignant diagnosis were graded as intermediate, and the remaining one was a benign thyroid cyst that was placed under surveillance. There were no initial ultrasound reports graded as malignant (3).

The first malignant diagnosis was a metastatic breast cancer which was discussed above in pathway 1. The other case was of small lymphocytic leukemia, which was explored above in pathway 2.

Discussion

This paper summarizes the outcomes of a novel straight‐to‐scan diagnostic pathway at our unit for urgent suspected head and neck cancer referrals. US is a minimally invasive diagnostic test that is widely available and can provide vital information about the nature of a neck lump. With an increasing incidence of head and neck cancers as well as unprecedented pressures placed on our health system, there is a need to develop efficacious diagnostic tools that will benefit and streamline services further. Head and neck cancer “two week wait” referrals (now known as the 28‐day Faster Diagnostic Pathway) have been inappropriately used and poor adherence to referral guidelines [9].

Following the incorporation of telephone triage and the nurse‐led clinic, this initiative hopes to further streamline the pathway for head and neck cancers [7, 8, 10]. Our study included a substantial sample set with suspected head and neck cancer referrals over an entire year, with clinical outcomes followed up for a 3‐year period. The demographics and clinical outcome from the data collected correspond to the data offered in the epidemiological data [11]. The primary aim of this service evaluation project was to explore the use of neck ultrasound as an initial validating tool. The use of electronic patient records throughout the entire referral pathway also meant that the loss to follow‐up rate was very low (< 0.001%). This study reviewed almost complete records of clinical events that occurred as we are the sole tertiary head and neck center in the region.

The demographics of patients in pathway 1 are opposite of what is expected of patients with higher risk of head and neck cancer. With an almost 1:3 male to female ratio and a mean age of 53.5, the lower rates of subsequent follow‐up (33.9%) are expected of the pathway. Following classification of all referrals into three pathways (pathway 1—low USS risk, pathway 2—intermediate USS risk, pathway 3—high USS risk), we observed how the demographics shift slowly into an older, male‐dominated sample as we move from pathway 1 to pathway 3 alongside the increasing conversion rates which correspond to the known epidemiology of head and neck cancers [4]. By classifying the US reports into three pathways, this could be a potential method to risk‐stratify and streamline services into facilitating a faster cancer diagnosis. The two cases of malignancy in pathway 1 also highlight the need to have safe discharge and re‐referral pathways in place.

Text messages were used as the primary form of communication for patients, and for those without, we would send letters. This form of instantaneous communication minimizes logistical delays and encourages better adherence to the NHS Faster Diagnostic Standard (FDS). However, repeated non‐attendances were a key issue, as well as the lack of list capacity being the key limiting factor to meeting the FDS. This could also be further streamlined by examining the location of the neck lump—Siddiqui and Chua propose that posterior neck lumps have a lower probability of malignancies; however, the literature is still lacking in higher‐powered studies [12].

Majority of patients (67.1%) were discharged after a single neck ultrasound, suggesting that the majority of neck lump referrals may not represent malignant pathology requiring further secondary care input. It prompts the question whether primary care can be taking the lead with pathway 1 patients unless clinical condition changes, whereby pathways 2 (intermediate) and 3 (pathological) group could be taken over by the specialist head and neck surgeons as they require further investigation.

Referrals sent as “neck lump” when in fact they indicate a “feeling of lump in the throat” are a challenge. This raises important questions such as the appropriateness of referrals whereby also demonstrating a limitation of these studies as triage was often done remotely where the accuracy of this form would not be validated. These findings align with the current literature on a low conversion rate from primary care referrals [13]. This likely reflects a cautious approach from primary care, as neck lumps have the highest positive predictive values and key symptom linked to head and neck malignancies [9, 10].

Patients with worrying features were immediately picked up and referred to MDT or had other investigations such as a staging CT scan to avoid further delay to a likely malignant diagnosis. This limits our pool of patients to a lower risk population. We decided that this methodology would be effective at picking up cases where malignant potential is lower and more generalizable to the population, as it is known that head and neck referrals have a low cancer conversion rate [3].

The results of this service evaluation, including the low re‐referral rate, demonstrate the high diagnostic value of using ultrasound as a first‐line investigation for patients with neck lumps. We therefore propose the STS pathway, where patients with a neck lump can have direct access to urgent neck ultrasounds prior to consultant triage. This can be facilitated by primary care clinicians who complete the referral form for a suspected head and neck cancer referral.

There is also an extent of inappropriately ordered neck ultrasound scans, which highlights the need for adherence to guidelines [14, 15]. The British Medical Ultrasound Society (BMUS) guide for the judicious use of ultrasounds can also be used to serve as a guide for referral if the pathway were further streamlined [16]. However, we have also observed cases of malignancies where lymph nodes were less than the recommended size for referral. Judicious use of referral guidelines and clinic appointments is therefore a cost‐effective solution as patients are seen by head and neck consultants following their neck ultrasound. Scan results could then be reviewed directly without the need for an additional appointment when the patient is referred into secondary care. Adeel et al. also highlight the efficacy of using ultrasound biopsies which is readily accessible following the widespread usage of neck ultrasonography [14]. This could translate to facilitating increasing training for head and neck surgeons as well as trainees to undertake further training in neck ultrasounds, to streamline diagnostic delays further.

The overall re‐referral rate was low (3.7%) and the two serious diagnoses made at re‐referral were metastatic breast cancer and lymphoma. Both ultrasound reports were graded as “intermediate” by at least two reviewers, which prompts the question of improving the re‐referral pathway further, such as placing patients on surveillance scans given the higher conversion rate, despite the smaller sample size. Although a grading system exists for thyroid lesions specifically, there is less generalizability with other lesions of the head and neck. There is scope for development of an objective ultrasonographic criteria for neck lumps to risk‐stratify the likelihood of malignancy (Figure 4).

Conclusion

Patients presenting with neck lumps through the 28‐day Cancer Diagnostic Pathway should have a neck ultrasound as a first‐line before triage and review by a head and neck consultant. This allows streamlining of the NHS FDS pathway and developing efficient pathways to increase the capacity of the health service as the incidence of head and neck cancer increases. Further work is required to examine effective triage and pathways from ultrasonography of neck lumps and ensuring appropriate referral criteria for the betterment of the system.
