Chromosome-level genome assembly and annotation of the schizothoracine fish Gymnodiptychus pachycheilus
Authors: Kuo Gao, Chengtao Lei, Xinglian Lin, Rukui Zeng, Chuang Zhou, Zhaobin Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGymnodiptychus pachycheilus, a specialized schizothoracinae fish, exhibits remarkable morphological regression, such as near-complete scale loss, making it an important model for understanding the genomic basis of adaptation and phylogeny in this lineage. Here, we assembled a high-quality chromosome-level reference genome of G. pachycheilus by Illumina short reads, PacBio HiFi long reads, and Hi-C technologies. The final genome assembly spans 1.83 Gb, with 95.52% of the sequences anchored onto 25 pseudochromosomes, a contig N50 of 71.36 Mb, and a BUSCO completeness of 98.40%. Repetitive sequences accounted for 47.75% of this genome. We predicted 48,952 protein-coding genes, of which 92.51% were functionally annotated using multiple public databases, together with diverse classes of non-coding RNAs. This genome assembly represents the first high-quality reference for G. pachycheilus and provides a valuable resource for exploring genome evolution, rediploidization processes, and adaptive mechanisms of schizothoracinae fishes on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau.Subject terms: Ichthyology, Data publication and archiving

Background & Summary

The unique and complex environmental conditions of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau have fostered extraordinary adaptive capabilities in organisms, playing a significant role in the evolution of species populations1–5. The schizothoracinae fishes are widely distributed in the water systems of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and its surrounding areas, making them endemic to the plateau6–9. The uplift of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau has led to the morphological diversification of schizothoracinae fishes. Based on different morphological characteristics, they can be classified into three groups: the primitive group, the specialized group, and the highly specialized group10,11. The higher the evolutionary level, the greater the degree of degeneration of body scales, hypopharyngeal teeth, and barbels10,12. These characteristics make schizothoracinae fishs an ideal model for studying the molecular mechanisms of adaptation to extreme environments.

Research shows that all known schizothoracinae fishs are polyploid, predominantly tetraploid, but there are significant differences in chromosome numbers among species, ranging from 66 to more than 4009,13,14. Recent studies have revealed that multiple independent polyploidization events in extant schizothoracinae occurring between 2.34 to 0.88 Myamillion years ago, all likely resulting from autopolyploidization. Following polyploidization, most species underwent rediploidization events to restore heritability15. The diploidization process involves chromosomal rearrangements, gene loss, subgenomic dominance, and differential expression among gene copies, which form the basis for species adaptation to diverse environments16–18. Multiple independent polyploidization events have occurred in schizothoracinae, with tetraploidy prevailing, suggesting rediploidization in their genomes as well. Differential gene expression or functional specialization post-rediploidization likely play a significant role in driving phenotypic diversity. Therefore, comprehensive understanding of schizothoracinae genome can serve as a foundation for future investigations.

Gymnodiptychus pachycheilus, a member of the specialized grade of schizothoracine fishes and an endemic species of China, is primarily distributed in upper reaches of the Yellow River and Yalong River19–21. Compared to other fish of the same specialized grade, such as Ptychobarbus fishs10,11, the scale characteristics have regressed further, with the body being naked and scaleless, except for 2–4 rows of irregular scales on the shoulders and anal scales (Fig. 1). This indicated that G. pachycheilus is undergoing a unique evolutionary stage, making it an excellent model for studying the phylogeny and adaptive evolution of Schizothoracine. Therefore, constructing its high-quality genome is necessary for studying the evolutionary process and environmental adaptation mechanisms of schizothoracine.

Morphological characters of the Gymnodiptychus pachycheilus.

In this study, a high-quality chromosome-level genome assembly of G. pachycheilus was generated by Illumina short reads, PacBio long reads, and Hi-C sequencing technologies. The final assembled genome in a size of 1,825,200,093 bp (1.83 Gb), with 95.52% of the sequences anchored onto 25 pseudo-chromosomes, a GC content of 38.03%, and a contig N50 of 71356585 bp (71.36 Mb). The genome contained 47.75% repeat sequences and 48,952 protein-coding genes, of which 45,284 genes (92.51%) were functionally annotated. This high-quality assembly provides a valuable genomic resource for investigating genome evolution and phylogenetic relationships within Schizothoracine fishes.

Methods

Sample collection and construction of sequencing libraries

The sequencing fish samples for this study were collected from the main stream of the Yalong River in Shiqu County, China. The fish was dissected promptly following anesthesia with MS-222. All animal experiments were conducted under the ethical guidelines of the Animal Care and Use Committee of College of Life Sciences, Sichuan University.

All DNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing procedures were conducted by Berry Genomics Co., Ltd (http://www.berrygenomics.com, Beijing, China), adhering strictly to the manufacturer’s instructions. Muscle tissue served as the source for DNA extraction for short-read sequencing, Hi-C, and HiFi sequencing. Libraries were constructed with insert sizes of 350 bp for Illumina paired-end sequencing, 150 kb for SMRTbell libraries and Hi-C library, depending on the sequencing approach. The sequencing of short-read and Hi-C libraries was executed on the Illumina NovaSeq. 6000 platform, while the PacBio Revio platform was utilized for HiFi library sequencing.

The raw short reads and Hi-C sequencing data were subjected to platform-specific filtering to eliminate low-quality reads, such as those with adapter sequences, paired reads with ≥20% of bases having a quality score below 5, and reads containing more than 3 ambiguous bases (N).

The short-read sequences were processed using fastp v1.0.022 for quality filtering, resulting in 105,804,898,200 bp (105.80 Gb) of clean reads at an average depth of 63.4×, and the Clean Q20 and Clean Q30 values were from 97.12% to 97.71% and 92.00% to 93.22%, respectively (Table 1). In the case of Hi-C sequences, the clean reads totaled to 212,012,884,800 bp (212.01 Gb), with an average sequencing depth of 127.0×, and the Clean Q20 and Clean Q30 values ranged from 97.55% to 98.36% and 92.86% to 94.87%, respectively (Table 1).

The raw reads obtained from PacBio sequencing underwent filtration based on default parameters. Following this, high-accuracy HiFi reads were produced utilizing the ccs software (available at https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/ccs). In total, 121,571,065,805 bp (121.57 Gb) reads were acquired, exhibiting an average read length of 16,247 bp, an N50 read length of 16,035 bp, and an average sequencing depth of 72.9× (Table 1).

For RNA sequencing, total RNA was extracted from muscle, liver, intestine, heart, spleen, kidney, brain, eye, skin, swim bladder, and gonads. The samples were promptly frozen in liquid nitrogen upon collection and then preserved at −80 °C to maintain their integrity. Berry Genomics Co., Ltd (http://www.berrygenomics.com, Beijing, China) conducted all RNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing procedures following the manufacturer’s guidelines. An Illumina paired-end sequencing library with an insert size of 350 bp was constructed and sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq. 6000 platform. A total of 93,693,503,100 bp (93.69 Gb) of RNA-seq sequencing reads were generated for comprehensive genome-wide prediction of protein-coding genes. The CleanQ20 and CleanQ30 ranges for all tissues were 98.38%~99.33% and 93.39%~96.33%, respectively (Table 1).

Genome survey and assembly

Based on short-read data, k-mer analysis (k-mer = 19, p = 4) was conducted using the Jellyfish v2.2.1023 and GenomeScope v2.024 software. The analysis revealed an estimated genome size of 1,668,157,292 bp, with a genome heterozygosity rate of 1.26% and a repetitive sequence proportion of 39.20% (Fig. 2).

K-mer distribution of whole-genome sequencing reads of Gymnodiptychus pachycheilus.

Subsequently, the contigs were assembled using Hifiasm v0.24.025 software based on Hifi sequencing data. Following the initial assembly, purge_dups v1.2.526 and Minimap2 v2.2227 were used to eliminate redundant overlaps, thereby producing a non-redundant overlap assembly. Next, based on short-read sequencing data, NextPolish v1.4.028 software was then used to correct and polish the contigs, ultimately producing a draft genome assembly with a total length of approximately 1,825,123,661 bp.

To obtain a chromosome-level genome, we further utilized Hi-C data. Specifically, the Hi-C data were aligned to the draft genome using BWA v0.7.1729, and the resulting alignment files were sorted with SAMtools v1.2130. Subsequently, chromosome-level genome assembly was performed using ALLHiC v0.9.831, followed by manual adjustment using Juicebox v1.11.0832. Ultimately, 95.92% of the sequences were mapped to 25 pseudo-chromosomes, with lengths ranging from 33,427,543 bp (33.43 Mb) to 102,901,563 bp (102.90 Mb), resulting in a total genome assembly length of 1825200093 bp (1.83 Gb) (Fig. 3). This genome size exceeds those of several other schizothoracine fishes, including Schizothorax macropogon5 (1.42 Gb), Aspiorhynchus laticeps (1.37 Gb)33, and Schizopygopsis younghusbandi(858.5 Mb)34, but is comparable to that of Schizothorax o’connor (1.94 Gb)3 and Oxygymnocypris stewartii (1.89 Gb)35.

Hi-C chromatin interaction heatmap of the Gymnodiptychus pachycheilus.

The quality of the genome assembly was assessed using QUAST v5.3.036 and BUSCO v5.2.237. QUAST analysis of the chromosome-level scaffold assembly showed a GC content of 38.03%, an N50 of 71356585 bp (71.36 Mb), and an N90 of 59,611,401 bp (59.61 Mb) (Table 2). BUSCO evaluation was performed with the actinopterygii_odb10 lineage dataset (3,640 benchmark genes). A total of 3,580 genes (98.40%) were identified as complete, while 17 (0.50%) were fragmented and 43 (1.10%) were missing, indicating that the assembly is of high completeness and quality (Table 2). The whole-genome Hi-C interaction matrix generated with the ALLHiC_plot program31 and Juicebox v1.11.0832 (Fig. 3).

Repetitive sequence annotation

Repetitive sequence annotation was performed using a combined strategy of homology-based alignment and de novo prediction. Tandem repeats were identified de novo with Tandem Repeats Finder v4.0938. A de novo transposable element (TE) library was constructed using LTR_retriever v2.9.039, LTR_harvest v1.5.940, LTR_FINDER v1.0741, RepeatScout v1.0.642, and RepeatModeler v2.0.143. For homology-based prediction, we employed the Dfam database v3.644,45, the RepBase database (v26.10)46, and RepeatMasker v4.1.847,48 to build a comprehensive homology library. The homology-based and de novo TE libraries were then merged into a non-redundant library and used for final annotation with RepeatMasker. Repetitive sequences that could not be clearly classified were further categorized using DeepTE v1.0.149.

The results of repetitive sequence annotation are summarized in Table 3. Tandem repeats predicted by TRF accounted for 295,659,331 bp, representing 16.19% of the genome. De novo prediction identified 874,800,484 bp of repetitive sequences (47.93% of the genome), while homology-based annotation revealed 537,191,415 bp of repetitive sequences (29.43% of the genome). After integrating both datasets into a non-redundant library, the final annotation indicated a total of 871,472,447 bp of repetitive sequences, corresponding to 47.75% of the genome.

Among the classified repeats, long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons represented the predominant category, with 1,022,473 elements spanning 286,442,139 bp, accounting for 15.69% of the genome. DNA transposons constituted the largest proportion of repetitive elements, comprising 24.34% of the genome, with a total of 2,403,782 elements.

Gene prediction and annotation

Gene annotation was performed using a comprehensive strategy that integrated de novo prediction, homology-based prediction, and transcriptome-based prediction. After quality control of RNA-Seq datasets from 11 tissues, HISAT2 v2.2.050 and StringTie v2.2.151,52 were employed to align and assemble the filtered reads against the reference genome. Subsequently, TransDecoder v5.5.053 was used to predict open reading frames (ORFs) from the assembled transcripts.

For homology-based gene prediction, we downloaded protein sequences from six cyprinid fish species: Danio rerio, Sinocyclocheilus anshuiensis, S. maitianheensis, S. rhinocerous, S. macropogon, and S. younghusbandi. Gene structure annotation was conducted using BRAKER3 v3.0.854–60, an automated pipeline that integrates RNA-seq evidence and protein homology to predict gene models. The pipeline requires three inputs: the assembled genome sequence, RNA-seq data, and a homologous protein database. The core process involves first using GeneMark-ETP v4.058 to generate preliminary predictions by combining RNA-seq and protein evidence. Subsequently, AUGUSTUS v3.5.061,62 is trained on high-confidence gene models and then applied to perform genome-wide predictions (also guided by the same RNA-seq and protein evidence to enable homology-based prediction), thereby integrating the two strategies of ab initio and homology-based prediction. Finally, all predicted results were integrated using EVidenceModeler v2.1.063, yielding a total of 48,952 high-confidence protein-coding genes, with a BUSCO completeness of 97.5% based on the actinopterygii_odb10 database.

For functional annotation, EggNOG-mapper v2.1.1260,64–67 was used to annotate protein-coding genes by aligning them to the eggNOG database, which integrates functional information from multiple resources such as COG, KEGG, GO, and Pfam, using an E-value threshold of 1 × 10−5 (containing 43,917 entries). We then employed DIAMOND v2.1.1268 to align the predicted proteins against the UniProt database69 (containing 30,491 entries). In addition, functional domains and motifs were identified using InterProScan v5.59-91.070,71, which incorporates multiple databases, including Pfam, SMART, CDD, SUPERFAMILY, PANTHER, TIGRFAM, PROSITE Patterns, and PROSITE Profiles (containing 44,339 entries). In total, 45,284 protein-coding genes (92.51%) were successfully annotated (Table 4). Chromosomal colinearity and gene distribution plots were generated using Circos v0.69-972 (Fig. 4).

Data Records

The sequencing data used for genome assembly, including Illumina, PacBio Hifi, Hi-C, and RNA-seq, have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information’s (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession numbers SRR3643540477, SRR3649851878, SRR3646311479, and SRR36440547-SRR3644055680. This Whole Genome sequence data has been deposited at DDBJ/ENA/GenBank under the accession JBTJBU00000000081. The results of the genome annotation have been made available in the Figshare database82.

Technical Validation

High-quality DNA and RNA were obtained for downstream sequencing. For DNA intended for short-read and PacBio Revio sequencing, quality and purity were assessed using pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (Bio-Rad, 1703672), which showed a main band size of >23,130 bp, and NanoDrop spectrophotometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, ND2000). Final DNA concentrations were quantified using a Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen, Q33238), yielding 65.1 ng/μL. For RNA, integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) and agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA purity and concentration were determined using NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific, ND2000) and Qubit (Invitrogen, Q33238), revealing RNA integrity numbers (RIN) ≥ 6.7. These rigorous quality control measures ensured the preparation of high-quality nucleic acids for library construction and high-throughput sequencing.

The assembled genome was primarily evaluated using BUSCO37, QUAST36 and Merqury v1.4.183. Merqury analysis showed an overall QV of 39.1, indicating high base-level accuracy. To assess potential contamination, genome sequences were aligned against the NT database. Gene structure annotations were evaluated using BUSCO to determine annotation completeness. The genome assembly exhibited a high degree of completeness and continuity, with a BUSCO-based genome completeness of 98.40%, N50 of 71,356,585 bp, and an annotation completeness of 97.50%. These results confirm that both the genome assembly and gene annotations are of high quality and continuity.
