Four heavyweight guests in the field of public health gathered together. Nobel Laureate Jules A Hoffmann, Academician George Fu Gao, Professor Chris Walzer and Professor Jiahai Lu discussed how to deal with global public health crises, such as the recent coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, as well as the role of international organizations. The concept of “One Health” emerged as the latest approach to disease prevention and control. This concept emphasizes the intertwined health of humans, animals and the environment. The experts shared their thoughts and insights on how humanity can address emerging global public health crises through international cooperation.

What lessons and references can we draw from the ongoing global public health crisis for the development of our public health system?

Jules A Hoffmann: First, in terms of preparedness, we have the best response system for infectious diseases this time. Compared to previous instances, our level of preparedness is the highest, allowing us to respond to the epidemic step-by-step. Let us be reminded that invertebrate eukaryotes (representing some 95% of estimated present day species on earth) appeared on earth approximately 2.5 billion years ago and had to fight against prokaryotes with the help of what we refer to as “innate immunity”; only around 500 million years ago vertebrates appeared on earth (representing only 5% of present day species on earth) and developed adaptive immunity with its hallmark of immune memory, which is the basis for the process of vaccination. We can now observe that many diseases originate from invertebrates. From this historical perspective, we can gain valuable scientific knowledge and gradually understand how in mammals the two forms of immunities (non-species specific innate immunity, conserved in all animals and specific adaptive immunity restricted to vertebrates) interact and concur to fight infections of interest to the medical world. This interaction has been revealed both at the cellular and the molecular levels in recent years and helps to orient our fight against many zoonoses (diseases resulting from transfer of infectious microbes from invertebrates to mammals for instance). Importantly, the adaptive immune response of vertebrates (and namely of mammals which include us humans) carries a specific memory of the infecting organism. Globally, the knowledge is both types of immune responses and of their interactions in humans is of paramount importance in responding to public health crises.

In the context of the current COVID-19 pandemic, our first line of defense is that of innate immunity, which informs our organism that an infection has started. The initial response to this is the production of interferons in the case of viral infections such as that of SARS-CoV-2. When this innate immune response is insufficient (genetic deficiencies, co-morbidities, etc.) to clear the infection, the adaptive immune response comes into place mostly after 5–7 days, namely by producing antibodies specifically targeting the infecting virus and by instructing specific lymphocytes to kill infected host cells. These defense processes require the coordinate interventions of several pathways which have attracted significant attention and research interest in recent years generating a better understanding as to how our bodies respond to this type of infection and more generally to many other types of infections.

As compared to the past, we are now much better prepared in terms of the receptors recognizing the infectious agents and of the intracellular defense pathways which they activate. This enhanced preparedness provides us with targets to act on specific steps within these pathways and also to generate specific antibodies in a shorter time periods that formerly possible (eg mRNA strategies of generating antibodies), thus increasing our ability to develop effective vaccines more rapidly. The speed of vaccine development during the current COVID-19 pandemic has by far surpassed that of the past. Additionally, we have gained insights into immune escape mechanisms, which have proven instrumental in effectively addressing the crisis caused by the current COVID-19 pandemic.

We have also invited Academician George Fu Gao to propose insights based on his experience as the former director of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention regarding the construction of a robust public health system in China and the lessons we can offer to the world in responding to public health emergencies.

George Fu Gao: Firstly, I would like to address Prof. Hoffmann's earlier discussion on the history of acquired and innate immunity in humans. The construction of this system must be founded on science, as Prof. Hoffmann mentioned. Investing in science and basic research enables breakthroughs that can be translated into practical technologies, ultimately improving humanity.

Unlike the severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) outbreak in 2003, this time, when the pandemic emerged, we quickly identified the pathogen owing to the significant role played by scientific and technological advancements. As long as science and technology continue to progress and the public health system advances, we can effectively control major outbreaks such as COVID-19, but this requires the cooperation of the general public.

Secondly, effective public cooperation is crucial. Public cooperation requires understanding and active participation. If any measures are implemented, the public will be willing to comply. For example, the importance of wearing masks exemplifies the vital dialogue between public health authorities and the general population. This aspect of public health and engagement is pivotal.

Finally, a strong administrative decision-making process is essential. Political willingness and support play a significant role in responding to public health emergencies. Considering its national conditions, China has charted its own path and achieved significant progress in controlling large-scale outbreaks in a populous country. It has not been an easy journey and there are numerous lessons worth summarizing.

In conclusion, I would like to add that this pandemic is not limited to one country or region. This requires global cooperation, with people worldwide uniting and working together. Viruses are united in their actions.

These are my initial comments. Thank you.

Host: Thank you for your comment. I'm glad you found the previous response interesting, vivid and well-summarized.

This year, we all know that the World Health Organization (WHO) declared that the COVID-19 pandemic is no longer an international public health emergency, but the virus has not disappeared from our lives. In this new situation, how can our public health system respond better to potential risks? We invited Prof. Jiahai Lu to address this question, particularly regarding our current situation facing the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic once again. What should we do in such circumstances?

Jiahai Lu: This is an important question. Throughout the COVID-19 pandemic, we should be acutely aware that infectious diseases have had a significant impact on human health. Among various events, such as infectious diseases, wars and famines, infectious diseases have been the most influential.

What lessons can be learned during the COVID-19 pandemic? Since the SARS outbreak in 2003, our team has conducted research in this field. We should form a consensus that, with the advancement of human science and technology, we must strive to end infectious diseases such as COVID-19 and prevent their recurrence. Instead of waiting for another pathogen to emerge and spread among humans in another 20 years, we should act to eliminate the recurrence of such diseases.

How can this be achieved? More than 75% of newly emerging infectious diseases, including SARS and Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS), originate from animal transmission. In other words, over 75% of newly emerging infectious diseases are transmitted from animals. How can we control these diseases from becoming pandemics in humans? Currently, the typical pattern involves the disease first emerging in animals and then being identified when humans seek medical treatment. However, by that time, the disease had already infected doctors and had subsequently spread to the entire human population. We should focus on early detection and prevention, moving the critical control point earlier to eliminate transmission from animals to humans. An important strategy in this regard is the “One Health” approach, which has been promoted in recent years.

What does “One Health” mean? It emphasizes the interconnectedness of human, animal and environmental health. Only through interdisciplinary, intersectoral and cross-regional collaboration can we truly achieve the goals of a human health community. Therefore, it is crucial that we reflect on this pandemic and work together to prevent the recurrence of emerging infectious diseases such as COVID-19 in the future. We must join hands and strive collectively to achieve this goal.

Host: Just as Prof. Lu mentioned the concept of One Health, the very notion Prof. Chris Walzer has been promoting for a long time, I would like to ask Prof. Chris Walzer how we should handle the boundary issue between humans, nature and wildlife during the process of rapid urbanization and development.

Chris Walzer: We coexist with animals in the same environment. While we may perceive humans and the world as separate entities, we live within the same environment, as highlighted by the COVID-19 pandemic. Currently, we are well aware of the zoonotic pathogens responsible for a significant proportion of emerging human diseases. When considering future outbreaks, such as SARS, we need to move upstream and incorporate the environments where humans come into contact with wildlife into our preventive measures. With the development of urbanization and our rapid progress, our interactions with animals have increased and viruses encounter novel evolutionary opportunities on these new interfaces. Therefore, we need to focus specifically on the environments in which human–animal interactions occur.

We have made tremendous progress in vaccine development, which is a notable achievement. We have excelled in diagnostic technology, data sharing and genetic sequencing. However, we have not effectively prevented diseases from spillover events, where they jump from animals to humans. Hence, we need to start upstream and foster interdisciplinary, intersectoral and cross-border collaboration. Once a virus begins to spread, it does not stop at any boundary and we must not forget this. Although vaccines against the novel coronavirus can be developed, there are other viruses for which vaccine development is challenging. Therefore, we must bear in mind that relying solely on vaccines is not the ultimate solution.

Host: Prof. Chris Walzer mentioned the importance of One Health in addressing the boundary issues between humans, environment and wildlife. Let’s welcome Prof. Jiahai Lu to further supplement and explain the practical application of One Health in China.

Jiahai Lu: One Health is an international concept; however, its application in China is extensive and has a long history. For instance, the control and prevention of major infectious diseases, such as schistosomiasis and malaria, were effectively managed through collaboration among various regional government departments. This is a practical manifestation of One Health or the concept of the unity of health. The COVID-19 pandemic has taught us how to achieve this process effectively. Instead of devising strategies after the disease emerges, these strategies should be implemented at the earliest stages. For example, as mentioned earlier, 70% of viruses are transmitted from animals to humans, but who are the first to be exposed to these viruses? It is the individuals who come into contact with animals. Currently, our approach is to seek medical treatment after the disease onset. However, can we not turn animal workers into subjects of observation and detect potential outbreaks at an early stage? For instance, our research team is developing a wearable device, such as a smartwatch, that can measure body temperature and connect to the BeiDou navigation system. If we equip animal workers nationwide or globally with such wearable devices, we can promptly identify fever symptoms and determine whether their illnesses are of animal origin. The implementation of advanced technologies and devices requires collaboration between departments and regions.

Furthermore, most diseases, such as COVID-19 and SARS, originate in animals and understanding which pathogens have the potential to become human-transmissible is crucial. However, this data may be known to wildlife or animal departments but is lacking in the public health sector. Establishing a data-sharing system is an excellent means of bridging this gap. Through this process, we transit from a passive state to proactive monitoring, ultimately ending the transmission of infectious diseases from animals to humans.

George Fu Gao: Building upon Prof. Lu's explanation, One Health is an internationally recognized concept. In China, we have followed this concept for over 70 years, successfully controlling and eradicating many diseases, especially major parasitic diseases, as Lu mentioned. There are several translations of One Health in Chinese, including unity of health, collaborative health, one health and global health. Regardless of the translation, it is important to fully understand the profound meaning of Chinese traditional culture's emphasis on “One.” This “One” represents the ultimate unity of humanity, the Earth, animals and the environment, which is of utmost importance. In this context, “One Health” particularly emphasizes the two key terms mentioned by Prof. Chris Walzer. He mentioned that urbanization has provided many opportunities for viruses, pathogens and humans to interact with each other. By offering these opportunities, we have provided platforms for viral evolution and adaptation. Originally, these viruses were confined to wildlife in forests. However, owing to opportunities for interaction and evolution, these viruses have spread relentlessly among humans, leading to outbreaks such as COVID-19, SARS, avian influenza and Ebola. These viruses are typical examples that One Health should focus on.

Host: Thank you both. We have discussed the foundation of the public health system and I now have a question for Prof. Hoffmann. What is the significance of medical advancements in the response to sudden public health emergencies? How can relevant scientific research outcomes such as the development of immunology be used to build a more robust public health system?

Jules A Hoffmann: This is an interesting question, but it is also quite challenging. Several panelists have discussed this issue in various ways. Improving the knowledge of healthcare professionals and the public is essential. In my region, for example, some people refuse to be vaccinated and do not believe that vaccines are an effective response. Therefore, the general public's understanding of these issues is still limited and they do not fully trust what healthcare professionals say. Therefore, I believe that public education is an aspect that needs to be addressed first.

Another aspect is that our healthcare system requires competent staff who can effectively utilize technologies that have already been developed, whether through university research or by companies. This is also a positive aspect. Additionally, we are not only facing infectious diseases, viruses, bacteria, and parasites. Over the last two decades it has become apparent that our immune system is also involved in the recognition of abnormal developments such as cancer and neurodegeneration. These aspects have now become topics of intense research in the biomedical community.

Host: Thank you for your suggestion. Indeed, the immune system is a critical issue. Countries have shown substantial differences in their approaches to addressing public health events and the sudden outbreak of COVID-19 pandemic. How can we enhance a country’s ability to respond to emergencies through international cooperation? What role do international organizations play? We would like to hear the response from Academician George Fu Gao.

George Fu Gao: We have touched upon this question earlier, moving from One Health to “One.” We deeply understand the profound meaning of “One” in China, especially within Taoism, as viruses have no borders. In this situation, it is impossible for a single country to handle a viral pandemic such as COVID-19 on its own. Let me add to the opinion expressed by Prof. Jules A Hoffmann regarding how medical advancements can help us establish a prevention and control system and tackle challenges such as the COVID-19 pandemic. It is crucial to recognize that scientific knowledge is shared globally and it is through scientific progress that we quickly understood the COVID-19 pandemic. Many technologies, such as genetic sequencing and paternity testing that you may be familiar with, have been rapidly developed and shared globally. For instance, with the representative technology of genetic sequencing, viruses can be immediately identified through sequencing. This technology and its related techniques have been used worldwide. How can you conduct sequencing? After sequencing, you immediately perform PCR testing and the sequences of all testing reagents are shared globally.

International organizations play a vital role in coordinating these efforts, transcending individual national governments. Therefore, coordination is necessary. In such circumstances, international organizations will play a crucial role. For example, we need to establish a global website for data sharing where shared data can be accessed. If international organizations can coordinate and facilitate the sharing of testing reagent sequences developed by different countries, it would help avoid errors, such as those experienced by some countries in the early stages of the pandemic. International organizations will undoubtedly play an important role in data sharing and the implementation of prevention and control measures. Each country has taken a different approach to control the COVID-19 pandemic with varying measures. China implemented city lockdown measures in the early stages, while other countries did not take similar actions. The virus was effectively eliminated in China by lockdown in Wuhan. Later, China implemented a dynamic “zero-COVID” policy, followed by proactive easing of restrictions. Each country has implemented different policies, but it is crucial to note that these actions are guided by the overall recommendations of international organizations, with WHO as the representative. Each country devises specific plans based on its unique circumstances.

For example, in China, implementing city lockdown measures was possible due to the relatively strong capacity at the community level. Not all countries can achieve the community level of China. Today, we should sit down together, represented by international organizations such as the WHO and reflect on the experiences and lessons learned during the fight against the COVID-19 pandemic. By doing so, we can improve our future work. All decisions should be based on facts and adhere to the fundamental principles of evidence-based medicine.

Host: Thank you, Academician George Fu Gao. This current situation is a reflection of the shared destiny of humanity and all humans are enduring the test of this virus. I would like to ask Prof. Chris Walzer for his perspective on this issue and how to bridge the gap between nations.

Chris Walzer: Indeed, this question is difficult to answer. This global public health crisis affects practically all countries around the world. Some countries may not have performed equally well in terms of capacity building, training and equitable resource sharing. However, this may be due to lack in resources and capacity leaving their foundations weakened. In such circumstances, we need to invest in capacity bridging and the equitable sharing of data and knowledge. However, while these issues are complex they are also critical as we plan for the next crisis.

Academician George Fu Gao also mentioned the importance of cooperation, both across departments and disciplines. This can be done in the long term. Over the past 20 years, we have seen many interdisciplinary collaborations; however, collaboration across industries and domains is still needed. We also need reforms in the organizational structures of governments, institutions and international organizations. For example, the current organizational structure may not fully empower international organizations to fulfill their responsibilities and play their due roles. Therefore, we need closer collaboration between departments, such as the environmental protection, health and animal sectors, rather than working in isolation. In the future, we need to further strengthen cross-departmental and cross-industry cooperation, as well as the cooperation between North and South, South and South, North and North. As pandemics do not recognize borders, they affect every country in the world. No country can be self-sufficient; therefore, we need multi-country cooperation. As Prof. Hoffmann mentioned earlier, there are many social issues that need to be addressed and the WHO has identified the phenomenon of an “infodemic,” where information during a pandemic seems to become a virus itself. People lack trust in scientific information, which is a significant problem. Education is also crucial and all of these require rapid response when facing a sudden epidemic. In the current pandemic, we have indeed seen that the speed of misinformation spreading, which is not based on facts, is not slower than the actual spread of the virus. Therefore, we need to pay special attention to the phenomenon of an “infodemic” proposed by the WHO.

Host: I do not know if Prof. Jiahai Lu has anything to add regarding this question.

Jiahai Lu: Everyone knows that international cooperation is crucial. Let us provide a simple example. In the 13th and 14th centuries, diseases such as the infectious diseases devastated humanity, but the discovery of antibiotics prevented or significantly reduced the occurrence of bacterial-based infectious diseases. However, the widespread use of antibiotics has led to the major public health problem of antibiotic resistance. This also applies to the changes occurring in the global food structure. In China, before the 20th century, our food structure was primarily based on grains because of food crises. However, most of our essential nutrients come from animals. This process has brought us face-to-face with more significant public health issues, including emerging infectious diseases like the current one, food safety issues related to foodborne diseases and antibiotic resistance. All these problems require international cooperation. In recent years, especially after the COVID-19 pandemic, the WHO, Food and Agriculture Organization, World Organization for Animal Health and United Nations Environment Programme have vigorously promoted the concept of One Health and reached a consensus. It is essential to achieve global security through One Health. Therefore, I hope that through such activities, various sectors, whether government or scientific, can reach a consensus. Ending future pandemics is a key priority.

Host: Sure. Thank you! Prof. Jules A Hoffmann would you like to answer this question as well? There are differences in capabilities among countries in the public health system and each country has a different capacity to deal with public health crises. How can countries address public health crises through international cooperation? What role do international organizations play in this process? Could you please answer this question, Prof. Hoffmann?

Jules A Hoffmann: I agree with the points raised by previous experts. We should not only understand viruses and infections from the perspective of transmission. As mentioned earlier, most people have innate immunity and can effectively respond to infections, while only about 10% of individuals have weaker innate immunity and are more severely affected. Additionally, some immune pathogens can cause a small fraction of the population to develop severe cases or even fatalities. Therefore, immunopathogenic diseases can affect a portion of the population and lead to severe outcomes. There is a lot of data showing this phenomenon, so we need to pay special attention not only to virus transmission but also to vulnerable populations lacking immune resistance. We should focus on how to help these individuals avoid the impact of epidemics or viruses. Therefore, in response to your question, I would say that international organizations can play a role in addressing public health crises by focusing on vulnerable populations and aiding. However, I am not the most qualified person to answer this question, as there are many international organizations and experts dedicated to data exchange and sharing. I believe that others, such as Academician George Fu Gao, may provide better, more accurate and more detailed answers.

Host: Sure, we have one final question: Are we prepared to handle a sudden public health crisis?

Jules A Hoffmann: I believe that we have been prepared. At least from my observations, globally, the people around me are aware of this crisis and its potential dangers and risks. How can we prepare better? First, we already have vaccines and we can rapidly research and produce vaccines. To the best of our knowledge, there are at least five rapid testing methods available to help evaluate new vaccine formulations. In the future, when we encounter other viruses, the speed of vaccine development, testing and production can be increased. Therefore, although it is inevitable that there will be more zoonotic microbes that could spread to the human world, with similar events occurring again, we can respond quickly. Once our speed increases, we can save more lives, develop and produce vaccines rapidly, protect people's health and reduce economic losses. This is particularly important for impoverished countries, as accessibility to these resources is crucial for them. It is important to effectively provide accumulated capabilities to countries with relatively weaker capacities, making these medical resources more accessible to them. As mentioned earlier by experts, we still need to change people's consciousness and strengthen relevant education to promote global cooperation between the North and the South.

We would also like to ask Academician George Fu Gao to answer this question. After going through the past three years and the preceding years, do we have the confidence to deal with even more challenging situations in the future?

George Fu Gao: Regarding this question, I believe that it can be answered with both “yes” and “no.” I agree with Prof. Hoffmann's viewpoint from a scientific perspective; therefore, my answer is yes. Since the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic, the speed of virus discovery, vaccine development and drug discovery has been unprecedented in human history. I believe that if another crisis occurs, we will be even faster than before. In this sense, we are indeed prepared. The virus can come and we know how to fight it. However, when we examine the overall response to the COVID-19 pandemic, there are still many gaps between scientific knowledge and practical implementation. These gaps must be bridged. I mentioned earlier the “Three Steps” for epidemic prevention and control, where the first step is based on science. We are prepared for this aspect. However, can humanity unite and implement universally recognized measures? This is something we need to contemplate.

In the future, epidemic prevention and control will require truth-seeking, science-based practicality and practical administration. The administrative process should be aligned with the national conditions of each country. Since international cooperation was mentioned earlier, I would like to add my perspective. I often talk about the “Four Cs” that are needed to promote international cooperation: cooperation, competition, communication and coordination. Today, we hope that through communication, we can find a path to success. Therefore, I call for more meetings in the future, where we can sit together and think about how, besides the scientific aspect, we can unite all other elements of humanity. This requires cooperation and communication. If these “Four Cs” are achieved, perhaps we can also solve the coordination issues mentioned earlier. Like Prof. Hoffmann, my answer would then be “yes” to all aspects.

I would also like to announce that Prof. Dong Chen, Prof. Hoffmann and I have jointly launched a journal called hLife The three “h” stand for healthier, happier and harmonious life. Host: Thank you for your suggestion. Let us give applause to Academician George Fu Gao for his insightful sharing. Prof. Walzer, how would you answer this question?

Chris Walzer: I do not want to dampen everyone's spirits, but I'm not as optimistic as the two previous speakers. I want to reiterate that science, diagnostics and treatment have been excellent, fantastic and remarkable, respectively. It is crucial for us scientists to step out of our scientific work and engage policy and decision makers because it is not just about science. Host: Thank you for your suggestion. Therefore, it is clear that this is not a simple “yes” or “no” question. Prof. Jiahai Lu, what are your thoughts?

Jiahai Lu: I believe that through communication, we must reach a consensus to end pandemics. Such occurrences should not be allowed to occur again and we must form a consensus to end them. It is not just about preparing for the future, but we must put an end to it. There are other public health issues, such as the potential emergence of new challenges owing to artificial intelligence. However, in terms of epidemic outbreaks, a consensus must be reached to end them.

Although the COVID-19 pandemic has prompted cooperation and accelerated vaccine development, the role of vaccines in ending pandemics is limited. Historically, vaccines have been effective in controlling infectious diseases; however, developing a successful vaccine requires a long time. Therefore, the impact of vaccines on preventing outbreaks is limited. However, an effective vaccine that can prevent severe cases and deaths is crucial. Hence, forming a consensus to end a pandemic is important. This is the first point.

The second point is to shift the focus to the source, to people who have contact with animals, as transmission occurs from animals to humans. If vaccines are developed, especially for individuals in close contact with animals, they can create an immune barrier to prevent pandemics.

The third point is crucial: How can we achieve this shift in focus? Various technologies can be applied. We expect to have more interactions with nature and global exchanges will be more extensive, with 24-h connectivity to different parts of the world. This also brings more characteristics to the spread of the disease. This is why this pandemic has caused a global outbreak due to extensive global interactions. Do you want another pandemic in the future? We must form a consensus and emphasize again that ending pandemics is the only way to effectively prevent such disasters from occurring again.
