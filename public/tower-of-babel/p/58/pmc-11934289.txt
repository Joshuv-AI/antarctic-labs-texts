Radiomics-based machine learning role in differential diagnosis between small renal oncocytoma and clear cells carcinoma on contrast-enhanced CT: A pilot study
Authors: Roberto Francischello, Salvatore Claudio Fanni, Martina Chiellini, Maria Febi, Giorgio Pomara, Claudio Bandini, Lorenzo Faggioni, Riccardo Lencioni, Emanuele Neri, Dania Cioni
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate the potential role of radiomics-based machine learning in differentiating small renal oncocytoma (RO) from clear cells carcinoma (ccRCC) on contrast-enhanced CT (CECT).Material and methodsFifty-two patients with small renal masses who underwent CECT before surgery between January 2016 and December 2020 were retrospectively included in the study. At pathology examination 39 ccRCC and 13 RO were identified. All lesions were manually delineated unenhanced (B), arterial (A) and venous (V) phases. Radiomics features were extracted using three different fixed bin widths (bw) of 25 HU, 10 HU, and 5 HU from each phase (B, A, V), and with different combinations (B+A, B+V, B+A+V, A+V), leading to 21 different datasets. Montecarlo Cross Validation technique was used to quantify the estimator performance. The final model built using the hyperparameter selected with Optuna was trained again on the training set and the final performance evaluation was made on the test set.ResultsThe A+V bw 10 achieved the greater median (IQR) balanced accuracy considering all the models of 0.70 (0.64–0.75), while A bw 10 considering only the monophasic ones. The A bw 10 model achieved a median (IQR) sensitivity of 0.60 (0.40–0.60), specificity of 0.80 (0.73–0.87), AUC-ROC of 0.77 (0.66–0.84), accuracy of 0.75 (0.70–0.80), and a Phi Coefficient of 0.38 (0.20–0.47). None of the nine models with the lowest mean balanced accuracy values implemented features from A.ConclusionThe A bw 10 model was identified as the most efficient mono-phasic model in differentiating small RO from ccRCC.

Introduction

Renal tumors encompass a diverse spectrum of neoplasms with varying clinical behaviors and histopathological characteristics [1]. Although a confident diagnosis is possible in several cases, certain renal masses, especially small lesions, appear indeterminate in imaging, posing management challenges for both radiologists and referring clinicians [2]. Indeed, >30 % of surgically excised small renal masses diagnosed as malignant tumors at contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) are diagnosed as benign at pathological examination [3]. Specifically, distinguishing between small renal oncocytoma (RO), a prevalent benign lesion, and clear cell carcinoma (ccRCC), the most frequent subtype of renal carcinoma, poses a significant difficulty [4]. Although presence of a central scar has been described as pathognomonic for RO, only 25–30 % of these tumors exhibit this characteristic [5].

Furthermore, central scar is an aspecific sign, as approximately 20 % of chromophobe renal cell carcinoma may feature it [6]. Biopsy could be a potential approach for managing small renal masses; however, it comes with inherent invasiveness and risk of complications. Additionally, the diagnostic accuracy of renal mass biopsy may be significantly impacted by intra-tumoral heterogeneity [7]. All these limitations could be overcome by integrating radiomics into the diagnostic process. Radiomics is a medical image analysis method that involves the high-throughput extraction of a vast number of quantitative features that are imperceptible to the naked eye [8], [9].

Given the large number of features extracted from images, such as shape, intensity, and spatial relationships of voxels, radiomic-based approaches usually implement machine learning (ML) algorithms to derive meaningful connections between these features and clinical outcomes [7], [10]. The growing interest in radiomics has propelled its extended applications across diverse medical imaging domains, with a particular focus on oncologic imaging [11], [12], [13]. This pilot study aims to investigate the potential role of radiomics in differentiating small RO from ccRCC on CECT images.

Material and methods

Patients selection and study design

This study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, and it was approved by the local ethics committee. Written informed consent has been obtained from the patients to participate in this study.

All consecutive patients with a single renal mass referred to the Urology Department of Pisa University Hospital and underwent CECT before surgical treatment between January 2016 and December 2020 were considered eligible for this retrospective study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: having been diagnosed at CECT with a T1a renal mass (i.e., tumor confined to the kidney, < 4 cm) within 1 month before surgery, confirmed as ccRCC or RO at histopathological examination [14]. Patients with missing unenhanced phases and either one between arterial or venous phases on CECT examinations, or poor image quality (e.g., motion artifact), were excluded. Finally, 52 patients met the inclusion criteria and were finally included, not exhibiting overlap with other published studies. At histopathology examination 39 ccRCC and 13 RO were identified. Image acquisition and segmentation

All CECT examinations were acquired using as scanners a General Electric Healthcare Revolution EVO 64 s (Milwaukee, USA), a General Electric Healthcare Discovery CT750 HD dual-energy (Milwaukee, USA) - SW Version HD2.0, and a 64-slice General Electric Light Speed scanner (General Electric Co., Boston, USA) with the following acquisition parameters: 120 kVp, pitch 0.98 s, reconstruction slice thickness 0.625–1.25 mm, FOV 360 × 360 mm, 512 × 512 matrix. Automatic exposure control was adopted for tube current modulation.

After unenhanced CT acquisition, iodinated contrast agent was administered at a rate of 3.0–3.5 ml/s via an antecubital vein, and post-contrast phases were acquired, including arterial (30–45 s), and venous (70–90 s) phases. For each patient, two senior radiology residents, under the supervision of a board-certified radiologist with over 10 years of expertise in oncologic imaging, manually outlined a volume of interest (VOI), to overlay the entire lesion on all available phases. The segmentation was performed using the opensource software ITK-SNAP 3D software v3.8.0 [16].

Pre-processing and features extraction

The radiomics features were extracted from all the segmented images available. Images were resampled to a uniform voxel size selected as the median values of all the image’s voxel sizes and equal to 0.73242199 mm, 0.73242199 mm, and 2.5 mm.

Pyradiomics (vrs 3.1.0a2) was used to extract the radiomics features using three different fixed bin widths (bw) of 25 HU, 10 HU, and 5 HU [17].

A total of 108 features belonging to the following features family First Order Statistics, Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix, Gray Level Run Length Matrix, Gray Level Size Zone Matrix, Neighbouring Grey Tone Difference Matrix, and Gray Level Dependence Matrix, were extracted from the VOIs.

Features selection and model construction

An analysis pipeline was developed for each of the acquisition phases, i.e. unenhanced (B), arterial (A) and venous (V). Then, four combined models were developed as follows: B+A, B+V, A+V, and B+A+V. Subsequently, a distinct analysis pipeline was constructed for each of the seven models and for each of the three bw (i.e., bw 5, 10 and 25) employed for feature extraction, thereby yielding a total of 21 different classifiers.

A Montecarlo Cross-Validation (CV) scheme was used to quantify each classifier’s performance and its dependency on the data splitting into the training (60 %, 31/52), and testing (40 %, 21/52) set; the splitting was stratified according to the target label and repeated 300 times. For each splitting a full data processing pipeline was set up, and the test set was used only for the evaluation of the model performance.

First, a feature selection step based on the Featurewiz (version 0.3.1) library was carried out using the training set. The selected features were normalized using the z-score calculated on the training set.

The models were built using the scikit-learn (version 1.2.0) pipeline function by concatenating two steps: a features selection based on L1 regularized logistic regression using the SelectFromModel function and a logistic regression model with elastic net regularization.

Then, the models hyperparameters were selected using a 5 repetition two-fold CV using a grid search algorithm. The average of the balanced accuracy was calculated on the hold-out folds of the five times 2-fold CV for the hyperparameter optimization for each Montecarlo CV sample. The hyperparameters considered were the intensity of the L1 regularization term in the features selection step, the ratio between the L1 and L2 regularization term in the classifier step, and the strength of the regularization terms in the classifier step.

The final models were built using the selected hyperparameters, trained on the whole training set, and tested on the test set.

Finally, the best performing model was also tested on the same dataset after the target label.

The methodological rigorousness of this radiomic pipeline was self-assessed using the methodological radiomics score (METRICS) [18].

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were reported by absolute and relative (%) frequency. Continuous variables were summarized by mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile (IQR) range, as appropriate. Differences in age, gender, treatment, and location between ccRCC and RO were assessed using the chi-square and the t-test for independent samples, as appropriate.

The classification performance of the model has been evaluated using the specificity, sensitivity, accuracy, the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUC-ROC), and the Phi coefficient also called the Matthew Correlation Coefficient (MCC) which is equal to the Pearson correlation coefficient between two binary variables. All the performance metrics were reported as the median and interquartile range (IQR) and the standard deviation of their distribution as estimated using the Monte Carlo CV. The best model was selected by combining two criteria: (A) the lower number of phases (B) the higher mean value of balanced accuracy.

The mean value of balanced accuracy value was computed over the averages of the 5 times 2 folds CV hold-out sets for the hyperparameter optimization.

Then, the best model was compared to all the others through an independent permutation test.

Significance was fixed at 0.05, and all analyses were performed with SPSS v.28, Numpy v. 25.0, Scipy v. 1.10.1, and Matplotlib v. 3.7.1.

Results

The final cohort included 52 patients, with a mean age of 67.19 ± 11.07 years (range 27–84) and prevalent male sex (35, 66 %). The majority of patients were conservatively treated with partial nephrectomy (44, 83 %). No significant differences in age distribution were observed between ccRCC and RO (p = 0.942). The singular clinical variable associated with the diagnosis of ccRCC was the male sex (p = 0.010). Discussion

In this pilot study, 21 radiomics-based models based on contrast-enhanced CT phases were developed to differentiate small masses of RO and ccRCC. The study’s population was found to be comparable to those described in the literature, demonstrating a male predominance of renal tumors (66 %) and mean age of 67.19 years [19].

Overall, the mono-phasic model based on arterial phase using a fixed bw of 10 HU for features extraction achieved the best accuracy and specificity. In addition, models not implementing features extracted from the arterial phases were characterized by lower performance. The value of arterial phase in this setting has already been demonstrated by Ren et al., who found a greater arterial lesion-cortex attenutation ratio in ccRCCs compared to ROs [20]. In the same study, ccRCCs featured a decrease in lesion-cortex attenuation ratio in the venous compared to arterial phase, while ROs an increase [20].

Similarly, Sasaguri et al. found that the lower degree of enhancement reduction in the venous phase increase the probability of diagnosing RO in a logistic regression model [21]. In this study, adding features extracted from the venous phase resulted in a slight improvement. Still, considering models using a fixed bw of 10 HU, the one implementing solely features extracted from the venous phase one was the worst performing. This result may be partly explained by the atypical enhancement that may be observed in small renal masses Indeed, both small ROs and ccRCCs may present as hypervascular in the arterial phase and hypodensity in the venous phase [22]. For this reason, several previously published studies have investigated the potential role of radiomics in differentiating ccRCs and ROs [23], [24], [25].

Li et al. built a nomogram combining radiomics features extracted from tri-phasic contrast-enhanced CT and clinical variables and obtained an AUC of 0.898 in the validation set [24]. However, the adoption of multi-phasic imaging may unnecessarily increase data dimensionality, with a higher risk of overfitting [18]. Moreover, given that a multiphasic approach requires time-consuming segmentation across multiple phases, a single-phase approach is ideal, with the arterial phase being the most suitable for the discrimination task between RO and ccRCC. Indeed, in this study, the single-phase A bw 10 model has been demonstrated able to achieve performance almost superimposable to the A + V bw 10.

A similar mono-phasic approach was adopted by Carlini et al., who developed a machine-learning model based on features extracted from the arterial phase [23]. The model achieved and an AUC of 0.62 ± 0.17 when implementing feature selected only on the training set, which is slightly lower compared to the model developed in this study Interestingly, the authors integrated also features extracted from the tumor’s zone of transition, which have been shown to play a key role in the differentiation of these two tumors.

A similar single-phase approach was taken by Ulhig et al. However, they developed their model by extracting features from the venous phase [25]. This difference may be justified by the multiclass task of the study, which aimed to distinguish ROs not only from ccRCC, but also from other renal tumor subtypes. An extreme gradient boosting algorithm achieved the greatest AUC of 0.72 [25]. The discrepancy in these results might suggest that different tasks correspond to different optimal CECT phases from which to extract features. Due to the heterogeneity of studies in the literature, it may be difficult to draw firm conclusions. For this very reason, in this study, 21 different models were proposed based on the phase, different combinations of phases, and bw used for features extraction.

However, this study is not free from limitations: first the retrospective nature of the study, which may have led to unidentified selection bias. Second, the small sample size and the lack of an external validation set, limiting the generalization of these results. Third, the binary output (ccRCC vs RO) limits the applicability of the model in clinical practice whereas many other differential diagnoses, both benign and malignant, need to be considered when evaluating indeterminate renal masses. Finally, multiple segmentations were not employed, which would have allowed us to study the reproducibility of the features and thus provide additional information on the generalizability of the model.

In conclusion, this pilot study identified the A bw 10 model as the most efficient mono-phasic model in differentiating between small RO and ccRCC and can be used to guide future work. Indeed, a more robust analysis with a larger sample size and an external validation are required to identify radiomics model able to advance the care of patients with indeterminate small renal masses in clinical practice.
