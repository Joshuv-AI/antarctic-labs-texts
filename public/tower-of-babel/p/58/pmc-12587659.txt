Extraction of platelet-rich plasma retaining physiological-concentration erythrocytes and enriched leukocytes enhances its functional capabilities
Authors: Chaolong Lu, Yunhua Luo, Xiao Wei, Lugen Li, Zhengbo Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Conventional platelet-rich plasma (PRP) typically requires erythrocyte removal in clinical practice. However, emerging evidence suggests that erythrocytes and leukocytes play key roles in tissue repair. This study investigated whether PRP retaining physiological concentrations of erythrocytes and enriched leukocytes (PRP-RE&EL) affects its functional capabilities. An automatic hematology analyzer and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay were used to detect differences in component composition and cytokine levels between PRP-RE&EL and conventional PRP. The CCK-8 assay, vascular capillary network formation assay, and Western blot analysis were employed to evaluate their performance differences. The regenerative repair effects of PRP-RE&EL versus conventional PRP on fresh articular osteochondral injuries were compared in vivo. Compared with conventional PRP, PRP-RE&EL contained higher cytokine concentrations. In vitro, it more effectively promoted cell proliferation and facilitated capillary network formation. PRP-RE&EL also significantly influenced the abundance of HIF1-α and VEGF proteins in related cells and the differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells. In vivo, PRP-RE&EL was more effective than conventional PRP in treating fresh articular osteochondral injuries. Thus, PRP-RE&EL significantly increases cytokine concentrations, thereby enhancing its ability to promote the regenerative repair of articular osteochondral injuries and holding potential for clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06406-7.

Introduction

Knee joint articular osteochondral defects are common issues—with full-thickness cartilage involvement seen in over 10% of patients undergoing knee arthroscopy—and they often cause knee pain, swelling, and dysfunction that significantly affect quality of life, while untreated lesions may progress to advanced osteoarthritis due to cartilage’s limited regenerative potential [1]. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), chondrocytes, osteoblasts, synovial fibroblasts, and vascular endothelial cells (VECs) in knee joint tissue are the primary cellular components involved in its regeneration and repair [2–6]. Recent reports indicate that the activation and expression regulation of various intracellular and extracellular cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-10 (IL10), hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF-1α), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)—are key molecular mechanisms for the regenerative reparation of articular osteochondral injuries [6–10].

Conventional platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is an autologous product derived from centrifuged whole blood, containing numerous growth factors that promote tissue repair [11–16]. Over the past decade, with the improvement of Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) therapy, its clinical application has been increasingly favored by scholars, especially in the treatment of osteochondral injuries and chronic wounds [13–16]. Based on its white blood cell content, conventional PRP can be categorized into two types: pure PRP and leukocyte- and platelet-rich plasma (L-PRP). Platelets contain a substantial amount of cytokines, and activated conventional PRP can upregulate the expression of basic fibroblast growth factor (FGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1), insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), and VEGF [12, 15–19]. The primary mechanism by which conventional PRP exerts its effects is through the secretion of large amounts of cytokines by activated platelets, which promotes angiogenesis, accelerates cell recruitment, proliferation, and differentiation [12, 17].

Conventional PRP has demonstrated significant efficacy in treating osteochondral injuries [13–19]. However, current formulations of conventional PRP universally exclude erythrocytes. Leukocytes in blood are the main inflammatory cells and immune functional components of the human body. Research has shown that L-PRP exhibits regenerative and reparative functions similar to those of pure PRP, particularly in chronic wound repair. As is well established, erythrocytes are the most abundant cellular component in blood, and in addition to their critical roles in transporting oxygen and nutrients, they have been shown to regulate endocrine functions, modulate inflammatory immune responses, and maintain homeostasis. Erythrocytes can activate thrombin, which in turn affects tissue cells survival [20–23]. Here, we hypothesize that PRP extracted with retention of physiological concentrations of erythrocytes and enriched leukocytes (PRP-RE&EL) possesses regenerative and reparative functions comparable to those of conventional PRP while enhancing its performance.

Methods and materials

Ethics statement

This project was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shaoguan First People’s Hospital, Southern Medical University (approval No. 2023-003). The procedures used and the care of animals were approved by the institutional Animal Care and Use Committee in Southern Medical University (approval No.B202409-13).

Experimental model and study participant details

A total of 50 healthy volunteer blood donors with blood type O were selected to prepare the required PRP, L-PRP, and PRP-RE&EL. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) All donors met the national standards for physical examination and blood testing in China; (2) aged between 18 and 50 years; (3) body weights between 50 and 72 kg; (4) physically healthy individuals free from infectious diseases, who had not taken any medications that interfere with platelets within one week, and who did not use smokeless tobacco or consume alcoholic beverages; (5) platelet counts ≥ 150 × 109/L.

Preparation of PRP-RE&EL, conventional PRP and their gels (Fig. 1)

Preparation of PRP-RE&EL, conventional PRR and their gels

PRP and L-PRP were prepared using a two-step centrifugation method. For PRP preparation, 8 mL of venous blood was drawn into a tube containing 1 mL of 1.5 mg/mL potassium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid anticoagulant and gently mixed. The tube was then transferred to a sterile EP tube and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 10 min, resulting in three distinct layers from top to bottom: plasma, buffy coat, and erythrocytes. The upper plasma and middle buffy coat were carefully transferred to a new sterile EP tube (discarding the lower erythrocyte layer), and a second centrifugation was performed at 2000 rpm for 5 min. The upper and middle supernatants were discarded, leaving 2.6 mL of bottom liquid and cell pellet in the tube, yielding a total of 2.6 mL PRP.

For L-PRP preparation, the first centrifugation was identical to that for PRP. After the first centrifugation (2000 rpm for 10 min), the upper plasma and middle buffy coat were carefully transferred to a new sterile EP tube, and the lower erythrocyte layer was discarded. A second centrifugation was performed at 3000 rpm for 10 min; the upper serum was discarded, and 2.6 mL of the lower liquid and cell pellet was retained in the tube, yielding a total of 2.6 mL L-PRP.

PRP-RE&EL was extracted using a one-step centrifugation method. The procedure was the same as the first centrifugation for PRP, but the centrifugation speed and time were increased to 3000 rpm for 10 min, resulting in three layers: plasma, buffy coat, and erythrocytes. The upper serum was aspirated from top to bottom until 1.43 mL of liquid was retained, and then erythrocytes were aspirated from bottom to top until 1.16 mL of erythrocytes were retained. The remaining 1.43 mL of supernatant and 1.17 mL of cells (1.16 mL erythrocytes and 0.01 mL buffy coat) were mixed to obtain a total of 2.6 mL of PRP-RE&EL.

In this study, PRP, L-PRP, and PRP-RE&EL required for each experiment were all prepared according to the proportions mentioned above. A fully automated blood cell analyzer was used to measure the platelet concentration in each sample. The final concentrations of 5% CaCl₂ and 500 U of porcine thrombin lyophilized powder (Hunan Yige Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd, Cat# H43020122) were used as activators and added to PRP, L-PRP, and PRP-RE&EL for 10 min to form a gel. The gel was left undisturbed for 60 min, crushed, and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 30 min. The supernatant was collected. These solutions obtained by centrifugation after activation of each PRP were used for subsequent cell culture and experiments. Whole blood (WB) was retained to quantify the platelet, leukocyte, and cytokine concentrations as the control.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

All ELISA kit were purchased from Shanghai Lianmai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Following the instructions provided with the ELISA kit, the concentrations of VEGF (Cat# LM8K2590H), FGF (Cat# LM8K2410H), PDGF (Cat# LM8K268H), TGF-β1 (Cat# LM8K3195H), IGF-1 (Cat# LM8K2131H), TNF-α (Cat# LM8K5696H), IL-1β (Cat# LM8K2124H) and IL-10 (Cat# LM8K1968H) were measured. Absorbance values were determined using an ELISA reader at a wavelength of 450 nm. The 10% calcium gluconate injection (Cat# 20110536) was obtained from the same company, while the porcine thrombin lyophilized powder was sourced from Hunan Yige Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd (Cat# H43020122).

Extraction and passage culture of primary cells related to articular tissue regenerative reparation

Primary cells relevant to articular tissue regenerative repair, including BMSCs, chondrocytes, osteoblasts, fibroblasts, and VECs, were derived from discarded healthy tissues from a patient with knee osteoarthritis undergoing knee joint replacement (the patient had no underlying diseases and had signed informed consent forms). Detailed methods, steps, and verification for cell extraction are provided in the Supplementary Fig. 1. Cells at the fifth passage were used in the present study.

It should be noted that the missing information for the cell lines is their official names and RRIDs. The rationale for using primary cell lines instead of immortalized commercial cell lines stems from the specific requirements of our cellular experiments: to preserve the natural biological characteristics of cells, simulate in vivo physiological/pathological states, and ensure the reliability of clinical translation. Immortalized commercial cell lines, which may harbor genetic/functional abnormalities, could potentially interfere with the experimental conclusions. To make the research results more consistent with the in vivo environment and more relevant to clinical applications, we utilized primary cell lines involved in regenerative repair after knee joint injury.

CCK-8 assay

BMSCs, chondrocytes, osteoblasts, fibroblasts, and VECs in the exponential growth phase were harvested, digested, and counted. The cells were then diluted into a cell suspension with a concentration of 1 × 10⁷ cells/L and inoculated into 96-well plates at a rate of 100 µL/well. After 24 h of incubation in a cell culture incubator, the culture medium was discarded, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, α-MEM culture medium (Cat# 10–009-CV, Shanghai Pufei Biotech Co., Ltd) containing 10% (v/v) of various PRP supernatants and α-MEM culture medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) were added as controls, with 24 wells allocated to each group. The cells were cultured for 1 week. Every other day, six wells from each group were selected, and the absorbance (A) value at 450 nm was measured by a multimode microplate reader (VT Synergy 2, BioTek, Winooski) using the CCK-8 kit (Cat# K1018, Apexbio) after 2 h of incubation. The OD values were recorded on Day 1, 3, 5, and 7.

Vascular capillary network formation assay

VECs were seeded onto Matrigel-coated 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well using EGM-2 medium (Cat# CC-3162, Shanghai Pufei Biotech Co., Ltd). Prior to cell seeding, various PRP-EGM-2 culture media were prepared with a final concentration of 10%, and EGM-2 culture medium supplemented with 10% FBS was used as the blank control. After 72 h of incubation, images of the capillary network structures were captured for quantitative analysis. Capillary network lines were defined as clusters of more than five cells arranged in a linear pattern. Ten fields of view were randomly selected per well for counting. Capillary network formation was imaged using a Nikon Eclipse Ti microscope (Ti-U, Nikon Corporation, JP). Angiogenic activity was quantified by counting the number of capillary network lines.

Western blot

Western blotting was used to detect protein levels in the samples. Briefly, after treating cells with different PRP supernatants for 24 h, cells were lysed using NP-40 lysis buffer to prepare cell lysates. Thirty micrograms of protein were subjected to SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and transferred to a PVDF membrane, which was then blocked in 5% skim milk powder at room temperature for 1 h. Primary antibodies against HIF-1α (Cat# MA1-516, Life Technologies Corporation), VEGF (Cat# ab69479, Abcam), SOX9 (Cat# MA5-17,177, Life Technologies Corporation), Collagen II (Cat# MA5-12,786, Life Technologies Corporation), OCN (Cat# MA1-43,028, Life Technologies Corporation), OPN (Cat# MA5-17,180,Life Technologies Corporation), and β-actin (as an internal reference, Cat# ab8226 Abcam) were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing the membrane, secondary antibodies were added and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Protein expression was detected using the ECL luminescence method. The secondary antibodies used were anti-rabbit-594 (Cat# zf-0416, ZSGBBIO) and anti-mouse-488 (Cat# zf-0412, ZSGBBIO), selected based on the species of the primary antibodies.

Animal experiment

A total of 18 mature male New Zealand white rabbits were randomly divided into 3 groups: control group, PRP group, and PRP-RE&EL group according to the treatment, with 6 rabbits in each group. PRP and PRP-RE&EL gels were prepared as follows: 3 mL of venous blood (containing 0.334 mL of 1.5 mg/mL potassium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid anticoagulant) by collecting from the marginal ear vein of each rabbit respectively, and the gels were then prepared using the method described above. To obtain 0.866 mL of gel, 5% final concentration of CaCl₂ and 200 U of thrombin were added to the processed blood; subsequently, 0.05 mL of the resulting gel was taken and filled into the defect lesion. Simultaneously, an osteochondral injury model was created in the left hind knee joint of each rabbit. The procedure was as follows: Rabbits were anesthetized with intramuscular injection of 15 mL/kg of Zoletil ®50 (Virbac, Nice, France) and 0.2 mg/kg of Meloxicam (Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) and secured in a supine position on the operating table. The left hind knee joint was shaved, disinfected, and draped. A 3 cm longitudinal incision was made on the medial side of the knee joint, adjacent to the patella. The skin, subcutaneous tissue, fascia, and joint capsule were sequentially incised. The patella was retracted laterally, the knee joint was flexed, and the tibial joint surface was exposed. A sterile drill was used to create an osteochondral defect with a diameter of 4 mm and a depth of 4 mm on the tibial plateau. After cleaning the defects with normal saline, the defects in different groups were treated with PRP gel, PRP-RE&EL gel, or blood coagulum (as a control), respectively. The patella was then repositioned, and the incision was closed layer by layer, followed by application of a sterile dressing. Gentamicin was administered intramuscularly for 3 consecutive days postoperatively to prevent wound infection.

At 12 weeks after surgery (designated as the study endpoint), the rabbits were sacrificed, and the lesion sections were harvested and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E, Cat# 1120, Sparkjade Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), Masson’s trichrome (Cat# G1340, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China), and Safranin O-Fast Green (Cat# EZWB08-1,Solarbio, China). MicroCT (Kontich) scanning was performed using a Skyscan 1076 scanner to analyze subchondral bone regeneration in the samples collected after surgery. The samples were fixed for 72 h, and the tibial axis was positioned perpendicular to the scanning plane. Bone regeneration within the lesion was assessed by measuring the ratio of bone volume to tissue volume (BV/TV) and the number of trabeculae connectivity. Staining procedures for tissue blocks after scanning were conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After H&E staining, cells, cartilage matrix, and subchondral bone fibers in osteochondral tissue appeared pink. Semi-quantitative analysis was performed using the Mankin score to assess the regenerative reparation of these osteochondral injuries. After Masson’s trichrome staining, cartilage matrix appeared blue. Semi-quantitative analysis was conducted by observing the percentage of regenerative repair of cartilage matrix on articular surfaces, evaluated microscopically by pathologists. After Safranin O-Fast Green staining, proteoglycans in the hyaline cartilage matrix were stained orange-red, while collagen fibers in the subchondral bone were stained green. Semi-quantitative analysis was performed by observing the percentage of regenerative repair of proteoglycans in the hyaline cartilage matrix on articular surfaces, evaluated by pathologists under a microscope. Two attending pathologists blindly reviewed each stained slide and assigned Mankin scores, as well as evaluated the percentage value of regenerative repair.

Statistical analysis

G*Power (version 3.1.1) was used to a priori calculate the required sample size for each experimental group, with a statistical power of 80% and an α level of 0.05. Animals were randomized into different treatment groups before surgery by generating random numbers for each individual. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 5. Each cell sample was tested in six replicates. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. A two-tailed t-test was used to compare differences between two groups. For comparisons among more than three groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) combined with Dunnett’s post hoc test, repeated-measures ANOVA was applied. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, with * denoting P < 0.05 and ** P < 0.01.

Results

PRP-RE&EL increases platelet, leukocyte, and cytokine concentrations in conventional PRP

The animal experiments described above indicate that PRP-RE&EL exhibits tissue regeneration and repair functions comparable to PRP. Therefore, we further investigated the cellular components and cytokine concentrations associated with the regenerative repair functions of PRP-RE&EL.

The average erythrocyte concentration in the WB group was (5.35 ± 0.74) × 1012/L. Erythrocyte counts were negligible in PRP (0.0013 ± 0.0005) × 1012/L and L-PRP (0.0054 ± 0.0043) × 1012/L but matched physiological levels in PRP-RE&EL (5.40 ± 0.40) × 1012/L (Fig. 2B). These results confirm that PRP and L-PRP contain minimal erythrocytes, whereas PRP-RE&EL retains physiological erythrocyte concentrations(F = 484.4, P < 0.01).

The average leukocyte concentration in WB was (6.64 ± 0.72) × 109/L (Fig. 2C), compared to (0.76 ± 0.28) × 109/L in PRP, (14.79 ± 2.24) × 109/L in L-PRP, and (29.18 ± 1.85) × 109/L in PRP-RE. Leukocyte levels in PRP reflected residual processing contaminants, while L-PRP had ~ twofold higher leukocyte concentrations than those in WB (t = 21.81, P < 0.01), and PRP-RE&EL had ~ fourfold higher levels (t = 17.34, P < 0.01). These findings indicate that PRP-RE&EL—requiring only single centrifugation—yields significantly higher platelet and leukocyte concentrations than conventional double-centrifugation PRP.

The platelet extraction rates for the PRP, L-PRP, and PRP-RE&EL groups were (70.69 ± 4.24)%, (78.03 ± 4.58)%, and (95.52 ± 3.91)%, respectively (Fig. 2D). The leukocyte extraction rates for the three groups were (8.79 ± 1.62)%, (53.1 ± 6.97)%, and (98.06 ± 3.14)%, respectively (Fig. 2E). These data show that PRP-RE&EL exhibits the highest platelet (F = 158.3, P < 0.01) and leukocyte (F = 1246.5, P < 0.01) extraction rates, with a statistically significant difference. Besides, the neutrophil-to-monocyte relative ratios in the WB, PRP, L-PRP, and PRP-RE&EL groups were 4.98 ± 0.68, 29.27 ± 18.20, 6.19 ± 1.08, and 4.92 ± 0.61, respectively (Fig. 2F). Obviously, the neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio was the smallest in the PRP-RE&EL and WB groups, followed by the L-PRP group, and the highest in the PRP group, with a statistically significant difference (F = 43.6, P < 0.01). However, there was no statistically significant difference in this ratio between the WB and PRP-RE&EL groups (t = 0.18, P = 0.987). This indicates that the proportions of neutrophils and monocytes in PRP-RE&EL and WB are basically consistent. However, the proportion of neutrophils is increased while the proportion of monocytes is decreased in L-PRP and PRP, particularly in PRP.

The average concentrations of IL10 (Fig. 3F), IL-1β (Fig. 3G) and TNF-α (Fig. 3H) in the control WB group were 4.37 ± 1.76 pg/mL, 60.17 ± 11.71 pg/mL and 6.33 ± 2.34 ng/mL, respectively. Besides, the concentrations of IL10 (F = 50.8, P < 0.01) increased sequentially.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that PRP-RE&EL retains physiological erythrocyte components, achieves higher platelet and leukocyte concentrations via single centrifugation, and upon activation, produces significantly elevated tissue-regenerative cytokine levels compared to conventional PRP.

PRP-RE&EL enhances conventional PRP activity in promoting proliferation of articular osteochondral regenerative repair-related cells in vitro

Knee joint tissue cells influencing osteochondral injury regeneration and repair include BMSCs, fibroblasts, chondrocytes, osteoblasts, and VECs. To assess the effects of PRP-RE&EL and conventional PRP on these cells’ proliferative activity, FBS served as a control for continuous cell culture over one week. Results showed (Fig. 4) that cells in the control group reached peak OD values on Day 3, followed by a gradual decline, with the WB group exhibiting a similar trend. In PRP or L-PRP groups, Osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and endothelial cells peaked at Day 5. Fibroblasts peaked between Days 3–5. BMSCs in the PRP group peaked between Days 5–7, while L-PRP group BMSCs peaked at Day 7. Notably, all cells in the PRP-RE&EL group reached peak OD values at Day 7. From Day 3 onward, PRP-RE&EL significantly increased OD values in BMSCs and VECs compared to other groups. Chondrocytes and fibroblasts showed comparable OD values in PRP and PRP-RE&EL groups within the first 3 days, but PRP-RE&EL exhibited higher values after Day 5. Discussion

Conventional PRP has demonstrated significant efficacy in promoting osteochondral repair [24, 25], soft tissue regeneration [26, 27], pain relief, and joint function improvement [28]. L-PRP contains higher levels of platelets, leukocytes, FGF, TGF-β1 than pure PRP [29, 30]. Concurrently, studies have shown that conventional PRP effectively stimulates lesion-associated cell proliferation, induces vascular regeneration [31, 32], and promotes wound healing [28, 33, 34]. Notably, platelet function in blood is intricately linked to components such as fibrin, leukocytes, and erythrocytes [20, 35–37]. This study demonstrates that PRP combined with physiological concentrations of erythrocytes and enriched leukocytes exhibits stronger tissue regeneration and repair potential than conventional PRP.

Conventional PRP has been established to facilitate bone and cartilage injury regeneration [38]. L-PRP, which contains leukocytes, also demonstrates positive effects on early-stage bone and cartilage injury repair [29, 30], consistent with our findings for conventional PRP. Importantly, PRP-RE&EL effectively repaired fresh articular osteochondral injuries and restored normal joint physiological structure, suggesting that retaining physiological erythrocyte concentrations may optimize platelet and leukocyte function, aligning with innate tissue regeneration mechanisms.

Tissue regeneration and repair arise from the synergistic interplay of multiple cytokines, including growth factors and inflammatory-immune mediators [39]. The inflammatory- immune response initiates signal transduction for regenerative molecules after tissue damage. As inflammation subsides, tissue cell proliferation and regeneration intensify, followed by tissue remodeling to restore functional demands. This process represents a dynamic, multi-stage progression with overlapping phases [40–42].

Numerous studies [17] have shown that conventional PRP contains elevated concentrations of growth factors TGF-β1, EGF, PDGF, VEGF, FGF compared to whole blood, promoting proliferation of BMSCs, chondrocytes, osteoblasts, fibroblasts, and VECs. L-PRP, while containing PRP-like growth factors, exhibits higher levels of inflammatory mediators IL-1β, TNF-α [29, 30]. Despite these inflammatory components, both L-PRP and PRP promote lesion-associated cell proliferation and migration. PRP is typically recommended for aseptic cartilage injuries, whereas L-PRP is suited for lesions with acute inflammation or infection [40, 43]. Notably, PRP formulations containing erythrocytes have not been previously reported. Besides, the platelet and leukocyte extraction rates of PRP-RE&EL are also significantly higher than those of L-PRP and PRP, which is probably one of the reasons why the cytokine level in PRP-RE&EL is the highest.

This study reveals that PRP-RE&EL, with physiological erythrocyte concentrations, harbors higher levels of growth factors TGF-β, EGF, PDGF, VEGF, FGF than conventional PRP and exerts prolonged effects on cell proliferation. Intriguingly, PRP-RE&EL also exhibits higher IL10, IL-1β and TNF-α levels than L-PRP, potentially attributed to erythrocyte-associated cytokine reservoirs [44], erythrocyte swelling-induced plasma concentration, enhanced cell–cell interactions (erythrocytes, platelets, leukocytes), or cytokine-protective effects of erythrocytes.

By releasing IL10, IL-1β and TNF-α, key early-stage inflammatory mediators, PRP-RE&EL initiate repair signaling more rapidly, aligning with its superior repair efficacy observed in this study. HIF-1α and VEGF are critical for bone and cartilage repair [45, 46]. Conventional PRP upregulates HIF-1α to protect chondrocytes, inhibit apoptosis, and promote extracellular matrix secretion [47, 48], while HIF-1α enhances VEGF activity [49, 50]. Cell–cell communication via molecular signaling pathways ultimately drives tissue repair [15, 51–55]. Our data show that conventional PRP increases HIF-1α in chondrocytes, fibroblasts, and BMSCs, and VEGF in endothelial cells, osteoblasts, and BMSCs. PRP preferentially induces chondrogenic markers SOX9, Collagen II in BMSCs, whereas L-PRP promotes osteogenic markers OPN, OCN. PRP-RE&EL enhances these effects, likely due to its elevated cytokine profile. Emerging evidence links erythrocytes to PI3K/HIF-1α/VEGF pathway regulation via IL-1β/TNF-α, influencing inflammation, redox balance, and cytokine production [56, 57]. Erythrocytes also directly upregulate osteogenic markers OPN and OCN [58].

Angiogenesis is essential for tissue repair [59, 60]. VEGF stimulates endothelial cell proliferation and neovascularization [61], while HIF-1α regulates VEGF transcription at all stages of angiogenesis [62]. Inflammatory factors TNF-α, IL-1β, VEGF directly influence vascular formation [63]. Consistent with prior reports [64, 65], PRP induces angiogenesis, with L-PRP demonstrating enhanced effects [40]. Notably, PRP-RE&EL surpasses conventional PRP in angiogenic promotion, potentially via erythrocyte-intrinsic properties, such as endothelial nitric oxide (NO) synthase-mediated NO synthesis, which regulates vascular dilation and remodeling [66, 67]. Thus, PRP-RE&EL, likely via retaining physiological erythrocyte concentrations and enriched leukocytes, enhances HIF-1α and VEGF abundance in target cells, promotes cell proliferation and lineage-specific differentiation, facilitates capillary network formation, and accelerates osteochondral repair. Besides, it is reported that the ratio of neutrophils to monocytes has a significant impact on tissue regeneration [68]. Our data show that the leukocyte extraction rate of PRP-RE&EL is significantly higher than that of PRP and L-PRP. Leukocytes, like platelets, are enriched in PRP-RE&EL; furthermore, the relative ratio of neutrophils to monocytes in PRP-RE&EL is consistent with that in normal peripheral blood. This is in contrast to PRP and L-PRP, in which the proportion of monocytes is reduced—which may be one of the reasons why the PRP-RE&EL group shows a better osteochondral regeneration effect than the other groups.

A caveat of this study is that the investigation was exclusively focused on the experimental application to acute osteochondral injuries, with the generalizability of the observed effects to other diseases remaining undefined. Besides, the differential regenerative mechanisms between PRP-RE&EL and conventional PRP remain to be further elucidated. Another limitation is that the observation period of the animal experiments was only 12 weeks, and it remains uncertain whether the repair effects of the two will tend to be consistent over time. It should be noted finally that the cells in the study were derived from a single patient, and thus the results may have biases.

In summary, human blood possesses the intrinsic potential to regulate tissue growth, development, regeneration, and repair. Platelets are rich in essential factors driving cell growth, while other blood components—including erythrocytes and leukocytes—play indispensable roles in modulating these processes. Conventional PRP, abundant in cell growth factors, facilitates articular osteochondral injury regeneration and repair. Notably, PRP retaining physiological concentrations of erythrocytes and enriched leukocytes enhances cytokines concentrations in conventional PRP, augments its regenerative performance, and demonstrates substantial potential for clinical application in osteochondral repair.
