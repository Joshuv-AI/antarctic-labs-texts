Effect of moisture content on N₂ injection displacement efficiency of methane in bituminous coal
Authors: Kaijun Miao, Lucheng Guo, Huan Wang, Ye Tian, Songzhao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydraulic fracturing is a commonly used technique to improve coalbed methane extraction permeability, but it significantly alters coal seam water content, thereby affecting subsequent gas injection displacement efficiency. To clarify the regulatory mechanism of water content on nitrogen (N₂) injection displacement, this study used bituminous coal from Xutuan Coal Mine, Anhui Province, as samples. Coal samples with three water content states (dry, moist, and saturated) were prepared, N₂ displacement of CH₄ experiments were conducted, and the dynamic responses of tail gas composition, flow rate, and adsorption pressure were monitored. The results show that with increasing water content, the gas injection displacement rate exhibits a V-shaped characteristic of “first decreasing and then slightly increasing”, the percolation capacity generally weakens, and the CH₄ adsorption equilibrium pressure rises systematically—confirming that moisture impairs the adsorption capacity of coal samples. Comprehensive analysis reveals that free water inhibits gas migration by blocking percolation channels in the early stage of displacement, while adsorbed water accelerates CH₄ desorption by competing for adsorption sites after the middle stage. This study clarifies the dynamic mechanism of moisture affecting coal seam gas injection displacement and provides important theoretical support for optimizing gas injection stimulation strategies in water-bearing coal seams.

Introduction

China ranks among the top three countries globally in terms of coalbed methane (CBM) reserves1–3. As the main component of CBM, methane emissions directly exacerbate global warming. Therefore, improving CBM recovery efficiency through effective technical means to achieve the coordinated development of coal resource exploitation and ecological environmental protection is of great practical significance4,5.

Gas drainage is the core method for CBM utilization and gas accident prevention6. However, coal seams in China generally exhibit low permeability7–9, which significantly impacts drainage efficiency. Enhancing coal seam permeability via artificial fracturing and extracting gas through gas injection displacement has been proven to be an efficient and feasible approach10,11. Nevertheless, in high-water-content coal seams or those treated with technologies such as hydraulic slotting12–14, the presence of substantial moisture or changes in water content can significantly affect gas drainage performance15–17. Thus, clarifying the role and mechanism of moisture in the gas injection displacement process of such coal seams has become a crucial issue that urgently needs to be addressed.

Previous studies have shown that moisture exerts its effects by influencing two fundamental properties of coal: adsorption characteristics and seepage behavior18,19. Scholars have confirmed the existence of a competitive adsorption mechanism between water molecules, carbon dioxide, and methane on coal surfaces20–23. Under low-pressure conditions, water molecules occupy more adsorption sites, inhibiting the adsorption and transport of carbon dioxide and methane while reducing the surface stability of methane. Adsorption experiments on coal samples with different water contents have revealed a direct correlation between methane adsorption capacity and water content24–26. Specifically, the impact of moisture adsorption on methane adsorption depends on the adsorbed water volume and the proportion of hydrophilic pores27. Meanwhile, moisture alters the pore structure28–30 and seepage characteristics of coal samples31–35. Studies have verified that coal permeability decreases with increasing water content36. Some scholars have explored the influence of water content on methane displacement by injected carbon dioxide, finding that the occurrence of moisture in coal is closely related to oxygen-containing functional groups and mesopores37–40. Yang et al.41. conducted competitive adsorption experiments of CH₄ and N₂ on coal samples with low and high-water contents, revealing that in the low water content stage, the adsorption potential of moisture exceeds that of CH₄ and N₂, leading to competitive adsorption; at high water contents, moisture condensation and pore blockage become the dominant factors.

However, a relatively overlooked yet engineering-critical scientific question remains: during the entire dynamic N₂ displacement process, how does water content, as a continuous variable, systematically and nonlinearly regulate displacement efficiency? Although existing studies have involved water-bearing coal samples, most focus on comparing the endpoint states of “dry” and “wet” or only examine the impact of moisture on a single static property (e.g., initial adsorption capacity). Based on this, this study takes bituminous coal from Xutuan Coal Mine in Anhui Province as the research object, conducts methane displacement experiments using nitrogen as the injection gas, and analyzes changes in tail gas composition, adsorption characteristics, and seepage capacity of coal samples with different water contents. The aim is to clarify the parameter evolution law during the displacement process, dialectically reveal the dual roles of moisture in this process, and provide theoretical support for optimizing gas injection displacement schemes in water-bearing coal seams. It is important to note that the experimental design of this study adopts the sequence of “first saturating with water and then adsorbing methane”, which is intended to control variables and reveal the intrinsic influence mechanism of the presence of moisture on the gas injection and displacement process, belonging to mechanism-exploratory research. Despite the difference from the timeline of “first gas-bearing and then water intrusion” in practical engineering, the influence mechanism of moisture on adsorption and seepage behaviors observed in the experiment can still provide important references for understanding gas-water interaction.

Experimental setup and procedure

The gas injection tester, illustrated in Fig. 1, was utilized for conducting displacement experiments. Bituminous coal samples with varying moisture content were saturated with CH4(Huang M et al.,2020). N2 served as the displacing agent during the experiments, facilitating the collection of the resulting tail gas. Gas chromatography was employed to analyze the composition of the collected tail gas. Key subsystems of the tester include the loading system, autoclave system, temperature control system, evacuation system, acquisition and control system, strain testing system, software system, safety system, and supporting auxiliary systems.

Preparatory Phase.

(1) Multi-directional fixed-point photographs of coal samples were taken to document surface integrity and crack development. The mass, radius, and height of the coal samples were then measured. Coal samples with different moisture contents were prepared by the procedure described previously.

(2) Parameter Settings.

The experimental design strives to simulate key field conditions while also simplifying complex geological factors to highlight the role of the core variable (moisture).

Axial and Confining Pressure (5 MPa): Coal samples were collected from the − 670 m level of the 3₂ coal seam in Xutuan Coal Mine. Although the original in-situ stress at this depth is relatively high (approximately 16–18 MPa), coal masses typically enter stress relief zones or mining-affected zones during coal mining and gas extraction, leading to a significant reduction in effective stress. The confining pressure of 5 MPa was set to simulate the typical effective stress state of coal after mining. Experimental steps

(1) Pre-Adsorption of CH4 in coal samples: Placed the prepared coal samples with different moisture content in the designated location within the loaded coal body gas injection drive gas tester (see Fig. 1). Applied a pressure of 5 MPa to the loading shaft, subjecting it to vacuum treatment. Opened valve 3 and introduced CH4 gas at 3 MPa into the coal bodies. Monitored downstream flowmeter changes for a specific duration, closing valve 2 when required. Sealed both upstream and downstream valves (valve 2 and valve 3), creating a closed environment for CH4 saturation within the coal body.

(2) Recorded adsorption pressure changes: Monitored the pressure decrease within the kettle after sufficient CH4 adsorption in the coal samples. The computer recorded pressure changes throughout this process. The criterion for judging that the adsorption process has reached equilibrium is: at a constant temperature, the variation range of the system pressure is less than 0.02 MPa within 2 consecutive hours. The pressure value recorded at this time is the adsorption equilibrium pressure of the coal sample under the current conditions.

(3) N2 injection to displace CH4: After CH4 saturation of the coal samples, adjust the gas source to 4 MPa of N2. Opened valve 3 to introduce N2 gas into the coal body and opened the downstream valve 2 to collect the resulting tail gas.

(4) Tail gas collection: Tail gas was collected according to a prescribed schedule. Within the first hour, it was collected every 5 min; between 1 and 2 h, it was collected every 10 min; between 2 and 3 h, it was collected every 30 min; and between 3 and 5 h, it was collected every 1 h.

(5) Flow data collection: Simultaneously, flow rate change data were collected from the flow meter to the computer during tail gas collection. Upon completion of the process, air and shaft enclosure pressures were released, coal samples were removed, and the data were exported.

(6) Analysis of tail gas components: Passed the collected exhaust gas from the gas sample bag through the gas chromatograph to determine N2 and CH4 percentages. Analyzed and concluded the experiment.

Note

Before collecting the tail gas at any displacement time point, a flushing procedure is performed on the gas sampling bags using high-purity helium (He, purity > 99.999%). The specific steps are as follows: fill the bag with helium to approximately 80% of its volume, allow it to stand for a short period, then evacuate the sampling bag to vacuum, and repeat this “filling-evacuating” process at least three times.

Before analyzing the tail gas samples, a gas sampling bag filled with high-purity CH₄ is first used as a “reference sample”. This pure CH₄ sample is analyzed to record the signal value detected near the expected peak position of N₂, which corresponds to the interference value from residual air and the system. Since pure CH₄ theoretically contains no N₂, any signal detected at this position is regarded as interference. When calculating the true concentrations of N₂ and CH₄ in each tail gas sample, the aforementioned interference value is first subtracted from the apparent N₂ signal obtained from the original chromatographic analysis. The corrected signal values are then substituted into the calibration curve to calculate the corrected volume percentages of N₂ and CH₄. All composition data presented in the report have been calibrated through this procedure. For each moisture content group (W1, W2, W3), the curves and data points shown in the figures are the arithmetic mean of the experimental results from all parallel samples (S1-S4) within that group.

Results and discussion

Variation of gas injection and displacement tail gas component patterns in bituminous coal with varied moisture content

The test program involves subjecting coal sample groups with varying moisture content to N2 injection for CH4 displacement experiments. This process collected data on the alterations in tail gas components among different coal sample groups during gas injection and displacement, as depicted in Fig. 2.

Fig. 2Variation curve of gas injection and displacement tail gas composition of coal samples with different moisture content.

Variation curve of gas injection and displacement tail gas composition of coal samples with different moisture content.

As shown in Fig. 2, in the experiment of coal samples with different moisture content, the content of CH4 in the exhaust gas continues to decrease with the extension of time, while the content of N2 continues to increase with the passage of time. After a certain degree, the ratio of the two gases remains basically unchanged.

When observing the time point at which the percentages of N2 and CH4 gases are equal, we find that the W1 group reaches the intersection in approximately 22 min, the W2 group in approximately 27 min, and the W3 group in approximately 20 min. Considering the time nodes when the percentages of the two gases remain relatively stable, it takes approximately 57 min for Group W1 to reach this state, 81 min for Group W2, and 50 min for Group W3. Regardless of other factors, the overall performance of the curve indicates that with the increase in the relative moisture content of bituminous coal, the gas injection displacement rate exhibits a “V-shaped” trend of first decreasing and then increasing.

The above phenomenon is closely related to the adsorption characteristics and seepage characteristics of coal samples with different moisture content. Firstly, as the moisture content of bituminous coal rises, water molecules progressively occupy the cracks, thereby diminishing the seepage capacity of the coal samples and subsequently leading to a decrease in the gas injection displacement rate. Secondly, an increase in moisture content can reduce the CH4 adsorption of coal samples, thereby accelerating the gas injection displacement speed. Finally, the “V” shaped trend occurs because with the increase of moisture content, the displacement promotion effect due to the decrease of adsorption capacity gradually increases, and the displacement hindering effect due to the decrease.

Ease of seepage capacity gradually decreases, however, in the bituminous coal moisture content of W2, the displacement hindering effect dominates over the displacement facilitating effect. The promoting effect is dominant at a bituminous coal moisture content of W2. The subsequent sections will further discuss the changes in seepage capacity, adsorption capacity, and displacement effect of coal samples with different moisture content concerning the flow rate, adsorption capacity, and displacement velocity.

Variation of seepage characteristics of bituminous coals with different moisture content

Gas injection displacement experiments were conducted using coal samples with different moisture content, and data on gas flow rate changes were collected, as shown in Fig. 3. From Fig. 3, it can be seen that the flow rate variation trend of various moisture content coal sample groups is roughly consistent. Firstly, from the perspective of total flow rate, all three sets of coal samples with moisture content have gone through the following processes: The flow rate decreases and then increases, which is Phase Ⅰ; The continuous decline in flow is Phase Ⅱ. In Phase Ⅰ, the overall N2 flow rate shows an upward trend, while the CH4 flow rate shows a downward trend. In the later stage of Phase Ⅰ, the trend of N2 flow rate is basically the same as the trend of total flow rate, and the numerical value is also basically the same. In Phase Ⅱ, the total flow rate and N2 partial flow rate still maintain a basically stable trend in terms of evolution and numerical values, while the CH4 partial flow rate remains unchanged at a lower level.

Fig. 3Variation curve of gas injection and displacement flow rate of coal samples with different moisture content.

Variation curve of gas injection and displacement flow rate of coal samples with different moisture content.

The flow rate variation curve (Fig. 3) reveals the complexity of water’s impact on seepage behavior. This effect cannot be merely characterized as “channel blockage”; its essence lies in water’s dynamic regulation of the effective seepage path and the gas migration mechanism.

During the flow rate decline phase in the initial stage of displacement, the observed flow rate reduction is mainly attributed to the preferential production of free CH₄ in coal fractures, while the injected N₂ has not yet achieved effective breakthrough at this stage. Since N₂ fails to displace and desorb CH₄ in a timely manner, the partial pressure of CH₄ in fractures continues to decrease, resulting in a rapid attenuation of the CH₄-dominated outflow rate. Notably, the rate of flow rate decline exhibits a distinct non-monotonicity with respect to moisture content: the decline is the slowest in coal samples with moderate moisture content (W2), while the decline rate of nearly saturated coal samples (W3) is close to that of dry coal samples (W1), forming a unique “V-shaped” initial decline pattern.

This indicates that the effect of moisture on seepage strongly depends on its occurrence state and saturation. In the W2 state, moisture mainly exists in the form of discrete droplets, discontinuous water films, or adsorbed water films on pore-throat walls or in smaller pores. This state greatly enhances the Jamin effect and flow path tortuosity for gas migration, and traps a large number of medium-to-small pore throats through capillary force, thereby imposing the strongest physical retardation on gas-phase seepage, which is manifested as the slowest initial flow rate decline.

In contrast, in the nearly saturated W3 state, although the total water content is higher, moisture forms a continuous aqueous phase that occupies most of the pore space. At this point, the gas-water two-phase system is closer to a water drainage process. Gas tends to migrate along paths with the least resistance, preferentially displacing or bypassing the continuous water body in macropores and microfractures to form preferential seepage channels. Therefore, under high water content conditions, gas can instead establish an effective seepage network relatively quickly, resulting in an initial flow rate decline rate close to that of dry coal samples and significantly faster than that of W2 coal samples dominated by discrete water retardation. As displacement proceeds, the continuous aqueous phase is partially displaced or redistributed, and the effect of moisture on seepage gradually transitions from being dominated by intense “physical plugging” to being dominated by relatively stable “wettability control”.

Coupled with the fact that the nitrogen breakthrough time of coal sample W3 is shorter than that of coal sample W2, this further confirms that the effect of moisture content on N₂ displacement of CH₄ is not a simple linear inhibition or promotion, but rather a complex non-monotonic influence.

Variation of adsorption characteristics of bituminous coal with different moisture content

As can be seen from Fig. 4, coal samples with different moisture content have different pressures to reach adsorption equilibrium after a long enough adsorption time under the same axial pressure, confining pressure, and pore pressure.

Fig. 4Changes in adsorption equilibrium pressure and adsorption capacity of coal samples with different moisture content.

Changes in adsorption equilibrium pressure and adsorption capacity of coal samples with different moisture content.

The “competitive” effect of moisture on CH₄ adsorption is directly reflected in the systematic variation of adsorption equilibrium pressure, as shown in Fig. 4. Experimental data were correlated with the classical Langmuir monolayer adsorption model.(2)\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V={V_L} \cdot \frac{{b \cdot P}}{{1+b \cdot P}}$$\end{document}

Where V represents the adsorption capacity, P denotes the equilibrium pressure, VL indicates the maximum adsorption capacity (reflecting the total number of available adsorption sites), and b signifies the adsorption constant (related to the affinity between adsorbent and adsorbate as well as the adsorption heat). In this experiment, under identical initial gas pressure (3 MPa) and stress conditions, W1 exhibited the lowest equilibrium pressure (2.48 MPa) after reaching adsorption equilibrium, indicating the highest CH₄ adsorption capacity. Conversely, W3 displayed the highest equilibrium pressure (2.63 MPa) with the lowest adsorption capacity. Inverse analysis using the Langmuir model reveals the underlying mechanism of moisture influence:

1.Moisture primarily reduces the maximum adsorption capacity (VL): The surface of the coal matrix, especially the sites bearing oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., -COOH, -OH), are high-energy active sites for CH₄ adsorption. Water molecules (H₂O) exhibit strong polarity and the ability to form hydrogen bonds, and their affinity for these high-energy sites is far greater than that of non-polar CH₄ molecules. With the increase in moisture content, water molecules preferentially and irreversibly occupy these high-energy adsorption sites. From the perspective of the Langmuir model, as shown in Fig. 5, this is equivalent to effectively reducing the total number of available adsorption sites for CH₄ molecules in the coal, i.e., a decrease in the VL value. Therefore, under the same gas phase pressure, the total amount of CH₄ adsorbed by moist coal samples (V) is inevitably lower than that of dry coal samples.

Fig. 5Adsorption of water vapor molecules in coal pores12.

Adsorption of water vapor molecules in coal pores12.

2.Moisture may alter the adsorption constant (b): The aggregation and film formation of water molecules on the coal surface may change the surface energy distribution and chemical environment of the coal matrix. This modification effect may weaken the van der Waals forces between the coal surface and CH₄ molecules, thereby reducing the adsorption enthalpy. In the Langmuir model, this is reflected in a decrease in the adsorption constant b. A reduction in the b value indicates that under the same pressure, the “ease of gas molecule adsorption” decreases, and the adsorption isotherm becomes flatter.

Thus, the increase in adsorption equilibrium pressure (P) is direct macroscopic evidence that moisture essentially weakens the CH₄ adsorption capacity of coal through two pathways: depriving adsorption sites (reducing VL) and potentially weakening adsorption forces (reducing b).

Analysis of gas injection displacement effect

The evaluation of gas injection displacement effect, including CH4 cumulative production, CH4 production rate, injection displacement ratio and other indicators, is of great significance to the investment time, cost, return on investment and income speed of gas injection displacement projects, the engineering significance of these indicators will be discussed below.

Fig. 6Performance indicators of gas injection displacement for coal samples with different moisture content.

Performance indicators of gas injection displacement for coal samples with different moisture content.

As illustrated in Fig. 6, a continuous rise in moisture content leads to a decrease in the cumulative production of CH₄ in the coal sample. In the case of bituminous coal, the cumulative production amounts to 523.35 ml for the dry coal sample, 385.31 ml for the water-containing coal sample, and 311.32 ml for the saturated coal sample. An elevation in the moisture content of coal samples impedes the gas injection displacement effect. Although an increase in moisture content reduces the initial CH₄ adsorption capacity of coal samples, which promotes the gas injection displacement effect to a certain extent, on the whole, the increase in moisture content exerts a negative influence on the gas injection displacement effect. Consequently, from the perspective of CH₄ cumulative production, the gas injection displacement effect of dry coal samples is relatively more favorable compared to that of water-containing and saturated coal samples.

After analyzing the cumulative production of CH4 in the evaluation of gas injection displacement effect, the gas injection displacement effect of coal samples with different moisture content can be compared from the perspective of CH4 production rate. In the above section, the adsorption capacity of coal samples with different moisture contents were summarized. The production rate of coal samples is obtained by dividing the cumulative amount of CH4 mentioned in the previous section by the adsorption capacity of CH4. Due to the time limit of gas injection displacement in the experiment, the CH4 production rate may decrease compared to long-term displacement. However, considering that the gas injection displacement curve is approaching a plateau, injecting gas again will indeed improve the production rate, but its cost is relatively high. Thus, the CH4 production rate here still has significance for gas extraction.

As depicted in Fig. 6, it is evident that with the augmentation of moisture content, the output rate of CH₄ exhibits a continuous decline. In bituminous coal, the CH₄ production rate of dry coal samples is 59%, that of water-containing coal samples is 45%, and that of saturated coal samples is 38%. The increase in moisture content impedes gas migration. Consequently, the CH₄ production rate decreases progressively with the rise in moisture content. From this perspective, the displacement effect of dry coal samples is still superior to those of water-containing and saturated coal samples.

The previous two sections analyzed the cumulative output of CH4 and the CH4 output rate from the perspectives of quantity and percentage. The quantity can provide a clear understanding of the amount of CH4 obtained, while the percentage reflects the residual CH4 in coal samples, which is of great significance for coal mine safety production. This section analyzes the gas injection displacement effect from the perspective of injection ratio and economic input and output. The injection ratio refers to the amount of N2 required to displace a unit of CH4. The injection of coal samples with different moisture contents is shown in Fig. 6.

As the moisture content increases, the injection ratio of coal samples before and after gas displacement continues to rise. The injection ratio of dry bituminous coal samples is 1.54, that of moist coal samples is 1.63, and that of saturated coal samples reaches 1.72. Combined with the flow rate changes and methane production rate, it can be clearly recognized that the adsorption capacity of CH₄ in coal samples containing moisture is significantly reduced. In the middle stage of displacement, when large-scale replacement of adsorbed CH₄ is required, the N₂ molecules on the surface of W3 are confronted with CH₄ molecules that have been severely “passivated” by moisture and have extremely weak binding forces. Therefore, the kinetic resistance for N₂ to displace these CH₄ molecules is lower, and the desorption rate is relatively faster. This qualitative mechanism provides a microscopic explanation for the observed phenomenon that the displacement rate of W3 surpasses that of W2 in the middle stage. It should be emphasized that this promotional effect in the middle stage is offset by the enormous initial seepage resistance (inhibitory effect) of W3. Ultimately, W3 becomes the sample with the worst overall economic efficiency.

Discussion on the role of moisture in gas injection displacement process in coal samples with different moisture content

The ' V-shaped ' displacement efficiency trend observed in this experiment cannot be explained by a single ' promotion ' or ' inhibition ' effect. To this end, we integrate the adsorption and seepage mechanisms and propose a ' stage-dominant mechanism ' conceptual framework to systematically explain. The framework innovatively divides the whole dynamic displacement process into three stages according to the content of N2 in the exhaust gas. This framework divides the displacement process into three stages according to the N₂ content in the off-gas and reveals the dominant controlling factors of each stage: These phases are demarcated as follows: Phase Ⅰ, during which the N₂ content escalates from 0% to 50%; Phase Ⅱ, wherein the N₂ content spans from 50% to 95%; and Phase Ⅲ, in which the N₂ content is sustained at 95% until the termination of the experiment, as depicted in Fig. 2.

In Phase Ⅰ and Phase Ⅱ, with respect to the increase in bituminous coal moisture content, the trends manifested in both the methane displacement volume and displacement rate are analogous. There is an initial diminution succeeded by an augmentation, presenting a uniform “V” - shaped variation throughout these two phases. Nevertheless, in the third phase and the overall trend, a continuous downward trajectory is observed. In general, the rate and volume of CH₄ displacement associated with different moisture content coal sample groups decline as the moisture content ascends. The influence of water on the gas injection and displacement process can be classified into two principal effects.

Firstly, the promotion effect: As expounded in Part C of this section, the elevation of moisture content within coal samples restrains methane adsorption. As the moisture content rises, a greater number of adsorption sites on the coal matrix are occupied by water molecules. Since water molecules possess a stronger adsorption affinity for the coal matrix than methane, the equilibrium adsorption capacity of coal samples for methane progressively declines. This reduction in adsorption capacity expedites the displacement rate during gas injection and displacement, thereby manifesting the promotion effect.

Secondly, the hindering effect: This effect stems from the fact that free water impedes the transportation of methane within pore spaces. This effect also encompasses potential slippage and an increment in the starting pressure requisite for gas flow. In the context of gas injection displacement, this effect essentially serves to diminish the displacement rate, thus exemplifying its obstructive role.

The impact of these two effects varies remarkably across different stages of gas injection and displacement, a disparity vividly illustrated in Fig. 7.

In Phase Ⅰ and Phase Ⅱ of gas injection and displacement, the displacement rate and methane displacement for coal sample groups with varying moisture content exhibit a “V” shape. This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the combined impact of the hindering and promoting effects. To discern the dominant influence at each stage, the dry coal sample group is considered the control.

Stage I (N₂: 0–50%): Seepage-dominated Period.

This stage is characterized by the rapid production of free CH4. The influence of water is mainly manifested in the physical occupation and blockage of the seepage channel. The pre-existing water lock effect (free water blockage) constitutes a strong seepage resistance, and the ' hindrance effect ' caused by it is absolutely dominant. Therefore, Group W2, which contains a certain amount of free water and can effectively increase flow resistance, exhibits the slowest displacement rate.

Stage II (N₂: 50–95%): Adsorption-desorption Transition Period.

This stage is a critical period for the massive displacement and desorption of adsorbed CH₄. The positive impact brought by moisture’s “deprivation effect” on adsorption sites—weakening CH₄ adsorption capacity and promoting desorption—begins to stand out and strengthen rapidly. At the same time, as the gas continues to scour, some of the free water in the seepage channel is expelled or redistributed, which leads to the weakening of the water lock effect, the decrease of the seepage resistance, and the relative weakening of the hindrance effect. The two effects wax and wane: at moderate moisture content (W2), the impeding effect still slightly prevails; while at high moisture content (W3), the strong adsorption-weakening effect can overcome the remaining seepage resistance, making its displacement rate surpass that of W2 and approach that of W1, forming the overall “V”-shaped trend.

Stage III (N₂ > 95%): Residual-dominated Period.

Adsorbed CH₄ is basically exhausted, and moisture’s “desorption-promoting effect” fades. At this point, the weak impeding effect of residual adsorbed water films on subsequent N₂ migration, as well as the slow adjustment of pore structure possibly caused by the long-term hydration effect of the coal matrix, become the main reasons for the slow decline in flow rate. Since the pore spaces of high-water-content coal samples have been fully occupied by water, their structure tends to be stable with limited subsequent changes, thus the flow rate declines most gently.

By establishing three typical moisture states (W1, W2, and W3), this study has preliminarily revealed the “V-shaped” trend and stage-dependent dominant mechanisms of moisture affecting gas injection displacement. However, limited by experimental conditions, the number of moisture gradients is relatively small, and the nonlinear relationship inferred from three data points requires further verification under more moisture gradients. Future research can establish continuous moisture gradients (e.g., 10%–20% saturation intervals) to systematically investigate the quantitative relationship between moisture content and displacement efficiency, thereby more robustly revealing its nonlinear characteristics and inflection points.

In addition, this experiment adopts the “water-first then gas” sequence, focusing on revealing the intrinsic mechanism of moisture presence on the gas displacement process, which differs from the “gas-first then water” timeline in practical engineering. Although this limits the direct extrapolability of the study’s conclusions to specific engineering scenarios to a certain extent, the revealed dual effects of moisture (impeding seepage and promoting desorption) and their stage transition mechanism can still provide an important theoretical basis for understanding gas-water competitive adsorption and migration. Subsequent research can incorporate the experimental design of “adsorbing CH₄ first followed by water intrusion” to further simulate the dynamic process of water intrusion into gas-bearing coal seams after hydraulic fracturing, enhancing the engineering guiding value of the research.

Research limitations - model validation

This study reveals the non-monotonic law of moisture’s effect on gas displacement efficiency through controlled experiments, and proposes a conceptual model of the stage-dominated mechanism. As a mechanism study centered on experimental exploration, the generalizability of the conclusions of this study across a wider parameter range still needs systematic verification and further analysis via numerical simulation. We clearly recognize that there are significant challenges in constructing a numerical model that can accurately reproduce the water-gas-coal multi-physical field coupling process. It is necessary to integrate key elements such as competitive adsorption kinetics, gas-water two-phase seepage and dynamic pore structure characterization. Meanwhile, the high-quality calibration of the model relies on more systematic microstructural parameters. These challenges mean that a reliable numerical simulation study needs to be carried out as an independent and in-depth follow-up research topic. The complete experimental phenomena and data obtained in this study just provide a key validation benchmark and starting point for the construction of such models.
