Safety evaluation of repeated administration of umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes
Authors: Ting Lan, Tao-Tao Tang, Jing-Jing Di, Suo-Fu Qin, Aihemaiti Muzaipaier, Qin-Yu Zhou, Bi-Cheng Liu, Qiu-Li Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (MSC-Exos) have shown considerable therapeutic potential in regenerative medicine. However, the biosafety profile of these exosomes following repeated administration has not been adequately characterised, and this knowledge gap continues to hinder clinical translation.MethodsHuman umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (hucMSC-Exos) were produced on a xeno-free platform by replacing fetal bovine serum (FBS) with human platelet lysate (hPL). A relatively comprehensive and integrated repeated-dose preclinical biosafety profile assessment was then conducted using polyethylene glycol (PEG)-Liposomes as reference nanovesicles. The assessment included pharmacokinetic and distribution analyses using DiD labelling, haematological cytology testing, quantitative measurement of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, histopathological scoring, and RNA sequencing.ResultsIn this study, the safety assessment revealed that: (1) hucMSC-Exos primarily accumulated in the liver and spleen and exhibited an approximately 4-h circulation time; (2) repeated intravenous administration of hucMSC-Exos for 30 days did not result in overt systemic inflammation, and the evaluated haematological and biochemical parameters remained within the normal range; (3) RNA sequencing showed that hucMSC-Exos avoided immune pathway activation (e.g., IL-17/antigen presentation) triggered by PEG-Liposomes, instead enriching complement-coagulation cascades (e.g., C1qc, C6 upregulation) and metabolic pathways (steroidogenesis, retinol metabolism). Together, these findings suggest that hucMSC-Exos have a favourable biosafety profile in a murine model and support further preclinical evaluation for regenerative medicine applications.ConclusionThis study suggests that repeated intravenous administration of hucMSC-Exos confers a relatively favourable biosafety profile in a murine model and provides preliminary preclinical evidence for further investigation towards clinical translation.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13287-026-05172-5.

Introduction

Mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (MSC-Exos) have emerged as promising nanotherapeutic agents in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. Their ability to deliver bioactive cargo, including nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids, orchestrates tissue repair through multiple mechanisms [1]. MSC-Exos may retain some of the regenerative capacity of parental cells while potentially reducing some risks associated with cell transplantation [2, 3]. Their therapeutic potential has been reported in a range of disease contexts, including neurological, cardiovascular, and cutaneous disorders. These effects have generally been attributed to immunomodulation, pro-angiogenic signalling, and direct stimulation of cellular proliferation and differentiation [4–10]. Compared with living-cell therapies, MSC-Exos therapies offer several advantages, including simplified storage, enhanced biostability, and improved clinical handling efficiency [11].

However, MSC-Exo’s therapeutic application still faces major challenges, particularly regarding biosafety and standardisation. While early-phase clinical trials have suggested that MSC-Exos exhibit low acute toxicity [12, 13], their inherent compositional heterogeneity as natural nanoparticles still raises unresolved safety concerns. MSC-Exos may carry various uncharacterised bioactive components, such as donor-specific proteins and non-coding RNAs, which may trigger unintended immunorecognition or inflammatory cascades [14]. These concerns may be particularly relevant in chronic diseases that require repeated intravenous administration.

Current safety assessments of MSC-Exos remain fragmented and have focused mainly on acute toxicity endpoints or limited immune parameters [15–17]. Because MSC-Exos may accumulate in organs and may induce delayed host responses, more comprehensive safety studies under repeated intravenous administration conditions are needed. To address these gaps, we produced hucMSC-Exos in a human platelet lysate (hPL)-based xeno-free culture platform [18] and used pharmacokinetic-matched PEG-Liposomes as reference nanoparticles [19–21].

The novelty of this study lies in establishing a relatively comprehensive and integrated safety evaluation framework for hucMSC-Exos, covering biodistribution, haematology, serum cytokine and chemokine responses, multi-organ histopathology, and transcriptomic signatures following repeated intravenous administration. We hypothesised that hucMSC-Exos produced under xeno-free conditions would show a favourable safety profile in a murine model under the tested regimen. This study aimed to provide preliminary experimental evidence to support the development of hucMSC-Exos.

Materials and methods

hucMSC extraction and culture

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University (2023ZDSYLL118-P01), with written informed consent obtained. Umbilical cord tissues were rinsed twice with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the vascular components and amniotic membrane were removed. The remaining tissue was minced into 1–3 mm³ fragments and centrifuged in PBS (1,000 rpm, 5 min, 4 °C). After removal of the supernatant, the tissue fragments were cultured in T75 flasks containing complete medium composed of MSC medium (MSC NutriStem® XF Medium, 05-200-1 A, Sartorius, Germany) supplemented with 5% hPL (PLTGOLD100R, Sartorius, Germany). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO₂, and the medium was replaced every 4 days. Cell outgrowth from the explant was typically observed within 7–14 days. When cultures reached 80–85% confluence, cells were passaged after PBS washing and digestion with TrypLE Express (A12859-01, Gibco, USA) for 2 min. The enzymatic reaction was neutralised with fresh medium, followed by centrifugation (1,000 rpm, 5 min) and resuspension in complete medium for further expansion.

MSC characterisation

To verify the characteristics of the parental cells used for exosome production, P6 hucMSCs were characterised by flow cytometry and tri-lineage differentiation assays. For immunophenotypic analysis, MSCs were harvested using TrypLE, resuspended at 1 × 10⁶ cells/100 µL, and incubated for 30 min in the dark with PE-conjugated antibodies against CD29, CD44, CD73, CD90, CD105, CD11b, CD34, and CD45 (555443, 555479, 561014, 561970, 580839, 555388, 560941, 555483; BD Biosciences, USA), as previously reported [18, 22, 23]. After two washes with PBS, the cells were analysed using a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). For tri-lineage differentiation, P6 hucMSCs were cultured in osteogenic differentiation medium (7531, ScienCell, USA), adipogenic differentiation medium (7541, ScienCell, USA), or chondrogenic differentiation medium (05455, STEMCELL, Canada), according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Differentiation was evaluated on days 28, 10, and 21, respectively, using Alizarin Red S staining, Oil Red O staining, and Alcian Blue staining.

hucMSC-Exos isolation, purification, and storage

To isolate hucMSC-Exos, P6 hucMSCs at 75–80% confluence were washed twice with PBS and cultured in serum-free DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, 11320033, USA) at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 48 h. The cell supernatant underwent sequential centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 20 min, 13,500 × g for 30 min, and 100,000 × g for 2 h at 4 °C. The resulting hucMSC-Exos were resuspended in 0.22 μm-filtered PBS. For further purification, a size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) column (Echo9101A-S10, Echo Biotech, China) was pre-equilibrated with 20 mL PBS. After loading 1 mL exosome suspension, PBS-eluted fractions (500 µL per tube) were collected, and fractions 4–7 were retained as purified hucMSC-Exos. Freshly prepared hucMSC-Exos were aliquoted and stored at -80 °C for less than 1 month. Purified hucMSC-Exos obtained from independent donor-derived hucMSC cultures were combined as pooled exosome preparations before use. This strategy was adopted to minimise donor-related variation and to improve the representativeness of the tested material.

Characterisation of hucMSC-Exos and PEG-Liposomes

Exosome-specific proteins (Alix [ab117600, Abcam, UK], CD9 [ab236630, Abcam, UK], CD81 [ab97559, Abcam, UK], TSG101 [ab125011, Abcam, UK]) and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) marker Calnexin (ab22595, Abcam, UK) were detected by Western blotting (WB). For nanoparticle characterisation, exosome samples were analysed using the NanoAnalyzer U30E (NanoFCM, Xiamen, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PEG-Liposomes (300205 S, Sigma, Germany) were measured by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and showed an average particle size of 137.23 ± 5.23 nm (Fig. S1A) and a zeta potential of -53.21 ± 6.21 mV (Fig. S1B).

Nano flow cytometry

Before immunofluorescence staining, exosome concentrations quantified by NTA were adjusted to 1–3 × 10⁸ particles/mL with incubation buffer. 30 µL hucMSC-Exos (1010 particles) were loaded into an EP tube for each component, and one test volume of antibody (CD9, 555371, BD Pharmingen, USA; CD81, 551108, BD Pharmingen, USA) was added according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After incubation for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark, the samples were diluted with 1 mL of 0.22 μm-filtered PBS and ultracentrifuged (100,000 × g, 75 min, 4 °C). Light exposure was minimised throughout the procedure, and the exosomes were resuspended in 30–50 µL PBS in 600 µL EP tubes. After instrument start-up and quality control procedures, the samples were analysed by nanoflow cytometry, with fluorescence channels selected according to antibody labels. Data acquisition was performed using the automated threshold settings.

Fluorescent labelling and free dye purification of hucMSC-Exos and PEG-Liposomes

hucMSC-Exos and PEG-Liposomes were stained with 1 mM DiD fluorescent dye (C1039, Beyotime, China) and incubated for 2 h at room temperature under gentle shaking (40 rpm) in the dark. Unbound dye was removed by SEC according to established protocols (Fig. S1C). Fractions 1–15 were collected, and their fluorescence intensity was measured using a multifunctional microplate reader (Fig. S1D). WB detection (Fig. S1E) and transmission electron microscopy (Fig. S1F) confirmed that exosomes were predominantly enriched in fractions 4–7. These fractions were therefore pooled for further analysis. The labelling efficiency of DiD-modified liposomes and hucMSC-Exos was preliminarily assessed by nano flow cytometry (Fig. S1G).

Blood metabolism and distribution imaging after MSC-Exos or PEG-Liposomes injection

The animal experiments in this study were reported in accordance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines 2.0. Eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., China) weighing 20–23 g were used after a 12-hour fast. Mice were randomly allocated to the control and treatment groups using a random-number method. Following tail-vein injection of 200 µL DiD-labelled MSC-Exos, PEG-Liposomes (1 × 10¹⁰ particles/dose), or PBS, mice were euthanized, and tissues were harvested at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h. Anesthesia was induced by intraperitoneal injection of 1.25% tribromoethanol (200 µL/10 g body weight). Whole blood was collected by cardiac puncture into 1.5 mL EP tubes. Circulating residual particles were then cleared by cardiac perfusion with saline, followed by collection of the brain, heart, liver, lung, spleen, kidney, and gastrointestinal tissues. Whole blood samples were left at room temperature for 30 min and were then centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min to separate serum. Serum and tissue samples underwent optical imaging, and fluorescence intensity was normalised to the 0.5 h group baseline to calculate relative blood retention percentages.

Routine blood and serum biochemical analyses

Blood samples for routine blood testing were collected into 1.5 mL EP tubes pre-filled with anticoagulant (YA1461, Solarbio, China). Haematological measurements were performed using an automated blood-cell analyser (DF62 Vet, Dymind, China). Serum was separated from non-anticoagulated blood samples and used for biochemical assessment of liver and kidney function, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), serum creatinine (Scr), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN). Serum biochemical parameters were measured according to the standard procedures of the testing platform.

Determination of serum cytokines by liquid-phase suspension microarray technology

The microsphere suspension was shaken at 1,400 rpm for 30 s, and 50 µL was dispensed into each well of a 96-well plate. According to the kit instructions, test samples were diluted fourfold, and 50 µL of each diluted sample, standard, and blank control were added to the plate. All plates were sealed and incubated at room temperature for 30 min under light protection at 850 rpm. Three washes were then performed using a plate washer. Diluted detection antibody (25 µL/well) and streptavidin-PE (50 µL/well) were added according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The plate was incubated in the dark with shaking for 30 min and 10 min, respectively. After a final three-wash cycle, the beads were resuspended in 125 µL of assay buffer. The plate was vortexed at 850 rpm for 30 s in the dark, and detection was performed using the Bio-Plex 200 system. Data normalisation was performed using Z-score transformation according to the formula Zi = (Xi - µ)/σ. Serum cytokine and chemokine profiling was performed using samples collected after the 5th and 10th injection.

Haematoxylin and eosin staining

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections (3 μm) were baked at 60 °C for 1 h, then dewaxed twice in xylene for 15 min each. The sections were rehydrated through a graded ethanol series and rinsed with deionised water after each step. The sections were then stained with haematoxylin for 10 min, differentiated with 1% acid alcohol for 20 s, and counterstained with 0.2% ammonia water for 3 min. After eosin staining for 2–3 min, the sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral resin.

Histopathological damage score

A systematic histomorphological evaluation was performed on paraffin-embedded sections of six major organs after 5 and 10 injections. Tissue injury was graded using a 5-tier histopathological scoring system: 0, intact; 1, slight damage (occasional degeneration or inflammation); 2, mild damage (localized cellular degeneration or mild inflammation); 3, moderate damage (marked cellular degeneration or moderate inflammatory cell infiltration); and 4, severe damage (extensive necrosis or major architectural disruption). All assessments were performed independently by two pathologists who were blinded to group allocation.

Transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol, and RNA purity and concentration were assessed using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Transcriptome libraries were constructed using the VAHTS Universal V5 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The sequencing reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2, and gene expression levels, expressed as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM), were quantified using HTSeq-count. Principal component analysis (PCA) and sample clustering were performed in R (v3.2.0) to evaluate the biological replicates. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as genes meeting the thresholds of |log2 fold change| > 1 and an false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p value < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction). Hierarchical clustering of DEGs was visualised using radar plots. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was then conducted using a hypergeometric test to identify significantly enriched functional terms (FDR < 0.05), and the results were visualised as bar charts and chord diagrams with key pathway genes listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Normality was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance by Bartlett’s test. Intergroup comparisons were performed using two-tailed unpaired t tests (two groups) or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test (three or more groups). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Isolation and validation of hucMSCs

hucMSCs were isolated and expanded under xeno-free conditions using hPL as a serum substitute (Fig. S2A). Flow cytometry showed positive expression of mesenchymal stromal cell markers, including CD29, CD90, CD105, and CD73, and negative expression of CD11b, CD45, HLA-DR, CD34, and CD14 (Fig. S2B). Tri-lineage differentiation assays further confirmed osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation capacity in P6 exosome-producing MSCs, indicating that these cells retained multilineage differentiation potential at the passage used for exosome production (Fig. S2C).

Characterisation and labelling of hucMSC-Exos

hucMSC-Exos were isolated by sequential ultracentrifugation and SEC. Purity and yield were quantified by the particle-to-protein ratio (5.02 ± 1.2 × 108 particles/µg) and total particle concentration (1.05 × 10¹¹ ± 0.19 × 10¹¹ particles/mL), respectively. Their zeta potential was also measured (Fig. 1A). The isolated vesicles were characterised in accordance with the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) 2023 guidelines [24]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed the characteristic cup-shaped morphology of hucMSC-Exos (Fig. 1B), whereas nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) showed a median diameter of 145.5 ± 1.12 nm (Fig. 1C). WB confirmed the expression of exosome-associated markers (Alix, CD9, CD81, and TSG101) and the absence of the ER marker calnexin (Fig. 1D). Nano-flow cytometry showed positivity rates of 29.2% for CD81 (Fig. 1E) and 20.8% for CD9 (Fig. 1F).

Pharmacokinetics and biodistribution of hucMSC-Exos

To evaluate the pharmacokinetic profiles of hucMSC-Exos as natural bioactive nanocarriers, DiD-labelled hucMSC-Exos and PEG-Liposomes were administered intravenously to 8-week-old C57BL/6J mice at equivalent doses, and serial blood fluorescence was measured using the in vivo imaging system (IVIS)‌‌. The blood half-life of hucMSC-Exos was approximately 3 h, compared with approximately 6 h for PEG-Liposomes, and both formulations were almost completely cleared from the circulation within 24 h (Fig. 2A). The slower clearance of PEG-Liposomes is consistent with previous studies showing that PEGylation enhances hydrophilicity and prolongs plasma half-life [25–27].

Ex vivo biodistribution profiling demonstrated predominant hepatic accumulation of hucMSC-Exos, whereas PEG-Liposomes localised to both the liver and the spleen (Fig. 2B, C; the DiD-injected groups served as negative controls, Fig. S3A). Negligible fluorescence signals were detected in the non-mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) organs, including the brain and heart. Consistent with whole-organ imaging, histological sections from the major accumulation sites at 8 h post-injection confirmed predominant hepatic deposition of both formulations (Fig. 2D). Notably, fluorescence imaging of the intestine showed negligible signal (Fig. S3B), indicating limited accumulation of intravenously administered hucMSC-Exos and liposomes in the digestive tract. Together, these findings identified the liver as the major site of hucMSC-Exos accumulation under the tested conditions.

Assessment of the safety of repeated administration of hucMSC-Exos from haematology, serum biochemistry, and cytokine and chemokine analyses

To evaluate the biosafety profile of hucMSC-Exos under repeated intravenous administration, we established a repeated-dosing regimen in male BALB/c mice. Mice in the low-dose (10⁹ particles/injection) and high-dose (10¹⁰ particles/injection) groups were received intravenous administration every 3 days for 30 days (10 injections in total). In vivo safety was assessed by haematological parameters, serum biochemistry, cytokine and chemokine analyses, histopathology, and RNA sequencing at the midpoint (the 5th injection) and the final time point (the 10th injection) (Fig. 3A).

Body weight remained stable in all groups, with less than 5 g variation from baseline (Fig. 3B), and comparable terminal weight gain indicated no treatment-related metabolic disturbances (Fig. 3C). No mortality occurred during the observation period. Complete blood counts after the 5th injection revealed no significant leukocyte elevation in the hucMSC-Exos-treated groups. In contrast, PEG-Liposomes increased blood inflammatory indices: low-dose Liposomes (L-Lipo) increased white blood cell counts compared with PBS controls (7.13 ± 1.68 vs. 4.93 ± 0.81 × 10⁹/L, p < 0.05), with concurrent increases in intermediate cells and granulocytes (Table 1). A similar pattern was observed in the high-dose Liposome group (H-Lipo) after the 10th injection (Table 2). Notably, organ function biomarkers, including hepatic enzymes (ALT and AST) and renal parameters (Scr and BUN), remained within the normal ranges across all groups (Fig. 3D-G).

To assess cytokine and chemokine responses, we quantified these mediators after repeated administration (Fig. 3H, I). CCL27 (Fig. 3J), IL-10 (Fig. 3K), and CCL22 (Fig. 3L) increased in the hucMSC-Exo group after 5 injections compared with the PBS group. Similar increases were observed in the Liposome group. Histopathological evaluation of the safety profile of hucMSC-Exos

Histopathological evaluation of major organs collected after 10 injections was conducted to assess potential tissue damage caused by repeated intravenous injections of hucMSC-Exos. Neither hucMSC-Exos nor PEG-Liposomes induced histopathological alterations in the brain, spleen, or heart, and the histology scores were indistinguishable from those of PBS controls (score 0) (Fig. 4A). This absence of damage may have reflected the minimal retention of both PEG-Liposomes and hucMSC-Exos in these organs, as indicated by the biodistribution analyses.

Hepatic evaluation demonstrated preserved radial plate architecture of hepatocytes with intact sinusoids in PBS controls. The hucMSC-Exo groups showed histology comparable to that of the controls, with only sporadic, non-progressive inflammatory foci, whereas H-Lipo induced statistically significant periportal inflammatory infiltration (score: 0.63 ± 0.74) (Fig. 4A, B).

Pulmonary analysis showed intact alveolar morphology in PBS controls. The hucMSC-Exo groups displayed histopathological changes (L-MSC-Exos: 1.29 ± 1.11; H-MSC-Exos: 0.86 ± 1.07), with alveolar wall thickening and inflammatory cell infiltration. In contrast, the L-Lipo group showed more pronounced parenchymal injury, characterised by inflammatory cell infiltration, alveolar congestion, and alveolar wall thickening (score: 2.6 ± 0.55) (Fig. 4A, C).

Renal histology revealed patent capillary loops without proteinaceous deposits in the control group. After 10 injections, the L-MSC-Exo and H-MSC-Exo groups showed only a few scattered inflammatory foci (score: 0.4 ± 0.53), whereas H-Lipo induced significant inflammatory infiltration (score: 1.2 ± 0.84) (Fig. 4A, D).

Taken together, histological evaluation showed that repeated intravenous administration of hucMSC-Exos caused no marked pathological changes in the brain, spleen, or heart, with only mild alterations observed in the liver, kidney, and lung under the tested conditions.

Transcriptomic profiling revealed organ-specific responses to hucMSC-Exos and PEG-Liposomes

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying tissue responses, transcriptome sequencing was performed on liver, lung, and kidney tissues from mice receiving 10 injections of hucMSC-Exos or PEG-Liposomes. PCA revealed strong within-group clustering across all organs (Fig. S4A-C).

Differential expression analysis showed 654 DEGs (327 upregulated and 327 downregulated) in hucMSC-Exos-treated liver tissues, versus 669 DEGs (492 upregulated and 177 downregulated) in the PEG-Liposome-treated group. Notably, 12 inflammation-related genes (e.g., H2-Aa, Cd74, Mpo, Ccl5, and Saa2) were specifically upregulated in the PEG-Liposome group, while remaining unchanged or being downregulated in the hucMSC-Exo group (Fig. 5A), as confirmed by reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) (Fig. S4D). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis demonstrated conserved activation in pancreatic secretion, protein and fat digestion, and MAPK pathways, whereas PEG-Liposomes uniquely enriched immune pathways, including IL-17 signalling, antigen presentation, and T-cell differentiation. By contrast, hucMSC-Exos were associated with homeostatic pathways, including circadian rhythm, cardiac contraction, and endocrine signalling (cortisol and oxytocin signalling) (Fig. 5B).

In the lung, 989 DEGs (431 upregulated and 558 downregulated) were identified in the hucMSC-Exo group, compared with 467 DEGs (315 upregulated and 152 downregulated) in the PEG-Liposome group. KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the hucMSC-Exogroup was enriched for genes involved in the complement and coagulation cascade and in metabolic pathways related to retinol, linoleic acid, ascorbate, and aldarate metabolism. In addition, three complement activation-associated genes, including C1qc and Serpina3b, were upregulated in the hucMSC-Exo group without corresponding expression changes in the PEG-Liposome group, and RT-qPCR confirmed these expression patterns (Fig. S4E, F). In contrast, PEG-Liposomes were enriched in IL-17 signalling, cytokine signalling, Toll-like receptor signalling, and other immune- and inflammation-related pathways (Fig. 5C, D).

In the kidney, 713 DEGs (308 upregulated and 405 downregulated) were identified in the hucMSC-Exo group and 539 DEGs (247 upregulated and 292 downregulated) in the PEG-Liposome group. Similar to the hepatic response, hucMSC-Exos mainly affected steroidogenesis and lipid metabolism, whereas PEG-Liposomes maintained immune pathway activation. Additionally, hucMSC-Exos-treated kidneys were enriched in complement components, with kidneys showing differential expression of 11 complement-related genes, such as C6, C9, Ahsg, and Vtn, showing differential expression across the treatment groups (Fig. 5E, F). These genes were further validated by RT-qPCR (Fig. S4G).

Overall, the transcriptomic data suggested that hucMSC-Exos were associated with weaker inflammatory signalling than PEG-Liposomes and with greater enrichment of complement-related and metabolism-related pathways in the tested organs.

Discussion

The clinical translation of MSC-Exos has been impeded by the lack of comprehensive safety evaluations under repeated-administration conditions. In this study, we performed a relatively integrated repeated-dose safety assessment in a murine model, including biodistribution, haematology, serum biochemistry, cytokine and chemokine profiling, histopathology, and transcriptomic analysis. Under the tested 30-day repeated intravenous administration regimen, hucMSC-Exos did not induce overt systemic toxicity in the evaluated readouts. Previous studies have shown that MSC-Exos generally carry a lower risk of haemolysis, platelet aggregation, and endotoxin-related adverse reactions. Several preclinical studies have also reported no significant hepatic or renal toxicity after systemic administration [12, 13, 30]. Our results are largely consistent with the existing literature and suggest that hucMSC-Exos showed limited systemic toxicity following repeated intravenous administration. At the same time, the mild cytokine changes, pulmonary microstructural alterations, and complement-coagulation markers observed in this study suggest that repeated administration of MSC-Exos may still carry potential safety risks. These findings provide preliminary preclinical evidence to support further investigation of hucMSC-Exos under defined experimental conditions.

Notably, the hucMSC-Exo groups maintained stable peripheral blood leukocyte counts and did not induce systemic tissue damage. However, cytokine analysis revealed changes in IL-10, CCL27, and CCL22 after repeated exposure, and similar trends were observed in the PEG-Liposome groups (Fig. 3J-L). This pattern suggests that repeated intravenous administration of hucMSC-Exos may elicit a limited rather than broad proinflammatory response. In addition, repeated intravenous administration of PEG-Liposomes induced more severe pulmonary pathology at low doses than at high doses (Fig. 4). This may be attributed to the shorter circulatory half-life of low-dose PEG-Liposomes, which renders them more susceptible to capture by the mononuclear phagocyte system. This process may exacerbate pulmonary injury through activation of the complement system and generation of anti-PEG IgM antibodies. In contrast, high-dose PEG-Liposomes, owing to their higher surface PEG density, may prolong circulation by creating steric hindrance. This reduces immune recognition, mitigates accelerated blood clearance (ABC) effects, and diminishes the tissue damage they mediate [31–33]. Furthermore, high-dose PEG-Liposomes may activate antioxidant pathways or enhance autophagy, thereby triggering adaptive cellular protective mechanisms. Low-dose PEG-Liposomes may be insufficient to induce such protective responses, leading to cumulative injury signals [34]. These findings suggest that future studies should evaluate the dose-response relationship of liposomes systematically while remaining alert to the potential risks associated with low-dose intravenous administration.

The in vivo biodistribution profile of MSC-Exos is a critical pharmacokinetic foundation for clinical safety assessment. Previous studies have generally reported that intravenously administered MSC-Exos are enriched in the liver, spleen, and lungs [35–37]. In our study, hucMSC-Exos also exhibited a distribution pattern associated with mononuclear phagocyte system-related organs; however, hepatic accumulation appeared more prominent than pulmonary or splenic signal under the tested conditions. This difference may be related to multiple experimental variables, including the source and culture conditions of the parental cells, xeno-free production setting, particle size distribution, purification strategy, DiD-based fluorescence labelling, sampling time points, and protein corona-dependent tissue uptake. Previous studies have shown that smaller extracellular vesicles (EVs) preferentially accumulate in the liver, whereas larger exosomes are more likely to localise in the lungs [35, 38, 39], which is broadly consistent with our findings. Furthermore, protein corona formation significantly modulates exosomal behaviour [40, 41]. For instance, albumin pre-coating strategies enhance hepatic uptake [42]. Moreover, engineering modifications (including surface homing peptides such as RGD [43, 44], CMP [45], ASSLNIA, CSTSMLKAC, T140 variants [46], PTHTRWA [47], and Lys-Leu-Ala lipoprotein mimetics [48, 49]) enable tissue-specific nanoparticle delivery (including activated endothelium, myocardium, CXCR4+ cells, lung cancer, aneurysmal smooth muscle, and glioma). Additionally, glycosylation modifications such as the use of neuraminidase-treated exosomes can effectively regulate their biodistribution [50]. Therefore, the biodistribution pattern observed here should be interpreted as reflecting the specific preparation and experimental conditions used in this study.

Histopathological examination further confirmed the overall tolerability of hucMSC-Exos. Following repeated administration, no significant structural abnormalities were observed in the brain, spleen, or heart, and only mild changes were detected in the liver, kidney, and lung. These findings are generally consistent with previous reports indicating limited tissue toxicity following intravenous administration of hucMSC-Exos. The mild inflammatory changes observed in the lungs may reflect the entry of intravenously administered exosomes into the pulmonary vascular bed, where they are recognised and phagocytosed by circulating and tissue-resident phagocytes. In contrast, PEG-Liposomes induced more pronounced pulmonary inflammation in this study, which may be related to differences in circulation time, immune recognition, and tissue adaptation. A shorter circulatory half-life at low dose may increase exposure to the mononuclear phagocyte system and thereby aggravate pulmonary injury, whereas a higher surface PEG density at high dose may partially reduce immune recognition and mitigate accelerated blood clearance [31–33]. In addition, higher-dose PEG-Liposomes may trigger adaptive protective responses, such as antioxidant or autophagy-related pathways, that are not sufficiently activated at low dose [34].

This study still has several limitations. First, PEG-Liposomes share structural similarities with MSC-Exos as lipid bilayer vesicles and have been used as pharmacokinetic references in prior studies [19–21]. However, they do not fully replicate the molecular complexity and biophysical properties of MSC-Exos; therefore, their value as equivalent controls remains limited. Second, this study included only male mice, making it impossible to assess potential sex-related differences in biosafety [51, 52]. Third, although cytokine and chemokine profiles did not indicate significant systemic inflammatory activation, immune cell subsets were not analysed; consequently, the immunological effects following repeated intravenous administration remain inadequately characterised. Fourth, although two dose levels and a fixed dosing frequency were used, a broader dose range and alternative dosing regimens were not evaluated. Fifth, coagulation-related safety was not directly assessed. Given that hucMSC-Exos were administered by repeated intravenous administration, early coagulation markers such as TF/CD142 and other coagulation-related parameters are important for a more comprehensive safety evaluation. In addition, because the observation window in this study was limited to preset post-injection time points, delayed coagulation abnormalities or longer-term haematological effects cannot be ruled out. Finally, several other important safety dimensions were not fully addressed, including batch-to-batch variation among exosome preparations, passage-dependent changes in exosome-related effects, and potential tumorigenic risk.

In conclusion, the present study provides a relatively comprehensive and integrated preclinical evaluation of repeated intravenous administration of hucMSC-Exos in a mouse model. Under the tested conditions, hucMSC-Exos demonstrated an overall favourable tolerability profile with limited systemic toxicity and only mild tissue reactions. However, the subtle cytokine alterations, pulmonary changes, and complement-coagulation-related signals observed herein suggest that the safety of repeated exposure to hucMSC-Exos warrants further investigation. Accordingly, future studies should aim to more precisely define these safety margins by including immune cell profiling, coagulation-related biomarkers, oncological assessments, lot-to-lot consistency, passage-dependent variations, and longer-term follow-up evaluations.

Conclusion

In this study, we compared the biological safety features of hucMSC-Exos and PEG-Liposomes through a multidimensional assessment system including pharmacokinetics, haematology, inflammatory cytokine assays, histopathology, and transcriptomics. The results showed that hucMSC-Exos exhibited rapid clearance and predominant hepatic accumulation under the tested preparation and tracing conditions. More importantly, no overt organ toxicity, haematological abnormality, or systemic inflammatory response was observed under the tested 30-day repeated intravenous dosing regimen in mice. Transcriptomic sequencing suggested that hucMSC-Exos may act through regulation of key biological processes, including metabolic pathways and the complement cascade. Taken together, these findings suggest that hucMSC-Exos have a favourable biosafety profile in a murine model and provide preliminary preclinical evidence to support further investigation towards clinical translation.
