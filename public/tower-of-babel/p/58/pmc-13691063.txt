Choline-dependent methionine metabolism supports leukemia progression
Authors: Nirmalya Saha, Lauren Lachowski, Franchesca Franzen, Sydney Alibeckoff, Victor Vitvitsky, Roshan Kumar, Melanie Hutchings, Malathi Kandarpa, Hsiangyu Hu, James Ropa, Marisa J L Aitken, Mark Y Chiang, Li Zhang, Costas A Lyssiotis, Shannon A Carty, Moshe Talpaz, Smiti Vaid Gupta, Ruma Banerjee, Andrew G Muntean
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor cells must occupy and thrive in a competitive microenvironment marked by limited metabolites, including essential amino acids like methionine. Using a leukemia suppression model and CRISPR screening, we found that the choline transporter SLC44A1 is overexpressed in leukemia patients and impacts leukemogenesis. Choline is an important nutrient for membrane synthesis and less commonly contributes to the methionine cycle. A metabolic analysis demonstrated that metabolites of the methionine pathway are significantly elevated in leukemic cells. Surprisingly, dietary restriction of methionine accelerated leukemogenesis in vivo. Choline can serve as an alternative source for methionine via the enzymatic activity of CHDH and BHMT. Under restrictive methionine conditions, BHMT and CHDH are significantly upregulated. In vivo, BHMT and CHDH are necessary for leukemia progression where they utilize choline as an alternative source to satisfy increased methionine demand. This pathway represents a vulnerability in cancer cells that may be exploited for therapeutic intervention.Subject terms: Leukaemia, Cancer metabolism

Introduction

Tumor cells reside in a competitive microenvironment with poor access to nutrients and diminished vascularization1. Diet provides all amino acids; however, synthesis provides opportunities to supplement nutrient intake and highlights metabolic adaptability often observed in cancer cells 2,3. Dysregulation of metabolic pathways and/or generation of oncometabolites is commonly observed in cancer cells and remains a major focus for therapeutic intervention4. Further, epigenetics is inextricably linked to metabolism since most epigenetic writers require metabolite cofactors derived via cellular carbon metabolism 5,6.

Methionine is an essential amino acid and part of one-carbon metabolism. It is obtained through the diet and found in high concentrations in some meats, eggs and nuts. Homocysteine can also be recycled by methionine synthase to regenerate methionine7. The methionine-derived one-carbon unit is important for purine and thymidine biosynthesis, while its sulfur supports antioxidant defense via incorporation into cysteine and glutathione8. Methionine is also important for global methylation through MAT2A-mediated generation of S-adenosyl methionine (SAM), the universal methyl donor for most cellular methyltransferases9. While methionine is important for cancer progression, its role is not completely understood. For example, tumor cells upregulate methionine transporters, like SLC43A1, to outcompete neighboring cells for limited methionine10. Further, the rate of methionine uptake can predict therapeutic response in multiple myeloma patients11. Cancer stem cells were found to have higher activity of the methionine cycle, leading to increased methionine consumption and are exquisitely susceptible to MAT2A inhibition12. One-carbon metabolism is the target of cancer chemotherapies, such as 5 fluorouracil (5-FU), and the targeting of methionine metabolism has revealed its importance for the progression of several types of cancers and leukemias 8,13,14. Indeed, dietary methionine restriction inhibits the growth of colorectal and breast cancer and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) in immune-compromised xenograft mouse models 15–18. Therapeutic targeting of one-carbon metabolism has focused on enzymes like MAT2A in hepatocellular carcinoma and colon cancer19, SHMT2 in hepatocellular carcinoma20 and MTHFD2 in AML21. However, biologic challenges remain for the therapeutic targeting of methionine metabolism for cancer treatment. For example, methionine restriction using immunocompetent mouse models accelerates intestinal tumor growth and impairs anti-tumor immune response22. This occurs, in part, due to tumor cells outcompeting cytotoxic CD8+ T cells for available methionine, which lowers intracellular SAM, resulting in loss of demethylation of H3K79 (H3K79me2). This leads to decreased expression of STAT5 and impaired T-cell-mediated tumor immunity10. Thus, other metabolic pathways may offer more tumor-specific targeting.

Choline offers an alternate supply chain for one-carbon metabolism via conversion to betaine and then methionine23. Choline uptake and metabolism are essential for phospholipid generation and cell membrane synthesis through the Kennedy pathway. Choline is also important for the generation of acetylcholine in cholinergic neurons24. Abnormal choline phospholipid metabolism, termed “cholinic phenotype”, is a hallmark of tumorigenesis associated with increased proliferation and tumor progression24. Choline uptake is accelerated in solid tumors and leukemia by overexpression of the CTL/SLC44 family of choline transporters, most notably CTL1/SLC44A1 25–27. SLC44A1 is responsible for increased choline uptake observed in lung adenocarcinoma, colon carcinoma and leukemia 26,28. Once choline enters the cell, it is phosphorylated by choline kinase alpha (CHKA) in the Kennedy pathway, which promotes synthesis of phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine vital to cell membrane integrity25. Sustained expression of CHKA plays a role in oncogenesis by activating signaling pathways and promoting metastasis24 and is overexpressed in breast, prostate, colon and lung cancer 29,30. Importantly, the expression of CHKA has been correlated with patient survival31. Inhibition of CHKA induces apoptosis in T-ALL cells and impairs ovarian cancer but not normal ovarian cells 32,33. Thus, CHKA has been proposed as a therapeutic target, and preclinical studies established an important role for CHKA in mouse xenograft solid tumor models34.

Intracellular choline can also contribute to methionine metabolism. Choline dehydrogenase (CHDH) converts intracellular choline to betaine, which enters the methionine cycle following conversion to methionine by betaine-homocysteine S-methyltransferase (BHMT)35. The only source of betaine is via choline oxidation by CHDH. The expression of CHDH and BHMT is primarily found in the liver and kidney to support higher demands for SAM-dependent methylation 36,37. However, choline oxidation to betaine has also been detected in tumor cells, although its role is unknown35. Consistent with tumor cells diverting choline to betaine and methionine, AML cells that experience methionine restriction decrease phosphocholine synthesis without affecting total intracellular choline15. Further, altered dietary choline uptake impacts the epigenetic landscape and cancer development, consistent with the use of choline to supplement methionine metabolism38. Recently, betaine-dependent one-carbon metabolism was found to be utilized by ground-state pluripotent stem cells, and inhibition of BHMT decreases global H3R17me2a and differentiation39. It is also intriguing that BHMT null mice display altered DNA methylation profiles consistent with choline-dependent SAM production40. In fact, the anti-apoptotic gene, MCL1, which we and others have shown to be important in acute myeloid leukemia 41,42, is differentially methylated in the livers of Bhmt knock-out animals40. Importantly, Chdh knock out in mice does not impact development with the exception of diminished sperm motility, indicating that its function is dispensable43. Similarly, Bhmt knockout mice are viable while accumulating betaine in most tissues. Bhmt knock out mice that are >1 year old eventually developed hepatic neoplastic lesions44. These data suggest metabolic plasticity in primitive pluripotent cells that may be commandeered by AML cells and targeted therapeutically with minimal toxicity.

Results

The choline transporter SLC44A1 is epigenetically regulated by SETDB1 and affects leukemic cell proliferation

To identify genes that contribute to leukemogenesis in vivo, we used a mouse model of epigenetically mediated suppression of disease. We previously showed that overexpression of the H3K9 methyltransferase, SETDB1, suppresses leukemogenesis in vivo45. We interrogated our RNA-seq, ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq data following SETDB1 overexpression and identified 359 potential direct target genes (Fig. 1a). These genes were then examined for their importance in leukemia cell proliferation using a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 negative selection screen carried out in the MV4;11 human leukemia cell line46. To identify those genes most essential for leukemic proliferation, we focused on those that ranked in the top 3000 in at least one replicate of the CRISPR-Cas9 negative selection screen, which resulted in 39 candidate genes (Fig. 1b)46. To evaluate the significance of the top hits in leukemic cell proliferation, we performed an arrayed CRISPR-Cas9 screen. Human leukemic cell lines; MV4;11, K562 and KOPN8 constitutively expressing SpCas9 (Supplementary Fig. 1a), were transduced with six unique guide RNAs (sgRNA) targeting each of the 39 genes. The effect of CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene targeting on cell survival was measured by GFP signal over 21 days. Cells transduced with positive control guides targeting RPS11 dropped out within 21 days, while negative control sgRNA targeting luciferase did not result in significant effects (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Among the 39 targets, we found sensitivity of cell lines targeting genes including HOXA9, MEIS1, and SOX4, which have been previously implicated in leukemogenesis 47–49. Interestingly, we also observed multiple solute transporters important for leukemic cell survival. Among these, we found SLC44A1 (CTL1), a choline transporter, important for leukemic cell growth (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Indeed, Slc44a1 displayed epigenetic and transcriptional responsiveness to SETDB1 overexpression in murine MLL-AF9 leukemic cells, including reduced H3K9ac, chromatin accessibility and transcription (Fig. 1c). To explore the sensitivity of cell lines to loss of SLC44A1, we performed CRISPR-Cas9 dropout assays in MV4;11 and K562 cell lines. CRISPR-Cas9-mediated targeting of SLC44A1 was confirmed by analyzing the cell surface protein expression of CTL1/CD92 (protein name for SLC44A1) (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Reflecting the initial screen, we found that MV4;11 proliferation is mildly sensitive to SLC44A1 targeting with more modest effects on K562 proliferation (Fig. 1d).

SLC44A1 is elevated and important for AML progression

Sensitivity to SLC44A1 targeting led us to explore the expression of the SLC44A gene family members A1-5 in leukemic cells. A meta-analysis revealed that only SLC44A1 and SLC44A2 are predominantly expressed in most human leukemia cell lines (Fig. 2a). Compensation by other family members may account for modest proliferation defects following CRISPR targeting of SLC44A1 above (Fig. 1d). Next, we performed qRT-PCR expression analysis of the SLC44A family in leukemic MV4;11, MOLM13, KOPN8 and K562 cells compared with cord blood derived CD34+ hematopoietic progenitors. SLC44A1 expression is significantly elevated in MV4;11, MOLM13 and KOPN8 cell lines compared to CD34+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 2a). SLC44A1 protein expression on the cell surface was also elevated in leukemic cell lines compared to CD34+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 2b). We next analyzed the expression of the SLC44A family using 5 AML patient samples and one B-ALL patient sample compared to CD34+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Notably, we observed elevated expression of SLC44A1 and/or SLC44A2 in all AML and ALL leukemia patient samples compared to CD34+ cells (Fig. 2b, c). Consistent with the expression analysis, cell-surface expression of SLC44A1 was elevated in all primary AML and ALL leukemia samples compared to CD34+ cells as detected by flow cytometry (Fig. 2d, e).

Analysis of AML patient survival found higher expression of SLC44A1 trends with poorer survival rates (Fig. 2f). To test this directly, we performed xenotransplantation assays with MV4;11 cells following sgRNA targeting. As predicted, CRISPR targeting of SLC44A1 extended MV4;11 disease latency compared to control ROSA26 targeting (Fig. 2g). The median survival for mice receiving SLC44A1-targeted MV4;11 cells was extended by ten days compared to ROSA26-targeted mice (ROSA26 = 72 days; SLC44A1 = 82 days). Most mice eventually succumbed to leukemia with characteristic splenomegaly and CD45+ cell occupancy in the bone marrow (Supplementary Fig. 2d–f). These data demonstrate that primary human leukemia cells display elevated levels of SLC44A1 compared to normal CD34+ cells, which plays a role in leukemia progression in vivo.

CHKA plays an important role in leukemogenesis

Intracellular choline is primarily shuttled through the Kennedy pathway for phosphatidylcholine synthesis (Fig. 3a). Given that cancer cells display elevated choline metabolism characterized by increased phosphocholine24, we hypothesized that our leukemic cell lines may be sensitive to genetic disruption of CHKA. We found CRISPR-Cas9 mediated targeting of CHKA disrupted proliferation of MV4;11, K562 and KOPN8 cells (Fig. 3b, Supplemental Fig. 3a). CHKA targeting for sgRNAs was confirmed in MV4;11 and K562 cells by TIDE analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3b). We also found CHKA expression was significantly elevated in AML patients compared to normal bone marrow tissue (Fig. 3c). We next performed xenograft transplantation of K562-Cas9 cells into NCG mice following CRISPR targeting of CHKA or control ROSA26. While all but one control animal receiving ROSA26-targeted K562 cells succumbed to disease, all mice receiving CHKA-targeted K562 cells survived until the experimental endpoint at 142 days (Fig. 3d). Disease was confirmed by detection of K562-derived hALU elements from bone marrow of moribund mice (Supplementary Fig. 3c). We also performed xenograft transplantation of MV4;11-Cas9 cells in NCG mice following sgCHKA targeting. Again, targeting of CHKA extended MV4;11 mediated disease latency (Supplementary Fig. 3d). These data point to an important role for CHKA in acute leukemia.

We next aimed to inhibit CHKA with the small molecule, RSM-932A, which entered phase1 clinical trials for the treatment of solid tumors (NCT number: NCT01215864)34. We first assayed the response of leukemic cell lines to RSM-932A in vitro and found inhibition of cellular proliferation of MV4;11, MOLM13 and K562 and induction of apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 3e, f). We next tested whether RSM-932A affects leukemogenesis using MV4;11 xenotransplantation assays in NCG mice, which was administered at a concentration of 7.5 mg/kg after 12 days. The treatment regimen was 5 days dosing, 9 days rest, followed by another 5 days of dosing as described in pre-clinical studies34. Disappointingly, RSM-932A administration did not provide a survival advantage with all mice appearing moribund, necessitating sacrifice by day 32 of treatment (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. 3g). It will be important for future studies to determine why RSM-932A failed to extend disease latency when inducing cell death in vitro. Of note, we observed restless behavior in mice treated with RSM-932A compared to vehicle control.

Methionine is elevated in leukemic cells and affects disease latency

We next aimed to capture a metabolic profile comparison of leukemic to normal stem/progenitor cells. We performed an unbiased NMR-based metabolomic analysis on extracts prepared from murine lineage-depleted hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) and murine leukemic cells transformed by the MLL-ENL fusion (Fig. 4a). Multivariate analysis confirmed the clustering of metabolomic profiles for the two groups in this study (Supplementary Fig. 4a). We predicted that intracellular choline and phosphocholine may be elevated in the leukemic cells. While several metabolites displayed differential concentrations in leukemic cells (Supplementary Data 1), choline or phosphocholine were not significantly altered (Fig. 4b). This likely reflects the rapid metabolism of choline through the Kennedy pathway or one-carbon metabolism50. A variable importance of projections plot identified metabolic variables that separated HSPCs and murine AML cells. Amongst pathways commonly misregulated in cancer (e.g., glutamate), we found cysteine and methionine metabolism as one of the most altered pathways in leukemic cells (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Data 1). We found homocysteine and methionine were significantly increased in leukemic cells compared to normal HSPCs (Fig. 4d). Methionine metabolism is central to several critical cellular functions including glutathione and polyamine biosynthesis and methylation metabolism (Fig. 3a)9. The methionine cycle includes the generation of SAM from methionine and ATP by methionine adenosyltransferase II alpha (MAT2A). SAM is utilized in methylation reactions producing S-adenosyl homocysteine (SAH), which is converted to homocysteine and back to methionine by AHCY and methionine synthase using methyl-tetrahydrofolate (5-methyl-THF) as a methyl donor (Fig. 3a). However, an alternative supply chain for the methionine cycle involves CHDH and BHMT, which recycle homocysteine to methionine using betaine (Fig. 3a). This prompted us to hypothesize that increased choline transport in leukemic cells, facilitated by increased SLC44A1 expression, is used to satisfy the high methionine demand via CHDH and BHMT-mediated oxidation of choline to betaine to methionine (Fig. 3a). Recent studies show that dietary methionine restriction inhibits colorectal carcinoma and AML in xenograft assays using immune deficient animals15,16. Given the importance of these studies from a therapeutic perspective, we tested how methionine restriction would affect AML in an immunocompetent model. Using a syngeneic transplantation system, we injected C57Bl/6 derived MLL-ENL leukemic cells into C57Bl/6 recipient mice that received a normal (0.86% methionine) or methionine-restricted (0.12% methionine) diet. To our surprise, we found that methionine restriction accelerated murine MLL-ENL leukemia (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 4b). All the mice fed a diet containing 0.12% methionine succumbed to leukemia within 24 days compared 35 days for mice fed a normal 0.86% methionine diet. We performed a similar experiment using a NOTCH1-induced model, using a C57Bl/6 syngeneic model and same diet regimen51. Again, methionine restriction accelerated leukemic disease onset in comparison to mice fed a standard methionine diet (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 4c). Recent studies showed that methionine restriction accelerates colorectal carcinoma, in part, due to impaired T cell abundance22. We examined T and B cells from the bone marrow of our leukemic mice fed a normal or reduced methionine diet and failed to detect a significant difference in CD19 B cells or CD3, CD4 or CD8 T cells (Supplementary Fig. 4d). Thus, methionine restriction accelerates both AML and T-ALL progression in immunocompetent animals.

BHMT and CHDH are induced in cancer cells upon methionine restriction

We next investigated how cancer cells respond to methionine depletion, as found in a competitive microenvironment. First, we assayed leukemic cell growth in vitro using MV4;11, MOLM13 and K562 cells at decreasing methionine concentrations. All cells displayed sensitivity to methionine deprivation, albeit to different degrees (Fig. 5a, b, Supplementary Fig. 5a). Choline can be metabolized to betaine, which acts as a methyl donor to re-methylate homocysteine and replenish the cellular methionine pool (Fig. 3a). This occurs through the enzymatic activity of BHMT and CHDH. However, BHMT and CHDH expression remains low in leukemic cells, likely due to the high abundance of methionine in culture media, negating the need for alternative methionine supply pathways. We hypothesized that BHMT and CHDH are induced in cancer cells upon methionine deprivation. Indeed, we observed a significant dose-dependent upregulation of both BHMT and CHDH in MV4;11, MOLM13 and K562 cells upon lowering the extracellular methionine concentration in media (Fig. 5c, d, Supplementary Fig. 5b). Notably, both BHMT and CHDH expression is induced when medium methionine concentrations were below normal plasma concentrations of 13-45 μM52. To understand whether this is a general phenomenon in cancer cells we tested BHMT and CHDH expression in a prostate cancer cell line (LNCAP), breast cancer cell line (MFM223) and an Ewing sarcoma line (A673). Both BHMT and CHDH are significantly transcriptionally induced upon methionine restriction in LNCAP and MFM223 cells (Fig. 5e, f) but not in A673 cells (Supplementary Fig. 5c). These data suggest that cancer cells respond differently to methionine deprivation by inducing the expression of BHMT and CHDH to establish an alternative supply for methionine from choline.

Inhibition of BHMT and CHDH suppresses leukemogenesis

We next asked whether BHMT and CHDH were important for leukemic cell growth. CRISPR-mediated targeting of BHMT or CHDH led to a significant decrease in the growth of MV4;11-Cas9 cells with less significant changes observed in K562-Cas9 cells (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). CRISPR targeting was confirmed by TIDE analysis in MV4;11 and K562 cells (Supplementary Fig. 6c). To address decreased sensitivity of K562 cells to CHDH and BHMT targeting compared to MV4;11, we investigated compensatory supplies for one-carbon metabolism. Indeed, the folate transporter, FOLR, is highly expressed in K562 cells, which may negate the need for the choline/betaine supply chain (Supplementary Fig. 6d). To evaluate the importance of BHMT and CHDH in vivo, we performed a leukemogenesis assay by targeting BHMT or CHDH in MV4;11-Cas9 cells and transplantation into NCG mice. We reasoned that in vivo analysis is a more efficient way to interrogate the functionality of these enzymes in a microenvironment with scarce resources where cancer cells can upregulate BHMT and CHDH to supplement methionine production. As predicted, all control mice injected with MV4;11-Cas9 cells transduced with sgROSA26 succumbed to leukemia with a median survival of 72 days (Fig. 6a, b). Strikingly, mice transplanted with MV4;11-Cas9 cells in which BHMT or CHDH were targeted, survived significantly longer than control mice with median survivals of 95 days and 90 days for BHMT and CHDH, respectively (Fig. 6a, b). Splenomegaly was observed in all sacrificed moribund mice (Supplementary Fig. 6e). Notably, one BHMT-targeted mouse was euthanized at the conclusion of the experiment without showing signs of disease or splenomegaly (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Fig. 6e). Based on this data, we conclude that BHMT and CHDH play a critical function for leukemia progression in vivo.

BHMT enables the use of choline as an alternative supply for the methionine cycle

We predicted that inhibition of BHMT in leukemic cells would alter methionine metabolism, the SAM/SAH ratio, and global histone methylation. To test this genetically, we established stable MV4;11, K562 and MOLM13 BHMT-targeted cell lines (BHMTmut), using CRISPR/Cas9 and confirmed targeting by Sanger sequencing of clones (Supplementary Fig. 6f). We transplanted BHMTmut or ROSA26 targeted MV4;11 clones into recipient NCG mice and assessed disease latency. BHMTmut displayed a significantly longer median disease latency of 85 days compared to 50 days for ROSA26 cells (Fig. 6c). We also noted smaller spleen sizes at the time of sacrifice and a lower percentage of hCD45+ cells in the bone marrow of BHMTmut MV4;11 mice compared to ROSA26 (Fig. 6d, e) confirming loss of BHMT impairs MV4;11 cell propagation in the bone marrow. Of note, mice in both groups displayed obvious tumors in the body cavity except for one BHMTmut animal. We cultured control ROSA26-targeted and BHMT-targeted cells in standard (100 μM) and methionine restricted conditions (3.1 μM or 6.3 μM) informed by our analysis described above (Fig. 5). BHMT targeting disrupted the growth of MV4;11, MOLM13 and K562 cells in methionine restricted conditions, consistent with a role for choline/betaine in supporting methionine metabolism (Fig. 6f, g, Supplementary Fig. 6g). Notably, when BHMT-targeted cells were cultured in 100 μM methionine, the growth defect was significantly attenuated (Fig. 6f, g, Supplementary Fig. 6g). This is consistent with a model where choline/betaine-dependent methionine metabolism is not needed when extracellular methionine is abundant. The above cell lines are grown in RPMI culture media containing 21 μM choline, which is similar to the range found in human serum (7.1–20.0 μM)53, suggesting a sufficient concentration in our growth conditions to support methionine production. To understand how loss of BHMT impacts methionine metabolism, we performed metabolic analyses using wild-type and BHMTmut MV4;11 cells grown in methionine-restricted culture medium (3.1 μM) for 24 h. We evaluated spent methionine in the culture media by HPLC and detected lower methionine in culture medium for MV4;11 BHMTmut cells compared to wild type cells (Fig. 6h). The lower methionine concentration in the extracellular medium is consistent with increased methionine consumption by BHMTmut cells compared to wild type cells which instead use choline as an alternative source for methionine production. Consistently, BHMTmut MV4;11 cells displayed increased intracellular methionine concentrations compared to control ROSA26 MV4;11 cells when cultured in restricted methionine (3.1 μM) (Fig. 6i). Further, we observed a dose-dependent increase in label transfer from 13C3-choline to betaine when MV4;11 cells were cultured in reduced methionine (Supplementary Fig. 6h). Despite several attempts we were unsuccessful detecting label transfer to methionine. This may be due to detection limits associated with single 13C transfer. We next asked whether loss of BHMT affects global histone methylation. We collected lysate from control and BHMTmut MV4;11 and K562 cells after methionine restriction. MV4;11 cells displayed reduced global H3K79me2, without appreciable changes in H3K36me3, H3K9me3 and H3K4me3 (Supplementary Fig. 6i). Interestingly, we did not observe alteration in methylation in K562-BHMTmut cells (Supplementary Fig. 6j). Finally, we evaluated therapeutic targeting of BHMT by treating MV4;11 ROSA26 or BHMTmut cells with S-(4-Carboxybutyl)-D, L-homocysteine (CBHcy) during methionine restriction. MV4;11 ROSA26 cells displayed significant growth inhibition in response to 100 μM CBHcy, while MV4;11 BHMTmut cells did not (Fig. 7a). This suggests MV4;11 cell sensitivity to CBHcy is dependent on BHMT. Murine MLL-ENL cells also displayed sensitivity to CBHcy (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Further, treatment of a primary B-ALL patient and AML sample (described in Supplementary Fig. 2) with CBHcy resulted in a dose-dependent induction of apoptosis (Fig. 7B). To understand how inhibition of BHMT impacts colony formation potential of AML cells, we performed semi-solid colony formation assays with patient sample AML-2 or normal CD34+ controls in the presence and absence of CBHcy. We noted a dose-dependent decrease in colony formation capacity of AML-2 in the presence of CBHcy, consistent with an active role for BHMT in maintaining pluripotency of leukemic cells (Fig. 7c). Notably, CD34+ cells displayed reduced sensitivity to CBHcy compared to AML-2 (Fig. 7c, Supplementary Fig. 7b). Together, this data shows that cancer cells utilize BHMT to metabolize choline as an alternative source to supplement the methionine cycle in nutrient deprived states and that BHMT is a viable therapeutic target.

Discussion

Altered cellular metabolism is a hallmark of cancer cell growth that is ripe for exploration and therapeutic development1. Methionine dependence has been described for several tumor models, including AML and colon cancer15,16,22. Cancer stem cells also display a metabolic dependency on methionine12. We found increased methionine and homocysteine in leukemic cells compared to normal hematopoietic cells, consistent with methionine dependence in leukemia (Fig. 4). The prospect of targeting enzymes within the methionine cycle is dampened due to the essential function of methionine in cytotoxic T cells10. Our study revealed an important role for choline transport as an alternative means of supplying methionine in human leukemic patient samples and cell lines through homocysteine remethylation via BHMT (Fig. 1). We also found that CHDH and BHMT, which are the enzymes critical for metabolizing choline to methionine, are rapidly and robustly induced in cancer cells upon methionine starvation, pointing to a metabolic plasticity to circumvent a nutrient deprivation (Fig. 5).

We established an essential role for CHDH and BHMT for leukemic progression in vivo (Fig. 6). These data confirm that although expression of CHDH and BHMT is limited to the liver and kidney in normal tissues, cancer cells can reactivate this pathway to open an alternative supply chain for methionine. BHMT activity is also activated in pluripotent stem cells39, suggesting cancer cells may adopt a more stem cell-like, dynamic metabolic state. It is noteworthy that both Chdh and Bhmt knockout animals are viable, albeit with some developmental defects44. However, no hematopoietic defect was reported in these mice. This suggests the therapeutic targeting of CHDH or BHMT may be well tolerated in patients with minimal effects on cytotoxic T cell activity and general toxicity.

Epigenetic control of gene transcription depends on methylation of histones through the actions of methyltransferases that utilize SAM as a methyl donor. BHMT, which catalyzes the conversion of betaine to methionine, is predominantly expressed in the liver to support increased demand for SAM and methylation36. Of note, Bhmt knock-out results in a 43% decrease in hepatic SAM and a 3-fold increase in SAH44. This is consistent with the utilization of choline to supplement the methionine cycle in hepatic tissue, which has high transmethylation rates, and prompted our investigation of methylation reactions in leukemic cells with and without BHMT (Fig. 7). It was interesting to see a specific reduction in H3K79me2 in MV4;11 cells but no significant changes in K562 cells. It is possible that these two cell lines compensate for methionine restriction using different mechanisms (i.e., increased expression of different nutrient transporters). To this point, K562 cells express the SLC44A1 choline transporters at one of the lowest levels of human leukemia cell lines (Fig. 2). It is also possible that subtle changes in local chromatin architecture are not detected when probing global levels of histone methylation. Further investigation is required to understand the broader impact of BHMT on the epigenetic landscape.

We noted that higher expression of SLC44A1 trends with a poorer prognosis among AML patients (Fig. 2). Mechanistically, we would predict that higher SLC44A1 expression affords leukemic cells increased choline uptake that would support both the Kennedy pathway and the methionine cycle during nutrient starvation. In support of this, SLC44A family members are overexpressed in many solid tumors, including breast cancer, melanoma, renal, colon cancer and leukemia27. In fact, SLC44A1 is responsible for increased choline uptake in lung adenocarcinoma, colon carcinoma and leukemia 26,28. Importantly, transmembrane choline transport has been identified as the rate-limiting step for phosphocholine formation in the Kennedy pathway54. Further, a CRISPR screen found targeting of SLC44A1 sensitized leukemic cells to several AML drugs, suggesting combinatorial targeting may be effective in leukemia patients55. We propose that the aforementioned “cholinic phenotype” includes both increased activity of the Kennedy pathway, as well as, the methionine cycle, which are fueled by increased choline transport.

Methods

Cell culture

All human leukemia cell lines were cultured in standard RPMI-1640 media with 10% FBS and 1% P/S under standard conditions of 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cell lines expressing spCas9 constitutively were generated by lentiviral transduction of Cas9 expression plasmid under blasticidin selection (8 ug/ml). All cell lines but KOPN8, expressing Cas9 were clonally propagated. 293 T cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco), 10% FBS (Corning) and 1% P/S (Gibco) for maintenance and RPMI-1640 (Gibco) media with 10% FBS for production of lentivirus. Mouse leukemic cell lines were cultured in IMDM (Gibco) media with 15% stem cell FBS, 1% P/S and supplemented with 10 ng/ml mouse IL3 (R&D cat#403-ML). Methionine-free RPMI-1640 media (Gibco A14517-01) with added 10% FBS and 1% P/S was supplemented with different concentrations of L-Methionine (Millipore Sigma M5308-10MG). Leukemic patient samples were grown in RPMI-1640 powder media (USBiological C24011201) was supplemented with L-Glutamine (Gibco 25030-081), Folic acid (Millipore Sigma F8758-5G), Cyanocobalamin (Thermofischer 405920010), unlabeled Choline chloride (Thermofischer 110290500) and L-Methionine to produce fully reconstituted appropriate media. Frozen apheresis from normal or leukemia patients were thawed in standard media (RPMI-1640, with 10% FBS and 1% P/S) supplemented with DNAse (Roche Cat#10104159001) and after 24 h healthy cells were isolated using ficoll extraction for downstream processing. MV4;11 and MOLM13 cell lines were kindly gifted by Dr. Tomasz Cierpicki. LNCAP, MFM223 and A673 were gifted from Dr. Abhijit Parolia. 293T, KOPN8 and K562 were gifted from Dr. Jay Hess. All human cell lines were confirmed by STR sequencing at Labcorps and confirmed mycoplasma-free by PCR testing (ABM G238).

Generation of BHMT mutant cell lines

MV4;11, K562 and MOLM13 cells constitutively expressing spCas9 were transduced with small guide RNA targeting BHMT (sgBHMT.1) or ROSA26 locus, using the method described under CRISPR-Cas9 drop-out assay section. Two days following transduction, cells were sorted based on GFP signal and cultured for clonal BHMT mutant lines. Targeting of BHMT was confirmed by Sanger-based sequencing.

CRISPR-Cas9 drop-out assay

293 T cells were plated at an optimal density for 24 h in antibiotic-free media and transfected with plasmids encoding the guide-RNA, lentiviral coat proteins and lentiviral gag-pol using Mirus TransLT transfection reagent (MIR2300) following manufacturer protocol. Antibiotic-free media was replaced with antibiotic-containing media after 6 h of transfection. Supernatants containing virus were collected after 48 hours of incubation and transduction of human leukemic cell lines were performed using standard spinoculation protocol. Briefly, leukemia cells constitutively expressing Cas9 were mixed with the viral supernatant and centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 90 min at 25 °C. Polybrene (Santa Cruz SC134220) was added before centrifugation at a concentration of 5 μg/ml. Cells were then washed off to remove viral supernatant and plated in 96 well plates for subsequent flow cytometry analysis. Data is presented as a normalized value to the first day of flow readings (Days 3 or 5) and then normalized to a non-targeting control. sgRNA sequences are found in Supplementary Data 2.

Flow cytometry

Cells were washed in flow buffer (1X PBS + 2%FBS + 1 mM EDTA) once and resuspended in flow buffer containing DAPI. Flow cytometry analysis was performed using BD Celesta. Flow cytometry-based cell sorting was performed using Sony SH800 Cell Sorter. Antibodies used in this study are human PE-conjugated CD92 antibody (Biolegend 371403) and complementary Isotype control (Biolegend 401207) and human APC-conjugated CD45 antibody (Biolegend 304011) and complementary isotype control (Biolegend 400120). GFP was detected using the FITC channel and TAG-RFP657 was detected on the APC channel. Annexin V staining was performed using Annexin V staining kit (Thermofisher) following the manufacturer's protocol. B and T cell staining was performed with the following antibodies/stains: FITC CD19 (Biolegend 152404), PE/Dazzle 594 CD4 (Biolegend 100566), APC CD3 (Biolegend 100236), AF700 CD8a (Biolegend 100730) and Live/Dead Aqua viability dye (FisherScientific 50-112-1526) and were run on a BD Fortessa.

Cell proliferation

Proliferation assays were performed by seeding 25 × 103 cells/ml in different methionine concentrations. 1 × 105 cells/ml were plated for assaying differential proliferation effects due to BHMT targeting. Leukemic cell lines were treated with RSM-932A (AOBIOUS AOB0228) for 72 h followed by MTT assay. For CBHcy (AOBIOUS AOB2142) treatment, cells were grown in standard or restricted methionine media for one day, and 1 × 105 cells/ml were plated in standard or restricted media with or without CBHcy. Cells were counted using Trypan Blue staining. CBHcy was dissolved in water at a concentration of 3.5 mM. After resuspension the solution was gently agitated for ~45 min at room temperature. Once dissolved, single-use aliquots of the 3.5 mM CBHcy solution were stored at −20 °C.

Western blot

0.5–1×106 cells were lysed in 50–200 μl of SDS sample lamelli buffer. Western blot analysis of histone marks in control and MV4;11 BHMTmut cells following culture in 3.1 μM methionine and protein extract was prepared after 5 days. Cell-lamelli buffer mixture was sonicated for 10 min followed by centrifugation at 10,000 g for 10 min. Top clear sample was used for performing western blot analysis and transferred to PVDF or nitrocellulose membrane. Blots were probed with antibodies against spCas9 (Active Motif 61758), β-Actin (Sigma A2228), histone H3 (Abcam 1791), H3K79me2 (Abcam ab3594), H3K36me3 (Abcam 9050), H3K9me3 (Abcam ab8898), and H3K4me3. Blots were re-probed following stripping (Thermofischer 21059). Secondary antibodies used are anti-mouse (Millipore Sigma 12-349) and anti-rabbit (Sigma SAB3700928). Molecular weight ladders were imaged and aligned with chemiluminescent blots.

Gene transcription analysis

RNA was extracted from cells using RNAeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen 74101) following the standard protocol. cDNA synthesis was performed using Superscript III (Invitrogen 18080051) or Superscript IV (Invitrogen 18091050) First Strand Synthesis system followed by Sybrgreen based qPCR analysis using Applied Biosystems qPCR machine. Primers are listed in Supplementary Data 2.

Leukemogenesis assay

For in vivo assay using CRISPR-Cas9 mediated targeting, GFP-guide RNA transduced cells were sorted on day 2 followed by 0.7-1×106 cells/mouse were transplanted on day 3 into female NOD-Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22/NjuCrl NCG mice (CRL 572). To test the effect of dietary methionine restriction on leukemogenesis, murine MLL-ENL AML cells [Tumor #4 1000 cells/mouse (from previous study56)] and NOTCH1-induced murine T-ALL cells [Tumor #177 10,000 cells/mouse (from previous study 51,57)] from primary leukemogenesis assays were transplanted into female C57BL/6 mice from Taconic or Jackson laboratory. 3 days following injection, mice were either moved to feed on methionine restricted diet (0.12% methionine; Research Diet A11051301B) or on standard methionine diet (0.86% methionine; Research Diet A11051302B). For in vivo experiment testing the effect of RSM-932A administration, 1×106 cells/mouse was transplanted into female NCG mice. Cells were transplanted via tail vein injections. Post transplantation, RSM-932A or DMSO was administered starting at day 13 for 5 consecutive days followed by 9 days of rest, followed by the last five daily doses of the drug. RSM-932A or DMSO was administered via intraperitoneal injections. All experiments were performed in mice of 10–12 weeks old, except one experiment was performed in mice of 8–10 weeks old. All animal studies were approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Animal Care & Use Committee (IACUC). Maximal tumor burden for internal/non-visible tumors followed the ‘ULAM End-Stage Illness Scoring System’. Mice were sacrificed due to liquid tumors at first sign of morbidity including hunched posture, labored breathing and loss of body weight. Maximal tumor burden was not exceeded. Source data for experiments involving animals are found in Supplementary Data 3. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

NMR metabolomic analysis

NMR-based metabolomic analysis was performed on murine hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) and murine AML cell line driven by MLL-ENL fusion (MLL-ENL). For isolation of HSPCs, mice were injected with 5-Fluorouracil (5-FU Millipore Sigma) to enrich for progenitor cells. Five days after 5 FU administration, mice were euthanized to collect femurs and tibiae for isolation of HSPCs. On the same day, we used Easysep mouse HSPC (Stemcell Technologies) kit to isolate cKit+ HSPCs. HSPCs collected from 2 mice were pooled together to get 2×106 cells for one biological replicate. The same number of MLL-ENL cells were used. Five replicates for each cell type were collected, then the cell extract was freeze-dried using the CentriVap benchtop vacuum concentrator (Labconco). The cell extract was reconstituted in a 0.5 mM solution of 4,4-dimethyl-4-silapentane-1-sulfonic acid (DSS) in deuterium oxide (D20, Cambridge Isotopes). High-resolution 1H-NMR spectra of the intracellular metabolites were acquired using an Agilent DD2 600 MHz NMR spectrophotometer, using pulse sequence of nuclear Overhauser effect spectroscopy (TNNOESY), at a temperature of 25 °C, frequency of 599.773 MHz, and 256 scans. The spectra were imported into Spectrus Processor (ACD/Labs 2019.1.1) where the free induction decay (FID) files were Fourier transformed. Spectra were batch processed, and intelligent bucketing was used to generate a table of common integrals. This table was imported into Soft Independent Modeling of Class Analogy (SIMCA) (Version 17.0.2) for multivariate analysis. The FID files obtained in ACD were imported into Chenomx (Chenomx version 8.4). Spectra were individually processed, and metabolites were identified and quantified. Metabolite concentrations obtained from Chenomx were imported into MetaboAnalyst software (version 5.0) to conduct pathway analysis, where the significant and impacted pathways between the two conditions were identified.

Methionine quantitation

For consumption assays, MV4;11 and MV4;11BHMTmut cells were plated in media containing 3.1 μM methionine and media was analyzed after 24 h after cell pellet weight measurement. Methionine was quantified by mixing 100 μL of the conditioned medium with 100 μL of 10% TCA solution and stored at −80 °C. Samples were thawed on ice, vortexed and centrifuged at 12,000x g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was neutralized to pH 7-8 with a saturated K2CO3 solution and diluted 2.5-fold with sodium borate buffer (0.2 M, pH 9.6). The samples (50 μL each) were derivatized by mixing with 25 μL of o-phthaldialdehyde solution (prepared by adding 15 mm o-phthaldialdehyde, 0.2% β-mercaptoethanol (v/v), 10% methanol (v/v) in 0.2 M sodium borate buffer pH 9.6). The stock solution of o-phthaldialdehyde was either prepared fresh or stored for up to one week at −20 °C. The sample was incubated for 1 min at 10 °C. A 10-μl aliquot of the derivatized sample was then injected onto a 4.6 × 150 mm ZORBAX Eclipse XDB-C18 (5 μm) analytical HPLC column (Agilent) and eluted at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The column was eluted with Buffer A (0.1 M sodium acetate in 20% methanol in water, pH 4.75) and Buffer B (0.1 M sodium acetate in 80% methanol in water, pH 4.75) using the following gradient: 0–5 min, 50% B; 5–20 min, linear 50 to 100% B; 20–25 min, 100% B; 25–27 min, linear 100 to 50% B; 27–35 min, 50% B. The eluted peaks were detected by fluorescence at 340 nm excitation and 450 nm emission. For intracellular methionine, cell pellets were resuspended in 1 ml 80% MeOH and lysed for 10 min on ice with vortexing. Following centrifugation, 900 μL of lysate is transferred to fresh tube, processed with speedvac and resuspended in 100 μL 50% MeOH for LCMS as described above. All samples were normalized to cell pellet weight and ROSA26 control.

13C3Tracing

For tracing experiments, MV4;11 cells were prepared as in Methionine quantification above expect cells were grown in choline free medium supplemented with 13C3 choline (MedChem Express; Cat# HY-B1337S4). RPMI 1640 Medium w/o L-Glutamine, L-Methionine, Choline Chloride, Folic Acid, Cyanocobalamin (Powder) was purchased from USBiological Cat# R8999-21 and supplemented with L-Glutamine (Thermofisher Scientific cat# 25030081); Choline Chloride (Thermo Scientific cat# 110290500); Folic Acid (Millipore Sigma cat# F8758-5G); Cyanocobalamin (Thermo Scientific cat# 405920010). Cells were centrifuged in pre-weighed tubes at 350 x g, the pellet was rinsed 2x with PBS and the wet weight of the cell pellet was noted. The cell pellets were then resuspended in 1000 µl 80% methanol, vortexed, and incubated on ice for 5 min. Samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 16,000 x g at 4 °C, then 900 µl of the supernatant was transferred and lyophilized using a SpeedVac concentrator at room temperature for 4 h. Samples were stored at -80 °C prior to LC-MS analysis. Samples were resuspended in 100 µl 50% LCMS-grade methanol, centrifuged at 21,000 x g for 5 min at 4 °C and the supernatants were transferred to LCMS vials for analysis.

LCMS parameters

The Thermo Scientific LC-MS system consists of two Vanquish Horizon UHPLC binary pumps coupled to IQX Orbitrap HRMS, controlled by Excalibur software version 4.6.67.17. A Waters Acquity UPLC BEH amide 1.7 um 2.1 × 100 mm column was used with an affixed Waters UPLC BEH Amide 1.7 um VanGuard pre-column. Solvent A contained 20 mM ammonium acetate in water, pH 9.2. Solvent B contained 100% acetonitrile. The pump seal wash and autosampler wash contained 0.1% formic acid in 50% isopropanol. The needle wash solvent contained 80% acetonitrile, 20% water. The LC flow gradient was as follows: 0.2 ml/min of 85% B at 0–0.5 min, 85–30% B linear gradient from 0.5 to 12 min, hold at 30% B from 12-14 min, 30–85% B linear gradient from 14 to 14.1 min, hold at 85% B from 14.1 to 22 min. The column compartment temperature was 35 °C and the autosampler temperature was 5 °C. The sample injection volume was 3 µL. The IQX Orbitrap source parameters were as follows: H-ESI Positive Static Voltage 3400 V; Negative Static Voltage 3500 V; Sheath Gas (Arb) 40, Aux Gas 5 (Arb), Sweep Gas 1 (Arb); Ion transfer Tube Temp 300 °C and Vaporizer Temp 350 °C. The Orbitrap resolution is 60 K, with a mass scanning range of 65–1000 Da. A centroid data type and polarity switching mode of acquisition was used. Data analysis was performed using Skyline 25.1.0.237. Briefly, peaks were detected and quantified using the following positive mode m/z values for identification with a mass tolerance of 10 ppm: methionine = 150.0588 m/z12,C-5 betaine = 118.0868 m/z, and 13C-3 betaine = 121.0970 m/z. Methionine and betaine peaks were verified by matching the retention times and m/z with neat methionine and 12C-5 betaine standards. The following formula was used to calculate the % 13C-3 label: 100 * 13C-3 peak area/(13C-3 + 12C-5 peak area). The normalized intracellular methionine values were calculated by dividing the methionine peak area by the cellular wet weight, then setting the ROSA26 average value at 1 and reporting the BHMTmut values relative to ROSA26 values.

Primary AML cells and normal cells

All patient samples were collected with informed consent and with approval from the Institutional Review Boards of the University of Michigan Medical School. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed. Patient samples were stored in the AML tissue repository approved by the University of Michigan IRB. Peripheral blood or bone marrow aspirate samples were collected and processed. Leukemic stem cells were enriched by magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS, Miltenyi Biotec) for CD34-positive cells. Enrichment was determined by flow cytometry staining with anti-CD34-PE antibody and ranged from 70% to 98% (Biolegend). Normal CD34-positive cells were obtained from normal donor mobilized peripheral blood or cord blood samples. Normal or leukemic stem cells were plated in Iscove’s medium containing granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GMCSF), 200 pg/mL, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (GCSF) 1 ng/mL, stem cell factor (SCF) 200 pg/mL, Leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) 50 pg/mL, macrophage inflammatory protein a (MIP1α) 200 pg/mL, interleukin 6 (IL-6), 1 ng/mL and 5% BIT (StemCell Technologies). Primary human leukemia patient cells were plated in 1 ml of leukemia growth supporting media with or without CBHcy. After 48 to 96 h, cells were collected and stained with Annexin V and DAPI for flow cytometry analysis. Colony formation assays were performed by plating 40,000 primary human leukemia cells per well. Vehicle or drug and cells and FBS (5%) were mixed with human methylcellulose enriched media (R&D Systems 390398) and 1 ml was plated per well in a 12 well plate.
