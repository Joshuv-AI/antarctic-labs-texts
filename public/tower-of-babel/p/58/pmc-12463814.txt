Recent development in the synthesis of twisted Van der Waals heterostructures
Authors: Sourav Bhakta, Kushal Mazumder, Mukesh Kumar, Pramoda K. Nayak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Twisted van der Waals (t-vdW) heterostructures (HSs), where the electronic and optical properties can be modulated by the interlayer twist angle, have led to the emerging field of “Twistronics.” By precisely controlling the twist angle in stacked vdW materials, researchers are uncovering novel properties and quantum phenomena such as superconductivity, magnetism, and unique charge transport behaviors. This review presents recent advancements in the growth and synthesis of t-vdW HSs focusing mostly on chemical vapor deposition (CVD) and mechanical exfoliation (ME) along with some other techniques including Metal Organic CVD (MOCVD) and molecular beam epitaxy (MBE). We discuss various methods used to control the twist angles, including mechanical stacking and rotational assembly. Each technique’s strengths and limitations are evaluated, particularly in the context of producing high-quality HSs with tunable properties. Special attention is given to optimizing CVD processes to achieve reproducible growth of twisted HSs with precise twist angles. Additionally, this review also explores the theoretical and experimental insights into the influence of twist angles on physical, optical, and electronic properties of vdW HSs. By examining the progress and challenges in this field, we highlight future directions and the potential of t-vdW HSs in advancing next-generation opto-electronic and quantum devices.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Introduction to VdW HSs

Layered vdW materials like graphene, transition metal dichalcogenide (TMD), hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN), etc. are unique because they consist of extended two-dimensional (2D) crystalline planes held together by weak out-of-plane vdW forces, while the in-plane bonds are strong and covalent. This allows individual layers to be easily separated without damaging the overall structure, making them highly versatile for various applications. For instance, graphene layers can be peeled off from bulk graphite using a simple adhesive tape, a process known as ME. This ease of separation enables the transfer of these atomically thin layers onto different substrates, opening up possibilities for creating diverse materials and HSs. Although thin layers and multilayers have been used in technology for decades, recent advances have allowed researchers to isolate and manipulate atomically thin layers of various vdW materials with high precision. These advancements have led to the ability to assemble new solids with unique physical properties, such as tunable electronic, optical, and mechanical characteristics, which are not present in the bulk material [1]. While initial interest in “simple graphene” has peaked, the focus has now shifted towards applications and using graphene as a platform for studying different phenomena [2]. Despite this shift, fundamental graphene science, particularly in many-body physics, remains active, and further breakthroughs are anticipated as device quality improves, though at a slower pace [3, 4].

With most of graphene’s easily accessible discoveries already made, attention has gradually turned to other 2D materials like h-BN [5], Molybdenum Disulfide (MoS2) [6], and other dichalcogenides [7]. Research in these materials is intensifying, especially as their electronic quality continues to improve. A new research field focusing on vdW HSs has emerged alongside graphene research [8]. This approach involves stacking different 2D crystals, layer by layer, to create custom artificial materials with atomic-scale precision, similar to building with Lego. VdW HSs of 2D materials are created by vertically stacking atomically thin layers on top of each other, held together by vdW interactions [9]. This stacking technique allows the formation of both homo- and heterobilayers (HBLs) i.e. HSs, which retain the properties of the individual monolayers while introducing new characteristics by combining the benefits of different materials. In the last decade, the ability to create such HSs has been demonstrated [10–12], and the technique has proven to work remarkably well, surpassing expectations.

Twisted VdW HSs (Twistronics)

By stacking different 2D materials with controlled twist angles, researchers can now tailor properties like superconductivity, magnetism, and charge transport, leading to the exploration of novel quantum phenomena [13]. This growing ability to control the composition and orientation of these layered materials has broad implications for future technologies, including flexible electronics, photonics, quantum devices, and more [14–16]. In HSs of 2D materials, electronic properties can change significantly with the interlayer twist angle, enabling a new class of twistable electronics where properties are tuned by rotation [17]. The weak vdW forces between layers allow devices to be fabricated with arbitrary rotational alignment, giving control over electronic behavior through the twist angle [18]. When interlayer coupling is weak, each layer retains its intrinsic properties with minimal interaction from stacking [19]. This twist-induced coupling is crucial in defining the characteristics of twisted 2D materials, sparking the development of the subfield known as ‘Twistronics’ [20]. Twistronics explores how twist angles in stacked 2D materials affect their properties and functionalities, offering unique opportunities to customize and fine-tune material characteristics. Interestingly, a new type of pattern called as Moiré patterns in t-vdW HSs arise from the interference between the atomic lattices of stacked 2D materials when they are slightly misaligned by a twist angle or have a lattice mismatch, creating a new periodic superlattice that modifies the material’s properties [21, 22]. The concept of the moiré superlattice arises from in-plane periodic variations due to atomic alignment between the top and bottom layers [23, 24]. This occurs as a result of rotational misalignment or lattice mismatch between the layers, creating a powerful platform for studying new physical phenomena and precisely engineering material properties. Although the periodic moiré pattern in bilayer graphene was observed a decade ago [25], recent attention has focused on the fascinating effects resulting from moiré structures. These include modulations in electronic band structures [26, 27], formation of localized states [28–30], emergence of long-range periodic potentials [31], altered interlayer coupling [32, 33], and unconventional superconductivity [34, 35] and topological conducting channels [36, 37] in strongly correlated regimes [38]. A key feature of t-vdW materials is their high tunability, setting them apart from their pristine forms. Even without strong moiré effects, the properties of the stacked structure can be significantly modified by adjusting the twist angle, affecting charge transfer [21], interlayer coupling [39], charge redistribution [40], lattice reconstruction [41, 42], and band hybridization [34, 43].

Synthesis of t-vdW HSs and its importance

Various methods such as mechanical stacking, liquid-phase exfoliation [44–46], molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) [47, 48], pulsed laser deposition (PLD) [49], ME [50, 51], and CVD [52, 53] have been developed to produce t-vdW HSs. However, mechanical stacking [54] and liquid-phase exfoliation often result in random locations and disordered assembly of 2D layers [55, 56], leading to limited control over stacking orientation and interfacial structures [7, 57, 58]. MBE and PLD, while promising, still face challenges in achieving universal applicability across different types of 2D HSs [59, 60]. Among these, CVD has shown significant potential due to its ability to offer controlled synthesis of various 2D materials [61–63]. Advances in the CVD process have allowed for precise control over key factors such as growth time, gas flow rate, temperature, precursors, and substrates [64–66]. These variables play a crucial role in determining the size, number of layers, stacking or merging modes, twist angles, and spatial distribution of 2D HSs [67]. As a result, CVD has emerged as a reliable technique for producing 2D HSs with desired properties. However, CVD demonstrates a relatively low degree of controllability and reproducibility [68–70]. Metal Organic CVD (MOCVD) enables uniform, large-area growth of 2D HSs with precise control over precursor partial pressures, layer thickness, and composition [71]. Its ability to switch metal-organic precursors ensures sharp, contamination-free interfaces and tunable superlattice periodicities, surpassing orthodox CVD capabilities. In contrast, MBE grows 2D materials by evaporating high-purity elemental sources in ultra-high vacuum [72], achieving monolayer precision with advantages like controlled doping [73], metastable phase synthesis [74], epitaxial alignment, in situ electron diffraction monitoring, and excellent crystalline quality [75]. However, there are no reports available to the best of our knowledge to fabricate t-vdW HSs using MOCVD and MBE.

In addition, several advanced techniques are employed to fine-tune the twist angles and structural alignment of 2D layers. These include (i) optical alignment of crystal edges [76–78], (ii) rotational alignment during assembly [79], (iii) self-rotation through thermal annealing [80, 81], (iv) in-situ rotation of 2D layers [17], and (v) tuning homo or heterosite nucleation by pulsed carrier gas flow [82–84]. These techniques are particularly useful for designing twisted HSs, such as twisted graphene/h-BN, graphene/TMD, TMD/h-BN, TMD/TMD which are studied for twist-dependent quantum phenomena. Over time, these approaches have evolved from static analyses to enable dynamic twist angle manipulation, allowing deeper exploration of moiré physics in t-vdW HSs.

In recent years, the integration of t-vdW materials into practical devices has shown great promise due to the unique properties emerging from moiré superlattices. These materials have broadened the scope of quantum science, sensor technology, and photonics, leading to the creation of devices with unprecedented functionalities. T-vdW materials offer new opportunities in quantum technologies, including quantum communication [85], simulation [86], and sensing. Their diverse physical and mechanical properties, atomically thin nature, and ease of integration enable the development of 2D quantum devices with exceptional capabilities. Additionally, these materials are highly sensitive to external stimuli, making them ideal for applications in biosensors [87] and other sensor technologies [88, 89]. They also show potential in gas and chemical sensors due to their high surface area, tunable bandgaps, and crystal structures, offering high sensitivity and selectivity [90, 91]. Beyond these applications, t-vdW materials are useful in technologies like ultrafast modulators [92], LEDs [93], lasers [94, 95], and photodetectors [96], further expanding their impact across various fields.

Objectives of the review

The objective of this review is to provide a thorough and detailed examination of the growth techniques used for synthesizing t-vdW HSs, focusing on their significance and applications in emerging fields such as electronics, optoelectronics, and quantum technologies. This review will systematically analyze the synthesis methods for monolayer vdW materials, particularly through ME and CVD, and how these techniques contribute to the fabrication of twisted HSs. Furthermore, it will explore the different transfer processes, including wet and dry transfer methods, as well as in-situ growth techniques, and discuss their roles in achieving precise control over the twist angle and interfacial alignment of stacked layers. Also it will cover the MOCVD and MBE growth of 2D vdW HSs in brief. By evaluating both theoretical and practical aspects, this review will offer insights into the advantages and limitations of these methods, while addressing the current challenges in optimizing the growth of t-vdW HSs. Ultimately, the review aims to highlight future prospects and the potential impact of these structures in next-generation technologies.

Synthesis techniques for VdW HSs

Since the successful exfoliation of graphene by Novoselov et al. in 2004 [97], various synthesis methods for vdW materials have emerged, broadly categorized into top-down and bottom-up approaches. Top-down techniques, such as mechanical, liquid-phase, and electrochemical exfoliation, involve breaking down bulk materials, while bottom-up methods, including CVD, epitaxial growth, and wet chemical synthesis, build 2D materials from atomic or molecular precursors. Bottom-up methods are more scalable for mass production. Among these, ME and chemical CVD are the most widely used techniques [98]. Other bottom-up techniques, such as MOCVD and MBE, offer precise control over composition and interface quality, enabling the growth of uniform, high-purity 2D HSs [71, 72].

ME remains the most effective method for producing highly crystalline, atomically thin nanosheets of layered materials. This process works by overcoming the vdW forces that bind adjacent layers together, allowing the material to be peeled away when the adhesion force of the tape exceeds the surface energy required to create new surfaces. As depicted in Fig. 1a [99], the transfer of monolayer MoS2 nanosheets with clean, smooth surfaces were successfully transferred onto SiO2/Si substrate. The typical detailed mechanism of ME technique is shown in inset of Fig. 1a [100]. The pioneering use of this method was the exfoliation of monolayer graphene from graphite [97], which set a precedent for exfoliating other 2D materials like h-BN, TMDs such as WS2, NbSe2, and MoS2.

Pang et al. [102] have demonstrated an extrinsic corrugation-assisted ME (ECAME) method for synthesizing monolayer graphene on SiO2/Si substrates. This approach involves a straightforward thermal treatment of graphite flakes deposited on a silicon wafer, followed by a tape-peeling process using wafer-processing tape. To evaluate the ECAME process, graphite flakes with lateral dimensions in the tens of micrometres and thicknesses ranging from 10 to 100 nm were transferred onto a pre-cleaned silicon substrate and then thermally annealed at 350 °C for two hours. The AFM images of the monolayer graphene is shown in Fig. 1c. The red square is marked as the interface between substrate and monolayer graphene. Green and blue square demonstrate the SiO2 substrate and monolayer graphene, respectively.

The CVD is crucial for synthesizing high-quality 2D materials due to its ability to produce atomically thin layers with precise control over thickness, composition, and uniformity [103]. It allows for large-area growth, making it ideal for scalable production of materials like graphene, MoS2, and other TMDs. CVD also offers flexibility in tailoring properties by controlling parameters such as temperature, pressure, and precursor materials. This method is vital for synthesizing 2D materials for applications in electronics, optoelectronics, and catalysis, where material quality and reproducibility are essential. Zhou et al. [104] demonstrated the conventional CVD method to synthesize the MoS2 samples under atmospheric pressure. Figure 1d-1 illustrates a schematic representation of the CVD setup [104]. The growth process was carried out inside a 3.0 cm in diameter sized quartz tube, which was positioned within a larger quartz tube of diameter 8.0 cm inside a three-zone tube furnace. For a typical growth procedure, high-purity 18 mg MoO3 powder was taken and kept in a quartz boat located in the central heating zone, while 120 mg sulfur powder was placed in a separate upstream zone. An ultraclean Si/SiO2 substrate was inverted and positioned on a square quartz boat in the downstream zone, as shown in Fig. 1d-1. The central heating zone’s temperature was slowly ramped up to a target value, ranging from 700 °C to 850 °C in increments of 25 °C, over a duration of 60 min. The temperature of the upstream sulfur zone was regulated at approximately 180 °C, as depicted in Figs. 1d and 2 [104]. Once the central zone reached the desired temperature, it was held constant for 20 min before cooling down the system naturally. Throughout the growth process, high-purity argon served as the carrier gas, with the pressure maintained at ambient levels. The gas flow rate was kept constant at 25 sccm. Additionally, to explore the effect of gas flow on the growth process, the carrier gas flow rate was varied between 15 and 50 sccm, in steps of 5 sccm. During these experiments, the temperature in the sulfur zone was maintained at 180 °C, while the central heating zone was set at 750 °C. Figures 1d, 2 and 3 presents a schematic representation of a SiO2/Si substrate used as a base for MoS2 growth, demonstrating the influence of varying temperatures and gas flow rates on the deposition process. Figures 1d, 2, 3 and 4 presents an atomic-scale image of the MoS2 monolayers synthesized through CVD, captured using aberration-corrected annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (ADF-STEM) [105]. In the image, the bright spots represent molybdenum atoms, while the dimmer spots correspond to pairs of stacked sulfur atoms. The lattice structure consists of hexagonal rings, with alternating molybdenum and sulfur atom positions. At this level of resolution, no point defects, atomic substitutions, or voids were detected within the individual crystal domains, indicating the high crystalline quality of the grown MoS2 monolayers.

Nayak et al. illustrates the monolayer growth of MoSe2 of a triangular grain, as shown in AFM image of Fig. 1e [21]. The step height of about 0.8 nm indicates the monolayer growth. Monolayer MoSe2 crystals were synthesized using an atmospheric pressure CVD (APCVD) process. In a typical experiment, 60 mg of MoO₃ powder was placed in a ceramic boat at the center of a 2-inch diameter quartz tube furnace, while another ceramic boat containing 100 mg of Se powder was positioned in the low-temperature upstream zone of the same tube. The sapphire substrate was placed downstream near the MoO3 powder to capture the deposition. During the first stage, the MoO3 was heated to 600 °C at a rate of 25 °C/min, and then further raised to 700 °C within 10 min. Simultaneously, the Se powder was heated to 275 °C using an external heating coil within this 10 min period. In the second stage, the MoO3 and Se temperatures were held at 700 °C and 275 °C, respectively, to promote MoSe2 growth. The vapor-phase reactants were carried by flowing argon gas with a flow rate 60 sccm, while hydrogen gas flow rate at 12 sccm acted as a reductant for selenization, leading to the formation of 2D MoSe2 crystals. Once growth was complete, the furnace was rapidly cooled to room temperature under an argon atmosphere.

Cong et al. demonstrated a CVD method for synthesizing monolayer WS2, as shown in the AFM image in Fig. 1f [106]. In their setup, WO3 powder was placed on a SiO2/Si substrate and covered by another face-down substrate, with sulfur powder placed upstream in a quartz tube. The system was heated in two stages − 550 °C at 20 °C/min and then 750 °C at 3 °C/min, held for 5 min for WS2 growth, and cooled naturally. Argon gas (100 sccm) was used throughout to maintain the reaction atmosphere, resulting in the formation of triangular WS2 monolayer flakes.

Liu et al. [107] grew the monolayer WSe2 using a three-zone furnace setup in the CVD system. Figure 1g presents a summary of the average flake sizes of WSe2 grown at various temperatures, derived from the analysis of several hundred individual flakes [107]. The figure shows that, as the growth temperature increases, there is a corresponding rise in the number of WSe2 layers. At temperatures below 950 °C, the WSe2 flakes are predominantly monolayers. However, when the growth temperature drops below 850 °C, the products primarily consist of small particles with only a few tiny flakes. In contrast, at temperatures equal to or exceeding 1050 °C, the resulting flakes consist of multiple layers. Additionally, some bilayer WSe2 samples were observed in the temperature range between 950 °C and 1050 °C. Hence, the growth temperature plays a significant role in the morphology of the WSe2 flakes.

Liu et al. [108] illustrate the copper-catalysed CVD process to synthesize graphene under atmospheric pressure, employing a gas mixture of Ar, H2, and CH4. The variations in the average domain size of the first-layer graphene domains are depicted in Fig. 1h [108]. As the growth temperature and partial pressure of CH4 increase, the average size of the graphene domains correspondingly becomes large. However, the area density of these domains decreases as the temperature rises and shows little sensitivity to the CH4 partial pressure [108]. In general, higher growth temperatures result in a lower density of graphene nuclei, while promoting the formation of larger domains. The partial pressure of CH4 primarily influences the size of the graphene domains. When both the growth temperature and CH4 partial pressure fall below a certain threshold value, no graphene domains can be observed in OM image (as shown in inset of Fig. 1h). Conversely, the appearance of second-layer graphene domains occurs once the temperature or CH4 partial pressure surpasses the critical point. Therefore, there exists a narrow window in which the growth conditions allow for the graphene domains to maintain a monolayer structure.

Ji et al. [109] discuss the CVD growth process for monolayer, bilayer, and tri-layer h-BN, as well as the stacking of the interlayers. Figure 1i shows the SEM image of the CVD grown single crystal monolayer h-BN domains with a lateral size of approximately 300 μm [109]. The precursor employed for h-BN growth was ammonia borane. The method employed enables precise control over layer thickness and crystal size, crucial for growing high-quality h-BN.

Apart from mechanical stacking and CVD, techniques such as MOCVD and MBE have emerged as versatile methods for the controlled synthesis of vdW HSs. These approaches offer precise control over layer thickness, composition, and interface quality, enabling the fabrication of wafer-scale, high-purity HSs with tailored electronic and optical properties. Metal-organic precursors are highly advantageous for the controlled growth of large-area TMD materials, as their delivery into the CVD chamber can be precisely regulated and maintained over extended durations. Eichfeld et al. [110] successfully demonstrated the growth of WSe2 monolayer and few-layer films with domain sizes reaching 8 μm using MOCVD, employing W(CO)6 and (CH3)2Se as precursors. In a significant advancement, Kang et al. [111] utilized gas-phase Mo(CO)6, W(CO)6, and (C2H5)2S with H2/Ar carrier gas to grow continuous, wafer-scale (4-inch) homogeneous MoS2 and WS2 films via a layer-by-layer growth mode. From these MoS2 films, a batch of 8100 high-performance MoS2 FETs with global back-gate architecture was fabricated using photolithography.

The continuous growth of MoS2/WS2 HSs was attained by Hutten et al. [112] on sapphire substrates using MOCVD in an AIXTRON Planetary hot-wall reactor configured for 10 × 2″ wafers. This technique ensures atomically clean, sharp interfaces and enables the fabrication of homogeneous, wafer-scale devices. To realize a HS suitable for photodetector integration, two distinct TMD materials with different conduction and valence band offsets were selected, facilitating the separation of photogenerated electron-hole pairs across the interface. In this study, a WS2 monolayer was epitaxially grown on the top of a MoS2 monolayer to form the HS. AFM micrograph shown in Fig. 2a confirms the formation of a continuous MoS2 layer fully covering the sapphire substrate, overlaid by triangular WS2 crystallites ranging from 100 to 300 nm in size with a height of (0.6 ± 0.1) nm, indicating predominantly monolayer WS2 and a surface coverage of 60–70%. Such partial coverage when growing TMDs on the top of another 2D material is consistent with previous reports [110]. In Fig. 2b, the PL spectra averaged over 10,000 points within a 30 μm × 30 μm area reveal three emission peaks at 1.810, 1.846, and 1.993 eV. The 1.993 eV peak corresponds to monolayer WS2 [113], closely matching values from MOCVD-grown WS2 [114], while the lower-energy emissions originate from the underlying MoS2 monolayer. These can be deconvoluted into a neutral exciton at 1.846 eV and a trion at 1.810 eV [115]. The overall red-shift of MoS2 emissions compared to isolated monolayers is attributed to changes in dielectric screening induced by the stacked WS2 top layer [116]. Recently, Xie et al. [117] demonstrated the synthesis of coherent monolayer WS2-WSe2 superlattices using a modulated MOCVD process, achieving precise control over both the supercell dimensions and lattice coherence. In this work, the MOCVD system proved essential for enabling tunable and spatially controlled growth by accurately regulating the flow and switching of gaseous precursors. By adjusting the type and concentration of precursors during growth, the spatial arrangement and periodicity of the WS2-WSe2 superlattices could be directly modulated, offering exceptional control over the HS architecture. As shown in Fig. 2c, annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (ADF-STEM) imaging at the WS2/WSe2 heterointerface reveals an atomically sharp, coherent junction without any observable misfit dislocations, confirming the formation of a high-quality, single-crystalline monolayer HS.

MBE is a highly precise, ultra-high-vacuum technique crucial for the growth of high-purity, atomically sharp 2D materials and their HSs, offering supreme control over thickness, composition, and interface quality. MBE grows 2D materials by evaporating high-purity elemental sources in an ultra-high vacuum, allowing atoms to condense layer-by-layer on a heated substrate with precise control over growth parameters. Its ability to fabricate lattice-mismatched and strain-free vdW HSs makes it indispensable for exploring novel quantum phenomena and device applications. Nie et al. [118] synthesized a CrTe2/Bi2Te3 HS using a standard MBE process within an ultrahigh vacuum chamber maintained at a base pressure of 5 × 10− 10 Torr. Initially, the SiC substrate underwent a degassing step at 600 °C for 3 h, followed by annealing at 950 °C under a Si flux for ten cycles, and a final flash to 1400 °C for 10 min to achieve a clean, well-ordered surface. High-purity Bi (99.999%) and Te (99.999%) sources were then co-evaporated from custom-built thermal effusion cells, maintaining a high Te: Bi flux ratio of ~ 15. The graphene-coated SiC substrate was held at 260 °C during Bi2Te3 growth, monitored in situ via infrared spectrometry with an emissivity setting of 0.9. Post-growth, the Bi2Te3 films were annealed at the growth temperature for 5 min under a Te flux to enhance crystallinity, confirmed by STM characterization prior to CrTe2 deposition. Subsequently, CrTe2 layers were grown by co-evaporating high-purity Cr (99.995%) and Te (99.999%) with a Te: Cr flux ratio of ~ 20, while maintaining the substrate temperature at 300 °C. Figure 2d presents a simulated moiré pattern for the CrTe2/Bi2Te3 HS, exhibiting a periodicity of 3.5 nm, obtained by superimposing the atomic lattices of Bi2Te3 and CrTe2. A closer inspection of the CrTe2 surface reveals distinct periodic patterns corresponding to atomically sharp interfaces between the CrTe2 and Bi2Te3 layers, as evident in the STM image shown in Fig. 2e. The atomically resolved STM measurements indicate a moiré periodicity of 3.50 ± 0.02 nm, which precisely matches nine times the CrTe2 lattice constant (0.389 × 9 = 3.501 nm) and eight times the Bi2Te3 lattice constant (0.438 × 8 = 3.504 nm), unambiguously confirming the formation of a well-defined moiré superlattice in the CrTe2/Bi2Te3 HS. Besides, vdW epitaxy is a highly adaptable thin-film growth technique that facilitates the fabrication of 2D HSs by depositing layered materials onto substrates without the necessity for strict lattice matching. This approach takes advantage of weak interlayer vdW interactions at the interface, enabling the formation of high-quality, atomically sharp HSs with varied lattice constants and minimal strain. Barton et al. [119] demonstrated the growth of monolayer HfSe2 on various substrates using MBE, confirming that vdW epitaxy supports the formation of defect-free, strain-free thin films even with substantial lattice mismatches of up to 41%. As shown in Fig. 2f, a high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) image of HfSe2 grown on MoS2 clearly displays distinct, well-ordered HfSe2 layers on the top of MoS2 without observable dislocations, grain boundaries, stacking faults, or antisite defects.

Synthesis of t-vdW HSs

T-vdW HSs have become a prominent research focus in materials science, owing to their distinctive optoelectronic properties and the emergence of exotic phases, including topological behaviour [9, 22]. The development of “twistronics” [17, 18] has further advanced this field by leveraging moiré patterns generated through slight misalignments between vdW layers to enable precise tuning of the band structure via controlled twist angles. These HSs are formed by stacking 2D materials at specific rotational angles.

Fabrication of t-vdW HSs from ME flakes using the transfer method

Mechanically exfoliated 2D flakes can be assembled into t-vdW HSs using dry or wet transfer methods. The wet transfer method is widely favoured for its gentle handling of delicate materials. It involves using a polymer support layer, typically polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA). As illustrated in Fig. 3a [120], the process begins by spin-coating the 2D nanosheet with PMMA, followed by baking at an elevated temperature to evaporate residual solvents. The PMMA-coated substrate, with the 2D nanosheet, is then immersed in deionized (DI) water (Fig. 3a(i)). As water penetrates the interface between the hydrophilic substrate (SiO2/Si) and the hydrophobic PMMA, the PMMA/2D nanosheet detaches and floats on the water’s surface (Fig. 3a(ii)). This floating stack is carefully adhered to a glass transfer slide (Fig. 3a(iii)), which was clamped onto the arm of a micromanipulator mounted on an optical microscope. Using the microscope to optically locate the position of the 2D nanosheet on the suspended polymer film, the nanosheet was precisely aligned to the target hBN and carefully transferred at the desired position, followed by mild baking to eliminate trapped moisture and gases. Finally, acetone dissolves the PMMA layer, revealing the assembled h-BN/Graphene vdW HS (Fig. 3a(iv)). Using the above mentioned transfer process, Fang and co-workers have examined the optical transition properties of the WSe2/MoS2 heterojunction (Inset of Fig. 3(a)) [121]. The image captures the HS formed by mechanically exfoliating MoS2 and WSe2, followed by the transfer of WSe2 onto MoS2. For more precise twist-angle control and contamination-free interfaces, dry transfer and sequential pick-up methods are frequently adopted. In Figs. 3(b) and 3(c), monolayer MoSe2, WS2, and few-layer hBN were mechanically exfoliated and sequentially picked up using a polyethylene terephthalate (PET) stamp under an optical microscope. The top hBN, WS2, MoSe2 monolayer, and bottom hBN were stacked in sequence before the complete assembly was released onto a sapphire substrate, followed by PET dissolution in dichloromethane. The resulting hBN-encapsulated WSe2/MoSe2 heterobilayer (Fig. 3(b)) exhibited a twist angle of 58.7° ± 0.7°, determined by angle-dependent second-harmonic generation [122]. This HS shows strong PL from interlayer excitons, ultrafast charge separation, and narrow linewidths (~ 6 meV), leading to valley polarization >80%. In Fig. 3(c), a WS2/MoSe2 HS was carefully aligned to create a moiré superlattice with a spacing similar to the exciton Bohr radius. This alignment enables mixing between intra- and interlayer excitons at small twist angles (θ ≈ 0°), giving normally weak interlayer excitons enough strength to be seen in reflectance and PL measurements. The inset shows an atomic model of the lattice arrangement that defines the moiré unit cell [123].

Precise twist control can also be achieved through in situ rotational tuning, as demonstrated by Yaping and co-workers in the fabrication of an hBN/graphene/hBN heterostructure (Fig. 3(d)). In their approach, a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) hemisphere mounted on a glass slide was used as a manipulator. To restrict contact and prevent accidental flake movement, an epitaxial PMMA patch was patterned directly onto the top hBN using electron beam lithography. This patch confined the PDMS–flake interaction to the targeted region, enabling controlled rotation. The HS was initially assembled with nonzero twist angles (0° < θt, θb < θtb < 60°), the top hBN was then rotated until θt = θb = θtb = 0°, achieving a commensurately aligned “self-locking” state where interlayer vdW forces surpassed PMMA adhesion [124].

Figure 3e presents an optical image of a WS2/MoS2 HS of twist angle 30°, fabricated using the oxygen-plasma-assisted water-vapor intercalation (WVI) transfer method. In this approach, mechanically exfoliated 2D monolayers are first supported on a PDMS/PPC stamp and then exposed to controlled water vapor conditions, enabling clean separation from the initial substrate without direct liquid contact. The PPC/2DM film is subsequently aligned and transferred onto a target 2D material flake to form the HS, followed by PPC removal through thermal annealing. This technique yields atomically clean interfaces. The method enables precise control over layer alignment and twist angle, essential for studying interlayer coupling phenomena, and offers superior cleanliness compared to conventional wet or dry transfer processes [125]. Figure 3f displays an OM image of a WS2/MoS2 HS assembled using the same dry-transfer approach. To control the twist angle during stacking, the crystallographic orientation of each monolayer must be accurately identified. This is demonstrated in Fig. 3f: panel (i) shows a WSe2 monolayer with two sharp edges meeting at 60°, which were determined optically to correspond to zigzag terminations; panel (ii) shows a MoS2 flake lacking clear 60° edges but containing a smooth crack propagating along the zigzag direction, panel (iii) shows a WSe2 monolayer without sharp 60° edges but containing a nanoscale scroll oriented 90° relative to the zigzag axis, indicating that the fold follows an armchair direction. Finally, Fig. 3(g) demonstrates the stacking of two TMD monolayers at ~ 0° and ~ 60° twist angles, using zigzag edges as orientation guides. Overlaid optical images confirm the controlled alignment, enabling reproducible fabrication of moiré superlattices for systematic interlayer coupling studies [126].

Synthesis of CVD grown t-vdW HSs by transfer method

T-vdW HSs of CVD-grown layers, such as Gr/TMD, TMD/TMD, Gr/h-BN, and TMD/h-BN, offer significant potential for exploring novel quantum phenomena and device applications. These HSs can be assembled through various techniques, including wet transfer, dry transfer, and in-situ growth processes. The wet transfer process involves dissolving a sacrificial layer beneath the 2D material grown on a substrate and transferring it onto a target substrate, thereby introducing some bubbles and wrinkles at the HS interface. Dry transfer, on the other hand, uses a more controlled mechanical or thermal approach to place the CVD-grown layer onto the target substrate. As a result, it reduces contamination but also requires accurate alignment. In-situ growth represents the most integrated approach, where vdW HSs are directly synthesized during the CVD process, allowing for better control over twist angles and minimizing the need for post-growth manipulation. This method also offers the advantage of avoiding transfer-related challenges, paving the way for high-quality, large-area twisted HSs with tunable electronic and optical properties.

In TMD HBLs, the stacking arrangement can be either perfect or imperfect, depending on the alignment between the two layers. Perfect stacking occurs when the layers are aligned in an energetically favourable configuration, typically at twist angles close to 0° or 60°. Figure 4a presents an optical image of two typical WS2-WSe2 HBLs grown via the CVD method on a Si wafer [28]. The larger, bottom layer is a monolayer of WS2 (1 L-WS2), while the smaller, top layers are monolayers of WSe2 (1 L-WSe2). The twisting of the HS results in the formation of a moiré superlattice for some commensurate angles. Broadly, there exists two types stacking alignments in HSs: a twist angle close to 0° corresponds to an R-type (rhombohedral stacking), while a twist angle close to 60° leads to an H-type (hexagonal stacking) configuration [127].

The photoluminescence (PL) imaging using bright field optical microscope is an efficient technique to probe the interlayer coupling strength in vdW HS. Figure 4b shows the PL imaging of MoSe2/WS2 HS grown by CVD method using bright-field optical microscope [128]. They can be easily distinguished by their emission color in the PL image. Room-temperature PL emission of WS2 is centered at 630 nm and appears dark red in the PL image, whereas MoSe2 PL peaks at 790 nm and has a pale pink color. Here, the MoSe2 monolayers have a slightly blurry appearance due to the chromatic aberration, caused by the difference in their PL peak wavelength. The inset in Fig. 4b displays a bright field optical image of the same HS. The large triangle in the center of the image corresponds to single layer WS2, while smaller flakes around it are MoSe2 monolayers. When the twist angle is close to 0° and 60° (perfect stacking), the interlayer coupling is strong which gives rise to large charge transfer between the two corresponding TMD monolayers resulting in a diminish in the PL intensity. As a result, dark lower contrast MoSe2 triangles can be seen over big WS2 triangle. On the other hand, imperfect stacking arises when the layers are misaligned at arbitrary twist angles. In this case, the atomic layers are not in ideal registry, and the interlayer coupling is also weak resulting in less charge transfer between the two layers. Those can be identified as pale contrast MoSe2 triangles over WS2 triangle.

PL spectroscopy is also a sensitive probe of charge transfer in TMD based HSs, as the spatial separation of electron-hole pairs between two TMD layers prevents efficient emission causing PL quenching in HSs, which occurs when the emission of light is suppressed due to charge transfer, energy dissipation, or exciton separation at the interface between the two materials. Figure 4c presents the PL intensity map at the MoS2 A-exciton resonance (1.93 eV) at 77 K under excitation with 2.33 eV photons [129]. Strong PL signals are detected in the MoS2-only region, while significant quenching occurs in the MoS2/WS2 HS region signifying efficient charge transfer in this heterojunction which is further evident in Fig. 4d which presents the PL spectra of MoS2/WS2 HSs alongside isolated MoS2 and WS2 monolayers under 2.33 eV excitation. In the spectra, the monolayers of MoS2 and WS2 exhibit distinct PL peaks at their respective A-exciton resonances: 1.93 eV for MoS2 and 2.06 eV for WS2. These strong luminescent signals are characteristic of efficient radiative recombination within each monolayer. However, in the MoS2/WS2 HS, both PL signals are significantly quenched. This suppression of PL indicates that the electron-hole pairs in each layer are not recombining radiatively as efficiently as they would in isolated layers. Instead, the quenching effect strongly suggests that a highly efficient charge transfer occurs across the MoS2/WS2 interface. This process likely results in the rapid separation of photogenerated electron-hole pairs between the two layers, reducing radiative recombination and thus diminishing PL intensity. This observation of PL quenching provides clear evidence of charge transfer within the MoS2/WS2 HS, underscoring its potential for optoelectronic applications where interlayer charge dynamics are critical.

Gong et al. [130] developed vertically stacked WS2/MoS2 HBLs using high-temperature CVD. For MoS2 growth, MoO₃ powder was placed near a bare SiO2/Si wafer, while a tungsten and tellurium powder mixture was scattered for WS2 growth, with tellurium aiding tungsten melting. Sulfur powder was positioned upstream in a low-temperature zone, with argon used to shield from oxygen and carry sulfur vapor during the reaction. The distinct nucleation and growth rates of MoS2 and WS2 led to sequential growth, forming stacked bilayers at 850 °C. Figure 4e shows an optical image of as-grown WS2/MoS2 HSs, where spots 1 and 2 are located in the monolayer MoS2 region. Raman spectroscopy of these spots confirms pure MoS2, showing only MoS2 peaks [130]. In contrast, spots 3 and 4 indicate a bilayer region formed by overlapping monolayers of MoS2 and WS2. Raman spectra for these spots display peaks corresponding to both MoS2 and WS2 [130], verifying the successful fabrication of WS2/MoS2 HBLs. An AFM-based technique enables the creation of t-vdW HSs by manipulating the orientation of monolayer domains through precise AFM tip rotation, allowing controlled twist angles between stacked layers. In a twist-angle-dependent conductivity study of MoS2/graphene heterojunctions, Liao et al. [20] achieved various twist angles between MoS2 domains and the underlying graphene substrate by employing an AFM-tip manipulation technique. To rotate the MoS2 domains, they first applied an AFM tip to the graphene surface with a load of several tens of nN, then pushed the MoS2 triangle from one corner to initiate rotation. By precisely controlling the tip’s movement direction and distance, they were able to rotate the MoS2 domains on graphene to achieve specific, deterministic twist angles. One such example is shown in Fig. 4f where the AFM image shows the rotated MoS2 domain over graphene by 13.5° from its initial position depicted in blue dashed line to its final orientation indicated by red dashed line. This has been done by conductive atomic force microscopy measurements where the green rectangle denotes the area scanned in the course of the process.

Directly growing a TMD material on h-BN without a seeding method results in a clean interface, allowing seamless integration of the two layered materials. This approach enables the fabrication of high-quality devices without extensive annealing and facilitates intriguing physics due to the direct coupling of the TMD and h-BN lattices. Yan et al. [131] demonstrated the direct CVD growth of single- and few-layer MoS2 on exfoliated h-BN flakes, revealing different growth mechanisms for each. Single-layer MoS2 showed small relative rotation angles (< 5°) with respect to the h-BN lattice. Figure 4g shows an optical image where light green h-BN flakes (~ 20 × 10 μm2) host randomly scattered dark green MoS2 islands (2–3 μm), with occasional regions of thicker MoS2. The left inset of Fig. 4g displays an AFM image of the WSe2/h-BN HS [21], where the h-BN-inserted HS underwent an annealing process after the wet transfer method to remove any residual contaminants. The annealing was conducted under vacuum conditions (< 0.1 Pa) with a flow of Ar gas (100 sccm) at 300 °C for 12 h. This post-transfer annealing step is crucial for enhancing the interlayer coupling between WSe2 and h-BN, improving the overall interface quality and promoting stronger interaction between the two layers. Fu et al. [132] developed a direct CVD method to synthesize TMDCs/h-BN vertical HSs without requiring any intermediate transfer steps. This approach leverages a nickel-based alloy, which has high catalytic activity for h-BN growth and excellent resistance to sulfides. Using this strategy, they achieved the direct growth of single-crystal MoS2 grains up to 200 μm2 on h-BN. The growth follows a Frank-van der Merwe mechanism, where small 2D nuclei form and expand into large 2D crystals. The right inset in Fig. 4g shows TEM and SAED results for MoS2 grown on h-BN, revealing two distinct sets of hexagonal diffraction patterns. The inner hexagon (green) corresponds to the MoS2 (110) plane with a spacing of 1.6 Å, while the outer hexagon (blue) corresponds to the BN (10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\underset{\_}{1}$$\end{document}0) plane with a spacing of 2.5 Å [130, 133]. These patterns confirm the coexistence of single-crystal MoS2 and h-BN in the HS.

In 2015, Zhang et al. introduced a direct synthesis approach for large-area graphene/h-BN vertical HSs, known as the co-segregation method [134]. By annealing layered growth substrates (Ni(C)/(B, N)-source/Ni) in vacuum in a single step, wafer-scale graphene/h-BN films were directly formed on the metal surface. Figure 4h shows a high-resolution TEM image revealing a clear Moiré pattern in the graphene/h-BN HSs, with a wavelength of approximately 1.28 nm. The fast Fourier transform (FFT) of Fig. 4h further exhibits two hexagonal spot sets rotated by about 11°, indicating a slight rotation between the graphene and h-BN lattices due to their small lattice mismatch (~ 1.8%). This co-segregation method provides a novel route for scalable graphene/h-BN HS production and could be expanded for synthesizing other vdW HSs.

Nayak et al. [21] fabricated MoSe2/WSe2 HSs by first growing distinct triangular MoSe2 and WSe2 monolayers on two separate sapphire substrates via CVD, followed by a poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA)-assisted wet transfer process. The WSe2 monolayer was transferred onto a SiO2/Si substrate, and after removing the PMMA, the MoSe2 monolayer was similarly transferred on top, forming a MoSe2/WSe2 vdW HSs. As shown in the SEM images in Fig. 5a–e, a range of twist angles from 0° to 60° was observed when monolayers of MoSe2 were transferred onto monolayers of WSe2 and selected overlapping regions were considered for exploring angle dependency studies. The WSe2 layer, appearing with higher contrast in the images, forms the bottom layer, while the MoSe2 monolayer, with lower contrast, is positioned on top. In some instances, the MoSe2 top layer is perfectly aligned with the WSe2 bottom layer, creating a nearly seamless interface with minimal twist. In other cases, there is only partial overlap between the two layers, leading to a twist angle between them. The twist angle (θ) between the layers is calculated based on the relative orientation of the top MoSe2 triangle with respect to the bottom WSe2 triangle. Specifically, the twist angle is measured in a clockwise direction, considering the positioning of the MoSe2 triangle relative to the fixed WSe2 base. These variations in twist angle are important as they significantly affect the resulting electronic and optical properties of the HS due to the moiré patterns that emerge at selected commensurate twist angles. The accuracy of the twist angle measurements was estimated with an estimated error of only around 2°, ensuring precise evaluation of the twist-dependent behavior of the HS. This level of control and characterization of twist angles is essential for studying and tailoring the physical properties of vdW HSs, as different angles can lead to the emergence of new physical phenomena, such as altered band structures, exciton dynamics, and more. Figure 5f presents an AFM image of the MoSe2/WSe2 vdW HSs, clearly showing the distinct MoSe2 top layer and WSe2 bottom layer. The edge step heights measured at different interfaces provide key insights into the layer thicknesses. At the interface between the Si substrate and the bottom WSe2 layer (inset a), the step height is approximately 0.7 nm, which corresponds to the thickness of a monolayer of WSe2. Similarly, at the interface between the bottom WSe2 layer and the top MoSe2 layer (inset b), a step height of around 0.8 nm is observed, corresponding to the thickness of top MoSe2 layer and ensuring the formation of MoSe2/WSe2 HS. The interface between the two layers is mostly clean, although some bubbles and wrinkles are visible, likely trapped during the transfer process [135, 136]. These imperfections, such as bubbles and wrinkles, are common in assembled vdW HSs and generally do not affect their overall properties [136]. However, these defects can cause slight changes in the crystal edge direction due to puckering and localized strain. Such deformations introduce minor errors in the twist angle estimation, potentially leading to an error margin of up to 5°, as previously reported by Lui et al. [137].

These observations highlight the challenges of achieving perfect interfaces during the transfer process, though the overall impact on the HSs’s properties remains minimal. The clean interface and accurate layer thicknesses confirm the successful formation of the HS, while the small twist angle variations provide a manageable degree of uncertainty for further studies on twist-angle-dependent phenomena. This twist angle is critical because it influences the electronic and optical properties of the HBL by creating moiré patterns and modifying interlayer coupling. There are MoSe2 and WS2 monolayers in their triangular shapes, which are then stacked with a controlled twist angle between the layers. The color-coded representation of the atoms helps visualize the layered structure and the relative orientation of the two materials, making it clear how the lattice mismatch or twist between the layers is introduced. This schematic thus provides a fundamental understanding of the structural configuration of the twisted HBL system.

Figure 5g2 illustrates a schematic atomic model of a MoSe2/WS2 HS, where monolayers of MoSe2 and WS2 are vertically stacked [138]. In the model, Mo atoms are shown in cyan, Se atoms in brown, W atoms in blue, and S atoms in yellow. The dashed line between atoms depicts the alignment and relative orientation between the two layers. This configuration highlights the atomic interface structure of MoSe2 and WS2, revealing overlapping regions and a twisted stacking arrangement with an angle close to 16.1°. Figure 5g3 illustrates a schematic representation of an aligned MoSe2/MoS2 HS consisting of a monolayer (1 L) of MoSe2 on top (depicted as a blue layer) and a monolayer of MoS2 on the bottom (shown as a red layer) [139]. These two TMD layers possess distinct lattice constants, with MoSe2 at 3.32 Å and MoS2 at 3.18 Å [140]. Due to a lattice mismatch of approximately 4.4%, the MoSe2 and MoS2 layers form an incommensurate HS. This lattice mismatch generates a nanometer-scale moiré pattern with a periodicity of about 7.54 nm when the layers are aligned [141]. This periodic moiré pattern introduces regions with varying stacking configurations throughout the structure, leading to spatial variations in interlayer coupling and the local electrostatic potential [142]. Such moiré patterns in aligned TMD HSs are known to influence electronic properties, potentially giving rise to unique quantum phenomena by modifying the band structure and excitonic behavior across the HS. In the 0°-stacked MoSe2/MoS2 HS, valence band hybridization leads to the formation of two distinct interlayer excitons (ILEs). These excitonic states arise due to the interaction between the layers, influenced by the alignment and moiré pattern. The right inset in Fig. 5g3 presents the PL spectrum of the MoSe2/MoS2 HS, revealing unique excitonic peaks not present in the individual monolayer PL spectra. The first peak, labeled IX1, appears around 1.57 eV. This peak is notably absent in the PL spectra of standalone MoSe2 and MoS2 monolayers, indicating it results from interlayer interactions within the HS. IX1 is identified as a momentum-direct ILE, where the electron and hole have compatible momentum states between the layers, allowing efficient recombination and resulting in a strong PL signal [143]. A second, weaker peak at approximately 1.35 eV, labeled IX2, is also observed in the HS PL spectrum. Due to its low emission intensity, IX2 is attributed to a momentum-indirect ILE, where the electron and hole reside in states of different momentum, making recombination less efficient. Both peaks are unique to the HS, signifying their origin in the interlayer coupling facilitated by the periodic moiré potential at a nanometer scale. This moiré pattern, resulting from the slight lattice mismatch and precise stacking orientation, creates a spatially modulated potential that fosters hybridization of electronic states. This periodic potential landscape enables the formation of distinct ILEs, contributing to unique optical characteristics that are not seen in the monolayer constituents alone. Such moiré-induced ILEs provide valuable insights into the interlayer coupling mechanisms and the potential for tuning excitonic behavior in 2D HSs.

Theoretical prospects

At the mesoscale, a widely used and efficient model for understanding the growth of 2D materials is the classical Burton-Cabrera-Frank (BCF) theory [144]. This theory describes island growth through the deposition of adatoms from a supersaturated gas atmosphere and the incorporation of diffusing atoms at the step edge. Direct numerical implementation of the BCF model involves tracking the evolving step edges and enforcing boundary conditions, which can be complex due to the sharp interface nature of the model. In contrast, phase-field diffuse-interface models [145] avoid the need for explicit interface tracking. Since the 1990s, phase-field models based on the BCF theory have been developed for various crystal growth phenomena, including collective step movements in 1D step trains [146], spiral growth on surfaces during thin-film epitaxy [147], and step flow under different kinetic regimes [148]. More recently, these models have been extended to account for effects such as edge diffusion, the Ehrlich-Schwoebel barrier, deposition, and desorption in 2D epitaxial growth [149]. Meca et al. applied such a model to study 2D material growth with anisotropic diffusion on substrates, using the example of epitaxial graphene growth on copper foil during CVD, which was also experimentally examined [150, 151]. The applications of phase-field models in 2D material growth can be broadly categorized into three areas: (1) Replicating and explaining observed growth morphologies, (2) Exploring the influence of experimental parameters on growth patterns, and (3) Advancing the model to incorporate the influence of additional physical and chemical processes occurring during the growth phase. A significant challenge in applying phase-field models to 2D material synthesis lies in the difficulty of linking model parameters to experimentally controllable CVD conditions. This is primarily due to differences in the scale of interest, as most phase-field simulations only model the growth of small 2D islands (up to the micrometre scale), which limits their ability to capture the impact of macroscopic CVD parameters.

Phase-field models have also been successfully used to simulate phase transitions in 2D materials. The vdW-DFT calculation was performed for the MoSe2/h-BN HSs by few groups [153, 154]. The vertical HSs was prepared by positioning a (3 × 3) MoSe2 supercell, with a lattice constant of approximately 3.3 Å, on top of a (4 × 4) h-BN supercell, which has a lattice constant of around 2.5 Å. The simulation results indicate that the monolayer of MoSe2 is situated at a distance of about 3.6 Å above the h-BN surface. The interaction between the two layers has an energy of approximately 90 meV per formula unit (f.u.), while the adhesive energy is calculated to be around 9 meV/Å2 per f.u. This adhesive energy is notably lower than the typical range for vdW layered materials, which is estimated to fall between 13 and 21 meV/Å2. This lower value implies a relatively weak coupling between the MoSe2 and h-BN layers in this HSs [155].

Lower effective masses generally correspond to higher mobilities, which is crucial for optimizing the performance of components in electronic and optoelectronic devices. This tunability through twist angle provides a potential pathway for designing 2D HSs tailored for specific device functionalities.

Current challenges

The development of t-vdW HSs has opened new avenues for research in electronics, optoelectronics, and quantum devices. However, several challenges remain in the precise control of twist angles, scalability, and reproducibility of these structures. Issues related to the uniformity of stacking, interfacial contamination during transfer, and the limited understanding of twist-angle-dependent properties hinder progress. Addressing these challenges is critical for the practical application of twisted HSs.

First, the stability of certain 2D crystals needs to be addressed using interfacial engineering or encapsulation methods without disrupting the twisted HSs. For instance, while 2D magnetic materials and their twisted configurations offer excellent prospects for spintronics, they are unstable in ambient conditions [159]. Stabilization techniques include ultrathin MgO passivation for CrBr3 [160] and isolating air-stable 2D Ta3FeS6 with intrinsic ferromagnetic order [161]. Another major challenge is creating large-area twisted HSs without relying on transfer-based methods, as current methods primarily use ME, CVD and stacking [162, 163]. Achieving in situ control over small twist angles, while simultaneously visualizing moiré superlattices and studying quantum phenomena, will advance the field of moiré physics [164]. Additionally, the impact of defects, strain, and edges on twisted superlattices and quantum effects is of great interest [165, 166]. The structural, electronic as well as optical characterizations of moiré superlattices to study strongly correlated physics is also hindered by the need for sophisticated tools [35]. Finally, integrating moiré quantum materials into electronic or quantum photonic circuits could significantly expand their functionalities, but further research is required. Moreover, issues like 3D metal contacts and interfacing with 2D twisted HSs could hinder carrier transport and dynamics, making interfacial engineering crucial for improving device performance.

The large-area growth of vdW HSs remains a significant challenge for advancing device applications. Currently, most vdW HSs are fabricated using mechanical transfer techniques, first reported in 2010 for mono- and bilayer graphene devices on single-crystal h-BN substrates to fabricate graphene/h-BN HSs [3]. This method, however, is limited to producing micrometre-scale samples. Although new methods have emerged for large-scale transfer of CVD-grown samples, direct synthesis of vdW HSs with distinct materials on SiO2 substrates is still difficult. Additionally, creating multifunctional devices is crucial in the post-Moore era, but their fabrication is complex, requiring intricate mechanical transfers and vertical stacking of different 2D materials. These multiple transfer steps can degrade device performance, reduce yield, and result in higher power consumption for large-scale integration, as current demonstrations of multifunctionality are primarily focused on charge transport and photon-based applications.

Direct growth of bilayer graphene on transition metal substrates like Cu [167–169] or Cu-Ni alloy [170, 171] via CVD is a promising method due to its scalability and high-quality output [172, 173]. However, during high-temperature CVD, the bilayer graphene naturally favours an AB-stacked (non-twisted) structure, as rotation between layers requires overcoming a significant energy barrier [174, 175]. Consequently, previous studies have shown limited success, with only about 50% tBLG [176, 177]. Developing an efficient approach to grow tBLG with a high fraction of twisted layers and a full range of twist angles remains highly desirable. In CVD growth, bilayer graphene typically forms with either the same orientation or a 30° twist if nucleated from the same site. Sun et al. [178] introduces an in situ CVD method with a hetero-site nucleation strategy, where the two layers nucleate at different sites, boosting the fraction of tBLG to 88% with twist angles from 0° to 30°. A carbon-isotope-labelling technique verified the use of gas-flow perturbation to initiate this process, achieving high-quality tBLG with clear Moiré patterns and ultrahigh carrier mobility of 68,000 cm2 V−1 s−1 at room temperature. This approach offers promising control over interlayer twist for graphene. Likewise, Paul et al. [179] used Raman spectroscopy to study interlayer coupling in bilayer WSe2 with various twist angles. They found that interlayer interactions were weakest at a 30° twist, making the system nearly equivalent to two decoupled WSe2 monolayers. The WSe2 samples were grown by CVD on Si/SiO2 substrates, and twisted bilayer samples with twist angles from 0° to 60° were created using a modified pickup and transfer technique involving h-BN on a PDMS/PPC stack, along with a water delamination transfer method.

However, achieving in situ growth of t-vdW HSs such as graphene/TMD or TMD/TMD etc. remains challenging. Unlike bilayer graphene, where the twist angle can be controlled to a certain extent through advanced nucleation strategies, t-vdW HSs involve materials with different lattice structures, chemical compositions, and thermal properties, making the process significantly more complex. One major drawback of in situ growth for t-vdW HSs is the difficulty in precisely controlling the twist angle between two different 2D layers. In bilayer graphene, both layers have identical lattice parameters, so they can naturally align or twist at a set angle; however, in HSs like MoS2/WS2 or graphene/MoS2, lattice mismatches create additional energy barriers that make controlled twisting difficult. For instance, TMD layers often exhibit different lattice constants or crystal symmetries, which can cause strain at the interface when twisted, thus affecting the desired twist angle. Another issue arises from the differing growth conditions required for each material in the HSs. Materials like MoS2, WS2, and graphene each have unique optimal temperatures, pressures, and reactant flows for CVD growth. Aligning these conditions for a sequential or concurrent in situ growth without compromising the quality of either layer is challenging. For example, high temperatures that are favourable for one material may lead to degradation or unintentional doping in the other, leading to poor crystal quality or inconsistent electronic properties. Moreover, nucleation control becomes increasingly complex in HSs with different chemical compositions. Unlike homostructures, where nucleation sites are similar in composition, different TMDs or graphene/TMD combinations tend to nucleate in different ways and at different rates. This lack of synchronized nucleation makes it difficult to ensure uniform twist angles across large areas, leading to inconsistencies in twist-related properties such as Moiré superlattice effects. Additionally, interface cleanliness is a significant concern in in situ methods for vdW HSs. During growth, residual gases or by-products from one layer can contaminate the surface of the other layer, leading to impurities at the interface. This is particularly problematic for twisted HSs, where the electronic and optical properties are highly sensitive to interfacial quality. Future advancements in in situ techniques and growth environment control will be crucial to achieving reliable t- vdW HSs.

Future prospects

Moiré physics and quantum materials, driven by t-vdW HSs, represent a rapidly advancing field with vast technological promise. The expanding library of 2D materials and progress in stacking techniques are enabling complex, multifunctional HSs that exploit unique 2D degrees of freedom including charge, spin, and photon manipulation - beyond the reach of conventional systems. These developments open pathways to reconfigurable spintronic devices, spin-orbit torque memories, and hybrid 2D-3D structures for next-generation technologies. Moiré superlattices, formed by twisting stacked 2D crystals, create periodic potentials that precisely modulate electronic, optical, and mechanical properties at the nanoscale. This tunability, combined with diverse stacking options, enables tailored functionalities in nanoelectronics and optoelectronics, including flat-band correlated states like superconductivity in magic-angle graphene and dynamically tunable photonic devices.

The growth of high-quality, wafer-scale 2D films faces key challenges, including achieving continuous, large-grain films, as small grains introduce grain boundaries that degrade material performance. Ensuring uniform thickness and crystallinity over large areas remains difficult, hindering device integration and scalability. Additionally, transferring large-area films is laborious and risks contamination - an even greater concern for vdW HSs, where interfacial cleanliness and precise twist-angle control are crucial. Slow growth rates and strict conditions further limit the scalable production of continuous, defect-free 2D materials. To address these concerns, future efforts must focus on two promising directions. (1) The development of in situ growth techniques, such as CVD-based synthesis of t-vdW HSs, could overcome issues like interfacial contamination, preserve crystallinity, and enable the controlled growth of HSs at designed twist angles. Such methods would eliminate the need for post-growth transfer, enhancing structural quality and scalability. (2) Advancements in automated 2D material transfer and alignment systems capable of millidegree precision would revolutionize the fabrication of twisted HSs. This would enable deterministic assembly of high-quality, angle-engineered moiré superlattices, accelerating the practical integration of t-vdW systems into next-generation quantum, electronic, and optoelectronic devices. Future advances in the growth of 2D vdW HSs will focus on precise twist angle control through techniques such as thermal annealing-induced self-rotation, in-situ layer rotation during growth, and nucleation tuning via pulsed gas flow modulation.

In quantum computing, moiré physics offers the potential for qubits based on strongly correlated electronic states and topologically protected quantum phases, enabling fault-tolerant operations. Precise control of twist angles and layer compositions in t-vdW HSs can facilitate tunable quantum gates and non-volatile spintronic memory devices. However, current fabrication techniques, such as exfoliation and manual transfer, hinder scalability. Emerging CVD and MBE methods show promise for producing large-area, high-quality twisted HSs, and scalable, precise-angle fabrication will be pivotal for commercial applications. Moiré HSs also hold potential for quantum communication, where strong light-matter coupling enables devices like quantum repeaters and quantum key distribution systems, essential for future quantum internet networks. Beyond that, properties such as unconventional superconductivity and resonant tunnelling, driven by twist angles, have sparked interest, though many aspects remain unexplored. To realize these opportunities, three key advances are needed: (i) scalable, transfer-free growth of large-area moiré superlattices via direct CVD methods; (ii) dynamic in situ control of twist angles during measurements under extreme conditions, to probe tunable quantum states; and (iii) improved stability of 2D magnetic and air-sensitive materials through encapsulation or atomic layer passivation. Addressing these challenges will unlock the full potential of moiré materials for next-generation quantum, optoelectronic, and spintronic technologies.
