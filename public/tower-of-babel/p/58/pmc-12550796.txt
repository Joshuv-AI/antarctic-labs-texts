AlphaFold3 prediction of protein-protein complex: Is it ready for thermodynamic analysis?
Authors: Xiao Liu, Kaiwen Pang, Hangfei Wu, Bin Chong, Zhixiang Yin, Zhaoxi Sun, Xiaohui Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence has emerged as a pivotal tool for structure prediction of biomolecular assemblies. The AlphaFold (AF) framework as a landmark in this field has spurred the development and refinement of a variety of folding methods. The revolution is further sparked with the release of the substantially updated architecture, AlphaFold3 (AF3), which is reported as the state-of-the-art prediction model for protein-protein complexes. Here, we evaluate the quality of the deep-learning tool as well as its precedents on an extensive dataset of protein-protein complexes and assess whether they could replace structures deposited experimentally in various critical modelling and screening tasks, e.g., contacts prediction of interfacial regions, hot-spot identifications and binding affinities calculations. Structurally, although the prediction accuracy of these deep-learning tools seems high based on quality metrics such as DockQ and RMSD, major inconsistencies/deviations from experiment are observed in the compactness of the complex, the intermolecular directional polar interactions (>2 hydrogen bonds are incorrectly predicted) and interfacial contacts (especially the apolar-apolar packing for AF3), which adds a caution when applying AF predictions to understand key interactions stabilizing protein-protein complexes. Interestingly, while the latest AF3 exhibits a prediction accuracy obviously higher than its precedents in direct prediction-experiment comparisons, after simulation relaxation, the quality of structural ensembles sampled in molecular simulations drops severely. Such a deterioration could be attributed to many influential factors, e.g., the instability of the predicted intermolecular packing or the inaccuracy of force fields. Consequently, the quality of sampled structural ensembles from all predictions still exhibits noticeable differences from the experimental reference. Based on the simulation trajectories, an example of using AF predictions in practical virtual screening tasks is presented. A physics-based hot-spot scan with the alanine scanning with generalized Born and interaction entropy method is conducted, which provides mutation-induced affinity variations of protein-protein complexes. Face-to-face comparisons between computed affinity variations and experimental measurements reveal that predictions employing experimental structures as starting configurations outperform those with predicted structures, regardless of the version of the AF derivatives. As for hot-spot identification, the prediction quality using experimental structures is better than those based on predicted structures. More interestingly, we align the structural deviations of the predicted structures with quality metrics of affinity calculations and observe little correlations, which suggests that one cannot directly infer the quality of thermodynamic calculations with the quality of structure prediction. These observations provide a unique simulation perspective of the structure prediction tool, AF derivatives, and more importantly practical guidance of their applicability in molecular modelling of protein-protein assemblies (e.g., protein design).Keywords: AlphaFold3, Alanine scanning, Contacts, Protein-protein Interaction, Hot spot

Introduction

Molecular modelling has been one of the key tools for the investigation of various thermodynamic behaviors of protein assemblies, e.g., conformational changes [1], [2], [3]. It serves as a complementary tool to experimental measurements and has shown established predictive power in many aspects. For example, based solely on static structures of a bound protein-protein complex deposited experimentally, while the interaction networks could be described with high accuracy, the thermodynamic picture is incomplete, due to the neglect of the conformational ensemble that relate to the multi-state/conformation behaviors [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], solvation environment [10], [11] and the entropic contribution (the well-known enthalpy-entropy compensation [12], [13]). Another situation is the hot spots with significant contributions to the intermolecular packing among interfacial residues of biomolecular assemblies [14], [15]. The identification of these crucial residues is difficult through static structures. Experimentally, alanine scanning [16], [17] as a technique mutating the original residue to alanine eliminates the unique interaction feature of protein residues, e.g., charge-charge interactions (positively charged lysine and negatively charged aspartate) and hydrogen bonds (residues with side-chain -OH). Alanine mutations that trigger significant drops in binding affinities are thus classified as hot spots contributing significantly to the formation of the intermolecular assemblies.

Protein-protein complexes are huge biomolecular assemblies formed by multiple protein chains [18], [19]. Unlike protein-ligand complexes with deep cavities encapsulating external agents [20], [21], protein-protein interfaces are wider and shallower, with a large number of protein residues contributing to the stabilization effects. Such complex interaction networks are more difficult to model and predict accurately. AlphaFold (AF) 2.3 [22], [23] has been established as an accurate deep-learning model for the prediction of the protein-protein interaction (PPI). Although many derivatives [24], [25] or extensions of the AF implementation such as af2complex [26] and ColabFold [25] have been proposed, none of them has indisputably surpassed the AF performance. The latest AF3 model as a major update of the AF tool has been reported to achieve a state-of-the-art model for biomolecular assemblies, outperforming existing tools with similar aims including AF2. However, among independent benchmarks of its predictive power for protein-protein complexes reported in the literature, most benchmarks of AF3 [27] focus on static structures [28] and use metrics such as root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and DockQ [29] as quantitative evaluators, which do not provide the whole picture. In practical molecular modelling studies investigating thermodynamics and interaction mechanisms (e.g., hot spots), what really matters are actually the simulation-explored configurations, which could differ from the predicted static structure due to the relaxation process governed by force-field energetics. While experimentally deposited structures have been acknowledged as convincing starting points of molecular simulations in current practices, the usability and reliability of AF predictions remain unverified. To understand the practical applicability of AF predictions and check whether they can replace experimental measurements in practical simulation studies, we report a comprehensive simulation perspective on AF3-predicted structures and its precedents in this work.

Methodology

The PPI dataset

While there are many structure entries with hot spot information available, in many cases the sequences and mutational sites are very similar. Further, in many cases the temperature of the experimentally measured mutational thermodynamics is absent, which leads to uncertainties and potential systematic biases due to environmental variables. To form a PPI dataset of general interest, with both representativeness and more importantly reliability, we filter the publicly available databases including SKEMPI [30], ASEDB [31] and BID [32] with several criteria: a) the temperature of the measured affinity variation should be close to the room-temperature condition of 300 K (entries assuming room-temperature conditions would be excluded), b) similarities of protein sequences (a high sequence identity <90 % is selected due to the sensitivity of the thermodynamic data to protein sequences), c) the completeness of the thermodynamic data (e.g., binding affinities recorded without a specific value but using a < or > to specify a range would be excluded), d) the location of mutated residues (e.g., single-sided or double-sided information), e) the mutation target should be alanine (i.e., alanine mutations to be benchmarked in the lateral alanine scanning analysis). This leads to 61 PDB entries involving 804 alanine mutations with experimental measurements.

Details of molecular modelling and simulations

The first step is to secure the structure of the protein-protein complex. Although one of our data sources SKEMPI claims that they cleaned the original PDB structures and provides a mapping list connecting the cleaned sequences with the original sequence, there are flaws in the processed PDB files. The largest problem should concern the missing residues: SKEMPI does not add missing residues to the processed PDB files. Consequently, the gaps in the sequences would lead to huge distances of chemical bonds between sequentially neighboring residues, which would cause trouble in atomistic simulations. More importantly, the direct use of these incorrect sequences in structure predictions with AF derivatives would obviously lead to incorrect results. Therefore, instead of using the cleaned PDB files provided in some database (e.g., SKEMPI), we download the 3D coordinates of all systems in our PPI dataset from the original RCSB database. The missing residues are handled with MODELLER [33]. The atomistic simulations are performed with the AMBER22 code that are freely available for academic use, and post-simulation alanine scanning calculations are performed with home-made codes.

Apart from the experimental structures in the constructed PPI dataset above, a few AF derivatives were used to predict the structures of protein-protein complexes. The AF derivatives considered in this study include af2complex [26], AF2.3.1 [22], and the latest AF3. We used a locally built implementation for the AF2 derivatives (af2complex and AF2.3.1), while AF3 was assessed through its web server. In all predictions, the default or recommended parameters were applied.

With either experimental or predicted structures of protein-protein complexes for all systems in the PPI dataset, we construct the simulation cell by using AMBER14SB [34] to describe the protein, TIP3P water for solvation, and monovalent sodium/chloride ions [35], [36] parametrized for the water model for neutralization. During molecular simulations, we first energy-minimize the complex to get rid of unfavorable interactions, after which 120 ps of constant-volume heating and 2 ns of NPT equilibration are performed. During the simulations, we employ SHAKE [37], [38] constraints on bonds involving hydrogen, Langevin dynamics for temperature regulation at 300 K, isotropic scaling and Monte Carlo Barostat for pressure regulation at 1 atm, and the AMBER default 8 Å real-space cutoff for non-bonded interactions. The final sampling step constitutes of a 100 ns production run with a sampling interval of 10 ps.

In the post-simulation ASGB-IE analysis for thermodynamic information, we scan the interfacial heavy-atom pairs with a 5 Å threshold to probe potential hot-spot sites, mutate them to alanine and compute the energetic variation as the importance of individual sites in protein-protein packing. The ASGB-IE energetics describing the affinity drop upon mutations to alanine are given as ∆∆Gbindx→a=∆Gbinda−∆Gbindx=∆∆Eelex→a+∆∆EvdWx→a+lneβ∆Eelea+β∆EvdWa−lneβ∆Eelex+β∆EvdWxβ+∆∆Gsolvation,GBx→a+∆∆Gsolvation,surface_areax→a, where the first two terms are the gas-phase enthalpic contribution, the third term is the interaction entropy estimate of the gas-phase entropic contribution [39], and the last two terms belong to the polar solvation thermodynamics computed with the generalized Born (GB) formulism using the GBOBC parametrization [40], [41] and the solvent-accessible surface area framework for the non-polar solvation work [42]. The dielectric constants of the protein interior are adjusted following a calibrated regime proposed in our previous works.

Results and discussion

A structure perspective of static predictions

We constructed a PPI dataset with 61 PDB entries (protein complexes) and 804 alanine mutations as listed in Table S1. The AF derivatives under consideration include af2complex [26], AF2.3.1 [22], [23], and the latest AF3. We use a locally built implementation for the AF2 derivatives, while for AF3 the webserver is employed. In all predictions, the default/recommended parameters are applied. We first evaluate the quality of the predicted complex structure following the most straightforward and most common protocol: directly comparing the prediction with the experimental references. Metrics from various origins are considered for quality assessment. While the ordinary and probably most commonly applied metrics of DockQ and Cα RMSD are obviously included here, we consider additional structural observables that are directly related to crucial interaction components involved in protein-protein packing, in order to make the evaluation more physically meaningful. To this aim, we include the overall compactness of the system with the radius of gyration, strong intermolecular polar coordination with the interchain hydrogen bonds, and the general intermolecular coordination pattern with interfacial contacts under a charged-polar-apolar grouping regime for protein residues. Note that in the calculation of the gyration radius, the missing regions modelled with MODELLER are also included as the normal non-missing regions.

We first check two illustrative examples shown in Fig. 1b, i.e., 1A22_AB (a typical dimer protein-protein complex) and 2JEL_PLH (a typical antibody-antigen complex). The Cα contact maps computed using the switching function Csegment A−segment B=∑i∈A∑j∈B1−rijr061−rijr012 with the radius constant r0 defined as 8 Å are shown in Fig. 2a. While 1A22_AB as an easier case has extremely similar contact maps with all methods (both predictions and experiment), the antibody-antigen complex 2JEL_PLH exhibits certain differences in predicted and experimental maps. For the latter system, while the diagonal intra-molecular coordinations between sequential neighbors are similar for all methods, noticeable differences are observed in the inter-molecular packing regions, e.g., the top-right corner. This could be seen more clearly in the residue-specific contact profiles shown in Fig. 2b, where the contacts between each residue of one chain (chain A of 1A22_AB and the antigen P chain of 2JEL_PLH) and all residues in the other chain(s) (chain B of 1A22_AB and the antibody LH of 2JEL_PLH) are presented. The interfacial regions are those with high contacts. For 1A22_AB, while the interfacial residues of chain A in experimental and predicted structures are similar, noticeable differences could be observed in the exact numbers of interfacial contacts, hinting at differences in intermolecular packing. As for the antibody-antigen complex, none of the predicted structures is close to the experimental reference. Specifically, the coordination region is incorrectly predicted, not to mention the exact pairs of interfacial Cα-Cα coordination.

We then turn to the statistics of the whole PPI dataset. In Fig. S2, the distributions of the structural descriptors of predicted and experimental structures, including the radius of gyration, the number of interfacial hydrogen bonds and the interfacial contacts. The radius of gyration is computed with all heavy atoms of the complex and individual components. The directional polar interaction of utmost importance in biomolecular assemblies, the hydrogen bond interaction, is estimated with the Wernet-Nilsson [44] definition. In the calculation of contact numbers, we group naturally occurring residues based on their physiochemical properties: SER, THR, PRO, GLN, TRP, TYR, ASN as polar residues, VAL, PHE, MET, ILE, LEU, CYS, ALA, GLY as apolar residues and LYS, ARG, GLU, ASP, HIS as charged residues. While the radius of gyration of individual chains of predicted structures is very similar to the experimental reference, the distributions of the protein-protein complex exhibit obvious deviations. These phenomena suggest that the folding behaviors of monomers are correctly predicted but failures happen at the interfacial packing. The problems of interfacial coordination are made clearer in the distributions of intermolecular hydrogen bond interactions and contact numbers, where all physical observables exhibit a method-dependent behavior.

To quantitatively differentiate the performance of different prediction protocols, we compute the metrics evaluating the deviations and consistencies in Fig. 2c, including root-mean-squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), Pearson correlation coefficient (Pearson r) and Kendall ranking coefficient (τ). For the radius of gyration of the complex, while all prediction methods have ∼1.5 Å deviations with respect to the experimental reference, AF3 has the slightly larger error (1.60 Å for RMSE and 0.74 Å for MAE) compared with the two AF2 derivatives (1.48 Å and 1.43 Å for RMSE, and 0.68 Å and 0.64 Å for MAE). The performance differences between AF derivatives are small for the Pearson correlation and Kendall ranking coefficients. Thus, the general compactness predicted by different AF derivatives would be similar. More detailed insights could be obtained when checking the interfacial hydrogen bonds and contacts. For the number of hydrogen bonds, the deviations from experiment are ∼3 for all prediction methods, which indicates that a certain level of prediction errors is expected for the directional polar interactions stabilizing the assemblies. This phenomenon could provide a caution when using AF derivatives to predict structures of protein-protein complexes and investigate their packing details, especially when checking the hydrogen bonds. The deviations of the total contact numbers of AF derivatives are huge (RMSE∼24–32). The by-residue-type decomposition is more informative. Interestingly, while AF3 achieves top-level performance for polar-polar, polar-charged, polar-apolar, charged-charged and charged-apolar residue packing, its prediction of interfacial packing between apolar residues is very unsatisfactory. This phenomenon suggests that apolar packing could be a weakness of AF3 predictions, and again provides a caution for using AF3 to study interfacial packing of protein-protein complexes.

A structure perspective when considering the simulation ensemble

Compared with the predicted structures/snapshots, the trajectory ensembles explored in a simulation run are actually the crucial statistics employed in (thermo-)dynamics analyses. Certain rearrangements governed by force-field energetics (and sometimes empirical potentials) would occur, and the structures would differ from the initial condition. Further, these are situations that the predicted structures could be unstable or violate certain physical/chemical conditions (e.g., it’s not uncommon to observe unphysical geometry for chemical bonds in AF predictions). Therefore, in the evaluation of AF derivatives, it is crucial to do a simulation exploration and evaluate whether the deep-learning predictions are of a quality similar to experimental structures and thereby could replace the latter in molecular modelling investigations. We therefore perform molecular simulations to relax the predicted structures, project a 2D surface depicting the conformational space explored, and compare the outcomes with those using the normal simulation procedure that uses experimental structures as the initial condition.

We still use the two protein-protein complexes 1A22_AB and 2JEL_PLH as illustrative examples. In a 100 ns simulation that are typical in molecular simulations investigating the stability of inter-molecular coordinations and virtual screening applications with the end-point or alchemical free energy calculations, the time series of the residue-specific contacts are shown in Fig. 3a. While in 1A22_AB the intermolecular contacts between the experimental and predicted structures are largely consistent, in 2JEL_PLH all prediction tools exhibit clear deviations from the experimental observations. For 1A22_AB, molecular simulations initiated from all four structural models show a similar pattern: residues 5–70 and 160–180 of chain A primarily stabilize the interaction with chain B, whereas the remaining residues are positioned farther from the interface. In contrast, for 2JEL_PLH, the experimental structure reveals that residues 1–8, 33–43, and 60–75 of chain P form the major interaction interface with the antibody HL chain. However, in the predicted complexes generated by af2complex and AF2.3.1, residues 37–43 are no longer stably engaged at the interface, residues 5–8 show weakened interactions, and the overall protein-protein interface appears less stable. Moreover, in the structures predicted by AF, an alternative binding mode is observed: the interface of chain P with the HL chain shifts to residues 9–23, 45–57, and 79–83, which differs substantially from the experimentally determined complex.

The 2D landscapes described by Cα RMSD (backbone deviation) and DockQ (interface similarity) for two illustrative examples (1A22_AB and 2JEL_PLH) are presented in Fig. 3b. Similar to the by-residue contacts, a satisfactory overlap in the landscape is observed for all AF derivatives in 1A22_AB, suggesting their close-to-experiment behaviors. By contrast, significant failures (mismatches between distributions of different simulations) are observed in 2JEL_PLH. We then turn to the statistics of the whole PPI dataset shown in Fig. 3c. We first consider ‘direct’ predictions that compare the predicted and experimental structures directly. Specifically, in this normal way for structural comparison, structural alignments are first conducted. Afterwards, RMSD are calculated for backbone atoms to provide a measure of the overall structural similarity. The integrative score DockQ combines RMSD, fraction of native contacts, and interface similarity into a single metric, offering a more robust evaluation of docking and complex prediction accuracy. For the DockQ evaluator, although the locations of the high-value peaks of all AF derivatives are similar, the intensity of the AF3 peak is much higher than the other. Consequently, AF2.3.1 and af2complex have more predictions in the low-DockQ region (low-quality predictions). The superiority of the AF3 regime is clearer with the RMSD evaluator. Therefore, AF3 outperforms its precedents for these ‘direct’ predictions. As for the simulation-explored structural ensemble, the DockQ and RMSD distributions using predicted starting configurations and experimental structures are shown in Fig. 3d. Here, the whole simulation trajectories initiated from individual starting points/configurations are compared with the reference experimentally deposited structures. Interestingly, based on the DockQ evaluator, the high-quality large-DockQ peak observed in direct AF3 predictions is lowered to a level similar to AF2.3.1, and that of af2complex also drops significantly and is lower than AF2.3.1 and AF3 by 0.5. This could possibly suggest the existence of a deterioration effect upon simulation relaxation, which could be triggered by many factors such as the insufficient force-field accuracy, the inappropriate environmental condition (e.g., certain cofactors or salt could be necessary to stabilize protein conformations), or simply the artifacts in these predictions (e.g., the predicted complex structure is simply unstable). Based on the RMSD evaluator, while AF3 performs still slightly better than the other two predictors, its superiority becomes less pronounced compared with the ‘direct’ case, again hinting at the deterioration effect. Taken together, based on both DockQ and RMSD metrics shown in Fig. 3c-d, none of the deep learning tools could achieve an accuracy level similar to the experimental structure. Therefore, deep learning structural predictors currently still cannot replace experimental structures.

Thermodynamic perspectives---affinities and hot spot identifications

End-point free energy techniques thrive on their high efficiency [45], [46], [47], [48], [49], [50]. Among end-point regimes, an updated protocol with acknowledged accuracy is the alanine scanning with generalized Born and interaction entropy (ASGB-IE) algorithm [51]. Briefly, the computational alanine scanning method works by simulating a single complex structure with molecular dynamics, propagating a trajectory lasting tens of ns, after which one post-processes the existing trajectories with alanine mutations and performs energetics calculations with implicit-solvent models based on the MM/GBSA framework. Unlike the naïve computational workflow [52], ASGB-IE adds an additional component named interaction entropy, which estimates the gas-phase entropic contribution from the energetic fluctuations [39], [51]. The ASGB-IE method enables the calculation of affinity variations (drops) upon alanine mutations in the interfacial region based on end-point trajectories. We thus post-process the simulation trajectories with experimental structures and predictions provided by AF derivatives, securing the alanine-mutation-induced affinity variations and compare them with experimental measurements. The detailed statistics of these post-processing calculations are provided in Table S1.

In Fig. 4a, the correlations between computed and experimental ΔΔG are provided. While the prediction quality with experimental structures outperforms those with predicted structures in terms of scoring (RMSD, MAE and Pearson r) and ranking (τ) alanine mutations, the performance differences between ASGB-IE predictions with different AF derivatives are relatively insignificant. In the correlograms shown in Fig. 4b, while ASGB-IE results with experimental structures still exhibit higher correlations with experimental measurements, the computational outcomes with all of the four structure sets (the experimental structures and the three prediction sets af2complex, AF2.3.1 and AF3) are also highly correlated for both scoring (Pearson) and ranking (Spearman) metrics.

Considering all the affinity and hot-spot statistics presented above, for hot spot identification and estimation of affinity variations, AF predicted structures still cannot replace experimentally deposited crystal structures.

Aside from the face-to-face comparison between calculated and experimental affinity data, another interesting point to investigate is whether the quality of predicted structures is directly correlated with the computed mutation-induced affinity variations. If this is the case, we can directly transform the quality metrics of structural similarities to the quality of affinity calculations, or at least have some guess about them. Therefore, investigating such a structure-affinity relationship could be pivotal for getting a deep understanding of the structure predictions. To this aim, we align the structural deviations (backbone RMSD) with the quality metrics of affinity calculations (RMSE, MAE, Pearson and Kendall correlation coefficients). The scatterings between the quality metrics of structure and affinity are shown in Fig. S5, and the pairwise correlation matrices are presented in Fig. 5. Clearly, there is little correlation between the structure quality and the computed mutational thermodynamics with correlation values in the range of −0.16–0.15, which suggests that we cannot directly transform the quality of structure predictions to that of thermodynamic calculations. Such a phenomenon, actually, is not difficult to understand intuitively, as thermodynamics especially in biomolecular assemblies with a huge number of coupled degrees of freedom is not a single-structure outcome but is related to various factors, e.g., conformational ensemble, the well-known entropy-entropy compensation and solvation environments.

A further possible influencing factor is the lack of conditional details in deep-learning predictions. Experimental data inherently capture the influence of environmental factors such as pH, ionic strength, and solvent molecules, which can critically modulate the conformations of protein–protein complexes. In contrast, current predictive approaches generally operate under idealized assumptions, omitting these variables. Therefore, discrepancies between predicted and experimental structures should not be interpreted solely as evidence of algorithmic shortcomings but also as a consequence of the absence of explicit environmental parameters in predictive frameworks.

In addition to our benchmark evaluation approaches, advanced machine learning and model are also used in other studies in the protein-protein complex property prediction, which are out of the scope of this work. Specifically, MpbPPI [53] as a multi-task pre-training-based equivariant-preserving framework integrates multi-scale geometric features (backbone, side chain, etc.) to improve the prediction of mutation-induced ΔΔG values. Their results demonstrated state-of-the-art performance across multiple benchmark datasets and highlighted the framework’s flexibility in handling mutant structures generated by different modeling tools. More recently, MCGLPPI [54], which integrates MARTINI coarse-grained molecular models into a geometric representation learning framework, significantly reduces computational cost (about one-third compared to atom- and residue-scale methods) while maintaining or even surpassing predictive accuracy for tasks such as binding affinity estimation and interface classification. Together, these works contribute important insights into the role of structural quality and representation learning in PPI ΔΔG prediction. These studies provide complementary perspectives to our work, as they evaluate AF-derived structural inputs in the context of mutation effect prediction and protein–protein property learning, respectively.

Concluding remarks

Structure prediction of biomolecular assemblies as a frontier of bioinformatics and molecular modelling researches has experienced a great step forward by the development of AF derivatives. However, despite the established performance of AF derivatives in structure prediction, their predictive power in protein-protein complexes remains unsatisfactory. One great step further recently is the AF3 model, which has been reported to achieve significant improvements on protein-protein assemblies. While in most benchmark studies the end users evaluate the prediction quality of AF derivatives with general structure evaluators such as RMSD and DockQ, in the current work we report a comprehensive simulation-based modelling perspective. Specifically, we consider not only those general metrics (RMSD and DockQ) and also many structural observables with biophysical insights, e.g., radius of gyration describing the overall compactness of the protein assemblies, the number of hydrogen bond formed between protein-protein complexes to quantify the accuracy of the predicted directionalized polar coordination, the variation of the protein surface area upon the formation of intermolecular packing (the so-called buried surface area), the interfacial contacts with a three-group charged-polar-apolar decomposition that enables the identification of the most problematic parts in the predicted structures. The detailed analyses reveal that while the prediction quality could seem good or satisfactory based on the commonly applied DockQ and RMSD evaluators, the detailed interaction components in the predicted structures could be problematic. For example, there are generally more than 2 hydrogen bonds incorrectly predicted with all AF derivatives; although AF3 achieves better accuracy for all residue-residue contacts involving polar and charged residues, it tends to provide much worse predictions for apolar-apolar interactions. Therefore, although AF derivatives could be applied to predict the general packing behavior of protein-protein complexes, their accuracies in key interaction patterns (especially apolar-apolar packing) could be unreliable. Thus, it could be unsafe to use these hypothetical predictions to understand key interaction components stabilizing protein-protein assemblies. More interestingly, when using molecular simulations to relax the predicted structures, a performance drop is observed for AF predictions in some systems. Specifically, while AF3 provides predictions much better than its precedents AF2 and af2complex according to DockQ and RMSD, its prediction quality drops significantly upon simulation relaxation. This deterioration could be related to various influential factors, such as the instability of the predicted complex structure and/or the inaccuracy of the Hamiltonian applied to atomistic simulations (e.g., force field). Consequently, simulation ensembles with all AF derivatives fail to reach a quality similar to those with experimental structures as starting configurations. Therefore, the current deep learning tools still cannot replace the experimental deposition for protein-protein complexes.

Additionally, we present a practical example of the task of hot-spot modelling and affinity calculation that is widely considered in virtual screening and protein design. We perform a comprehensive scan of single-site mutations in interfacial regions with the ASGB-IE method that extracts mutation-induced affinity variations from end-point trajectories. Face-to-face comparisons with experiment suggest that the ASGB-IE affinity estimates with experimental starting configurations achieve the top performance, based on either error or correlation metrics (RMSE, MAE, Pearson r and Kendall ranking coefficient). The performance difference between ASGB-IE outcomes with experimental structures and the AF predictions is significant, which suggests that AF predictions still have trouble in replacing experimental structures. In terms of hot spot identification, the ASGB-IE scan with experimental starting structures also outperforms the other. Based on commonly used measurements of classification performance (ACC, SE, PR, SP and MCC), a similar conclusion could be obtained, i.e., the hot-spot identification using experimental structures achieving a performance better than those using predicted structures.

Further, we probe the detailed correlations between the quality metrics of structure prediction and affinity calculations, aiming at investigating whether there is a possibility of directly transforming the structure quality into the accuracy of affinity calculations. Interestingly, there is little correlation between structural deviations and all quality metrics of affinity calculations (error and correlation metrics). Such a phenomenon is not unexpected, as the thermodynamics are not a single-structure determinant but related to various factors, such as the stability of the protein structure, the conformational ensemble, and more importantly the entropy-enthalpy compensation.

Overall, based on all structural, simulation and energetic considerations presented in the current work, we conclude that AF derivatives (regardless its version and thus including the most recent AF3) cannot replace experimentally deposited structures for protein-protein complexes.
