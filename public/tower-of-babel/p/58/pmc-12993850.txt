Crystal Structure of Měnglà Virus Nucleoprotein Bound by a Poorly Cross-Reactive Anti-Marburg Virus Nanobody Highlights a Single Amino Acid Affinity Switch, a Feature Also Evident in Dehong Virus
Authors: Laura Jo Sherwood, Alexander Bryan Taylor, Shaun K. Olsen, Andrew Hayhurst
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although Marburg and Měnglà viruses occupy different geographies and genera of the family Filoviridae, genetic and host similarities suggest spillover potential for the latter. While Marburg virus causes transmissible and often fatal hemorrhagic fever in humans, the pathogenicity of Měnglà virus is unknown. Understanding antibody cross-reactivity between the two viruses appears prudent in preparation for detecting the new virus and facilitating component-based studies of replication. Previously, while nanobodies to the monomeric nucleoprotein C-terminal domain (NPCTD) of Marburg virus recognized Měnglà virus NPCTD, cross-reactivity was too weak to quantify monovalent equilibrium concentrations. Here, using oligomeric NP in a nanobody-driven sandwich assay, the cross-reactivity deficit was essentially negated, suggesting we would be able to detect both viruses equally. Curious as to why monovalent reactivity was so disparate, we crystallized the Měnglà virus NPCTD-nanobody complex for X-ray crystal structure determination. Comparative analysis of the antibody–antigen interfaces revealed bonded and nonbonded opportunities at one location in the Marburg complex that were absent in the Měnglà complex. Mutagenesis of the NPCTDs, to make Marburg more Měnglà-like (H690N) and Měnglà more Marburg-like (N692H), resulted in dramatic ablation and restoration of nanobody binding, respectively, via changes in off-rates. Similar trends were observed for the more recently discovered Dehong virus, and dimeric enzymatic and fluorescent reporter fusions improved NP recognition potency within blotting and cell probing assays. Understanding the structural basis for cross-reactivity helps predict the likelihood of detecting viral variants based upon genomic sequence information and can inform the design of antibodies with broader recognition potential.

Měnglà virus (MLAV) is currently the sole member of the species Dianlovirus menglaense of the genus Dianlovirus, within the family Filoviridae. MLAV was identified through next-generation sequencing of liver tissue from Rousettus bats native to Yunnan Province, China.1 Genome organization of MLAV is similar to that of other family members, though the glycoprotein spike has no secreted version, as found in the genera Orthoebolavirus or Cuevavirus, making it more like the genus Orthomarburgvirus in that regard. With 32–54% nucleotide sequence homology to known filoviruses, the new Dianlovirus taxon was placed between the genera Orthoebolavirus and Orthomarburgvirus rather than joining either one. Both of these more familiar genera contain species that are capable of causing outbreaks of hemorrhagic fever in humans, with high case fatality rates and significant secondary negative impacts on the regional societies involved.2 Orthoebolavirus spp. can also decimate dwindling populations of nonhuman primates (NHP) in the wild3 (and references therein), and it is likely that Orthomarburgvirus spp. represent a similar existential threat based on pathogenicity studies within the laboratory setting.4,5

The genus Orthomarburgvirus contains the single species Orthomarburg marburgense comprising two strains, Marburg virus (MARV) and Ravn virus (RAVV), with nucleotide and amino acid homologies in the low 70% range, yet typically strong antibody cross-reactivities.6 In contrast, the genus Orthoebolavirus contains six species, four of which are known to cause human disease, having homologies in the 60% range.7 The six species are essentially immunologically distinct, with naturally occurring cross-reactive monoclonal antibodies being relatively rare, especially those with uniform reactivity across the board.8

Since its discovery in 1967, MARV has emerged more sporadically than any of the orthoebolaviruses, with the largest outbreak in Angola causing 227 deaths at a 90% fatality rate.9 The first MARV outbreak was a consequence of importing virus-infected NHPs from Uganda for tissue culture work in 2 laboratories in Germany and 1 in Yugoslavia (now Serbia) and represented the dawn of filovirus research.10,11 MARV has also been imported to the US12 and The Netherlands13 in 2008 by infected tourists returning from Uganda. RAVV emerged in 1987 with a single fatality and was identified alongside MARV lineages during the 1998–2000 Democratic Republic of Congo14 and 2007 Uganda15 outbreaks. Recently, MARV spillovers have been increasing in frequency and geographic range, with outbreaks in Guinea, Ghana, Equatorial Guinea, Tanzania, and Rwanda.16 At the time of writing, there is currently a MARV disease outbreak in Ethiopia which may also pose an importation risk to Jordan via the substantial migrant workforce.17

Rousettus fruit bats have been previously shown to be asymptomatic reservoir hosts for MARV15,18 and RAVN19 and colonize caves, mines, and abandoned buildings, flying up to 57 km on nightly forages.20 Many countries that have not yet experienced natural outbreaks have identified virus positive Rousettus bats, including South Africa,21 Gabon,22 Zambia,23 and Sierra Leone.24 Mapping the zoonotic niche for MARV disease transmission has predicted 27 suitable countries with around 105 million susceptible residents in total, and that was a decade ago.25 Increasing ecosystem destruction, elevated wildlife-human “interfacing” and human population growth are likely increasing the frequency and size of outbreaks as seen with the orthoebolaviruses.26,27 Although advances have been made in developing vaccines and therapeutics, none is yet approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for MARV/RAVV,28 suggesting that effective outbreak control will still rely on the speedy identification of infected individuals, contact tracing, quarantine, supportive care, and emergency use procedures.29

Naïve Rousettus bats have been established as captive-bred colonies to enable MARV infection to be studied more closely.30,31 While infectious particles of MLAV have yet to be isolated from the Yunnan bats, the potential for MLAV to cause disease has been studied using component-based systems.1,32 Findings are likely to be soon bolstered by challenge studies with recombinant virus made using reverse genetics, in a similar manner to Bombali virus that, like MLAV, has not yet been isolated by conventional virologic methods.33 Regardless of the pathogenicity of MLAV, the finding of a MARV/RAVV-like virus in a new ecological niche in China (population > 1.4 billion people) may well be a portent for similar viruses being found there. Indeed, during the course of this work, another new virus was isolated from Rousettus bats in Yunnan Province, China, and proposed as the founding member of yet another new genus, placed in between Dianlovirus and Orthomarburgvirus (Dehong virus—DEHV, genus Delovirus).34 With the discovery of such viral diversity, understanding limitations in laboratory methods and tools for recognizing MLAV and DEHV over just MARV/RAVV appears to be warranted. Such studies broaden our understanding of antibody–antigen recognition principles and prepare us for an outbreak ahead of time rather than being caught on the back foot. On a more esoteric track, understanding topologies of viral proteins by using antibodies as architectural probes can illuminate similarities that may not necessarily be obvious through sequence comparisons.

Our previous MLAV work had investigated the propensity of 3 anti-MARV/RAVV nucleoprotein C-terminal domain (NPCTD) nanobodies (single domain antibodies, sdAb; variable domain of heavy chain only antibodies, VHH) to react with MLAV NP.35 We demonstrated modest cross-reactivity of the sdAb with recombinant MLAV NP expressed in cells or on Western blots of lysates with dimerization of the sdAb enhancing cross-reactivity. However, using monovalent binding assays between sdAb and the MLAV NPCTD as a nanoluciferase (nLuc) fusion reporter protein, we were unable to establish equilibrium concentrations at 50% (EC50) as the affinity was so low. In contrast, similar monovalent MARV NPCTD-based assays had consistently yielded modest to low nanomolar EC50, depending on which sdAb was being used.36

Here, we first purified oligomeric NP and monomeric NPCTD of MLAV, MARV, and DEHV to compare their abilities to capture sdAb to determine if DEHV manifested the same cross-reactivity drop as MLAV. We then incorporated the oligomeric NP into a sandwich assay, as a viral surrogate, to indicate translational potential for MLAV and DEHV detection and whether monovalent cross-reactivity loss was an impedance. We then pursued a crystal structure of the MLAV NPCTD in complex with an sdAb, to help rationalize poor monovalent affinity, confirming structural findings with site-directed mutagenesis and binding assays for both MLAV and DEHV. Finally, we developed basic research tools useful for recognizing MLAV and DEHV NP in various experimental scenarios based on dimeric enzymatic and fluorescent reporter constructs that overcame deficits manifested by monomeric reporters.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

MLAV and DEHV NP and NPCTD Manifest Low Affinity for sdAb in Antibody Capture Assays Relative to MARV

The sdAb we use here, sdAb A, was originally derived from a semisynthetic library following panning on purified MARV Musoke within our biosafety level 4 (BSL-4) laboratory and was shown to be specific for NP.37 The sdAb was subsequently shown to recognize NP with an EC50 of 2.8 ± 0.7 nM and the NPCTD with an EC50 of 14.1 ± 1.2 nM or 24.7 ± 6.1 nM, depending on the assay format.36 This particular sdAb was chosen for the current study as it showed the best cross-reactivity to MLAV NPCTD from a panel of 3 anti-MARV NP sdAb.35 Since we had no live MLAV or DEHV, we overexpressed human codon-optimized MLAV and DEHV NP genes in mammalian cells and purified recombinant protein, culminating in banding on a CsCl density gradient to isolate oligomeric NP. The purity of the MLAV and DEHV NP was revealed following gel analysis in a denaturing system alongside control MARV NP36 and Orthoebolavirus zairense (Ebola virus, EBOV) NP38 purified previously (Figure 1A).

By using the NP as coating antigens and monomeric anti-MARV sdAb-Gaussia luciferase (gLuc) probes, we were able to confirm low monovalent affinity for MLAV and DEHV in an antibody capture ELISA (Figure 1B) with 100-fold more sdAb-gLuc required to generate 105 RLU compared to MARV. A control sham sdAb-gLuc fusion protein reactive to a coronaviral NP39 was used to demonstrate that responses generated at greater than single nM probe concentrations are nonspecific (Figure 1C). The nonspecific reactivity could be relayed to gLuc alone (Figure S1A) indicating that care must be exercised when interpreting gLuc-based immunoassays at high probe concentrations. To confirm that EBOV NP was present, the anti-EBOV sdAb R148 was employed (Figure S1B). This particular sdAb was derived from an immune llama library following panning on recombinant Reston virus NP, screening on NPCTD, and shown to be uniformly reactive against all known orthoebolaviral NP and NPCTD with EC50 of 1.8 ± 0.1 nM and 2.2 ± 0.7 nM, respectively, for EBOV used here. The plots reveal that 10,000 to 100,000-fold more sdAb-gLuc was required to generate 105 RLU on non-EBOV antigen than EBOV antigen, confirming it is essentially non cross-reactive to MARV, MLAV, and DEHV NP.

To examine whether the affinity difference sdAb A has for MLAV and DEHV relative to MARV is conserved when the CTD is not presented in the oligomeric NP format, we repeated the antibody capture assays using purified NPCTD (Figure 1D) as the coating antigens. Again, we see that around 100-fold more anti-MARV sdAb-gLuc is required to generate 105 RLU with MLAV and DEHV NPCTD than with MARV NPCTD (Figure 1E). We probed the NPCTD with the sham sdAb-gLuc (Figure 1F) and gLuc alone (Figure S1C) to confirm the nonspecific binding activity of gLuc at >1 nM concentrations. The anti-EBOV sdAb R14 was used to confirm the presence of EBOV NPCTD, and here we see around 10,000-fold less probe required to detect cognate EBOV NPCTD than MARV or DEHV NPCTD, and a 100-fold difference for MLAV NPCTD, although the latter plots tend to plateau at 106 RLU (Figure S1D).

While the performance of sdAb A on MLAV and DEHV relative to MARV is equivalent whether NP or NPCTD is targeted, NPCTD has somewhat muted signals across the board, approximately 10-fold lower compared to NP. Since the predicted molecular weights of NP and NPCTD are around 78 kDa and 12 kDa, respectively, the equivalent coating antigen concentrations used would be expected to provide 6- to 7-fold more NPCTD binding capacity than NP. However, it is known that even for hardy proteins, such as IgG, passive immobilization onto ELISA plate wells can eliminate 90–97% of the binding functionality.40,41 Furthermore, the small size of an immobilized NPCTD is likely to limit the number of orientations that can present the epitope without steric hindrance from the polystyrene. In contrast, while some of the NPCTD displayed on oligomeric NP are likely to be compromised and inaccessible on immobilization, it is likely that many are not involved, retaining functionality and epitope access, which explains the enhanced responses of NP.

MLAV and DEHV NP Display Equivalent Affinity to MARV NP within Antigen Sandwich Assays

One of our long-term interests has been to harness sdAb in monoclonal affinity reagent sandwich assays (MARSA), where a single sdAb clone serves as both a captor and a tracer by virtue of the antigen being oligomeric. Following mild detergent lysis of a filovirus to expose the ribonucleocapsid containing oligomeric NP comprising over 1000 monomers,42 we have shown that MARSA enables detection with high sensitivities down to a few plaque-forming units (pfu) of live MARV and several orthoebolaviruses.37,43 The sdAb is used to coat ELISA wells that act as the captor for the oligomeric NP which is subsequently detected with a sdAb tracer. The format of the tracer used here is sdAb displayed on the tips of filamentous phage particles comprising several thousand copies of the major coat protein (g8p). Tracer bound to NP in the ELISA well is then detected by an antiphage g8p horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugate that affords dramatic signal amplification compared to directly using sdAb-reporter enzyme fusion proteins.8 A schematic of the assay is shown in Figure 2A. It is likely, though unproven, that the bivalent nature of the IgG-based antiphage g8p antibody may cross-link neighboring particles, potentially reducing dissociation by stabilizing them as a matrix. Understanding the drivers of MARSA performance and whether ultrahigh-affinity antibodies are required seemed like an excellent question to explore with our anti-MARV sdAb plus MLAV and DEHV antigen pairing.

Examining the recombinant NP preparations by electron microscopy reveals their oligomeric nature, with all appearing as collections of loosely arranged coils (Figure 2B). Recombinant MARV NP derived from mammalian cell culture is known to form coils44 reminiscent of material released following detergent lysis of MARV particles.45 Using live virus, we had previously shown that exposure of MARV and RAVV to 0.1% Triton X-100 elevated MARSA signals by 100–1000-fold37 indicating that release of oligomeric NP is key to sensitivity. Using the MARSA format, we see an evening up of the MLAV and MARV curves, as the massively parallel avidity of the NP oligomer appears to overcome the monovalent affinity differences when using passively immobilized sdAb as the captor and phage-displayed sdAb as the tracer (Figure 2C). We subsequently employed DEHV NP in the MARSA system to show that it also tracked with MARV NP (Figure 2D). Collectively, the data indicate that, despite the low monovalent affinity that sdAb A has for MLAV and DEHV NP, if we were to use the current MARSA system for MLAV and DEHV detection, it is likely to equal MARV sensitivity. Such a finding may temper the continual pursuit of ultrahigh-affinity antibodies for pathogen detection, since we show that the low monovalent affinity of the nanobody appears compensated for by the avidity of the oligomeric antigen being targeted. It is generally agreed that, if it can be obtained, serum is the gold-standard diagnostic specimen for testing for filoviral infection.9,46,47 Therefore, we spiked pooled anonymous human serum with various recombinant NP and employed the mix in the MARSA format (Figure 2E). If we use an RLU that is around 10-fold background, we see an approximately 10-fold drop in sensitivity when working in this complex matrix, though again see parity among MARV, MLAV, and DEHV NP.

Using these reagents on live MARV Angola, previously37 we were able to detect around 10 pfu with signal-to-noise ratios of 10, which, assuming 1062 molecules of NP per particle29 equates to around 1e+4 molecules in total. Here, in PBS, a signal-to-noise ratio of 10 equates to around 2e−3 ng of NP, which, assuming a molecular weight of 78 kDa for NP, equals 1.5e+7 molecules. A thousand-fold or so disconnect between live virus and recombinant NP is not surprising since the live virus study used cell culture supernatant rather than purified virus and may well have contained additional NP released from cells experiencing cytopathic effects and lysis. Furthermore, it is also known that MARV has particle-to-pfu ratios of between 1 and 4 logs depending on passage number,48 further adding to NP registering within a live virus sandwich assay but not necessarily reflected by the pfu count. As long as these caveats are borne in mind, recombinant NP oligomers offer a safe, accessible, and biochemically quantifiable surrogate to path find detection systems and are especially useful in the absence of live virus (MLAV and DEHV) or biosafety level 4 capabilities.

MLAV and DEHV NPCTD Manifest Multiple Amino Acid Differences Compared to MARV and RAVV

Figure 3 is an updated summary of the predicted amino acid sequences of the NPCTD of all the known human MARV and RAVV from human disease outbreaks currently listed on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Website and available in GenBank. The limited variation between MARV and RAVV isolates since 1967 contrasts with the extensive differences apparent in MLAV (27/63 = 43%) and DEHV (22/63 = 35%) i.e., 57% and 65% homologous to MARV, respectively. Differences in contact residues and even noncontact residues, through second-shell effects, can create potential challenges for an anti-MARV/RAVV antibody to cross-react with these newly discovered viruses.

Periplasmic Coexpression of MLAV NPCTD and Anti-MARV NP sdAb A in E. coli Conveniently Generates a Complex for Crystallization

Our initial attempts at periplasmic expression of MARV NPCTD many years ago were unsuccessful, as we had utilized a suboptimal construct. Originally designed for epitope mapping by Western blotting cytosolic fractions of E. coli expression cultures, the presence of a His6 tag at the C-terminus may well have perturbed the tertiary structure since the last NPCTD residue L695 interacts with Y667 and R642 to close the geomorphic “basin”. Consequently, we used cytoplasmic expression of N-terminally His6-tagged MARV NPCTD to generate crystals of complexes after mixing with sdAb.36 Since then, we have successfully produced a range of N-terminally His6-tagged orthoebolaviral NPCTD in the periplasm, often coexpressing them with tagless sdAb to form purifiable complexes that crystallized and diffracted well.38 We therefore employed the same bait-and-prey strategy here for the MLAV NPCTD and anti-MARV sdAb A. Following immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) and size-exclusion chromatography (SEC), the sdAb-NPCTD complex peak was easily resolved from unbound NPCTD (Figure 4A) and was very pure with the sdAb and NPCTD bands overlapping (Figure 4B). Crystallization screens comprising 1,632 different conditions, each at two temperatures, resulted in crystals appearing in only a single well between 73 and 163 days (Figure 4C), which were subjected to X-ray diffraction (Table 1).

Crystal Structure of the MLAV-NPCTD and sdAb A Complex

A cartoon showing the overall arrangement of the complex reveals a dimer with packing occurring between NPCTD atoms (Figure 4D). During the course of this work, Ferrero and colleagues49 generated crystal structures of the MLAV NPCTD using amino acids 573 to 697 to 4.05 Å resolution, without sdAb or tags, and they too showed a similar dimeric arrangement, indicating that our sdAb had not perturbed the native structure. In fact, our NPCTD dimer aligns to theirs (PDB entry 8P10, chains A/B) with an RMSD of 0.51 Å over 833 of 1078 atoms aligned using PyMOL.50 We backtracked and performed analytical SEC to examine if the MLAV NPCTD was more prone to dimer formation than MARV and, although we see a small peak for MLAV that is not obvious for MARV (Figure 4E), the dimer did not significantly relay to the complex (and was not particularly evident in the more concentrated scaled-up complex SEC chromatogram of Figure 4A).

The sdAb A Epitope Appears Generally Conserved between MLAV and MARV NPCTD

Figure 5A reveals that most of the major interactions predicted to exist between sdAb and MARV NPCTD are conserved in the MLAV complex, except those revolving around T53 of complementarity determining region (CDR) 2. In the case of MARV, since the H690 NH group is 2.81 Å from the OH group of T53, it is expected to hydrogen bond. In contrast, MLAV has N692 at the equivalent position (MLAV NP is 2 amino acids longer than MARV NP), which retracts the side chain from CDR 2. Electrostatic surface renditions of the two NPCTDs, highlighting the hydrophobic basin and negatively charged overlook, reveal how similar the overall topographies of the two NPCTDs are (Figure 5B). On closer inspection, the NH2 and OD1 atoms of MLAV N692 are 5.4 Å and 4.1 Å (chains A:B) or 6.2 Å and 7.3 Å (chains B:C) from the OH group of T53, too distant to hydrogen bond, and also reducing nonbonded interactions, in contrast to MARV H690.

Chimerising the MLAV and MARV NPCTD Experimentally Confirms the N692/H690 Location as Pivotal for sdAb A Affinity

Using site-directed mutagenesis, we made MARV NPCTD more MLAV-like (H690N) and MLAV more MARV-like (N692H), purifying the variants as nLuc fusion proteins (Figure 6A). Titrating these nLuc-NPCTD over sdAb A reveals that mutant MARV H690N loses affinity and adopts a MLAV-like profile, while mutant MLAV N692H gains affinity and adopts a MARV-like profile (Figure 6B). In silico mutagenesis of the MLAV NPCTD using PyMOL50 reveals that N692H restores proximity to sdAb T53, potentially enabling hydrogen bonding and perhaps other nonbonded contacts to occur (Figure 6C). In contrast, by mutating MARV NPCTD in silico to H690N, we retract the side chain out of the range of T53 and lose interfacing opportunities (Figure 6D).

DEHV NPCTD Manifests the Same Trends as MLAV NPCTD for sdAb A Affinity

By comparing the amino acid composition of the expected DEHV epitope with reference to Figure 3, the main interface residues are either conserved or have side chains with similar characteristics, except for Q692 (like MLAV, DEHV NP is 2 amino acids longer than MARV). We first used in silico mutagenesis to mutate MARV NPCTD to H690Q, revealing that the NH2 group of the mutant side-chain residue would be distant and unlikely to engage with the CDR2 T53 OH group (Figure 7A). We then experimentally made MARV more DEHV-like (H690Q) and DEHV more MARV-like (Q692H) and purified the corresponding nLuc-NPCTD fusions (Figure 7B). Titrating the proteins over sdAb reveals that the MARV H690Q adopts a more DEHV-like profile, while the DEHV Q692H adopts a more MARV-like profile, confirming that DEHV Q692 poses a vulnerability similar to that of MLAV N692 for recognition by this sdAb (Figure 7C).

Kinetic Explanation for sdAb A NPCTD Recognition Patterns

Since the nLuc-NPCTD fusion proteins are monomeric, they offer a convenient route to determining monovalent affinities using biolayer interferometry (BLI), whereby the sdAb is immobilized on a streptavidin sensor surface as a ligand, and the fusion proteins are employed in solution as analytes. Titrating the “good” interactions from 25 nM down and the “poor” interactions from 200 nM down generated sensorgrams (Figure 8) that enabled global fitting of a 1:1 interaction model and estimation of kinetic constants (Table 2). The MLAV and DEHV NPCTD have approximately 11- and 7-fold lower affinity, respectively, for sdAb than the MARV NPCTD, with the more homologous DEHV sequence perhaps offering a slightly better fit. When the restorative mutations N/Q692H are made, the MLAV and DEHV NPCTD mutants approach affinities that are equivalent to MARV. Conversely, when the H690N/Q ablative mutations are made on the MARV NPCTD, the affinities approach those of the MLAV and DEHV NPCTD. While the on-rates are fairly similar among the various combinations of interactions, the off-rates are reduced by around 10-fold for MLAV, DEHV, and MARV H690N/Q, indicating that the absence of the histidine side chain at 690/692 leads to rapid dissociation of the NPCTD from the sdAb.

Compensating for Low Monovalent Affinity through Dimerization Facilitates DEHV NP Detection by sdAb A for Basic Research Endeavors

While we had previously been able to improve recognition of MLAV NP through dimerization of anti-MARV sdAb A, it was unclear if this strategy would be sufficient for DEHV NP. Understanding mechanisms of viral replication in their own right, or when teased apart using recombinant component-based strategies, often involves using immunoreagents in blotting and microscopy procedures. Unfortunately, there is a paucity of suitable and affordable commercial immunoreagents for several filoviral proteins especially those of MLAV and DEHV. Since sdAb are highly modular and can be inexpensively produced in E. coli to multimilligram levels, they make very convenient molecular probes when fused to enzymatic or fluorescent reporters. We therefore explored the ability of anti-MARV sdAb A to recognize denatured DEHV NP on Western blots when fused to monomeric enhanced ascorbate peroxidase (APEX2)51 or dimeric enhanced ascorbate peroxidase (dEAPX).52 While the monomeric fusion protein fails to detect anything but MARV, despite the long exposure time, the dimeric fusion successfully detects all three NP in short order (Figure 9A). The Coomassie-stained gel serves to confirm that equivalent amounts of cell lysate were loaded. While the denaturing Laemmli53 gel system used here will destroy the overall conformational epitope encoded by the NPCTD tertiary structure, the individual alpha helices may refold in the absence of SDS once transferred to the membrane. Even in the absence of refolding, small linear determinants may collectively enable some degree of sdAb binding especially at the high concentrations localized in a band on the blot and leveraging the avidity of dEAPX. That recognition of the MLAV and DEHV NP requires dimeric sdAb-dEAPX indicates that residue 692 is crucial, and the absence of the optimal histidine side chain is catastrophic for monomeric sdAb binding to denatured material. We further demonstrated that this is the case by Western blotting the repertoire of purified nLuc-NPCTD fusion proteins, showing the same need for dEAPX over APEX whenever the pivotal histidine was absent (Figure S2).

We also used the sdAb-APEX2 and sdAb-dEAPX2 fusions to probe HEK293T cells transiently expressing the various NP following fixation and permeabilization, developing signals with Amplex UltraRed (Figure 9B) that generate a soluble fluorescent product. Counterintuitively, we see less signal in the dEAPX-probed cells than in the APEX2 wells, though this can be rationalized as substrate exhaustion and soluble product diffusion over the 10 min development time, since the mean fluorescent intensities (MFI) of the supernatants are 2- to 3-fold higher for sdAb-dEAPX than sdAb-APEX2 (Figure 9C). Although not as quantitative, substrates that generate an insoluble precipitating product that remains within the NP-expressing cell can be used to allow visualization by light microscopy.35,52

To enable more localized fluorescence microscopy, we compared our existing sdAb-mNeonGreen fusion, previously used to detect MARV-infected cells8 with a tandem sdAb–sdAb-mNeonGreen fusion in their ability to detect the recombinant NP panel. Both sdAb fusion proteins were produced side-by-side to high purity (Figure 9D) and used to probe Vero cells transiently expressing the various NP. The tandem sdAb construct appeared to detect a broader swath of cells with both high and low expression levels, while the basic monomeric sdAb construct seemed only capable of detecting a few of the highest-expressing cells for MLAV and DEHV NP (Figure 9E). While semiquantitative in nature, these assays serve to demonstrate that we can now detect MLAV and DEHV NP as well as MARV NP to facilitate mechanistic studies of viral replication using component-based approaches involving blotting and fluorescence microscopy.

CONCLUSION

Understanding the molecular basis for antigen recognition by an antibody through analyzing predicted interface interactions coupled with strategic mutagenesis advances our knowledge of what makes a good antibody–antigen interaction (and what does not!). Here, after purifying the complex of MLAV NPCTD and sdAb A, we were fortunate to have stumbled upon conditions that allowed the formation of a crystal that enabled a high-resolution crystal structure to be determined. Our study illuminates the requirements for an equally panreactive antibody across three genera, and it appears that this sdAb would need to both accommodate the H690 side chain and “reach out” to contact N/Q 692. Such a feature is likely to be very challenging to engineer, and we may have to put this sdAb to one side or devise an alternative strategy that improves affinity to NPCTD with or without H690/692. An additional issue with this CDR is the significant amount of restructuring W55 undergoes,36 and so any gains by substituting T53 must not impede existing binding mechanics. Since we appear able to detect the more viral-like oligomeric NP in the avid sandwich assay with equivalent sensitivities across MARV, MLAV, and DEHV, the monovalent affinity deficit that sdAb A has for MLAV and DEHV NPCTD is not an immediate worry.

While the classical sandwich ELISA format in 96-well plates is sensitive and potentially can be made even more so,54,55 it involves many washing steps and is not readily amenable to fieldwork without a mobile laboratory. However, the process could potentially be miniaturized within microfluidic chip or paper-based ELISA systems56 or even adapted to supersensitive phage-based lateral flow assay (LFA) for point-of-care testing.57 Representing around a quarter of the total MARV particle protein,42 NP makes an ideal diagnostic target and was recently leveraged in a conventional IgG-based LFA for MARV and RAVV.58 It is fair to say that most antigen-based detection systems struggle to compete with the sensitivity offered by nucleic acid-based testing, especially during the early asymptomatic period of infection when viral titers are low. During the initial phase of an outbreak, testing typically involves RT-PCR to preliminarily confirm the nature of the infectious agent, followed by next-generation sequencing (NGS) for precise identification, as exemplified by the 2021 Guinea episode.59 As outbreaks escalate, e.g., during the large Sudan virus outbreak in Uganda 2000–2001, it was acknowledged that relying on a single RT-PCR test can be risky owing to false-negative and false-positive results that can escalate as sample processing increases, and that a combination of tests including ELISA should be used.46 Advances in antigen biosensing technology have been able to improve upon RT-PCR for EBOV in an NHP model of disease,60 and similar devices could bolster diagnostic reliability for outbreak control if fieldable, affordable, and retuned to an alternative antigen for MARV.

From an evolutionary standpoint, it can be tricky to decipher relationships between viruses based on genomic sequences alone since they have inherently fast mutation rates, and this is especially true of RNA viruses.61 Consequently, there is a movement to use protein structures in phylogenetic classification since these are more conserved.62,63 While comparison of the C-alpha trace via RMSD is a good start, the ultimate function of a protein is determined by its surface architecture. Here, we have used our sdAb A to map one landscape of MLAV and MARV through a combination of X-ray crystallography, interface prediction, confirmatory mutagenesis, and kinetic rationalization. Through this experimental pipeline, we have revealed a single location that varies between MARV, MLAV, and DEHV (for which we do not yet have a structure) that appears to mildly erode the epitope of this particular sdAb. Such high conservation between viruses originating in Africa and China over 50 years apart, relative to their discoveries, reaffirms our notion that this particular surface is likely serving a vital role in the viruses’ life cycles and makes an ideal diagnostic target to pursue.36 Furthermore, combined with dimeric reporter approaches, the anti-NPCTD sdAb may offer up a semiagnostic way of surveying animal tissues for the presence of MARV and MARV-like antigens to complement NGS approaches in future viral discovery campaigns.

METHODS

General Cloning Procedures

Recombinant DNA work followed established procedures and employed commercially available reagents: Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA); restriction enzymes and β-agarase (New England BioLabs, Beverly, MA); T4 DNA ligase and CIP (Roche, Nutley, NJ); Genetic Technology grade low melting temperature agarose for in-gel cloning (Lonza, Walkersville, MD); oligonucleotides and gBlocks (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA); plasmid miniprep purification kits (Qiagen, Venlo, Netherlands). Assemblies involving PCR amplification or gBlocks were always first mapped to confirm the junctions were intact and had the desired insert size, and then sent for Sanger sequencing (ACGT Technology Inc., Wheeling, IL) to ensure that the insert was as intended. Cloning was carried out in XL1-Blue cells using 2xTY medium (Difco), 2% glucose, solid media incubated at 37 °C, and liquid miniprep media at 30 °C with appropriate antibiotics. Full details of cloning, oligonucleotides, maps, and sequences of resulting constructs are available on request.

sdAb-gLuc and gLuc Capture ELISA

Recombinant NP or NPCTD at 1 μgmL−1 in PBS was used to coat wells of white ELISA plates (Costar, Corning, NY) at 4 °C overnight under ELISA plate seals and in a moistened, rubber-gasketed sandwich box. Plates were washed 3 times with a squirt bottle of PBS over the sink and then filled to brimming with 2% nonfat dried milk (Carnation) in PBS (MPBS) at room temperature. After 1 h incubation on the bench, the MPBS was discarded, the plates were thoroughly blotted dry on paper towels, and wells were probed with dilutions of sdAb-gLuc or gLuc in MPBS. Plates were sealed and placed in the sandwich box for 1 h. Plates were then washed 3 times with PBS + 0.1% Tween-20 (PBST), 2 times with PBS, and then tapped dry thoroughly. Signals were developed with an injection of 100 μL of coelenterazine (NanoLight Technology, Pinetop, AZ) solution (50 μL of 1 mgmL−1 stock in ethanol per 12 mL of lucky buffer; 10 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, 500 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) and captured with a 2 s integration using a GloMax Discover luminometer (Promega). Titrations were performed in duplicate wells, experiments were performed on 2 separate occasions, and curves are plots of means of the relative light units (RLU) with error bars representing ± standard deviation (SD).

NP Sandwich ELISA

Phagemid displaying the anti-MARV NP sdAb A36 or anti-orthoebolaviral NP sdAb R148 were generated previously and were retrieved from archived stocks stored at −80 °C. We used passive immobilization of 1 μgmL−1 sdAb-BAP in 100 μL volumes of PBS overnight at 4 °C. Following washing 3 times with PBS and drying, the plates were filled to brimming with MPBS for 1 h. The block was removed, the plates were dried, and different concentrations of recombinant NP in MPBS were added for 1 h. Following washing 3 times with PBST and 2 times with PBS, a constant amount of the relevant phage preparation (1 μL per 100 μL of MPBS) was added to each well, and the mixture was incubated for 1 h. After washing, 100 μL volumes of a 1 in 5000 dilution of anti-M13 g8p horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugate (GE Healthcare) in MPBS were added, and plates were incubated for 1 h. After washing, signals were developed with 100 μL of SuperSignal ELISA Pico chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher) and collected with a 2 s integration on the luminometer. The initial comparison of MLAV, MARV, and EBOV NP was performed on 3 separate occasions, while the comparison of DEHV and MARV NP was performed on 2 separate occasions. Duplicate wells were used for each dilution, and the curves are plots of mean RLU ± SD. Bait-Prey Production of the sdAb MLAV-NPCTD Complex

The gene encoding sdAb A was PCR amplified from pecan12664 to delete the His6 tag and leave TVSS as the last amino acids, and mobilized via Nco I and Hind III to ampicillin resistant periplasmic expression vector pecan21938 bearing the pel B signal sequence. The vector was cotransformed with pecan236 MLAV NPCTD into E. coli Tuner + pRARE and glycerol-stocked. A healthy dollop of a glycerol stock was grown up in 50 mL of terrific broth (TB) plus 2% glucose at 30 °C for 18–24 h with both ampicillin and hygromycin at 200 μg mL−1, plus chloramphenicol at 30 μg mL−1 in 250 mL Bellco baffled flasks. The saturated cultures were poured into 450 mL of glucose-free TB in 2.5 L Bellco baffled flasks with antibiotics and shaken at 175 rpm for 3 to 3.5 h at 25 °C. Expression was induced by the addition of IPTG to 1 mM, and incubation continued for 3 to 3.5 h at 25 °C. Cells were pelleted at 3,500 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C (Beckman Allegra 6R swing-out rotor), drained, and weighed. Cells were osmotically shocked65 by resuspension in 14 mL ice-cold 0.75 M sucrose in 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, followed by the addition of 1.4 mL of 1 mg mL−1 hen egg lysozyme (Sigma), followed by dropwise addition of 28 mL of 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0, while swirling on ice for 15 min. 2 mL of 0.5 M MgCl2 was added, swirling continued for an additional 15 min, and cells were pelleted as above. The 45 mL supernatant (osmotic shockate) was mixed with 5 mL of 10× IMAC (immobilized metal affinity chromatography buffer – 0.2 M Na2HPO4, 5 M NaCl, 0.2 M imidazole, 1% Tween-20, pH 7.5). A 0.5 mL aliquot of High-Performance Ni Sepharose (GE Healthcare, Pittsburgh, PA) was added, and the suspension was gently mixed on ice for 1 h. Resin was pelleted at 3,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C and washed twice with 50 mL of 1× IMAC solution before elution with 2 mL of 0.5 M imidazole in 1× IMAC buffer, pH 7.4, in Poly-Prep columns (Bio-Rad). Proteins were concentrated in an Amicon 10 kDa ultrafiltration device (Millipore, Billerica, MA) to 200 μL for separation by a Superdex 200 16/600 column (GE Healthcare) operating in 10 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.5. Peak fractions of the complex were further concentrated before submission to crystallization trials. Proteins were quantified by UV absorption and analyzed by SDS-PAGE and Coomassie blue staining for impurities.

Crystallization, Structure Determination, and Refinement

Automated screening for crystallization was carried out using the sitting drop vapor-diffusion method with an Art Robbins Instruments Phoenix system in the Structural Biology Core at UT Health San Antonio. Crystals were obtained at 22 °C from the Molecular Dimensions MIDASplus Screen condition E7, containing 0.1 M sodium malonate dibasic monohydrate, 0.1 M HEPES pH 7, and 30% w/v poly(acrylic acid sodium salt) 2100. The crystals were transferred to undersized cryoloops and manipulated to wick off excess mother liquor prior to flash-cooling in liquid nitrogen. X-ray diffraction data were acquired using our home source Rigaku MicroMax 007HF X-ray Generator, and data were integrated and scaled using XDS.66 The structure of sdAb A + MLAV NPCTD was determined by the molecular replacement method implemented in PHASER67 using Protein Data Bank entry 6APP.36 Coordinates were refined using PHENIX,68 including simulated annealing with torsion angle dynamics, and alternated with manual rebuilding using COOT.69 Data collection and refinement statistics are listed in Table 1.

Generation of nLuc-NPCTD Fusion Proteins

pENCO29 is a T7 vector expressing nLuc-His6-NPCTD fusions, and we had used this system successfully for MARV and MLAV previously. Here, the DEHV NPCTD was amplified from the NP gene, while splice overlap extension PCR or gBlocks were used to introduce H692 mutations into the MLAV and DEHV NPCTD and 690 mutations into the MARV NPCTD. Fragments were inserted via Not I and Hind III, and constructs were mobilized to BL21 (DE3) STAR plus pRARE for production. Starter cultures were grown for 18–24 h at 30 °C in 50 mL of TB with 2% glucose, ampicillin at 200 μgmL−1, and chloramphenicol at 30 μgmL−1 until saturation. Cultures were poured into 450 mL glucose, and antibiotic-free TB and grown with vigorous aeration in 2.5 L Bellco baffled flasks for 3.5 h at 30 °C and 0.5 h at 25 °C, and then induced for 3 to 3.5 h with 0.1 mM IPTG. Cells were pelleted at 3,500 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, the pellets drained of excess media, and stored at −80 °C until ready for bead beating (BioSpec Products, Bartlesville, OK). Once thawed, the pellets were resuspended in 20 mL 1× IMAC buffer plus complete protease inhibitor (Roche) to 1× concentration and added to a 15 mL chamber filled halfway with 0.1 mm glass beads. The chamber was topped off with 1× IMAC buffer to remove any air bubbles, and the cell/bead mixture was blended on ice within a 4 °C fridge for a total of 12 min with 2 min on and 2 min cooling on ice in between. Once the contents settled, the cell debris was transferred to a 50 mL conical tube and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was decanted into a new 50 mL tube and centrifuged at 9,500 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C (Sorvall RC 6+, F13 FiberLite rotor). The supernatant was filtered through a 32 mm diameter 0.8/0.2 μm filter (Pall, Port Washington, NY) and applied to a 1 mL HisTrapHP column equilibrated in 1× IMAC buffer. Protein was eluted with a 0–500 mM imidazole gradient in 1× IMAC buffer. The peak fractions were pooled, concentrated to 800 μL, and purified with 4 runs on a Superdex Increase 10/300 GL column in PBS. Protein was pooled, made to 15% glycerol, aliquoted for storage at −80 °C and quantified by UV adsorption, and analyzed by SDS-PAGE to assess purity.

nLuc-NPCTD Capture ELISA

White ELISA plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with 100 μL of 1 μgmL−1 neutravidin in PBS. Plates were washed three times with PBS and then blocked by filling to brimming with Bioplex Buffer (Bb – 2% BSA, 0.05% Tween-20 in PBS) for 1 h. The block was removed, plates thoroughly dried, and 100 μL of 100 nM anti-MARV sdAb-BAP A in Bb was then added to wells for 1 h. Wells were washed 3 times with PBST and 2 times with PBS. MPBS was added to the wells to brimming for 1 h to further block the sdAb, and then dilutions of nLuc-NPCTD or nLuc in MPBS were added for 1 h. Following washing, signals were developed and captured as for the gLuc assay described above. Dilutions used duplicate wells, and each set of titrations was repeated on 3 separate occasions (for the MLAV vs MARV comparison) and 2 separate occasions (for the DEHV vs MARV comparison). Curves are plots of the mean RLU, minus nLuconly controls, and ±SD.

Biolayer Interferometry (BLI)

An Octet R4 (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany) was used to examine the interaction between sdAb A (ligand) and the nLuc-NPCTD fusions (analytes) in 1× kinetics buffer, shaking at 1000 rpm at 30 °C. Streptavidin sensors were sensor-checked for 180 s before being loaded with 100 nM singly biotinylated sdAb to yield a response of approximately 0.5 nm in 180 s. Antibody-loaded sensors were then baselined for 180 s in fresh buffer before being transferred to wells containing varying concentrations of analyte: low nM affinity clones, 12.5 to 0.1953 nM; high nM affinity clones, 200 to 3.13 nM; and a zero-concentration reference well. The association phase was 180 s (low nM clones) or 360 s (high nM clones), followed by 360 s for dissociation by returning to the baseline buffer. Double referencing was employed with a set of streptavidin sensors processed as above but remaining in the sensor check for an additional 180 s rather than being loaded with sdAb. To minimize the impact of nonspecific binding on the off-rates, where responses failing to return to zero can generate overly long values, we only analyzed 120 s (low nM clones) or 30 s (high nM clones) in the 1:1 interaction model. Steady-state equilibrium Kd could also be obtained for the high nM clones. A control of unfused nLuc, using dilutions from 200 to 3.13 nM, was similarly analyzed to ensure no responses were due to the generic fusion partner.
