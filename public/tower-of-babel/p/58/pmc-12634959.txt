Outcomes and Usability of Formoterol-Budesonide Delivered Through Synchrobreathe® in Patients With Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease: The EMERGE Study
Authors: Santhalingam Balamurugan, Himanshu Pophale, Utsav Samariya, Komalkirti Apte, Sheetu Singh, Raja Dhar, Sujeet Rajan, Rajesh Swarnakar, Deepak Talwar, Meena Lopez, Vaishali Naik, Sandesh Sawant, Sushmeeta Chhowala, Abhijit Vaidya, Jaideep Gogtay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a significant public health concern, and inhalation therapy is a critical component of its management. However, using an inhaler is challenging, especially for patients with COPD, who are often elderly and experience reduced dexterity, visual and cognitive impairment, and difficulty synchronizing inhalation with dose actuation. Synchrobreathe® is an easy-to-use, breath-actuated inhaler that releases medication during inhalation, addressing these challenges. Although its use in asthma is well documented, evidence in COPD is limited.MethodsThis prospective, multicenter, observational study carried out during August 2021 and August 2022 evaluated the clinical outcomes of the budesonide/formoterol combination (BUD/FORM) delivered through Synchrobreathe® in patients with COPD in India. The primary endpoint was disease control, that is, change from baseline in COPD Assessment Test (CAT) score at 12 weeks. Secondary endpoints included changes in CAT and modified Borg Dyspnea Scale (mBDS) scores and device usability. Adverse events were monitored over 12 weeks.ResultsIn the study population (N = 250), significant reductions from baseline were observed in mean CAT (−6.56 ± 0.33; p < 0.001) and mBDS (−1.60 ± 0.09; p < 0.0001) scores at 12 weeks. Almost all (98%) patients were satisfied with Synchrobreathe® and preferred it over their previous inhalers. No significant adverse events were reported.ConclusionBUD/FORM delivered through Synchrobreathe® significantly improved CAT and mBDS scores, with no reported serious adverse events in patients with COPD. Its simple usage makes it an effective option for this patient population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40801-025-00522-w.

Key Points

In this study of Indian COPD patients, 12 weeks of treatment with budesonide/formoterol via Synchrobreathe® led to significant improvement in symptoms and health status in those inadequately controlled on prior therapy or dissatisfied with their previous inhaler.The majority of patients found the Synchrobreathe® inhaler easy to use and preferred it over their previous inhalers, highlighting its suitability for individuals who experience difficulty with conventional inhalers.The treatment using Synchrobreathe® inhaler was well tolerated, with no serious adverse events reported, supporting its practical use in routine clinical settings.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive respiratory condition characterized by restricted airflow and pulmonary tissue damage [1]. COPD is defined by the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) as “a heterogeneous lung condition characterized by chronic respiratory symptoms (dyspnea, cough, sputum production, and/or exacerbations) due to abnormalities of the airways (bronchitis, bronchiolitis) and/or alveoli (emphysema) that cause persistent, often progressive, airflow obstruction” [2, 3]. It is associated with substantial chronic morbidity and mortality. In 2019, an estimated 391.9 million global cases of COPD were defined based on the GOLD criteria, and almost 81% of these cases were from low- and middle-income countries [2]. In the same year, the COPD prevalence in India was 37.8 million, and almost one in ten deaths in the country was attributed to COPD [3].

Inhalation therapy remains the cornerstone of COPD management [4]. Although inhalation therapy with bronchodilators is the mainstay treatment for COPD, the addition of inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) is beneficial for patients who have been hospitalized previously for exacerbations, for those with two or more moderate exacerbations, those who have ≥ 300 cells/µL blood eosinophils, or those with concomitant/history of asthma [5]. Results of the TORCH trial noted the benefits of combining ICS with a long-acting β2-‍adrenergic receptor agonist (LABA) in relation to a reduction in exacerbation rates and improvement in health status and lung function [6]. Budesonide/formoterol, an ICS-LABA combination, has been extensively studied in the management of COPD. Clinical trials have demonstrated the efficacy and safety of budesonide/formoterol in enhancing lung function, decreasing exacerbations, and improving quality of life in individuals with moderate to severe COPD [7–11]. This combination has shown significant improvements in pulmonary function, COPD symptoms, and health status compared with individual components or placebo [8, 9].

While the choice of medication is important, reports highlight that the type of delivery device prescribed also has a substantial bearing on adherence to treatment and, subsequently, treatment outcomes. Inhaler devices ensure the targeted delivery of medication to the lungs, optimizing therapeutic efficacy and minimizing systemic exposure [12]. Inhaler devices, such as dry powder inhalers (DPIs), pressurized metered-dose inhalers (pMDIs), breath-‍actuated inhalers (BAIs), and nebulizers, provide various options for physicians based on patient requirements and preferences. However, device selection and acceptance play a crucial role in the treatment of COPD [13]. Patient surveys have revealed disparities in patients’ opinions about different devices. Moreover, less favored devices are associated with poor adherence. Device characteristics such as usability and ease of training could positively impact adherence [12]. The frequency of errors with the inhaler technique is reported in to be the range of 71–86% in patients older than 65 years, with the error frequency proportionally increasing with age. The common types of errors reported with the pMDI are actuation of the pMDI at the end of inspiration, too rapid inhalation, premature actuation, actuation during expiration, and multiple actuations [14]. The most common errors associated with DPIs include insufficient inspiratory effort, exhaling into the device before inhalation, incorrect dose preparation, and errors related to the inhalation maneuver [15]. Given that COPD is most prevalent among the elderly, special attention should be given to treatment considerations for this vulnerable population [16]. Reduced dexterity, including suboptimal fine motor skills and hand and finger force, as well as visual and cognitive impairment and a reduced ability to generate the minimum recommended peak inspiratory flow, further complicate inhaler device usage in this population [14, 17]. Therefore, the use of novel and more user-friendly inhaler devices is considered an effective measure to improve treatment adherence and outcomes, especially among elderly patients with COPD.

BAIs overcome the limitations that complicate inhaler device usage by sensing the patient’s inhalation effort and releasing the drug at low inspiratory flow rates [18]. The advantages of BAIs over pMDIs comprise their ease of use, reduced frequency of errors, and increased acceptability in patients with obstructive airway diseases (OADs) [19]. Synchrobreathe® is one such BAI that addresses the challenges in synchronization between inhalation and actuating the inhaler, commonly associated with pMDIs, and requires less inspiratory effort (27–30 L/min) compared with DPIs [20, 21]. It also has a dose counter that enables the patient to know the number of remaining doses. In a comparative study, 68.19% of subjects using Synchrobreathe® made no errors, compared with 56.21% with pMDIs [20]. Additionally, real-world evidence suggests that the salmeterol/fluticasone fixed-dose combination (FDC) delivered via Synchrobreathe® is both safe and effective. Most patients (91%) preferred Synchrobreathe® over previously used inhaler devices (DPI, pMDI, Soft mist™ inhaler, nebulizer), with over 75% of patients citing ease of use, confidence, and satisfaction with the device [22]. While randomized controlled trials remain the cornerstone of clinical evaluation, real-world observational studies play an important role, especially in assessing device usability and patient-reported outcomes. Synchrobreathe® use has been evaluated in asthma and in a device-handling study comparing it with conventional pMDI among asthma/COPD patients. Although such data support the design and usability of Synchrobreathe®, clinical data on its performance in the COPD population remain sparse. To address this evidence gap, the present study was conducted to evaluate the outcomes and usability associated with budesonide/formoterol FDC delivered through Synchrobreathe® in patients with COPD.

Methodology

Study Design

This prospective, open-label, observational, noncomparative, multicenter study was conducted across 23 sites in India. The study’s observation period was 12 weeks, with the first patient’s first visit in August 2021 and the last patient’s last visit in August 2022. The study was conducted as per the protocols of the International Council for Harmonization, Good Clinical Practice Guidelines (India), New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules 2019, and the Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research on Human Subjects, as issued by the Indian Council of Medical Research. Details of investigators, sites, and ethical approval are provided as electronic supplementary material (ESM; supplementary information I).

Patient Population

Inclusion Criteria

Eligible patients were those of either sex and ≥ 40 years old, with a documented diagnosis of COPD as per GOLD 2020 guidelines and a COPD Assessment Test (CAT) score of ≥ 10 points, and willing to provide a signed data-sharing consent form. In addition, they had to be prescribed formoterol/budesonide FDC through the Synchrobreathe® inhaler for COPD due to any of the following reasons: not controlled with current treatment or current ICS/LABA and requiring the addition of budesonide/formoterol FDC; or unable to use or not satisfied with the previously prescribed inhaler.

Exclusion Criteria

Patients with known hypersensitivity to the study drug or any of its components were excluded. Patients who had experienced an acute exacerbation of COPD within the 4 weeks before study initiation were excluded. Patients who had previous or current experience with the use of Synchrobreathe® inhalers were also excluded.

Withdrawal Criteria

Patients were allowed to exit the study at any point in time if they wished to do so. Patients who experienced adverse events (AEs) that prevented them from continuing to participate were withdrawn from the study. Individuals lost to follow-up and those considered unfit by the investigators were also withdrawn from the study.

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome was disease control, which was assessed by the mean change in the CAT score from baseline at week 12. The CAT score includes the following eight items: phlegm, cough, breathlessness while climbing stairs or hills, chest tightness, confidence while leaving home, activity limitations at home, energy, and sleep [23]. COPD impact on a patient based on the CAT scores may be categorized as follows: 0–10 points, low impact; 11–‍20 points, medium impact; 21–30 points, high impact; and 31–40 points, very high impact [24]. Secondary outcomes included mean changes in the CAT scores from baseline at weeks 4 and 8, mean changes in modified Borg Dyspnea Scale (mBDS) scores from baseline at weeks 4, 8, and 12, and device usability assessed using a device usability questionnaire at week 4. AEs were monitored over 12 weeks. The survey questionnaire used in this study was adapted from a similar published work [23]. Although the questionnaire was not formally validated for this specific study, its design and structure were modified to fit the context and objectives of the current research.

The number of patients who reached a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of ≥ 2 units for the CAT score and an MCID of ≥ 1 point for the mBDS score was also assessed [25, 26].

Study Procedures and Data

During the 12-week treatment period, patients were instructed to administer two puffs of budesonide/formoterol FDC (Foracort®, 200/6 µg) delivered through Synchrobreathe® twice daily or as per the investigator’s discretion, according to routine clinical practice. Training on the use of the Synchrobreathe® device was provided to the patients at each visit. All patients provided a written data-sharing consent form and willingly contributed their data.

On the first routine clinical visit (within ± 2 days of day 0), the following baseline characteristics were recorded: demographics, medical and surgical history, previous or concomitant medications, physical examination details, pulse, blood pressure, and respiratory rate. Patients reported to the site for follow-up visits at weeks 4 (± 2 days), 8 (± 2 days), and 12 (± 2 days) as per routine clinical practice.

The history of exacerbations and hospitalizations in the previous year before screening was recorded in the data capture form. Patient symptoms were recorded based on the self-administered CAT and mBDS scores. The type of inhaler previously used (DPI, metered dose inhaler [MDI], MDI with spacer device, nebulizer, or Soft mist™ inhaler) by the patient and the reason for discontinuation of the previous inhaler were captured in the data form.

Patients were followed up for 12 weeks. At the end of weeks 4, 8, and 12, vital signs, CAT scores, mBDS scores, and changes in concomitant medications were noted. The investigator/study team ensured that the patients used the Synchrobreathe® device correctly throughout the study period. Patients were asked to rate Synchrobreathe® via a device usability questionnaire at week 4. Physical examinations of the patients were carried out at week 12. AEs were monitored throughout the observational period of 12 weeks.

Data Analysis

Patients who met the inclusion criteria and completed at least one treatment visit were grouped in the intention-‍to-‍treat (ITT) population. The outcomes associated with the budesonide/formoterol FDC were analyzed based on data from patients in the ITT population. For the safety analysis, AEs and laboratory values for patients receiving single or multiple doses of FDC were considered. Patients who met all the inclusion criteria and completed the study with no protocol violations that would affect the treatment evaluation were included in the per-protocol (PP) set.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS (Version 9.4). Data were summarized for baseline characteristics and primary efficacy variables. Descriptive statistics were calculated for continuous variables with available data. The frequency and percentage for each category within a parameter were calculated using only nonmissing data for categorical variables. The primary efficacy assessment, change in mean CAT scores for COPD from baseline to week 12, was presented as descriptive statistics. Data normality was checked using the Shapiro–‍Wilk test; nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for further analyses, as the data were non-normal. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. To facilitate the interpretation of the primary endpoint, a post-hoc power calculation was performed based on the PP population. With a sample size of 243 patients and an observed mean reduction of 6.56 units in the CAT score from baseline to week 12, the analysis demonstrated a statistical power exceeding 90% at a 5% level of significance. This suggests that the study was adequately powered to detect a clinically meaningful improvement in disease control.

Results

Of 254 screened patients, 250 were enrolled, and 243 completed the study. The reasons for study discontinuation were AEs (mouth ulcer, breathlessness, and loss of appetite; n = 1), consent withdrawal (n = 4), and lost to follow-‍up (n = 2). Figure 1 shows the patient disposition flowchart.

Baseline Characteristics

The mean patient age was 64.19 ± 9.47 years, and most of them were men (78.0%). Mean COPD duration was 8.7 ± 7.95 years, with 51.2% and 41.2% of the patients presenting with moderate and severe COPD, respectively. A majority (61.6%) of the patients were smokers, and 8% of the patients had a history of asthma or atopy. The most commonly reported symptoms were cough (71.6%), dyspnea (71.2%), wheezing (35.2%), and sputum production (34.8%). One-third of the patients had a history of exacerbations, and 14% of patients had exacerbations leading to hospitalization. Prior use of DPI, pMDI, and pMDI with spacer was reported by 52.4%, 30.4%, and 13.6% of the patients, respectively. Mean CAT and mBDS scores at baseline were 17.78 ± 4.17 and 4.21 ± 1.54, respectively. The ITT patients’ baseline characteristics are listed in Table 1. Outcomes

Primary Outcome

A significant decrease of 6.56 ± 0.33 units (p < 0.001) was observed in mean CAT scores from baseline to 12 weeks (Fig. 2). Regarding the reduction in the impact of COPD on a patient’s life, assessed based on the CAT score, most patients with very high (CAT > 30) and high (CAT 21–30) scores at baseline improved to medium (CAT 10–20) or low (CAT 5–9) scores by week 12. The percentage of patients with high CAT scores at baseline decreased with treatment, with a 91% reduction reported at week 12 (Fig. 3).

The change in mean CAT scores over the study period is shown in Fig. 2. Mean CAT score was significantly reduced at week 4 (3.79 ± 0.28; p < 0.0001) and week 8 (5.47 ± 0.29; p < 0.0001) from baseline. Out of 243 patients with COPD in the study, 211 (86.8%) achieved an MCID of ≥ 2 units for the CAT score.

The change in mean mBDS scores from baseline to weeks 4, 8, and 12 is shown in Fig. 4. A statistically significant reduction in mean mBDS score was observed from baseline to week 4 (0.88 ± 0.07; p < 0.0001), week 8 (1.30 ± 0.08; p < 0.0001), and week 12 (1.60 ± 0.08; p < 0.0001), indicating improvement in COPD symptoms. An MCID of ≥ 1 points for mBDS was achieved by 188 (77.4%) subjects of the study population at week 12.

Results of the device usability questionnaire at week 4 showed that almost all (98%) patients (ITT and PPS populations) preferred the Synchrobreathe® inhaler over other previously used inhalers for COPD maintenance therapy, and more than 90% of the patients found Synchrobreathe® easy to use. Approximately 90% of the patients reported that they had no difficulty understanding or recalling how to operate the Synchrobreathe® inhaler, inhaling from its mouthpiece, using it overall, and carrying it in their pocket/bag. Safety

The study reported no serious AEs. A total of 37 nonserious AEs were reported in 28 (11.2%) patients during the study. The summary of AEs is listed in Table 2. Out of 37 AEs, the most frequently reported were pyrexia in six (2.4%) patients and headache in six (2.4%) patients. Of the total 37 events, two events (dry mouth and mouth ulcer) were potentially associated with the drug used in the study. The study reported no deaths or other serious AEs. No new safety signals were reported in relation to the study drug. One patient discontinued the study at visit 2 due to AEs, such as mouth ulcers, loss of appetite, and breathlessness.

Discussion

Inhalation therapy is the cornerstone of COPD management. Nevertheless, following the correct inhaler technique is essential to achieving optimal therapeutic outcomes. An inadequate inhaler technique poses a challenge in COPD management by reducing medication delivery, leading to increased suboptimal therapeutic outcomes, hospitalization risks, or necessitating the use of oral steroids [27, 28]. Commonly observed errors in inhaler technique stem from various factors, including the complexity and multiple steps required for inhaler use, inadequate time for patient training, limited understanding of inhaler devices among healthcare practitioners, and lack of evaluation of patient technique during follow-up visits [29]. Patient-related errors may involve inadequate coordination between inhalation and triggering the inhaler while using MDI, failure to hold one's breath after delivering the dose, and failure to inhale rapidly while using a DPI. A study reported that 46% of patients with COPD using a pMDI and 17% of those using a DPI found their devices challenging to use, which may result in suboptimal therapeutic outcomes [30]. BAIs were developed to eliminate the need for forceful inspiration and coordination between actuation and inhalation [31]. A study in patients with severe airway obstruction (FEV1 < 1 L) demonstrated promising results with BAI (Autohaler®), with most patients reporting ease of triggering and preferring it to conventional inhalers [32]. Another evaluation of the use of this BAI in patients with COPD revealed a better handling score compared with the use of multidose DPI Turbuhaler®. Additionally, in another study of 100 patients with airflow obstruction, BAI was reported to be preferred to DPIs and MDIs [33].

Synchrobreathe® is a BAI that features an integrated dosage counter and senses inhalation flow, releasing the drug at low flow rates (27–30 L/min), which are suitable for most patients with OAD across age groups, including those with severe airflow limitations. It combines the ease of use of DPIs with the portability of pMDIs. The use of Synchrobreathe® in patients with OAD was shown to be associated with fewer errors and higher patient preference [20]. Synchrobreathe®, being a simple-to-use device, would be especially useful for patients with COPD, who are often elderly and face difficulties using conventional inhaler devices due to reduced dexterity, visual and cognitive impairment, and the need for synchronization between inhalation and dose actuation. The benefits of fluticasone/salmeterol administered with the Synchrobreathe® device have been documented in patients with asthma [23]. The current study examined the outcomes associated with administering budesonide/formoterol FDC via the Synchrobreathe® BAI in the management of patients with COPD in India.

The combination of ICS budesonide, which reduces airway inflammation, and LABA formoterol, which relaxes airway smooth muscles, was first approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration in 2009 as maintenance inhalational therapy for severe COPD [34]. The use of budesonide/formoterol via Synchrobreathe® in this study must be interpreted within the context of evolving GOLD guidelines. ICS/LABA was prescribed based on the discretion of the treating physician and patient-specific clinical profiles during 2021–2022. While GOLD 2020 emphasized LABA/long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA) as preferred therapy in symptomatic COPD, ICS-containing regimens remain indicated for patients with frequent exacerbations or eosinophilic inflammation. This study provides evidence on symptom control and device usability in a subset of COPD patients receiving ICS/LABA therapy due to suboptimal control with their previous treatment.

The CAT score is user-friendly and beneficial in assessing the health of patients with COPD and predicting the risk of exacerbations [23, 35]. The primary outcome of this study was disease control, assessed based on the change in CAT scores from baseline to 12 weeks after treatment with budesonide/formoterol FDC administered through a Synchrobreathe® inhaler. The CAT scores were significantly reduced in patients with mild, moderate, and severe COPD (p < 0.001). The mBDS score has long been used as a patient self-reported measure of the perceived difficulty in breathing upon exertion, which is typically a 6-minute walk distance test [36]. Treatment with budesonide/formoterol FDC administered via Synchrobreathe® in the present study significantly reduced the mBDS score at weeks 4, 8, and 12. Given that the MCID for mBDS is ≥ 1 point [37], an MCID was seen in more than two-thirds of the patients (77%). These findings collectively suggest that the budesonide/formoterol FDC delivered through Synchrobreathe® performs well in achieving clinically relevant improvements in both CAT scores and mBDS, underscoring its potential efficacy in managing OAD and enhancing patient well-being.

The usability of an inhaler device significantly affects therapeutic outcomes in patients with OAD, independent of the drug administered [38]. A device-handling study of Synchrobreathe® in patients with OAD reported its ease of use, with patients finding the technique of usage easy to learn and remember. Most (> 95%) of the patients preferred the Synchrobreathe® over the pMDI [20]. Similar results were observed in the real-world EVOLVE study, which evaluated the effectiveness of salmeterol/fluticasone FDC via Synchrobreathe® in Indian patients with asthma. After 4 weeks of use, a majority (> 90%) of patients found the Synchrobreathe® inhaler intuitive to use and easy to inhale, demonstrating its user-friendly design. Further, 91% of patients reported preferring Synchrobreathe® to their previous inhalers, and the device was rated ‘high’ for patient confidence (92%) and patient satisfaction (95%) [22].

‘Device usability’ includes subjective factors such as preference, acceptance, dexterity, satisfaction, willingness to use, and ease of use, as well as objective factors such as the price of the inhaler. The INhalation deviceS: Physicians’ PErCepTions (INSPECT) survey was a cross-sectional survey conducted to understand the preferences of physicians (n = 83) and patients (n = 1486) on currently available inhaler devices in India, including pMDIs, pMDI + spacer, DPIs, and BAIs [39, 40]. Physicians in the survey highlighted the inability of patients to use their inhalation device correctly as a major reason for poor control of their respiratory symptoms. Patients in the survey also expressed a desire for a device that was easy to use and required them to follow fewer instructions. The patient experience with Synchrobreathe® in this study suggests that it addresses many limitations of pMDIs and DPIs, probably improving OAD management and enhancing patient quality of life.

The safety profile of the budesonide/formoterol FDC delivered through Synchrobreathe® appeared to be relatively favorable based on the reported AEs. Nonserious AEs were reported in 11.2% of the patients; only two events (dryness of mouth and mouth ulcer) were deemed possibly related to the study drug, indicating a low likelihood of adverse effects associated with Synchrobreathe® use. The reported AEs were also generally manageable and of a transient nature. No serious AEs were observed during the entire study. The findings of this study reveal that the budesonide/formoterol FDC delivered through Synchrobreathe® is effective in Indian patients with COPD. The real-world study design helps evaluate the performance of budesonide-formoterol Synchrobreathe® in actual clinical practice, incorporating the diverse patient populations, day-to-day patient challenges, and real-life complexities in healthcare settings.

Study Limitations

The data presented here are based on a short study duration of 12 weeks, which limits the ability to draw long-‍term conclusions. In the context of an observational study, the lack of randomization makes it challenging to establish causal relationships. Moreover, the absence of a control group or comparative data with other inhalers limits the ability to draw conclusions about the specific effectiveness and safety of the budesonide/formoterol FDC administered via the Synchrobreathe® inhaler in comparison with alternative treatments. To establish a more robust understanding of the inhaler’s effectiveness and safety, future research designs should consider incorporating comparative groups and randomization to provide a more comprehensive evaluation. While patient-‍reported usability and satisfaction were captured, the study did not include an objective evaluation of inhaler technique or user-related errors. Given that improper inhaler technique can be a barrier to optimal COPD management, future real-world studies should include standardized, objective assessments of Synchrobreathe® use to better evaluate technique-related challenges and their potential impact on outcomes. The inclusion criteria allowed for a broader patient population who either required the addition of a budesonide/formoterol FDC or had challenges with their previously prescribed inhaler device, including dissatisfaction or inability to use it correctly. While this reflects real-world clinical decision making, the lack of structured documentation of prior inhaler techniques or specific reasons for dissatisfaction may limit the interpretability of the findings.

Conclusion

The administration of budesonide/formoterol FDC through the Synchrobreathe® inhaler effectively controlled COPD, as evidenced by a significant reduction in CAT scores after 12 weeks of treatment. Most patients rated the device highly for usability and preferred it over previous inhalers, highlighting its user-friendly design. No serious AEs were reported. These findings suggest that the Synchrobreathe® inhaler offers an effective, user-friendly, and safe option for delivering budesonide/formoterol FDC in the management of patients with COPD in India.
