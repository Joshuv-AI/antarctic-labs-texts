Layer 1 NDNF interneurons modulate thalamocortical input to the auditory cortex
Authors: Jie Tao, Yiheng Chen, Mahiber Polat, Sunny C Li, Xiaowei Chen, Zhikai Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNeural microcircuits in the auditory cortex (AuC) are essential for auditory information processing. Recent studies identified the basal region of the ventromedial nucleus of the thalamus (bVM), which affects AuC neuronal responsiveness and contributes to sound discrimination. However, the microcircuit organization by which bVM influences AuC neuronal responsiveness remains unclear. Here, using anatomical tracing, slice electrophysiology, and optogenetics, we found that bVM formed monosynaptic connections with both neuron-derived neurotrophic factor-positive interneurons (NDNF-INs) in L1 and pyramidal neurons (PYRs) in L2/3, with polysynaptic engagement of other L2/3 interneuron subtypes. Notably, NDNF-INs selectively regulated bVM-driven inputs to parvalbumin-expressing interneurons (PV-INs), but not to vasoactive intestinal peptide-expressing interneurons (VIP-INs) or somatostatin-expressing interneurons (SST-INs), forming a possible cell-specific disinhibitory circuit in AuC L2/3 circuits. These findings define a circuit diagram of the bVM → AuC pathway and establish NDNF-INs as a key regulator of thalamocortical information flow.Keywords: the basal region of the ventromedial nucleus of the thalamus, auditory cortex, NDNF interneurons, disinhibitory, microcircuit

Introduction

The auditory system depends on precise thalamocortical communication for accurate sound processing.1,2,3 The auditory cortex (AuC) integrates inputs from multiple thalamic nuclei, most prominently the medial geniculate body (MGB) and the ventromedial thalamus (VM).4,5,6,7 The ventral division of the medial geniculate body (MGBv) is regarded as the first-order auditory thalamic nucleus, providing frequency-tuned, feedforward inputs to the middle cortical layers of the AuC.4,8,9 Beyond this canonical pathway, our previous work uncovered a parallel circuit in which the basal region of the ventromedial thalamic nucleus (bVM) provides noncanonical, tonotopically organized input to the AuC and supports sound discrimination.6

The VM has long been considered a “nonspecific” thalamic system that promotes cortical arousal and thalamocortical synchrony through widespread projections,10,11 particularly to cortical layer 1 (L1).12,13,14 L1 of the AuC is the thinnest cortical layer, consisting mainly of apical dendrites of deep pyramidal neurons (PYRs), long-range axonal inputs from distal regions, and a small population of interneurons.15,16,17,18 Among the interneurons in L1, neuron-derived neurotrophic factor-positive interneurons (NDNF-INs) are the dominant neuron type.19,20 NDNF-INs receive thalamic and long-range cortical inputs and project densely to apical dendrites of excitatory neurons across layers, positioning them to integrate afferent signals and regulate dendritic excitability.7,12,19,20,21 In addition to their effects on PYR dendrites, NDNF-INs are increasingly recognized as components of subtype-specific inhibitory microcircuits involving other interneuron classes. Recent studies indicate that NDNF-INs can inhibit parvalbumin-expressing interneurons (PV-INs) and vasoactive intestinal peptide-expressing interneurons (VIP-INs), thereby influencing superficial disinhibitory motifs and the balance between dendritic and somatic inhibition.19,20,21,22,23,24,25 By contrast, their relationship with somatostatin-expressing interneurons (SST-INs) appears to be distinct. Rather than constituting a broadly established downstream target class of NDNF-INs, SST-INs have been proposed to interact with NDNF-INs through asymmetric and context-dependent circuit motifs, including complementary control of dendritic inhibition and, in some settings, stronger SST-to-NDNF than NDNF-to-SST connectivity, these observations suggest that L1 NDNF-INs do not uniformly recruit all inhibitory subtypes, but instead participate in layer- and subtype-specific microcircuits that may differentially coordinate bottom-up and top-down signaling.19,20,26,27

Our prior work revealed that bVM axons target AuC in a layer-specific manner, densely innervating L1, layers 2/3 (L2/3) and layer 5 (L5), but sparing layers 4 and 6,6 suggesting a layer-specific organization distinct from the classical MGBv pathway. However, a critical unanswered question concerns how this noncanonical thalamic pathway is integrated into cortical circuits. Specifically, does bVM input directly drive PYR firing, and how specific inhibitory interneuron classes are recruited into the modulation of cortical information processing by bVM? Resolving this microcircuit-level logic is essential to understanding how bVM input enhances auditory discrimination.

In this study, we aimed to delineate the circuit mechanism through which the bVM modulates auditory cortical processing. To this end, we combined anatomical tracing, slice electrophysiology, and optogenetics to dissect the connectivity between bVM and specific neuronal subtypes in the AuC. We found that bVM provides direct excitatory inputs to L1 NDNF-INs and L2/3 PYRs, as well as indirect inhibitory input to L2/3 PYRs. In addition, bVM recruited both indirect excitation and inhibition of L2/3 VIP-INs, PV-INs, and SST-INs. We further examined the potential postsynaptic targets of L1 NDNF-INs among L2/3 neuronal subtypes. We then activated bVM while optogenetically suppressing L1 NDNF-INs and recorded from L2/3 interneurons. Suppression of NDNF-INs selectively reduced bVM-evoked inhibitory inputs onto PV-INs, but not onto VIP-INs or SST-INs. Together, these results have identified a specific functional bVM-NDNF-PV microcircuit in the AuC and demonstrated that NDNF-INs can functionally modulate thalamocortical signaling to PV-INs. These findings define a circuit diagram of the bVM → AuC pathway and establish NDNF-INs as a key regulator of thalamocortical information flow.

Results

bVM inputs innervate and activate distinct neuronal subtypes in the AuC

To determine whether bVM projections in the AuC target distinct neuronal subtypes, we injected AAV-ChR2-mCherry into the bVM and a Cre-dependent EGFP reporter into the AuC of NDNF-Cre, VIP-Cre, PV-Cre, or SST-Cre mice (Figure 1A; STAR Methods). One month later, we identified L1 NDNF-INs and L2/3 VIP-INs, PV-INs, and SST-INs by fluorescence, while L2/3 PYRs were identified by morphology (Figure 1B; STAR Methods).19 Whole-cell recordings were then performed to measure postsynaptic currents evoked by optogenetic stimulation of bVM axon terminals (Figure 1C; STAR Methods). To account for variability in viral expression, axonal density, slice condition, and light transmission across recordings, we generated input-output curves by measuring synaptic responses over a range of light intensities in each recorded cell type.28,29 Both raw and normalized excitatory postsynaptic current (EPSC) and inhibitory postsynaptic current (IPSC) amplitudes increased progressively with light intensity, representative IPSCs and EPSCs recorded across multiple light intensities are provided in Figure S1 (STAR Methods). Because maximal responses varied across cells, we selected a fixed stimulation condition (10 mW/mm2) that, based on the input-output analysis, fell within the dynamic range of the circuit and evoked submaximal responses under most recording conditions.6,30,31,32 Optogenetic activation (473 nm blue light) of bVM terminals elicited IPSCs in L2/3 PYRs, VIP-INs, PV-INs, and SST-INs, but no IPSCs were observed in L1 NDNF-INs. EPSCs were detected in all recorded neuronal subtypes, including L1 NDNF-INs, L2/3 PYRs, VIP-INs, PV-INs, and SST-INs (Figure 1D).

To investigate the monosynaptic connectivity, the sodium channel blocker TTX (1 μM) and the potassium channel blocker 4-AP (100 μM) were applied to the bath solution. Under these conditions, monosynaptic currents persisted while polysynaptic currents were abolished.33 In L2/3 PYRs, IPSCs recorded in ACSF were eliminated by TTX and 4-AP. Similar effects were observed in VIP-INs, PV-INs, and SST-INs. These results confirm that bVM establishes polysynaptic inhibitory connections with L2/3 PYRs, VIP-INs, PV-INs, and SST-INs (Figure 1E).

Excitatory connections displayed distinct profiles. In NDNF-INs, persistent EPSCs remained unaffected by TTX and 4-AP, confirming monosynaptic connectivity. L2/3 PYRs showed similar persistence of EPSCs under TTX and 4-AP. In contrast, EPSCs in VIP-INs were strongly reduced, and those in PV-INs and SST-INs were completely abolished, indicating that bVM establishes direct excitatory inputs to NDNF-INs and PYRs, a weak monosynaptic excitatory component together with polysynaptic excitation in VIP-INs, and indirect excitatory inputs to PV-INs and SST-INs (Figure 1F). Latency analysis further distinguished the response timing across neuronal types (Figure 1G). Together, these findings demonstrate that bVM provides monosynaptic excitatory input to L1 NDNF-INs, as well as monosynaptic excitation and polysynaptic inhibition of L2/3 PYRs. Additionally, bVM establishes weak monosynaptic excitation of L2/3 VIP-INs and recruits polysynaptic excitatory and inhibitory pathways to VIP-INs, PV-INs, and SST-INs.

Analysis of response timing and kinetics further distinguished bVM-driven excitation across neuronal subtypes. EPSC latency were shortest in NDNF-INs and PYRs, and longer in VIP-INs, PV-INs, and SST-INs, whereas IPSC latency in PYRs and interneurons were consistently delayed relative to EPSCs (Figure 1G). In addition, EPSC kinetics differed across neuronal subtypes. Compared with NDNF-INs and PYRs, EPSCs recorded in VIP-INs, PV-INs, and SST-INs exhibited slower rise times, shallower rising slopes, larger decay time constants, and greater latency jitter (Figures 1H–1K), further supporting the idea that bVM input reaches these cell classes through distinct synaptic pathways.

We next examined whether inhibitory responses also differed in their temporal properties across postsynaptic targets. IPSCs recorded in PYRs, VIP-INs, PV-INs, and SST-INs exhibited comparable rise times, latency jitter, and decay time constants, whereas IPSC rising slope was greater in PYRs than in PV-INs and SST-INs (Figure S2). Thus, compared with the marked cell-type dependence observed for EPSC kinetics, IPSC kinetics were relatively similar across postsynaptic targets.

Together, these results indicate that bVM afferents directly excite L1 NDNF-INs and L2/3 PYRs, recruit mixed direct and indirect excitation together with polysynaptic inhibition in VIP-INs, and engage predominantly indirect excitation together with polysynaptic inhibition in PV-INs and SST-INs.

L1 NDNF-INs mediate target-specific inhibition of L2/3 neuronal subtypes in the AuC

L1 of the AuC contains dense apical dendrites of PYRs, along with the dendrites and somata of inhibitory interneurons.15,34 Most L1 interneurons are NDNF-positive,19 and play a key role in top-down modulation.21 As shown in Figure 1 and in our previous work, bVM provides strong monosynaptic input to NDNF-INs in the AuC.6 We next selectively expressed ChR2 in NDNF-INs of NDNF-Cre mice by injecting AAV-DIO-ChR2 into the AuC, while simultaneously labeling L2/3 interneurons (VIP-INs, PV-INs, SST-INs) with AAV-EGFP (Figure 2A; STAR Methods).35 After viral expression, whole-cell recordings were performed, L2/3 interneurons were identified by EGFP, and PYRs were identified by morphology and filled with biocytin for post hoc reconstructions. Representative post hoc reconstructions and firing patterns are shown in Figure 2B.

Optogenetic activation of NDNF-INs evoked IPSCs in all L2/3 neuron subtypes. Functional inhibitory connections from NDNF-INs were detected in most recorded PYRs, VIP-INs, and PV-INs, but less frequently in SST-INs; to isolate monosynaptic inputs, TTX and 4-AP were applied (Figures 2C and 2D). PYRs exhibited large IPSCs that were nearly abolished by TTX and 4-AP, indicating that their inhibition includes substantial polysynaptic components. VIP-INs showed a similar pattern, with only small IPSCs remaining in the presence of TTX and 4-AP. SST-INs exhibited only small IPSCs under both conditions, suggesting that they are weakly influenced by NDNF-IN activation in this circuit. In contrast, IPSCs in PV-INs were unaffected, suggesting direct monosynaptic connections (Figure 2E).

We next compared the temporal properties of NDNF-driven IPSCs across postsynaptic targets. IPSC 10–90 rise times were similar across PYRs, VIP-INs, PV-INs, and SST-INs (Figure 2F). However, IPSC rising slope differed across cell types, with PYRs showing steeper rising slopes than interneuron subtypes (Figure 2G). Decay time constants did not differ significantly among cell types (Figure 2H). Response latency was shorter in PV-INs than in PYRs and SST-INs (Figure 2I), and latency jitter was smaller in PV-INs and VIP-INs than in PYRs, while SST-INs exhibited greater latency jitter than VIP-INs and PV-INs (Figure 2J). These temporal features further support the conclusion that NDNF-INs directly and reliably inhibit PV-INs, while inhibition of PYRs and VIP-INs includes additional polysynaptic components and inhibition of SST-INs is comparatively weak and temporally less precise.

Together, these results indicate that L1 NDNF-INs inhibit multiple L2/3 neuronal subtypes in the AuC, with particularly strong direct inhibition of PV-INs and weaker or more indirect inhibition of PYRs, VIP-INs, and SST-INs.

L1 NDNF-INs mediate bVM-driven inhibition onto PV-INs

Given that NDNF-INs receive direct bVM input and inhibit multiple L2/3 subtypes, we next tested whether they mediate bVM thalamocortical signaling to PV-INs, which receive direct monosynaptic inhibition from NDNF-INs. First, to verify the effectiveness of eNpHR, we expressed it in NDNF-INs of NDNF-Cre mice by injecting AAV-DIO-eNpHR-BFP into the AuC. We confirmed that yellow light (594 nm) robustly hyperpolarized NDNF-INs and blocked action potential firing evoked by current injection (Figure S3; STAR Methods). Then we injected AAV-ChR2-mCherry into the bVM to express ChR2 in bVM axons, AAV-DIO-eNpHR-BFP into the AuC to express eNpHR in NDNF-INs, and AAV-PV-EGFP into AuC to label PV-INs (Figures 3A and 3B; STAR Methods). Higher-magnification images and full-depth distributions of bVM axons and labeled PV-INs are shown in Figure S4. Using dual-color optogenetics,36 we activated bVM terminals while suppressing L1 NDNF-INs and simultaneously recorded the IPSCs and EPSCs of L2/3 PV-INs (STAR Methods). Stimulation of bVM axons alone (blue light, 473 nm) evoked robust IPSCs in PV-INs. Optogenetic suppression of NDNF-INs (yellow light, 594 nm) significantly reduced the amplitude of these evoked IPSCs without altering their latency or latency jitter (Figures 3C–3E). In contrast, EPSCs evoked by bVM axon activation were unaffected by NDNF-IN suppression, with no significant changes in amplitude, latency, or latency jitter (Figures 3F–3H). Together, these results identify a functional bVM-NDNF-PV inhibitory microcircuit in the AuC.

L1 NDNF-INs do not mediate bVM inputs to L2/3 VIP-INs or SST-INs

To determine whether this functional microcircuit is specific to PV-INs or extends to other subtypes, we next investigated the role of L1 NDNF-INs in relaying bVM inputs to L2/3 VIP-INs and SST-INs. ChR2 was expressed in bVM axons, eNpHR in AuC NDNF-INs, and EGFP in either VIP-INs or SST-INs for targeted recordings (Figures 4A, 4B, 4I, and 4J). Higher-magnification images and full-depth distributions of bVM axons and labeled VIP-/SST-INs are shown in Figure S4. In VIP-INs, suppression of NDNF-INs did not significantly alter the amplitude, latency, or latency jitter of bVM-evoked IPSCs, nor did it affect bVM-evoked EPSCs (Figures 4C–4H). Similarly, in SST-INs, suppression of NDNF-INs had no significant effect on the amplitude, latency, or latency jitter of either bVM-evoked IPSCs or EPSCs (Figures 4K–4P). Thus, under these recording conditions, L1 NDNF-INs contribute to a component of bVM-evoked inhibition onto PV-INs but do not make a detectable contribution to bVM-evoked synaptic responses in VIP-INs or SST-INs. Together, although optogenetic activation of NDNF-INs can inhibit VIP- and SST-INs in Figure 2, suppressing NDNF-INs did not detectably alter bVM-evoked synaptic responses in either cell type under our recording conditions; these results demonstrate that L1 NDNF-INs do not mediate thalamocortical signaling from the bVM to VIP-INs or SST-INs, highlighting the remarkable subtype-specificity of the microcircuit.

Discussion

Auditory information processing in the AuC relies on specialized microcircuits that make extensive use of disinhibitory mechanisms.37,38,39 L1 interneurons have long been recognized as critical regulators of cortical gain and disinhibition,12,21 because they integrate diverse afferents6,7,12,40,41,42 and regulate deeper-layer circuits.12,19,21,43 Building on our recent work identifying a parallel, tonotopically arranged bVM→AuC pathway that enhances the processing of basic auditory features and supports high-acuity tone frequency discrimination,6 we sought to define the underlying local circuit mechanism. To this end, we combined anatomical tracing, slice electrophysiology, and optogenetics to map connectivity from bVM to distinct neuronal subtypes in the AuC. We found that bVM axons establish distinct connections with multiple neuronal subtypes and that NDNF-INs selectively gate the bVM-driven inputs to L2/3 PV-INs, thereby forming a bVM-NDNF-PV-PYR disinhibitory pathway (Figure 5).

Working model of the bVM → AuC circuit

bVM axons project to L1 of the AuC and provide monosynaptic excitatory input to NDNF-INs (green). Activated NDNF-INs strongly inhibit L2/3 PV-INs (blue), forming a disinhibitory pathway (bVM-NDNF-PV-PYR).

Although VM has traditionally been considered as a nonspecific thalamic nucleus that broadly innervates L1.10,13,14 Work in the PFC has revealed that VM selectively recruits L1 NDNF-INs, thereby establishing a thalamocortical disinhibitory motif.12 Consistent with this revised perspective, our previous anatomical work demonstrated that bVM axons densely innervate L1 NDNF-INs and contact L2/3 and L5 neurons in the AuC while largely sparing L4 and L6, indicative of a layer-specific organization.6 Here we extend these observations by systematically defining the cell-type-specific connectivity from bVM to identified interneuron classes and PYRs in L2/3 of the AuC. Our recordings reveal that bVM provides robust monosynaptic excitation to L1 NDNF-INs and L2/3 PYRs, and a weaker direct component to L2/3 VIP-INs, whereas inputs to SST-INs appear comparatively sparse. Furthermore, optogenetic activation of bVM uncovered prominent polysynaptic inhibitory responses across L2/3 (Figure 1). Recordings from L2/3 neurons indicate that NDNF-INs broadly suppress multiple L2/3 cell types (Figure 2), consistent with an L1-centered inhibitory network.19,20

Furthermore, we demonstrate that L1 NDNF-INs act as selective mediators for bVM-evoked inhibition targeting PV-INs. When we silenced NDNF-INs, bVM-driven IPSCs in PV-INs were markedly reduced (Figure 3). This finding is consistent with previous work showing that NDNF-mediated disinhibitory circuits can be recruited by thalamic inputs across cortical regions.12,20,21 However, the IPSCs were not completely eliminated, likely because of lateral inhibition within the NDNF network or experimental factors such as variable eNpHR expression, as reported previously.36,44 Because PV-INs provide powerful somatic inhibition to L2/3 PYRs, thereby exerting strong control over their spiking output,45,46,47,48 selective suppression of PV-INs by the bVM→NDNF pathway would effectively disinhibit the pyramidal population. Notably, bVM input to VIP-INs was largely unaffected by NDNF silencing (Figure 4), even though direct optogenetic activation of NDNF-INs evoked measurable IPSCs in VIP-INs (Figure 2). This apparent dissociation likely reflects the distinction between potential synaptic connectivity and functional circuit recruitment. Direct ChR2 activation depolarizes the entire NDNF population, thereby revealing inhibitory outputs onto VIP-INs that include a substantial polysynaptic component, which was largely abolished by TTX and 4-AP. By contrast, IPSCs in PV-INs were predominantly monosynaptic and were preserved under these conditions. During bVM-driven activity, however, inhibition of VIP-INs is mediated predominantly by other local circuits, while the inhibition of PV-INs depends more specifically on the disynaptic bVM-NDNF-PV pathway. Additionally, L1 NDNF-INs are not homogeneous and may participate in partially distinct microcircuits,20,49 such that direct NDNF photo stimulation (Figure 2) may recruit a broader NDNF population than the subset naturally engaged by bVM input. Taken together, our data indicate that direct mapping of synaptic connectivity and assessment of functional recruitment during bVM-driven activity provide different but complementary views of NDNF circuit organization, highlighting the cell-type specificity of this mechanism.

Our findings in the AuC are consistent with VM-driven L1 motifs described in the PFC,12 suggesting that noncanonical thalamic pathways may commonly recruit L1 NDNF networks to mediate cortical inhibition. This conserved microcircuit thus serves as a flexible computational substrate, tuned by area-specific connectivity to produce divergent functions, from cognitive control in the PFC to sensory refinement in the AuC. Consistent with our prior findings that the bVM→AuC pathway acts as a parallel computational channel supporting high-acuity tone-frequency discrimination,6 the circuit logic uncovered here provides a mechanistic substrate by which bVM input directly excites PYRs while selectively relieving PV-mediated inhibition to enhance the gain and precision of auditory representations. Our characterization of the bVM→NDNF pathway reveals a specific strategy for noncanonical thalamic regulation of the AuC. In parallel, a recent study shows that MGBv also directly targets L1 interneurons, including both VIP and NDNF populations.7 Strikingly, this implies that multiple thalamic streams converge onto overlapping L1 networks, raising the compelling question of how these parallel inputs are integrated or segregated to support a division of labor in auditory processing.

In conclusion, our study identifies a cell-type-specific disinhibitory microcircuit as the key mechanism underlying the role of the bVM→AuC pathway. We show that bVM input targets both L1 NDNF-INs and L2/3 PYRs, with NDNF-INs acting as selective gates for PV-mediated inhibition. This NDNF-dependent, PV-specific disinhibitory motif provides a microcircuit mechanism that links bVM input to enhanced cortical sound representation.

Limitations of the study

First, although the TTX and 4-AP protocol confirms monosynaptic connectivity, slice recordings may underestimate the strength, dynamics, and state-dependence of these connections in vivo. Second, we note that viral expression and efficacy likely differ across interneuron classes, potentially biasing apparent connectivity. Third, our experiments primarily targeted superficial layers, leaving open the question of how bVM influences L5 output neurons and how it interacts with other auditory thalamic and corticocortical inputs. Fourth, electrophysiological recordings were performed at room temperature, which can slow synaptic transmission and may contribute to the relatively long 3–4 ms latencies observed for some putative monosynaptic responses. Fifth, given evidence for learning-related plasticity in dendrite-targeting L1 interneurons19 and behavioral-state-dependent modulation of cortical gain,21 it will be critical to determine whether bVM-driven NDNF-INs gating of PV-INs is dynamically regulated by behavioral state and experience, and how such plasticity contributes to the enhancement of auditory discrimination performance. Finally, this study was performed exclusively in male mice, and future studies including female mice will be needed to assess the generalizability of these findings across sexes.

Method details

Stereotaxic virus injections

Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane delivered in oxygen, positioned in a stereotaxic frame, and maintained on a heated pad at 36 - 37°C. Isoflurane was reduced to 1.5% - 2% during surgery. After shaving and disinfecting with iodine, the scalp was incised, the skull leveled, and burr holes drilled at designated coordinates. Injections were performed with glass capillaries pulled on a micropipette puller. The AuC injections occurred at anterior-posterior (AP) -3.10 mm, mediolateral (ML) -3.80 mm, and dorsoventral (DV) -1.40 mm from the dura, with a 26° medial angle, delivering 300 nL. For bVM injections, the coordinates were AP -1.70 mm, ML - 0.70 mm, and DV -4.40 mm from the dura. A total volume of 70 nL of virus was delivered through a glass micropipette with a tip diameter of 10-20 μm at a rate of 10 nL/min. To reduce reflux and improve confinement of the viral solution within the bVM, the injection was paused for 5 min after each ∼10 nL increment. After delivery of the full volume, the pipette was left in place for ∼15 min before withdrawal. During withdrawal, the pipette was slowly retracted, with an additional 5-min pause after each 0.5-mm step.6,50 Injection sites were targeted using stereotaxic coordinates and adjacent anatomical landmarks and were verified in serial coronal sections. Only animals with viral expression centered in the bVM were included in subsequent recordings; animals with off-target expression were excluded.

For bVM → AuC connectivity experiments, NDNF-Cre, VIP-Cre, PV-Cre, and SST-Cre mice received AAV2/9-hSyn-hChR2(H134R)-mCherry in the right bVM (titer: 2.07 × 1013 vg/mL, Taitool Bioscience, Shanghai, China) and rAAV2/9-EF1α-DIO-EGFP in the right AuC (titer: 5.68 × 1012 vg/mL, BrainVTA, Wuhan, China). For AuC NDNF-INs → interneuron mapping, NDNF-Cre mice received AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-hChR2(H134R)-mCherry (titer: 5.08 × 1012 vg/mL, BrainVTA, Wuhan, China), along with rAAV2/9-VIP-EGFP (titer: 5.96 × 1012 vg/mL, BrainVTA), rAAV2/9-PV-EGFP (titer: 3.0 × 1012 vg/mL, BrainVTA), or rAAV2/9-SST-EGFP (titer: 5.74 × 1012 vg/mL, BrainVTA) in the right AuC, to label VIP-INs, PV-INs, and SST-INs, viral preparations were diluted 1:10 before injection to reduce labeling density and facilitate identification of individual labeled neurons and their responses. For dual-color optogenetics, NDNF-Cre mice received AAV2/9-ChR2-mCherry in the right bVM, rAAV2/9-EF1a-DIO-eNpHR3.0-BFP (titer: 5.46 × 1012 vg/mL, BrainVTA) in the right AuC, and interneuron markers as above, viral preparations were diluted 1:10 before injection to reduce labeling density and facilitate identification of individual labeled neurons and their responses.

Slice preparation

Four weeks after viral injection, acute coronal brain slices (280 μm) were prepared. Mice were deeply anesthetized and decapitated; brains were rapidly removed into ice-cold oxygenated cutting solution containing (in mM): 110 NMDG, 2.5 KCl, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 25 NaHCO3, 25 glucose, 10 MgSO4, 0.5 CaCl2 (pH 7.3). After trimming, brains were glued to a platform and sectioned with a vibratome (VT-1200s, Leica). Slices were incubated in cutting solution at 35°C for 5 min, then transferred to oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF in mM: 119 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 25 NaHCO3, 12.5 glucose, 2 MgSO4, and 2 CaCl2, pH 7.3) at 35°C for 30 min. All drugs were purchased from Sigma unless stated otherwise. Slices showing substantial surface damage, edema, poor optical contrast, or widespread unhealthy-appearing cells were excluded before recording.

In vitro electrophysiology

Slices were transferred to a submerged chamber continuously perfused with oxygenated ACSF at room temperature (23 ± 1°C). Neurons were visualized using an upright microscope (BX51WI, Olympus) equipped with infrared differential interference contrast (DIC) optics, and whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed with an EPC-10 amplifier (HEKA Elektronik). Patch pipettes were pulled from borosilicate glass capillaries (BF 150 - 86 - 10, Sutter Instruments) using a P-97 puller (Sutter Instruments), the resistances ranging from 4 to 6 MΩ.

To investigate the connections between bVM inputs and AuC GABAergic interneurons or pyramidal cells, blue-light-evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) were recorded from neurons voltage-clamped at -70 mV, and inhibitory postsynaptic currents (IPSCs) were recorded from neurons voltage-clamped at +10 mV in response to 10 ms blue light pulses.51 The following pharmacological agents were added to the ACSF at the indicated concentrations: 1 μM TTX to prevent action potential generation, and 100 μM 4-aminopyridine (4-AP) to block potassium channels. Pipettes were filled with a cesium-based internal solution containing (in mM): 130 cesium methanesulfonate, 5 KCl, 10 HEPES, 2 NaCl, 5 Mg2ATP, 0.4 Na2GTP, 0.1 EGTA, and 10 disodium phosphocreatine (pH 7.3).

For recordings of intrinsic membrane properties, the pipette solution contained (in mM): 125 K-gluconate, 20 KCl, 10 HEPES, 4 Mg2ATP, 1 EGTA, 2 MgCl2 (pH 7.3). Biocytin (4 mg/mL) was added to the internal solution for morphological reconstruction. After at least 15 min of recording, the pipette was carefully retracted to improve subsequent labeling of biocytin-filled neurons. Depolarizing current (200 pA, 1 s) was applied to elicit action potentials. All signals were filtered at 2 kHz and digitized at 20 kHz. Recordings were excluded if series resistance exceeded 20 MΩ, if leak current became unstable, or if light-evoked responses could not be reliably distinguished from baseline noise across repeated trials. Series resistance was left uncompensated, and values were not corrected for liquid junction potentials. For connectivity analyses, only neurons with stable baseline and anatomically confirmed recording locations within the AuC were included. Cell identity was determined by reporter fluorescence, laminar position, electrophysiological features, and, where applicable, post hoc biocytin reconstruction.

Optogenetics

Optogenetic manipulation was performed in acute brain slices using laser-coupled optical fibers (473 nm and 594 nm) controlled by a pulse generator (Master-8, A.M.P.I.). Light was delivered through a 200-μm-core, 0.37-NA optical fiber positioned approximately 1 mm above the slice surface.

The laser output at the fiber tip was measured and calibrated before each experiment using a power meter, and stimulation intensity was adjusted to 10 mW/mm2 at the fiber tip for both 473-nm and 594-nm illumination unless otherwise noted. Because the optical fiber was positioned above the slice, the effective light intensity at the recorded region within the tissue was lower than the value measured at the fiber tip. To ensure comparability across experiments, the same fiber geometry, slice configuration, fiber-to-slice distance, and calibrated tip power were used throughout the study. Input-output measurements were performed to verify that the stimulation condition used for subsequent recordings was within the dynamic range of the circuit.

Channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) expressing bVM axons were activated with 473-nm blue light pulses (MBL-III-473, Changchun New Industries; 10 mW/mm2 at the fiber tip, 10-ms pulse duration). For suppression of NDNF-INs expressing eNpHR, continuous yellow light (594 nm; MBL-III-594, Changchun New Industries; 10 mW/mm2 at the fiber tip) was applied. In each recorded slice, the efficacy of yellow light-induced hyperpolarization in NDNF-INs was verified electrophysiologically. For eNpHR validation, firing frequency was measured during baseline, yellow-light illumination, and recovery periods in the same recorded NDNF-INs.

Analysis of synaptic response properties

Synaptic response properties were quantified from cell-averaged evoked traces generated from at least 5 sweeps obtained under the same recording condition. Peak amplitude was defined as the difference between baseline holding current immediately before stimulation and the maximal light-evoked current within the response window.20

Response onset latency was measured as the time from light onset to the first sustained deviation from baseline in the expected direction of the evoked current, using identical detection criteria across cells within each analysis set. Latency jitter was calculated for each cell as the standard deviation of onset latency across 5 sweeps.36,40,52

The 10-90 rise time was measured from the interval required for the response to increase from 10% to 90% of its peak amplitude. Slope was calculated from the ascending phase of the evoked current over the same response interval.36 The decay time constant (decay tau) was obtained by fitting the decay phase of the evoked current with a single-exponential function.7,12 For plots comparing EPSCs and IPSCs or different interneuron classes, measurements were derived from the average trace for each recorded cell and then summarized across cells.

Connection probability was calculated as the proportion of recorded cells in a given class that showed a reproducible light-evoked synaptic response. A cell was classified as connected when time-locked responses were consistently observed across repeated trials and could be clearly distinguished from baseline noise. All analyses were performed using Igor Pro custom scripts.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were deeply anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (10 mg/kg, i.p.), and transcardially perfused with 0.9% saline for 5 min, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) perfusion for 10 - 15 min. Brains were carefully removed and post-fixed in 15% sucrose/PFA solution at 4°C. Coronal sections (40 μm thick) containing the AuC were prepared, washed three times in PBS (10 min each) to remove residual fixative, mounted on microscope slides, air-dried, sealed with antifade mounting medium, and imaged using a confocal microscope.

For biocytin reconstruction after patch-clamp recordings, slices were briefly rinsed in ACSF (2 - 3 min) and then fixed overnight at 4°C in 4% PFA. After 1 h of equilibrium at room temperature, slices were washed three times in PBS (5 min each) and then permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100 (500 μL/slice) for 2 h at room temperature. After three additional PBS washes, slices were blocked with Immunol Staining Blocking Buffer (P0102, Beyotime) for 30 min at 37°C. Streptavidin-Alexa Fluor 594 (S11227, Thermo Fisher) was diluted 1:1000 in primary antibody dilution buffer and applied overnight at 4°C. Finally, slices were washed three times in PBS (5 min each), mounted on slides, air-dried, sealed with antifade mounting medium, and imaged using a confocal microscope.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using Igor Pro and GraphPad Prism. Results were expressed as mean ± SEM. The number of animals and specific details for each experiment are provided in the figure legends. For paired comparisons, the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used, and for unpaired samples, the two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied. For comparisons among more than two independent groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparison test was used. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. In all graphs, significance levels are denoted as follows: ns (not significant, p > 0.05), ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗ p < 0.001.
