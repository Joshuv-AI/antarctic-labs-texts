Malignancy in Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathies: Recent Insights
Authors: Hongzheng Wu, Xiaomeng Li, Honglin Xu, Zhan Li, Futai Feng, Jingdi Zhang, Zhixin Xu, Haoxi Ni, Ye Guo, Yongzhe Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIMs) are a group of rare autoimmune diseases in which several subtypes, primarily dermatomyositis (DM), are associated with a high risk of malignancy. Although the relationship between the pathogenesis of IIMs and co-occurring malignancy is poorly understood, there is a growing evidence base for specific risk factors which are associated with cancer-associated myositis (CAM), including myositis-specific or myositis-associated autoantibodies (MSAs/MAAs). In addition, given that patients with different subtypes of IIMs, as classified according to clinical manifestations or serum autoantibody measurement, exhibit heterogeneity in tumorigenesis, further research is urgently needed to explore the pathogenesis of CAM, in order to develop novel biomarkers and therapeutic alternatives for patients. This review discusses the risk and protective factors for CAM using the available evidence, thereby offering fresh insights into its pathogenesis, screening, and management strategies and highlighting possible future breakthroughs and obstacles in this field.Keywords: Cancer, Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, Cancer-associated myositis

Introduction

Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIMs), also termed broadly myositis, are a heterogeneous group of rare connective tissue diseases characterized by muscle weakness and systemic clinical features that can impact the skin, lungs, and joints [1]. As reported in the literature, the annual incidence of IIMs was previously estimated as 0.2–2/100,000, and the prevalence is estimated to range from 2 to 25/100,000 [2]. Based on clinical, histopathological, and serological features, IIMs can be classified into several primary subtypes, including dermatomyositis (DM), polymyositis (PM), immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), and inclusion body myositis (IBM) [3]. Recent studies have identified specific autoantibodies associated with the clinical characteristics of these diseases [4], supporting further in-depth investigations into the underlying immunopathogenic mechanisms and related comorbidities, including cancer.

Notably, since Stertz initially described the relationship between DM and malignancy in 1916 [5], more studies have found an increased risk of cancer in IIM patients [6–9]. However, there are still disparities in the understanding of the cancer risk in IIM patients and the pathophysiological interactions between IIMs and cancer. In addition, the specific reasons for the frequent coexistence of tumors during the disease course of IIMs and the underlying pathogenic mechanisms are still unclear; some studies have proposed hypotheses based on the influence of autoimmune regulatory disorders and genetic factors [10, 11]. Furthermore, there is inadequate clinical evidence to support screening and management strategies for IIM patients with cancer, commonly referred to as cancer-associated myositis (CAM).

Therefore, in this review, we discuss the epidemiology of CAM, the association between IIMs and malignancy, the pathogenesis of CAM, current screening approaches, and therapeutic regimens for CAM.

Epidemiology of Cancer in IIMs

Until now, multiple case series and epidemiological retrospective studies have explored the link between IIMs and malignancies. This section focuses on the timing of tumor onset in IIM patients, the risk of tumor development among different subtypes of IIMs, and the most common cancers associated with IIMs.

Timing of Malignancy

Epidemiological observations indicate that the risk of developing cancer is at its highest within the first year of IIMs diagnosis. The majority of malignancies in IIM patients occur within 3 years of diagnosis, and the risk of cancer generally decreases over time after this period. Therefore, most studies define CAM as a malignancy that is diagnosed within 3 years of IIM diagnosis [12–14], and, as such, they focus primarily on this subgroup of patients as a means to explore the pathophysiology of CAM in order to guide clinical diagnosis and treatment. However, since there is a lack of recognized and uniform criteria for accurately categorizing CAM patients in current clinical trials, the reliability of conclusions drawn from the available data is still uncertain [15].

Over recent decades, population-based studies focused on CAM have been conducted globally. One 2018 study based on data from the EuroMyositis registry found that 13% of patients with myositis developed malignancies during follow-up, and only 46% of these patients fell within the CAM definition given above. Among these CAM patients, the median interval between the diagnosis of IIMs and malignancy was 1 month (IQR = [− 3, 12] months) [12]. Furthermore, a recent large cohort study from China showed that the time interval between the diagnosis of IIMs and malignancy was 7.2 months (IQR = [0, 20.4] months) [7]. These data are generally consistent with the findings from earlier studies in Europe, East Asia, and Australia [16–19].

Risk and Types of Malignancy in Different Subtypes

Numerous retrospective studies have shown that the risk of malignant tumors in patients with various clinical subtypes of IIMs has increased to varying extents, with DM showing the greatest increase in risk. According to a 2017 meta-analysis of 5 studies including 4538 patients with DM or PM, the standardized incidence rate (SIR) for DM was 4.66, which is higher than the 1.75 SIR reported for PM [20]. Another meta-analysis of 12 publications found similar results, showing that the pooled RR for DM and PM patients was 5.50 (95% CI 4.31–6.70) and 1.62 (95% CI 1.19–2.04), respectively [21]. Moreover, the risk of cancer associated with IBM was also increased (SIR, 2.4 [95% CI, 1.2–4.9]), but to a lesser extent compared with the risk of malignancy in DM (SIR, 6.2 [95% CI, 3.9–10.0]) [19]. In contrast, a 2013 study of the South Australian myositis database (n = 373) identified an increased cancer risk among patients with biopsy-confirmed IIMs, showing that DM patients had the highest risk of cancer (although not statistically significant), whereas PM or IBM were not independently associated with cancer [22]. This disparity between studies may be explained by the inclusion of some patients whose cancer was detected 5–16 years after diagnosis of IIMs (27.9% (12/43)), which is significantly beyond the traditional definition of CAM, as highlighted above. Interestingly, Naddaf et al. further discovered that IBM patients had a 3.9-fold higher risk of hematologic malignancies than the population controls (P = 0.021), though the risk of solid tumor development remained comparable [23]. This elevated risk of hematologic malignancies may stem from an aberrant T-cell large granular lymphocytic (T-LGL) clone driven by the chronic inflammation present in IBM, potentially serving as a shared pathological basis for T-LGL leukemia [24, 25].

Some reports have also demonstrated that distinct IIM subtypes correspond to specific cancer subtypes with high incidence. A 2001 study of IIMs case statistics using national data from Sweden, Denmark, and Finland revealed that ovarian cancer was the most frequently occurring malignancy in DM patients (SIR, 10.5 [95% CI, 6.1–18.1]), followed by lung cancer, pancreatic cancer, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, gastric cancer, and colorectal cancer. In PM patients, non-Hodgkin lymphoma was the most commonly associated (SIR, 3.7 [95% CI, 1.7–8.2]), followed by lung cancer and bladder cancer [16]. Interestingly, the type of tumor with the highest risk of development in IIM patients varies significantly by ethnicity or region. Unlike studies conducted in Western countries, the malignancies most significantly associated with DM in Asian and North African case series were nasopharyngeal carcinoma, breast cancer, lung cancer, ovarian cancer, and colon cancer [26]. In northern China, lung cancer was found to be the most commonly diagnosed cancer among DM patients (6/43, 13.95%) [27]. Furthermore, in the Taiwan (China) population, nasopharyngeal carcinoma and colorectal cancer were the most common tumor types found in DM and PM patients, respectively [18]. Moreover, evidence from a study conducted in Norway revealed that the most frequently detected tumors in IBM patients included gastrointestinal, prostate, and hematologic malignancies [28]. Meanwhile, in the USA, skin cancer and prostate cancer were found to be the most common IBM-associated malignancies [29].

It is important to highlight that current observations of the occurrence of malignant tumors in IIMs are mainly focused on DM and PM, while there are sparse data available from IBM and other IIM subtypes. In addition, the discrepancies in results from various races and regional cohorts indicate that genetic and environmental factors have a significant impact on the incidence of tumor types in distinct IIM subtypes.

The Relationship Between IIMs and Cancer

Since a large number of epidemiological reports demonstrate the frequent coexistence of IIMs and malignancies, there have been many studies exploring the key influential factors resulting in their association, with the aim of identifying high-risk patients who can benefit most from tumor screening and early intervention, in order to improve poor outcomes. Risk Factors for Malignancy in IIMs

Age/Gender

According to prior studies, advanced age is the most commonly recognized demographic parameter for heightened cancer susceptibility in DM and PM patients [30–33]. Notably, even in patients with DM/PM-associated interstitial lung disease (ILD), older age is a significant predictor of increased cancer risk [34]. In addition, most studies from the literature conclude that male IIM patients are more likely to develop cancer than females [32, 35]. In parallel, a 2013 meta-analysis of 20 studies by Wang et al. and a 2021 meta-analysis of 69 studies by Oldroyd et al. both noted that older age and male gender are the primary risk factors for developing malignant tumors in IIM patients [36, 37]. However, some studies have reported conflicting findings. Stockton et al. reported that among PM patients, young people aged 15–44 years had a higher risk of cancer, and a gender comparison in DM/PM patients showed that risks were elevated significantly in females [38]. Meanwhile, Sigurgeirsson et al. demonstrated that women with DM have a higher chance of concurrent malignancy, whereas the risk of malignancy for women with PM is equivalent to that for men [39].

Clinical Manifestation

It has been reported that more severe muscle injury or the presence of muscle involvement in patients with IIMs may be related to underlying cancer risk [33, 40]. Skin necrosis, ulceration, and/or erythema were also identified as risk factors for cancer [35–37, 41, 42]. A recent study comparing the skin biopsy results from DM patients either with or without co-occurring cancer revealed that CAM patients displayed increased basement membrane thickening and pigment incontinence [43]. Additionally, accumulating evidence also highlights a role for dysphagia in the development of malignancy in IIMs [36, 37, 44, 45]. Other relevant research has also identified that Raynaud’s phenomenon [30] and shawl sign [31] correspond with a higher prevalence of cancer in IIMs.

Serum Biochemical and Immunological Characteristics

Laboratory tests from CAM patients reveal changes that might point to a predisposition to malignancy.

Previous studies have shown that DM patients with higher serum creatine phosphokinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels have an elevated risk of developing concomitant malignancies [32, 41, 42]. In addition, there is evidence showing that the values of CK and LDH dramatically decreased in IIM patients 1 month post-surgery for the resection of a concurrent primary tumor [46]. This phenomenon gives indirect support for the claim that CAM may be a paraneoplastic syndrome. In contrast, some studies have proposed that lower levels of CK and LDH are linked to an enhanced chance of cancer [37, 44, 45, 47, 48], indicating a lesser extent of muscle destruction in these patients. One explanation is that this could be secondary to tumor cells activating the PD-L1 pathway, which, in turn, induces the dysfunction of T-cells that mediate immune responses in myofibers.

In recent years, there have been numerous discoveries relating to the differences in the immunological properties of serum between patients with and without CAM. For example, Kaneko et al. found that a low serum Krebs von den Lungen-6 (KL-6) level at the point of diagnosis of myositis could predict the risk of concomitant malignancy in DM/PM-associated ILD patients [34].

Moreover, evidence has emerged showing that myositis patients carrying anti-TIF1γ antibodies [31, 49, 50] are more likely to develop malignant tumors during the course of IIMs. Antinuclear matrix protein 2 (anti-NXP2) antibodies, one of the major MSAs found in juvenile dermatomyositis (JDM) patients, are also present in adult IIMs, although relatively rare, and are correlated with adult CAM [51, 52]. Another study demonstrated a 3.68-fold increase in cancer risk in anti-NXP2-positive DM patients compared to the general population [53]. However, in contrast, a recent meta-analysis has found that the presence of anti-NXP2 autoantibodies is a non-significant factor for malignancy risk [37], even after controlling for the heterogeneous composition of anti-TIF-1γ-positive cases. As a result, larger study cohorts and refined statistical screening are necessary to make further inferences about the utility of anti-NXP2 antibodies in this context.

In addition to this, Chinoy et al. demonstrated that the absence of anti-Jo-1, anti-PM-Scl, anti-U1-RNP, anti-U3-RNP, and anti-Ku antibodies substantially increased the likelihood of malignancy in patients with IIMs (especially in DM) [13]. In particular, this study focused on a novel antibody against 155 kDa and 140 kDa nuclear proteins (anti-155/140 Ab), which was previously shown by Kaji et al. to have a relationship with cancer-associated DM [54], thereby highlighting its superior diagnostic efficacy in predicting the risk of CAM and recommending its inclusion in routine testing. In some studies, other autoantibodies, such as anti-3-hydroxy 3-methylutaryl coenzyme A reductase (anti-HMGCR) [55], anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (anti-SAE1) [52, 56], and anti-Mi-2 [57], have been found to contribute to an increased risk of malignancy in IIM patients, although a meta-analysis by Oldroyd et al. classified these indicators as non-significant factors [37]. In addition, negative antinuclear antibody (ANA) was reported to be related to an increased likelihood of developing cancer within 3 years of DM diagnosis [58].

Genetic Features

Genetic risk factors may also play a role in the development of CAM. For instance, the HLA-A28 allele variant was proven to have strong associations with malignancies in patients with IIMs [22]. In addition, Preusse et al. used NanoString technology to analyze skeletal muscle biopsy samples from DM patients who were both anti-TIF1γ-positive and anti-Mi-2 positive and discovered that the two clinical subgroups with 60% and 20% CAM frequency, respectively, had distinct immune response-related gene expression signatures [59]. In particular, the study highlighted cancer-associated HLA alleles (HLA-DQA1 and HLA-G) involved in IIM susceptibility variants [60, 61], as well as BAGE, which has been identified in multiple cancers [62, 63], were specifically upregulated in anti-TIF1-positive patients.

Protective Factors for Malignancy in IIM Patients

In addition to risk factors, researchers have found that several factors may lessen the likelihood of malignancies in IIM patients, such as ILD [31, 32, 44], arthritis [34], arthralgia [27], and Raynaud’s phenomenon [37]. Of these, ILD and arthritis were also verified as low-risk factors in a meta-analysis by Wang et al. [36].

Together, these studies exploring the multiple risk/protective factors that form the association between myositis and malignancy will assist clinicians in predicting and screening patients with myositis to identify those who are at higher risk of developing cancer during diagnosis and follow-up.

Outcomes of IIM Patients with Cancer

The overall prognosis of IIMs is poor, and cancer remains the leading cause of death in these patients [64, 65]. A cohort study conducted in Western Australia reported that patients with CAM had a 20% higher absolute rate of death than those without cancer, and the diagnosis of cancer shortened the life expectancy of IIM patients by 59 months. However, it is worth noting that this study only observed reduced survival in DM and IBM patients, providing a reference for recommending which clinical subtypes of IIMs should be focused on for cancer screening [6]. Regarding the high mortality rate, it is likely due to the lack of a comprehensive clinical system for the management of high-risk patients within the IIM cohort, resulting in delayed diagnoses of most IIM-related cancers. According to a study by Kang et al., 83% of CAM patients were in stage III or IV malignancy at the time of tumor diagnosis, and just 17% of these patients experienced cancer remission [66]. Thus, these findings underscore the importance of early cancer screening in improving IIM outcomes.

Screening for IIM-Associated Cancer

Given that early detection and treatment of cancer are key to decreasing overall mortality, it is essential to target patients newly diagnosed with myositis using a validated risk factor–based tailored malignancy screening strategy.

In this context, the first international guideline on IIM-associated cancer screening, scientifically reviewed by the IMACS Scientific Committee and endorsed by the International Myositis Society, was published in 2023, providing recommendations on risk stratification of IIMs-associated cancer, screening modalities, and screening frequency [67]. This guideline assesses the individual cancer risk of IIM patients as standard, intermediate, or high risk, based upon the IIM subtype, autoantibody status, demographic information, and clinical features. As the definition of risk stratification relies on patients with IIMs, it should be noted that even if a patient is judged to be at “standard risk,” the risk of cancer is still likely to be higher than that of the general population. Therefore, all IIM patients require more rigorous cancer screening (i.e., “basic cancer screening” as referred to in the guidelines, supplementary to regional screening programs offered to the general population). For intermediate-risk patients, additional “enhanced cancer screening” should be performed at the time of diagnosis. Moreover, high-risk patients should be screened not only at the time of diagnosis but also annually for 3 years after diagnosis with “basic cancer screening.”

However, despite the progressiveness of this guideline, there are some limitations to building consensus on screening strategies based on available evidence. For instance, none of the recommendations cited high-quality evidence; the geographical diversity of the referenced studies was limited, and there was a lack of empirical studies on the effectiveness of recommended screening modalities and frequencies, along with the potential harm that screening might cause. Furthermore, available studies have not determined whether screening programs should be tailored to account for the heterogeneity of outcomes among different subtypes of IIMs concurrent with cancer.

Additionally, the effect of combining multiple indicators for cancer diagnosis is unclear from this guideline alone. Interestingly, new research on autoantibodies, particularly anti-TIF1γ, suggests that the risk of cancer in myositis patients may decrease with an increase in the quantity of distinct autoantibodies detected in a patient. For example, patients who were positive for both anti-TIF1γ and cell division cycle and apoptosis regulator protein 1 (CCAR1)/specificity protein 4 (Sp4) had a lower risk of cancer compared to those with only anti-TIF1γ [68–70]. The discovery of these coexisting autoantibodies sheds light on the role of host immune response diversity on cancer development in IIM patients and may help define a subgroup with low cancer risk. In addition to other autoantibodies, the combined detection of serum soluble programmed death ligand 1 (sPD-L1) and anti-TIF1γ significantly improved the specificity and positive predictive value of malignant tumor diagnosis in DM patients, reaching 95% and 70%, respectively [71]. These findings above imply that more study is needed to propose more detailed combinations of indicators for clinical cancer risk stratification.

Of note, Aussy et al. further identified anti-TIF1γ IgG2 as a promising biomarker for cancer in adult anti-TIF1γ-positive DM patients, with a positive predictive value of 100% for cancer [72]. The cut-off values of this indicator in ALBIA and ELISA have also been confirmed in an ENMC multinational research [73]. However, this marker could not be used in juvenile DM [74].

Altogether, it is certain that despite many challenges, advancing the implementation of screening strategies has important clinical implications for efficiently tracking myositis populations approaching the high-risk stage, enhancing cancer surveillance, and making timely adjustments to therapeutic regimens.

Pathogenesis

To further comprehend the relationship between myositis and malignancies and to revise screening guidelines for high-risk groups, numerous studies have focused on the specific pathogenesis of CAM. Among them, some studies have suggested that CAM may be a paraneoplastic syndrome based on the coincidence of the occurrence time and the disappearance of muscle symptoms following tumor treatment [11, 75]. However, this conclusion requires more in-depth research to offer a complete chain of evidence. In recent years, some hypotheses expounded from the perspectives of immunity and genetics have received attention from researchers. Therefore, this section will explore the possible pathogenesis of CAM regarding these two aspects.

Immunopathogenic Mechanisms

In immune-related pathogenesis research, most literature refers to three states to describe the balance of the bodily anti-tumor immune response and tumor growth (Fig. 1). Among these stages, “elimination” denotes a state where the immune system overreacts in such a way that tumorigenesis is prevented but myositis is induced, whereas the persistence of the tumor in the body under immune surveillance is considered as “equilibrium.” This equilibrium stage may last for years and is difficult to diagnose clinically due to immune-tumor interactions, which are only observable at a molecular level. Patients at this stage have so-called “occultic” tumors and are at high risk for CAM. When the immune-tumor balance is disrupted, the cancer develops uncontrollably, and then, patients are clinically diagnosed with CAM [8, 76]. Given this context, it is essential to clarify the critical interactions within the immune homeostasis framework to aid the earlier identification of high-risk candidates for CAM in clinical practice.

Immunoediting hypothesis of tumorigenesis in IIMs. The hypothesis of immunoediting is one of the prevailing views on the development of malignant tumors in IIM patients. Shown are three main stages: escape, where tumor growth in IIM patients grows out of the control of the anti-tumor immune response and continues to expand; equilibrium, whereby immune monitoring is balanced with tumor proliferation, limiting malignant growth and underlying a clinically undiagnosed latency period; and elimination, whereby a strong autoimmune response stops tumor growth, but this manifests as symptoms of myositis. Abbreviations: IIMs, idiopathic inflammatory myopathies; NK cells, natural killer cells

Regarding the interpretation of the “elimination” and “escape” phases in CAM, it is proposed that anti-tumor immunity cross-reacts with muscle tissue, in cases where the tumor and muscle tissues express shared antigens, leading to autoimmune responses (Fig. 2). Casciola-Rosen et al. observed that Mi-2 antigen was overexpressed in muscle (concentrated on regenerated muscle cells at the cellular level) and tissues of several types of malignancies associated with myositis (e.g., lung and breast cancer) as compared to controls [77]. This “shared antigens” hypothesis states that under the pathological conditions of muscle fiber injury, degeneration, and regeneration in myositis patients, damaged muscle cells or their products may be overexpressed after uptake by antigen-presenting cells. As a result, cross-reactive anti-tumor antibodies and immune cells recognize and attack these antigens more easily in this pro-inflammatory environment, whereas these antigens would normally be expressed at lower levels [78]. A similar phenomenon occurs with the TIF-1γ antigen. In patients with cancer-associated DM, it was found that the immunohistochemical staining of TIF1γ in both tumor and muscle tissues of DM patients was more intense compared to controls [79]. In particular, certain genetic mutations in tumor cells encode foreign proteins (i.e., tumor neoantigens), and in order to evade the immune system’s response, the tumor initiates a loss of heterozygosity, triggering an orphan immune response. As a result, this response harms specific tissues such as muscle and skin, where the expression of these proteins (wild-type antigens) is upregulated due to tissue damage or cellular stress induced by infection, trauma, or toxin exposure (often described as the “second hit”) [80, 81]. As such, these genetic changes aggravate symptoms associated with myo-inflammation while fueling the anti-tumor response, initially causing patients to present primarily with myositis, but eventually promoting immune escape due to an unbalanced anti-tumor immune response, leading to tumor outgrowth. Additionally, while the precise mechanism of action of TIF1γ in human cells remains to be elucidated, it has been observed in mice that TIF1γ is immunogenic enough to induce experimental myositis, which requires CD8+ T-cells and type I interferons (IFN-I); this may provide some clues for human cells [82]. Notably, compared with anti-TIF1γ-positive DM patients, anti-Mi-2-positive patients exhibited a prominent IFN-I inflammatory pathologically related signature [59]. In light of evidence that IFN-I-regulated genes may suppress cancer progression, investigating the interplay between IFN-I and TIF-1γ/Mi-2 in CAM pathogenesis represents a promising mechanistic avenue for exploration [83].

Hypothetical model of tumor-induced cross-reactive autoimmunity in CAM. In patients with CAM, overexpression of autoantigens that were previously low in expression occurs in muscle fibers due to muscle injury, degeneration, and regeneration. These antigens are “shared” with similar tumor antigens (e.g., TIF-1γ, Mi-2) that have undergone abnormal changes such as overexpression, higher antigenic protein exposure, mutation, modification, or loss of heterozygosity in cancer cells. Therefore, when a strong anti-tumor immune response is induced to kill cancer cells, autoantibodies produced by B cells against such antigens simultaneously target muscle tissue, causing cross-reactive autoimmunity, leading to persistent muscle damage and aggravating symptoms of myositis. Among these antigens, TIF-1γ or Mi-2 may induce myositis through type I IFN and CD8+ T-cells. In addition, the inflammatory site of the muscle tissue of IIM patients plays a protective role by regulating the expression of PD-L1 through INFγ, while a large number of infiltrating PD-1+CD8+ T-cells cause the intensification of myo-inflammatory symptoms. Moreover, dysregulation of the programmed cell death 1 or its ligand (PD-1/PD-L1) signaling pathway may contribute to CAM. In DM patients without malignancy, serum sPD-L1 levels were found to be significantly higher compared to healthy controls, and even higher in patients with de novo cancer-associated DM, although substantially lower than in patients with solid tumors [71]. This suggests persistent activation of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis in CAM, potentially reflecting compensatory immunosuppression during chronic autoimmune and anti-tumor responses. In particular, a new study revealed that PD-L1 is expressed at the inflammatory sites of muscle tissue in IIM patients and that there are increased PD-1+ T-cells, which are highly expressive of cytolytic molecules and infiltrate the affected muscles in patients with active-phase PM/DM. Paradoxically, PD-L1 appears protective in myositis, as shown by the fact that PD-L1-deficient mice developed more severe C-protein-induced myositis with excessive PD-1+CD8+ T-cell infiltrates and cytolytic damage. Further experiments in this study confirmed that this protection is mediated by IFN-γ-dependent PD-L1 upregulation in muscle tissue (Fig. 2) [84]. The coexistence of elevated sPD-L1 (indicating pathway activation) and PD-L1’s protective role implies that in CAM, sustained autoimmune stimulation may overwhelm endogenous PD-L1-mediated immunosuppression. This imbalance could permit the parallel progression of myositis and cancer.

Genetic Influences

To date, there have been few studies on the genetic factors underlying CAM-related pathogenesis, and the evidence available appears to contradict expectations. According to data from a Swedish population-based study including 8460 first-degree relatives of IIM patients and 41,127 matched relatives of individuals without IIMs, there was no shared familial susceptibility between IIMs and cancer overall, and thus, it may not be the major pathological mechanism causing the co-occurrence of the two [85].

However, there are still some valuable findings identifying relevant genes related to CAM, which can be divided into two categories. In one category, there are tumor-expressed genes that correspond to CAM-related autoantibodies, such as TRIM-family genes, MORC-family genes, and HMG-CoA reductase (HMGCR) genes. Among them, the study confirmed that more frequent somatic mutations and loss of heterozygosity of the TRIM33 gene (encoding TIF1γ) have been documented in anti-TIF1γ+ CAM patients compared with the negative myositis patients [79]. Since TIF1γ protein expression has been linked to cancer development [86], some researchers hypothesize that the genetically modified region of TIF1 protein may serve as the immune-targeted antigen leading to paraneoplastic myositis. In addition, MORC3, which is a MORC-family gene encoding NXP2 that can activate the tumor suppressor p53 under conditions of DNA damage and inhibit cancer cell growth, may also be involved in the etiology of CAM [87]. This is consistent with the association between anti-NXP2 antibodies in IIM patients and an elevated cancer risk [52]. Interestingly, similarly to NXP2, TIF-1γ has a functional relationship with p53 [80]. Since these two autoantibodies are co-associated in CAM and anti-tumor immune responses, they may share similar mechanisms of action in the pathogenesis of malignancies co-occurring with IIMs. Moreover, aberrant HMGCR expression has been reported to induce malignant transformation and proliferation of normal breast epithelial cells or promote glioblastoma cell growth and migration [88, 89].

The other category of genes includes a range of oncogenes or immune genes that have shown differential expression in muscle/skin biopsy samples from myositis patients. Aljabban et al. found that the oncogenes MYC, IDO1, NPM1, POSTN, IFI44, and IGLC1 were overexpressed in DM muscle samples and that these changes were correlated with IFN signaling. These abnormal expression patterns suggest the activation of various tumorigenic pathways in DM patients, which could partly explain their increased risk of malignancy [90]. Further to this evidence, biopsy samples from anti-TIF1γ-positive participants showed higher expression of cancer-related markers, including DDX58/RIG-1 and VEGF, compared to the anti-Mi-2-positive group. The discovery of these specific genetic alterations has significant implications for understanding the distinct pathogenesis of clinical subgroups with a high prevalence of CAM.

Overall, research into the pathogenesis of myositis is still restricted to exploring the correlation of numerous factors, and so, there is a lack of high-quality evidence to confirm the exact pathogenic process at both the cellular and molecular levels.

Management of IIM Patients with Cancer

Treatment Strategies for IIM Patients with Cancer

In the current treatment guidelines for IIMs, immunosuppressive medication (such as prednisolone, azathioprine, methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, ciclosporin, and tacrolimus) is advised for patients based on subtype characteristics, clinical symptoms, and CK level, despite the fact that these immunosuppressive drugs are unapproved off-label [91]. Meanwhile, considering that impaired immune surveillance and subsequent malignant clonal proliferation may already occur in diseases with altered immune status, long-term intense immunotherapy with azathioprine in such cases may further increase the risk of malignancy [92]. Early immunoregulatory therapy with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) offers a relatively reasonable alternative to immunomodulation to reduce the risk of CAM [93], but validation in cohort studies is needed.

In recent years, myositis has also been discussed as an immune-related adverse event in cancer patients, known as immune checkpoint inhibitor-related myositis (ICI-myositis) [94]. Although ICI-myositis is a non-classical IIM, it has a similar comorbidity status to CAM, and so, current research and discussion on its treatment methods may provide a reference for developing treatment options for patients diagnosed with CAM. The overall principle of treatment is to strike a balance between alleviating organ inflammation and preserving the anti-tumor response. Specifically, ICIs are frequently held or discontinued in patients who cannot taper steroids or require steroid-sparing medications (such as hydroxychloroquine, methotrexate) [95]. For those who can gradually reduce steroids or control symptoms with prednisone, ICIs can be given simultaneously based on the oncologist’s recommendation [96].

Meanwhile, considering the dual therapeutic application of B-cell-depleting agents (primarily rituximab) in both treating B-cell malignancies and inducing remission in IIMs [97, 98], these agents hold significant promise for CAM treatment. However, as this therapy currently fails to achieve long-term drug-free remission, recent investigations have further explored anti-CD19 CAR T-cell therapy. Such studies indicate that CD19 CAR T-cells not only effectively treat B-cell leukemia but also enable IIM patients to achieve sustained and deep remission [99–101]. Although the current research cases are very limited, these findings provide preliminary evidence for developing such novel therapies for CAM patients.

Furthermore, therapeutic strategies that target cytokines and their downstream pathways using monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and small-molecule inhibitors are being actively explored. Emerging approaches include type I interferon-targeted therapies for DM [102], though their efficacy in CAM requires validation. A study demonstrates that sifalimumab, an anti-IFN-α mAb, suppresses the IFN-I gene signature in the blood and muscle tissue of myositis patients, with target neutralization correlating with improved muscle strength [103]. Moreover, a systematic review of 53 refractory DM patients (38 female, 15 male) treated with Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors, which block downstream IFN signaling, reported no serious adverse events and significant improvements in muscle strength and skin lesions in most studies [104]. However, cytokine-specific interventions show variable efficacy. For example, anti-interleukin-6 receptor (anti-IL-6R) therapy reduced the serum biomarkers (CK, aldolase, and troponin-T) in ICI-myositis patients (n = 5), but failed to alleviate clinical symptoms [105]. Future clinical trials are needed to better understand how to uncouple autoimmune pathology from anti-tumor immunity at the cytokine level, for the benefit of more CAM patients.

Notably, a recent study conducted hierarchical clustering using machine learning algorithms in a retrospective cohort of Chinese adult DM/PM patients and identified six subgroups, including malignant overlapping DM, classical DM, and PM with severe muscle involvement [49]. This subclassification system demonstrates the substantial heterogeneity of myositis and lays the groundwork for therapy stratification by distinguishing high-risk CAM patients.

Prognostic Monitoring Biomarkers

One study indicated that anti-TIF1γ-positive DM patients were more common in the advanced cancer group than in the non-advanced cancer group, suggesting that the presence of anti-TIF1γ antibodies was associated with the malignant progression of CAM [50]. Interestingly, patients in remission had the lowest levels of anti-TIF1γ, which even became undetectable in some patients [106]. These results hint that anti-TIF1γ could be used as a prognostic marker to monitor the disease course in CAM patients. Furthermore, the presence of anti-TIF1γ IgG2 has been independently associated with mortality in CAM patients [72]. Additionally, serum sPD-L1 levels may also be utilized to monitor treatment outcomes in CAM patients. For example, Chen et al. reported that as patients’ clinical conditions improved following treatment, sPD-L1 in stable cancer-associated DM showed an absolute decrease and a decrease relative to tumor-free patients, and it declined more in four of these patients after radical cancer treatment [71]. With the discovery of more myositis autoantibodies that can predict the risk of CAM in recent years, there is hope that related markers will be further explored to facilitate prognostic monitoring or therapeutic guidance of CAM.

Future Perspectives

Overall, although the association between IIMs and malignancies has been established for decades, there is still a lack of further in-depth research to enhance the understanding of the pathogenesis of CAM and the immune status of the affected patients, thereby facilitating the publication of evidence-based screening and management guidelines. Specifically, the urgent problems to be solved in this field include the following aspects. Firstly, sufficient data for high-quality research findings require a large clinical cohort of IIM patients who are phenotypic and have diagnostic information available. Secondly, larger and more targeted studies are needed to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of long-term tumor screening, thereby clarifying reasonable diagnostic intervals that can be implemented in the accepted definition of CAM. Meanwhile, the most effective combination of risk indicators should be identified to stratify high-risk patients and make detailed tumor screening tests more affordable. Further, clarifying the combination medication strategy for CAM patients is a huge problem to be solved in balancing patients’ immune status to achieve the optimal therapeutic effect.

Looking ahead, research strategies such as pharmacogenomics, genome-wide association studies, and multi-omics data integration studies can be applied to compare IIM patients with and without cancer, as well as the general cancer population, thus specifically characterizing disease-specific molecular pathways. In addition, these strategies can identify genetic variants directly associated with CAM, along with potential immune-related markers, and ultimately enable the optimal individualized screening and treatment strategy formulation.
