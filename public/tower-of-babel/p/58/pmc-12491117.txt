Gene-edited hematopoietic stem cells for leukemia and lymphoma treatment: a systematic review of preclinical and translational evidence
Authors: Hassan Nourmohammadi, Mashallah Babashahi, Mohammad Panji, Safa Radmehr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) are pivotal in regenerating the blood and immune systems and have been widely used in treating hematologic malignancies like leukemia and lymphoma. Advances in gene-editing technologies, such as CRISPR/Cas9 and lentiviral vectors, have enabled the modification of HSCs to enhance their therapeutic potential, offering a continuous source of tumor-targeting immune cells. However, challenges related to long-term engraftment, safety, and delivery mechanisms remain.MethodsThis systematic review, following PRISMA guidelines, evaluated 19 preclinical and translational studies on gene-edited HSCs for leukemia and lymphoma treatment. The review focused on gene-editing methodologies, preclinical outcomes, delivery challenges, and translational barriers. Databases such as PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase were searched using keywords related to gene editing, HSCs, and hematologic malignancies.FindingsGene-edited HSCs demonstrated promising preclinical efficacy, with CAR-engineered HSCs showing durable tumor clearance and multilineage immune reconstitution. Lentiviral vectors were the most common delivery method, but concerns about insertional mutagenesis persist. CRISPR/Cas9 offered precise editing but faced challenges with low homology-directed repair (HDR) efficiency in quiescent HSCs. Non-viral delivery methods, such as electroporation, showed potential for safer gene editing but require further optimization. Base editing technologies, while not requiring HDR, present their own delivery challenges that need to be addressed. Suicide gene strategies were effective in mitigating safety risks, while preconditioning regimens improved engraftment success.ConclusionsGene-edited HSCs hold significant promise for treating leukemia and lymphoma, offering long-term immune persistence and tumor clearance. However, challenges in gene delivery efficiency, safety, and engraftment must be addressed for clinical translation. Future research should focus on improving editing precision, scalable manufacturing, and robust safety monitoring to advance these therapies toward clinical trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03529-5.

Introduction

Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) play a critical role in the regeneration of the blood and immune systems and have been widely utilized in stem cell transplantation for hematologic malignancies, including leukemia and lymphomas [1, 2]. With the advent of gene-editing technologies such as CRISPR/Cas9, TALENs, and lentiviral-mediated integrations, it has become possible to enhance the therapeutic potential of HSCs by modifying them to express tumor-targeting receptors or correct disease-associated mutations [3–5]. A significant area of exploration involves engineering HSCs to produce immune effector cells, such as T cells, NK cells, and myeloid cells, that persistently target malignant cells [6–8]. Unlike conventional chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T therapies, which rely on the infusion of modified mature T cells and face challenges like transient persistence and antigen escape, gene-edited HSCs can provide a continuous and self-renewing source of therapeutic immune cells, potentially overcoming limitations of current CAR-based immunotherapies [9–11].

Despite advancements in gene-editing methods and preclinical evaluations demonstrating the feasibility of generating tumor-targeting cells from gene-modified HSCs, several key gaps remain [12, 13]. While CAR-engineered immune effector cells derived from gene-edited HSCs have shown antitumor potential, there is still limited understanding of their long-term engraftment, hematopoietic differentiation, and safety in translational models [6, 14]. Efforts to establish effective delivery mechanisms, maintain HSC stemness during ex vivo manipulation, and optimize preconditioning regimens for transplantation require further examination. Additionally, while suicide gene strategies such as HSV-sr39TK and EGFRt have been introduced to mitigate risks such as uncontrolled proliferation or insertional mutagenesis, their impact on therapeutic efficacy and immune functionality remains incompletely assessed [9, 15, 16]. A systematic synthesis of the available data is required to determine the state of preclinical and translational research on gene-edited HSC therapies for leukemia and lymphoma treatment.

The purpose of this systematic review is to compile and evaluate the current evidence on the therapeutic application of gene-edited HSCs in leukemia and lymphomas. Specifically, this study aims to examine the methodologies utilized to modify HSCs, assess preclinical outcomes in terms of efficacy and safety, and identify the translational barriers that must be addressed for clinical application.

Search strategy and keywords

Our search strategy utilized a comprehensive combination of keywords related to gene editing, hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), and hematologic malignancies, connected using Boolean operators. Primary keywords included “gene editing,” “hematopoietic stem cells,” “HSC,” “leukemia,” “lymphoma,” and “cancer immunotherapy.” Specific gene-editing methods such as “CRISPR,” “TALEN,” “base editing,” “prime editing,” and “lentiviral vectors” were also incorporated. Study selection

The study selection process followed a structured PRISMA-guided approach to ensure rigor and transparency. Initially, relevant full-text articles and abstracts were retrieved through database searches. Duplicate screening was then performed to eliminate redundant studies. In the subsequent phase, titles and abstracts were carefully reviewed to assess their relevance based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies that met these criteria advanced to the next stage, where a full-text analysis was conducted to evaluate their eligibility, methodological rigor, and alignment with the study objectives. To minimize bias and enhance reliability, two independent reviewers carried out the screening process. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus, ensuring a consistent and objective selection of studies for inclusion in the review. Finally, studies that fully met the inclusion criteria were selected for data extraction.

Result

Article selection

The systematic search across multiple databases identified an initial pool of 2,487 articles, distributed as follows: 789 from PubMed (MEDLINE), 612 from Scopus, 423 from Web of Science, 321 from Embase, 215 from Google Scholar, and 127 from ClinicalTrials.gov. After removing 1,243 duplicates, 1,244 unique articles were retained for further evaluation.

The first phase of screening involved applying predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, which led to the exclusion of 1,012 articles. Among these, 147 were non-English publications, and 865 were deemed irrelevant to the study’s focus. This left 232 articles for title and abstract screening.

During the title and abstract screening phase, 178 articles were excluded due to insufficient relevance or misalignment with the study’s objectives. The remaining 54 articles underwent a full-text review to assess their eligibility based on methodological rigor, relevance, and scope. Of these, 35 articles were excluded for failing to meet the predefined criteria, leaving 19 articles that were ultimately included in the systematic review.

Key gene-editing methodologies used for modifying HSCs in leukemia and lymphoma treatment

The modification of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) for leukemia and lymphoma treatment relies on several gene-editing techniques, each with distinct advantages and limitations. Across the 19 reviewed studies, the most commonly used methods include lentiviral vector-mediated gene delivery, CRISPR/Cas9-based editing, and non-viral gene transfer strategies such as electroporation and base editing.

Lentiviral vectors are widely applied for stable integration of therapeutic genes, particularly for the insertion of chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) into HSCs. This method has been documented extensively in preclinical studies for its efficiency in generating multilineage immune cells expressing anti-CD19 CARs, which can target B-lineage malignancies [9–11]. While lentiviral transduction provides durable gene expression, concerns about insertional mutagenesis persist, necessitating safety mechanisms such as co-delivered suicide genes [15, 16].

Specific preclinical studies have highlighted the risk of insertional mutagenesis associated with lentiviral vectors in HSC gene therapy. For instance, in a preclinical model of X-linked severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID-X1) gene therapy using gammaretroviral vectors, which share similar integration mechanisms with lentiviral vectors, insertional mutagenesis led to T-cell leukemia in treated mice due to the activation of proto-oncogenes such as LMO2 [20]. Similarly, in a study involving lentiviral vector-mediated gene transfer in HSCs for sickle cell disease, integration analysis revealed a preference for integrating near transcriptionally active regions, raising concerns about potential oncogenic activation, although no malignancies were observed in the short term [21]. These examples underscore the need for careful integration site analysis and the implementation of safety measures, such as suicide genes or safer vector designs, to mitigate the risk of insertional mutagenesis in HSC-based therapies for leukemia and lymphoma.

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated editing represents a more precise and flexible approach compared to viral gene transfer, enabling targeted modifications such as gene knockouts (e.g., PD-1 deletion to enhance immune persistence) or precise insertions through homology-directed repair (HDR) [22, 23]. However, challenges with CRISPR editing in HSCs include relatively lower HDR efficiency and potential off-target effects, which some studies have attempted to mitigate through high-fidelity Cas9 variants and optimized DNA repair modulation [23].

Emerging non-viral methodologies, particularly electroporation-based CRISPR/Cas9 delivery and epitope-specific base editing, offer enhancements in safety and specificity. For example, targeted base editing of the CD45 epitope enables universal CAR-T/HSC systems by preventing fratricide while preserving normal leukocyte functions [22]. Similarly, knock-in strategies at selective loci such as CD33 in myeloid-directed therapies improve tumor microenvironment engagement [24].

Preclinical outcomes of gene-edited HSCs

Preclinical studies have demonstrated promising results for gene-edited hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) in the treatment of leukemia and lymphomas. Across multiple studies, three critical aspects have been evaluated: efficacy in tumor targeting, safety considerations, and multilineage immune reconstitution.

Efficacy: tumor targeting and immune clearance

Several studies confirm that gene-edited HSCs can generate persistent, multilineage immune effector cells capable of recognizing and destroying leukemia/lymphoma cells. In particular, CAR-engineered HSCs targeting CD19 in B-lineage malignancies show robust antigen-specific cytotoxicity in humanized mouse models, leading to measurable tumor regression [9– [11, 27, 28]. Knockout-based gene modifications, such as PD-1 deletion via CRISPR, also improve immune persistence and tumor clearance [22]. Furthermore, the introduction of HSC-derived myeloid CAR cells has been tested for their ability to enhance tumor microenvironment infiltration, potentially overcoming immune suppression [24].

Safety: off-target risks and suicide gene strategies

Gene-edited HSC therapies present safety challenges, mainly related to off-target editing, insertional mutagenesis, and immune overactivation. Studies using lentiviral vectors emphasize the need for suicide gene mechanisms like HSV-sr39TK and EGFRt, which enable controlled elimination of modified cells in case of adverse effects [9, 10, 15, 16]. CRISPR-based approaches, while allowing specific gene knockouts, require strict off-target assessments, particularly in cases where unintended mutations in HSCs could introduce leukemogenic risks [23].

Multilineage immune reconstitution and long-term persistence

One of the major advantages of HSC-based gene therapy over conventional CAR-T therapy is the potential for long-lasting immune reconstitution. Studies demonstrate that CAR-engineered HSC-derived cells efficiently differentiate into multiple lineages—including CAR-T, CAR-NK, and CAR-myeloid cells—providing durable tumor surveillance [6, 11, 26, 27]. However, some studies highlight concerns regarding ex vivo manipulation, as prolonged culture may compromise HSC engraftment and competition with wild-type or malignant HSCs following transplantation [25]. Preconditioning regimens (e.g., myeloablative chemotherapy, CD117 antibody-mediated niche clearance) are being explored to improve transplant success [6]. Challenges in delivering gene-editing cargo to HSCs and comparison of techniques

Delivering gene-editing cargo efficiently into hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) remains a central challenge in the development of gene-edited HSC therapies for leukemia and lymphomas. The primary difficulties involve ensuring efficient gene delivery while maintaining HSC viability and engraftment potential. Current delivery strategies include viral-based approaches (particularly lentiviral vectors) and non-viral methods such as electroporation and nanoparticle-mediated delivery. These methods can be used to deliver various gene-editing technologies, including CRISPR/Cas9 systems, base editors, and prime editors, each with unique advantages and drawbacks that influence their feasibility in therapeutic applications.

Key challenges in gene delivery to HSCs

One of the primary hurdles in editing HSCs is low uptake efficiency due to the quiescent nature of stem cells. Unlike activated T cells, which are more readily modified, HSCs are often in a resting state, making it difficult for gene-editing cargo to penetrate and integrate efficiently [12, 29]. Lentiviral vectors offer relatively high efficiency in transducing HSCs, providing stable long-term expression, but they come with concerns about insertional mutagenesis and random integration, which could potentially trigger oncogenic effects [9, 11, 25].

Non-viral methods, such as CRISPR/Cas9 delivery via electroporation, provide precise genome editing without the risk of random integration, but these methods introduce their own limitations. Electroporation can cause significant cell toxicity, reducing the engraftment potential of HSCs post-editing [12, 23]. Additionally, CRISPR-based editing frequently relies on homology-directed repair (HDR), which has low efficiency in non-dividing HSCs. Approaches to enhance HDR efficiency, such as modulating DNA repair pathways and using high-fidelity Cas9 variants, have been explored but still require optimization [23].

Emerging strategies include nanoparticle delivery of gene-editing reagents and base editing strategies that do not rely on double-strand breaks. These methods show promise in enhancing precision while reducing toxicity, but their clinical scalability remains under investigation [6, 10, 12, 22]. Translational insights for future clinical applications of gene-edited HSC therapies

The reviewed studies provide several key insights into the clinical translation of gene-edited hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) therapies for leukemia and lymphoma. While preclinical research demonstrates strong proof-of-concept data, clinical application faces multiple challenges, including ensuring long-term safety and persistence, optimizing transplantation efficiency, and refining gene-editing precision. Three key aspects emerge from these studies: (1) scalability and clinical feasibility, (2) regulatory and safety considerations, and (3) strategies to improve persistence and engraftment in patients (Table 4).

Scalability and clinical feasibility

One of the primary barriers to clinical translation is efficient large-scale manufacturing of gene-edited HSCs. While lentiviral vectors have high gene transfer efficiency, they pose risks of insertional mutagenesis, and their production remains costly [11, 30]. The recent FDA approval of Casgevy (exagamglogene autotemcel) demonstrates CRISPR/Cas9’s clinical potential for hematopoietic stem cell therapies, with durable therapeutic effects in patients. Similarly, numerous preclinical studies have shown that base-edited HSCs achieve long-term engraftment with high efficiency, suggesting this approach may soon be ready for clinical translation [12, 22]. Alternative non-viral methods continue to be explored to reduce costs and improve accessibility, though further validation of long-term genetic stability in engrafted HSCs remains important for broader clinical adoption.

Regulatory and safety considerations

Ensuring patient safety is a major concern. Off-target effects from CRISPR-based gene editing, risks of insertional mutagenesis from viral vectors, and immune-related toxicities all pose challenges for clinical approval. Suicide gene strategies (e.g., HSV-sr39TK, EGFRt) have been developed as protective mechanisms for viral-mediated or HDR-based approaches, allowing selective elimination of modified cells if adverse effects arise [9, 15, 16]. However, these strategies are not applicable to base editing (BE), prime editing (PE), or NHEJ-based methods, which require alternative safety measures such as improved off-target analysis and high-fidelity Cas9 variants [23].

Strategies to improve persistence and engraftment

One of the biggest limitations of gene-edited HSC therapies is ensuring effective engraftment in the bone marrow niche, particularly when competing with unedited or malignant HSCs. Studies suggest that preconditioning strategies, such as antibody-mediated niche clearance (e.g., CD117 blockade), could improve engraftment without requiring toxic chemotherapy regimens [6]. Furthermore, the introduction of HSC engineering techniques aimed at increasing hematopoietic persistence, such as epitope editing (e.g., CD45 or myeloid-specific CAR expression [22, 24], may help create universal donor systems for broader clinical use.

Discussion

Gene-editing strategies for hscs: strengths and challenges

The modification of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) using gene-editing technologies has emerged as a promising approach for the treatment of leukemia and lymphomas. The risk of insertional mutagenesis, particularly with lentiviral vectors, remains a significant concern for clinical translation. Preclinical studies provide evidence of this risk; for example, Hacein-Bey-Abina et al. (2003) reported cases of T-cell leukemia in a SCID-X1 gene therapy trial due to gammaretroviral vector integration near the LMO2 proto-oncogene, highlighting the potential for insertional mutagenesis to drive oncogenesis [20]. Although lentiviral vectors have a safer integration profile compared to gammaretroviral vectors, studies such as Leonard et al. (2018) have shown that lentiviral integration in HSCs can still occur near transcriptionally active regions, posing a theoretical risk of oncogene activation [21]. These findings emphasize the importance of ongoing efforts to develop safer vector systems, such as self-inactivating lentiviral vectors, and to conduct long-term monitoring of integration sites in preclinical and clinical settings to ensure the safety of gene-edited HSC therapies.

Various methodologies have been explored to enhance the therapeutic efficacy, durability, and safety of engineered HSCs. Lentiviral vectors remain the most commonly used technology for gene transfer, particularly for the stable integration of chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) into HSC genomes. Studies by Larson (2015) [9] and De Oliveira (2013) [11] demonstrated that lentiviral-based CAR-HSC modification achieves effective multilineage differentiation into antigen-specific effector cells, providing long-term immune surveillance against B-lineage malignancies. However, concerns regarding insertional mutagenesis remain a major translational barrier, as viral vector integration can potentially induce oncogenic transformation or genomic instability.

CRISPR-based editing offers a more precise and flexible alternative to viral transduction, allowing for gene knockouts (e.g., PD-1 deletion to enhance immune persistence) and site-specific insertion of therapeutic constructs using homology-directed repair (HDR). Wellhausen (2023) [22] successfully applied targeted CRISPR adenine base editing to create CD45-modified HSCs that evade CAR T-cell fratricide, opening possibilities for universal HSC donor systems. However, the efficiency of HDR remains low in quiescent HSCs, as noted by Lomova (2018) [18], making optimization of DNA repair mechanisms a critical area for future research. Non-viral methods such as nanoparticle-mediated CRISPR delivery, as reviewed by Murugesan (2023) [12], highlight promising safety advantages but underscore the need for further validation of scalability and in vivo persistence.

Preclinical efficacy: tumor clearance and multilineage differentiation

Several studies confirm that gene-edited HSCs can produce durable and functional immune effector cells capable of attacking leukemia and lymphoma cells. Liu (2019) [2] demonstrated that CD19 CAR-engineered HSCs engraft successfully in NSG mouse models, differentiating into tumor-specific T and NK cells. Oliveira (2018) [28] expanded on this by showing that HSC-derived effector cells exhibit prolonged activity, overcoming the transient persistence limitations of conventional CAR T-cell therapies. Additionally, Truscott (2016) [10] provided evidence that suicide gene incorporation, such as HSV-sr39TK, allows for controlled elimination of CAR-HSC-derived cells in case of adverse reactions.

Beyond direct tumor targeting, epigenetic and cytokine-based modifications can enhance the tumor microenvironment, improving CAR-HSC therapeutic impact. Welty (2024) [24] demonstrated that myeloid-lineage HSC modifications (via CD33-specific CAR knock-in) improve T-cell infiltration into solid tumor microenvironments, reinforcing the idea that multi-lineage targeting strategies could enhance outcomes.

Despite strong preclinical evidence, some challenges persist in maintaining hematopoietic stemness post-editing. Kao (2014) [25] reported that prolonged ex vivo HSC culture can impair engraftment efficiency, reducing long-term immune reconstitution. Kohn (2014) [6] similarly noted that competition with wild-type HSCs affects the proportion of modified cells within the hematopoietic niche, potentially limiting their overall therapeutic impact.

Current clinical progress and challenges in HSC-derived therapies

While preclinical and translational studies demonstrate the potential of gene-edited HSCs, clinical translation has faced significant hurdles. For example, NK-cell therapies derived from HSCs, such as NK-CAR19 and NK-CAR33, have entered early-phase trials but have not advanced due to challenges in persistence, scalability, and tumor microenvironment resistance [27, 32]. Unlike CAR-T cells, NK cells exhibit limited in vivo expansion and often require cytokine support, which complicates their clinical application [33]. Additionally, manufacturing complexities and safety concerns, such as off-target effects from gene editing, have delayed broader adoption [34]. These barriers highlight the need for improved ex vivo expansion protocols and enhanced gene-editing precision to bridge the gap between translational success and clinical validation.

Challenges in gene-delivery and engraftment optimization

Delivering gene-editing cargo into HSCs efficiently while maintaining normal differentiation capacity is a significant hurdle. While lentiviral vectors provide high transduction rates (~ 40–50%), studies by De Oliveira (2013) [11] and Gschweng (2014) [6] caution against the long-term risks associated with insertional mutagenesis. Electroporation-based CRISPR delivery, as examined by Lomova (2018) [18], offers greater precision in gene modifications but suffers from low HDR efficiency and significant cellular toxicity, reducing HSC engraftment potential.

To overcome these limitations, researchers are exploring preconditioning regimens that enhance HSC transplantation success. Koniali (2021) [35] discusses the use of CD117 antibody-based niche clearance as a potential alternative to myeloablative chemotherapy, allowing gene-modified cells to better engraft without toxic conditioning protocols. Additionally, Ferrari (2021) [29] and Murugesan (2023) [12] propose the use of biochemical enhancers (such as small molecules that modulate DNA repair pathways) to improve CRISPR editing efficiency in HSCs, though these strategies require further validation.

Translational insights: addressing gaps for clinical trials

As gene-edited HSC therapies progress toward clinical translation, critical considerations include manufacturing scalability, regulatory safety, and long-term engraftment success [36]. Zhang (2017) [19] highlights the cost and complexity of large-scale lentiviral production, pushing the field toward non-viral gene-editing platforms that balance safety and efficiency. Giommetti (2024) [30] further emphasizes the need for standardized protocols in HSC transduction, ensuring that modifications are reproducible across patient cohorts.

On the regulatory front, advances in suicide gene strategies continue to mitigate safety concerns. Larson (2013) [15] and Kao (2019) [16] both demonstrate that HSVsr39TK and EGFRt systems provide effective safeguards against runaway immune activation while maintaining CAR functionality. Wellhausen (2023) [22] further argues that epitope-specific editing (e.g., CD45 modifications) may provide universal donor compatibility, reducing the risks of graft-vs-host disease in allogeneic settings.

Finally, the issue of long-term persistence and tumor surveillance remains a key focus. Studies by Oliveira (2018) [28] and Truscott (2016) [10] confirm that CAR-expressing HSC-derived immune cells provide sustained tumor clearance, though monitoring for clonal hematopoiesis remains necessary due to the risk of leukemogenic transformation over time. Daniel-Moreno (2019) [37] notes that continuous safety tracking through deep sequencing across patient cohorts will be essential in future clinical trials to assess unintended genomic alterations and maintain long-term oversight of gene-modified clones.

Gene-edited HSC therapies present a transformational approach to leukemia and lymphoma treatment, offering advantages in long-term immune persistence, tumor clearance, and multilineage differentiation. While studies by De Oliveira, Liu, and Wellhausen [22, 27, 28] confirm the therapeutic potential of CAR-HSCs, regulatory and manufacturing challenges remain, particularly in vector design, engraftment competition, and off-target safety management [12, 23].

Future directions should focus on improving precision editing (e.g., base/prime editing), optimizing clinical-grade delivery methods, and enhancing engraftment through non-toxic conditioning protocols. Advances in epitope engineering (Wellhausen [22]) and myeloid-targeted microenvironment modulation (Welty [11]) may further enhance durability and applicability in solid tumor contexts, broadening the scope beyond liquid cancers.

To ensure successful clinical translation, a multi-pronged approach integrating improved gene delivery, enhanced safety mechanisms, and engineered niche preparation will be necessary. Continuing research into CRISPR refinements, off-target profiling, and patient-derived HSC models will further shape the evolution of gene-modified HSC therapies as viable clinical treatments.

Limitations of the study

This systematic review has several limitations. First, the inclusion of only preclinical and early translational studies limits the generalizability of findings to clinical settings. Second, the heterogeneity in study designs, gene-editing methodologies, and outcome measures across the reviewed articles complicates direct comparisons and synthesis of results. Third, the focus on English-language publications may have excluded relevant studies published in other languages. Additionally, the reliance on publicly available data means that unpublished or ongoing studies with potentially significant findings were not included. Finally, while efforts were made to minimize bias through independent screening and consensus-based decision-making, the possibility of selection bias cannot be entirely ruled out.

Conclusion

Gene-edited hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) represent a transformative approach to treating leukemia and lymphomas, offering the potential for durable immune reconstitution, sustained tumor clearance, and multilineage differentiation. This systematic review synthesizes preclinical and translational evidence from 19 studies, highlighting the promise of gene-editing technologies such as CRISPR/Cas9, TALENs, and lentiviral vectors in modifying HSCs for cancer immunotherapy. Key findings demonstrate that gene-edited HSCs can generate persistent, tumor-targeting immune effector cells, overcoming limitations of conventional CAR-T therapies, such as transient persistence and antigen escape. However, challenges remain, including optimizing gene delivery efficiency, ensuring long-term safety, and improving engraftment success in the bone marrow niche. Suicide gene strategies, high-fidelity editing tools, and preconditioning regimens have shown potential in addressing these barriers, but further research is needed to refine these approaches for clinical application. The findings of this review underscore the need for continued innovation in gene-editing precision, scalable manufacturing, and robust safety monitoring to advance gene-edited HSC therapies toward clinical trials and eventual patient use.
