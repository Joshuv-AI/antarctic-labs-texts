Establishment of a versatile virus-based system for elucidating herbicide resistance in Galium aparine
Authors: Ying Liu, Kerong Shi, Ming Zhang, Jianguo Wei, Mingfeng Feng, Franck E Dayan, Xiaorong Tao, Zhike Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHerbicide resistance challenges sustainable weed management in crop fields. However, the lack of gene manipulation tools for weeds hinders studies of herbicide resistance evolution and regulation. Here, we identified that tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) can systemically infect Galium aparine, a noxious broadleaf weed common in wheat and rapeseed fields, among 13 broadleaf weeds. The TMV-based expression system can be used to elucidate herbicide resistance in G. aparine. We demonstrated that virus-based expression of BAR, ALS, and its site-directed mutagenized form in G. aparine plants can impart herbicide resistance. To expand the application of this system in metabolic resistance studies, 10 upregulated P450 genes in the tribenuron-methyl-resistant G. aparine population plants were identified. Expression of these P450 genes in G. aparine via this system enhanced tribenuron-methyl resistance in G. aparine plants. Additionally, we confirmed Eleusine indica CYP81A104 functions in herbicide resistance through heterologous expression in G. aparine plants, suggesting that this system can be used to investigate the roles of genes from other weed species. The TMV vector-based assay system enabled the functional validation of weed genes within 2 months, which is shorter than the time required in other heterologous expression systems (ie, stable expression of weed genes in rice or Arabidopsis). Thus, this virus-based system provides insights into target-site and/or metabolic-based resistance mechanisms to herbicides in G. aparine and possibly in other weed species.

Introduction

Weed infestation often negatively impacts crop productivity. Although many herbicides are currently available for weed managements in crop fields, the prolonged use of these herbicides often results in the evolution of herbicide resistance in weeds (Powles and Yu 2010; Délye et al. 2013; Gaines et al. 2020). To date, herbicide-resistant (HR) broadleaf weeds are common in all major cropping systems (Heap 2025). However, elucidation of the mechanisms of resistance remains an elusive and complicated process.

The 2 main mechanisms driving herbicide resistance in weeds include target-site resistance (TSR) and non-target-site resistance (NTSR) (Délye et al. 2013; Gaines et al. 2020). TSR is often mediated by mutations in target genes and/or upregulation of target gene expression. Target mutations always decrease the binding of herbicides with targets and result in herbicide resistance (Murphy and Tranel 2019; Lonhienne et al. 2022). Specifically, mutations of any of 9 specific amino acid residues in the acetolactate synthase (ALS) induce resistance to group 2 herbicides (Tranel and Wright 2002; Yu et al. 2010; Fang et al. 2022; Yin et al. 2025). Meanwhile, increased expression of target genes in weeds results in more herbicide required to achieve a lethal effect and leads to herbicide resistance (Gaines et al. 2010; Laforest et al. 2017; Johnson et al. 2025). NTSR is associated with reduced absorption and translocation of herbicides and herbicide metabolism. For example, cytochrome P450 monooxygenase (P450) and glutathione S-transferase (GST) regulate herbicide metabolism, leading to the metabolic resistance (Rigon et al. 2020; Han et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2024). Recent reports have also indicated that some P450s and GSTs are involved in many herbicide detoxification steps (Dimaano and Iwakami 2021; Pelon et al. 2023).

Upregulation of HR-related genes such as P450, GST, transcription factors, and ATP-binding cassette transporters contributes to herbicide resistance (Cummins et al. 2013; Iwakami et al. 2014; Pan et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2023; Gupta et al. 2025). Current studies of weed herbicide resistance rely mainly on stable expressions of weed genes in rice, Arabidopsis or yeast (Iwakami et al. 2014; Pan et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2024; Rigon et al. 2025). However, stable transformations of these plants require several months to complete, which delays research progress in the understanding for resistance mechanisms. Also, the accuracy of the heterologous expression system requires further validation in the original weed species. Consequently, developments of rapid and easy-to-use methods for weed herbicide resistance studies are greatly needed.

Many plant viruses have broad host ranges including weeds, and some of them can be engineered as the transient expression or gene silencing vectors (Tao and Zhou 2004; Ding et al. 2018; Abrahamian et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2020; Fang et al. 2021). For example, tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) can infect many plant species and can be modified to express proteins in tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana), tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), and many other plants (Lindbo 2007). The potato virus X (PVX)-based vector has also been used to express proteins in potato (S. tuberosum), tomato, tobacco, and other plants (Chapman et al. 1992). The tobacco rattle virus-based vector is perhaps the most widely used viral vector for gene function studies in plants (Liu et al. 2002). Brome mosaic virus has been engineered to silence genes in maize (Zea mays) (Zhu et al. 2014). Barley stripe mosaic virus-based gene silencing and foxtail mosaic virus-mediated gene overexpression have been used successfully in blackgrass (Alopecurus myosuroides) (Mellado-Sánchez et al. 2020). Because transient expression or silencing genes in plants using plant virus-based vectors are easy-to-use, require only a few weeks to complete, and can be done in different plant species, many research laboratories have utilized these transient methods to perform functional genomics in many plants.

In this study, we first tested TMV and PVX infections in different summer or fall broadleaf weed species and found that TMV can cause systemic infection in G. aparine and Malachium aquaticum plants efficiently. ALS and bialaphos resistance (BAR) genes, respectively, were cloned in the TMV-based expression vector and these viruses were inoculated into G. aparine plants. The TMV-based vectors are indeed able to express these genes in G. aparine plants to confer herbicide resistance, which also verifies the reliability of this TMV-based system. Furthermore, we tested the functions of ALS-EL (a site-directed mutagenized ALS gene) in the TSR and 10 tribenuron-methyl responsive P450 genes in tribenuron-methyl resistance in G. aparine, using the TMV-based system. This assay system can be directly used to dissect herbicide resistance in G. aparine plants. In comparison with other reported systems for weeds, this system only requires 2 months to complete and is easy-to-use and low cost.

Results

Tobacco mosaic virus could systemically infect Galium aparine, one of the most common broadleaf weeds in wheat and rapeseed fields

TMV and PVX were selected because of their reported broad host ranges, to test whether they could infect broadleaf weeds systemically. A total of 13 broadleaf weed species were inoculated with crude N. benthamiana leaf extracts infected with TMV-GFP (Table 1). After 21 d, GFP (green fluorescence) was measured in the newly developed young leaves of G. aparine plants, indicating that this virus caused a systemic infection in this plant (Fig. 1a–c). This finding was further confirmed through fluorescence microscopy and Western blot assays (Fig. 1d–e). It is noteworthy that the TMV-GFP-infected G. aparine plants did not develop disease symptoms (Fig. 1c). In this experiment, systemic TMV-GFP infection was also observed in Malachium aquaticum plants, but not in other summer annual broadleaf weeds, including Geranium carolinianum, Descurainia sophia, Capsella bursa-pastoris, Vicia sativa, and Veronica polita (Figure S1 and Table 1). Similarly, no systemic TMV-GFP infection was observed with UV lamp and fluorescence microscopy in the fall annual broadleaf weeds Eclipta prostrata, Ammannia baccifera, Ammannia arenaria, Amaranthus retroflexus, Cucumis melo var. agrestis, and Ludwigia prostrata (Figure S2 and Table 1). Meanwhile, PVX-GFP could not cause systemic infection in G. aparine (Figure S3 and Table 1). Therefore, we selected TMV-GFP and G. aparine for use in the subsequent studies.

Virus-based expression of BAR gene in G. aparine using the TMV-based vector induced glufosinate tolerance

Stable expression of bialaphos resistance (BAR) gene in plants imparts glufosinate tolerance (Block et al. 1987; Mohapatra et al. 1999). In this study, TMV-based vector was used to express the BAR gene in G. aparine. N. benthamiana crude extracts infected with TMV-BAR were inoculated to G. aparine plants. Plants inoculated with the TMV-GFP-infected extracts were used as controls. After glufosinate treatment, TMV-GFP-infected plants were all killed, while the TMV-BAR-infected G. aparine plants showed a significant fresh weight loss but survived, with the resistance index (RI) (defined as the factor by which the herbicide concentration inducing 50% growth reduction (GR50) differed from the concentration required in the control plants) of 13.69 (Fig. 2, Figure S4, and Table S1), indicating that this TMV-based vector can express the BAR gene in G. aparine to induce glufosinate tolerance.

Transient expression of ALS or ALS-EL in G. aparine conferred resistance to ALS-inhibiting herbicides

Tribenuron-methyl is an ALS-inhibiting herbicide and is frequently used to manage G. aparine problem in wheat fields. To date, several G. aparine populations had been reported to have high levels of resistance to tribenuron-methyl application (Sun et al. 2011; Deng et al. 2019). In this study, we tested whether overexpressing ALS could induce a tribenuron-methyl resistance using the TMV-based vector. The recombinant TMV-ALS virus was propagated in N. benthamiana plants and then inoculated to herbicide-sensitive (HS) G. aparine plants. After tribenuron-methyl application, the TMV-ALS-inoculated plants survived, whereas the TMV-GFP-inoculated or the non-inoculated control plants died (Fig. 3a–e), the RI of TMV-ALS-inoculated plants was 1.86 (Table 2 and Table S2). ALS overexpression was validated by RT-qPCR and Western blot assays (Fig. 3b–c). We also tested whether the TMV-ALS-inoculated G. aparine plants were resistant to other ALS-inhibiting herbicides, which belong to different chemical groups while with same modes of action. These plants were also resistant to group 2 herbicides bensulfuron-methyl and florasulam (Figure S5 and Table 2, Table S2). To further characterize the resistance spectrum of ALS overexpression, we sprayed the assayed plants with different concentrations of fluroxypyr (a synthetic auxin herbicide) and bipyrazone (a 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase-inhibiting herbicide); no significant difference was observed between TMV-ALS-inoculated and control plants after been treated with fluroxypyr or bipyrazone (Figure S6 and Table 2, Table S2). These results further confirm that ALS overexpression does not confer multiple resistance to fluroxypyr and bipyrazone.

Because target amino acid substitution is a major cause of TSR in weeds, an ALS mutant with D376E and W574L was cloned into the TMV-based vector to produce TMV-ALS-EL (Sun et al. 2011; Deng et al. 2019) (Fig. 3a). We also compared its function in herbicide resistance with the wild-type ALS. Although both TMV-ALS- and TMV-ALS-EL-inoculated G. aparine plants had resistance to tribenuron-methyl, the resistance imparted by the TMV-ALS-EL-inoculated G. aparine plants was much stronger, with the RI of 5.47 (Fig. 3d–e and Table 2, Table S2). Stronger resistance was also observed in the plants treated with bensulfuron-methyl or florasulam, with RI of 5.56 or 13.80, respectively (Figure S5 and Table 2, Table S2), but not fluroxypyr or bipyrazone (Figure S6 and Table 2, Table S2).

Tribenuron-methyl treatment upregulated the expressions of 10 P450 genes in the resistant G. aparine plants

P450-mediated metabolic resistance has been reported to involve, in different modes of action, in the resistance to multiple herbicides in weeds, and weeds with metabolic resistance are difficult to manage in fields (Han et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2023; Rigon et al. 2025). To test whether this assay system can be used to investigate P450-mediated metabolic resistance, multiple G. aparine populations were collected from wheat fields. Through the whole-plant dose-response assay, one population (HR) was resistant to tribenuron-methyl treatment, with the RI of 303.58 (Fig. 4a–c and Table S3). To test the involvement of P450 genes in this resistance in G. aparine HR population, we pretreated HS and HR plants with a P450 inhibitor PBO or ABT. After 1 h, these plants were sprayed with tribenuron-methyl. As expected, the herbicide resistance in HR plants was reversed by both P450 inhibitors, the RI values decreased from 303.58 to 50.83 or to 41.75, for PBO and ABT, respectively (Fig. 4c and Table S3), indicating that P450s are involved in tribenuron-methyl resistance in the HR population. To validate the function of P450s in tribenuron-methyl resistance, the residue of tribenuron-methyl in HS and HR G. aparine plants was determined, we found that the amount of tribenuron-methyl residue did not significantly differ at 6 h, while the residue in the HR plants was significantly lower than that in the HS plant at 3 d and 5 d post tribenuron-methyl application (Figure S7), suggesting enhanced tribenuron-methyl metabolism in HR plants. To identify the role of individual P450 genes in tribenuron-methyl resistance, we performed RNA-seq from both population HR and HS plants treated with tribenuron-methyl at the concentration of 22.5 g a.i. ha−1. A total of 10 different P450 contigs (CYP81Q32, CYP76A26, CYP76B6, CYP38, CYP86A22, CYP89A9, CYP77A3, CYP19-4, CYP73A4, and CYP37) were upregulated P450 genes in HR plants (Table S4). The tribenuron-methyl-upregulated expressions of these contigs were further validated through RT-qPCR (Fig. 4d). Phylogenetic analysis indicated that these 10 P450 genes belonged to different subgroups (Figure S8).

Virus-based overexpressing the P450 genes in HS G. aparine plants resulted in tribenuron-methyl resistance

We cloned individual P450 ORFs into the TMV-based expression vector and propagated the recombinant viruses individually in N. benthamiana plants (Fig. 5a). Crude extracts were then inoculated to the leaves of HS plants followed by tribenuron-methyl application as described above. G. aparine plants transiently overexpressing individual P450 genes survived with the treatment using recommended tribenuron-methyl dose (22.5 g a.i. ha−1), while all the TMV-GFP-infected plants were killed by the herbicide (Fig. 5b–c). When 4-fold or 8-fold concentrated tribenuron-methyl was applied to the plants, G. aparine plants infected with TMV-CYP81Q32, TMV-CYP38, TMV-CYP76A26, TMV-CYP89A9, TMV-CYP77A3, TMV-CYP19-4, or TMV-CYP76B6 still survived (Fig. 5d–g and Figure S9). To test whether these P450 genes could confer resistance to the synthetic auxin herbicide fluroxypyr, we sprayed this herbicide to HS plants transiently overexpressing individual P450 genes. Transiently overexpressing most of these P450 genes did not confer a fluroxypyr resistance, except the CYP38 gene (Figure S10). Among the uninduced P450 genes in the HR population, CYP76BK1 was tested for its potential to confer tribenuron-methyl resistance through TMV-mediated expression (Figure S11). Specifically, G. aparine plants expressing CYP76BK1 were treated with a 1-fold concentration of tribenuron-methyl. We found that TMV-CYP76BK1-infected plants exhibited no survival, consistent with control results (Figure S11). These data from the TMV expression system support that the 10 P450 genes are involved in tribenuron-methyl resistance in the HR G. aparine population, albeit at varying effectiveness.

Expression of Eleusine indica CYP81A104 gene in G. aparine plants reduced their susceptibility to imazamox and tribenuron-methyl

CYP81A104 is originally identified in an imazamox-resistant E. indica population collected from a field with an imidazolinone-resistant rice cultivar. Its function in imazamox and tribenuron-methyl resistance has been studied using transgenic Arabidopsis plants (Yao et al. 2024). We would like to test whether our assay system is able to characterize the function of EiCYP81A104 in G. aparine. To do this, we produced a TMV-EiCYP81A104 expression vector, propagated the recombinant virus in N. benthamiana plants, and then inoculated the recombinant virus to G. aparine plants (Fig. 6a). G. aparine plants transiently expressing EiCYP81A104 had a partial resistance to the imazamox or tribenuron-methyl treatment at the different doses (Fig. 6b–e and Figure S12), a phenotype consistent with that observed in EiCYP81A104-transgenic Arabidopsis plants (Yao et al. 2024). Therefore, our data support that the assay system can be used to study herbicide resistance in G. aparine plants for candidate genes identified in other weed species.

Discussion

Prolonged use of herbicides often leads weeds to evolve herbicide resistance. Our current understanding of the mechanism governing herbicide resistance evolution and regulation is still very limited (Délye et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2019; Gaines et al. 2020). Due to the lack of direct, fast, and easy-to-use systems for weeds, validation of herbicide resistance candidate genes requires stable expressions of these candidate genes in model plants (ie, rice or Arabidopsis). Although heterologous expression systems are useful, these studies are time-consuming and require further validations in the original weed plants (Iwakami et al. 2014; Pan et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2024; Rigon et al. 2025). To overcome this limitation for broadleaf weeds, we developed an herbicide resistance assay method for G. aparine using a reported TMV-based expression vector (Figs. 1 and 2) (Lindbo 2007). This herbicide resistance assay system is highly efficient and versatile for G. aparine plants. Because the TMV-based recombinant viruses only systemically infect 2 assayed weed species, other published plant virus-based expression vectors can also be tested in future to meet the requirement for many other weed species. Alternatively, weed-derived plant viruses, after thorough characterization, can be harnessed to develop viral vector systems for genetic manipulation in different weeds.

Plant virus-based expression vectors are widely recognized for their speed and convenience in plant gene function research (Faivre-Rampant et al. 2004; Yuan et al. 2011; Abrahamian et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2020). As the first reported case of virus-based gene silencing in weeds, the knockdown of glutathione transferase gene AmGSTF1 significantly enhances the susceptibility of A. myosuroides to fenoxaprop (Mellado-Sánchez et al. 2020). Notably, in current study, we confirmed TMV infectivity in G. aparine and further demonstrated that TMV-based expression of ALS or P450 genes confers herbicide resistance (Figs. 3–5). Crucially, this system enables functional validation of weed genes within 2 months, a rapid turnaround is particularly advantageous for studying HR weeds. These findings validate the TMV-based expression system as a valuable tool for elucidating herbicide resistance in G. aparine. TMV-based expression systems offer distinct advantages for gene functional studies. First, TMV infects over 100 plant species, demonstrating extensive adaptability across diverse hosts (Holmes 1946; Gibbs et al. 2015). Second, efficient replication of TMV enables substantial accumulation of recombinant proteins, with high-yield protein production (Knapp and Lewandowski 2001; Lindbo 2007). Third, TMV vectors support the synthesis of recombinant proteins exceeding 50 kDa (Knapp and Lewandowski 2001; Lindbo 2007).

The developed herbicide resistance assay system demonstrates significant potential for elucidating both target-site resistance (TSR) and metabolic resistance. ALS substitutions in 2 amino acid residues at the position 376 and 574 in G. aparine caused herbicide resistance (Tranel and Wright 2002; Sun et al. 2011; Deng et al. 2019), through TMV-based transient expression. This ALS mutant (ALS-EL) resulted in a higher tribenuron-methyl resistance than wild-type ALS-expressed plants (Table 2), indicating that our herbicide resistance assay system can be used to analyze the functions of other TSR-associated candidate genes/mutations. Metabolic resistance is dependent on the activity of detoxifying enzymes (Cummins et al. 2013; Yu and Powles 2014; Pan et al. 2019; Han et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2024). The usefulness of this system in metabolic resistance studies is illustrated in the difference in P450 gene expression between the tribenuron-methyl resistant G. aparine plants with that in the HS G. aparine plants. The 10 upregulated P450 genes upon tribenuron-methyl treatment were then tested using this assay system. Our data suggest that the 10 P450 genes are tightly associated with tribenuron-methyl resistance, with effectiveness varying across genes, while most may not be involved in fluroxypyr detoxification (Fig. 5 and Figure S10). Three P450 genes (CYP86A22, CYP73A4, and CYP37) exhibited weaker phenotypes in response to tribenuron-methyl treatments (Fig. 5). For these weaker phenotypes, further verification with additional techniques (eg, gene silencing and gene knockout) and the inclusion of a negative control would be helpful to validate their effectiveness. Additionally, CYP76BK1 showed no association with tribenuron-methyl resistance in our TMV expression system (Figure S11), thereby highlighting the functional diversity among G. aparine P450s in herbicide resistance. Based on these TMV system results, we cannot conclude whether these P450s are directly involved in metabolizing tribenuron-methyl; thus, their molecular mechanisms of these P450s in metabolic resistance require further investigation. Collectively, this assay system also serves as a useful tool for herbicide metabolic resistance studies in weeds. While a virus-based expression system has been successfully established for G. aparine in this study, the development of gene silencing, gene knockout, or stable transformation systems is still required. The implementation of a comprehensive genetic manipulation toolkit would markedly improve our understanding of G. aparine responses to herbicide application, providing critical insights into adaptive mechanisms and resistance evolution.

Along with rapid development of the high-quality genome sequencing technologies, the genomes of many weeds have been assembled to great resolution (Ye et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2023; Montgomery et al. 2024; Montgomery and Gaines 2024; Brunharo et al. 2025; Johnson et al. 2025), and many resistance-related genes have been identified, yet the biological functions of most remain to be characterized. Versatile gene manipulation systems, such as those based on plant viruses, may accelerate the genome-wide functional studies for weeds by enabling rapid evaluation to determine which genes are worth further characterization. Originating from bacterial antiviral defense mechanisms, CRISPR technology has revolutionized gene editing, its most significant agricultural application lies in the development of HR crops (Kuang et al. 2024; Luo and Liu 2025). Extending this technology to weeds will provide unprecedented opportunities to dissect the molecular basis of herbicide resistance at the genetic level. These techniques developed for weeds will provide the multi-dimensional investigative tools to unravel the evolutionary trajectories and regulatory networks of herbicide resistance.

In summary, a versatile and robust TMV-based gene expression system for directly understanding herbicide resistance in broadleaf weed G. aparine was developed. Target-site resistance and metabolic resistance to tribenuron-methyl in G. aparine were investigated via the developed approach. The established system will significantly enhance our understanding of how weeds rapidly adapt to environmental cues, such as herbicide application.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and chemicals

Galium aparine populations were collected from wheat fields in the Jiangsu and Henan provinces. A tribenuron-methyl-resistant G. aparine population (HR) was obtained from the Xiannv Town, Jiangsu Province where tribenuron-methyl had been applied for many years. A tribenuron-methyl susceptible G. aparine population (HS) was obtained from a noncultivated land in the Funing County, Jiangsu Province. All weed species used in this study were listed in Table 1. Seed dormancy was broken by burying them in soil for 1 month. Subsequently, seeds were dried and stored at 4 °C for further use.

Tribenuron-methyl was purchased from the Jiangsu Repont Agrochemical Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). Fluroxypyr and imazamox were purchased from the Jiangsu Flag Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). Glufosinate-ammonium and florasulam were from the Sichuan Lier Crop Science Co., Ltd. (Sichuan, China) and the Xianglin Meifeng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China), respectively. Bensulfuron-methyl was from the Shandong Weifang Rainbow Agrochemical Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). Bipyrazone was from the KingAgroot Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). P450 inhibitors piperonyl butoxide (PBO) and 1-aminobenzotriazole (ABT) were from the Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Whole-plant dose-response assays

This assay was conducted to determine the resistance level of HR and HS plants to tribenuron-methyl. Twenty G. aparine seeds were sown in a plastic pot (7 cm × 7 cm × 8 cm) filled with a nutrient soil (1:1 organic fertilizer and soil) and the pots were placed in a greenhouse maintained for 12 h at 20 °C in the daytime and 12 h at 15 °C in the dark. Seedlings were thinned to 5 plants per pot and treated with various herbicides at the 2 to 3 whorl leaf stage.

HR and HS plants were pretreated with the P450 inhibitor PBO at the concentration of 2,600 g ha−1 or ABT at the concentration of 600 g ha−1. After 1 h, these plants were treated again with tribenuron-methyl at the concentrations of 0, 45, 90, 180, 360, 720 and 1,440 g a.i. ha−1, for the HR population or the concentrations of 0, 0.70, 1.41, 2.81, 5.62, 11.25 and 22.5 g a.i. ha−1, for the HS population. The recommended field application concentration of tribenuron-methyl is 22.5 g a.i. ha−1. Aboveground fresh tissues were collected from individual assayed plants and measured at 21 d post tribenuron-methyl treatment. The GR50 values (herbicide caused 50% growth reduction) were calculated for each treatment. Each experiment had at least 2 independent replicates and each replicate had 4 technical replicates.

Tribenuron-methyl residue in G. aparine plants

When the G. aparine plants (HS and HR) reached the 2-whorl leaf stage, they were sprayed with tribenuron-methyl at the concentration of 45 g a.i. ha−1. Samples were collected at 6 h, 3 d and 5 d post treatment, with 4 biological replicates (2.5 g) per time point.

For tribenuron-methyl extraction, 10 mL of acetonitrile and a salt packet (4 g anhydrous MgSO4, 1 g NaCl, 1 g trisodium citrate dihydrate, and 0.5 g disodium hydrogen citrate sesquihydrate) were added to each sample. The mixture was homogenized for 1 min and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 5 min. An aliquot of the supernatant was transferred to a cleanup tube (150 mg anhydrous MgSO4, 25 mg primary secondary amine, and 2.5 mg graphitized carbon black per mL of extract), vortexed for 1 min, and centrifuged again at 10,000 × g for 5 min. The final supernatant was filtered through a microporous membrane for analysis.

Analysis was performed using a Waters UPLC system coupled with a Waters TQ-S MS/MS system. Chromatographic separation used a Waters BEH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 50 mm) with a mobile phase of acetonitrile and aqueous solution (0.01% formic acid and 5 mM ammonium acetate). The gradient elution program at 0.40 mL/min was: 5% acetonitrile from 0 to 0.5 min, increased to 95% at 2.0 to 3.0 min, and returned to 5% at 4.0 to 5.0 min.

Mass spectrometry used electrospray ionization in positive mode with the following parameters: drying gas temperature 500 °C, gas flow 1,000 mL/min (recommended unit), ion source temperature 150 °C, capillary voltage 2.9 kV, and cone voltage 24 V for tribenuron-methyl. Detection used multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) with the retention time at 2.03 min. Qualitative transitions were m/z 396.098→154.9 (14 eV) and 396.098→181.0 (22 eV), while the quantitative transition was m/z 306.145→261.

RNA-seq and expression validation of P450s

HR and HS G. aparine plants (2 to 3 whorl leaf stage) were treated with tribenuron-methyl at the concentration of 22.5 g a.i. ha−1. At 12 and 24 h post tribenuron-methyl treatment, the plants were harvested. Three biological samples (pooled leaf tissues from 2 to 3 plants each) were collected at each time point from each treatment. About 0.1 g leaf tissue was taken from each biological sample and frozen in liquid nitrogen. The samples collected from the non-tribenuron-methyl-treated plants (0 h) were used as the controls. The frozen samples were sent to the Genedenovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China) for the paired-end RNA sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq machine and the full-length transcriptome sequencing on a PacBio Sequel II machine. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined using the edgeR software based on the false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 1. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis were then conducted to further define the DEGs. Genes with >2-fold upregulation in the HR population, compared to the HS samples, were selected for further validation (Table S4).

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed to validate the expressions of the selected DEGs, the TATA-box binding protein (TBP) gene as the internal control. The relative expression levels of the assayed DEGs were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method (Wei et al. 2024). Each RT-qPCR had 4 technical replicates and 2 biological replicates. Statistical significance (P < 0.05) among the treatments was determined using the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in the SPSS v26.0 package. Genes with >2-fold changes in relative expressions were classified as the upregulation or the downregulation genes. The primers used in RT-qPCR are listed in Table S5.

Infectivity of tobacco mosaic virus in broadleaf weeds

A total of 13 broadleaf weed species were selected and tested (Table 1). An infectious clone expressing TMV-GFP (Lindbo 2007) was used to monitor TMV local and systemic infections in various broadleaf weeds. Briefly, TMV-GFP was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain (GV3101) cells via electroporation. The transformed Agrobacterium cells were cultured, diluted to OD600 = 1.0, and then agroinfiltrated into the leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana plants. Seven d later, green fluorescence from TMV-GFP infection was monitored using a LUYOR-3410 portable UV light.

For virus inoculation to G. aparine, leaves of G. aparine plants (2-whorl true leaf stage) were dusted with carborundum. Leaves of the agroinfiltrated N. benthamiana plants were collected, grounded in PB buffer (118 mM Na2HPO4 and 23.5 mM NaH2PO4, pH = 7.5), and gently rub-inoculated onto the G. aparine leaves. The inoculated G. aparine plants were grown inside a greenhouse for 7 d, 14 d and 21 d. TMV-GFP infection in the assayed G. aparine plants was monitored under a LUYOR-3410 portable UV light or an Axio Observer3 inverted fluorescence microscope. Similar methodology was used for other weed species.

Phylogenetic analysis of G. aparine P450 genes

Multiple sequence alignment of the candidate P450 sequences was conducted with MAFFT v7.520, and 38 nonredundant CYP genes were selected for phylogenetic analysis. A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using IQ-TREE2 v2.3.6, with the best-fit substitution model determined as LG + R4 (ΔAIC > 15). The final tree topology was visualized and optimized using iTOL, and key evolutionary branches and functional clustering patterns were highlighted.

Constructions of TMV-based vectors and preparations of recombinant viruses

The TMV-GFP infectious clone was digested with the PacI and PspXI enzymes at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by gel extraction using the Gel DNA Extraction Kit v.4.0 (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The BAR gene was PCR-amplified from the pTF101.1 plasmid, gel purified, and recombined into the linearized TMV-GFP clone using a homologous recombinase (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) at 37 °C for 30 min. The re-ligated product was transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α cells (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) and the transformed cells were cultured on the LB plates followed by an overnight incubation in a 37 °C incubator. The colonies were tested by colony PCR, and the plasmids were extracted using a Plasmid Extraction Kit (Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China).

To construct a TMV-GaALS expression vector, total RNA was extracted from a G. aparine plant using an RNA Simple Total RNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and cDNAs were synthesized using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix supplemented with a + gDNA wiper (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). The full-length GaALS sequence was PCR-amplified and the resulting product was purified and cloned into the pClone007 T-vector (TaKaRa Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, China). The pClone007-GaALSD376E&W574L (ALS-EL) plasmid was generated through PCR-mediated site-directed mutagenesis using the pClone007-GaALS plasmid as the template. In addition, the P450 genes were amplified individually from the G. aparine cDNA, while the EiCYP81A104 was amplified from the Eleusine indica cDNA. The resulting PCR products were individually cloned into the TMV-based vector. All primers are listed in Table S5.

Plasmid sequencing ensured correct assembly of these expression vectors (Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Each of these TMV-based expression vectors was transformed into A. tumefaciens strain (GV3101) cells and the transformed Agrobacterium cultures were infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. At 7 d post agroinfiltration, the infected leaves were harvested and stored in a −80 °C freezer for further use. The primers used in vector constructions are listed in Table S5.

Analyses of the functions of G. aparine genes in herbicide resistance

For gene expression assays, all leaves of HS G. aparine plants (2-whorl true leaf stage) were rub-inoculated with crude N. benthamiana leaf extracts infected with one of the recombinant TMV viruses. The inoculated G. aparine plants were grown inside a plant incubator for 7 d and the inoculated leaves were analyzed for protein accumulation through Western blot assays. Briefly, 0.2 g leaf tissues were collected, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then grounded in a mortar. The frozen powder was transferred into a 1.5-mL centrifuge tube placed on ice. The protein extraction buffer was freshly prepared using 9.7 mL GTEN buffer (10% glycerol, 25 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, pH = 7.5), 100 µL 1 M DTT, 200 µL protease inhibitor (100× dilution), and 0.2 g poly-vinylpolypyrrolidone and then added into the centrifuge tube (500 µL buffer per tube). The tube was vortexed for 1 min, incubated on ice for 15 min, and then centrifuged at 4,080 × g and 4 °C for 10 min. After boiling for 5 min, a 20 µL sample was analyzed by SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis followed by Western blot assays using an anti-HA or anti-GFP antibody.

For herbicide dose-response assays, the G. aparine plants were sprayed with different concentrations of herbicides at 10 d post virus inoculation using a 3WP-2000 sprayer equipped with a flat fan nozzle (Nanjing Research Institute for Agricultural Mechanization, Nanjing, China). For glufosinate-ammonium, the TMV-BAR-infected HS G. aparine plants were sprayed at the concentration of 0, 14.06, 56.25, 225.00 or 900.00 g a.i. ha−1 (the recommended field application dose is 450 g a.i. ha−1) (Table S1). For tribenuron-methyl, bensulfuron-methyl (the recommended field application dose is 75 g a.i. ha−1), florasulam (the recommended field application dose is 4.5 g a.i. ha−1), fluroxypyr (the recommended field application dose is 210 g a.i. ha−1), and bipyrazone (the recommended field application dose is 37.5 g a.i. ha−1), the TMV-GaALS- or TMV-GaALS-EL-infected HS plants were sprayed with these herbicides at different doses (Table S2). The HS plants without TMV-GaALS and TMV-GaALS-EL infection or with TMV-GFP infection were also sprayed with one of the above herbicides and were used as the controls.

To study the functions of 10 P450 genes in herbicide resistance, tribenuron-methyl was applied at the concentration of 0, 22.5, 90 or 180 g a.i. ha−1 to the HS plants infected with 1 of 10 TMV-P450 recombinant viruses. Fluroxypyr was applied at the concentration of 0 or 13.125 g a.i. ha−1. In addition, tribenuron-methyl was applied at the concentration of 0 or 22.5 g a.i. ha−1 to the TMV-CYP76BK1- or TMV-EiCYP81A104-infected HS plants. Imazamox was applied at the concentration of 0 or 60 g a.i. ha−1 (the recommended field application dose is 120 g a.i. ha−1) to the TMV-EiCYP81A104-infected HS plants.

Fresh aboveground biomass of the assayed plants was measured at 21 d post herbicide treatment to calculate the growth inhibition rate, except for glufosinate and fluroxypyr, which were measured at 5 d post treatment. These experiments were conducted at least 3 times using a completely randomized design with 4 replicates per treatment.
