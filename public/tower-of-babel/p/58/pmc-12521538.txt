Quantitative analysis of ferrohydrodynamics of blood containing magnetic nanocarriers for advanced drug delivery design via hybrid machine learning
Authors: Hashem O Alsaab, Yusuf S Althobaiti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFerrohydrodynamics of blood containing magnetic nanocarriers was carried out in this study to evaluate the influence of magnetic field on the nanocarrier drug delivery formulation, with the goal of improving spatial control and efficiency in advanced targeted drug delivery systems. The model of magnetic field was carried out by Maxwell’ model, while the blood velocity through the vessel was modeled using Navier-Stokes equations. Then the results of computations were used as input data for development of machine learning (ML) models. Indeed, a comprehensive study on the prediction of velocity (U) based on input variables (x, y) using three different ML models: K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN), Decision Tree (DT), and Gradient Boosting (GB) was studied. To achieve this, a systematic approach is followed, including pre-processing tasks such as normalization, outlier detection, and data splitting into training and testing sets. Hyper-parameter optimization is performed using the Rain Optimization Algorithm (ROA) to fine-tune the models and improve their predictive capabilities. The final findings show the robustness of the DT, KNN, and GB models in predicting velocity. The DT model achieved an R2 value of 0.90278, highlighting a robust correspondence between model and expected values. The KNN model outperformed with an R² of 0.99088, showcasing its superior predictive accuracy, which is critical for precisely predicting nanoparticle trajectories and optimizing magnetic guidance in targeted cancer drug delivery. The GB model also exhibited strong performance with an R2 score of 0.96168.Keywords: Blood velocity, Ferrohydrodynamics, Machine learning, Cancer treatment, Gradient boostingSubject terms: Biomedical engineering, Chemical engineering, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

Nanotechnology has played major role in drug delivery applications where drugs can be loaded into nanoparticle (e.g., polymeric particles) for preparation of engineered particles for therapeutic applications1. These nanoparticles can encapsulate the medicine and deliver it to the cancer cells provided that those are designed properly. To avoid damaging normal cells in cancer therapy, targeted drug delivery systems have been on the focus of research and development over the last decade2–4. The systems can be controlled via external stimuli such as electrical and magnetic fields to release the loaded drug. Development of magnetic bio-compatible nanocarriers have been studied recently for controlled release of drugs loaded onto the structure of magnetic nanoparticles. In this case, external magnetic force can be also utilized for better efficiency of drug to the cancer cells5–7. To understand the behavior of this system, experimental studies as well as theoretical works can be done. For the modeling, some methods have been developed which can be used to manipulate the release of drug and find out the effect of importance parameters on the nanoparticle agglomeration and drug release8,9.

Modeling can be also performed on the fluid flow via computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to evaluate the flow pattern in blood vessel where the nanoparticles move and carry the drug for cancer treatment. The presence of nanoparticles can change the flow pattern and cause a change in the velocity profile inside the vessel due to the effect of solid particles which cause additional solid-fluid interactions in the solution and should be considered in the momentum transfer equation. Moreover, the effect of external field can be added to the model to consider its effect and find out its relationship with the motion of magnetic nanoparticles in the blood vessel. The integration of CFD technique and machine learning (ML) is an emerging idea which has been used recently for modeling fluid flow. This modeling framework is based on training ML algorithms using CFD outputs dataset10–12. This new method would be useful for computational modeling of complex systems as the CFD method if computationally expensive and ML models are facile to be implemented. The method of integrating CFD and ML can be also used for modeling fluid flow in targeted drug delivery applications where the motion of particles in blood vessel can be simulated via this novel technique13.

ML has grown into one of the most influential sub-fields of artificial intelligence (AI), enabling computers to learn complicated processes such as chemical processes14. In recent years, ML techniques have been extensively applied to solve complex problems and improve decision-making processes15,16. ML models are versatile and powerful for designing cancer treatments. One particular area of interest in ML is the development of predictive models that can accurately forecast outcomes based on input variables. This hybrid methodology has been successfully used for modeling magnetic nanoparticles13,17,18.

In this study, we focus on predicting velocity (U) based on input variables (x, y) using three different ML models: Decision Tree (DT), K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN), and Gradient Boosting (GB). The goal is to create models capable of effectively capturing the intricate relationships between input variables and velocity of fluid, ensuring accurate and dependable predictions. Prediction of velocity field can help understand how the magnetic nanoparticles move in the blood vessel so that their motion can be controlled to reach the desired target site for increasing delivery and treatment efficiency.

These methods were selected based on their distinct strengths and relevance to the problem at hand. ROA was chosen for hyperparameter optimization due to its ability to efficiently explore solution spaces and improve model performance by simulating natural raindrop behavior, offering a balance between exploitation and exploration. The DT model provides interpretability and excels in discovery of non-linear relationships. The KNN model was included for its simplicity and strong performance in data-driven prediction tasks, particularly where local patterns are critical. GB, an ensemble learning method, was selected for its capacity to iteratively reduce errors and deliver high accuracy through boosting techniques. The DT model partitions data based on feature conditions and is effective for capturing nonlinear or complex interactions. In contrast, the KNN model operates by leveraging the similarity between data points in order to generate predictions. The algorithm assigns a novel data point to the majority class of its k nearest neighbors, rendering it appropriate for data analytics. Ultimately, Gradient Boosting is an ensemble approach that combines multiple weak (base) models to form a resilient predictive model. The model’s performance is enhanced in a step-by-step manner by prioritizing the misclassified data points. For maximizing the effectiveness of these models, we employ the Rain Optimization Algorithm (ROA) for hyper-parameter tuning. This algorithm aids in finding the optimal combination of hyper-parameters for each model, enhancing their predictive accuracy and robustness. The primary aim of the current work is to build a new modeling framework for simulation of magnetic targeted drug delivery system for cancer therapy. CFD modeling approach is combined with ML model for prediction of fluid velocity containing magnetic nanoparticles which is under external magnetic force. The data are obtained from modeling of magnetic fields as well as fluid flow. Finally, the data are used for training ML models, and the models are tested and compared to find out the best one in terms of accuracy in predicting velocity filed in blood stream. For the first time, we utilized Rain Optimization Algorithm (ROA) with three aforementioned ML models integrated to CFD for simulation of magnetic-based drug delivery system for targeted cancer therapy.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows: Sect. “Problem description” describes the problem formulation and computational methodology, including the governing equations for ferrohydrodynamics, CFD simulation setup, and the integration of machine learning models with the Rain Optimization Algorithm for hyperparameter tuning. Section “Computational methodology” presents the results of CFD simulations, model optimization, and comparative performance analysis of the Decision Tree, K-Nearest Neighbor, and Gradient Boosting algorithms. Section “Results and discussion” discusses the findings in the context of targeted drug delivery applications and highlights the practical implications of the proposed hybrid framework. Finally, Sect. “Conclusion” provides concluding remarks, acknowledges limitations, and suggests directions for future research.

The selection of DT, KNN, and GB over more complex approaches such as neural networks was intentional. Neural networks, while powerful, generally require substantially larger datasets to achieve stable performance and are less interpretable, which can hinder understanding of feature–response relationships in scientific modeling. In contrast, the chosen algorithms provide a balance between predictive accuracy, computational efficiency, and interpretability, making them well-suited for CFD-generated datasets where physical insight is as important as numerical precision. Moreover, these methods are robust to moderate dataset sizes and noise, ensuring reliable performance without the extensive tuning and computational overhead often required by deep learning architectures.

Following the pre-processing phase, the models undergo hyper-parameter optimization using the Rain Optimization Algorithm (ROA). This systematic approach ensures that the models are developed and evaluated in a methodical and rigorous manner. The final evaluation and testing phase provide insights into the models’ predictive capabilities and their suitability for the given problem. By following this systematic methodology, this study aims to provide reliable and robust models that can effectively capture complex patterns and make accurate predictions. The methodology ensures that the models are developed, optimized, and assessed in a consistent and transparent manner, enhancing the credibility and validity of the study’s findings.

Rain optimization algorithm (ROA)

ROA27 is inspired by raindrops’ natural actions and their interactions with the lowest points of terrain they encounter. Individual raindrops are viewed as potential solutions in this approach. Random initialization is used to introduce a set of raindrops into the algorithm. These raindrops are linked to a parameter known as the “radius,” which influences the raindrop’s lifespan. The radius is used to change the position of the solution and gradually decreases over iterations. As long as the solution’s fitness improves with each iteration, the solution continuously adapts its position by moving in a consistent direction. Furthermore, ROA incorporates three additional imitated behaviors: (i) amalgamation of nearby raindrops, (ii) elimination of raindrops with radius less than a specified threshold, and (iii) generation of new raindrops. The addition of new raindrops ensures diversity, while consistent movement in a specific direction amplifies the algorithm’s exploitation aspect.

In this study, the ROA was implemented with the following parameters: a population size of 100 raindrops, a maximum of 80 iterations, and an evaporation coefficient of 0.12 to regulate raindrop elimination. Additionally, the radius decrement factor was set to 0.95 to control the reduction of raindrop radii over iterations, and the minimum radius threshold for raindrop survival was established at 0.01.

To compare the performance of different machine learning models, we used a fitness function defined as the mean R² score obtained from 5-fold cross-validation (CV). This approach evaluates how well each model predicts the target variable (e.g., velocity U) across multiple subsets of the data. By averaging the R² scores from the five folds, the fitness function ensures that the selected model generalizes effectively to unseen data, balancing accuracy and robustness. This method provides a reliable metric for model selection in our study.

K-nearest neighbor (KNN) regression

KNN technique is widely used in various regression models, combining aspects of multiple supervised learning methods. It acts as a core method for tasks involving both classification and regression. KNN is often referred to as a “lazy algorithm” because it doesn’t generalize from training examples and retains all the data collected during the testing process30. Utilizing KNN regression is straightforward. It entails assessing the count of nearest neighbors that possess identical numerical target values as the focal data point. Alternatively, one has the option to assign weights to the nearest neighbors in an inverse manner, considering their distances from the central point. To apply regression, distance metrics similar to those used in KNN classification are employed to measure the proximity between samples. Two common distance metrics are the Euclidean distance and the Manhattan distance31.

Gradient boosting (GB) regression

Ensemble learning stands as a widely embraced strategy, particularly beneficial when the endeavor of constructing a model using a singular method becomes exceedingly formidable. This approach harnesses a varied array of learning algorithms to produce a more accurate estimation by amalgamating predictions derived from these diverse algorithms. Through the integration of multiple ML into a cohesive model, the primary objective of ensemble models is to alleviate biases and diminish discrepancies in the ultimate predictions32,33. Gradient Boosting (GB) is a sequential ensemble method comprised of an iterative series of base models. In this structured approach, each subsequent model in the sequence learns from the errors made by its predecessor, aiming to iteratively enhance the overall predictive performance of the ensemble34. This specific ML model generates predictions by employing a boosting technique on an ensemble of prediction models, often comprising less reliable individual models, commonly in the form of decision trees. These results suggest that the K-Nearest Neighbor model performed the best, with the maximum R2 and minimal RMSE and MAE values. The superior performance of the KNN model can be attributed to specific characteristics of the dataset. The underlying velocity field exhibits smooth, locally varying patterns in relation to spatial coordinates, meaning that nearby points in the (x, y) domain tend to have very similar velocity values. This favors KNN’s instance-based learning approach, which directly leverages local neighborhood similarity rather than relying on a global parametric fit. Furthermore, the relatively low noise level and high spatial continuity in the CFD-generated data allow KNN to achieve high predictive accuracy without overfitting, particularly when optimized with a small number of neighbors through ROA tuning. This consistent performance underscores its robust capability to generalize across test data, making it the optimal choice among the evaluated models.

KNN shows no signs of overfitting, as evidenced by its robust performance and rigorous evaluation. The R2 score of 0.99088 reflected a strong alignment between predicted and CFD velocity values, alongside low error metrics (RMSE: 5.3835E−04, MAE: 2.02000E−04), indicating high predictive accuracy. The use of 5-fold cross-validation ensured the model was tested across multiple data subsets, yielding consistent performance and confirming its ability to generalize to unseen data. Additionally, hyperparameter optimization via the ROA fine-tuned the model effectively, balancing exploration and exploitation. These factors collectively affirm that the KNN model is not overfitted and reliably predicts velocity in this dataset.

As can be observed, the variations of velocity field calculated by ML models are in agreement with those results obtained by CFD which can confirm the validity of the employed modeling strategy in this work. Indeed, combination of CFD and ML models indicated to be a great tool for obtaining computational results with less computational efforts17. Indeed, one can benefit the advantages of both models when they are both integrated for modeling fluid flow. This method and the similar results were reported in other studies with the same prediction which confirm our results for hybrid modeling of nanoparticles13,17,18.

Figure 11 illustrates the partial dependence of velocity on each spatial coordinate, offering insight into how variations in x (axial) and y (radial) positions independently influence the KNN model’s predictions. The curve for y shows a steeper gradient, indicating that small changes in radial position cause pronounced shifts in velocity. This is consistent with the parabolic profile typical of viscous flow in cylindrical channels, where velocity peaks at the centerline and drops toward the vessel wall. In contrast, the x-coordinate displays a more gradual change in velocity, reflecting the smoother axial variations in flow induced by the magnetic field along the vessel length.

Figure 12 illustrates the permutation importance of the input variables x and y, representing the axial and radial coordinates in a blood vessel, respectively, for predicting velocity U in a machine learning model. The figure displays these scores, likely as a bar chart, revealing that both x and y are critical for capturing the velocity distribution. This reflects the complex interplay between spatial position and velocity under an external magnetic field, where y may dominate due to the parabolic radial velocity profile, while x captures axial variations influenced by the magnetic field. This analysis emphasizes the need to consider both coordinates for effective modeling of fluid dynamics in magnetic targeted drug delivery systems.

The findings of this study have practical significance for both industrial and engineering applications where precise control of fluid-particle interactions is critical. For example, in the pharmaceutical sector, the integration of CFD and ML models can accelerate the design of magnetically guided nanocarriers for cancer therapy, enabling cost-effective pre-clinical testing before moving to experimental trials. In biomedical engineering, the framework can be applied to optimize catheter-based magnetic targeting systems, improving drug localization and reducing systemic side effects. Beyond healthcare, the methodology can be extended to industrial processes such as wastewater treatment, where magnetic nanoparticles are used to capture and remove heavy metals, or in petrochemical engineering for enhanced oil recovery where ferrofluids are injected into reservoirs. By providing fast, reliable predictions of particle motion under external magnetic fields, this approach reduces reliance on resource-intensive CFD simulations, thereby facilitating rapid prototyping, process optimization, and real-time decision-making in diverse engineering domains.

Conclusion

We developed a hybrid computational methodology based on CFD and ML for simulation of magnetic nanoparticles motion in blood. The system has wide application in targeted drug delivery formulations. The modeling was initially performed via solution of governing equations via finite element method, and then the outputs were used for training some ML models. The ML models were tuned and optimized to get the best accuracy of fitting. For the models, i.e., DT, KNN, and GB, the following conclusions can be drawn:

DT model obtained an R2 of 0.90278, showing a good fit. It had a low RMSE of 1.6994E−03 and MAE of 1.13673E−03, indicating accurate predictions of the target variable U(m/s).

DT model obtained an R2 of 0.90278, showing a good fit. It had a low RMSE of 1.6994E−03 and MAE of 1.13673E−03, indicating accurate predictions of the target variable U(m/s).

The K-Nearest Neighbor model outperformed the Decision Tree model with an outstanding R2 of 0.99088, suggesting a strong agreement between the input variables and the output variable. Additionally, it had a lower RMSE of 5.3835E−04 and MAE of 2.02000E−04, indicating even more accurate predictions.

The K-Nearest Neighbor model outperformed the Decision Tree model with an outstanding R2 of 0.99088, suggesting a strong agreement between the input variables and the output variable. Additionally, it had a lower RMSE of 5.3835E−04 and MAE of 2.02000E−04, indicating even more accurate predictions.

The Gradient Boosting model achieved an R2 score of 0.96168, demonstrating a good fit to the data. It had an RMSE of 1.0864E−03 and MAE of 6.78800E−04, indicating relatively accurate predictions.

Overall, all three models exhibited strong performance in simulation of velocity based on the given inputs. However, the K-Nearest Neighbor model stood out as the best-performing model with the maximum R2 score and minimal RMSE and MAE. In summary, the utilization of the ROA in optimizing the K-Nearest Neighbor model demonstrates its potential for accurately estimating velocity using the given dataset.

While the present study provides valuable insights, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The CFD simulations were based on simplified vessel geometry and assumed steady flow conditions, which may not fully capture the complexity of real physiological environments. Additionally, only two spatial coordinates were considered as model inputs, whereas other biological factors—such as blood rheology, vessel elasticity, and cellular interactions—were not included. Finally, the lack of direct experimental or in vivo validation restricts the immediate clinical translation of the findings.

Looking forward, several avenues merit further exploration. Future studies could extend the framework to three-dimensional and pulsatile blood flow conditions, which would more closely replicate physiological environments. Incorporating additional biological factors—such as nanoparticle–cell interactions, blood viscosity variations, or immune response—could enhance the applicability of the model to real clinical scenarios. Moreover, testing other advanced ML methods, such as deep neural networks or ensemble hybrid models, may uncover further improvements in predictive performance. Finally, validation of the computational results through experimental or in vivo data would strengthen the clinical translation of this work for targeted drug delivery applications.
