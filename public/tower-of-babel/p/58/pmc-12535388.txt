P450 Enzyme LyoI Performs Hydro‐2,2′‐Bifuran Oxidation in the Polyether Ionophore Lysocellin
Authors: Michelle H Rasmussen, Søren L B Møller, Esben B Svenningsen, Thomas Tørring, Thomas B Poulsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolyether ionophores are potent antimicrobials, albeit also cytotoxic against mammalian cells. We have identified several polyether ionophores containing a common hydro‐2,2′‐bifuran‐2‐ol (hemiketal) moiety, which cannot be derived from the canonical biosynthetic steps observed for the compound class, suggesting an unusual oxidative transformation. To identify the responsible enzyme, we applied CRISPR–BEST to knock out genes in the lysocellin‐producing strain S. longwoodensis. This allowed us to propose the first annotation of the lysocellin biosynthetic gene cluster and identify the responsible P450 enzyme, LyoI, through reconstitution of the function in vivo. LyoI knockout provided access to the non‐oxidized precursor (pre‐lysocellin) which allowed both in vitro validation of the unusual direct hydro‐2,2′‐bifuran to hydro‐2,2′‐bifuran‐2‐ol oxidation and investigation of the impact on biological activity. Interestingly, absence of the LyoI‐mediated oxidation greatly reduced the biological potency of the compound. Closer investigation of the sequence revealed that LyoI lacks a key conserved acidic residue, which proved essential for the unusual oxidative function of the enzyme. Through a sequence similarity network of LyoI, we were able to identify a wide range of non‐canonical P450 enzymes, highlighting the possibilities of a biosynthesis‐focused approach to discovering novel enzymes.Keywords: Bioactivity, Biosynthesis, Chemoenzymatic synthesis, Natural products, P450 enzyme

Introduction

Enzymes catalyze a wide range of chemical transformations with exceptional regio‐ and stereoselectivities, including C─C bond formations, cyclizations, and C─H bond activations, which can allow for the introduction of chemical diversity into otherwise inaccessible positions in complex molecules.[ 1 ] In addition to the fascination of Natures impressive (bio)synthetic repertoire, the interest in applying enzymes in synthetic reactions, termed chemoenzymatic synthesis, has recently increased in both academia and industry driven by increasing demands for sustainable production processes.[ 2 , 3 , 4 ] The discovery of novel enzymes is therefore of substantial interest with one approach being through identification of an unusual in vivo enzymatic transformation and the subsequent identification of the responsible biosynthetic gene.[ 5 , 6 , 7 ] Specifically, cytochrome P450 enzymes, and the great variety of oxidative reactions they catalyze, has recently been garnering focus in the field.[ 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 ] Here, we highlight the identification of an intriguing enzymatic transformation by a P450 enzyme in the family of polyether ionophores, inspired by our previous work on this compound family.

Polyether ionophores are polyketide natural products, biosynthetically produced by members of the Streptomycetaceae family.[ 12 ] They are oxygen‐rich molecules, containing a variety of oxygenated functional groups, such as alcohols, ketones, and ether rings. The oxygen groups are distributed throughout the polyketide hydrocarbon backbone, which is generally polycyclic, saturated, and rich in stereocenters.[ 13 ] Polyether ionophores display broad biological activities, which are believed to primarily be driven by their ability to perturb ion gradients[ 14 ] although other mechanisms have also been reported.[ 15 , 16 ] They are particularly potent anti‐microbials,[ 12 ] and selected members are applied heavily in agriculture.[ 17 , 18 ] However, due to general uncertainty about their mammalian activities, polyether ionophores have not been applied in human clinical trials. We,[ 19 ] as well as others,[ 20 ] have recently studied the ability of polyether ionophores to target bacterial persisters and biofilms. Furthermore, we have applied synthesis to understand the fundamental impact of stereochemistry on polyether ionophore activity[ 21 ] and shown that semi‐synthetic analogues of natural polyether ionophores can possess enhanced antibacterial selectivity. The latter studies suggest that the therapeutic window of these compounds can be widened.[ 22 ] Consequently, we have a keen interest in methods allowing for further exploration of the chemical space around the natural polyether ionophores, with the identification of new biocatalytic methods for late‐stage modifications having particular appeal.

Through our work, we have observed that several family members, including the potent anti‐viral compound X‐206[ 23 ] and lysocellin,[ 24 ] share a special hydrofuran‐2‐ol (5‐membered hemiketal) group with an attached furan ring (Figure 1a). Unlike the related six‐membered hemiketal (a tetrahydro‐2H‐pyranol‐2‐ol derivative) also present in both X‐206 and lysocellin, this particular motif cannot readily result from the characteristic cyclization reactions that occur during the biosynthesis of these polyketides,[ 25 ] and we therefore speculated that its formation involved an unidentified oxidative tailoring enzyme operating directly on a hydro‐2,2′‐bifuran (HBF) substrate. The introduction of hydroxyl groups by tailoring enzymes is well precedented in the compound class,[ 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 ] but the specific scaffold (subsequently referred to as a hydro‐2,2′‐bifuran‐2‐ol, HBFO, moiety) has not been previously investigated. Our interest was further piqued by the fact that the putative hydro‐2,2′‐bifuran (HBF) oxidation substrate, which interestingly is present in other ionophores, is highly hindered and inaccessible for diversification by classic synthetic methods, suggesting that the responsible oxidative enzyme might be a valuable catalyst to functionalize complex molecules.

Natural carboxyl polyether ionophores containing the five‐membered hemiketal scaffold (HBFO) (highlighted in green). The underlined compound names indicate the drawn structure, while the names below indicate structural analogues.

Here, we utilize the ionophore lysocellin and its producing strain, S. longwoodensis DSM 41677,[ 30 ] as a model to study this unusual oxidative transformation. In practice, several biosynthetic genes were knocked out to define the biosynthetic gene cluster and to discover the oxidative enzyme, a cytochrome P450. This also allowed us to characterize the hydro‐2,2′‐bifuran precursor, C17‐des‐oxy‐lysocellin (henceforth: pre‐lysocellin), and to study the role of the oxidation in the biological activity of lysocellin. The enzyme was expressed heterologously to investigate its chemoenzymatic potential, showing a fast and highly specific tailoring enzyme. Investigations into the amino acid sequence of the P450 enzyme showed that the sequence is missing a key conserved residue in the highly conserved “acid–alcohol” pair, that typically is involved in the last step of the catalytic cycle in P450 enzymes.[ 31 ]

Results and Discussion

Identification and Bioinformatic Investigation of the Biosynthetic Gene Cluster of Lysocellin

To identify the biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) of lysocellin, we analyzed the full genome of S. longwoodensis using antiSMASH (version 7.0).[ 32 ] A total of 33 BGCs were identified, seven within the category of polyketide synthase (PKS) producers, one of these–a type 1 PKS BGC–was predicted to give a chemical structure in concordance with lysocellin. Within the BGC, we predicted one ketoreductase (KR) and two dehydratase (DH) domains as inactive due to mutations in an active site conserved amino acid sequence[ 33 , 34 ] (Figure S1). Combining the predicted chemical structure with knowledge of the inactive domains, the full structure of lysocellin–except for the oxidation–was biosynthetically accounted for. This allows us to propose the first annotation of the lysocellin BGC (Figures 2a and S2).

Each circle represents an enzymatic domain: AT (acyl transferase), T (thiolation), KS (ketosynthase), DH (dehydratase), ER (enoyl reductase), KR (ketoreductase), and TE (thioesterase). White circles indicate inactive domains. The stereochemistry of the lysocellin backbone is based on the stereochemistry of lysocellin.[ 35 ] b) Proposed tailoring reactions of lysocellin. The order by which the cyclization reactions occur is unknown. All tailoring reactions are portrayed to occur on the assembly line, though it is currently uncertain at which point the compound is released as the free acid.

Following assembly of the linear polyketide, we propose the cyclization of lysocellin to occur directly in the western part of the structure, forming the aforementioned six‐membered hemiketal, and through two epoxidations (by lyoK) followed by a cyclization cascade (by lyoL) in the eastern part of the structure (Figure 2b), as is commonly observed for the family of polyether ionophores.[ 25 ] We propose lysocellin to be released as the free acid as the last step of the biosynthesis, as observed for multiple carboxyl polyether ionophores.[ 27 , 28 , 36 , 37 ] Analysis of the BGC led us to identify two additional genes (lyoI and lyoJ) in proximity of the BGC (Figure S2), which were both predicted to encode cytochrome P450 enzymes and thereby being candidates for mediating the cryptic hydro‐2,2′‐bifuran (HBF) oxidation.

The P450 Enzyme LyoI is Involved in the Biosynthesis of Lysocellin

To probe the role of lyoI and lyoJ, we applied the Streptomyces‐specific method CRISPR–BEST[ 38 , 39 ] to achieve their individual knockouts. In addition, the core biosynthetic gene, lyoA, was inactivated to confirm that the correct BGC had been identified. In practice, single‐guide RNAs (sgRNAs) for the three genes were designed using CRISPy‐web,[ 40 ] separately bridged into the pCRISPR–cBEST plasmid and introduced into E. coli ET12567/pUZ8002. The plasmids were subsequently transferred from the E. coli strain to S. longwoodensis by interspecies conjugation. Positive knockout mutants were confirmed by sequencing.

Fermentation broths of the three knockout mutants, ΔlyoA, ΔlyoI, and ΔlyoJ, along with the wild type (wt) S. longwoodensis were investigated by HPLC‐MS for the production of secondary metabolites (Figure 3a). Knockout of the core biosynthetic gene, lyoA, resulted in a complete loss of lysocellin production, confirming that the correct BGC had been identified. Knock‐out of the P450 enzyme in closest proximity to the core BGC, lyoI, resulted in the production of a new species with a mass of 16 Da less than lysocellin–possibly corresponding to the loss of a single oxygen atom. The knockout of lyoJ did not directly affect lysocellin production, thereby excluding it from involvement with the BGC.

Fermentation results from wild type (wt) and knockout strains of S. longwoodensis. a) MS chromatograms from fermentations of S. longwoodensis wt, a strain with a knockout in a core biosynthetic gene (ΔlyoA), and two strains with knockouts in P450 enzymes (ΔlyoI and ΔlyoJ). b) Results of complementation of S. longwoodensis ΔlyoI with pGM1190_lyoI. For full dataset, see Figure S3. Two different HPLC‐MS methods were used for obtaining the data in a) and b).

To validate these results in vivo, we complemented S. longwoodensis ΔlyoI with the lyoI sequence inserted in the pGM1190 plasmid through interspecies conjugation. The complementation resulted in the reconstitution, albeit at lower production titers, of the wt lysocellin phenotype (Figure 3b), thereby confirming the role of the lyoI gene product's involvement in the biosynthesis of lysocellin.

Structural Investigation Pinpoints Oxidation of Pre‐Lysocellin

We initiated large‐scale fermentations to isolate both lysocellin (from S. longwoodensis) and the lysocellin analogue pre‐lysocellin (from S. longwoodensis ΔlyoI). The fermentations of both strains were performed in 4 L bioreactors, to ensure sufficient oxygen supply to the bacteria, crucial for successful productions. Successful fermentations were achieved by using a fed‐batch protocol and finishing the production 7 days after initiation of feeding. Lysocellin and pre‐lysocellin were both isolated from fermentation broths (4 L, 26 and 8 mg, respectively) using an altered version of the protocol used in the original isolation paper.[ 24 ]

Through this approach, lysocellin (1) (Figure 4a) was obtained as an amorphous white solid with a molecular formula of C34H60O10, as established by the positive HR‐ESI‐MS ion peak at m/z 646.4522 [M + NH4]+ (calculated 646.4525), indicating five double bond equivalents (DBEs). This is in accordance with the reported structure of the compound, which has been unambiguously assigned through X‐ray diffraction.[ 35 ] Other studies have reported NMR‐data for lysocellin but these differ with respect to overall conditions used (including field strength) and associated counter‐ions.[ 41 , 42 ] As we were aiming to conclusively identify the precise location of the LyoI‐mediated oxidation, we therefore conducted our own detailed NMR‐based structural assignment of both lysocellin and pre‐lysocellin (see supplementary information for detailed analysis, Tables S2, S3, Figures S12, S13).

NMR evaluation of lysocellin and pre‐lysocellin. a) Structures of lysocellin and pre‐lysocellin with numbered carbon atoms. HMBC and NOE correlations can be found in Figure S12 and S13. b) Key difference observed between 1H (top) and 13C (bottom) NMR spectra of lysocellin and pre‐lysocellin. c) Differences in chemical shifts in the 1H (top) and 13C (bottom) NMR spectra of lysocellin and pre‐lysocellin.

Pre‐lysocellin (2) (Figure 4a) was likewise obtained as an amorphous white solid, however exhibiting a molecular formula of C34H60O9, as determined by the positive HR‐ESI‐MS ion peak at m/z 635.4131 [M + Na]+ (calcd. 635.4130), also indicating five DBEs and showing the absence of an oxygen atom relative to lysocellin. Overall, 1 and 2 exhibited high spectroscopic similarities with a few key differences (Tables S2, S3). Notably, the absent oxygen atom in pre‐lysocellin evidently originated from a hemiketal (δC 108.1), which is immediately apparent by comparing the 13C NMR spectra (Figure 4b). In addition, pre‐lysocellin contains one more methine carbon with a corresponding signal doublet (δH 2.96) (Figure 4b) as opposed to lysocellin. Comparing the chemical shifts between the two compounds generally showed minor differences though with higher variations in the region around C17 (Figure 4c), as expected. The relative stereochemistry of C17 was based on NOE‐correlations from H17 to Me‐27, Me‐26, H22, H23 as well as absence of NOE‐correlations from H17 to H24, H25 and H18 (Figure S13). The data was further underscored by the observation of similar fragmentation patterns in lysocellin and pre‐lysocellin in HPLC‐MS/MS analysis (Figure S4). Viewed overall, our assignments clearly pinpointed a differential oxidation at C17 as being the key structural difference between lysocellin and pre‐lysocellin.

C17‐Oxidation has a Major Impact on Biological Activity

To understand the potential consequence of this unusual oxidation on biological activity, we evaluated the antibacterial efficacy of lysocellin and pre‐lysocellin against both Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative bacteria. Lysocellin displayed potent antibiotic activity in minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC, 1 µg mL−1) assays against Gram‐positive strains (S. aureus and B. cereus), in line with other natural polyether ionophores[ 19 ] (Figure 5a,b). In contrast, pre‐lysocellin had a drastic loss in potency (>32‐fold change).

To expand on the potency of lysocellin, we explored the compound's impact on biofilms of S. aureus (Figure 5b). Lysocellin was highly potent, both in regards to inhibition (0.5 µg mL−1) and eradication (64 µg mL−1), in line with the potency of other natural polyether ionophores, such as lasalocid and nanchangmycin.[ 19 ] Given pre‐lysocellin's low potency in the MIC assays, the compound was not tested against biofilms. Finally, we also evaluated the compounds’ activities in three mammalian cell lines. In line with the trend observed in the antimicrobial studies, lysocellin was a more potent inhibitor of cell viability than pre‐lysocellin. For a more nuanced picture of the activities of ionophores in mammalian cells,[ 22 ] we have previously applied the cell painting assay.[ 43 , 44 ] The assay detects morphological changes induced by bioactive compounds by staining with fluorophores, fluorescence microscopy, and image analysis, which results in a phenotypic fingerprint that represents the bioactivity of the compound. Applying lysocellin in this assay, we observed strong induction of a canonical “ionophore”‐profile, which is, for e.g., characterized by strongly increased MitoTracker staining (Figure 5c images). In comparison, pre‐lysocellin was much less active, but at 12.5 µM, >100‐fold higher than lysocellin, a weak perturbation of the mitochondria was also observed. The profile of lysocellin correlated strongly (∼0.8) to other ionophores such as salinomycin, X‐206, nanchangmycin, monensin, and narasin (Figure 5c heatmap). These data support the function of lysocellin as an ionophore in mammalian cells and the loss of ionophore activity of pre‐lysocellin. Viewed overall, these studies clearly highlight the significance of the hydrofuran‐2‐ol (five‐membered hemiketal) for the biological potency of lysocellin. In the reported crystal structure,[ 35 ] the hemiketal OH‐group is directly involved in ion‐binding which supports that its presence may play a stabilizing role. This is particularly interesting as the underlying hydrobifuran (HBF) motif is present in many other polyether ionophores which still possess potent antibiotic activity. LyoI thus appears to have been evolutionarily provided with a decisive role for triggering antibiotic potency in the specific context of lysocellin.

LyoI Generates Lysocellin from Pre‐Lysocellin In Vitro

We next pursued in vitro experiments to directly validate the activity of LyoI to perform oxidation of the hydrobifuran (HBF) motif to generate lysocellin from pre‐lysocellin. LyoI was expressed in E. coli to give a purified enzyme with the distinctive red color associated with cytochrome P450 enzymes. This was further underscored by observation of the characteristic switch in UV absorbance from 450 to 420 nm upon activation of the reduced enzyme with CO[ 45 ] (Figure S5). Having confirmed that the enzyme could undergo activation, we surveyed conditions for the oxidative chemoenzymatic assay, finally applying LyoI with ferredoxin/ferredoxin reductase (from S. oleracea), glucose‐6‐phosphate (G6P)/glucose‐6‐phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) and NADPH. The in vitro activation was successful, resulting in full conversion of pre‐lysocellin to lysocellin in <12 min (Figure 6a). Subsequent experiments revealed that conversion was also possible using putidaredoxin/putidaredoxin reductase as regeneration system.

Direct oxidation of pre‐lysocellin to lysocellin by LyoI. a) HPLC‐MS chromatograms (TIC) of the chemoenzymatic conversion of pre‐lysocellin to lysocellin using LyoI, ferredoxin/ferredoxin reductase, glucose‐6‐phosphate/glucose‐6‐phosphate dehydrogense and NADPH at different time points. b) Polyether ionophores containing unhydroxylated hydrobifuran moieties similar to the ones of pre‐lysocellin.

Having established a reliable assay, we sought to investigate the promiscuity of the enzyme. Toward this end, we tested the enzyme on other polyether ionophores containing unhydroxylated hydrobifuran (HBF) moieties similar to the one of pre‐lysocellin, specifically ionomycin, maduramycin, and monensin (Figure 6b). Interestingly, none of these polyether ionophores underwent oxidation (Figure S6), demonstrating that LyoI appears to be highly specific regarding its substrate acceptance.

LyoI is Missing a Key Amino Acid in a Conserved P450 Sequence

Investigating the P450 sequence conservation of LyoI, we identified a key amino acid change in the highly conserved “acid–alcohol” pair motif (Figure S7).[ 46 ] Instead of aspartic acid (D) or glutamic acid (E) as the acidic residue, LyoI contains the neutral amino acid isoleucine (I), I237. The alcohol–acid pair is involved in the last step of the catalytic cycle, a protonation step, which prepares the reactive P450 species.[ 31 ] Few bacterial enzymes have been identified where the alcohol,[ 10 , 47 , 48 , 49 ] the acid,[ 50 ] or both[ 51 ] is not conserved, and it is currently unknown whether this affects the mechanism of the enzyme. Previous studies generating mutations of the acidic residue have identified that the acidic residue is essential for the rate of NAD(P)H oxidation activity.[ 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 ] The rare lack of the feature is apparent when comparing LyoI to P450 enzymes introducing C─H oxidations in other polyether ionophores in the form of either secondary alcohols (SnlF, in salinomycin[ 29 ]) or a primary alcohols (MonD, in monensin,[ 26 ] NanP, in nanchangmycin,[ 27 ] and NigD, in nigericin[ 28 ]) (Figure 7a). To gauge the effect of the lack of the acidic residue, we expressed mutants of LyoI replacing isoleucine with either acidic residue (I237D/E). Both mutant enzymes could be activated with CO (Figure S5), but neither were able to transform pre‐lysocellin to lysocellin (Figure S8). The “lack” of the acidic residue therefore appears essential for the transformation, which can be underscored by the close proximity of I237 to the active site heme group (Figure 7b).

In the cases, where most enzymes of a specific cluster are found in proximity to a specific type of BGC, this has been noted next to the cluster number. The full SNN can be found in Figure S9.

We further investigated LyoI by generating a sequence similarity network (SSN) using the EFI‐Enzyme Similarity Tool (Figures 7c and S9).[ 57 ] Based on the amino acid sequence of LyoI, 1000 sequences were identified using BLAST and clustered. LyoI (in cluster LII) only clustered to three other sequences, all P450 enzymes found in other Streptomyces strains. Evaluating the genomic context of these enzymes, they are all found in proximity to BGCs, which we predict to produce compounds with polyketide backbones highly similar to the one of lysocellin (Figure S10). To the best of our knowledge, none of these predicted compounds have been isolated. Most enzymes of clusters I, IX, and X are also found in the proximity of predicted BGCs with IX and X being in proximity to PKS BGCs. Several members in cluster IX are found in known BGC associated with macrolides such as rosaramycin/rosamicin A[ 58 , 59 ] and tylosin,[ 60 ] where they oxidize the terminal methyl group of an ethyl‐substituent to generate an aldehyde. In contrast, the enzymes of cluster I are found next to non‐ribosomal peptide synthase (NRPS) BGCs, while the enzymes of cluster II–VIII generally are not found in proximity to any BGCs.

Investigating the acid–alcohol pair in the enzymes of the LyoI‐cluster, they all contain the same “AGHITT”‐sequence as found in LyoI. Further investigation of the acid–alcohol pair showed that in all clusters containing more than four nodes, 17 out of 60 clusters exclusively contained the “AGHITT”‐sequence, corresponding to 155 proteins. Five clusters, including the two largest clusters, contained enzymes with either “AGHITT” or “AGH‐V/L‐TT”, resulting in the observation of the specific “AGHITT”‐sequence in >200 proteins. In comparison, performing a similar SNN analysis of MonD (Figure S11), which contains the canonical acid–alcohol pair (Figure 7a), did not identify any enzymes with the “AGHITT”‐sequence–all contain the canonical “ET” acid–alcohol pair. Overall, this analysis shows that the “AGHITT‐motif” is not exclusive to LyoI and it highlights how LyoI in this case can be an effective hook to identify other enzymes with isoleucine–as well as a broader range of amino acids–replacing the acidic residue. The closer scrutiny of the activity of these enzymes will be of substantial interest for future investigations.

Conclusion

Here, we have proposed the notation of the lysocellin biosynthetic gene cluster–the first of any biosynthetic gene cluster in the lysocellin compound family–and the following cyclization reactions. We have identified a new cytochrome P450 enzyme (LyoI) responsible for C17‐hydro‐2,2′‐bifuran (HBF) oxidation in lysocellin, confirmed it in vivo, and demonstrated its critical importance for the biological (antibacterial) activity of lysocellin. The enzyme has been applied in a chemoenzymatic assay to show efficient and substrate‐specific oxidation of pre‐lysocellin in vitro. Furthermore, sequence and mutational investigations revealed LyoI to be missing a key conserved acidic residue, which not only proved essential for the function of the enzyme but also represents a larger P450‐subclass that may be involved in other unusual oxidative transformations. All in all, our study highlights the possibilities of taking a biosynthesis‐focused approach to discovering novel enzymes.
