Diagnostic value of integrated albumin-to-leukocyte ratio risk modeling stratification and machine learning approaches for prediction of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis in cirrhotic patients

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) remains a life-threatening complication of liver cirrhosis, requiring accurate and rapid prediction. Albumin and leukocyte count reflect systemic inflammation and liver dysfunction; combining them into an Albumin-to-Leukocyte Ratio (ALR) may enhance early risk stratification.AimTo evaluate the diagnostic value of the ALR for predicting SBP in patients with cirrhosis and develop an ALR-based risk score. We will compare its predictive accuracy with machine-learning models to establish a practical triage tool for SBP risk stratification.Patient and methodsThis cross-sectional study included 240 cirrhotic patients, categorized into SBP (n = 152) and non-SBP (n = 88). Demographic, clinical, hematological, and biochemical variables were analyzed. ALR was calculated, and its predictive accuracy was assessed using ROC curves. A risk score integrating ALR, hemoglobin, bilirubin, and Child-Pugh class was generated. Logistic regression identified independent predictors. Diagnostic performance of ALR was compared with decision tree and random forest models.ResultsSBP patients had significantly lower albumin, ALR, hemoglobin, and platelets, and higher leukocyte count, bilirubin, INR, and creatinine (all p ≤ 0.05). ALR demonstrated moderate diagnostic performance with fair discrimination; AUC of 0.707, achieving a sensitivity of 82% at a cutoff of ≤ 0.728. Multivariate analysis identified ALR (OR = 0.114, p = 0.001), hemoglobin (OR = 0.847, p = 0.024), bilirubin (OR = 1.319, p = 0.004), MELD score (OR = 0.998, p = 0.042) and Child-Pugh class C (OR = 4.86, p = 0.001) as independent predictors. The generated ALR risk score (0–7 points) categorized patients into low, intermediate, and high-risk groups with increasing diagnostic accuracy (AUC range 0.63–0.75). Machine-learning models outperformed ALR alone; random forest showed the highest discrimination (AUC = 0.901), followed by decision tree (AUC = 0.852).ConclusionALR is a simple and accessible biomarker with moderate accuracy for predicting SBP in cirrhotic patients and may serve as a practical bedside triage tool when integrated into a composite risk score. However, machine-learning models, particularly random forest, demonstrated superior predictive performance, suggesting that combining conventional biomarkers with advanced algorithms may enhance SBP risk stratification.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) is a common and severe bacterial infection in cirrhotic patients, accounting for 10–30% of all bacterial infections in hospitalized cirrhotic patients [1]. The outpatient prevalence of SBP is 1.5–3.5% and exceeds 10% in hospitalized patients [2–4]. SBP is associated with a poor prognosis, with mortality rates ranging from 20% to 30% despite improvements in management and prevention [5]. Furthermore, SBP negatively impacts the prognosis of cirrhosis patients, leading to more liver decompensation, associated sepsis, and ultimately multi-organ failure [6]. While various markers have been explored for diagnosing SBP [7–10], the gold standard method, as per international guidelines, is diagnostic paracentesis [11].

Patients with advanced cirrhosis experience a decrease in albumin production and impaired liver function, leading to a significant reduction in albumin levels. Research consistently demonstrates that low albumin levels are a reliable predictor of mortality in cirrhotic patients, highlighting its significance as a prognostic marker. Additionally, reduced albumin levels are associated with lower serum complement levels, potentially raising the risk of infections, especially spontaneous bacterial peritonitis [12, 13].

White blood cells (leukocytes) are vital for defending the body against bacterial infections. In individuals with liver cirrhosis, bacterial infections can cause an increase in white blood cell count, particularly in those with low counts due to hypersplenism [14, 15].Elevated white blood cell counts have been demonstrated to be a predictive factor for the development of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) and its associated mortality. It is a valuable diagnostic marker for SBP in suspected patients [16, 17].

Prior researcheshave highlighted albumin and leucocyte counts as predictors of SBP [7, 9, 10]. The albumin-to-leucocyte ratio (ALR) is a valuable indicator that represents a composite inflammatory–hepatic dysfunction index. The ALR reflects both systemic inflammation and the liver’s ability to produce albumin. Elevated leukocyte counts indicate increased inflammation, while low albumin levels can indicate malnutrition or liver dysfunction, both of which contribute to bacterial translocation and infection risk. The ALR provides a single value that combines leukocyte count (inflammatory response indicator) with albumin levels (nutritional and hepatic status marker) [18, 19].

Machine learning (ML) such as Decision Tree and Random Forest integrating multiple data can achieve higher diagnostic accuracy and refine diagnosis when laboratory and clinical data are available. A previous study highlights the potential of ML models, such as Decision Tree and Random Forest, to enhance the accuracy of SBP diagnosis in cirrhotic patients [20, 21].

This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of ALR for predicting SBP in patients with cirrhosis and to develop an ALR-based risk stratification score. Additionally, machine-learning algorithms, including decision tree and random forest models, were applied to establish an optimized triage tool for SBP risk assessment.

Patients and methods

In this cross-sectional study, we included 240 patients with liver cirrhosis and ascites who were referred to the Tropical and Internal Medicine Mansoura University Hospital and the Egyptian Liver Research Institute and Hospital (ELRIAH) in Egypt betweenApril 2024 and august 2025. All patients underwent a comprehensive medical history, physical examination, abdominal ultrasonography, and triphasic CT scans when necessary.

Inclusion criteria

Adults ≥ 18 years, Diagnosed liver cirrhosis based on clinical, laboratory, imaging, or endoscopic criteria, Presence of ascites confirmed by ultrasound, Underwent diagnostic paracentesis.

Exclusion criteria

Secondary peritonitis, recent antibiotic use (within 2 weeks), active gastrointestinal bleeding, malignancy-related ascites, incomplete laboratory data, distinct infections affecting white blood cell levels (e.g., skin or lung infections), patients with heart or renal failure, neoplastic, hematological, or autoimmune disorders, unrelated infections affecting white blood cell levels (e.g., skin or lung infections).

Sampling

Ascitic fluid samples (15 ml) were aspirated at the bedside using paracentesis. Ten ml was inoculated in bedside aerobic and anaerobic blood culture bottles, while the remaining amount was sent for biochemical and cytological examination in tubes containing EDTA and analyzed within 3 h of aspiration. For microbiological examination, inoculated blood culture bottles were incubated for 3 days at 37 °C with daily subculture on blood, MacConkey, and chocolate agars. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing and bacterial identification were conducted following standard procedures.

Sample size calculation

The study used a cross-sectional design and calculated the sample size using G*Power (version 3.1) for a two-sample comparison of means with a significance level of α = 0.05 and statistical power of 0.80. Assuming a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5) for the primary outcome (albumin-to-leukocyte ratio, ALR) based on previous research, the minimum required sample size was 210 participants (approximately 105 per group). To account for missing data and enhance precision for subgroup and multivariable analyses, the target sample size was increased to 240 patients. The final sample included 152 patients with SBP and 88 patients without SBP, recruited consecutively as part of the cross-sectional sampling frame. This sample size provided sufficient power (approximately 80–90%) to detect clinically significant differences in ALR and related laboratory parameters between the groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 and Python-based libraries (Scikit-Learn) for machine learning modeling. Data normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± SD and compared using the t-test, while non-normal variables were shown as median (IQR) and analyzed with the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (%) and compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Univariate logistic regression identified potential predictors of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP), with significant variables (p ≤ 0.05) included in a multivariate logistic regression model using the enter method to determine independent predictors (adjusted odds ratios [AOR] with 95% confidence intervals [CI] .For diagnostic evaluation of the albumin-to-leukocyte ratio (ALR), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis calculated the area under the curve (AUC), optimal cutoff, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and overall accuracy.

In the machine-learning workflow, Decision Tree and Random Forest algorithms were developed using clinical and biochemical predictors (albumin, leukocyte count, hemoglobin, bilirubin, creatinine, INR, platelets, Child–Pugh class, and MELD score). The dataset was split into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets with stratified sampling. Continuous variables were standardized, and categorical variables were encoded before model training. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using grid search with 5-fold cross-validation. The Decision Tree model used the CART algorithm, while the Random Forest model was an ensemble of 500 bootstrapped decision trees with feature-bagging. Model performance was evaluated on the test set, with metrics including sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, confusion matrix, and AUC from ROC curves. Feature-importance analysis identified ALR, bilirubin, hemoglobin, and Child-Pugh class as key predictors. Significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 240 cirrhotic patients with ascites were included, of whom 152 (63.3%) had spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) and 88 (36.7%) had non-SBP ascites. Table 1 summarizes the demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics of both groups. Age and sex distribution did not differ significantly between SBP and non-SBP patients. Patients with SBP demonstrated significantly elevated leukocyte counts and markedly reduced serum albumin levels, resulting in substantially lower albumin-to-leukocyte ratios (ALR) compared with non-SBP patients (all p < 0.001). Hepatic injury markers, including bilirubin, AST, and ALT, were significantly higher in the SBP group. Serum creatinine and INR were also significantly worse among SBP patients. Hematological parameters showed lower hemoglobin and platelet counts in SBP cases compared with non-SBP individuals. A significantly higher proportion of SBP patients were categorized as Child–Pugh class C and had higher MELD scores.

The diagnostic validity of ALR is presented in Table 3. ALR showed fair discriminatory ability, with moderate AUC of 0.707 (95% CI: 0.634–0.781; p < 0.001). The albumin-to-leukocyte ratio scoring system (ALRc score)

A composite ALRc risk stratification score was developed as practical risk stratification tool, incorporating independent predictors identified in the multivariate model (Table 4). The final model included four significant variables: ALR, hemoglobin concentration, total bilirubin level, and Child–Pugh class. ALR was categorized based on its ROC-derived cutoff (≤ 0.728), hemoglobin < 10 g/dL, bilirubin ≥ 1.5 mg/dL, and Child–Pugh class as C versus B. The ALRc score combines markers of inflammation, liver function, hematologic status, and cirrhosis severity for SBP risk stratification. Each predictor was assigned a weighted point value proportional to its β coefficient in the multivariate regression model. ALR had the highest weight (0–2 points), followed by Child–Pugh class (0–2 points), bilirubin (0–2 points), and hemoglobin (0–1 point). The total score ranged from 0 to 7 points, categorizing patients into low risk (0–2 points), intermediate risk (3–4 points), and high risk (4 points). ALR was stratified into three categories: > 0.728 (0 points), 0.51–0.728 (1 point), and ≤ 0.50 (2 points). Hemoglobin < 10 g/dL contributed 1 point, while ≥ 10 g/dL contributed 0 points. Total bilirubin ≥ 1.5 mg/dL was assigned 2 points, and < 1.5 mg/dL contributed 0 points. Child–Pugh Class C patients received 2 points, while Class B patients received 0 points.

Performance of ALRc risk categories

Table 5 summarizes the simplified ALRc score (0–7 points), which stratifies cirrhotic patients with ascites into progressively increasing SBP risk categories using four routine parameters;: ALR, hemoglobin, total bilirubin, and Child–Pugh class.

The low-risk group (0–1 points; ALR > 0.728) showed low sensitivity (18%) but high specificity (82%) with an AUC of 0.63, indicating limited ability to detect SBP but good reliability in excluding infection. Discussion

Liver cirrhosis is a significant global health issue, especially in endemic areas where chronic hepatitis B and hepatitis C are prevalent, largely driven by past exposure to unsafe medical practices and unscreened blood transfusions [22, 23]. Advanced cirrhosis can lead to portal hypertension, ascites, acute-on-chronic liver failure, multi-organ dysfunction, adverse pregnancy outcomes and risk of bacterial infections specially spontaneous bacterial peritonitis that can worsen the disease and lead to higher mortality rates [24–26].

Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis remains a major cause of morbidity, mortality, and hospitalization among patients with liver cirrhosis. Given its often subtle presentation and rapid progression, early identification of at-risk patients is critical to optimize timely diagnostic and therapeutic interventions. Consistent with prior evidence, SBP occurred predominantly in individuals with advanced liver disease, worse inflammatory profiles, and impaired hepatic synthetic function [27–28].

In the present study, we investigated the ALR as a simple, readily available, and cost-effective biomarker for SBP risk stratification. Furthermore, we compared its predictive performance with established clinical variables and developed an integrated ALRc score, designed to support clinical decision-making using machine learning models. Importantly, this tool is intended to facilitate early triage and risk assessment within routine clinical workflows, serving as an adjunct and must not replace guideline-recommended diagnostic paracentesis.

Multivariate analysis identified five independent predictors of SBP: reduced ALR, low hemoglobin, elevated bilirubin, MELD score, and Child–Pugh class C. These findings consistent with previous studies underscore the multifactorial nature of SBP development, integrating systemic inflammation, hepatic synthetic dysfunction, and global disease severity [29–31].

In this study, ALR emerged as an independent predictor of SBP with fair discriminatory ability (AUC 0.707). This observation aligns with previous reports indicating that composite inflammatory ratios incorporating albumin and leukocyte parameters may outperform isolated markers in cirrhosis [32, 33]. Hypoalbuminemia reflects impaired hepatic reserve and reduced immunomodulatory capacity, while leukocytosis represents the host inflammatory response [14, 19]. By integrating these two components, ALR captures both inflammatory burden and hepatic dysfunction-two central drivers of SBP pathogenesis. Additionally, in terms of assessing disease severity, the MELD and Child-Pugh scores have distinct yet complementary clinical roles. The MELD score is mainly utilized to forecast short-term mortality and determine the priority for liver transplantation. On the other hand, the Child-Pugh score is still useful for bedside evaluation, categorizing decompensation, and identifying infection-related complications like SBP [31].

Although ALR demonstrated acceptable discriminatory performance (AUC 0.707), its diagnostic performance should be interpreted with caution. The relatively low specificity of ALR (approximately 52%) warrants careful clinical interpretation, as false-positive classification may result in an increased number of diagnostic paracenteses. However, given the high morbidity and mortality associated with delayed diagnosis of SBP [5], a strategy favoring sensitivity may be clinically acceptable. However, ALR should not be used as a confirmatory diagnostic test nor to safely exclude SBP or defer paracentesis in patients with suspected infection.

To enhance clinical applicability, we constructed the ALRc score by integrating ALR with bilirubin, hemoglobin, and Child–Pugh class. The inclusion of bilirubin and Child–Pugh class is well supported previous studies linking advanced hepatic dysfunction with SBP occurrence and adverse outcomes [34–36]. Anemia in cirrhosis is multifactorial and may result from portal hypertension–related hypersplenism, chronic inflammation, nutritional deficiencies, or bone marrow suppression. In this context, hemoglobin should be interpreted as a global marker of disease severity rather than a specific mechanistic predictor of SBP, and future studies should explore anemia subtypes in greater detail [37].

The resulting ALRc score demonstrated a stepwise increase in AUC (from 0.63 → 0.71 → 0.75) and accuracy (61% → 65% → 71%) across low, intermediate, and high-risk categories, supporting its ability to meaningfully stratify SBP probability. Clinically, the high-risk category achieved 82% sensitivity, reinforcing its potential role in prioritizing urgent paracentesis and empiric therapy. This finding is clinically meaningful, as delayed diagnosis remains an important contributor to SBP-associated morbidity and mortality [38, 39]. The intermediate-risk category provided moderate discriminative power with overall accuracy equal to 65%. This group shows a moderate balance between sensitivity and specificity, indicating fair predictive ability for SBP. These patients fall into a “grey-zone” where the likelihood of SBP is clinically relevant. Therefore, close clinical monitoring is recommended as this group cannot be definitively classified as low or high risk. Patients classified as low risk (0–1 points) demonstrated low sensitivity (18%) but high specificity (82%), with an AUC of 0.63 and overall accuracy of 61%. While the model has limited ability to detect SBP in this group, its high specificity supports reliable exclusion of infection. Accordingly, low-risk patients are unlikely to have SBP and may be safely monitored, unless clinical symptoms strongly indicate infection.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that the ALR-based risk stratification model provides clinically meaningful discrimination and effectively categorizes patients according to their likelihood of developing SBP. Importantly, the ALRc score is intended as a triage-support tool to complement, rather than replace diagnostic paracentesis.

The foundation ALR score on objective and routinely available laboratory parameters enhances its generalizability across diverse clinical settings and underscores its potential as a rapid, noninvasive tool for SBP risk stratification. In contrast to earlier regression-based models, such as the Mansoura scoring system [9], the ALRc score consolidates inflammatory and liver function markers into a streamlined framework, with variable weighting derived from multivariate analysis within the same cohort.

When compared with advanced ML models, the predictive performance of ALR and ALRc was inferior. The Random Forest model achieved excellent discrimination (AUC 0.901), followed by the Decision Tree model (AUC 0.852), substantially outperforming ALR alone. These findings are consistent with recent studies demonstrating superior SBP prediction using ML algorithms that integrate multidimensional laboratory and clinical data [20, 21, 40]. Feature-importance analysis in our Random Forest model highlighted albumin, leukocyte count, bilirubin, hemoglobin, creatinine, INR, Child–Pugh class, and MELD score as key contributors, reflecting the interplay between inflammation, hepatic dysfunction, renal impairment, and systemic decompensation in SBP pathophysiology.

Regardless of the superior discrimination achieved by ML models, practical considerations remain important. Machine-learning approaches necessitate computational infrastructure, standardized preprocessing, and external validation before widespread implementation. In contrast, the ALRc score is simple, transparent, and easily applicable at the bedside using universally available laboratory data. Therefore, the two approaches should be viewed as complementary; ALRc as a practical triage tool in routine or resource-limited settings, and ML models as high-precision decision-support systems in centers with digital infrastructure.

The clinical implications of this study are twofold. Firstly, the ALRc score is proposed strictly as a risk stratification and triage tool, not as a replacement for diagnostic paracentesis. Its intended role is to prioritize early paracentesis and prompt empiric management in high-risk patients, while emphasizing that low-risk classification does not justify deferring paracentesis. Secondly, the ALR score exhibited excellent diagnostic accuracy in machine learning for SBP, with an AUC of 0.90, surpassing many commonly used routine laboratory markers. A high AUC suggests that ALR is highly effective in distinguishing between patients with and without SBP. This level of accuracy positions ALR as reasonable triage tool, particularly in emergency or resource-limited settings. The outstanding predictive performance of machine learning models underscores the potential for future integration of artificial intelligence into SBP prediction frameworks, potentially enhancing early detection and therapeutic decision-making.

Study limitations should be acknowledged. Given that the study population was derived from a single geographic region with a predominance of HCV-related cirrhosis [41], external validation in multicenter and etiologically diverse cohorts (e.g., HBV-related or non-viral cirrhosis) plus clinical symptoms and dynamic bedside parameters is required before broad generalization of the ALRc score and machine-learning models. Because this study employed a cross-sectional design, temporal and causal relationships between ALR/ALRc and SBP onset cannot be established. So that, prospective longitudinal studies evaluating dynamic changes in ALR or ALRc over time may provide greater insight into early SBP, additionally future multicenter studies incorporating decision curve analysis and cost-effectiveness evaluation are needed to better quantify net clinical benefit. We acknowledge our study may partially reflect inflammatory neutrocytosis rather than microbiologically confirmed infection alone. Subgroup analyses were limited by sample size and should be explored in future studies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that ALR is an independent and accessible biomarker with moderate diagnostic performance for SBP risk assessment. The ALRc score improves stratification using routine laboratory parameters and may facilitate early triage and prioritization of diagnostic paracentesis. However, ML-based models, particularly Random Forest, provide superior predictive accuracy and highlight the potential role of artificial intelligence in refining SBP risk prediction frameworks; however, external validation is required before clinical implementation.
