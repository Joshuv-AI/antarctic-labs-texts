A comparative evaluation of the GINI index and the modified Glasgow prognostic score in predicting postoperative sarcopenia in rectal cancer patients undergoing NACRT
Authors: Mehmet Kemal Yener, Hüseyin Kılavuz, Burak Bilir, Ayşe Gökçe, Bilir Yıldız, Ali Erol, Alper Sözütek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePostoperative sarcopenia following neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy for locally advanced rectal cancer negatively affects recovery and oncological outcomes. Both the Global Immune Nutrition and Inflammation Index (GINI) and the modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS) are derived from routine laboratory data. There are no studies comparing the performance of these scores and indices in predicting sarcopenia in patients with CT-confirmed sarcopenia. The aim of this study is to determine which of these two indices is a more specific predictor of postoperative sarcopenia in patients who underwent surgery after NACRT in this patient population.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study (January 2019–December 2025) included 134 adults with locally advanced rectal adenocarcinoma who underwent total mesorectal excision after completing standard 5-fluorouracil/leucovorin/oxaliplatin-based neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (NACRT). GINI and mGPS values were calculated from fasting blood samples collected one month after NACRT. Postoperative sarcopenia was defined using gender-specific skeletal muscle index (SMI) cutoff values at the level of the third lumbar vertebra, as determined by CT scans performed three to six months after surgery.ResultsThe prevalence of postoperative sarcopenia was 30.6% (41/134). Sarcopenic patients had lower albumin levels (35.76 vs. 38.07 g/L; p = 0.046) and higher GINI values (median 146.70 vs. 68.39; p = 0.018); there was no difference between mGPS categories (p = 0.558). The GINI index yielded an AUC value of 0.629 (95% CI 0.541–0.711; sensitivity 48.78%, specificity 79.57% when the cutoff value was > 156.25; Nagelkerke R² = 0.140), whereas mGPS showed no discriminatory power (AUC 0.551; p = 0.307). In the adjusted analysis, a high GINI value independently predicted sarcopenia (aOR 4.93, 95% CI 1.72–14.13; p = 0.003).ConclusionIn patients with locally advanced rectal cancer, a high GINI value after NACRT was independently associated with sarcopenia assessed by postoperative CT, whereas no such association was found with mGPS. Moderate discriminatory performance and a wide 95% confidence interval indicate that the GINI, in its current form, is insufficient as a standalone clinical decision-making tool; therefore, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and must be supported by a prospective, multicenter validation study before being implemented in clinical practice.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is the third most common malignancy worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths [1]. Locally advanced rectal cancer is traditionally treated with perioperative chemotherapy—with or without radiation therapy—and total mesorectal excision [2]. Current consensus defines sarcopenia as a multidimensional construct encompassing loss of skeletal muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical performance, and sarcopenia is increasingly recognized as a clinically significant predictor of perioperative and oncological outcomes [3, 4].

Sarcopenia assessed by CT has been reported in 34–59% of patients with resectable rectal cancer and in 16–60% of patients who completed neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy [5–7]. In this population, preoperative sarcopenia has been associated with shorter overall and recurrence-free survival times and higher rates of postoperative complications, whereas sarcopenia occurring during or after NACRT has been linked to lower rates of pathological complete response and treatment-related toxicities such as thrombocytopenia and cystitis [8, 9]. Recent multicenter data also demonstrate that physiological reserve phenotypes—which combine frailty status, handgrip strength, and dynamic performance—classify perioperative risk more accurately than chronological age alone, underscoring the importance of comprehensive biological frailty assessment in surgical patients [10–14].

The Global Immune Nutrition and Inflammation Index is a more comprehensive composite index that combines albumin, C-reactive protein, and lymphocyte, neutrophil, and platelet counts, thereby simultaneously identifying chronic inflammation, malnutrition, and immune dysfunction [15, 16]. In contrast, the modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS) reflects a more limited inflammation-nutrition status derived solely from CRP and albumin levels [17–19]. Both indices have demonstrated prognostic value in cancer populations, and the mGPS, in particular, has shown a close association with sarcopenia in gastrointestinal cancer studies [19, 20]. However, to date, no study has directly compared the discriminatory performance of GINI and mGPS in predicting postoperative sarcopenia following NACRT in patients with locally advanced rectal cancer. This study fills this gap by examining, within a single cohort, which index (if any) provides a more specific prediction of sarcopenia—as assessed by postoperative CT—in this setting.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective, single-center cohort study was conducted at Bursa High Specialization Training and Research Hospital following approval by the institutional ethics committee. Adults (≥ 18 years of age) with histologically confirmed locally advanced rectal adenocarcinoma who had completed treatment consisting of 50 Gy of pelvic radiation therapy in 25 fractions followed by chemotherapy with 5-fluorouracil, leucovorin, and oxaliplatin, and who subsequently underwent total mesorectal excision between January 2019 and December 2025, were identified from hospital records. Exclusion criteria included the following: (i) histologically confirmed distant metastasis (M1) [8]; (ii) postoperative R1 or R2 resection margins; (iii) concurrent malignancy or metachronous malignancy within the previous five years; (iv) absence of an evaluable postoperative contrast-enhanced CT image covering the third lumbar vertebra (L3) region [9]; (v) Lack of one-month post-NACRT laboratory data (albumin, C-reactive protein, lymphocyte, neutrophil, or platelet counts) required for the calculation of the Global Immune Nutrition and Inflammation Index and the modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS); (vi) Lack of postoperative follow-up; and (vii) CT artifacts that interfere with segmentation.

Sarcopenia assessment and CT image analysis

The primary outcome was sarcopenia, as assessed by postoperative CT at the L3 vertebral level (Fig. 2). The gender-specific SMI cutoff values—<39.8 cm²/m² for men and < 28.4 cm²/m² for women—were derived from a healthy young Korean reference population (T-score ≤ − 2.0) [21]; normative threshold values specific to the Turkish population are currently unavailable and are considered one of the limitations of this study [8, 9]. Although current consensus frameworks—namely, the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older Adults and the Asian Sarcopenia Working Group—define sarcopenia as a multidimensional construct combining muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical performance, only the muscle mass dimension was assessed in this analysis. Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics v26.0. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate; effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d (thresholds 0.2/0.5/0.8) for parametric comparisons and as the ordinal-biserial correlation r = Z/√N (threshold values 0.1/0.3/0.5) ; categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. The discriminatory performance of the GINI index for postoperative sarcopenia was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, and the optimal cutoff value (> 156.25) was determined by maximizing the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). Since this cutoff value was derived and evaluated within the same sample, the corresponding sensitivity (48.78%) and specificity (79.57%) values contain some optimism bias; therefore, the cutoff value is exploratory in nature and is not intended for immediate clinical application (Table ).

The sample size was determined using a pragmatic approach based on an appropriate retrospective cohort rather than a predefined power calculation. Patients with missing laboratory or imaging data were excluded from the study according to predefined criteria, and a complete-case analysis was performed without using multiple imputation. Multivariate binary logistic regression included four predefined covariates (age, TNM stage, mGPS, GINI) against 41 outcome events; this meets the traditional minimum EPV ≥ 10 threshold, with an event rate of approximately 10.25 per variable. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and additional goodness-of-fit was characterized by Nagelkerke R². A backward elimination sensitivity analysis (retention threshold p < 0.10) yielded qualitatively consistent coefficient estimates. Odds ratios were reported as crude (cOR) and adjusted (aOR) values with 95% confidence intervals; statistical significance was set at a two-sided p < 0.05.

Results

The prevalence of postoperative sarcopenia in the study cohort was determined to be 30.6% (n = 41). There were no statistically significant differences between the sarcopenia and non-sarcopenia groups in terms of age (67.37 ± 10.42 vs. 64.01 ± 12.00 years, p = 0.123), gender distribution (p = 0.559), tumor site (p = 0.668), TNM stage (p = 0.435), or modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS) categories (p = 0.558). Muscle measurements

Skeletal muscle area was statistically significantly lower in patients with sarcopenia compared to those without sarcopenia (median: 90.0 [69.0–103.0] vs. 120.0 [104.5–139.5] cm²; p < 0.001); which corresponds to a large effect size (r = 0.58). (Table 3)

The GINI index demonstrated statistically significant discriminatory ability with an area under the curve (AUC) value of 0.629 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.541–0.711; p = 0.024; Fig. 4). The optimal cutoff value, determined using the Youden index, was found to be > 156.25, yielding a sensitivity of 48.78% and a specificity of 79.57%. At this threshold, the positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) were determined to be 51.3% and 77.9%, respectively. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of preoperative sarcopenia, including age, TNM stage, mGPS, and the GINI index.

In the univariate analysis, a high GINI index (> 156.25) showed a statistically significant association with an increased likelihood of sarcopenia (odds ratio [OR]: 3.71, 95% [CI]: 1.68–8.20; p = 0.001). In this study, age (p = 0.125), TNM stage (p = 0.435), and mGPS (p = 0.365) did not show a significant association with sarcopenia.

In the multivariate model, after adjusting for potential confounding factors, the GINI index remained a significant independent predictor of sarcopenia (adjusted OR: 4.93, 95% CI: 1.72–14.13; p = 0.003). No significant association was observed with age (p = 0.261), TNM stage (p = 0.433), or mGPS categories (p = 0.386). The overall model was statistically significant (omnibus test, p = 0.007) and demonstrated good calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow test, p = 0.628); the Nagelkerke R² value was 0.140. (Table 5)

Discussion

Sarcopenia is increasingly recognized as a clinically significant predictor of treatment tolerance, postoperative recovery, and long-term oncological outcomes in rectal cancer patients receiving neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy [6, 9]. In this patient population, the prevalence of clinically detectable muscle loss following multimodal therapy has been reported to range from 16% to 60%, depending on the imaging modality, timing of assessment, and reference thresholds applied [7, 9, 23].In this context, the ability to identify patients at high biological risk for low muscle mass using routinely available laboratory parameters could meaningfully complement current risk classification methods and guide preoperative rehabilitation strategies.

In this study, two indices derived from inflammation and nutrition—Global Immunity, Nutrition and Inflammation Index, and the Modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS)—calculated one month after completion of the NACRT—were examined to determine whether they were independently associated with sarcopenia assessed by postoperative CT scans performed 3–6 months after surgery.

One month after completion of NACRT, a high GINI value was found to be independently associated with sarcopenia assessed by postoperative CT (aOR 4.93, 95% CI 1.72–14.13; p = 0.003), whereas no such association was detected with mGPS (AUC 0.551, p = 0.307). The GINI had a modest AUC value of 0.629 (sensitivity 48.78%, specificity 79.57% at a cutoff value > 156.25); the Nagelkerke R² value of 0.140 indicates a significant amount of unexplained variance. The findings are hypothesis-generating in nature and insufficient for immediate clinical application [5, 6, 8, 9].

The strengths of this study include a homogeneous, single-center cohort; a standardized NACRT protocol; surgeries performed by a single experienced colorectal team; and the application of a validated CT-based body composition methodology [24–27].

Limitations include the absence of matched pre- and post-operative SMI measurements; this prevents differentiation between pre-existing and newly developed sarcopenia and limits causal inferences regarding treatment-related muscle loss [8, 9]; although sarcopenia is multidimensional, the assessment is limited to muscle mass alone [3]; the application of Asian reference threshold values [21] due to the lack of Turkish normative data; unmeasured confounding factors [5, 9]; derivation of the GINI threshold value from the same cohort (optimism bias); CT segmentation performed by a single observer without an assessment of reproducibility; variability in imaging at 3–6 months postoperatively; and the absence of an association with chronological age—all of which should be interpreted with caution; as recent multicenter data indicate that integrating frailty and grip strength into physiological reserve phenotypes for perioperative risk classification performs better than using age alone [10–14].

Other inflammation-derived indices have also been investigated in the context of colorectal cancer and sarcopenia. The Prognostic Nutrition Index (PNI; serum albumin + 5 × lymphocyte count) overlaps with both the GINI and mGPS indices in terms of its components; however, it does not include an explicit marker of inflammation, such as C-reactive protein. The Neutrophil-Lymphocyte Ratio and the Platelet-Lymphocyte Ratio have shown inconsistent associations with sarcopenia in heterogeneous cancer cohorts [22]. The Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII; neutrophil × platelet / lymphocyte) overlaps with the platelet and neutrophil components of GINI. Whether the improved classification performance provided by GINI compared to these simpler indices justifies the additional computational complexity is a question of comparative effectiveness that the current retrospective single-cohort design cannot resolve. A prospective comparative evaluation of GINI versus PNI, NLR, PLR, and SII—ideally combined with matched functional assessment—is a natural next step required by the current findings.”

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first direct comparison between GINI and mGPS in this context; future prospective multicenter studies incorporating matched CT scans, functional assessment, and frailty phenotyping are needed prior to the evaluation of clinical endpoints.

Conclusion

In this cohort of 134 patients with locally advanced rectal cancer who underwent NACRT followed by total mesorectal excision, a high Global Immune Nutrition and Inflammation Index one month after completion of NACRT was independently associated with sarcopenia as assessed by postoperative CT, whereas no such association was found with the modified Glasgow Prognostic Score. The GINI index demonstrated modest discriminatory power (AUC 0.629; sensitivity 48.78%, specificity 79.57%) and explained only 14% of the variance in outcomes ; this indicates that, in its current form, this index is insufficient as a standalone clinical decision-making tool. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution and considered hypothesis-generating.

Before implementation into routine clinical practice, prospective, multicenter validation studies are needed that include direct comparisons of skeletal muscle measurements obtained from matched pre- and post-operative CT scans with multidimensional sarcopenia assessment (mass + strength + performance), frailty phenotyping, and other inflammation-based indices.
