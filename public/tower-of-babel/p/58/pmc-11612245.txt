Histopathological and immunohistochemical examination of the brains of rabid dogs in the Philippines
Authors: Boonkanit MARKBORDEE, Alpha Grace B CABIC, Nuttipa IAMOHBHARS, Nozomi SHIWA-SUDO, Kazunori KIMITSUKI, Mark Joseph M ESPINO, Leilanie B NACION, Daria Llenarresas MANALO, Satoshi INOUE, Chun-Ho PARK
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Dogs are the primary transmitters of the rabies virus in the Philippines; however, to the best of our knowledge, no published studies have examined its detailed neuropathology. The present study analyzed the neuropathology in the cerebrum, hippocampus, thalamus, and brainstem of 70 rabid dogs with confirmed rabies infection in the Philippines. Histopathologically, inclusion bodies (Negri bodies) were detected in the hippocampus (87.14%), cerebrum (70%), and thalamus (2.86%) of the dogs. The inclusion bodies in the cytoplasm of the hippocampal and cerebral cortical pyramidal cells were large and round to oval in shape. Whereas the inclusion bodies in the neurons of the thalamus and brainstem were small, fine, and granular. In contrast to the high prevalence of inclusion bodies in the hippocampus and cerebrum, perivascular cuffing and glial nodules were more prominent in the thalamus and brainstem. Immunohistochemically using the anti-phosphoprotein (anti-P), the sensitivity of viral antigen detection was 100% in the hippocampus, thalamus, and brainstem and 97.14% in the cerebrum. Our findings confirmed that observing the inclusion bodies in the hippocampus and cerebral cortex by histopathology could facilitate rabies diagnosis in the dogs in the Philippines, and furthermore, using immunohistochemistry on the brainstem could also be useful to detect rabies virus antigens with high sensitivity.

Rabies is a zoonotic disease caused by a neurotrophic virus that infects all mammalian species. This disease is almost 100% fatal once clinical symptoms appear in affected humans and animals. The virus is transmitted via the bite or scratch of an infected animal; further, infection can occur through the direct contact of virus-laden saliva with mucosa. Dogs are the principal transmitters of rabies to humans, accounting for approximately 99% of all cases [40]. Globally, canine rabies is estimated to cause 59,000 human deaths annually, primarily in Africa (36.4%) and Asia (59.6%) [15].

The most characteristic histopathological findings of the central nervous system (CNS) in patients and animals with rabies are intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies (Negri bodies) and non-suppurative encephalitis. Despite exhibiting severe clinical symptoms, the histopathological changes observed in the CNS are relatively mild or absent. Moreover, Negri bodies, which are unique to rabies, exhibit marked variations of pleomorphism in both size (1–27 µm) and shape [21]. Although Negri bodies can be found in virtually any neuronal population within the CNS or peripheral nerve ganglia, their pathogenic significance remains unclear, especially since they are rarely seen in infections with fixed virus strains. Before the advent of modern diagnostic tests, detecting Negri bodies through histological stains on smears or brain tissue sections was an essential diagnostic process. However, microscopic examination for Negri bodies with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) or Seller’s stain is not as sensitive or specific as a fluorescent antibody test (FAT) or immunohistochemistry (IHC). Approximately 53.7–70% of rabies-positive animals present Negri bodies in their brains [3, 5, 9, 10, 12, 18]. Negri bodies are sometimes confused with Negri-like inclusion bodies, which are indistinguishable from each other [26]. Therefore, HE and Seller’s stains are no longer recommended for diagnosis by the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH) and the World Health Organization (WHO) [41, 43].

The direct FAT (dFAT) is the gold standard for rabies antigen detection due to its high sensitivity and specificity, providing reliable results within a few hours on fresh specimens [41, 43]. However, under tropical field conditions without cold chain maintenance, high temperatures promote tissue decomposition, which reduces the sensitivity of dFAT [11, 24]. Additionally, dFAT requires expensive ultraviolet microscopes and presents hazards to laboratory staff handling live virus samples [41]. Tissue fixation offers a safe and simple alternative by preserving samples for later analysis. The dFAT performed on formalin-fixed or glycerol-preserved specimens requires additional processing and yields less reliable results. The WOAH recommends collecting a pool of brain tissues, with the brainstem being the most crucial component for FAT [43]. Molecular techniques, which require strict quality control in the laboratory and substantial expertise, can be utilized for confirmatory testing and epidemiological surveys [42]. IHC on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue serves as a secondary or confirmatory method for rabies antigen detection.

The rabies virus is an RNA virus belonging to the genus Lyssavirus and family Rhabdoviridae. It encodes five proteins: nucleoproteins (N proteins), phosphoproteins (P proteins), large proteins (L proteins), matrix proteins (M proteins), and glycoproteins (G proteins). The N, P, and L proteins bind to genomic RNA to form ribonucleoproteins that are involved in transcription and replication [1]. According to a previous study, non-flying mammalian rabies viruses were classified into six major clades: Asian, Cosmopolitan, Indian subcontinent, Africa 2, Africa 3, and Arctic-related clades [8]. Rabies viruses isolated from dogs in the Philippines are genetically distinct from isolates found in other Asian countries, including Thailand, Malaysia, Nepal, and Sri Lanka. The Asia clade is divided into SEA 1 to 5 subclades, with only the SEA4 subclade circulating in the Philippines and not detected in other countries [29, 45].

To date, few pathological studies of naturally infected dogs have been reported in Africa and Asia. According to a large case series from Sri Lanka [5] and South Africa [6], the brainstem was the most frequently positive for antigen or immunoreactivity, whereas the hippocampus contained the majority of Negri bodies detected. Small case series from Thailand [34] and India [36] suggested that viral antigen distribution was more extensive in the brainstem than in other brain areas. The study from the USA indicated that the hippocampus contained the most intense signal [35]. The Turkish study revealed the cerebellum and hippocampus mainly contained positive neurons [3]. It is unknown whether this variation is due to differences in study design, infection stage, degree of postmortem changes, virus strain, or other factors.

Rabies remains a public health problem in the Philippines, with 250–300 Filipinos dying annually [44]. Dogs are the primary transmitters in the Philippines. To the best of our knowledge, no published studies have examined rabies neuropathology in the Philippines.

Therefore, in this study, we analyzed the neuropathology using FFPE tissue in different brain regions from 70 confirmed rabid dogs in the Philippines.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals and direct FATs (dFATs)

Seventy suspected rabid domestic dogs were submitted to the Research Institute for Tropical Medicine (RITM) in the Philippines for routine postmortem rabies diagnosis, which was confirmed by using dFAT. The data from individual dogs were recorded. Small transverse sections (2–3 mm thick) of the Ammon’s horn and medulla were excised. Touch imprint slides prepared from the excised surface of the sections were placed on cold acetone overnight for fixation. After fixation, the slides were air-dried at room temperature (RT). Then, 45 μL of fluorescein isothiocyanate conjugate (FITC) anti-rabies monoclonal antibody (Fujirebio®, Malvern, PA, USA) was added to the slides. The slides were incubated for 30 min at 37°C in a high-humidity chamber. The slides were dipped and rinsed twice in PBS, and then washed with distilled water. Small amounts of the mounting medium (20% glycerol-Tris buffered saline pH 9.0) were placed on the slides before covering them with coverslips for examination. The slides were examined under a fluorescence microscope (80i; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Histopathological examination

Brain tissue from rabid dogs (n=70) containing cerebrum, hippocampus, thalamus, and brainstem was fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for at least 72 hr at RT. All the specimens were embedded in paraffin. The FFPE tissues were transversely sectioned (2–3 µm), stained with HE, or labeled using the IHC method described below. All brain samples were selected based on minimal postmortem changes, as determined by gross and histological observations. The samples from one beagle dog, aged 10 months and vaccinated against rabies, were used as the negative controls. The cerebrum, hippocampus, thalamus, and brainstem were available for all dogs.

Immunohistochemistry

The polymer-based immunohistochemical method was used to detect rabies viral antigens and cell types. Primary antibodies were utilized: anti-rabbit phosphoprotein (anti-P) [7, 32, 33], a CD3 antibody (Agilent DAKO, Kyoto, Japan) for T lymphocytes; a CD20 antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Fremont, CA, USA) for B lymphocytes; and an antibody against ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba1) (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd., Osaka, Japan) for microglial cells [7, 32]. Briefly, to activate the antigen in tissue sections, 0.25% trypsin was used for 30 min at RT for anti-P, and microwave treatment at 170 W for 10 min for CD3 and CD20. Subsequently, 0.3% or 3% hydrogen peroxidase in methanol was used to remove endogenous peroxidase. Tissue sections were treated with 10% normal goat serum to block non-specific reactions before being incubated in a humidity chamber overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (1:1,000 for anti-P, 1:400 for CD20, 1:500 for Iba1, and predilute CD3). The Envision + System HRP labeled polymer rabbit antibody (Agilent Dako) was used for CD3, CD20, and Iba1 detection. The Histofine® Simple Stain Max PO (Rabbit) (Nichirei Biosciences, Tokyo, Japan) was used for anti-P and detection. Antigen-antibody reactions were visualized using 3,3'-Diaminobenzidine (DAB; Nichirei Biosciences). The tissue sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted.

Histopathological (HE) and IHC analysis

The presence or absence of Negri bodies was recorded. Perivascular cuffing and glial nodules were visually semi-quantitatively graded on scales of 0 to 3 and 0 to 2, respectively. The number of anti-P immunoreactive neuronal cells was visually semi-quantitatively scored on a scale of 0 to 3 as follows: no positive neurons as 0, 1–30% neurons as 1, 30–60% neurons as 2, and 60–100% neurons as 3. All brain regions were independently analyzed by three pathologists, with discrepancies resolved by consensus.

Statistical analysis

The differences in median HE and anti-P scores among different brain locations were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis H Test. Post-hoc multiple comparisons were performed using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro 16 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

RESULTS

Clinical characteristics of the dogs

Thirty-two dogs were found dead, 12 died of natural causes (illness), 23 were euthanized, and 3 died of an unknown cause. There were 31 males, 23 females, and 16 of unknown sex. The mean age of the 54 dogs (males + females) was 1.89 ± 2.59 years (range: 1 month–10 years), with 16 dogs of unknown age. Three dogs had a vaccination history, 37 had no history, and 30 had no relevant information. Twenty-three were domestic dogs, 17 were free-roaming, 11 were strays, and 19 were unknown. Major neurological symptoms were observed in 36 of the 70 dogs, including unprovoked aggressiveness, excessive drooling, mad biting at inanimate objects, paralysis, and aimless running. Non-specific clinical symptoms, such as decreased appetite, weakness, and restlessness, were observed in 13 of the 70 dogs. Data on the clinical signs of the remaining dogs were unavailable.

Immunohistochemical findings of rabid dogs

All dFAT-confirmed rabies cases were confirmed using IHC anti-P (100%) in at least one positive anatomical region. This result indicates that the sensitivity of the IHC method was 100%. The immunoreactivity was 100% (70/70) in the hippocampus, thalamus, and brainstem and 97.14% (68/70) in the cerebrum. The mean rank of the percentage of immunoreactive cells was not statistically significantly different between anatomical regions (χ2 [3]=7.75, P>0.05).

DISCUSSION

The present study analyzed the neuropathology in different brain regions of 70 rabid dogs in the Philippines. The percentage of Negri bodies observed in most of the dogs (88.57%) was higher than reported in Turkey, Sri Lanka, South Africa, Jordan, and previous studies on other infected animals [3, 5, 9, 10, 12, 18]. Consistent with findings from other countries, the Negri body was notably detected in the hippocampus [3, 5, 10, 22, 37]. However, inclusions commonly found in the brainstem were not observed in our study [3, 5, 12]. In the present study, Negri bodies demonstrated pleomorphism, ranging from a few large round to oval inclusions that were easily observable in the cerebrum and hippocampus. Occasionally, inner body-like basophilic vacuoles were observed inside the inclusions. Inversely, Negri bodies in the thalamus neurons presented small, fine, granular shapes without basophilic vacuoles, and these morphological findings were similar to lyssa bodies. Lyssa bodies are known as small aggregates of virions and matrix material without inner bodies and are undetectable by light microscopy in neuronal perikarya and dendrites [30]. Light microscopy was unable to detect the Negri body in brainstem neurons in this study; nevertheless, the IHC results showed much more extensive distribution in infected neurons. The cause of the variable size, number, and shape of Negri bodies in different brain regions remains unknown but may be influenced partly by natural infection mode. Caudal-level nerve nuclei in the brainstem and thalamus are mainly intermediate neurons, and neurons in these areas are infected before higher neurons in the hippocampus and cerebral pyramidal neurons during retrograde infection. Therefore, it is possible that inflammatory cells infiltrate during the early stage of infection in the brainstem and thalamus, and that the early immune response inhibits virus replication and virus protein synthesis. More advanced inflammation in the lower brains likely leads to the destruction and obscuration of Negri bodies. The effect of lower brain inflammation could manifest before the upper brain lesion has fully developed. Indeed, our results indicated that the prevalence of Negri bodies was inversely proportional to the degree of inflammation, similar to the experimental dogs [14]. The differences in the number and distribution of cytoplasmic organelles, which are essential for negrigenesis, between neurons in different brain locations may play a role. The Negri bodies represent viral factories [27, 28] or might be cellular defense mechanisms [20, 39]. This compartmentalization, demarcated from the surrounding cytoplasm, may be an escape strategy employed by the virus to evade cell-intrinsic defense during replication and assembly, or it might represent a protective strategy by the neuron to degrade viral component, both involving aggresome pathway [20, 23, 25, 39]. In cases where Negri bodies were not observed in any area of the brain, several factors may have contributed to this finding. The stage of rabies infection could impact the visibility of Negri bodies. During the early stages, Negri bodies may not yet have formed, whereas in later stages, they might be cleared or obscured by immunity and inflammation. Additionally, the immune response of the individual hosts could play a crucial role in the detectability of Negri bodies as an aggressive immune response may inhibit their formation or lead to their destruction. Further investigation using an electron microscope is required to examine the ultrastructural differences between Negri bodies—such as large round to oval inclusions observed in the cerebrum and hippocampus, and lyssa bodies—such as small, fine, granular inclusions found in the thalamus and brainstem.

The anatomical distribution of inflammation is relevant to pathogenesis. In this study, perivascular cuffing and gliosis in the cerebrum and hippocampus, where Negri bodies were more frequently detected, were generally mild or absent. In contrast, perivascular cuffing and microglial nodules were more prominent and frequently observed in the brainstem.

As previously stated, it is proposed that the natural course of the virus results in more pronounced inflammation in the lower brain [5, 34]. Our results are partially similar to those of naturally infected rabid dogs from Thailand and India [34, 36]. No significant differences were observed between the cerebrum, hippocampus, and brainstem in the study of canine series from Sri Lanka [5]. Our percentage of cases with perivascular cuffing and gliosis was higher compared to that of the study from South Africa [10]. The differences in brain inflammation were demonstrated between two virus strains in an experimental study on rabies-infected dogs [14]. These results indicated that the degree of perivascular inflammation and microglial cell reactivity was possibly affected by the strain type. Other factors such as study design, clinical form, and infection stage variation factors should be considered.

The immunohistochemical viral antigen detection exhibited 100% sensitivity in the hippocampus, thalamus, and brainstem, matching the sensitivity of dFAT. It confirmed the diagnosis when the Negri bodies could not be seen. Several animal species from Germany showed the amounts of antigen were highest in the hippocampus, diencephalon, and tegmental part of the brain stem [13]. In contrast, the studies from Sri Lanka, South Africa, Thailand, and India, showed that the brainstem demonstrated a higher viral antigen positivity, immunoreactivity score, or antigen distribution than that of other areas [5, 10, 34, 36] or the others from Turkey, India, and the USA reported that the hippocampus or cerebellum or both contained the most intense signal [3, 17, 35]. In our two cases of the cerebrum (cases No. 2 and 3), the tests for the rabies antigen yielded negative results. The IHC was conducted on the same slides for the cerebrum, hippocampus, and thalamus. Notably, although the hippocampus and thalamus exhibited positive immunoreactivity, the cerebrum did not. This discrepancy could be attributed to tissue decomposition, which was confirmed using HE stains and significantly impacted the diagnostic results, contrasting with Sri Lanka studies [5]. The factors aforementioned in the inflammation section, differences in sampling and processing methods, and the degree of postmortem changes and inflammation should be considered owing to the variations in IHC results. Additionally, inconsistencies may result from studies that include species other than domestic dogs.

According to the rabies virus strain, phylogenetic analysis reveals that the Philippines exclusively contains the SEA4 subclade, whereas Sri Lanka is characterized solely by the Indian subcontinent clade. SEA3 is primarily found in Thailand, and the Indian subcontinent and AL1 subclades are predominant in India [45]. In Turkey, Jordan, Georgia, South Africa, and Europe, the virus mainly belongs to the different clades from the Philippines [2, 4, 8, 16, 19, 31, 38]. Our pathological findings suggest that the observation of inclusion bodies in the hippocampus and cerebral cortex during histopathological examination could facilitate diagnosis. However, we support the suggestions from other countries [5, 10] because using IHC on the brainstem is a valuable method for practical rabies diagnosis, which offers high sensitivity comparable to dFAT. This is particularly advantageous in countries where cold chain transportation is impractical and composite sampling in the field is unattainable. Using the brainstem instead of deep internal brain tissues facilitates easy sample collection and reduces hazards for practitioners by avoiding the need to access and sample deeper brain areas compared to that of other techniques.
