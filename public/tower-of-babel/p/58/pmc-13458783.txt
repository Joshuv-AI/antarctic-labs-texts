Interpretable white-box modeling for nitrogen storage in metal–organic frameworks
Authors: Arefeh Naghizadeh, Fahimeh Hadavimoghaddam, Meftah Ali Abuswer, Ahmad Mohaddespour, Abdolhossein Hemmati-Sarapardeh, Saeid Atashrouz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The efficient removal of low-concentration nitrogen (N2) is a critical and challenging task in the industrial production of high-purity oxygen (O2), particularly in air separation processes and in natural gas purification for generating high-purity methane (CH4). In this work, three advanced modeling techniques, namely the group method of data handling (GMDH), gene expression programming (GEP), and genetic programming (GP) were applied to develop user-friendly mathematical correlations for predicting nitrogen storage capacity of MOFs. A broad dataset of 3073 laboratory measurements was employed. The developed models were further evaluated for their accuracy and reliability using various statistical and graphical methods. Among the models, the GEP correlation provided the most reliable outcomes with superior statistical metrics, yielding mean absolute error (MAE) values of 0.9924, 1.0101 and 0.9959 for the training, testing, and overall datasets, and R2 values of 0.9703, 0.9750, and 0.9714, respectively. All the models closely followed the expected trend of N2 storage under varying pressure. Additionally, the Pearson, Spearman, and Kendall correlation analyses were employed to examine the influence of individual input factors on the model outputs. The findings revealed that temperature has the most substantial impact on storage capacity across both linear and non-linear dimensions, whereas pressure primarily affects it through nonlinear interactions. At last, the credibility of the collected databank and the applicability of the recommended correlations were confirmed using the leverage method, demonstrating more than 95% of the collected data fell within the acceptable range of the Williams plot.

Introduction

Natural gas, an affordable and environmentally friendly energy source, is anticipated to become the fuel of the future for transportation. However, a significant challenge in this sector remains refining this fossil fuel, as numerous methane deposits contain excessive levels of contaminants such as carbon dioxide, nitrogen, and water1. The recovery of N2, as a common industrial impurity gas, is significant for various applications in the chemical and energy industries, including methane upgrading (N2/CH4) and air separation (N2/O2). Currently, distillation technology remains the primary method for large-scale N2/O2 separation in the chemical industry2. Nevertheless, this approach requires substantial energy and financial resources due to its low energy efficiency3. Cost-effective and advanced adsorption-based separation methods are now available for the removal of CO2 and H2O from natural gas; However, the same is not true for the selective extraction of N2 from CH4, which has mainly relied on cryogenic separation. Among the available technologies, adsorption stands out as a favorable approach for gas separation, owing to its low energy consumption and operational cost efficiency4. The removal of nitrogen-rich gas mixtures, including natural gas, shale gas, and dry air, requires adsorption-based separation techniques that employ adsorbents with stronger affinities toward nitrogen compared to hydrocarbons and oxygen.

Advanced porous materials have gained increasing global attention, leading to substantial progress in their research and development. In recent years, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) have garnered considerable interest as potential materials for gas storage, including CH4, H2, and CO25. MOFs have been extensively recognized for their superior permeability, unique architecture, large surface area, tunable pore size, substantial micropore volume, and wide range of potential applications6–8. These porous materials are crystalline structures consist of metal ions or clusters connected to organic ligands via coordination bonds. The organic ligands function as linkers, joining the metal nodes to generate a three-dimensional framework. Due to the vast range of possible metal and organic ligand pairings, MOFs are highly versatile materials.

Some MOFs exhibit unique properties, such as enhanced catalytic performance or selective affinity for specific gas molecules9. By carefully choosing proper metal ions and ligands, MOFs can be tailored and designed to possess desired characteristics. Adsorptive removal or separation is a key application of MOFs, as many of these materials exhibit high porosity and diverse chemical activities. Both properties are essential for facilitating the adsorptive interactions with a broad range of adsorbates10–12. Burd et al.13 analyzed the adsorption behavior of CO2 and CH4 on [Cu(bpy-1)2 (SiF6)] and found a CO2 uptake of 23.1 wt% and a CO2/CH4 selectivity of 10.5 at 298 K and 1 atm, respectively. Conventional experimental approaches for studying gas adsorption, are often labor-intensive, costly, and may involve destructive analysis. Moreover, variables such as temperature, pressure, and surface characteristics complicate precise predictions across various materials. Consequently, these approaches frequently fail to accurately predict nitrogen adsorption capacities, emphasizing the necessity for more advanced predictive strategies.

Recent studies have reported the widespread use of ML for modeling gas adsorption on porous carbon-based materials14. Building on this approach, Rahimi et al. demonstrated that coupling density functional theory (DFT) with ML models allows highly accurate prediction of methane adsorption on N/O-functionalized graphene, achieving an R2 value close to 0.99 and identifying key structural parameters governing CH4 uptake15. Similarly, for CO2 adsorption, ML models have been successfully applied to biomass-based activated carbons, where the RBF model achieved strong agreement with experimental data, with R2 values in the range of 0.97–0.99, highlighting both predictive accuracy and material sustainability16. In the case of hydrogen adsorption, ML-based models have also shown high reliability on activated carbons, with low RMSE values (0.06–0.19), high accuracy (R2 ≈ 0.97), and minimal prediction error (MAPE ≈ 3.15)17. Overall, these findings confirm the robustness and generalizability of ML-based frameworks across different gas–adsorbent systems.

Beyond adsorption studies, recent research has also shown that advanced ML techniques, particularly ensemble and deep learning approaches, can effectively optimize complex energy and thermochemical processes. Methods such as XGBoost, Random Forest, CatBoost, and deep learning have achieved high predictive accuracy in applications including biomass gasification, hydrothermal carbonization, chemical looping for hydrogen production, and steelmaking optimization with reported R2 values reaching up to 0.9918–21. These findings highlight the strong potential of ML for adsorption modeling and process optimization5,22–24.

Despite these promising results, forecasting nitrogen uptake in MOFs remains largely unexplored. To date, no precise and user-friendly mathematical correlation, trained on a comprehensive dataset, has been developed for predicting nitrogen adsorption in MOFs.

The development of such predictive correlations is essential for accelerating the design of advanced MOFs with enhanced nitrogen adsorption capacities. By providing a rapid and reliable estimation framework, researchers and industry practitioners can optimize material selection and operational conditions without relying solely on time-consuming experiments. Moreover, integrating ML-based predictive models with advanced porous materials offers the capability to bridge the gap between laboratory-scale synthesis and large-scale industrial applications. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of advanced correlative approaches, including genetic programming (GP), the group method of data handling (GMDH), and gene expression programming (GEP), in predicting nitrogen adsorption across different MOF types. An extensive database including 3073 data points is employed to develop precise correlations for estimating nitrogen uptake based on temperature (T), pressure (P), surface area (m2/g), and the pore volume(cm3/g) of the MOFs.

A key advantage of the symbolic regression–based models used here is their transparency, which aligns with the White-Box concept and makes them interpretable, accessible, and user-friendly.

The proposed correlations are examined for reliability using both statistical error measures and graphical illustrations. Pearson, Spearman, and Kendall correlation coefficients are calculated to evaluate the connections between inputs and outputs in both linear and non-linear forms. Moreover, trend analyses are performed to test the reliability of the best-performing model. Finally, using the leverage approach, both potential outliers and the domain of applicability of the constructed models are evaluated. A comprehensive databank was compiled under a wide range of operational conditions to guarantee the generalizability of the identified trends. The databank, which contained 3073 data points, was sourced from open-access literature25–47. To determine the optimal train-to-test split for the models, several ratios were evaluated, including 70/30, 75/25, 80/20, 85/15, and 90/10. To reduce the risk of overfitting and ensure robust generalization, a sixfold cross-validation strategy was employed during model development. In this approach, the dataset was divided into six subsets, with five folds used for training and one fold reserved for validation in each iteration. This process was repeated until all folds were used for validation once, and the final model performance was evaluated based on averaged metrics across all folds. This strategy ensures that the developed correlations generalize well beyond interpolation of the training data and reliably capture the underlying physical trends of nitrogen adsorption in MOFs.

Methodology

Group method of data handling (GMDH)

GMDH aims to address various problems mathematically through a range of polynomial procedures. This algorithm is capable of managing the complexity and non-linearity of networks by generating clear and precise correlations between input and output variables49. The GMDH features a layered structure in which each layer contains quadratic polynomials that combine to produce a new set of neurons in the subsequent layer. These layers and associated neurons connect the inputs to the expected output. Figure 2 depicts a functional flowchart for the GMDH method. Genetic programming (GP)

Koza52,53 proposed GP to tackle challenges across various engineering fields, automatically. This robust, nature-inspired algorithm is capable of simulating systems exhibiting a significant level of fuzziness. The method consists of creating an equation that represents the phenomenon under study to obtain solutions. Tree-based representations are employed in GP systems. Within these trees, nodes are divided into two types depending on their placement: internal nodes, which carry out operations on incoming data, and external nodes, which function as the tree’s terminal points54. This reliable, nature-inspired technique utilizes an iterative process to generate an accurate mathematical model. Once the data is input, the procedure begins by creating a random initial population, which is then assessed for performance using an objective function. High-fitness individuals are subsequently selected as parents and undergo genetic operations to produce offspring55. Subsequent generations are generated in the same manner, with each generation exhibiting improved fitness as favorable traits are inherited. The procedure terminates once the error criterion is satisfied or the predetermined generation number is achieved. Gene expression programming (GEP)

Ferreira introduced GEP in the early twenty-first century56. This approach involves the combination of linear and basic fixed-length chromosomes, similar to genetic algorithms, along with branch structures of varied sizes and shapes, reminiscent of decomposition trees in GP. However, the GP framework employs non-linear response structures, specifically parse trees. In practice, chromosomes identify the choice or preliminary estimates for a given problem. By applying an interpretation strategy, expression trees can generate more precise solutions. The two main components in GEP are the expression trees (ETs) and the chromosomes57. In a typical GEP, each computer program is represented by a fixed-length gene expression string, which is generally evolved using nature-inspired operators such as crossover and mutation58. Because of the straightforward rules that define the platform of the ETs and their interactions, it allows rapid identification of phenotypes derived from the gene sequence56. Each GEP framework comprises different genes, each featuring a head in which both a terminal and a function are included. Graphical error assessment

Moreover, alongside the statistical assessment of the correlations’ performance, several plots were used to visually illustrate their effectiveness.

In cross-plots, also known as equality diagrams, the model-predicted values are compared with the actual experimental values. A strong model prediction is characterized by the data points being tightly clustered around the line where X equals Y.

In the error distribution plots, the analysis focuses on identifying any trends in the errors by examining the distribution of errors around the zero-error line. In this study, the error is represented as a residual error between experimental and predicted data.

In the trend analysis plot, measured and estimated data are shown against a chosen input factor, allowing assessment of the model’s ability to represent that variable’s trend.

Results and discussion

Developed correlations

This study gathered 3073 experimental data points from multiple literature sources to develop white-box models for estimating nitrogen uptake over a wide range of temperatures and pressures. To achieve the objectives of the study, three robust correlation techniques were investigated: genetic programming (GP), the group method of data handling (GMDH), and gene expression programming (GEP). A major merit of these white-box approaches is their forecasting mechanisms are fully transparent and can be readily applied using straightforward, interpretable equations. Sensitivity assessment

The sensitivity analysis clarifies the magnitude and orientation of the relationships among variables. The degree to which each input factor affects the model’s forecasted result is quantified by a relevance factor. As the Pearson correlation coefficients rise, the impact of the independent variable on the dependent variable intensifies61. Moreover, input parameters with positive relevancy factors directly influence the predicted nitrogen uptake values, whereas those with negative relevancy factors affect the predictions in the opposite direction. The Pearson correlation coefficient evaluates the linear connection between two variables, indicating both its strength and direction and revealing the closeness of their relationship. The formula used to calculate this coefficient is listed below61,62:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r(x_{m} ,y) = \frac{{\sum\nolimits_{i = 1}^{n} {(x_{m,i} - \overline{x}_{{_{m} }} )(y_{i} - \overline{y})} }}{{\sqrt {\sum\nolimits_{i = 1}^{n} {(x_{m,i} - \overline{x}_{{_{m} }} )^{2} } \sum\nolimits_{i = 1}^{n} {(y_{i} - \overline{y})^{2} } } }}$$\end{document}

Here, the variable m can represent any of the specified input parameters. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{x}_{m}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{m,i}$$\end{document} represents the mean and i-th value of the m-th input variable. Pearson analysis shows that temperature (0.7) has a strong linear relationship with N2 storage, while surface area (0.45) and pore volume (0.42) exhibit moderate linear correlations. In contrast, pressure, with a Pearson coefficient of 0.16, demonstrates a weak linear correlation. Additionally, surface area and pore volume positively contribute to increasing storage capacity. Spearman coefficients reveal a moderate non-linear relationship between N2 uptake and both pressure (0.32) and pore volume (0.4).

Temperature significantly influences nitrogen adsorption in MOFs through both linear and non-linear effects. Adsorption is mainly governed by physisorption driven by van der Waals interactions; lower temperatures favor adsorption, while higher temperatures increase molecular kinetic energy and reduce uptake. Consequently, temperature affects adsorption both gradually and through non-linear mechanisms related to adsorption site activation and structural flexibility of the framework. Pressure primarily contributes through non-linear effects due to the saturation-type nature of adsorption. At low pressures, micropore filling leads to a sharp increase in adsorption, whereas at higher pressures, multilayer adsorption and pore saturation result in diminishing gains in storage capacity.

Overall, linear correlations highlight temperature, pore volume, and surface area as the dominant factors influencing N2 uptake. However, when non-linear effects are considered, temperature emerges as the most significant parameter, exerting a predominant influence on nitrogen uptake. For pressure, the higher values of Spearman and Kendall compared to Pearson reflect the nonlinear yet monotonic nature of adsorption isotherms, which rank-based correlations capture more effectively than linear correlation.

Physical trend of the GEP model

To further evaluate robustness, a pattern analysis was performed to determine how effectively the newly developed correlations capture and represent the actual physical behavior and changes of nitrogen uptake under different pressures.

As pressure increases, the density of nitrogen molecules near the MOF pores rises, enhancing adsorption on internal surfaces. Initially, adsorption is dominated by van der Waals interactions and monolayer formation, while at higher pressures, progressive pore filling and multilayer adsorption occur. From a thermodynamic perspective, the increase in chemical potential at higher pressures drives adsorption, resulting in a higher nitrogen uptake.

Summary and conclusions

In this work, three white-box correlations, including GMDH, GEP, and GP, were developed to establish user-friendly mathematical relationships for predicting nitrogen storage in MOFs. A thorough dataset comprising 3073 experimental results of nitrogen storage in various MOFs was analyzed, considering pressure, temperature, pore volume, and surface area as critical factors.
