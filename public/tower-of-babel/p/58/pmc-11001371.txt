Supporting professionals to implement integrated, person-centered care for people with chronic conditions: the TARGET pilot study
Authors: Rowan G. M. Smeets, Dorijn F. L. Hertroijs, Dirk Ruwaard, Sophie L. W. Spoorenberg, Arianne M. J. Elissen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe TARGET program for integrated, person-centered care for people with chronic conditions offers primary care (PC) professionals a set of tools and trainings to actively engage in population segmentation and person-centered needs assessments (PCNAs). A pilot study was conducted to gain insight into the program’s feasibility and acceptability, and identify preconditions for successful implementation.Design and settingSeven Dutch PC practices participated in a half-year pilot study starting in August 2020. We performed a review of the population segmentation tool, observed four training sessions and 15 PCNAs, and interviewed 15 professionals and 12 patients.ResultsRegarding feasibility and acceptability, we found that the tools and trainings provided professionals with skills to use the segmentation tool and take a more coaching role in the well-appreciated PCNAs. Concerning implementation preconditions, we found that team commitment and network connections need improvement, although work pleasure increased and professionals generally wanted the program to continue.ConclusionsWhile the content of the TARGET program is supported by its users, the implementation process, for instance team commitment to the program, needs more attention in future upscaling efforts.

Introduction

People with chronic disease tend to use health care more regularly than the general population [1]. In particular, there is a small subgroup of patients – often referred to as the ‘high need high cost (HNHC)’ population – which has a disproportionally high care use and accounts for a large proportion of healthcare spending [2]. These people usually experience multiple chronic conditions and encounter challenges in many aspects of life, ranging from medical issues to socio-economic problems [3]. To date, the system of care is still largely fragmented and medical-oriented and it fails to adequately accommodate the complex needs of this population [2,3]. As the general practitioner (GP) is an easily accessible and often highly trusted care professional, GPs are overwhelmed by the sometimes primarily social problems of HNHC patients [4]. Almost one-fifth of GP’s time is spent on primarily social issues, as the absence of strong network ties with mental health and social services impedes them to adequately refer patients with these issues [5]. As a result, care professionals’ workload remains high, with risks for productivity and patient-centeredness [6,7].

Integrated care is seen as a promising approach to improve both patient and professional experience [8,9]. Integrated care is defined as ‘patient care that is coordinated across professionals, facilities and support systems; continuous over time and between visits; tailored to the patients’ needs and preferences; and based on shared responsibility between patient and caregivers for optimizing health’ [10]. An essential starting point to integrated care is to understand patient heterogeneity in terms of health needs and care preferences [11]. Segmenting the population with chronic disease into distinct subgroups that are relatively homogeneous in their needs and preferences allows for the development of tailored care and intervention programs [11,12]. These programs should not just integrate services within the health system, but also bridge the gap between the health and social systems, thereby combining the scope of integrated care with population health management. This supports the integration of services across health care, prevention, social care and welfare [13]. To make integrated care work, it is also important that professionals acquire new skills and roles, linked to efficient cooperation with and referral to other domains of care [14,15].

Although integrated, person-centered care is widely advocated as ‘the way forward’, actually implementing integrated care remains a widespread challenge [16–18]. In response to this, we have developed the TARGET integrated care program, including several concrete tools and trainings that can support professionals to acquire the skills and confidence needed to ‘practice’ integrated care [19]. TARGET – which is an acronym for ‘Targeting Advanced Resources in General practice to create Efficient, Tailored and holistic care for chronically ill patients’ – uses a data-driven approach to specifically target the chronically ill population with diverse healthcare needs to offer them well-targeted (i.e. tailored) care and support [18].

Description of the TARGET program

From 2016, the authors, in co-creation with primary care (PC) group ‘Dokter Drenthe’, which covers a total of 130 PC practices, developed the TARGET program [20]. In the Netherlands, PC groups, consisting of primary care professionals (PCPs), such as GPs and practice nurses, have bundled payment contracts with health insurers for the delivery of several disease management programs [21,22]. The program was scientifically shaped by the insights from various studies [17,19,23,24], and co-created with the support of different steering groups with various stakeholders, ranging from PCPs to policy makers and health insurers.

TARGET is intended for adults (>17 years) with at least one of 13 common chronic conditions (see Figure 1). To ensure that we select people who currently have chronic condition(s), we only include people if they used care for one or several of these conditions from their general practice in the previous two and a half year. Dutch statistics point to a prevalence of chronic conditions (based on recent care consumption) of around 30% [25]. However, as we have selected 13 conditions from a larger variety of 28 conditions that are usually seen as chronic [26], the prevalence is somewhat lower, i.e. around 24%. We have chosen these 13 conditions as these are common conditions and this selection constitutes a manageable number for daily practice. During the pilot study, the focus was on patients in the ‘high care need’-subgroup who have, based on previous studies, the most urgent needs for an integrated, person-centered organization of care [24,27–29]. The program is based on a theory-driven framework (Figure 1) and has three linked components. Furthermore, the framework distinguishes a set of intermediate, more short-term goals per patient subgroup (i.e. population segment), for instance to prevent hospital submissions for the high care need subgroup, as well as overall objectives linked to the quadruple aim of health system performance. TARGET is described in detail elsewhere [19].

During the pilot study of TARGET, PC practices were offered tools and trainings to implement the first two components: (1) population segmentation and (2) person-centered needs assessment (PCNA). Population segmentation serves as an information tool to give insight into the population with the highest and most complex needs (the high care need subgroup, as highlighted in green in Figure 1) and to determine which patients to invite for a PCNA or other interventions. See Appendix 1for additional information about the care need subgroups. To identify the high care need patients, practices received a digital tool containing a real-time and quarterly updated overview of people belonging to this subgroup. The tool visualizes a report – that is uploaded quarterly by PCPs – containing data from the electronic health records. The report includes all registered patient data related to healthcare use (e.g. number and types of consultations), health issues (e.g. chronic conditions) and demographic characteristics (e.g. date of birth). The tool uses this report to build several visualizations providing insight into the nature of patients’ care needs (e.g. type of complaints, type of care professional(s) involved in the consultations; see Appendix 2). Due to this large amount of information, practices can use the tool to get more insight into patients and select them for interventions based on the specific (combined) information (e.g. care needs, chronic conditions and demography) they find most relevant. At the start of the pilot study, PCPs from the participating PC practices received an online 2-h training on the use of the population segmentation tool.

The PCNA includes a comprehensive conversation of 30–45 min to get insight into the biopsychosocial needs of people with chronic conditions. The ‘My Positive Health’ tool was offered to structure and guide the PCNA [30,31]. This tool includes a patient questionnaire on how patients perceive their health, according to six domains, such as bodily functions and mental well-being. A dialogue tool (the ‘spider web’) serves to visualize perceived health. To measure a patient’s biopsychosocial problems identified during the PCNA, and discuss and plan follow-up actions (e.g. referral to social domain), the Dutch version of the Patient Centered Assessment Method (PCAM) was used, also because ‘My Positive Health’ does not support validated measurement [32–34]. The PCAM contains 12 items, largely based on the biopsychosocial model of illness [35], and four open-ended questions, for instance about ‘What action is required?’ [32]. The 12 items are scored on a four-point traffic light-style system indicating the growing need for (professional) action. More details can be read in a psychometric paper of this instrument, published in 2021 [34].

To equip professionals with the required skills and confidence to engage in the PCNA, professionals received two interactive PCNA trainings and two e-learning modules inspired by the philosophy of positive health [30,31]. Dokter Drenthe facilitated the implementation of TARGET by for instance organizing peer meetings to share experiences and tips. The third component of the TARGET program, network support, aims to support PCPs and patients in realizing the required ‘tailored care, referral and coordination’. As work pressure is generally high in general practice, it is in particular important that PCPs can refer patients with primarily non-medical needs to the designated (social) services. As the pilot was relatively short and was therefore mainly focused on implementing the first two components of TARGET, this third component was not widely evaluated during the course of this pilot.

Research aims

Given the complex nature of this program [18], the Medical Research Council guidelines for evaluating complex interventions recommend the inclusion of a piloting phase [19,20]. This helps to test program feasibility, and potentially make adjustments, before larger-scale implementation and evaluation. Therefore, a pilot study of the TARGET program was performed in seven PC practices in the Netherlands. We aimed to gain insight into the feasibility and acceptability of the first two components of TARGET – a (1) segmentation approach to divide people with chronically ill conditions in care need subgroups and (2) a PCNA – including the tools and trainings offered to professionals and patients within those components. A second aim of this study was to gain first insights into the preconditions needed for future successful implementation of the full TARGET program.

Methods

Setting and recruitment

The pilot study took place between August 2020 and February 2021 in PC practices in the Dutch rural province of Drenthe, located in the north of the Netherlands, covered by PC group ‘Dokter Drenthe’ [20]. PC practices were recruited for the pilot by Dokter Drenthe via a digital newsletter. Because of the small-scale design of the pilot study, we aimed to include a small number (i.e. no more than five to eight) of highly motivated PC practices. They were eligible to participate if they adequately registered patient data in their electronic health records and had no major internal organizational problems. Twelve PC practices expressed interest in participation, of which seven matched the eligibility criteria and were invited to participate in the pilot. All seven practices agreed to participate.

Study design

A multiple case pilot study was conducted, in which a ‘case’ was defined as a PC practice involved in the implementation of TARGET. A pilot study revolves around the question whether a program, in this case TARGET, can be implemented in practice, should be implemented, and if so, how [36,37]. A mixed-method approach was used to examine the program’s feasibility and acceptability among PCPs and patients of the involved PC practices. Feasibility was tested by assessing the recruitment process and the feasibility of program components and related tools. Acceptability in this study was defined according to the definition from Sekhon et al.: ‘a multi-faceted construct that reflects the extent to which people delivering or receiving a healthcare intervention consider it to be appropriate, based on anticipated or experienced cognitive and emotional responses to the intervention’ [37]. Approval of the study was obtained from the Medical Ethical Committee of the Maastricht University Medical Center (METC 2020-1468).

Data collection

Non-participant observations

Two researchers (DH and RS) individually conducted non-participant observations of the trainings (about the population segmentation and PCNA). Furthermore, per participating PC practice, two PCNAs, were observed at three-month follow-up. During each observation, field notes were taken to ensure that all different elements of the observation – people, place, activities, words and things [38] – were captured. Prior to the observations of the PCNAs, PCPs asked their patients for informed consent. Due to the measures against COVID-19, the PCNA observations took place online via Microsoft Teams. The observations were used as input for semi-structured interviews with PCPs and patients.

Semi-structured interviews

Semi-structured interviews were conducted between February and April 2021. Only patients who had received a PCNA were interviewed, at most two months after the date of the PCNA. Two researchers (DH and RS) each interviewed one PCP and one patient per participating PC practice. In one PC practice, no patients were interviewed, since the practice did not provide any patient contact details. All PCPs and patients were interviewed individually, except for one interview in which two PCPs of the same practice were involved. The researchers conducted the first interview with a PCP together. The interviews were guided by a topic list, which was developed based on the TARGET-framework and the outcomes of the observations. It covered topics like the population segmentation tool and implementation support. The interviews lasted approximately 60 and 30 min, for PCPs and patients, respectively. Again, due to the measures against COVID-19, the interviews with PCPs took place online via Microsoft Teams and those with patients via telephone.

Review of the digital population segmentation tool

The PCAM open fields about ‘Who needs to be involved?’ and ‘Barriers to action?’ were analyzed. We made use of the dataset of PCAMs as previously used for psychometric tests of the PCAM, reported elsewhere [34]. To ensure independence of observations, this dataset was limited to unique patients, implying that – in a small number of cases – only the most recent PCAM for a unique patient was included in case a patient had several PCAMs registered. For all included patients, we retrieved the following descriptive information from the electronic health records, processed in the digital population segmentation tool: age, sex and top 10 most prevalent chronic conditions. All variables were measured at the time of the PCNA.

Data analyses

For the non-participant observations, a general review and discussion took place of the field notes between the two reviewers (DH and RS). The semi-structured interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. To analyze the interviews, the two researchers performed a thematic analysis with an inductive approach following five steps [39–41]. First, the researchers independently ‘familiarized’ themselves with the transcripts. Hence, each researcher read and re-read the first PCP interview transcript and the first patient transcript. Second, the researchers independently developed initial codes for the first PCP interview and the first patient interview transcript. In this step, phrases, sentences and paragraphs with meaningful topics were isolated and labeled by a code. These codes were compared and discussed to make sure both researchers used a similar level of detail in coding. Third, the remaining interview transcripts were equally divided between the researchers who then familiarized themselves with those transcripts and developed initial codes. Fourth, based on the applied codes and the findings from the observations, the two researchers independently created a descriptive summary of the main findings. Those summaries were compared and discussed, and provided the foundation for a mindmap of clustered codes. From the mindmap, a range of main themes was retrieved.

To analyze the two PCAM open fields, researcher RS categorized the answers given by PCPs (see Table 1). The categories were determined inductively, meaning that the categories were created based on the data. In case of doubt, researchers DH and AE were consulted to make a decision. It was discussed whether and how the PCAM findings could be connected to the existing themes. During reporting of the results, the themes were further divided into overarching clusters to improve structure and readability.

Feasibility and acceptability of the TARGET program

We identified 11 themes in the analysis of the interviews and observations. The insights gained from the PCAM open field review were subsequently connected to those themes. The themes were categorized into five overarching clusters: clusters 1–3 belong to the first study aim and clusters 4 and 5 to the second study aim (see Figure 2).

Eleven themes as identified during the qualitative analysis and observations allocated to five overarching clusters.

Cluster 1: feasibility and acceptability of population segmentation tool

All PC practices used the population segmentation tool as a guide to determine which patients to select for a PCNA (theme 1), in addition to other ‘soft’ criteria such as their impression of a patient’s willingness to engage in a PCNA. The selection was often made by practice nurses, sometimes in consultation with a GP. At the start of the pilot, practices often invited people with the highest care needs. During the course of the pilot, practices noticed that these patients were not always eligible patients for the PCNA – due to for instance cognitive impairment. Therefore, most practices decided to invite patients in the middle layer of the list or people who were not on the high care need list, but had socio-economic issues. As some practices wanted to screen based on social indicators (e.g. financial circumstances, social security benefits), PCPs suggested to add more socio-economic patient information to the tool. Initially, the tool did not contain an option to filter patients based on social health issues. The graphs indicating the number of consultations per type of PCP and the health complaints addressed in the consultations were considered most interesting, see Appendix 2. Although some graphs were incomplete due to suboptimal data registration, they provided new insights to PCPs about a patient’s care use (theme 2). In particular, PCPs were surprised by the large number of PCPs patients had contact with. The graphs were usually briefly inspected to prepare the PCNA, although sometimes PCPs deliberately chose not to, in order to engage in the PCNA with an ‘open mind’.

Cluster 2: feasibility and acceptability of PCNA, including tools

Some PCPs indicated that they felt uncomfortable mentioning patients’ high care needs as a reason to invite them. Instead, they often stressed that they wanted to have a ‘different’ conversation than the usual, more biomedical consultations. Nonetheless, almost all patients who were invited for a PCNA agreed to participate. PCPs and patients valued the PCNA (theme 3), among others due to the comprehensive and positive focus on health, which provided new insights. However, some patients said that they were used to have such broad conversations with their PCP. On average, PCNAs lasted 40 min, providing sufficient time for patients to talk elaborately about their health and well-being.

All PCPs used the Positive Health tool as a conversation guide during the PCNAs (theme 4). In most cases, all six domains of Positive Health were discussed during the PCNA, in which the patient was sometimes in the lead. Both PCPs and patients saw the tool as helpful to support a broad conversation, although some patients considered it challenging to rate their health and understand the six domains. Most PCPS asked patients to complete (a digital version of) the My Positive Health questionnaire at home prior to the PCNA. Those who did not, let patients fill in the questionnaire during the PCNA or, in most cases, only the dialogue tool (the ‘spider web’). Both the interviews and observations of PCNAs revealed benefits and drawbacks of completing the questionnaire prior to the PCNA. For instance, it allowed patients to think deeply about the questions and they felt well prepared for the PCNA. But there were also drawbacks: The chances are high that at home [filling in the questionnaire] I would have experienced a downward spiral with more negativity. However, is this truly the case? Is my life that negative? Those questions were answered during the PCNA. So yes, the moment of answering the questionnaire is important. (Patient 5) When the Positive Health tool, either the questionnaire in combination with the dialogue tool or only the dialogue tool, was used during the PCNA, patients appreciated that PCPs could help them understand the tool. However, the observations showed that patients may also feel somewhat overwhelmed by the extensiveness of both the questionnaire and dialogue tool.

The chances are high that at home [filling in the questionnaire] I would have experienced a downward spiral with more negativity. However, is this truly the case? Is my life that negative? Those questions were answered during the PCNA. So yes, the moment of answering the questionnaire is important. (Patient 5)

It was observed that toward the end of the PCNA, PCPs together with their patients started to think about possible actions, taking into account patients’ needs and preferences and available (professional) resources, although actions were often deemed unnecessary (theme 5). This made some PCPs feel unsatisfied. PCPs often asked patients if they, as professionals, could change or arrange something differently in the care patients received. But PCPs also focused on what patients could do themselves: […] and then she [PCP] said: ‘start working on it [book writing], just start’. That happened like three weeks ago […] and now I work on it [book writing] around three, four days a week for a few hours in the morning. I am very happy I started that. It is going well. (Patient 2) In addition, there were discussions between PCPs and patients about possible referral options. The most common conclusion registered in the PCAM (in 44.8% of 232 PCNAs) was that involvement of another discipline/professional was unnecessary (see Table 1). If involvement or referral to another discipline was needed (in 49.1% of 232 PCNAs), mental healthcare was most often suggested (in 8.2% of 232 PCNAs). Additionally, in 70 out of 114 PCNAs (61.4%) in which a discipline/professional needed to be involved, barriers were identified to realizing the action, see Table 1. These barriers were most often related to the patient and his/her network, such as a lack of motivation or not being open to the suggested action (in 27.2% of the 114 PCNAs).

[…] and then she [PCP] said: ‘start working on it [book writing], just start’. That happened like three weeks ago […] and now I work on it [book writing] around three, four days a week for a few hours in the morning. I am very happy I started that. It is going well. (Patient 2)

Cluster 3: feasibility and acceptability of trainings

Both the segmentation tool training and PCNA training were valued by the PCPs, mostly as those were considered practical trainings necessary to apply TARGET components (theme 6). The trainers played an important role in the positive evaluations of the trainings, as the trainers were regarded knowledgeable and supportive (theme 7). For instance, PCPs felt free to ask questions or discuss challenging situations they encountered. Most PCPs also complimented the PCNA trainer for her ability to connect well with the group and her non-pedantic attitude. Some saw her as a role model. Although it was necessary for an adequate PCNA to gain experience with it in daily practice, the trainings allowed PCPs to implement tips and working methods in practice. For example, PCPs practiced how they could change their traditional ‘caregiving’ role into a more ‘coaching’ role by trying not to judge, establish an opinion, and create advice while the patient is talking:

That [practicing how not to judge, establish an opinion, and create advice] is very concrete and specific […], it is something you remember. (PCP 6)

Preconditions for successful implementation of TARGET in the future

Cluster 4: preconditions for successful implementation of TARGET

This study revealed first insights into the preconditions for future successful implementation of the full TARGET program. One of those preconditions pertains to a team-based commitment to the implementation of TARGET (theme 8). In practices with regular team meetings where TARGET is discussed, PCPs seemed to be more satisfied with TARGET and willing to continue with the program. After all, the team meetings in these practices helped to develop a clear vision regarding person-centered and integrated care. In practices with a lack of commitment to the program as a team, only one or a few PCPs were involved in the program, who sometimes felt resistance or poor support from colleagues. Reasons for a lack of commitment of some PCPs were related to insufficient time or interest to learn about the program and implement it. Also, some PCPs were doubtful about the value of TARGET. Social support for TARGET was not only valued within practices, the meetings with peers from other practices, where experiences and tips were shared, also helped the implementation.

Another precondition relates to the successful functioning of the network of care professionals surrounding the PC practice. While PCPs often indicated to be satisfied with their network, the number and quality of referrals was limited (theme 9). Some professionals gave an example of a poor referral as experienced during the pilot: after referral to social domain, the patient was not contacted by any social worker, after which the patient returned to PC. In addition, it was observed that PCPs mostly considered referrals ‘close by’, e.g. to the mental health practice nurse. Only in practices in which multidisciplinary teams were already organized (for instance in the context of integrated elderly care), the network relations, for instance with the social domain, seemed more comprehensive and sustainable.

Cluster 5: future expectations of TARGET

With regards to future expectations of TARGET, most PCPs indicated that TARGET does lead to improved work pleasure and patient experience, although they expected that a decrease in work pressure takes time (theme 10). The extensive attention for patients during the PCNAs was considered an important reason for improved work pleasure and patient experience. Overall, TARGET was considered an investment to work toward a lower work pressure on the longer term – if preconditions for successful implementation (such as team commitment) are met. Furthermore, most PCPs are positive and willing to continue with the implementation of TARGET (theme 11). Nonetheless, to do so, PCPs find it important to, for instance, improve the implementation process and ensure team commitment and shared responsibility:

So we soon have another meeting [with the team of PCPs] and then we will discuss this [continuation of TARGET]. I like the program, but whether we will continue with it will depend a bit on how it will be embedded. (PCP 6)

Discussion

With this study, we aimed to create insight into the feasibility and acceptability of the TARGET integrated care program and learn first lessons about the preconditions needed for successful implementation of TARGET in the future. To this end, seven PC practices engaged in a half-year pilot study to experiment with two TARGET components, a population segmentation tool and a PCNA, including accompanying tools and trainings.

Principal findings

Over the course of the pilot study, signs of adequate feasibility and acceptability became visible. For example, all practices felt enabled to implement the two TARGET components by the trainings, tools and meetings with peers. With regards to acceptability, the program was appreciated by most PCPs as it supported approaching patients in a more meaningful, comprehensive way, which led to increased work pleasure. Patients also valued the broad and positive focus on health and well-being in the PCNAs. No clear differences in experiences of feasibility and acceptability were found between different types of PCPs. Hence, a basic foundation of feasible and acceptable components, tools and trainings seems to be in place, which only need minor revision to be implemented on a larger scale. Nonetheless, in some practices, only one or a few PCPs were actively involved in the implementation of TARGET, and a minority of PCPs was doubtful about the program’s added value. Hence, important preconditions for successful implementation of the program, such as full team commitment to the program, but also strong network ties, need more attention in future implementation.

Strengths and weaknesses

A strength of this study was triangulation [42] both in methods and participant perspectives. This led to a reflective analysis of the pilot results. However, some patient interviews lacked depth, as patients could not clearly remember the PCNA that in some cases took place 2 months ago. A second weakness relates to the implementation of TARGET at the start of the COVID-19 crisis in the Netherlands. This has led to digitalization of almost all trainings, observations and interviews. There were no clear signs that the digital format deprived PCPs of in-depth learning. But, as supported by other studies, more informal ways of interaction and learning were less accommodated [43,44]. A third weakness is that only highly motivated, well-functioning PC practices participated in the pilot. This may have led to an overly positive estimation of the feasibility and acceptability of TARGET, but it also means that involved practices may function as local ‘innovation champions’ for further implementation [45].

Findings in relation to other studies

As advocated internationally, this study underlines the value of a comprehensive, biopsychosocial approach to patients, enabled by the TARGET segmentation tool and PCNA [46,47]. To design the TARGET segmentation tool as truly holistic, it should be expanded with social data, as acknowledged by other studies into segmentation tools as well [48]. However, these data are often lacking as the internationally classified codes for registering social issues, Z-codes, are systematically underutilized by healthcare professionals and exchange of patient information between domains is complicated [17,49–52]. Instead, the PCAM can be used for registering social data: we recently found evidence that PCAM has adequate measurement properties [34]. Nonetheless, the PCAM is not equally appreciated by PCPs as a conversation support tool, which limits its use and thus the registration of social data [34]. This corresponds with other studies demonstrating the inherent challenges in building instruments that support both a holistic conversation with patients and robust measurement of relevant patient data [34,53].

In line with other integrated care studies and the program theory of TARGET, which we previously elicited by means of realist evaluation, this study suggests that the contextual factors of team commitment and network ties influence the success of the program [19,54–59]. Time constraints were one important reason for the lack of team commitment to the program. However, it is likely that the innovation itself has also impacted commitment and thereby adoption [57,60]. Working from Roger’s diffusion of innovation theory [60], it appears that the relative advantage of TARGET compared to current practice (e.g. more attention for positive sides of health and well-being) has increased commitment to the program [60]. Within some of the participating practices however, some PCPs were doubtful about the value of TARGET, although most of them did not participate at all, hence could not fully estimate the relative advantage of the program. A characteristic of TARGET that may have decreased commitment is its complexity [60]. The tools and trainings enabled professionals to implement the program, but TARGET remains complex as it is a multicomponent program and contains relative flexibility to tailor the components to the particular practice’s needs [57]. Research shows that tailoring can positively impact commitment, but it also requires a time investment and creative thinking [57]. Especially when there is no team-based belief in the relative advantage of the program, this time investment may not be considered worthwhile.

With regards to the second contextual factor, strong network ties, scientific studies increasingly show that organizational strategies (like shared information systems) are equally important as ‘soft’ factors, like ‘trusting multidisciplinary team relationships’, to strengthen cooperation and referrals [57,61]. Besides, while many current integrated care studies mainly focus on how professionals can do better at cooperation, this study draws our attention to barriers to cooperation and referrals on a patient level, like resistance to be referred to other services, like mental healthcare [62–64]. Resistance to referrals was also suggested in previous studies, where among others, found that patients perceive the GP or practice nurse as more accessible and less stigmatizing than social care or more specialized mental healthcare [64–66]. For patients to become receptive to referrals, it is suggested to have an open conversation about the referral, provide information, and to make sure the referral is presented in an acceptable way and in line with the patient’s needs and expectations [64,66].

Implications

One of the strategies to enhance commitment to the program both within PC practices and the broader network could be to support professionals in tailoring the program. PCPs have shown different preferences as to how specific components and tool are implemented. The findings of this study can thus be a first source of inspiration for tailoring the program for PCPs who are new to the program. Furthermore, a digital forum may be developed, where professionals can inspire each other by sharing tips and asking implementation questions. Also, the need to support PC practices to enhance their network ties (TARGET’s third component) was underlined in this study. The next stage of the implementation and research will therefore focus more on inspiring practices with regional ‘best practices’ of how network ties can be enhanced as well as individual guidance to tailor network efforts to practices’ needs and circumstances. Related to this, it is needed to give professionals time and support to get used to their new role and interaction with patients in integrated care. Many professionals may still be highly ‘action-minded’ from a traditional physician point of view – demonstrated by the fact that some professionals are unsatisfied if a PCNA does not lead to a follow-up action or referral – but activating and motivating patients as a coach during such a PCNA can be considered an action and result in itself. Moreover, further research should focus more on how patients experience and influence cooperation and referrals in the context of integrated care, as this seems a relatively unexplored but important topic to improve effective referrals from PC. In addition to this, the impact and functioning of the program should be studied on a larger scale and in a realist evaluation study combining quantitative and qualitative methods [54,55]. Doing so can, among others, help to find out whether and how work pressure can actually be lowered on the longer run, as was expected by interviewed professionals in this study. Additionally, on a policy level, it is important to accommodate registration of and efficient exchange of social patient information, not in the least to design segmentation tools in a holistic way. As discussed by Truong et al. [51], the use of Z-codes may increase by providing more guidance on how and when to code them and incentivize utilization financially.

Conclusions

The TARGET integrated care program seems ready for the big stage: the two implemented program components are largely feasible and acceptable, and no major adjustments were suggested. To fully embed an integrated way of working in PC, the TARGET implementation process should be given more attention in the future, which means strengthening team commitment to the implementation and removing barriers for successful cooperation and referral.
