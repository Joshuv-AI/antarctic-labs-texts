Draw on advantages and avoid disadvantages: CT-derived individualized radiomic signature for predicting chemo-radiotherapy sensitivity in unresectable advanced non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Liping Yang, Mengyue Li, Yixin Liu, Zhiyun Jiang, Shichuan Xu, Hongchao Ding, Xing Gao, Shilong Liu, Lishuang Qi, Kezheng Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPresently, the options of concurrent chemo-radiotherapy (CCR) in patients with locally advanced non-small cell lung cancer (LA-NSCLC) are controversial and there is no reliable prediction tool to stratify poor- and good-responders. Although radiomic analysis has provided new opportunities for personalized medicine in oncological practice, the repeatability and reproducibility of radiomic features are critical challenges that hinder their widespread clinical adoption. This study aimed to develop a qualitative radiomic signature based on the within-sample rank of radiomics features, and to use this novel method to predict CCR sensitivity in LA-NSCLC, avoiding the variability of quantitative signatures to multicenter effect.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 125 patients with stage III NSCLC who received treatment from our hospital. Radiomic features were extracted from pretreatment plain CT scans and constructed as feature pairs based on their within-sample rank. Fisher and univariate Cox analyses were performed to select feature pairs significantly associated with patients’ overall survival (OS). NSCLC-Radiomic (R422) cohort including 104 NSCLC patients was used as an independent testing cohort. NSCLC-Radiogenomic (RG211) cohort with matched RNA sequencing profiles, was used for functional enrichment analysis to reveal the underlying biological mechanism reflected by the signature.ResultsA qualitative signature, consisting of 15 radiomic feature pairs (termed as 15-RiFPS), was developed based on the Genetic Algorithm, which could optimally distinguish responder from non-responder with significantly improved OS if they received CCR treatment (log-rank P = 0.0009, HR = 13.79, 95% CIs 1.83–104.1). The performance of 15-RiFPS was validated in an independent public cohort (log-rank P = 0.0037, HR = 2.40, 95% CIs 1.30–4.40). Furthermore, the transcriptomic analyses provided biological pathways (‘glutathione metabolic process’, ‘cellular oxidant detoxification’) underlying the signature.ConclusionsWe developed a CT-derived 15-RiFPS, which could potentially help predict individualized therapeutic benefit of CCR in patients with LA-NSCLC. Additionally, we investigated the underlying intra-tumoral biological characteristics behind 15-RiFPS which would accelerate its clinical application. This approach could be applied to a wider range of treatments and cancer types.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00432-024-05971-4.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer, Concurrent chemo-radiotherapy, Qualitative, Radiomics, Bioinformatic analyses

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the most common type of lung cancer with the greatest mortality, of which about 1/4 are locally advanced NSCLC (LA-NSCLC) (Bade and Dela Cruz 2020). Presently, international guidelines has recommended concurrent chemo-radiotherapy (CCR) as a standard first-line treatment option for LA-NSCLC patients (O'Rourke and Macbeth 2010). Although it has been demonstrated that the 5-year survival rate of LA-NSCLC patients receiving CCR has been greatly improved, the current situation is still not optimistic (Ruysscher et al. 2009). A series of studies have demonstrated that there is a subgroup of LA-NSCLC patients who do not benefit from CCR strategy (Liu et al. 2020). Currently, the efficacy evaluation of clinical treatments often employs the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) standard, which permits the comparison and classification of changes in tumor and lymph node diameters on computed tomography (CT) images before and after treatment. However, NSCLC frequently exhibits a relatively slow response to chemoradiotherapy, leading to a delay in efficacy evaluation. Some studies suggest that hematological indicators can be used to predict treatment response, but there is still a lack of large-scale and unified studies to confirm their reliability (Laar et al. 2020). Therefore, there is still a lack of effective biomarkers to accurately predict therapeutic sensitivity and efficiency in clinical practice.

Although artificial intelligence itself has been regarded as a black-box approach, it provides the access to investigate the correlation between imaging features with molecular mechanism of the disease, thus offering a closer correlative link with the deeply tumor biology (Ben Amar et al. 2016). Presently, radiomic analysis has provided insights for personalized medicine in oncological practice (Liu et al. 2019). As easily-obtainable and noninvasive prognostic biomarker, CT-derived radiomic signature can provide a more comprehensive view of the entire tumor, and has significant clinical potential without adding additional imaging studies (Gillies et al. 2016). Recent researches reported that radiomic signature had significant associations with underlying genomic patterns in various cancers, thus demonstrating strong prognostic performance (Davnall et al. 2016; Coroller et al. 2015; Huang et al. 2016). However, research of radiomic with respect to therapeutic benefits of CCR for NSCLC is still lacking.

Radiomic analysis has provided insights for personalized medicine in clinical practice, which integrates the comprehensive characterization of imaging phenotypes with machine learning algorithms, is increasingly recognized for its potential in the diagnosis and prognosis of oncological and non-oncological conditions. However, the repeatability and reproducibility of radiomic features are critical challenges that hinder their widespread clinical adoption. For instance, Rai and colleagues have investigated the repeatability and reproducibility of MRI-based radiomic features in rectal cancer. The results showed that even when using a consistent imaging protocol many radiomic features were unstable. Therefore, caution must be taken when selecting features for potential imaging biomarkers (Teng et al. 2024; Rai et al. 2022). To overcome this challenge, various researches has been conducted to apply data standardization (for instance, Combat harmonization method) to reduce the adverse impact of batch effects arising from multicenter. However, the risk classification of this kind of signature is greatly affected by the risk composition of other samples adopted for data standardization, as demonstrated in our previous study (Qi et al. 2016).

To evade this limitation, this study aimed to develop a CT-derived qualitative signature based on rank-based approach, which was robust against multicenter effects. In addition, this novel method was applied to stratify patients who might benefit from CCR, guiding the individualized treatment and avoiding these side effects of unnecessary treatment. Besides demonstrating the clinical usefulness of this qualitative signature, we also wanted to explore the biological underpinning of the radiomic features in this signature by investigating the available matched gene expression profile data.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). Our study was reviewed, approved and overseen by the institutional review board of Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital. Ethical approval was obtained for this retrospective study, and the need of written informed consent was waived. Inclusion criteria included (1) histologically confirmed NSCLC; (2) locally advanced NSCLC refers to stage III NSCLC according to AJCC 8th staging criteria; (3) standard CT scans were performed within 4 weeks prior to CCR treatment; (4) definitive concurrent chemo-radiotherapy was administered; (5) stay followed-up no less than 6 months since the start of chemo-radiotherapy treatment (unless death or disease progression was documented); (6) complete clinical records. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) insufficient image quality, such as artifacts; (2) treatment history of thoracic surgery; (3) the history of anti-tumor or anti-inflammatory therapy. Therapeutic regimen and follow-up

All patients were treated with concurrent CCR. Chemotherapy regimen included carboplatin + paclitaxel, cisplatin + etoposide and carboplatin + pemetrexed. The radiotherapy was delivered by three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy technique with a prescribed dose of 60–66 Gy. The primary endpoints of this study were overall survival (OS), which was defined as the time from the date of the initiation of CCR treatment until either the date of death or until the date that the patient was last known to be alive (censored). The follow-up period for OS was 60 months after the date of diagnosis. In our study, treatment response was evaluated by assessing the relative change in diameter between baseline and follow-up, using RECIST 1.1 criteria. Patients were divided into complete response (CR) group and non-CR group including of the status of partial response (PR), stable disease (SD) or progressive disease (PD) according to RECIST guideline.

Image acquisition and data pre-processing

The plain CT scans of “blinded” cohort were performed by using a LightSpeed VCT (manufactured by GE Medical Systems, USA). Specific scan parameters were as follows: tube voltage 100–120 kV, tube current 350–500 mA, slice thickness 3 mm, reconstruction interval 3 mm, matrix size 512 × 512 and field of view 450 mm. For patients who underwent contrast-enhanced CT (CE-CT), the non-ionic contrast media (Iohexeol, 300 mg/ml, GE, Boston, USA) was administered at a rate of 2.5–3.0 ml/s and 1.2 ml/kg.

Image segmentation and feature extraction

3D slicer software (version 4.8.1) was used for three-dimensional manual CT image segmentation. Tumor segmentation was independently performed by two experienced radiologists (both of whom have more than 10 years of practical experience in chest CT) without knowing the pathological results. The contoured volume of interests (VOIs) were checked and modified by another expert radiologist with 15 years of experience. Each VOIs was manually contoured along the boundary of the whole tumor. Before performing feature extraction, an extraction intensity bin width was set at 25 Hounsfield units and 3D VOIs were interpolated to a voxel size of 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 to normalize the voxel spacing. Radiomic features for each CT scan with VOIs were extracted using PyRadiomics software (version 2.0.1). The quantitative values of 1315 radiomic features were used to characterize the intra-tumoral complexity, including tumor intensity, shape, texture, wavelet and Laplacian Gaussian features.

Qualitative radiomic signature construction

First, Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to identify the response-related radiomic features whose values was significantly difference between CR and non-CR samples in the training cohort.

Then, the response-related features were ranked according to their values from low to high in each sample, and a qualitative feature pair (FP) matrix was constructed by combining these features in pairs and comparing their ranks (Ra and Rb with a ≠ b), where 1 represents Ra > Rb and 0 represents Ra ≦ Rb. For each FP, Fisher exact test and univariate Cox regression model was used to select the candidate prognostic-related FPs, whose relative ranks (e.g., Ra > Rb) were significantly different between CR and non-CR groups, and associated with OS of patients receiving CCR. For a prognostic-related FP, if its specific rank pattern (Ra > Rb) was associated with longer OS, this pattern in a sample would characterize it as a responder and the other rank pattern (Ra ≦ Rb) would characterize it as a non-responder.

Finally, we adopted four machine-learning methods, including Forward algorithm (FA), Backward algorithm (BA), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression (LASSO) and Genetic algorithm (GA), to construct an optimal predictive model for OS from the candidate prognostic-related FPs (Haixiao et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2023; Fernandez et al. 2011). The details of the machine learning methods were added in the Supplementary Methods. In brief, the samples in the training cohort were randomly split into discovery (training set size 80%) and inter-validation (the remaining 20%) sets, and the entire procedure was repeated for 10 rounds. The concordance index (C-index) value was used to evaluate the predictive performance of each model for patients’ OS. The method with the highest average C-index value in the 10 round inter-validations was selected to develop a radiomic feature pair signature (RiFPS) for CCR response based on all the 125 samples in the training cohort. The majority voting rule was adopted as follows: a sample was a responder or non-responder if more than half of the rank pattern of radiomic feature pairs in the signature voted for response or non-response.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0.3). Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and were compared using a log-rank test. A univariate Cox regression model was employed to analyze the associations between the factors and OS, and a multivariate Cox regression model was used to test the independent association of the radiomic signature with OS after adjusting for the OS-related clinical parameters recorded in the data. Hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were generated using Cox regression models. The concordance index (C-index) and the area under the time-dependent ROC curve (AUC) were used to estimate the prognostic performance of the radiomic signature. The decision curve analysis was conducted to evaluate its clinical usefulness. Spearman rank correlation was used to identify the genes related with the risk classification of the radiomic signature, and cumulative hypergeometric distribution was used to perform functional enrichment analysis. The functional terms were downloaded from the Gene Ontology database (GO, https://geneontology.org/).

P values were adjusted using Benjamini–Hochberg procedure for multiple testing to control the false discovery rate (FDR). The statistical significance was defined as two-side P < 0.05 or FDR < 0.05 for multiple testing.

Results

Baseline information

The training cohort (n = 125) were retrospectively reviewed and collected from the stage-III NSCLC patients with plain CT scans (Jul 2012 to May 2016), including 22 CR patients and 103 non-CR patients (PR, n = 62, SD, n = 22; PD, n = 19). Follow-up information was collected from the hospital’s information system.

Following the same criteria for the training cohort, an independent testing cohort (R422) cohort including 104 stage III NSCLC patients receiving CCR was downloaded from the Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA, https://www.cancerimagingarchive.net/, 2020). NSCLC-Radiogenomic (RG211) cohort, including 113 patients regardless of treatment strategy, had pre-treatment CT scans and RNA sequencing profiles (generated by an Illumina HiSeq 2500 system) before treatment, was used for biological analyses. Identification of a predictive RiFPS for effective CCR treatment

The workflow of this study is shown in Fig. 2. Firstly, in the training cohort, 371 candidate response-related features were identified as their feature values were significantly different between CR and non-CR patients (Wilcoxon rank sum test, P < 0.05). Then, we constructed FP matrix, and identified 139 response-related FPs, whose rank pattern (eg. Ra > Rb) were potentially significantly associated with response status (Fisher exact test, P < 0.05) and OS of patients receiving CCR (univariate Cox model, P < 0.05). Finally, the GA method outperformed the other three methods (FA, LASSO and BA), with the highest average C-index value of 0.74 in the inter-validation datasets after 10 rounds of sampling (Fig. 3A). Therefore, GA method was selected to develop a radiomic signature based on all the 125 samples in the training cohort. The predictive signature for CCR benefit included 15 FPs (15-RiFPS, Table 2), whose cut-off was set as 7.5 for classification; that is a sample was predicted as a responder or non-responder if more than 7.5 FPs in the signature voted for CCR responder or non-responder.

In the training cohort, according to the cut-off, 60 LA-NSCLC patients were stratified into a CCR-response group, and 65 patients were stratified into a CCR-non-response group with significantly different OS when they received CCR treatment (log-rank P = 0.0009, HR = 13.79, 95% CIs: 1.83–104.10, C-index = 0.74, Fig. 3B). The 3-year survival rate for patients predicted to have a CCR-response was 88.89%, which was obviously higher than the rate (68.36%) for patients predicted to have a CCR-non-response. Calibration curves of 15-RiFPS for 3-year survival, were displayed in Fig. 3C. Figure 3D shown the time-dependent ROC curve of 15-RiFPS under 3 years (AUC = 0.65). We performed multivariate COX analysis for 15-RiFPS and clinical prognostic factors (P < 0.05, Univariate Cox analysis) together and found that 15-RiFPS was an independent factor after adjusting T stage, N stage, overall stage, Lymph vascular invasion, smoking status and degree of differentiation (Table 3, Fig. 3E). Additionally, 15-RiFPS could distinguish responders and non-responders with significantly different OS in squamous cell carcinoma (log-rank P = 0.0072, Fig. 3F) and adenocarcinoma patients receiving CCR (log-rank P = 0.0598, Fig. 3G), respectively.

Validation of 15-RiFPS

One public independent cohort (R422) with 104 stage III patients receiving CCR was used for validation. In R422 cohort, 77 stage III patients who were classified into a response group by 15-RiFPS had significantly longer OS than the 27 patients classified into the non-response group (log-rank P = 0.004, HR = 2.40, 95% CIs: 1.30–4.40, Fig. 4A). Calibration curves of 15-RiFPS for 3-year survival, in R422 cohort, were displayed in Fig. 4B. The AUC values of 15-RiFPS at 3-year were 0.67 in R422 cohort (Fig. 4C). The risk scores of patients and their OS in R422 cohort are displayed in Fig. 4D. 15-RiFPS also exhibited favorable performance in predicting OS of ADC (log-rank P = 0.014, HR = 4.55, 95% CIs: 1.22–16.93, Fig. 4E) and SCC patients receiving CCR in R422 cohort (log-rank P = 0.0600, HR = 1.98, 95% CIs: 0.96–4.10, Fig. 4F), respectively. Univariate COX analysis showed that the clinical factors recorded in this cohort was not significantly associated with patients’ OS (all P > 0.05, Table 4).

Exploration of biological functions for CCR non-responders

First, we applied 15-RiFPS to the RG211 cohort with paired radiomic and transcriptomic data to estimate the risk scores of patients. Using Spearman rank correlation analysis, we identified 747 genes significantly correlated with risk scores and found that they were enriched in six GO terms (Hypergeometric distribution model, FDR < 0.05, Fig. 5A), including “glutathione metabolic process”, “cellular oxidant detoxification”, “cellular detoxification”, “cellular response to toxic substance”, “xenobiotic metabolic process” and “detoxification”. Details of functional enrichment analysis of genes related with 15-RiFPS are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Next, we constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of the signature-related genes in the enriched terms, and demonstrated that MGST1, GPX4, GSTA1 which have been reported to be related with CCR (Zhang et al. 2023; Gisbergen et al. 2016), were significantly associated with the scores of our signature, supporting our signature reflecting the intra-tumoral molecular characteristics.

Discussion

This study developed a first-of-its-kind CT-derived individualized radiomic signature (15-RiFPS), which is predictive of the therapeutic benefit of CCR treatment in patients with LA-NSCLC. The signature could determine a patient’s response status for CCR just based on the relative rank of 15 radiomic FPs, circumventing the clinical impracticality of quantitative radiomic signatures induced by multicenter batch effect. The signature was further validated in one external public cohort, demonstrating the robustness and clinical applicability of this methodology. This approach could be appied to a wider range of treatments and cancer types. Furthermore, transcriptomic analysis was successfully performed to elucidate the underlying intra-tumoral biological characteristics behind 15-RiFPS.

To provide additional support in patient management and achieve maximum clinical benefits, signatures need to be predictive of the potential benefit of administering CCR. So far, most researchers have developed prognostic radiomic signatures based on survival of patients without receiving CCR or adjuvant chemotherapy (ACT) and then have demonstrated that only the high-risk patients predicted by the signature showed significant survival benefit after CCR or ACT (Vaidya et al. 2020; Sun et al. 2019). Obviously, such prognostic signatures were just able to identify patients with poor prognoses who need CCR or ACT, but unable to identify patients who might be sensitive to the further treatment. In contrast, we developed a qualitative radiomic signature (15-RiFPS) based on both response status and OS of patients receiving CCR, which could identify patients who benefit from CCR. Limited by the public data source, we selected OS of patients receiving CCR as outcome to validate the survival benefit of response patients predicted by 15-RiFPS based on the findings that the response patients would have longer OS than the non-response patients when they revived CCR (Supplementary Fig. 1). In the independent public cohort, the predicted CCR-response by 15-RiFPS had significantly longer OS than the predicted CCR-non-response patients when they received CCR treatment. The 1-year survival rate and 3-year survival rate for the predicted CCR-response patients were 73.07% and 30.12%, respectively, obviously higher than the 1-year survival rate (46.80%) and 3-year survival rate (0.00%) for patients predicted to have a CCR-non-response, supporting that the signature could identify the patients who benefit from CCR. The further large-scale cohort recording both response status to CCR and OS should be collected for validation. Besides the plain CT images, the predictive performance of this signature was also successfully validated in patients with CE-CT images in the validation R422 cohort (log-rank P = 0.0002, HR = 5.01, 95% CIs: 1.98–12.68, Supplementary Fig. 2), indicating the universality of 15-RiFPS for CT scans, which merits further validation on a large sample size.

More important, to circumvent the clinical dilemma of quantitative radiomic signature, this study developed a rank-based qualitative radiomic signature, which was robustness to the multicenter batch effects and could be applied to an individual. Limited researches so far have investigated the role of CT-based radiomics analysis in predicting the therapeutic benefit of CCR treatment in NSCLC patients. Therefore, we additionally developed a quantitative signature, consisting of 10 radiomic features (termed as 10-RiFS) for CCR sensitivity in the training cohort (Supplementary method descriptions, Supplementary Fig. 3A). However, it does not work in the testing R422 cohort based on original quantitative feature values (log-rank P = 0.5081, Supplementary Fig. 3B). Even using the normalized feature values with Combat harmonization for removing the multicenter effects, the results are still not ideal (log-rank P = 0.4517, Supplementary Fig. 3C). What is clear from these analyses is that the qualitative radiomic signature we developed demonstrated powerful stability and generalization for universal application.

A radiomic signature that is precisely linked to the underlying progression would accelerate its clinical application (Yan et al. 2020). In this study, we developed and validated 15-RiFPS using the CT images before treatment, which aims to predict the CCR benefit in patients with LA-NSCLCs and guide them receiving CCR or not before treatment. Therefore, the difference in response state of patients predicted by the signature should reflect their underlying difference in molecular characteristics within tumors, independent of the patients' subsequent treatment. We applied 15-RiFPS to the all the LA-NSCLC patients in RG211 cohort with paired radiomic and transcriptomic data to identify the radiomic signature-related genes, and found that these genes were significantly enriched in ‘glutathione metabolic process’, ‘cellular oxidant detoxification’ and etc. Previous reports demonstrates that Glutamine metabolism influences DNA damage responses by mechanisms such as synthesis of glutathione for ROS detoxification, and meidates CCR resistance (Chung et al. 2009; Kery and Papandreou 2020). Among the signature-related genes, GPX4 is a critical gene that could interacted with several genes and participated in multiple enriched functions. GPX4 is an important regulatory molecule for ferroptosis and a potential biomarker for epithelial mesenchymal transition in tumor cells (Yang et al. 2014). Research has found that GPX4 mRNA and protein expression are significantly increased in colorectal cancer cell lines resistant to radiotherapy, suggesting that GPX4 is involved in the formation of radiotherapy resistance in colorectal cancer cells (Hangauer et al. 2017). GPX4 is also being induced by the hypoxia inducible factor HIF1 leading to extracellular matrix remodeling, and plays a role in enhanced migration and cell proliferation as well as decreased cisplatinum-induced apoptosis (Zhang et al. 2022). Another signature-related gene, MGST1, has also been shown to be involved in cisplatinum resistance and a shortened progression-free survival for several tumor entities with higher expression (Kuang et al. 2021). Although we mainly focused on molecular analysis at the transcriptome level, these findings shed important light on the underlying biology behind the radiomic signature (15-RiFPS), which may ultimately inform prevention and treatment strategies.

There are still some limitations. Firstly, the ranks of some feature pairs were complete consistent in all the samples because their range varies widely, thus this kind of feature pairs without classification ability would be filtered out by the Fisher’s exact test. Although the qualitative signatures consisting of feature pairs might lose some radiomic features, this kind signatures could predict the CCR benefits for each patient based on its CT image alone, which is more suitable for clinic. Secondly, three study cohorts are significantly different in histology of the LA-NSCLC (squamous carcinoma and adenocarcinoma), thus prospective study with larger sample size is still needed to provide additional sufficient evidence for clinical application. Thirdly, as tumor segmentation was performed in a manual manner, the exploitation of a more efficient method for tumor segmentation remains an important consideration.

Conclusions

Our results demonstrate that the qualitative radiomic signature we developed could individually stratify LA-NSCLC patients into response and non-response groups before treatment. Last but not least, the underlying biological basis of the selected features was proved to be associated with complex tumor signal pathways, which facilitates a deeper understanding and confidence in the correlation between molecular biological characteristics and qualitative radiomic signatures. Collectively, the robustness, generalization, biological interpretability and low-cost of this qualitative radiomic signature highlight its advantage for widespread clinical translation.
