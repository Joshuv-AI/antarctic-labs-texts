Environmentally Friendly Waterborne Polymer/Reduced Graphene Oxide Nanocomposite Anticorrosion Coatings for Q235 Carbon Steel
Authors: Siti Humairah Harun, Yasemin Fadil, Yingna Ding, Dipan Kundu, Per B. Zetterlund, Vipul Agarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCorrosion severely affects marine metallic infrastructures. Organic coatings offer a low‐cost, effective solution, but their solvent‐rich processing reduces sustainability. For polymer nanocomposite coatings, additional chemical modification of fillers is typically required to improve dispersibility. Herein, we report the synthesis of waterborne polymer/reduced graphene oxide (rGO) nanocomposites via a miniemulsion polymerization as an anticorrosion coating for Q235 steel. Using a vinyl copolymer based on styrene and n‐butyl acrylate, we successfully formulated kinetically stable latexes with inherent film‐forming ability at room temperature. The thermally annealed polymer/rGO coatings formed a uniform, defect‐free layer on the steel surface without rGO agglomeration and delamination. These polymer/rGO coatings, particularly at pH 10 demonstrated electrical conductivity, mechanical robustness, and highly effective anticorrosion performance. Polymer/rGO coating of pH 10 exhibited enhanced corrosion protection after 168 h exposure to 3.5 wt.% NaCl solution ((I corr = 0.01 µA cm−2, P.E of 99%), outperforming the corresponding pH 2, neat polymer coating, and bare metal, validated by post‐immersion analysis and marine field testing. This organic solvent‐free type of protective coating, comprising vinyl polymer and unmodified rGO represents a novel approach towards the development of advanced anticorrosion coating for critical metals.

Introduction

Marine architectures such as offshore platforms, pipelines, and terminals are highly susceptible to corrosion due to prolonged exposure to harsh marine environments. Among various corrosion mitigation strategies, the adoption of organic polymer coatings as a passive barrier on a metal substrate has gained widespread attention due to low cost, ease of processing, and effective corrosion protection [1]. The barrier effect provided by a polymer coating on a metal substrate can physically block the diffusion of corrosion ions, ultimately preventing corrosion initiation. A wide range of polymers, including epoxy [2], PDMS [3], and polyurethane (PU) [4], have been extensively employed as base matrices for anti‐corrosion coatings due to their excellent mechanical strength and chemical resistance. Intrinsically conductive polymers such as polyaniline (PANI) and polypyrrole (PPy) have also shown promising corrosion protection effectiveness by promoting the formation of a passive oxide layer on metal surfaces facilitated by their redox activity [5]. However, despite expansive studies on these polymers, persistent issues such as coating brittleness, delamination, and defect‐induced failures, especially under mechanical or environmental stress which severely limit their long‐term durability [6, 7]. Vinyl polymers such as polystyrene (PS) and acrylic polymers have also been explored as alternatives in the development of protective coatings. Yet, their synthesis typically requires organic solvents and harsh conditions, raising environmental and safety concerns [8, 9]. As a more sustainable solution, waterborne emulsion‐based polymerization has emerged as a promising technique to prepare polymer nanocomposite coatings. This method is a compelling alternative to existing melt blending and solution processing approaches as it uses water as the dispersion medium and operates under relatively mild conditions. Miniemulsion polymerization in particular is highly attractive due to its ability to promote better polymer‐filler interaction and compatibility with a wide range of polymers.

Graphene and its derivatives have attracted significant attention in the development of anti‐corrosion coatings due to their impermeability and high aspect ratio, which provide excellent barrier properties. Graphene oxide (GO), an oxygen‐rich graphene derivative, offers better functionality, compatibility, and dispersibility in a polymer matrix than pristine graphene due to its amphiphilicity, making it a great candidate as reinforcement filler in polymer‐based anti‐corrosion coatings. Numerous studies have demonstrated the application of GO in protective coatings with effective corrosion suppression effects [10]. In particular, reduced graphene oxide (rGO) has been reported to have better impermeability to gases, liquids, salts, and acids than GO [11]. This is supported by studies conducted by Chang et al. [12], and Kang et al. [13], which demonstrated that rGO can effectively block the penetration of gaseous compounds and reduce the rate of metal oxidation due to its impermeability. Additionally, very recent studies have further confirmed the effectiveness of rGO as an anti‐corrosion inhibitor in polymeric coatings [12, 13]. GO can be easily reduced to rGO by various methods to restore its intrinsic graphitic properties [14]. Due to its high surface area and strong van der Waal's interactions, GO/rGO have a strong tendency to form agglomerations within a polymer matrix, especially in highly viscous polymers such as epoxies. Such agglomeration may lead to the formation of pores or microdefects in a coating, compromising barrier performance. Consequently, most early studies focused on chemical or surface modification of GO/rGO to enhance their dispersibility and interaction with the polymer [15]. For instance, rGO modified with PANI has been reported to promote improved compatibility with epoxy resin through strong π‐π interactions between PANI and rGO sheets, resulting in effective corrosion protection [16, 17]. The decorations of rGO/GO with inorganic materials have also been demonstrated as a useful approach to prevent corrosion. Functionalization of rGO with zinc, as reported by Teng et al. [20], and Zhu et al. [21], has been shown to enable a dual protection mechanism in anti‐corrosion coatings. The interaction between rGO and zinc not only creates a physical barrier but also imparts a chemical passivation effect through active corrosion protection owing to the less noble nature of zinc [18, 19]. On the other hand, titanium oxide (TiO) is widely utilised as a pigment in the manufacturing of commercial protective paint. Several studies have demonstrated that rGO decorated with TiO nanoparticles promotes enhanced barrier performance [20], and overcomes micropore formation in epoxy‐based anti‐corrosive coatings [21]. Other materials such as silica and clay have also been investigated as modification agents for rGO in anti‐corrosive coatings [22, 23]. It is noteworthy that these studies require the functionalization of rGO, thus increasing the complexity of the fabrication process and limiting industrial implementation. Direct GO/rGO incorporation in coatings has been reported; however, very limited explorations have been conducted using polymers other than epoxy. Moreover, direct incorporation of GO/rGO requires a high volume of volatile organic solvents to disperse it in a polymer matrix, which remains a critical challenge [24, 25]. The presence of organic solvent in the coating is not only harmful but can also induce the formation of micropores upon solvent evaporation, which further suppress performance [26]. Therefore, there has been growing interest towards waterborne anticorrosion coatings, which has been predominantly based on water‐based PU and epoxy. For example, Wu et al. [4], and Cao et al. [27], prepared waterborne PU loaded with various fillers as anticorrosion coatings and observed a significant improvement in anticorrosion performance compared to neat PU coatings. In other studies, waterborne epoxy/CeO2/multiwalled CNT/8‐hydroxyquinoline sulfonic acid (8‐HQS) based anticorrosion nanocomposite coatings were prepared and compared against the neat waterborne epoxy coating. These nanocomposite coatings exhibited enhanced anticorrosion performance after immersion in 3.5% NaCl compared to the neat epoxy coating [28, 29]. There has been an interest in formulating anticorrosion coatings based on alternative polymers via a fully waterborne approach. For instance, vinyl polymer‐based anticorrosion coatings have been prepared using emulsion‐based polymerisation methods [9, 30]. However, these studies have generally relied on modified rGO and require organic solvents during coating formation. To overcome such limitations, preparation of polymer nanocomposite anticorrosion coatings using unmodified rGO via our well‐established, fully waterborne approach is highly attractive. This simple and eco‐friendly strategy has been previously demonstrated by us to enable precise control over nanofiller dispersion within polymer nanocomposite coatings without the need for either filler modification or organic solvents [31, 32, 33, 34]. In this work, we present a fully waterborne strategy for anticorrosion nanocomposite coatings preparation that eliminates the dependence on both pre‐modification of rGO and the need of organic solvents.

In the present work, we have investigated the synthesis of waterborne polymer nanocomposites comprising vinyl copolymers (styrene (St) and n‐butyl acrylate (nBA)) at St:nBA (40:60) mass ratio and unmodified rGO as the nanofiller via a one‐pot in situ miniemulsion polymerization. Polymer/GO nanocomposites obtained from miniemulsion polymerization were spray‐coated onto Q235 steel and thermally annealed to form polymer/rGO anticorrosion coatings. Tafel analysis confirmed effective anticorrosion performance of polymer/rGO coatings, especially at pH 10, showing a 99% reduction in i corr and a positive shift in E corr. Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) demonstrated a significant increase in the R ct and |Z|0.01Hz of pH 10 coating over 168 h of exposure to 3.5 wt.% NaCl solution. Anticorrosion properties of the polymer/rGO coatings were further validated through post‐immersion analysis and marine field testing. Overall, the present results demonstrate that the developed organic solvent‐free protective coatings formulated from vinyl polymers and unmodified rGO offer a novel and advanced strategy for developing anticorrosion coatings. The developed coatings can be applied in organic solvent‐sensitive environments, thus avoiding the potential fire risk.

Experimental Section

Materials

Styrene (St, Sigma–Aldrich, 99%) and n‐butyl acrylate (nBA, Sigma–Aldrich, 99%) were purified by passing through a column of activated basic aluminium oxide (Ajax) to remove the inhibitor. Azobisisobutyronitrile in acetone (AIBN, Sigma–Aldrich) was recrystallized in water prior to use. Hexadecane (HD, Sigma–Aldrich, 99%), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, Ajax Finechem), and graphene oxide powder (GO, Graphenea), ammonia solution (NH4OH, Sigma–Aldrich, 30%) were used as received. All experiments were carried out using Milli‐Q water. Q235 carbon steel (composition in Table S1) and dimensions of 5 cm × 5 cm were used as the metal substrate for corrosion studies.

Preparation of Neat Polymer Latex by Miniemulsion Polymerization

Miniemulsion latex comprising of St and nBA (P(St‐stat‐nBA), denoted as neat latex) at comonomer mass ratio St:nBA (40:60) was selected to enable nanocomposite film formation at room temperature directly from the latex, based on the theoretical glass transition temperature (T g) of −10.5°C, calculated from the Fox equation [35]. The organic phase containing monomers (10 wt.% relative to water), AIBN (0.25 m relative to organic phase), and hexadecane (5 wt.% relative to monomer) was mixed with the aqueous phase comprised of water (15 mL) and SDS (1 wt.% relative to organic phase). Ultrasonication was performed under ice for 10 min at an amplitude of 50%. The resulting miniemulsion was degassed under nitrogen purge for 20 min in an ice bath, followed by polymerization at 70°C for 24 h under constant stirring at 500 rpm.

Preparation of Polymer/GO Latex by Miniemulsion Polymerization

Miniemulsion latex with GO (P(St‐stat‐nBA)/GO, denoted as polymer/GO latex), was prepared using our typical protocol [31, 33, 36]. GO sheets with size range of 6‐28 µm (manufacturer size) were used (Figure S1). Briefly, a fixed amount of GO powder (5 wt.% relative to total monomer) was dispersed in 15 mL of water and ultrasonicated for 10 min on ice (Branson Digital Sonifier) at 70% amplitude to produce the aqueous phase. The organic phase containing AIBN (0.25 m relative to the organic phase), HD (5 wt.% relative to total monomer), SDS (1 wt.% relative to the organic phase), and monomers (St and nBA, total monomer of 10 wt.% relative to water), was prepared separately. The two phases were mixed by magnetic stirring for 15 min at 500 rpm, followed by ultrasonication for 10 min on ice at 70% amplitude. The obtained miniemulsion was degassed by nitrogen bubbling for 20 min, followed by polymerization in an oil bath at 70°C for 24 h under constant stirring. The as‐synthesised polymer/GO latex is denoted as pH 2 latex. A pH 10 latex was prepared by adding a few drops of ammonia solution to the polymer/GO latex. The miniemulsion recipes are listed in Table 1. Monomer conversions were determined by gravimetry after drying the latex in a vacuum oven at 30°C overnight.

Preparation of Neat and Polymer/rGO Coatings on Metal Substrates

Q235 grade low‐carbon steel was selected as the metal substrate for coating preparation (Figure S2 right). The metal was polished with 1000 mesh sandpaper and subsequently cleaned with ethanol to remove the surface oxide layer. The neat and polymer/GO (pH 2 and pH 10) latexes from miniemulsion polymerization were spray‐coated onto the substrates to form coatings with a thickness between 50–80 µm (spray conditions: pressure 5–30 psi, distance 5–10 cm, and approximately 30 layers). The polymer/GO coatings were allowed to dry at ambient temperature for 24 h before thermal annealing in an oven at 160°C for 24 h to reduce GO to rGO. The neat polymer‐coated metal is denoted as neat coating, whereas the polymer/rGO‐coated metal is denoted as pH 2 coating and pH 10 coating, respectively.

Characterizations

Gel Permeation Chromatography (GPC)

Number‐average (M n) and weight‐average (M w) molecular weights of polymers were determined by gel permeation chromatography (GPC). The system was equipped with a DGU‐12A solvent degasser, an LC‐10AT pump, a CTO‐10A column oven, an ECR 7515‐A refractive index detector, and a Polymer Laboratories 5.0 µm bead size guard column (50 mm × 7.8 mm), followed by four linear Phenogel columns (300 mm × 7.8 mm). Tetrahydrofuran (THF) was used as the eluent with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, with an injection volume of 100 µL, maintained at 40°C. The system was calibrated against linear polystyrene (PS) standards ranging from 500 g/mol to 106 g/mol. Polymer samples (4–5 mg) were dissolved in 2 mL of THF and filtered using a GPC syringe filter (0.45 µm PTFE membrane) to remove GO and other impurities prior to analysis.

Dynamic Light Scattering

Hydrodynamic diameters of monomer droplets and particles were measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS, Malvern Zetasizer Nanoseries). A drop of miniemulsion and latexes after polymerisation were diluted with water before measurement. Zeta potentials were measured using the same instrument and latex samples. Measurements were performed based on three runs with 12 sub‐measurements. Acquired data are reported as average values.

Raman Spectroscopy

Raman analysis was conducted on reduced polymer/rGO and neat GO films cast on glass slides, using a Renishaw in Via Qontor Raman Microscope with 514 nm argon laser. Spectra were recorded in the range of 0 to 4000 cm−1 with ten cumulative scans. The intensity ratio (ID/IG) was calculated after peak deconvolution using Origin software.

X‐Ray Diffraction Analysis (XRD)

X‐ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis was carried out on polymer/rGO and neat GO films casted on glass slides using a PANalytical Empryean IV machine with 5 kW rotating anode, 2θ range between 5° to 90°, and step size of 0.01°/s. XRD was also conducted on both bare and coated metal after immersion in 3.5 wt.% NaCl solution for 168 h, using a PANalytical Empryean II instrument equipped with a cobalt anode, 2θ range between 10° to 120°, and step width of 0.01°/s.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

SEM images were acquired via FEI Nova Nano SEM 230 at 5 mm working and 5 kV accelerating voltage. Samples were prepared by spray coating glass slides. For cross‐sectional analysis, spray‐coated glass slides were sectioned and mounted vertically on SEM stubs. All SEM samples were sputter‐coated with 10 nm platinum prior to imaging.

Water Contact Angle (WCA)

Water contact angles (WCA) were measured by depositing a 10 µL water droplet onto a spray‐coated glass slide. The contact angles were analysed using ImageJ software, and reported values were the average of three measurements.

Electrical Conductivities

Linear Sweep Voltammetry (LSV) was conducted on polymer/rGO coating casted on glass slides using VSP potentiostat (Biologic), performed within ±1 V with a voltage scan rate of 50 mV/s. Electrical conductivities were calculated following the method reported by us previously [36, 37]. The thicknesses of films were measured using a stylus profilometer (α‐Step D‐600), with the reference point set as the glass surface. Samples for LSV were prepared by casting the film on a glass slide using 50 µL of latex and spread evenly to create a square film with dimensions 15 mm × 15 mm. These films were allowed to dry at ambient temperature and thermally annealed to reduce GO to rGO. Two ends of the films were sputter‐coated with 100 nm gold to create the electrodes for LSV measurements. Data obtained from the instrument were plotted as current (A) versus potential (V), where the linear slope of the plot represents 1/R.

Uniaxial Tensile Tests

Mechanical properties of the nanocomposite coatings were evaluated using a Mark‐10 ESM303 tensile tester. Films were cast on silicone molds and trimmed to 5 mm × 20 mm dimensions. Neat polymer and thermally reduced polymer/rGO films were used as samples for the tensile test. Films were clamped at both ends and stretched at a rate of 20 mm min−1 until fracture. Representative stress–strain curves were plotted based on the measured data.

Electrochemical Testing

Electrochemical performance was investigated by potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) using a VMP‐3 electrochemical workstation (Biologic). All of the electrochemical tests were conducted in 3.5 wt.% NaCl solution using a three‐electrode corrosion cell consisting of saturated calomel as the reference electrode, graphite as the counter electrode, and bare or coated metal as the working electrode. The effective working electrode area was 1 cm2. The corrosion cell setup is shown in Figure S2. Tafel polarization curves were recorded by scanning the potential ± 0.10 V relative to the open circuit potential (OCP) at a scan rate of 1 mV/s. EIS experiments were carried out in the frequency range of 10 mHz to 1 MHz, and applied sinusoidal voltage of 50 mV. Impedance measurement was performed once on each sample after 1 and 168 h of immersion. Both Tafel and EIS were performed at room temperature. EIS data were plotted as Nyquist plot and fitted using Zfit function in EC lab software to obtain the EIS parameters.

Marine Field Test

Marine field testing was conducted at Port Botany, Sydney (33.956583° S, 151.197667° E). Bare and fully coated Q235 metals were immersed in seawater for 168 h under ambient conditions.

Results and Discussion

Miniemulsion Latex Synthesis

Polymer nanocomposites comprising P(St‐stat‐nBA), with and without GO, were synthesised via miniemulsion polymerization (Table 1). A schematic illustration of latex synthesis and coating preparation is shown in Figure 1. In miniemulsion polymerization, particle nucleation takes place within submicron‐sized monomer droplets, which differs from the mechanism of an emulsion polymerization where particles form in the aqueous phase [38]. Our previous investigations demonstrated that copolymerization of St and nBA in miniemulsion in the presence of GO (which acts as a surfactant) produces colloidally stable latexes with inherent film‐forming ability at ambient temperature and pressure [31, 32, 39]. The weight ratio of St to nBA was kept at 40:60, yielding a theoretical T g of −10.5°C as calculated from the Fox equation [35]. This ratio was selected to allow coating formation directly from the latex at room temperature, reduce the brittleness of the coatings, and boost the mechanical properties. AIBN‐initiated miniemulsion polymerization was conducted at 10% solids content at 70°C for 24 h in the presence of SDS as supplementary surfactant to improve the colloidal stability and increase the polymerization rate [40]. A neat P(St‐stat‐nBA) latex without GO was prepared as a control to study the effect of GO/rGO presence in nanocomposite coatings on the anti‐corrosion behaviour. The pH of as‐synthesised miniemulsion P(St‐stat‐nBA)/GO latex was 2. To explore the effect of pH on anticorrosion performance, the latex pH was adjusted to 10, based on previous literature reporting improved corrosion under alkaline conditions [41, 42]. As shown in Figure 2a–c, all latexes exhibited kinetic stability, with no visible sign of phase separation or coagulation after polymerization. The stability of the latexes was further supported by zeta potential measurements. The pH 2 latex exhibited a zeta potential of −32 mV, and pH 10 latex exhibited zeta potential of −49 mV (Table S3). The lower zeta potential of pH 10 latex compared to pH 2 is attributed to deprotonation of GO at high pH in aqueous conditions, leading to enhanced dispersion stability which is in strong agreement with earlier findings [43]. Gravimetric analysis revealed high monomer conversions for all latexes (>88%) with molecular weights within the expected range for radical polymerization under similar conditions (Figure 2g and Table S2) [32, 33].

Polymer Nanocomposite Coatings Preparation and GO Reduction

The latexes were spray‐coated onto Q235 metal substrate using a layer‐by‐layer technique until coatings reached the desired thickness between 50 to 80 µm (Figure S3). After spray coating, the samples were dried at ambient conditions for 24 h before undergoing thermal annealing at 160°C for 24 h to reduce GO to rGO. Figure 2d and Figure S3a revealed a relatively poor coating formation and surface non‐uniformity of the neat coating, whereas polymer/GO coatings (both pH 2 and pH 10) in Figure 2e,f exhibited homogenous coating formation on the metal substrate. The non‐homogeneous coating formation in the case of neat polymer is likely caused by poor spreading of latex droplets on the substrate during spraying, potentially due to the high surface tension of the latex. The introduction of GO in the formulation decreased the surface tension of the latex and improved the spreading of droplets on the substrate, thereby improving coalescence and resulting in a uniform and homogenous coating (discussed further below).

The extent of GO reduction in annealed coatings was confirmed by Raman spectroscopy and XRD analysis (Figure 3a,b). The Raman spectra of both pH 2 coating and pH 10 coating revealed the characteristic D and G bands of rGO at approximately 1350 and 1580 cm−1, respectively (Figure 3a). The D band corresponds to structural defects disorder and the presence of sp3 on the basal plane, whereas the G peak arises from the in‐plane stretching of sp2 hybridized carbon‐carbon bonds, indicative of graphitic domains in the material [44]. The intensity ratio of D to G peaks (I D/I G) provides information on the extent of rGO reduction. A lower ID/IG value indicates a successful reduction of GO to rGO, due to partial restoration of sp2 carbon domain [45]. The I D/I G values of the thermally annealed polymer/rGO coatings were 0.72 (pH 2) and 0.76 (pH 10), which were noticeably lower than for pristine GO (I D/I G = 0.85), thus suggesting successful reduction of GO to rGO. The obtained change in I D/I G values is in line with our previous report on similar nanocomposite coatings [36]. XRD analysis revealed a broad diffraction peak centered at 2θ = ∼20° for both polymer/rGO coatings, which can be ascribed to the combination of amorphous polymer and rGO, in agreement with previous work by us and others on similar polymer/rGO nanocomposites prepared via emulsion‐based polymerization [32, 36, 46, 47]. Notably, the characteristic GO peak (001) at 2θ = 10° was absent in both thermally annealed coatings, thus further confirming the successful reduction of GO to rGO (Figure 3b).

Coating Morphologies

SEM analysis was conducted to investigate the surface (Figure 4a–c) and cross‐sectional morphology (Figure 4d–f) of the nanocomposite coatings. The neat polymer coating in Figure 4a showed poor coating formation on the substrate, presumably due to insufficient coalescence during coating formation as described above. Both polymer/rGO coatings (Figure 4b,c) exhibited rougher surface morphologies, which can be attributed to the presence of rGO across the coating surface in line with our previous reports [31, 48]. Cross‐sectional SEM images (Figure 4e,f) further revealed no visible phase separation between the polymer and rGO in both polymer/rGO coatings. Notably, all coatings demonstrated good interfacial adhesion to the metal substrate, with no visible pores or delamination, suggesting strong interaction between coating and substrate. Water contact angle (WCA) measurements were carried out to assess the surface wettability of the coatings (Figure 4g–i). The presence of rGO can significantly improve the surface hydrophobicity and impermeability of nanocomposite coatings [49]. In the present study, the presence of 5 wt.% filler increased the contact angle from 76° (neat coating) to more than 100° for the two polymer/rGO coatings (pH 2–102° and pH 10–104°). The observed increase in the water contact angle can be attributed to the presence of rGO on the coating surface, which in turn led to a microscopically rougher surface and potentially enhanced water impermeability.

Electrical Conductivity and Mechanical Properties of Coatings

The electrical conductivity values obtained from LSV analysis for pH 2 and pH 10 coatings were 1.34 and 1.83 S/m, respectively. These values fall within the expected range for rGO‐based nanocomposite coatings prepared via our approach [31, 36, 37, 50]. The mechanical properties were evaluated using tensile testing. Visual assessments confirmed the strength, flexibility, and stretchability of the polymer/rGO coating (Figure 5a). Tensile testing results in (Figure 5b,c) revealed a relatively poor mechanical performance for the neat coating (not annealed), but very good mechanical properties for both polymer/rGO coatings. Polymer/rGO coatings demonstrated excellent mechanical resilience based on the obtained toughness and tensile strength values. In particular, the pH 2 coating exhibited toughness of 67.9 MJ m−3, more than two times higher than the pH 10 coating (30.65 MJ m−3) and ten times higher than the neat coating (6.51 MJ m−3). The tensile strength for both pH 2 and pH 10 coatings was within a similar range (4.2–4.5 MPa), outperforming the neat coating (0.26 MPa). The polymer/rGO coatings not only exhibited superior strength but also enhanced ductility, where the highest elongation at break of 2000% was recorded for pH 2 and 1000% for pH 10 coating. Despite containing filler at relatively high concentrations, the polymer/rGO nanocomposite coatings in this study showed great stretchability (elongation at break >1000%), which is in contrast with prior investigations that demonstrated nanocomposite coatings with similar filler (rGO) loadings (5 wt.%) tend to exhibit high brittleness [51, 52]. One reason for this observed difference is the presence of hexadecane in the present miniemulsion formulations (to prevent Ostwald ripening), which has been previously shown to significantly enhance the elongation at break [53]. Generally, mechanically robust coatings are highly desirable in anti‐corrosion applications due to their ability in mitigating cracks and pin hole formation.

Anti‐Corrosion Properties of Coatings

Tafel and EIS analyses were employed to evaluate the anticorrosion performance of coated and uncoated metal in a simulated corrosive environment. These tests were performed using a three‐electrodes electrochemical cell with 3.5 wt.% NaCl solution as the electrolyte at room temperature. For the Tafel test, the samples were exposed to the electrolyte for 1 h prior to measurement to stabilize the open circuit potential (OCP). The potential was scanned at a rate of 1 mV s−1 within ± 0.1 V of OCP window. Polarized Tafel plots were fitted using the Tafel equation (Stern method) based on the Butler‐Volmer derivation to extrapolate kinetic parameters, including corrosion slopes (βa and βc), corrosion current density (i corr), and corrosion potential (E corr) (Figure S4 and Table 2). Generally, i corr provides information on the corrosion rate, whereas E corr represents the susceptibility of a material to corrosion. Figure 6a shows the Tafel plots of bare metal and metal coated with neat polymer, pH 2 and pH 10 coatings, respectively. Additionally, the corrosion rate (CR, mm/year) was calculated based on Faraday's law (Equation 2) according to ASTM standard G102, where, k is 3270 (mm.g/A/cm.year), i corr is the corrosion current (A/cm2), ρ is the density of Q235 carbon steel (7.85 g/cm3) and EW is the equivalent weight of the metal, which is dimensionless in this case (27.36) [55]. CR quantifies the thickness loss of metal over time and can be calculated based on i corr obtained from Tafel. Corrosion parameters (βa, βc, i corr, and E corr), P.E., and CR obtained from Tafel plots and calculated using Equations 2 and 3 are listed in Table 2. Tafel slopes (βa and βc) provide insight on the reaction kinetic at both anodic and cathodic regions of the metal. An increase in anodic Tafel slope (βa) generally indicates suppression of metal dissolution, often resulting from the barrier effects of a coating or inhibitor. Based on the obtained Tafel slopes, pH 10 coating exhibited the highest βa compared to the pH 2 and neat coatings, confirming the inhibition at anodic reaction attributed to effective barrier formation [56]. In general, lower i corr and higher E corr values denote enhanced corrosion protection of a system [57]. Bare metal yielded E corr of −0.66 V and i corr of 24.80 µA, whereas the neat polymer coating (E corr of −0.49 V and i corr of 15.81 µA) exhibited a slight improvement in corrosion performance relative to the bare metal. Significant improvements in both corrosion parameters were observed after rGO incorporation in the coatings. E corr of both pH 2 and pH 10 coatings exhibited a major shift towards noble potential values (pH 2 coating 0.24 V and pH 10 coating 0.15 V) while i corr values were 0.03 and 0.01 µA for pH 2 and pH 10 coatings, respectively. The i corr values for pH 2 and pH 10 coatings were more than three orders of magnitude lower than the bare metal, indicating significant corrosion protection. The polymer/rGO coatings (both pH 2 and pH 10) demonstrated superior protection efficacy over the neat polymer coating based on the calculated P.E. at 99%, significantly higher than the neat polymer‐coated metal at 36%. Moreover, the corrosion rate (CR) estimations were 3.65 × 10−4 mm/year (pH 2) and 5.70 × 10−5 mm/year (pH 10), significantly lower than the bare metal (2.83 × 10−1 mm/year) and neat polymer coating (1.80 × 10−1 mm/year). It is noteworthy that the E corr, i corr, and P.E. values of polymer/rGO coatings obtained in the present study are markedly higher than previously reported values for vinyl polymer/rGO coatings (E corr range of −0.69 to −0.25 V, i corr range of 0.04 to 0.06 µA.cm2, P.E. range of 99.27% to 99.53%) [8, 9, 58].

Next, EIS measurements were conducted to elucidate the detailed anti‐corrosion behaviour of the coatings in the simulated corrosion environment (3.5 wt.% NaCl solution). EIS is a well‐established technique for evaluating the behaviour of organic coatings and the reaction at the electrolyte‐metal interface for a specific exposure time [59, 60]. To assess the stability of coatings over an extended period, impedance measurements were performed after 1 and 168 h by exposing the coated metals to the NaCl solution at room temperature. Generally, a larger capacitive arc in a Nyquist plot corresponds to a higher impedance and better corrosion protection. Based on the Nyquist plot after both 1 and 168 h exposure, the bare uncoated metal exhibited a smaller capacitive arc at both time intervals, indicating a mass transfer‐dominated diffusion mechanism (Figure 7a–d) [61]. In contrast, all coated samples (control neat polymer, pH 2, and pH 10) showed larger capacitive arcs compared to the uncoated bare metal. The increase in diameter of capacitive arcs observed for both nanocomposite coatings (pH 2 and pH 10) than the control neat polymer coating highlights the crucial role of rGO against corrosion. After 168 h of exposure to the corrosion environment, a significant reduction (>75%) in both imaginary and real impedance values was observed for pH 2 coatings compared to after 1 h exposure. Comparatively, the drop of only ∼36% in impedance values was observed in the case of pH 10 coatings under the same experimental conditions.

To quantitatively interpret the EIS results, Randles‐equivalent electrical circuits (Figure 6b,c) based on the Randles model were used to fit the Nyquist plot (Figure S5) and extract the critical EIS parameters (Table 3). The circuit models consist of Rs (solution resistance), Rc (coating resistance), CPEc (constant phase element of coating), Rct (charge transfer resistance), CPEdl (constant phase element of double layer (electrolyte‐metal interface) and WE (working electrode). To achieve better Nyquist plot fitting, a constant phase element (CPE) was applied instead of pure capacitance due to the non‐ideal nature of the capacitive response in this system. CPE comprises two parameters: the exponent (n) and the admittance (Y 0) [62]. The CPE exponent (0 < n < 1) reflects the degree of surface and interfacial heterogeneity, while the admittance (Y 0) represents the ease of charge and ions transport through the coating or metal interface. Changes in the CPE parameters, particularly a decrease in n and an increase in Y 0 are indicative of the progression of coating degradation due to water and ions ingress [63]. In this study, the obtained CPE at the metal interface (CPEdl) of coated samples exhibited n values within the range of 0<n<1 and consistently lower Y 0 relative to the bare metal across all immersion times, suggesting improved barrier characteristics (Table 3). The Rc and CPEc parameters were included in the circuit for coated metal samples (Figure 6c), representing the electrochemical behaviour of the coating itself. The Rct value is often used to quantify the resistance at the double‐layer region (electrolyte‐metal interface). In EIS, the impedance modulus |Z| is measured as a function of frequency (ω) after applying a small sinusoidal perturbation on the coated samples. The analysis of impedance at different frequencies reveals the diffusion mechanism of corrosion ions, which ultimately determines the effectiveness of a protective coating. The higher the value of |Z|, the better is the corrosion protection. The modulus of impedance at the lowest frequency (in this case, |Z|0.01Hz) represents the resistance of a metal at the diffusion‐dominated lowest frequency region. Both Rct and |Z|0.01Hz values presented in Figure 7e,f and Table 3 are valuable in determining the corrosion performance. As can be observed from fitted parameters, the lowest values for Rct and |Z|0.01Hz can be observed in bare metal at both 1 h and 168 h. Rct and |Z|0.01Hz values of the bare metal reduced by 90% and 28%, respectively after 168 h exposure, suggesting the metal underwent severe corrosion during immersion. A slight increase in both parameters was observed after applying a neat polymer coating on the metal. At 168 h, the neat polymer coating exhibited an increase in Rct (26 times) and |Z|0.01Hz (4 times) compared to the bare metal, providing protection to the metal to some extent. A significant improvement in both Rct and |Z|0.01Hz was further observed after applying polymer/rGO coatings. In particular, the highest values of Rct and |Z|0.01Hz was observed for pH 10 coating at both time intervals (1 and 168 h). After 168 h, the pH 10 coating exhibited the Rct of 1.00 × 106 kΩ.cm2 and |Z|0.01Hz of 916 kΩ.cm2 both of which at least one magnitude order higher than the pH 2 coating, confirming its (pH 10 coating) superior corrosion protection efficacy. It is worth noting that the Rct obtained for pH 10 coating is significantly higher than the recently reported bio‐derived chitosan/rGO anticorrosion coating at the same rGO loading (5 wt.%) [64]. and nacre‐biomimetic anticorrosion coating based on epoxy/GO [65]. In the case of pH 2 coating, a noticeable decline in both parameters (Rct decreased 62%, |Z|0.01Hz decreased 75%) was observed after 168 h exposure. However, these values remained higher than those of the neat polymer coating, which is attributed to the presence of rGO barrier that effectively blocks the intrusion of corrosive ions. This observation is consistent with earlier studies on polymer/rGO coatings, which reported superior anticorrosion performance relative to neat polymer coatings. For instance, Zhu et al. [66], reported epoxy/rGO (1 wt.%) exhibited improved Rct nearly 70 times than the neat epoxy coating after 10 h of exposure in 10 wt.% NaCl solution. Similarly, Ramírez–Soria et al. [67], reported that the Rct of epoxy/propyl‐rGO prepared via a solvent‐free approach was three orders of magnitude higher than the epoxy coating. These findings further confirmed the efficacy of rGO as corrosion inhibitor in organic coating.

The Bode plot in Figure S6 shows impedance vs frequency (Bode‐impedance) and phase angle vs frequency (Bode‐phase angle) plots of bare and coated metals at both time intervals (1 and 168 h). In general, the obtained Bode plot revealed that polymer/rGO coatings at both pH exhibited higher impedance and phase angles compared to the neat coating and bare metal. Bode‐impedance plot can be divided into two different regions:1) the linear region with slope of −1, which represents the capacitive region, 2) the region with a slope of ∼0 represents the resistive region [68]. In this study, after 1 h of exposure, the Bode‐impedance plot demonstrated that both pH 2 and pH 10 coatings exhibited capacitive behaviour over a wide frequency range, as adjudged from the observed slope of approximately −1. However, after 168 h of exposure, the pH 2 coating exhibited a significant decrease in impedance (by one order of magnitude) while pH 10 coating maintained the highest impedance at ∼103 kΩ.cm2. In the Bode‐phase angle plot, phase angle of 90° generally signifies an ideal capacitive behaviour. Accordingly, both polymer/rGO coatings exhibited markedly higher phase angles than the neat coating and bare metal. It is noteworthy that pH 10 coating exhibited the highest phase angles (80° at 1 h, 70° at 168 h), exceeding pH 2 coating (67° at 1 h, 66° at 168 h) at both exposure times, thereby demonstrating the superior anticorrosion performance of pH 10 coating.

Breakpoint frequency (ƒb) is an additional parameter used to determine the electrochemically active area of coated steel in seawater [69] ƒb is a frequency at which a capacitive behaviour transforms into a resistive at phase angle = −45° [70, 71]. In addition, ƒb is also strongly correlated with the nanoscale delamination or defect area of a coating, where the increase in ƒb generally corresponds to a relative expansion of coating delaminated area [72]. In this study, after 168 h of exposure, the ƒb of polymer/rGO coated metals (pH 2 and pH 10 coatings) were a few orders of magnitude lower than the bare metal and neat coating, with pH 10 coating exhibiting the lowest ƒb of 1.00 × 10−2 Hz. As depicted in Table 3, CDI of neat, pH 2, and pH 10 coatings were 24%, 75%, and 36%, respectively. Notably, CDI of pH 10 coating is 50% lower than that of the pH 2 coating, further supporting the above findings about superior protective performance against corrosion.

Taken together, the observed differences in the corrosion protection performance of pH 10 coatings compared to their pH 2 counterparts can be attributed to a combination of factors: (i) the dispersion and distribution of rGO sheets within a polymer matrix can significantly impact corrosion performance of the corresponding coating. It has been reported by Shih et al. [74], that the pH of a polymer/rGO aqueous dispersion prepared via emulsion polymerization can influence the dispersion of rGO sheets, where high pH (pH 10) yields better dispersibility compared to low pH (pH < 7). The better dispersibility at high pH was attributed to the electrostatic repulsion between deprotonated carboxylic acid groups on the edges of rGO sheets and OH− ions (from the base, e.g., NH4OH) at basic pH [75, 76, 77] (ii) Poor dispersion of rGO at low pH may increase rGO interparticle distance and create alternative pathways aiding the diffusion of corrosion ions. This hypothesis is supported by zeta potential measurements, which demonstrated a lower zeta potential of pH 10 (−49 mV) over pH 2 latex (−32 mV).

To investigate the corrosion products formed at a microscopic level, XRD analysis was conducted by first exposing the uncoated metal and coated metal samples to 3.5 wt.% NaCl solution for 168 h and then removing the coatings to investigate the underlying metal for corrosion (Figure 8c). The results revealed strong metallic iron peaks at approximately 2θ = 52° (110), 76° (200), and 99° (211) in all samples representing the metal substrate (Q235) used in the experiment, which are in line with the previous published reports [78]. Anti‐Corrosion Mechanism

Corrosion of iron steel involves an irreversible redox reaction resulting in the formation of iron oxide species. Corrosive ion diffusion to metal in the case of coated metal can take place via a few stages: (1) electrolyte adsorption at the coating surface, (2) diffusion of electrolyte through the coating (through defects), and (3) electrolyte absorption at the double layer region at the coating‐metal interface [55]. Uncoated bare metal exposed to corrosive environments undergoes severe corrosion due to direct electrolyte diffusion at the metal interface. Contrarily, coating on a metal can delay or prevent corrosion by creating a barrier layer at the first and second stage of electrolyte diffusion. A neat coating in this case provides some extent of protection by providing a barrier solely formed by the polymer. This will delay the diffusion, but due to coating imperfections (defects), the electrolyte will eventually reach the metal (anodic) and causing corrosion. The polymer/rGO coatings developed in the present work create an additional layer of barrier with a ‘labyrinth effect’ promoted by the lamellar shape of rGO, further enhancing the protection against corrosion [30]. This barrier will create torturous pathways for corrosion ions to reach the metal surface. Enhanced corrosion protection of pH 10 coating over pH 2 coating is proposed to be due to superior dispersion of rGO at basic pH facilitated by electrostatic repulsion between rGO and OH− within the coating. A lower zeta potential of pH 10 over pH 2 further validated this finding. This will in turn enhance the barrier performance in coating and subsequently improve the protection against corrosion. Additionally, the presence of negative ions on rGO edges, OH− and ammonium ions (from base) within the coating is postulated to create simultaneous protection by repelling the corrosive ions (Cl−, OH− and O2− from electrolyte) through electrostatic repulsion, while NH4 + ions from the base are trapping the corrosion ions through electrostatic attractions. Conclusion

Anti‐corrosive polymer nanocomposite coatings for Q235 grade low carbon steel were successfully prepared by employing vinyl copolymers (St and nBA) and unmodified GO via one‐pot water‐based in situ miniemulsion polymerization. The resulting latexes exhibited excellent kinetic stability without agglomeration even at high GO loading of 5 wt.%, in contrast to earlier findings in corrosion studies for coating with GO/rGO. Homogeneous anti‐corrosive coatings were prepared by spray coating on metal, followed by drying at ambient temperature before thermal annealing to reduce GO to rGO (confirmed by Raman and XRD analyses). SEM images of surface morphologies showed a homogenous distribution of GO at the coating surface, and cross‐sections of the coatings revealed no pore formation with strong adhesion to the substrate. LSV and tensile testing confirmed that the polymer/rGO coatings were electrically conductive and mechanically robust. Anti‐corrosion behaviour of coated metals after immersion in corrosive environment for 1 to 168 h was further investigated by Tafel and EIS testing – the results demonstrated excellent corrosion protection by the polymer/rGO coating relative to the neat coating, highlighting the effect of rGO on corrosion protection. The significantly improved protection of pH 10 coating over pH 2 coating is speculated to originate in superior rGO dispersion at basic pH, based on the obtained zeta potentials. Additionally, the presence of negative ions on rGO and OH− and NH4 + from the base provide simultaneous repulsion and attraction to corrosion ions is postulated to promote secondary protection of the metal. The anticorrosion performance of the polymer/rGO coatings was further supported by post‐immersion analysis by XRD. Marine field test results revealed a similar level of corrosion protection of pH 10 coated metals as observed in laboratory tests, confirming the ability of this coating to provide protection against corrosion in real marine environments. Comparison of corrosion parameters (E corr and I corr) with earlier studies revealed superior performance of the polymer/rGO anti‐corrosive coating prepared via our approach. This novel strategy of polymer nanocomposite coating preparation through a waterborne approach using vinyl polymers without requiring filler modification and organic solvents provides an attractive route for advanced protective coating development for essential metals.
