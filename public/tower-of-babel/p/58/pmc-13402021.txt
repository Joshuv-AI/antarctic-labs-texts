Trends in adult exclusive and dual use of combustible and non-combustible tobacco products in the United States
Authors: Kenneth Michael Cummings, Avery Roberson, Colin W Randol, Tracy T Smith, David T Levy, Yoonseo Mok, Steve Cook, Rafael Meza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs tobacco use patterns shift toward non-combustible tobacco products such as electronic cigarettes (ECs) and nicotine pouches, dual use with combustible products may undermine potential public health benefits. Herein we describe adult trends in exclusive and dual use of combustible and non-combustible products in the United States between 2015 and 2023.MethodsWe analyzed annual National Health and Interview Survey data including all adults with valid data on tobacco use, age, and sex. Joinpoint regression estimated annual percentage change (APC) and 95% CIs overall and by age group and sex. All tests were 2-tailed (P < .05).ResultsExclusive combustible use remained 2-3 times higher than exclusive non-combustible use or dual use across all years. From 2015 and 2023, combustible use declined in both sexes, with the steepest drops in younger adults after 2020. Exclusive non-combustible use increased in all age groups after 2017, especially among younger adults and females. The most common dual use pattern was cigarettes plus ECs, which was stable over time.ConclusionsExclusive use of combustible tobacco, primarily cigarettes, is declining but remains the most common pattern, especially in older adults. In contrast, exclusive EC use is displacing combustible use among younger adults. Dual use patterns were low overall and stable across the study period.

Exclusive use of combustible tobacco is declining while exclusive use of non-combustible products, particularly ECs, is increasing. The prevalence of combustible and non-combustible tobacco dual use was low and stable over time. Comparing the prevalence of any combustible tobacco use to exclusive non-combustible tobacco use suggests a population level shift toward lower-risk product use, indicating a potential net health benefit under accepted harm-reduction assumptions supported by biomarker evidence.

Introduction

Tobacco use remains a major contributor to morbidity and mortality in the United States. While both combustible and non-combustible tobacco products pose health risks to users, they confer vastly different risks.1-4 Factory made cigarettes are particularly harmful, responsible for about 480 000 deaths annually in the United States.4 Recent shifts in tobacco use patterns include declining rates of cigarette smoking and increased adoption of non-combustible products such as electronic cigarettes (ECs) and oral tobacco use.5-7 The differential risks of these products suggest that changing patterns of use could have important effects on future tobacco-related health outcomes.

While the prevalence of the more harmful combustible use is declining at the same time that the use of non-combustibles is increasing, reductions in tobacco-related morbidity and mortality may be limited if individuals use both combustible and non-combustible tobacco products.8-12 Biomarker studies suggest that smokers who switch completely to non-combustible tobacco products markedly reduce exposure to harmful and potentially harmful chemicals while those who dual use combustible and non-combustible tobacco do not get the same level of reduction in exposure to harmful chemicals.13-19 Several studies emphasize the importance of monitoring dual use, as it may undermine the risk reduction benefits of ECs and oral nicotine products.19-21

In this study, we describe population-level trends from 2015 to 2023 in exclusive and dual use of combustible and non-combustible tobacco among US adults, overall and stratified by age and sex. We also assess trends in exclusive and dual use of different product combinations, including cigarettes, other combustible tobacco products (ie, cigars, pipe and hookah tobacco combined), ECs and oral tobacco products. Finally, we introduce a harm-reduction-oriented surveillance indicator by comparing the prevalence of exclusive non-combustible tobacco use with the prevalence of any combustible tobacco use. Under commonly accepted harm-reduction assumptions supported by biomarker evidence,13-19 this prevalence-based ratio crudely describes population shifts toward lower-risk product use.

Methods

Study population and study design

In this cross-sectional trend-analysis study, we estimate the prevalence of current exclusive and dual use of combustible and non-combustible tobacco products using data from the National Health and Interview Survey (NHIS) from 2015 to 2023.22 The NHIS is a nationwide, annual, in-person survey of approximately 27 000 adults that collects data on a wide range of health topics including information on tobacco product usage of the civilian, non-institutionalized US population. The NHIS uses multistage probability sampling that incorporates stratification, clustering, and oversampling of some subpopulations (eg, Black, Hispanic, and Asian) in some years. Statistical weighting is used to generate representative national estimates.22 The US Census Bureau employs and trains field staff to conduct interviews. They follow standardized procedures and conduct the majority of interviews in person with some interviews done over the phone when an in person household interview is not possible.

In this pooled secondary data analysis, we chose to describe trends in exclusive and dual use of combustible and non-combustible tobacco product use beginning in 2015, when tobacco-use questions became consistent. In 2014, the NHIS survey combined the assessment of cigars and pipe use in a single question, whereas in 2015 separate questions were asked about cigars and pipe tobacco use. Previous studies have demonstrated that the formatting of questions about tobacco use can influence prevalence rates.23

Independent variables

The primary independent variable in this study was survey year, with 9 time points spanning from 2015 to 2023. In addition, we report tobacco use prevalence outcomes by age and sex. Age was measured in years and collapsed into 4 different age groups (ie, 18-24 years, 25-34 years, 35-54 years, and ≥55 years). Sex at birth was defined as either male or female. No other variables were included in this trend analysis.

Outcome variables

Table 1 provides the questions from the NHIS survey and definitions used for defining current prevalence of cigarettes, cigars, pipe/hookah tobacco, ECs, and smokeless tobacco. Current combustible tobacco use was defined as current use of any cigarettes, cigars, pipe and/or hookah tobacco. Current non-combustible use was defined as current use of any EC or smokeless tobacco. For each survey year, current tobacco use prevalence was categorized into 3 groups: (1) exclusive use of a combustible tobacco product; (2) exclusive use of a non-combustible tobacco product, and (3) dual use of both a combustible and non-combustible tobacco product. Exclusive use of a combustible tobacco was defined as current use of only combustible tobacco products, including cigarettes, cigars, pipe and/or hookah tobacco and no current use of non-combustible tobacco products. Exclusive use of a non-combustible tobacco was defined as current use of only non-combustible tobacco products, including EC or smokeless tobacco and no current use of combustible tobacco products. Dual use of combustible and non-combustible tobacco was defined as current reported use of both combustible tobacco and non-combustible products.

In addition, we also estimated the prevalence of exclusive use of individual combustible and non-combustible tobacco products as well as dual use as follows: (1) exclusive cigarette use; (2) exclusive non-cigarette combustible tobacco products defined as any current use of cigars, pipe tobacco, or hookah tobacco; (3) exclusive use of ECs; (4) exclusive use of oral tobacco defined as any use of chewing tobacco, moist snuff, or nicotine pouches; (5) dual use of cigarettes and ECs; (6) dual use of non-combustible tobacco products and ECs; (7) dual use of cigarettes and oral tobacco; and (8) dual use of non-combustible tobacco products and oral tobacco.

To assess the potential population health impact of shifting tobacco use patterns, we examined annual trends in the ratio of exclusive non-combustible tobacco prevalence to the prevalence of any combustible tobacco use (including exclusive and/or dual use), under the assumption that exclusive use of non-combustible tobacco use is less risky to health relative to combustible tobacco use based on biomarker evidence.13-19

Analyses

Descriptive statistics were generated using SAS 9.4 software (Cary, NC). For each NIHS survey year (2015-2023) we analyzed data using that year’s adult sampling weights and the NHIS-provided design variables for stratification and clustering (ie, strata and PSU). We estimated nationally representative annual prevalence and design-based standard errors using Taylor series linearization implemented using R survey package based on standard methodology (https://nhis.ipums.org/nhis/userNotes_variance.shtml). Because NHIS weights are cycle-specific, we did not pool the data across years for a single weighted analysis; instead, prevalence was estimated separately for each year using the corresponding annual weights. We then fit joinpoint regression models to the annual prevalence estimates and standard errors using weighted least squares estimates to estimate annual present change (APC) and identify potential inflection points. Joinpoint regression for trend analysis has been championed by the National Cancer Institute, which developed the Joinpoint regression software.

Joinpoint regression analyses were conducted to describe annual trends (2015-2023) of weighted prevalence estimates for the following categories: (1) exclusive use of any combustible tobacco product; (2) exclusive use of any non-combustible tobacco product; and (3) dual use of both combustible and non-combustible tobacco products. Additional joinpoint analyses were performed to assess prevalence trends of: (1) exclusive cigarette use; (2) exclusive use of non-cigarette combustible tobacco products (cigars, pipe tobacco, or hookah tobacco); (3) exclusive use of ECs; (4) exclusive oral tobacco use (chewing tobacco, moist snuff, or nicotine pouches); (5) dual use of cigarettes and ECs; (6) dual use of non-combustible tobacco products and ECs; (7) dual use of cigarettes and oral tobacco; and (8) dual use of non-combustible tobacco products and oral tobacco.

Joinpoint models do not incorporate individual-level covariates; therefore, our trend analysis did not “adjust” in the multivariable regression sense. Instead, to assess whether patterns differed across key demographic groups, we conducted joinpoint analyses stratified by age group and sex. For each analysis, we report the APC from 2015 to 2023 and corresponding 95% CIs, both overall (all age groups, sexes combined) and separately by age group (18-24, 25-34, 35-54, and ≥55) and sex (males, females). Joinpoint regression also distinguishes different time segments (ie, joinpoints) to identify significant differences between time points.24-26 We allowed for at most 1 change in trend (0 or 1 joinpoints). All statistical tests were 2-tailed and based on a P < .05 significance level. As in our previous work,7 statistical testing procedures followed Kim et al.25 as implemented in the NCI joinpoint regression software.24

Discussion

This study describes recent population-level trends in exclusive and dual use of combustible and non-combustible tobacco products among US adults, with analyses stratified by age and sex, as well as by different tobacco product combinations. We found that exclusive use of combustible tobacco, primarily cigarettes, declined between 2015 and 2023 but remained the predominant use pattern overall, particularly among older adults. In contrast, exclusive use of non-combustible tobacco products rose across all age groups after 2017, and by 2023, exclusive EC use was more common than combustible tobacco among adults aged 18-24. Dual use of combustible and non-combustible products remained relatively stable between 2015 and 2023 but was more prevalent among males and younger adults (18-34 years). These findings underscore the importance of monitoring evolving tobacco use patterns, as they have potential implications for population-level health risks, harm reduction strategies, and future policy effects.

The findings from this descriptive study indicate that US adult tobacco use is increasingly shifting away from combustible tobacco products, mostly cigarettes, toward exclusive use of non-combustible tobacco products, primarily ECs.7 While non-combustible tobacco product use can cause nicotine dependence and is not without health risks, the absence of burned tobacco reduces exposure to harmful and potentially harmful chemicals.13-19 Consistent with these harm-reduction assumptions, the increasing prevalence of exclusive non-combustible tobacco product use compared to any combustible tobacco product use as displayed in Figure 5 serves as a crude descriptive measure of population-level progress toward lower-risk product use. While the decline in combustible tobacco product use, either exclusively or as part of dual use, is encouraging news, the rate of decline was much less among those ≥35 years of age, highlighting the need for enhanced efforts to discourage the use of smoked tobacco by older Americans.27

While this study provides a descriptive assessment of population-level trends in tobacco product use among US adults, readers should not overinterpret these findings for several reasons. First, we utilized NHIS-defined prevalence of current tobacco product use, which differed for established current cigarette smoking (ie, 100 cigarettes over lifetime and currently smoking every day or some days) compared to non-cigarette tobacco products, which assessed prevalence based on ever use and current use every day and some days. The different definitions may affect comparability across products and classification of exclusive and dual use. In addition, prevalence measures do not capture information on the frequency and duration of tobacco product use, both of which are important determinants of future health risks.4 Second, self-reported tobacco use is prone to information bias and misclassification. For example, the negative stigma associated with smoking cigarettes has been found to be associated with underreporting smoking in surveys.28 Also, not all respondents will be aware of the different tobacco products asked about in the survey, which may contribute to measurement error. Third, in this study we only report on cross-sectional population level prevalence rates and do not consider individual patterns of tobacco product use, which are likely complex with people transitioning over time between different tobacco product use states, including using more than one product at a time (ie, dual use).10-12,29 For example, studies of people who report currently using cigarettes have found that most are still smoking cigarettes in the next year or even 5 years later.30,31 This finding might not apply to those using non-cigarette tobacco products, suggesting that different tobacco products might convey differential risks of abuse liability. Fourth, this study did not consider dual use of cannabis smoking, which could impact future health risks. There is an association between tobacco use and cannabis use and recent studies have found exclusive use of nicotine vaping is associated with the smoking of cannabis, especially in young adults.32 Fifth, there are inherent limitations of the joinpoint analyses presented in this paper, since we report on only 9 data points. Results may change as additional data points become available. For example, between 2015 and 2023 the prevalence of nicotine pouch use was low. Recent studies have found that the use of nicotine pouches has doubled in the past 2 years so it will be important to see how growth in this product category influences trends in tobacco use patterns.33 Sixth, the findings from this study may not be generalizable to countries outside the United States.

In summary, the findings from this study indicate a positive trend in tobacco use in the United States between 2015 and 2023, characterized by reduced use of smoked tobacco products, a pattern most evident among younger adults. Continuous surveillance of trends in the ratio of the prevalence of exclusive non-combustible tobacco use to the prevalence of any combustible tobacco use provides a straightforward, albeit crude, metric for assessing population-level progress in reducing the future harms associated with tobacco product use.
