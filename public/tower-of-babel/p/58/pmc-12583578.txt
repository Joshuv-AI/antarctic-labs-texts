The impact of social exclusion on malevolent creativity among Chinese university students: the roles of empathy, moral disengagement, and teacher support
Authors: Lin Li, Yanqin Hou, Linxia Ouyang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSocial exclusion, as a prevalent and profoundly detrimental situational factor, not only impairs the cognitive functions of individuals but also increases the likelihood of aggressive behaviors or malevolent creativity by eliciting negative emotions (e.g., anger, anxiety) and cognitive biases (e.g., hostile attribution). University students, who are situated in a unique social environment and a pivotal stage of personal development, exhibit distinct psychological and behavioral patterns. This highlights the critical need to explore the mechanisms underlying the relationship between social exclusion and malevolent creativity among Chinese university students. Based on 457 valid questionnaires, including 218 males and 239 females, this study utilized Mplus 8.0 for data analysis. The findings revealed that: (1) social exclusion is positively associated with malevolent creativity among university students; (2) empathy mediates the relationship between social exclusion and malevolent creativity; (3) moral disengagement also mediates this relationship; (4) empathy and moral disengagement function as sequential mediators in the link between social exclusion and malevolent creativity; and (5) teacher support moderates the relationship between social exclusion and malevolent creativity. This research not only enriches the theoretical framework of existing studies but also provides novel insights and evidence for the prevention and intervention of malevolent creativity.Keywords: Social exclusion, Empathy, Moral disengagement, Teacher support, Malevolent creativitySubject terms: Education, Psychology, Psychology, Sociology

Introduction

Malevolent creativity is defined as the intentional generation of novel ideas or behaviors aimed at causing harm to others or objects1. Its high novelty and unpredictability render it a substantial threat to social stability2, underscoring the need to investigate its underlying mechanisms and mitigation strategies. Research indicates that malevolent creativity is influenced by a combination of individual and situational factors. Individual traits such as high aggressiveness, psychopathy, anger, or approach motivation increase the propensity for malevolent creativity3,4, whereas traits like authenticity, conscientiousness, psychological resilience, and high emotional intelligence mitigate such behaviors5–7. Situational factors, such as unfair atmospheres8, competitive environments2, abusive leadership9, and social threats10, also significantly trigger malevolent creativity. Compared to relatively stable individual dispositions, situational factors exert a broader and more direct influence on malevolent creativity11. Social information processing theory posits that social contexts shape individual attitudes and behaviors, channeling morally neutral creative potential toward benevolent or malevolent ends12. For example, manipulating the goal framing of creative tasks (e.g., benevolent vs. malevolent) predicts malevolent creativity more effectively than cognitive abilities alone12, highlighting the importance of situational cues.

Social exclusion, a widespread and detrimental social phenomenon, occurs when individuals are rejected or ignored in interpersonal contexts13. It impairs cognitive function and evokes negative emotions such as sadness, anger, and loneliness, which can lead to aggression and antisocial behavior, thereby damaging both physical and mental health14. Recently, social exclusion has gained attention as a key situational antecedent of malevolent creativity. It fosters malevolent creativity through multiple pathways: by eliciting negative emotions (e.g., anger, anxiety) and cognitive biases (e.g., hostile attribution)15, and by amplifying desires for revenge and control15–17. For instance, Perchtold-Stefan et al.15 experimentally showed that excluded individuals generated more original and harmful responses in malevolent creativity tests, particularly in revenge scenarios. Furthermore, social exclusion indirectly facilitates malevolent creativity through activated negative emotions like hostility18.

Overall, existing research has provided substantial insights into this area; however, three particular limitations warrant further investigation. Firstly, while current studies have effectively demonstrated how emotions and cognitive biases, like anger and hostile attribution, contribute to malevolent creativity after social exclusion, they rarely take into account broader socio - cognitive adaptations. Although emotions and cognitive biases are adaptive in nature, limited efforts have been made to understand how long - term exposure to social exclusion can result in more complex operational processes within the social context. These processes include changes in empathy and moral disengagement, which ultimately promote harmful creative behavior. Secondly, the existing literature primarily focuses on internal mechanisms and gives inadequate attention to the moderating effects of external environmental factors19–21. This narrow focus impedes a comprehensive understanding of the multi - layered ways through which social exclusion leads to malevolent creativity, particularly the potential protective function of supportive environments. Thirdly, the majority of previous studies have utilized samples from the general population15,19,21–23, leaving the issue of generalizability to specific groups, such as university students, largely unaddressed. University students are in the stage of “late adolescence or just after adolescence”, and their psychological development is characterized by vulnerability24. Meanwhile, the collective living environment of Chinese university students (e.g., the compulsory dormitory system) exacerbates their sensitivity to social exclusion25. Relevant research has found that the incidence of social exclusion among university students is relatively high, with 68.09% of Chinese university students reporting that they have experienced exclusion26. Therefore, more targeted research is required to elucidate the distinct psychological and behavioral mechanisms underlying this relationship.

Guided by the triadic reciprocal determinism of Social Cognitive Theory, which posits that behavior emerges from the dynamic interaction among personal, environmental, and behavioral factors27, this study employs empathy, moral disengagement, and teacher support as key constructs to clarify how social exclusion leads to malevolent creativity. Social exclusion is conceptualized as an environmental factor capable of altering cognitive and affective processes27. Empathy, defined as the ability to perceive, understand, and care about others’ suffering28, represents a central personal factor within this framework. Moral disengagement, which refers to an individual’s cognitive tendency to redefine immoral acts, adjust internal responsibility attribution, downplay moral responsibility for consequences, and lessen the perception of the pain of those harmed29, further exemplifies a personal dimension that shapes behavioral outcomes. Conversely, teacher support serves as an environmental resource by providing emotional, academic, and informational assistance, thereby potentially mitigating the effects of negative social experiences30. Together, these variables, which encompass both personal and environmental aspects of social cognitive theory, offer a theoretically coherent perspective for investigating how social exclusion contributes to malevolent creativity as a behavioral outcome. Therefore, this research not only extends the theoretical foundations of existing literature but also offers novel insights and empirical support for preventing and intervening in malevolent creativity.

Theoretical foundation and research hypotheses

Social exclusion and malevolent creativity

Social information processing theory emphasizes the pivotal role of environmental factors in shaping individual behaviors. Building on this theory, Gutworth et al.12 highlighted that environments significantly influence whether the outcomes of creative activities are driven by malevolent intentions. Social exclusion, as a negative life experience, is pervasive in individuals’ daily lives, encompassing situations such as being ignored, persistently ostracized, or discriminated against31. As social beings, humans possess an inherent need to establish and maintain social connections13. However, social exclusion directly threatens the fulfillment of this need, exerting profound effects on individuals’ psychological and behavioral patterns32, and potentially triggering malevolent creativity. Research indicates that when individuals experience social exclusion, their creative thinking may be directed toward malevolent intentions, particularly in contexts of unstable power structures or perceived social threats10. This direct mechanism can be understood through the general aggression model of social exclusion: social exclusion activates negative emotions (e.g., anger or frustration), thereby increasing individuals’ tendency to express malevolent intentions through creative means33,34. Furthermore, social exclusion may weaken individuals’ moral constraints, making them more inclined to utilize creative thinking to harm others or disrupt social order35. Such malevolent creativity not only reflects the direct consequences of social exclusion but also demonstrates its profound shaping of behavioral patterns in specific contexts. Based on the above analysis, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H1: Social exclusion is positively correlated with malevolent creativity among university students.

The mediating role of empathy

From the perspective of the dynamic model of empathy36 and the emotional numbing theory of social exclusion37, individuals who experience prolonged social exclusion are more likely to attribute their negative emotional experiences to the indifference of the excluders through metacognitive reappraisal processes. During this process, they gradually develop a cold, unresponsive defensive emotional reaction pattern, thereby reducing their likelihood of empathizing with others’ emotional experiences38. Research indicates that when individuals experience negative emotions, particularly those similar to others’ negative experiences, it may lead to heightened emotional distress, impairing their ability to accurately recognize others’ emotions39. Furthermore, egocentric perspectives can introduce biases in emotion recognition, especially in negative emotional contexts, where individuals are more prone to egocentric thinking patterns, affecting their accurate judgment of others’ emotions40. The fluctuations in emotional differentiation are significantly associated with empathy accuracy, particularly under stress, where individuals’ emotional differentiation abilities decline, impairing their empathy performance41. In summary, social exclusion significantly diminishes individuals’ empathy abilities by activating negative emotions and reinforcing egocentric thinking.

The decline in empathy has a direct impact on the emergence of malevolent creativity. Studies show that individuals lacking empathy are more likely to engage in various forms of direct or indirect aggressive behaviors42 and may even exhibit socially inappropriate behaviors43. When faced with unfair situations, individuals with lower emotional recognition and regulation abilities are more easily provoked, generating more novel solutions to retaliate against others in malevolent creative problem-solving tasks5. Neuroscientific research further reveals that during the generation of malevolent creative ideas, brain regions associated with interpersonal emotion recognition and understanding are less activated44. This suggests that weakened empathy may predispose individuals to channel their creative potential toward malevolent purposes. Additionally, empathy, as the ability to cognitively and emotionally resonate with others and respond appropriately, significantly reduces aggressive behaviors and promotes interpersonal harmony45. Therefore, individuals with lower empathy levels, due to their difficulty in sensing others’ emotional experiences, are more likely to exhibit higher levels of malevolent creativity5. Synthesizing these findings, it can be inferred that social exclusion increases malevolent creativity directly or indirectly by impairing individuals’ empathy abilities. Based on the above analysis, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H2: Empathy mediates the relationship between social exclusion and malevolent creativity among university students.

The mediating role of moral disengagement

As a negative environmental factor, social exclusion may significantly influence individuals’ moral cognition, particularly by inducing moral disengagement. Research indicates that adverse environmental factors, such as experiences of exclusion, can lead to moral cognitive biases, including the rationalization of immoral behaviors46. Initially, victimized individuals may recognize the wrongfulness of exclusionary acts, but excluders often employ blame attribution strategies, listing the victim’s faults to evade their own responsibility, thereby rationalizing their exclusionary behavior47. This dynamic creates a perceived moral cognitive discrepancy between the victim and the excluder, triggering moral cognitive dissonance48. To alleviate the distress caused by this dissonance, victims may convince themselves that “exclusion is normal,” increasing their acceptance of such behavior and ultimately fostering moral disengagement48. In other words, social exclusion elicits negative emotions in the excluded, making them view immoral acts as acceptable49. Additionally, excluded individuals may experience reduced self-monitoring and regulatory capacities due to psychological stress and perceived injustice, further exacerbating moral disengagement and even justifying immoral behaviors50.

Elevated levels of moral disengagement significantly increase individuals’ malevolent creativity. On one hand, moral disengagement may influence malevolent creativity through dehumanizing cognitive processes. For ordinary harmful behaviors, their consequences often negatively impact individuals’ public image51. To mitigate these negative effects, individuals with higher moral disengagement are more likely to leverage their creativity to fabricate rumors or invent false faults of others, thereby defaming and discrediting their opponents while justifying their harmful actions under the guise of “fighting fire with fire”52,53. On the other hand, moral disengagement may also affect malevolent creativity through moral justification. Malevolent creativity inherently involves harmful acts, and planning such behaviors can lead to self-condemnation, inducing feelings of unease and anxiety54. To seek self-comfort and escape these negative emotions, individuals may employ moral justification and outcome distortion to reinterpret and downplay the harmfulness and immorality of their actions55. If this cognitive process progresses smoothly, it further increases the risk of malevolent creativity. In summary, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H3: Moral disengagement mediates the relationship between social exclusion and malevolent creativity.

The chain mediating role of empathy and moral disengagement

The interplay between empathy, as a critical psychological mechanism, and moral disengagement can be understood from three distinct perspectives. First, from a cognitive standpoint, empathy is closely linked to perspective-taking. Individuals with high empathy are better equipped to understand and interpret social situations from others’ perspectives, thereby reducing the tendency to rationalize immoral behaviors56. For instance, individuals with advanced perspective-taking skills are more adept at recognizing others’ emotional reactions, which makes it difficult for them to justify their actions through moral disengagement mechanisms such as “advantageous comparison” or “diffusion of responsibility”57,58. This enhanced cognitive ability not only increases sensitivity to immoral behaviors but also encourages individuals to consider others’ interests and feelings in their decision-making processes. Second, from an emotional perspective, individuals with high empathy are more likely to experience others’ pain and negative emotions. Such emotional experiences prompt individuals to reflect on their own behaviors and reduce their reliance on moral disengagement59. For example, when individuals realize that their actions may harm others, their feelings of guilt and compassion are significantly heightened, thereby curbing the tendency to justify their behaviors through moral disengagement mechanisms. Third, empathy can further inhibit moral disengagement by strengthening individuals’ moral identity. Research indicates that individuals with high empathy are more inclined to internalize moral values as part of their self-identity. This internalization process not only reinforces a sense of moral responsibility but also predisposes individuals to choose morally aligned behaviors when facing ethical dilemmas59. In summary, empathy suppresses the occurrence of moral disengagement through multiple mechanisms, including enhanced cognitive understanding, evoked emotional responses, and strengthened moral identity. Building on the above analysis and Hypotheses H2 and H3, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H4: Empathy and moral disengagement play a chain mediating role in the relationship between social exclusion and malevolent creativity.

The moderating role of teacher support

Teacher support represents one of the most critical social relationships that students form and maintain within the school environment30. As children grow older, their reliance on parents diminishes, and the role of teacher-student relationships becomes increasingly significant in their lives. These relationships play a vital role in students’ social development, the formation of a healthy personality, the attainment of self-identity, and their ability to adapt to school life and achieve a sense of well-being60. Particularly in the context of social exclusion, teacher support plays a pivotal role. Social exclusion, as a negative experience, often triggers adverse emotions such as loneliness and anger, which may subsequently lead to malevolent creativity among students61. However, teacher support can effectively mitigate these negative effects through multiple mechanisms, thereby serving as a crucial moderating factor in the relationship between social exclusion and malevolent creativity among university students. First, teachers’ emotional support helps students cope with the emotional stress induced by social exclusion, reducing feelings of loneliness and anxiety62. When students perceive emotional care from their teachers, they are more likely to view social exclusion as a temporary setback rather than a denial of their personal worth, thereby alleviating the generation of negative emotions. Second, teachers’ social support provides students with opportunities for positive social interactions, enhancing their sense of social belonging and reducing the impact of social exclusion60. For instance, through participation in classroom discussions or group activities, students can rebuild connections with others, diminishing feelings of exclusion. Third, teachers’ instrumental support enhances students’ ability to cope with social exclusion63. Specifically, by offering personalized assistance and targeted psychological guidance, teachers can help students develop greater psychological resilience, enabling them to respond to social exclusion in more constructive and positive ways rather than resorting to destructive behaviors. Finally, teachers’ informational support, through the transmission of knowledge and skills, boosts students’ self-efficacy, thereby reducing their sensitivity to social exclusion64. When students are confident in their abilities, they are more likely to respond to social exclusion in proactive ways rather than engaging in harmful behaviors. In summary, teacher support, by providing emotional, social, instrumental, and informational assistance, effectively mitigates the negative emotions induced by social exclusion and reduces the likelihood of students exhibiting malevolent creativity. Based on the above analysis, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H5: Teacher support moderates the relationship between social exclusion and malevolent creativity among university students.

Methods

Sample and data collection procedures

The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the College of Textile and Fashion at Hunan Institute of Engineering on February 8, 2025 (Approval No.: EA-2025-016). Data collection was conducted online from March 11 to April 26, 2025. The questionnaire used for online data collection in this study is publicly available at the following link: 10.6084/m9.figshare.29553707.v1. The questionnaire was divided into three sections: the first section outlined the research objectives and significance, assured participants that the data would be used solely for scientific purposes, and included informed consent clauses. The second section collected demographic information, while the third section covered various variables, with randomized item order to minimize response bias.

Prior to finalizing the questionnaire, a group of university students was invited to provide feedback on its quality in two rounds, and improvements were made based on their input. After finalization, 60 university students were recruited for a pilot test, with 48 providing valid responses. The results indicated that the Cronbach’s alpha values for all variables exceeded 0.7, and all item factor loadings were greater than 0.6. Additionally, 89.6% (43 participants) found the questionnaire easy to understand, while 10.4% (5 participants) encountered minor difficulties. Furthermore, 85.4% (41 participants) completed the questionnaire in 10 to 14 min. These findings suggest that the questionnaire exhibits good reliability and validity, making it suitable for formal surveys and research.

To enhance sample diversity, the study enlisted the assistance of friends and acquaintances employed at universities to facilitate data collection. After obtaining their consent, the questionnaire link was distributed to them, and they randomly invited 30 university students from their respective institutions to participate. Ultimately, the study included 20 universities, with 9 located in the eastern region, 6 in the central region, and 5 in the western region. During the formal survey phase, 600 questionnaires were distributed, with 492 returned, yielding a response rate of 82%. To show our appreciation and encourage participation, every respondent was presented with a small gift worth less than 5 yuan upon finishing the survey. This incentive measure, compliant with ethical guidelines and common practice in social science research65, effectively promoted engagement while ensuring voluntary involvement.

In this research, invalid responses were eradicated via a three - step data cleansing procedure that adhered to well-established methodological literature65–68. The criteria were implemented sequentially to identify and eliminate haphazard, negligent, or inattentive responses: (1) Completion time: Responses with a completion time shorter than 6 min or longer than 18 min were excluded. This time interval was determined based on the pilot study results, where 85.4% of the actively - engaged participants completed the survey within 10–14 min. (2) Consecutive identical answers: Questionnaires in which the same answer was chosen for 7 or more consecutive items were discarded. This pattern implies a rather perfunctory attitude of the respondents. (3) Obvious logical errors: This was defined as: (a) failing to meet one or more instructed - response items (e.g., “Please select ‘trongly disagree’ or this item”), which were embedded as attention - checking elements; or (b) giving extreme and contradictory responses to reverse - scored items within the same construct, indicating inattention or poor comprehension. This multi - stage screening method enhances the reliability and validity of the retained data by systematically filtering out low - quality responses. Based on the aforementioned principles, out of the 492 questionnaires retrieved, a total of 457 valid questionnaires were retained for subsequent analysis.

Among the 457 valid responses, the gender distribution was relatively balanced, with a slight majority of female participants (52.3%). The distribution across academic years was even, with proportions of freshmen to seniors being similar. The majority of participants were aged 18–23 (87.9%), and most were from rural areas (56.0%). In terms of academic disciplines, the sample was predominantly composed of students in science and engineering (40.9%) and humanities and social sciences (31.1%). Measurement

Social exclusion was measured using a scale adapted from Niu et al.69, which included 6 items. One example statement is: “People treat me as if I were invisible.”

Empathy was measured using the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI) developed by Davis28. This scale comprises four dimensions: perspective taking, fantasy, empathic concern, and personal distress. Based on prior research, perspective taking and empathic concern are most closely associated with intergroup attitudes70. Therefore, this study selected these two dimensions (totaling 14 items) as the measurement indicators. Representative items include: “When I’m upset at someone, I usually try to ‘put myself in their shoes’ for a while” and “I would describe myself as a pretty soft-hearted person.”

Moral disengagement was operationalized using Moore et al.‘s71 abbreviated 5-item scale. An example statement is: “Victims of bullying usually bring it upon themselves.”

Teacher support was measured using a scale developed by Sun et al.72, which includes 5 items. An example statement is: “When classmates are bullied, does the teacher intervene promptly to resolve the situation?”

Malevolent creativity was assessed using a scale adapted from Hao et al.73, which consists of 13 items. These items categorize malevolent creativity behaviors in daily life into three types: hurting people (e.g., “How often do you think about ideas to take revenge when being unfairly treated?”), lying (e.g., “How often do you fabricate lies to simplify a problem situation?”), and playing tricks (e.g., “How often do you have ideas about how to pull pranks on others?”).

All items were scored using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

Tools

This study utilized Mplus8.0 for data analysis.

Sample common method bias test

In the questionnaire design phase, this study implemented a series of procedural measures to mitigate the influence of common method bias. The order of questionnaire items was meticulously arranged to prevent the emergence of response patterns, and respondent anonymity was strictly maintained throughout the process. Beyond procedural controls, statistical remedies were also applied. Specifically, the ​​latent common method factor approach​​ was employed within the structural equation modeling framework. The results showed that after incorporating the method factor, the model fit improved: RMSEA decreased by 0.01 and SRMR decreased by 0.02. ​​Correspondingly,​​ CFI and NFI increased by 0.04 and 0.03, respectively. To further assess common method variance, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted74. The results indicated that the first factor accounted for 37.47% of the total variance, which is below the commonly accepted threshold of 50%. This outcome confirms that common method bias was not a critical concern in this study.

These results collectively support hypotheses H1 through H4, indicating that empathy and moral disengagement mediate the relationship between social exclusion and malevolent creativity, and that they also function as chain mediators.

Discussion

This study confirms a robust positive correlation between social exclusion and malevolent creativity, validating and extending previous empirical and theoretical work. Based on the social information processing theory12 and the general aggression model33,34, the research results indicate that social exclusion, as a strong environmental stressor, triggers a series of psychological responses conducive to harmful creativity. Specifically, exclusion elicits negative emotions such as anger, frustration, and anxiety15,33, which motivate individuals to engage in creative aggressive behaviors, often as a form of revenge or compensation for control17,34. Moreover, social exclusion undermines normative moral constraints, creating a permissive cognitive environment where individuals are more inclined to use their creative abilities for harmful purposes35. These results are consistent with experimental evidence; for example, the study by Perchtold - Stefan et al.15 showed that excluded individuals demonstrated higher originality and harmfulness in malevolent creativity tasks. However, previous studies mainly used samples from the general population15,19,21–23, while this study extends these conclusions to Chinese university students - a group highly sensitive due to developmental vulnerabilities and limitations in collective living and social environments24,25. This extends the ecological validity and contextual applicability of the relationship between exclusion and malevolence and emphasizes the importance of testing this link in specific populations at high risk.

The dual mediation of empathy and moral disengagement clarifies the detailed psychological mechanism by which social exclusion leads to malevolent creativity. Drawing on the dynamic empathy model36 and the emotional numbing theory37, social exclusion weakens empathy by fostering egocentric bias and impairing emotional differentiation ability39,41, thereby reducing individuals’ concern for others’ well - being and decreasing the inhibition against harm5,28. Meanwhile, exclusion promotes moral disengagement through cognitive restructuring mechanisms that rationalize immoral behavior29,48,55. The chain mediation analysis shows that the decline in empathy significantly promotes moral disengagement, revealing a clear “emotion - cognition” mediation mechanism. This mechanism begins with emotional numbing and reduced empathy (i.e., emotional desensitization) triggered by social exclusion36,37, which in turn weakens the moral awareness of others’ pain; this emotional change lowers the psychological threshold for moral neutralization, making individuals more inclined to use mechanisms such as moral rationalization and responsibility shifting to justify harmful behaviors29,48,59. This result not only confirms the theoretical connection between emotion and moral cognition but also reveals a progressive internal action path between the two in the process of social exclusion leading to malevolent creativity. Previous studies mainly focused on isolated emotional paths (e.g., anger or hostility15,18 or cognitive paths (e.g., hostile attribution15. By capturing the dynamic interaction between emotional desensitization and cognitive restructuring, this integrated path provides a new perspective for further understanding the transmission mechanism between social exclusion and malevolent creativity.

Teacher support buffers the adverse effects of social exclusion through multiple protective mechanisms, covering emotional, social, instrumental, and informational dimensions. Emotionally, a supportive teacher - student relationship provides affirmation and comfort, alleviating the loneliness, anger, and anxiety that usually accompany exclusion62. Socially, teachers create an inclusive environment, restoring students’ sense of belonging and reducing the salience of exclusion experiences60. Instrumentally, teachers provide practical guidance and coping strategies, enhancing students’ self - efficacy and problem - solving abilities, enabling them to constructively address social challenges63. Informationally, teachers, as role models, reinforce prosocial norms and moral awareness, directly countering the mechanisms of moral disengagement64. Existing studies mainly emphasize internal regulatory variables such as self - construal75,76, while this study highlights the crucial role of external relationship resources. The buffering effect of teacher support is consistent with the social support theory30, but extends its application to malevolent creativity, indicating that environmental interventions can effectively break the exclusion - malevolence cycle and provide a practical lever for prevention programs.

Theoretical contribution

On one hand, existing research has primarily emphasized the direct effects of social exclusion on malevolent creativity through emotional factors such as anger and cognitive biases such as hostile attribution14–17, paying less attention to more complex socio-cognitive regulatory mechanisms. By introducing the dual-mediation and chain-mediation roles of empathy and moral disengagement, this study systematically reveals the multi-path psychological mechanisms through which social exclusion influences malevolent creativity. Specifically, empathy, as an affective mediator, reflects the profound effect of social exclusion on individuals’ emotional responses, while moral disengagement, as a cognitive mediator, illustrates how social exclusion weakens moral restraint and facilitates malevolent creativity. Furthermore, the proposed chain-mediation model clarifies the dynamic interaction between empathy and moral disengagement within the social exclusion–malevolent creativity relationship, offering a more integrated and nuanced theoretical framework for understanding this complex process. These mechanisms provide clear targets for intervention: programs aimed at reducing malevolent creativity can be improved by combining empathy enhancement through emotional training with moral reasoning training to counteract disengagement, thereby simultaneously targeting the affective and cognitive pathways revealed in this study. These findings not only extend the theoretical scope of existing research but also provide new perspectives and directions for future investigation.

On the other hand, prior studies have largely focused on internal individual factors such as emotions and cognition14–17, with limited attention to the moderating role of external environmental factors such as social support. By incorporating teacher support as a moderator, this study expands the scope of scholarly discourse. The results demonstrate that teacher support can effectively buffer the positive relationship between social exclusion and malevolent creativity among university students, underscoring the vital protective function of external environmental resources in counteracting the adverse effects of social exclusion60,63,64. This insight directly contributes to the design of systemic interventions that extend beyond individual-level approaches to include the relational environment. It offers a theoretical foundation for developing teacher-led programs that foster supportive classroom climates and serve as protective factors against exclusion. This theoretical advancement not only deepens the understanding of how social exclusion leads to malevolent creativity but also provides a scientific basis for the formulation of preventive and interventional strategies, demonstrating substantial practical significance.

Practical implications

First, the study found a positive correlation between social exclusion and malevolent creativity among university students, indicating that reducing social exclusion in educational environments is crucial for mitigating malevolent creativity. Educators should actively foster an inclusive campus atmosphere by organizing team-based activities and establishing fair evaluation mechanisms to minimize exclusion among students. For instance, teachers can design classroom tasks centered on collaboration, encouraging mutual support among students to reduce negative emotions and behaviors triggered by exclusion. Additionally, schools can offer psychological counseling and social skills training to help students better cope with exclusionary situations, thereby enhancing their psychological resilience and reducing the likelihood of malevolent creativity.

Second, the mediating roles of empathy and moral disengagement in the relationship between social exclusion and malevolent creativity suggest that educators should prioritize cultivating students’ empathy and moral awareness in intervention strategies. Teachers can employ methods such as situational simulations and role-playing to help students understand others’ emotions and perspectives, thereby strengthening their empathy. Simultaneously, moral education should be integrated into daily teaching through case analyses and ethical discussions to encourage students to reflect on their actions and reinforce their moral constraints. For example, teachers can design moral dilemma scenarios in class, prompting students to discuss and reflect, thereby reducing the occurrence of moral disengagement. These measures not only help diminish malevolent creativity but also promote students’ mental health and social adaptability.

Third, the moderating role of teacher support in the relationship between social exclusion and malevolent creativity highlights the significant role teachers play in helping students cope with exclusion. Teachers should proactively monitor students’ psychological well-being, promptly identifying and addressing potential exclusion issues. For instance, teachers can provide emotional support by regularly engaging with students and building trusting relationships. Additionally, positive feedback and encouragement from teachers can boost students’ confidence and sense of belonging, alleviating the negative effects of social exclusion. Schools should also enhance teacher training to improve their skills in psychological counseling and crisis intervention, enabling them to better support students in exclusionary situations.

Finally, a multi-level intervention mechanism involving schools, teachers, and parents should be established. Schools, teachers, and parents should collaborate to create a supportive educational environment. Schools can implement anti-exclusion policies and conduct mental health education to address exclusion at an institutional level. Parents, on the other hand, should pay attention to their children’s psychological needs and maintain communication with schools to jointly support their development. For example, schools can regularly organize parent workshops to raise awareness about the harms of social exclusion and effective coping strategies, helping parents better understand and support their children. Through coordinated efforts at multiple levels, the impact of social exclusion on university students’ malevolent creativity can be effectively reduced, fostering their healthy growth and holistic development.

Limitations and future directions

On one hand, the cross-sectional design employed in this study, while capable of revealing correlations among variables, imposes limitations on inferring causal relationships. The dynamic interplay between social exclusion, empathy, moral disengagement, and malevolent creativity may evolve over time, which cross-sectional data cannot capture. Future research could adopt a longitudinal design, collecting data at multiple time points to more accurately uncover the causal mechanisms among these variables. For instance, tracking the psychological and behavioral changes of university students across different semesters or academic years could validate the stability and long-term effects of the findings.

On the other hand, the sample in this study was exclusively drawn from China, lacking cross-cultural comparisons, which may limit the generalizability of the conclusions. The manifestations of social exclusion, the mechanisms of empathy and moral disengagement, and the effectiveness of teacher support may vary across different cultural contexts. Future studies could expand the sample to include participants from diverse countries and cultural backgrounds for cross-cultural comparative analyses. For example, comparing the impact of social exclusion on malevolent creativity in collectivist cultures (e.g., China) versus individualist cultures (e.g., the United States) could test the cultural adaptability of the findings. These improvements would contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between social exclusion and malevolent creativity and provide more universally applicable guidance for educational practices.

Conclusions

This study introduces variables such as empathy, moral disengagement, and teacher support to construct a moderated mediation model, aiming to explore the intrinsic mechanisms underlying the relationship between social exclusion and malevolent creativity. The findings reveal that: (1) social exclusion is positively associated with malevolent creativity among university students; (2) empathy mediates the relationship between social exclusion and malevolent creativity; (3) moral disengagement also mediates this relationship; (4) empathy and moral disengagement function as sequential mediators in the link between social exclusion and malevolent creativity; and (5) teacher support moderates the relationship between social exclusion and malevolent creativity. These conclusions not only enrich the theoretical framework of existing research but also provide novel insights and empirical evidence for the prevention and intervention of malevolent creativity.
