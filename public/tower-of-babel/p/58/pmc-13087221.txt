Peer aesthetic and social perceptions of anterior open bite in children
Authors: Êmilly Faria, Karina Maria Salvatore Freitas, Paula Cotrin, Fabricio Pinelli Valarelli, Júlio de Araújo Gurgel, Célia Regina Maio Pinzan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis cross-sectional study aimed to assess children’s aesthetic and social perceptions of anterior open bite (AOB). The sample comprised 272 evaluators, divided into three age groups: 4–6, 7–9, and 10–12 years old. Artificial intelligence was used to generate the children’s facial images, and Adobe Photoshop was used to modify their smiles, obtaining paired images of AOB and normal overbite. Participants completed a questionnaire that included images of children in their age range. An illustrated 5-point Likert scale was used to assess aesthetic rating. Social and aesthetic perceptions were evaluated using objective questions. Independent t-tests and chi-square were applied for data analysis. The aesthetic rating differed significantly, with smiles showing normal overbite judged as statistically significantly more attractive across all age groups. Among the objective responses, only the questions related to aesthetic perception differed between the occlusion statuses depicted. The social judgments were similar between them. An anterior open bite showed a negative impact on the aesthetic perception of children aged 4 to 12 years. The findings suggest that during counseling about the importance of discontinuing deleterious habits, professionals may present smiling photographs of children with and without AOB to reinforce the aesthetic benefits of stopping those habits.Keywords: Anterior open bite, Malocclusion, Interceptive orthodontics, EstheticsSubject terms: Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Malocclusion can affect not only oral function but also psychological and social aspects1–12. An unpleasant dental appearance can stigmatize a person, encourage negative stereotypes, and act as a factor associated with bullying10,12. Recently, Al-Bittar et al.1 observed a high incidence of discrimination (58.8%) among students, and about 20% of these cases were linked to their dental or facial appearance.

The aesthetic patterns of contemporary society demand a beautiful and harmonious smile, encouraging early orthodontic treatment to correct malocclusions4. Physical appearance may significantly influence how individuals perceive themselves and how others perceive them2,13. Prado et al.14 observed that adolescent orthodontic patients’ peers influenced their motivation to seek orthodontic treatment, demonstrating the potential impact friends and peers have on dental appearance. Therefore, the factors influencing orthodontic treatment decisions must consider function, aesthetics, psychology, and social perceptions.

The anterior open bite (AOB) is a common malocclusion in primary and mixed dentition15–17 and is particularly noticeable in interpersonal interactions, as occurs in the anterior arch segment. Previous studies have shown that certain malocclusions in the anterior teeth can affect oral functions18,19, child’s psychosocial well-being9, social judgment2,6,10,12, and quality of life3,5,8,12.

Studies regarding AOB self-perception are available3,5,17, but the results express only the individual feeling. However, perceptions of individuals by others can significantly impact their social interactions, potentially influencing their social integration and overall quality of life. Dion13 investigated the facial attractiveness of preschoolers (3 to 6 years old) by their classmates, demonstrating that young children can discriminate differences in facial attractiveness and social behavior perception, with the judgments aligning with those of adults. Therefore, peers’ perceptions of malocclusion cannot be underestimated; it is important to understand how malocclusion might influence others’ perceptions, not just the children’s self-perception.

Despite children’s aesthetic sensibility7,9,19, little is known about AOB’s aesthetic experience during their development, even though positive relationships with peers, especially in childhood, are crucial for emotional and social growth. Therefore, the purpose of the present study was to evaluate the peer aesthetic and social perceptions of children in different age ranges. Additionally, to assess whether the evaluator’s sex influenced their aesthetic and social perceptions. The null hypothesis proposed was that the aesthetic and social judgements are similar among children of different age ranges.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted between June and October 2024. Before data collection, approval was obtained from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Ingá University Center (protocol number: 60402622.5.0000.5220), and written informed consent was obtained from the parents or guardians and the enrolled children. This study was performed following the Declaration of Helsinki.

Questionnaires were developed using Google Forms (https://www.google.com/forms), which automatically generated a database of participant responses. The introductory section included a description of the informed consent process, as approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee, and explained the study’s objectives to the participants. All questionnaires were completed anonymously, with no personal identification requested. Only information regarding the participant’s sex, age group, and the presence of AOB was collected.

The evaluators’ inclusion criteria were children aged between 6 and 12 years, along with the agreement of the participant’s legal guardian and the child to participate. If children could not read the assent document independently, their parents or guardians could read it aloud and explain it to them. Subjects with cognitive disorders, syndromes, or lack of visual acuity as reported by parents; individuals with a history of previous orthopedic or orthodontic treatment; and those who did not cooperate during the administration of questionnaires were excluded from the study. The sample size calculation was performed using G*Power (version 3.1.9.4; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). with effect size 0.5, α: 0.05, power: 0.95, allocation ratio of 0.5, resulting in 79 participants in each group.

Artificial intelligence (AI) randomly generated 105 white children’s smile faces using the website https://this-person-does-not-exist.com/en. The facial features used to create images by the different age ranges were similar. The ages of the faces were set at preschool (4 to 6 years old), elementary school (7 to 9 years old), and middle school (10 to 12 years old). The smile faces generated were projected in a classroom and evaluated by 16 experienced orthodontists (practicing for at least 3 years), based on their similarity in facial features (nose, eyes, hair). Only when there was agreement among all the professionals was the face selected. Images of five male and five female subjects for each age range were chosen, totaling 30 pictures.

Using Adobe Photoshop software (Adobe Systems, San Jose, Calif, USA, Version CS6), two versions of the same AI-generated face were created: one image with an anterior open bite (before treatment) and one with a normal overbite (after treatment)2,4,20,21. The alterations were made exclusively to the children’s smiles, with all other facial features remaining unchanged2,4,20,21. This procedure ensured that all characteristics, except for the malocclusion status, remained consistent across the images, allowing for an accurate assessment of the influence of malocclusion on person perceptions and behavioral intention ratings4,20. All final images were standardized to the same size, rendered in black and white, and had a resolution of 300 dpi, saved in the .tif format.

For each age group, two parallel surveys (A and B) were developed, presenting AI-generated images of the child’s face that show a mix of either an AOB or a normal overbite smile2,4. If a child’s AOB smile image appeared in survey A, the corresponding normal overbite smile image of the same child was included in survey B2,4. To assess intra- and interrater reliability, two facial images were repeated. Therefore, each evaluator assessed a total of 12 images (6 with an anterior open bite and 6 showing normal overbite).

The questionnaire comprised two sections. At first, the aesthetic rating was evaluated using a 5-point Likert scale illustrated with a face image scale22. As younger participants could not be able to read and/or understand numbers, face illustrations were added, facilitating interpretation and comparisons among the different participants’ age range tested22–26. The response categories were given in the form of five faces ranging from (1) very ugly, (2) ugly, (3) moderate, (4) beautiful, (5) very beautiful. Children were given information about the scale in simple language and instructed to point their finger at the illustrated face reflecting their aesthetic perception. The scale was used by asking the following questions to the child: “Is this child beautiful?”

In the second part, the questionnaire included two objective questions regarding aesthetic perception (“Does this child have a beautiful smile?” and “Does this child have beautiful teeth?”) and four questions regarding social judgment (“Is this child cool?”; “Is this child intelligent?”; “Would you like this child to be your friend?”; “Is this child happy?”). The answers were objective: a simple “yes” or “no” response was required.

The questionnaires were always administered in the presence of one of the two previously calibrated researchers (EF, PC) using a 9th-generation 10.2-inch iPad (Apple, California, USA). Evaluators received directions on how to complete the survey. The respondents were approached in schools and shopping malls. They were not informed that the study was related to the appearance of malocclusion. Each evaluator received either survey A or survey B.

Two hundred seventy-two peer evaluators (male: 125; female: 147) successfully rated the images in one of the surveys by indicating their aesthetic and social perceptions. Eighty-eight children were included between the ages of 4 and 6 (male: 46; female: 42), ninety-seven between the ages of 7 and 9 male: 49; female: 48), and eighty-seven between the ages of 10 and 12 (male: 30; female: 57). A pilot study was conducted to assess children’s comprehension of the visual scale, their ability to follow instructions by selecting the illustrated face that best represented their aesthetic perception, and the clarity of the questions and language used. A total of 60 children (20 from each age group) participated in this preliminary phase. As no issues were identified, no modifications were necessary. The participants from the pilot study were excluded from the final sample and were not included in the statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

The Kappa test was applied to assess the intra- and interrater agreement.

The scale ratings were averaged to obtain the mean scores for each image (AOB and normal overbite) in the different age ranges.

The data were initially subjected to a normality test to verify the distribution, and based on the results obtained, statistical tests were selected for the comparisons. The aesthetic rating (5-point scale results) was evaluated for each age range and evaluator’s sex using an independent t-test. The Chi-square test was applied to evaluate the answers to the objective questions for each range group.

The data obtained were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Science (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp, USA). A 5% significance level (α = 0.05) was adopted.

Results

The intrarater error ranged from 0.743 to 0.953, and the interrater error from 0.713 to 0.932.

The photographs of children with a normal overbite were consistently rated higher on average than those of the same subjects with AOB. The analysis of aesthetic rating is presented in Table 2. Discussion

This study investigated whether the same target child would be perceived differently depending on the presence or absence of AOB malocclusion across different age groups. Additionally, it examined whether the occlusal condition of the target child affected observers’ aesthetic and social perceptions, as well as their willingness to engage in interpersonal interactions. The null hypothesis was partially accepted, indicating that the presence of AOB had a variable impact depending on the assessed dimension.

The results indicated that a normal overbite was perceived as more aesthetically pleasing across all age groups, suggesting that even preschool children can recognize the presence of AOB. When asked to assess the images using rating scales and objective questions, participants consistently evaluated smiles with AOB as less attractive (Tables 2, 3 and 4). A systematic review corroborated these findings, demonstrating that children aged 8 to 16 years place a high value on smile aesthetics, and that even those under 10 years old are sensitive to dental appearance11.

Furthermore, prior research has shown that the presence of AOB negatively affects self-perceived oral aesthetics and is associated with a reduced quality of life, while orthodontic correction leads to notable improvements in these perceptions and outcomes5. Consistent with these findings, earlier studies evaluating various types of anterior malocclusion also reported that normal occlusal traits are rated more positively in terms of appearance2,6. Shaw6 assessed the aesthetic perception of 840 children aged 11 to 13 years and found that aligned anterior teeth were perceived as more attractive than misaligned counterparts. Additionally, the study by Vingilis-Jaremko and Maurer27 highlighted that facial averageness influences attractiveness judgments from as early as five years of age. This suggests that such perceptions are deeply rooted and may play a significant role in shaping children’s social interactions and peer acceptance from a young age.

The comparison of aesthetic perception between male and female evaluators revealed no statistically significant differences, supporting the results of previous studies13,28. Dion13 found that facial attractiveness was equally valued by boys and girls at the preschool level (ages 3–6), indicating that gender does not significantly influence the perception of facial esthetics from an early age.

Although children with a normal overbite generally received more favorable responses than those with AOB malocclusion, statistical analyses indicated that social perceptions did not differ significantly between the two occlusal conditions across the evaluated age groups (Table 4). While facial attractiveness is often considered influential in peer acceptance, it is essential to note that, in this study, children of the same age group conducted evaluations, many of whom shared similar dental characteristics due to the transitional nature of the mixed dentition period. These shared traits may have influenced social acceptance dynamics3.

Additionally, the authors hypothesize that the temporary changes inherent to occlusal development may have mitigated the impact of malocclusion on social judgment, as children’s perceptions are shaped by their routine interactions with peers exhibiting comparable dentofacial features29. Interestingly, Martins-Junior et al.19 reported no significant associations between malocclusion and self-reported difficulties in smiling, suggesting that, even when children were concerned with their dental appearance, they did not adopt behaviors to conceal their smiles or suppress expressions of joy. Supporting this, Sheera12 and Maia et al.10 found that the occurrence of bullying was not associated with the presence of AOB.

The alignment of the anterior teeth appears to exert a greater influence on social judgment than the presence of an AOB. Shaw6 observed differences in social perception when comparing smiles with normal incisal arrangement to those with prominent or crowded incisors. Similarly, Henson et al.2 assessed how dental aesthetics influence various dimensions of social judgment, including athletic performance, popularity, leadership, and academic success, in individuals aged 10 to 16 years. Their findings indicated no statistically significant difference in the perception of academic performance, which is typically associated with intelligence, between individuals with ideal and non-ideal smiles. However, individuals with well-aligned anterior teeth were consistently perceived as more popular, more athletic, and as having stronger leadership potential.

Verdecchia et al.21 examined the effect of anterior dental alignment in children aged 8 to 10 on peer first impressions. They found that traits such as honesty, personal happiness, and intelligence were rated more favorably in children with well-aligned teeth. In contrast, traits like pleasantness and extroversion showed no notable differences. The disparity between those findings and the present study may be attributed to differences in the types of dental anomalies examined (e.g., alignment vs. AOB) and the developmental stage of the evaluators. AOB may not carry as much social significance for young children, especially since anterior teeth often lack contact during the early stages of eruption.

Furthermore, the children in the present study had not yet entered adolescence, a developmental period characterized by role confusion and identity exploration30. During adolescence, concerns about body image increase, and social acceptance become more important, which may enhance the salience of dental appearance in peer evaluations. Cultural context may also account for divergences across studies. Chen et al.31 suggested that aesthetic standards and social expectations vary across populations. Additionally, Gomes et al.17 demonstrated that social factors may play a critical role in the development of harmful oral habits, which are commonly associated with AOB malocclusion. Collectively, these findings reinforce the understanding that social perception is multifactorial and cannot be attributed solely to dental aesthetics. Variables such as age, cultural background, peer group norms, and developmental stage all contribute to how individuals are socially evaluated.

The AI-generated smiling faces used in this study shared similar facial features, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader traits and characteristics. Facial attractiveness is understood as a multifactorial construct influenced by both metric and non-metric elements. Perceptions of beauty are shaped by a combination of attributes such as skin tone, hair type, and facial expressions, as well as cultural factors intrinsic to the evaluators’ backgrounds4,8,17,20,32. For this reason, a standardized facial model was adopted in the present study to minimize potential bias associated with individual facial variations. Nevertheless, this methodological choice also represents a limitation, as it does not capture the diversity of facial appearances across different ethnicities or types of malocclusions. Therefore, future studies are encouraged to explore a broader range of variables, including ethnic diversity and other dental anomalies.

The findings of the present study reinforce existing evidence that children are perceptive to the aesthetic implications of AOB malocclusion. These results highlight the importance of incorporating subjective perceptions into orthodontic diagnosis and treatment timing decisions. Dental professionals should be attentive to the psychosocial impacts of AOB and consider early preventive and interceptive strategies to minimize potential negative effects on children’s aesthetic self-perception.

Furthermore, dentists play a role in educating families about the consequences of deleterious habits. The results suggest that using visual tools, such as comparative images of smiles with and without AOB, may effectively enhance children’s understanding and motivation to discontinue them. Such interventions could serve as practical aids in both behavioral guidance and malocclusion prevention during early childhood.

Conclusion

Children aged 4 to 12 years demonstrated that anterior open bite malocclusions negatively affect aesthetic perception, highlighting the importance of incorporating subjective factors into orthodontic diagnosis and determining the optimal timing for intervention.
