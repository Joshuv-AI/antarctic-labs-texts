Advanced recovery of bioactive polyphenols: integrated optimization of drying and green extraction for avocado seed by-products
Authors: Jorge G Figueroa, Isabel Borrás-Linares, Jesús Lozano-Sánchez, Rosa Quirantes-Piné, Antonio Segura-Carretero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractResponse surface methodology using a central composite design was applied to optimize the accelerated solvent extraction (ASE) of phenolic compounds from avocado seed and seed coat by-products. The optimal extraction conditions for both matrices were achieved at 200 °C using as extractant solvent a mixture of ethanol:water (50,50, v/v). Additionally, the drying process was optimized for avocado seed samples at different temperatures (45, 55, 65, 75, and 85 °C). Changes in the phenolic profiles during the drying and extraction processed were monitorized using HPLC-TOF-MS. A total of 42 and 52 phenolic compounds were individually identified and quantified in avocado seed and seed coat, respectively. The optimal drying temperature for avocado seed was 85 °C. In contrast, the seed coat exhibited different optimal temperatures depending on the phenolic group under research, with 75 °C being the most favourable for the majority of compounds evaluated.Keywords: Accelerated solvent extraction, Avocado seed, Avocado seed coat, Phenolic compounds, Air drying

Introduction

Avocado (Persea americana Mill.) is a tropical fruit native to Central America and Mexico (Salazar-López et al., 2020). Among its many varieties, the Hass cultivar is the most widely consumed and commercially important worldwide (Olmedo et al., 2024). This fruit is highly nutritious, providing significant amounts of unsaturated fatty acids (oleic, linoleic and linolenic acids), vitamins (B6, B12, C and E), minerals (potassium, phosphorus, calcium, iron and sodium) and essential amino acids (Barbosa-Martín, Chel-Guerrero, González-Mondragón, & Betancur-Ancona, 2016). Globally, avocados are consumed both as fresh fruit and in a variety of value-added products, including guacamole, processed avocado pulp, and avocado oil. Nevertheless, the industrial processing of these products produces a large amount of by-products, accounting for approximately 21–30 % of the fruit's total weight, which are typically discarded (Salazar-López et al., 2020).

Nowadays, there is a considerable interest in valorizing by-products as a source of bioactive compounds, particularly because their phenolic content can exceed that of the edible portions of fruits and vegetables. For instance, the total phenolic content (TPC) in the avocado seed has been reported to be several-fold higher than in the pulp (Rodríguez-Carpena, Morcuende, Andrade, Kylli, & Estévez, 2011; Widsten, Cruz, Fletcher, Pajak, & McGhie, 2014). Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated the potent antioxidant capacity of this by-product, confirming that avocado seed extracts exhibit remarkable free-radical scavenging activity mainly associated with their high polyphenolic content (Figueroa, Borrás-Linares, Lozano-Sánchez, & Segura-Carretero, 2018). Nevertheless, several studies have demonstrated that the impact of drying on the concentration of individual phenolic compounds is compound-specific (Esparza-Martínez, Miranda-López, & Guzman-Maldonado, 2016; Figueroa, Borrás-Linare, Lozano-Sánchez, Quirantes-Piné, & Segura-Carretero, 2018; Joshi, Rupasinghe, & Khanizadeh, 2011; Wojdyło, Figiel, & Oszmiański, 2009). Therefore, for each matrix an optimization of the drying process (mainly of the temperature and time) is required to maximize the content of phenolic compounds in the matrix after this procedure. In this sense, a previous study investigated the effect of drying on phenolic compounds in avocado seed (Saavedra et al., 2017). Nonetheless, solely four phenolic compounds were evaluated.

In addition, the extraction of bioactive compounds plays an important and crucial role in determining both the quality and potential applications of the resulting extracts in relation to their bioactive content. Thus, conventional extraction methods have frequently been employed to evaluate the potential of avocado by-products as sources of phenolic compounds (Calderón-Oliver et al., 2016; Kosińska et al., 2012; Pahua-Ramos et al., 2012; Ramos-Jerz, Villanueva, Jerz, Winterhalter, & Deters, 2013; Saavedra et al., 2017; Torres, Mau-Lastovicka, & Rezaaiyan, 1987; Widsten et al., 2014). Nevertheless, conventional solid–liquid extraction techniques such as Soxhlet extraction and dynamic maceration are time-consuming and require large volumes of solvents (Wijngaard, Hossain, Rai, & Brunton, 2012). In addition, many of these studies have utilized organic solvents that are not compatible with food, pharmaceutical, or cosmetic applications. For this reason, the development of purified extracts enriched in phenolic compounds through green extraction approaches based on the use of GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) solvents is required. One of these green extraction techniques is accelerated solvent extraction (ASE), also known as pressurized liquid extraction (PLE). ASE operates by applying solvents at temperatures above their boiling points and under sufficient pressure to maintain the solvent in the liquid phase (Herrero, Castro-Puyana, Mendiola, & Ibañez, 2013). The main advantages of this technique include reduced consumption of extracting solvents, shorter extraction times, and improved extraction yield and efficiency (Azmir et al., 2013; Moraes, Zabot, Prado, & Meireles, 2013). Moreover, there is a growing consumer demand for natural, non-synthetic ingredients, particularly in the food sector, and simultaneously a more sustainable food industry by reducing food waste and promoting the use of by-products for the development of new value-added commodities (Chauhan & Rao, 2024).

Furthermore, as far as we are concerned, no studies have assessed the effect of drying on total phenolic content (TPC) and the profile of individual phenolic compounds in the avocado seed coat. Considering the high phenolic content of avocado seed, further research is required to optimize both the drying and green extraction processes, with the aim of maximizing the recovery of their bioactive compounds. Accordingly, the objective of the present study was to optimize the drying conditions and accelerated solvent extraction (ASE) of phenolic compounds from avocado seed and seed coat by-products, in order to obtain extracts enriched in bioactive compounds suitable for their use as functional ingredients in the food, pharmaceutical, or cosmetic industries.

Material and methods

Chemicals and reagents

To ensure compatibility of the extract with applications in the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries, only ethanol and water were employed as extraction solvents. Ottawa sand was used as a dispersing agent. Ethanol and Ottawa sand were obtained from Fisher Scientific (Leicestershire, UK). Ultrapure water, with a conductivity below 18.2 MΩ·cm, was produced using a Milli-Q purification system (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA) and used for both extraction and analytical procedures. For the determination of total phenolic content (TPC), Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, sodium carbonate, and gallic acid were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany). Acetonitrile and formic acid, both HPLC-MS grade, were used for mobile phase preparation and were sourced from Fisher Scientific (Leicestershire, UK) and Fluka Chemika (Buchs, Switzerland), respectively.

For identification and quantification purposes the standards benzoic acid, (+)-catechin, chlorogenic acid, dihydrocaffeic acid, (−)-epicatechin, gentisic acid, 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, naringenin, neohesperedin, procyanidin dimer A2, procyanidin trimer B2, protocatechuic acid, quercetin, quercetin-3-β-glucoside and rutin were purchase either from Extrasynthese (Genay Cedex, France), Fluka Chemika (Buchs, Switzerland) or Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany).

Samples

Fresh avocado fruits (Persea americana Mill. cv. Hass) were kindly provided by the commercial group La Caña, Miguel García Sánchez e Hijos, S.A. (Motril, Spain). The fruits were allowed to ripen at ambient temperature. Whole avocado seeds were manually separated, rinsed under a continuous flow of tap water, and immediately dried.

Drying experiments

Avocado seeds were dried in an oven (Memmert UNE 200, Memmert, Germany) at five different temperatures: 45, 55, 65, 75, and 85 °C. Due to the hardness of the seeds, the drying process was carried out in two steps. First, the whole seeds were dried until the seed coat was naturally detached. Once the seed coat was carefully separated, the seed kernels were sliced and further dried until a final moisture content below 8 % was reached. Samples were periodically turned to ensure uniform drying. Drying kinetics, expressed as grams of water per gram of dry solids, were determined by periodic weighing of the seeds throughout the dehydration period. Subsequently, the dried materials (avocado seed and seed coat) was independently milled using an ultra-centrifugal mill (ZM 200, Retsch GmbH, Haan, Germany), and the resulting powders had an average particle size of 500 μm. The obtained material was stored avoiding light and humidity exposure and kept at room temperature until extraction. All the drying experiments were done in triplicate.

Accelerated solvent extraction

Accelerated solvent extraction was performed using an ASE 350 system (Dionex Corp., Sunnyvale, CA, USA) equipped with a solvent controller. Extraction conditions were optimized by applying response surface methodology (RSM) to evaluate the relationship between total phenolic content (TPC) and two independent variables: extraction temperature (40–200 °C) and solvent composition of a hydro-ethanolic mixture (0–100 % v/v EtOH). A central composite design (CCD) with two axial points was employed to structure the experimental design. The specific conditions tested are summarized in Table 1. All experimental runs were randomized to minimize the influence of uncontrolled variability on the response variables. For avocado seed, optimization was conducted across all drying temperatures. By contrast, the 65 °C drying condition was selected as the representative intermediate temperature for the avocado seed coat to ensure adequate sample homogeneity and prevent the degradation of thermolabile compounds prior to extraction. In addition, the limited availability of seed coat material restricted the possibility of conducting ASE optimization at multiple drying temperatures. Therefore, this temperature was used as a reference point to evaluate extraction efficiency, while the subsequent drying optimization experiments with optimized ASE parameters were designed to assess the temperature-dependent variation of individual phenolic classes. For each extraction experiment, 1 g of dried avocado seed or seed coat was homogeneously mixed with 5 g of Ottawa sand and loaded into a 34 mL stainless steel ASE vessel. Disposable cellulose filters were placed at either end of the cell to prevent the metal frits and instrument conductors from becoming clogged. Additionally, two 5 g portions of sand were placed between the sample and the cellulose filters to aid the dispersion and flow of the solvent. Prior to use, all solvents were degassed for 15 min to eliminate dissolved oxygen and reduce the risk of oxidative degradation of the phytochemicals. Extractions were conducted under static conditions for 20 min at 11 MPa. Subsequently, the cell was flushed with solvent (60 % of the cell volume) and purged with nitrogen for 100 s. The obtained extracts were immediately cooled on ice to room temperature and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C using a Sorvall ST 16R centrifuge (Thermo Scientific, Leicestershire, UK). The resulting supernatants were evaporated to dryness under vacuum (13 kPa) at 35 °C using a SC250EXP SpeedVac system (Thermo Scientific, Leicestershire, UK). Dried extracts were stored at −20 °C until further analysis. Each experimental condition was performed in duplicate.

Determination of total phenolic content (TPC)

Total phenolic content (TPC) was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu colorimetric assay, following the procedure described by Rodríguez-Pérez et al. (2016). Briefly, each dried extract was reconstituted in an appropriate volume of its corresponding extraction solvent to obtain a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. An aliquot of 10 μL of the diluted extract was mixed with 600 μL of distilled water and 50 μL of undiluted Folin–Ciocalteu reagent. After 10 min of reaction, 150 μL of 2 % (w/v) Na₂CO₃ solution was added, and the final volume was adjusted to 1.0 mL with water. The mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 2 h. Subsequently, 200 μL of the reaction mixture was transferred to a 96-well polystyrene microplate. Absorbance was measured at 760 nm using a Synergy™ Mx Monochromator-Based Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). TPC was quantified using a calibration curve constructed with gallic acid (calibration range of 5–150 μg/mL) prepared under the same conditions. Results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per gram of dry mass and reported as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3).

Quantification of individual phenolic compounds by HPLC-TOF-MS

The dryed extracts were reconstituted in the appropriate solvent at a concentration of 10 mg/mL, and prior to their analysis a syringe-filtration step with 0.45 μm regenerated cellulose filters was performed. The analytes were separated on an Agilent Zorbax Eclipse Plus C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm, 1.8 μm particle size) maintained at 25 °C, using as a precolumn RRLC in-line filters, 4.6 mm, 0.2 μm (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The mobile phase was composed of solvent A (water plus 1 % of formic acid, v/v) and solvent B (acetonitrile) at a constant flow rate of 0.8 mL/min. The following multi-step linear gradient was used in order to achieve efficient separation: 0 min (95,5, A,B), 30 min (75,25), 35 min (65,35), and 40 min (5,95). Finally, initial conditions were kept for 5 min at the end of each analysis to equilibrate the system before the subsequent injection. The sample volume injected was 10 μL. The UV spectra were recorded from 190 to 950 nm.

The HPLC system was coupled to a microTOF™ mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in negative ionization mode. The HPLC effluent was split using a T-type phase separator at a 1:3 ratio prior to introduction into the mass spectrometer, ensuring a stable spray and improved signal reproducibility. The optimum ESI-TOF-MS parameters were as follows: capillary voltage, 4500 V; drying gas temperature, 190 °C; drying gas flow, 9 L/min; nebulizer pressure, 2.0 bar; and end plate offset, −500 V. Ion transfer parameters were set as: capillary exit, −150 V; skimmer 1, −50 V; hexapole 1, −23 V; RF hexapole, 199 V; skimmer 2, −22.5 V. The trigger time was 53 μs (50 μs for transfer time and 3 μs for pre-pulse storage). Data acquisition was performed within a mass range of 50–1000 m/z.

External mass calibration was conducted at the beginning of each run using a 74,900–00-05 Cole Palmer syringe pump (Vernon Hills, IL, USA) directly connected to the instrument. The calibration solution consisted of a sodium formate cluster prepared in a mixture of 5 mM sodium hydroxide in water:2-propanol (1,1, v/v) containing 0.1 % formic acid (v/v). This solution was injected prior to each analysis, and all spectra were externally calibrated before the compound identification. Owing to the instrument's temperature drift compensation, this external calibration provided accurate mass measurements (within 5 ppm) throughout the run, eliminating the need for dual sprayer internal calibration.

Data aquisition was performed with HyStar 4.0 software whereas accurate mass data of molecular ions were processed using Data Analysis 4.0 software (Bruker Daltonics), which generated a list of possible elemental formulas via the Generate Molecular Formula™ editor. The software also provided theoretical isotope patterns, which were compared to the experimental data using sigma value analysis to enhance confidence in the proposed molecular formulas.

For compound quantification, seven calibration curves were constructed using nine concentration levels, each analyzed in triplicate. Calibration curves were prepared for the following standards: (+)-catechin, 5-O-caffeoylquinic acid, (−)-epicatechin, procyanidin A2, procyanidin B2, quercetin-3-β-glucoside, and rutin. Neohesperidin was employed as the internal standard at a concentration of 10 mg/L. Calibration curves of individual standards were generated by plotting the concentrations of each level against the relative peak area, defined as the ratio of the analyte peak area to that of the internal standard obtained for this run. Supplementary Table 1 presents the analytical parameters of each standard, including the calibration range, calibration equation, and coefficient of determination (r2). All calibration curves demonstrated excellent linearity across the tested concentration ranges. The analytical method had been previously validated in a previous study (Jorge G Figueroa, Borrás-Linares, et al., 2018).

The compounds´ concentrations of the samples were calculated by interpolating the relative peak area of each analyte (n = 3) into the corresponding calibration curve. In this regard, catechin, 5-O-caffeoylquinic acid, epicatechin, procyanidin A2, procyanidin B2, quercetin-3-β-glucoside, and rutin were quantified using their respective calibration curves. The remaining compounds were tentatively quantified using calibration curves of structurally related standards. It should be noted that minor differences in ionization efficiency between analytes and standards may affect accuracy; thus, these values represent estimations of the actual concentrations but provide a reliable and commonly accepted approximation. Specifically, the calibration curve of (+)-catechin was used to estimate concentrations of catechin derivatives; 5-O-caffeoylquinic acid for phenolic acids and phenolic alcohol derivatives; procyanidin A2 for type A procyanidins; procyanidin B2 for type B procyanidins; and rutin for flavonol derivatives.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using MINITAB software version 16.0 (Minitab Inc., State College, PA, USA). All results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three replicates per chemical determination. The optimization of ASE conditions was carried out using response surface methodology (RSM) based on a central composite design (CCD) with two axial points. In addition, data obtained for total phenolic content (TPC) and individual phenolic compounds were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to evaluate the effect of the five drying temperatures tested (45, 55, 65, 75, and 85 °C). Tukey's post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons, and differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. In addition, complementary correlation analyses were performed using R 4.5.1 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) within RStudio 2025.09.1 + 401 (Posit Software, Boston, MA, USA). Pairwise associations were quantified using Spearman's rank correlation, and clustered heatmaps were generated with the pheatmap package using correlation-based hierarchical clustering.

Results and discussion

Optimization of accelerated solvent extraction

ASE was employed to optimize the extraction of phenolic compounds from avocado seed and seed coat. Among the operational parameters, temperature and solvent composition have been identified as the most influential factors in the ASE process (Bitwell, Indra, Luke, & Kakoma, 2023; Matić et al., 2024). On the other hand, extraction pressure and duration have been reported to exert comparatively less influence on extraction efficiency (Herrero et al., 2013; Moraes et al., 2013; Mustafa & Turner, 2011). Accordingly, in the present study, the extraction pressure was fixed at 11 MPa to ensure that the solvent remained in the liquid phase, and the static extraction time was set to 20 min, sufficient to achieve equilibrium between compounds still bound to the matrix and those already solubilized while minimizing their degradation (Luthria, Biswas, & Natarajan, 2007; Miron, Plaza, Bahrim, Ibáñez, & Herrero, 2011; Wijngaard et al., 2012).

In this regard, extraction temperature is one of the most important extraction variables to control to improve ASE efficiency (Herrero et al., 2013). In this sense, raising the temperature increase the mass transfer and extraction rates because it decreases the viscosity and surface tension of the solvents improving its diffusivity between the matrix (Ramos, Kristenson, & Brinkman, 2002). Therefore, in general, the extraction yield of target compounds could be higher when the process is performed with high temperatures, provided that a compromise should be reached in the case of thermolabile compounds (Herrero et al., 2013). Furthermore, ASE offers a wide range of solvent polarity by changing the temperature (Ramos et al., 2002). For instance, water at 25 °C has a dielectric constant of ∼80 whereas at 250 °C present a dielectric constant between 25 and 27, which are similar values to less polar solvents at room temperature (Ollanketo, Peltoketo, Hartonen, Hiltunen, & Riekkola, 2002). Thus, temperature extraction was selected as factor to optimize in ASE of bioactive compounds of avocado seed by-products selecting a variable range from 40 to 200 °C (full operation range of the accelerated solvent extractor used).

The type of solvent is also a critical factor in extraction processes including ASE and it has been one of the most extensively studied variables in previous studies (Matić et al., 2024). The solvent must effectively solubilize the target compounds while minimizing the co-extraction of undesired matrix components (Azmir et al., 2013; Skalicka-Woźniak & Głowniak, 2012). Furthermore, for applications in the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries, it is recommended solvents classified as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) (Wijngaard et al., 2012). In this regard, water, ethanol and their hydroethanolic mixtures are considered among the most suitable solvents for the ASE of bioactive compounds from plant-based materials planned for these applications. In the present study, the full range of EtOH:H2O mixtures (0–100 % EtOH) was evaluated.

To evaluate the interactive effects between the studied variables, response surface methodology (RSM) was applied with the objective of maximizing the total phenolic content (TPC) in extracts from avocado seed and seed coat. A total of 26 extraction conditions were tested according to the experimental design (Table 1). TPC was significantly influenced by extraction temperature, with higher values observed at elevated temperatures. Similarly, regarding the ethanol-to-water ratio (EtOH:H₂O), a notable effect on TPC was also observed, with both matrices showing greater phenolic content when hydroethanolic mixtures were employed. As shown in Fig. 1a and b, the predicted maximum TPC lies outside the evaluated temperature range. Nevertheless, since the independent variables were explored within the full operational range of the ASE system, the original experimental design could not be expanded to determine the theoretical optimum. The optimal ASE conditions predicted by the RSM models for both avocado seed and seed coat are presented in Table 1. Nonetheless, the highest TPC values for both matrices were experimentally observed at an axial point corresponding to 200 °C and 50 % EtOH. Although not predicted as optimal by the model, these conditions yielded exceptionally high TPC values, which were nearly twice as high as those observed under other conditions. This outcome may be attributed to localized effects or specific temperature–solvent interactions that particularly enhanced polyphenol extraction at this axial point.

The adequacy of the response surface models was evaluated through residual diagnostics and the lack-of-fit (LOF) test. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S1, the normal probability plots of residuals revealed that the points were approximately aligned along a straight line, indicating that the residuals followed a normal distribution. The plots of standardized residuals versus fitted values exhibited no systematic trends or funnel-shaped patterns, confirming the homogeneity of variance and the absence of bias in model predictions. Likewise, the residuals versus observation order plots showed random dispersion around zero, suggesting that no experimental or time-dependent errors occurred during data acquisition. The histogram of residuals corroborated these findings, evidencing a symmetrical distribution centered on zero. Overall, these results validate the fundamental assumptions of normality, independence, and constant variance, which support the statistical robustness of the fitted quadratic models. Although a significant lack of fit (p < 0.05) was detected for some models, the coefficients of determination confirmed a strong agreement between predicted and experimental data, with R2 = 96.14 % (seed 45 °C), 94.62 % (seed 55 °C), 88.14 % (seed 65 °C), 78.55 % (seed 75 °C), 89.21 % (seed 85 °C), and 90.93 % (seed coat 65 °C). The slightly significant lack of fit observed in certain cases is likely due to the minimal pure error arising from closely replicated center points, which increases the test's sensitivity. Considering the high R2 values, the lack of systematic bias, and the satisfactory residual distribution, the developed RSM models were considered statistically adequate for describing the combined effects of ethanol percentage in the solvent mixture and drying temperature on the TPC of avocado seed and seed coat extracts.

The TPC obtained for both avocado seed and seed coat was strongly influenced by the solvent dielectric constant, with both matrices exhibiting similar trends (Fig. 1c). For all the assayed ASE conditions, avocado seed coat exhibit higher TPC than the seed kernel along the experimental range. The highest TPC values for both matrices were observed under ASE conditions corresponding to a dielectric constant of 26.0, achieved at 200 °C with 50 % ethanol. These findings are consistent with those reported by Ciulu et al. (2017), for the extraction of polyphenols from Stevia rebaudiana Bertoni leaves, where the highest TPC was found at a dielectric constant of 26.0.

Moreover, TPC decreased at both higher and lower dielectric constant values. Rodríguez-Carpena et al. (2011) investigated the influence of various organic solvents (ethyl acetate and acetone/water or methanol/water mixtures) on the TPC of avocado seed and reported that the acetone/water mixture, with a dielectric constant of 35.70 (Akerlof, 1932), yielded the highest TPC. These results suggest that for organic non-GRAS solvents, higher dielectric constants in the range of 36 may enhance the extraction of polyphenolic compounds from avocado seed, while for GRAS solvents the optimum dielectric constant is found at lower values. For both matrices, a deviation from the expected trend was observed at a dielectric constant of 24. A similar anomaly was previously reported by Ciulu et al. (2017) and Jorge G Figueroa, Borrás-Linares, et al. (2018) in studies involving Stevia rebaudiana leaves and avocado peel, respectively. This deviation may be attributed to the low extraction temperature applied under this condition, as Moraes et al. (2013) noted that higher extraction temperatures generally result in greater recovery of antioxidant compounds.

In addition, extraction yields for samples dried at 65 °C are presented in Fig. 1c. For both avocado seed and seed coat, the highest yields were observed at dielectric constant of 26.0, whereas the lowest yield corresponded to a dielectric constant of 19.0. Furthermore, when using the same solvent mixture, extraction temperature had a statistically significant effect (p ≤ 0.05) on extraction efficiency for both matrices. This result can be attributed to the increased solubility of phenolic compounds and enhanced mass transfer at elevated temperatures (Luthria, 2008). In addition, the avocado seed coat consistently exhibited significantly higher extraction yields (p ≤ 0.05) than the seed across all experimental conditions.

Effect of drying temperature on the recovery efficiency and total phenolic content of avocado seed and seed coat

Once the optimal ASE conditions were established, the effect of drying temperature on the TPC was evaluated. The initial moisture content of avocado seed was 53.3 ± 0.7 g water/100 g sample, which is consistent with the value reported by Saavedra et al. (2017) (52.68 ± 1.00 g water/100 g sample). As this moisture level promotes microbial spoilage during storage, a drying step was necessary for further use. Fig. 2a presents the drying kinetics of avocado seed at five temperatures (45, 55, 65, 75, and 85 °C). As expected, drying temperature had a clear influence on the drying rate, in agreement with previous studies (López et al., 2010; Mrad, Boudhrioua, Kechaou, Courtois, & Bonazzi, 2012). Due to the use of whole avocado seeds at the beginning of the process, an initial induction period was observed during the first hours. This was followed by a constant-rate drying phase until the samples reached the critical moisture content (∼0.5 g water/g dry sample). Thereafter, a falling-rate period occurred, associated with the evaporation of bound water and the shrinkage of the material (Mrad et al., 2012). The equilibrium moisture content was similar across all conditions, averaging approximately 0.07 g water/g dry sample.

(a) Drying kinetics of avocado seed at various temperatures; data points represent mean values and error bars correspond to the standard deviation. (b) Effect of solvent dielectric constant and drying temperature on the TPC (mg GAE/ g dry mass) of avocado seed; bars represent mean values and error bars indicate the standard deviation.

The time required to reach a final moisture content of 0.07 g water/g dry sample decreased as the drying temperature increased. Specifically, drying times at 45, 55, 65, 75, and 85 °C were 114, 81, 57, 44, and 39 h, respectively. These values were lower than those reported by Avhad and Marchetti (2015), who observed that 96 h were needed to reduce the weight by 45.59 % in non-pretreated Hass avocado seeds dried at 80 °C.

In addition, Avhad and Marchetti (2015) reported that high drying temperatures (80 °C) led to hardening and charring of the outer surface of avocado seeds. In contrast, only negligible visual changes in color were observed in the present study. This difference may be attributed to the reduction in particle size applied during the drying process, which increased the surface area of the sample improving the efficiency. The resulting enhancement in drying rate likely contributed to a shorter exposure time at elevated temperatures, thereby minimizing the occurrence of hardening or charring reactions.

The effect of drying temperature on extraction yield and TPC is presented in Table 2, corresponding to extracts obtained under the optimal ASE conditions (solvent dielectric constant of 26.0). The results indicate that drying temperature had a negligible effect on extraction yield in avocado seed samples. In contrast, for avocado seed coat, drying temperature significantly influenced extraction yield (p < 0.05), with the highest yield observed at 65 °C.

Regarding TPC, drying temperature exhibited a pronounced effect on the recovery of polyphenols, with the highest values for both avocado seed and seed coat observed at 85 °C whereas the lowest TPC at 45 °C for both samples. This behavior may be attributed to the prolonged drying times associated with lower temperatures, which could promote the degradation of phenolic compounds and result in reduced TPC. In fact, the drying time at 45 °C was nearly three times longer than that required at 85 °C.

Furthermore, for avocado seed, the effect of drying temperature was evaluated across all dielectric constants described in Section 3.1. As shown in Fig. 2b, TPC values varied widely, ranging from 0.3 to 29.5 mg GAE/g dry mass, depending on the combination of solvent dielectric constant and drying temperature. For all dielectric constants, as previously mentioned, the best drying temperature for the recovery of polyphenols is 85 °C, and the least effective is 45 °C, with intermediate values for medium drying temperatures.

The avocado seed coat contained significantly higher levels of total phenolic content (TPC) than the seed (p ≤ 0.05). Under optimal extraction conditions, TPC values were 71 ± 2 and 29.5 ± 0.4 mg GAE/100 g dry mass for the seed coat and seed, respectively. The TPC obtained for the seed was higher than those reported by Calderón-Oliver et al. (2016) and Morais et al. (2015), who employed water and methanol as extraction solvents. In contrast, other studies have reported higher TPC values using solvent mixtures containing water and organic solvents, particularly acetone (Rodríguez-Carpena et al., 2011; Saavedra et al., 2017). Nevertheless, the TPC levels found in the avocado seed coat in the present study were substantially higher than those previously reported for avocado seed. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify the TPC specifically in the avocado seed coat.

Effect of drying temperature on individuals phenolic and polar compounds of avocado seed and seed coat

According to the TPC results described in Section 3.2, the best drying temperature for both avocado seed and seed coat was 85 °C. Nevertheless, TPC values may be affected by the presence of non-phenolic reducing substances in the extracts, such as reducing sugars and ascorbic acid (Zhao et al., 2017). Therefore, in order to investigate the effect of drying temperature on individual phenolic compounds with antioxidant potential, a detailed phenolic compound characterization was performed by HPLC–TOF–MS analysis. In this context, 42 and 52 phenolic compounds were tentatively identified and quantified in avocado seed and seed coat, respectively. These compounds were classified into six sub-families, including phenolic acids, phenolic alcohol derivatives, flavonols, flavanones, flavanols, and procyanidins. The predominant groups were procyanidins and phenolic acids, with 16 and 11 compounds identified, respectively.

These compounds were tentatively identified based on the information provided by the mass spectrometer, through comparison of their mass spectra and elution time with available standards and a comprehensive review of the literature. Details of the identified compounds, including retention time, experimental and theoretical m/z, molecular formula, mass error, and mSigma values, are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

The effects of drying temperature on the major phenolic families are summarized in Table 3, while detailed information on individual phenolic compounds is provided in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4 for avocado seed and seed coat respectively. The results clearly indicate that drying temperature significantly influenced the concentration of all evaluated phenolic compounds in both avocado seed and seed coat samples (p ≤ 0.05). In avocado seed, the highest concentrations of phenolic acids were observed when drying was conducted at 85 °C. The only exception was 5-p-coumaroylquinic acid, which showed a slightly higher concentration at 75 °C. In contrast, phenolic acids were markedly affected by drying at 45 °C, with notable degradation. Among them, caffeoylquinic acid isomers were the most sensitive to temperature, exhibiting reductions of up to 94 % at the lowest drying temperature. Similar findings were reported by Esparza-Martínez et al. (2016), who observed that chlorogenic acid was the most affected phenolic acid during air-drying of lime wastes.

Additionally, three phenolic alcohol derivatives were identified in both matrices. The avocado seed coat showed higher concentrations of these compounds than the seed, reaching 190 ± 10 and 169 ± 2 mg/100 g dry matter, respectively. In avocado seed, the predominant compound was hydroxytyrosol glucoside (77 ± 1 mg/100 g dry matter), while in seed coat it was tyrosol-glucosyl-pentoside (110 ± 10 mg/100 g dry matter). The applied drying temperature significantly affected the levels of these compounds. In the seed, the highest concentrations were found at 85 °C, where values were up to five-fold higher than those obtained at 45 °C. In the seed coat, however, the maximum concentrations for all phenolic alcohol derivatives were observed at 65 °C.

Furthermore, five flavonols were identified and quantified in both matrices, including kaempferol, quercetin and its glucoside derivatives. Unlike the other phenolic groups, the highest flavonol concentrations were found at 75 °C in both samples (Supplementary Table 3). This behavior was mainly driven by quercetin, which showed a 25 % higher concentration at 75 °C compared to 85 °C in the seed. In the seed coat, quercetin-3-β-glucoside and quercetin reached their highest concentrations at 65 °C and 75 °C, respectively. These results may be explained by the relatively consistent drying time required to remove the seed coat across treatments, suggesting that temperature was the primary factor influencing phenolic compound degradation.

The flavanones ( ± )-naringenin and sakuranetin showed an increase in concentration with higher drying temperatures. Consequently, the highest levels of both compounds were observed at 85 °C in both avocado seed and seed coat. Esparza-Martínez et al. (2016) reported a gradual increase in the concentration of non-extractable ( ± )-naringenin with increasing drying temperatures, ranging from 60 °C to 120 °C, in lime waste. Though, the same study also found that the levels of extractable ( ± )-naringenin remained relatively constant across different drying temperatures.

The flavanols (+)-catechin and (−)-epicatechin were identified in both avocado seed and seed coat, being (+)-catechin the predominant flavanol in both matrices. In the seed, the highest concentration of flavanols was observed at 85 °C (199 ± 2 mg/100 g dry matter), while in the seed coat the maximum was reached at 75 °C (161 ± 5 mg/100 g dry matter). In the seed, both flavanols were notably affected by temperature, with a reduction of approximately 78 % when comparing concentrations at 85 °C and 45 °C. In contrast, in the seed coat, the highest concentrations of (+)-catechin and (−)-epicatechin were observed at 55 °C and 85 °C, respectively.

According to the obtained results, type A procyanidins seemed to be more thermostable than type B. In fact, type B dimers were completely degraded at 45 °C in avocado seed. Additionally, the concentration of type A dimers at 45 °C was approximately two-fold lower than at 85 °C in the avocado seed. In avocado seed coat, degradation of type B procyanidins was also more pronounced than that of type A across all drying treatments, with average reductions of approximately 51 % and 20 %, respectively. This difference in thermal stability may be attributed to their structural characteristics: type A procyanidins consist of flavan-3-ol units doubly linked through a C4–C8 bond and an additional ether bond between C2–O7, whereas type B procyanidins are linked solely by a single C4–C8 bond (Gu et al., 2003). Furthermore, procyanidin trimers exhibited greater thermal degradation than dimers, with maximum reductions of 88 % and 39 %, respectively. It should be noted that the total procyanidin content in the seed coat is much higher than in the seed.

In summary, increasing the dehydration temperature had a positive effect on the phenolic compound content in avocado seed for all the phenolic classes (Supplementary Table 3). Specifically, the total contents of phenolic acids, phenolic alcohol derivatives, flavanols, and procyanidins were more than five-fold higher at 85 °C compared to 45 °C. Similar findings were reported by Qin et al. (2017), for the study of the influence of drying temperature on phenolic compounds in Cimicifuga dahurica (Turcz.). In this study a gradual increase in phenolic acid concentrations was observed with rising temperatures. This effect may be attributed to the prolonged drying times at lower temperatures, which likely promoted thermal degradation of phenolic compounds, as previously reported by Gunjal et al. (2025).

On the other hand, as previously described, the time required to dry the seed coat was shorter than that needed for the avocado seed. Consequently, the effect of dehydration resulted in less pronounced degradation of phenolic compounds in the seed coat. Moreover, each phenolic group in the seed coat exhibited a distinct optimal drying temperature. Specifically, the highest concentrations of phenolic acids, flavonols, flavanols, and procyanidins were obtained at 75 °C, while the optimal temperatures for phenolic alcohol derivatives and flavanones were 65 °C and 85 °C, respectively (Supplementary Table 3). It is worth noting that, in contrast to the results obtained for avocado seed, the seed coat exhibited significantly higher (p < 0.05) concentrations of phenolic acids, phenolic alcohol derivatives, flavanones, flavonols, and procyanidins. Only the flavanol group showed slightly higher levels in the seed compared to the seed coat, with concentrations of 199 ± 2 and 161 ± 5 mg/100 g dry matter, respectively.

With respect to previously reported data, the contents of phenolic acids and flavonols in avocado seed were higher than those reported by Rodríguez-Carpena et al. (2011) for the same avocado variety. In contrast, procyanidin levels were lower than those reported by López-Cobo et al. (2016) and Rodríguez-Carpena et al. (2011), which may be attributed to thermal degradation. Indeed, both studies employed lyophilization as the drying method, a technique known to better preserve thermosensitive compounds such as procyanidins. Nevertheless, this drying process is considerably more expensive than conventional thermal drying and it can not be industrially scaled, one of the purpose of the present study. In fact, in the present study, the total procyanidin content decreased by approximately 81 % when the sample was dried at 45 °C compared to 85 °C, suggesting a marked thermal instability of these compounds (Di Mattia et al., 2013).

Fig. 3 shows the Spearman correlation heatmaps of the main phenolic compounds identified in avocado seed (a-c) and seed coat (d-f) after drying at 45–85 °C. Red tones represent positive correlations, whereas blue tones indicate negative correlations. The hierarchical dendrograms reveal groups of compounds exhibiting similar behavior in response to the applied thermal treatments. In the seed, all compound groups displayed predominantly positive correlations, reflecting a homogeneous thermal response and high stability of the phenolic profile during drying. Among hydroxycinnamic derivatives (Fig. 3a), quinic acid and its caffeoyl- and p-coumaroylquinic derivatives showed strongly positive associations, indicating that reactions such as partial hydrolysis, acyl migration, and isomerization occurred simultaneously and proportionally. These results are consistent with the reversible interconversion of chlorogenic acid isomers reported by Clifford (2000) and Dawidowicz and Typek (2010). For flavan-3-ols and procyanidins (Fig. 3b), strong positive correlations were observed between monomers ((+)-catechin, (−)-epicatechin) and oligomers, suggesting that oxidation or extensive depolymerization was minimal within the studied temperature range. The coordinated variation indicates thermal robustness of the condensed fraction under moderate heating, in agreement with controlled studies on catechin conversions and on cocoa systems subjected to processing (Hurst, Payne, Miller, & Stuart, 2009). Quercetin derivatives (Fig. 3c) also showed nearly perfect positive correlations among the aglycone and its mono- and diglycosylated forms, indicating simultaneous thermal release without differential degradation. Such behavior aligns with the reported resistance of quercetin glycosides to moderate heating in plant matrices (Rohn, Buchner, Driemel, Rauser, & Kroh, 2007).

Spearman correlation heatmaps (45–85 °C) for phenolic classes in avocado seed and seed coat: (a) seed, hydroxycinnamate–quinic derivatives; (b) seed, flavan-3-ols and procyanidins; (c) seed, quercetin and diglucosides; (d) seed coat, hydroxycinnamate–quinic derivatives; (e) seed coat, flavan-3-ols and procyanidins; (f) seed coat, quercetin and diglucosides.

By contrast, the seed coat exhibited a more heterogeneous pattern, showing both positive and negative associations among related families. In the hydroxycinnamate panel (Fig. 3d), the cluster of the chlorogenic-acid family, comprising caffeoyl-, p-coumaroyl- and feruloyl-quinic isomers, forms a tightly correlated module similar to that observed in the seed. Conversely, p-coumaric acid, vanillin and vanillic acid show negative associations with this chlorogenic-acid cluster, indicating that partial hydrolysis of the p-coumaroyl-quinic esters and mild oxidative reactions occurred during the shorter drying of the seed coat. The release of p-coumaric acid from 3-, 4- and 5-p-coumaroylquinic acids is chemically plausible through cleavage of the ester bond, while vanillin and vanillic acid may arise from ferulic-type precursors via oxidative transformations (Gallage et al., 2014). Furthermore, quinic acid correlates positively with most acyl-quinic isomers and more weakly or negatively with the free and oxidized compounds, suggesting that temperature primarily promoted interconversion within the esterified pool rather than extensive breakdown to free acids. In the flavan-3-ol and procyanidin group (Fig. 3e), the temperature response was also heterogeneous, reflecting competing thermal and oxidative reactions rather than simple extraction effects. Both (+)-catechin and (−)-epicatechin increased at moderate temperatures, although catechin exhibited a much stronger enhancement, while epicatechin remained nearly stable or slightly increased at higher temperatures. This divergent pattern indicates that the apparent enrichment of catechin results primarily from partial epimerization of (−)-epicatechin into its trans isomer, a reversible process promoted by moderate heating during the early stages of drying. The stability of most dimeric and trimeric species, together with the absence of marked losses across temperatures, suggests that no extensive depolymerization occurred, but instead local rearrangements and interflavan bond migrations within the condensed fraction. Finally, in the flavonol group (Fig. 3f), the seed coat showed weaker and partially negative correlations between quercetin and its glycosylated derivatives, contrasting with the nearly perfect associations observed in the seed. Concentrations of quercetin and quercetin-3-β-glucoside reached modest maxima at 65–75 °C, while both diglucosides displayed slight decreases at higher temperatures. This behavior indicates that, although temperature promoted partial hydrolysis of conjugated forms, the shorter drying time of the seed coat limited the attainment of equilibrium among glycosylated and free forms. The mild anticorrelation between the aglycone and the diglucosides suggests that a small fraction of quercetin may arise from thermally induced cleavage of glycosidic bonds, rather than from enhanced extractability. Overall, these results indicate that flavonol interconversion in the seed coat was limited and kinetically restricted, reinforcing the high thermal stability of quercetin conjugates during short drying periods (Rohn et al., 2007).

Future studies should include the use of pure reference standards to verify the proposed transformation pathways and to quantify the extent of compound interconversion during drying. Such validation would help distinguish true structural changes from apparent variations related to extraction efficiency or matrix effects. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the effect of drying temperature on the concentration of more than forty phenolic compounds in avocado seed and seed coat individually.

Conclusions

Drying pre-treatment and ASE of phenolic compounds from avocado seed and seed coat were optimized. The effects of two key parameters, temperature and solvent composition, on the extraction efficiency were systematically evaluated. The ASE conditions that resulted in the highest total phenolic content (TPC) for both matrices were 200 °C with a 50:50 (v/v) ethanol:water mixture. Based on TPC results, the optimal drying temperature was 85 °C for both avocado seed and seed coat, at which drying time was considerably shorter (approximately 39 h compared to 114 h at 45 °C), improving process throughput and energy efficiency. Under these optimal conditions, the maximum TPC values were 29.5 ± 0.4 mg GAE/g dry matter for the seed and 71 ± 2 mg GAE/g dry matter for the seed coat.

In addition, more than forty phenolic compounds were identified and quantified by HPLC-ESI-TOF-MS to assess the effect of drying temperature on the individual compound concentrations. The optimal drying temperature for avocado seed was confirmed to be 85 °C for the studied phenolic families. In contrast, the seed coat showed different optimal temperatures depending on the phenolic group: 75 °C for phenolic acids, flavonols, flavanols, and procyanidins; 65 °C for phenolic alcohol derivatives; and 85 °C for flavanones.

Overall, drying at 85 °C proved industrially feasible and compatible with conventional hot-air dryers, providing a scalable and cost-efficient approach for recovering phenolic-rich extracts from avocado by-products. The integrated optimization of drying and ASE represents a sustainable valorization pathway using only GRAS solvents suitable for food, nutraceutical, and cosmetic applications.
