The relationship between Problematic Social Media Use and academic performance among university students in the MENA region: a systematic review and exploratory meta-analysis
Authors: Deyae Zerrouk, Kenza Kassab, Achraf El Asri, Btissame Zarrouq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe usage of social networking sites (SNSs) represents one of the most significant behavioral shifts in recent years. WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, X, and YouTube, among others, have become an inseparable part of our daily life, especially in the present hyperconnected era. However, the use of SNSs can shift from a normal daily habit to an excessive and hard-to-control behavior, negatively affecting physical and mental health and causing Problematic Social Media Use (PSMU). PSMU is a serious problem that continues to grow with the ongoing development of new SNSs and the increasing integration of artificial intelligence (AI) features, influencing the SNSs’ metrics and algorithms, personalizing content, strengthening platform engagement, and attracting an even greater number of users. In this context, exploring the effects of PSMU requires more attention, especially among a population at high risk: university students. One relevant question that arises when considering both social media use (SMU) and university life is academic performance (AP) and how PSMU affects it. The present review aims to address this question and explore the relationship between PSMU and AP, with a focus on university students in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, where social media adoption is widespread and the prevalences are continuously increasing.MethodologyThe present review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. We first developed our research equation via the Boolean research strategy. We subsequently conducted a deep search and careful filtration to select consistent studies that met our inclusion and exclusion criteria across four databases, namely, PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and ScienceDirect. We included only quantitative observational cross-sectional studies that (i) explored the association between PSMU and AP, (ii) included university students from the MENA region only, and (iii) evaluated PSMU relying on scales, not merely by time spent and frequency of use.ResultsOur screening process resulted in the selection of eleven studies. By analyzing and comparing their findings, most included studies reported a negative association between PSMU and AP among university students in the MENA region. An exploratory meta-analysis, conducted to examine variability in effect sizes, yielded a negative, non-statistically significant pooled association (r = -0.114, 95% CI: -0.283 to 0.054), with substantial heterogeneity observed across studies (I2 = 76.86%). These findings highlight marked variability in the reported associations and underscore the need for more standardized methodologies to support more robust future synthesis.ConclusionWhile our review identified a negative association between PSMU and AP among university students in the MENA region based on the narrative synthesis, findings from the exploratory meta-analysis highlighted substantial variability in effect sizes, limiting the ability to derive a consistent quantitative estimate. Furthermore, causal inferences remain limited due to the cross-sectional nature of the included studies. Given the limited number of available studies, inconsistency in PSMU measurement, variability in the operationalization of AP, and lack of data on mediating mechanisms, further region-specific research is essential. Beyond cross-sectional designs, longitudinal and interventional studies with clearer reporting practices are warranted to advance understanding of this relationship.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04354-3.Keywords: Problematic social media use, Social media addiction, Social networking site addiction, University students, Academic performance, Middle East and North Africa

Introduction

With the rapid growth of digital technologies, social media (SM) has occupied an important place in our routines; we have become increasingly dependent on social networking platforms, spending time browsing, posting, liking, sharing, etc.

Given this increasing dependence, tracing the origins of SM is important. The history of social networking sites (SNSs) began at the end of the 1990s, more precisely, in 1997, when Andrew Weinreich launched the first existing SNS, SixDegrees.com [1]. This shows how early these platforms emerged and how they quietly began to sneak into our lives. However, despite their omnipresence, the meaning of SM is rarely discussed currently, and when it is, it is often described through examples of SNSs, which limits its full understanding. A rigorous, wide-ranging, and timeless definition was developed by Carr and Hayes [2]: "Social media are internet-based channels that allow users to opportunistically interact and selectively self-present, either in real-time or asynchronously, with both broad and narrow audiences who derive value from user-generated content and the perception of interaction with others.” [2]. This definition captures the communicative conditions that make a medium social and highlights the gaps overlooked by earlier definitions that focused mainly on technological affordances, Web 2.0 ideation, and SNS examples, often conflating SM with SNSs and neglecting core communicative features such as distrainment, perceived interactivity, persistence, and mass personal communication [2].

After defining SM, it’s necessary to examine its increasing use, especially given the continuous emergence of new SNSs such as Threads, Noplace, and Bluesky, among others. According to the Digital 2025 July report, the number of active users reached 5.41 billion, representing 65.7% of the global population [3]. Moreover, the same report indicated that the number of user identities has increased by 4.7%, with approximately 241 million new users joining the SM compared with the same period last year [3]. Even more alarming is the amount of time spent on these platforms, as users spend an average of 141 min each day on them [4]. This daily usage is dominated by five platforms, headed by TikTok, followed by YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, and WhatsApp [3]. MENA countries are not an exception, as SM is highly prevalent, with several nations leading the world in its adoption and engagement. As of February 2025, the United Arab Emirates (UAE) had reached full SM penetration (100%), followed by Qatar (84%), Kuwait (80.1%), and Bahrain (73.2%), all of which had high levels of adoption [5–8]. SM’s high penetration in the MENA region is accompanied by intensive daily use, with users in Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Morocco, and Egypt spending nearly three hours per day on these platforms in February 2025, which is well above the global average [9].

Such intensive engagement has raised growing concerns over problematic patterns of use, with a growing body of evidence indicating that excessive SNSs use may produce symptoms comparable to substance-related addictions [10]. Importantly, this reflects a shift from normative SMU to PSMU, which involves impaired control and negative consequences. These PSMU-related symptoms have been described in the component model of Griffiths, based on a biopsychological framework, and include six components: salience, mood modification, tolerance, withdrawal, relapse, and conflict [11]. Although PSMU, also referred to as social media use disorder (SMUD), has not yet met the criteria of an officially recognized behavioral addiction, it is not classified as a disorder within the addiction framework in major psychiatric classification systems such as the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th edition, text revision (DSM-5-TR) and the International Classification of Diseases, 11th revision (ICD-11) [12, 13].

In parallel with the ongoing debate over its classification, epidemiological evidence highlights the global burden of PSMU. Multiple studies have consistently shown a high prevalence of PSMU across different age groups. For example, a systematic review conducted in 2021, which synthesized data from 32 nations, reported an overall pooled prevalence of 24% (95% CI: 21%−28%). They also performed a subgroup analysis and reported estimates of 19% (95% CI: 12%−27%) among community-aged adults and 23% (95% CI: 18%−28%) among university students [14]. This systematic review revealed an important finding, which is the high prevalence among university students. Moreover, another recent systematic review conducted in 2023, which included 35,520 students from several countries, reported a pooled prevalence of 18.4% (95% CI: 14.7%−22.6%) [15]. According to this review, the prevalence of PSMU in the MENA region was clearly higher than the global average, exceeding 30% in Lebanon (39.85%), Iran (39.22%), and the UAE (38.41%) and surpassing 50% in Oman (58%) and Saudi Arabia (57%) [16–20].

These high prevalence rates raise an important question: what drives PSMU, particularly among university students? The widespread and pervasive appeal of SNSs is largely explained by their role in facilitating social capital, defined as “the sum of the resources, actual or virtual, that accrue to an individual or a group by virtue of possessing a durable network of more or less institutionalized relationships of mutual acquaintance and recognition” [21]. Traditionally, social capital research has demonstrated the benefits of social networks, as they provide users with new information and different perspectives (bridging capital), in addition to emotional support (bonding capital) [22]. This finding shows that students use SNSs to meet their informational and emotional needs. Furthermore, biological processes, including the brain's reward system, also play a role in maintaining SNS engagement. Neuroimaging studies have shown that receiving and giving likes, for example, stimulates the reward system in the brain, especially in the striatum and ventral tegmental area, causing dopamine release and encouraging compulsive use [23]. However, those examples capture just one side of the phenomenon since the PSMU is molded by multiple factors.

By considering the generational profile of today's students, a further explanation emerges. Zoomers, Generation Z, or Gen Z students, have grown up surrounded by digital technologies, and SM has been integrated into their everyday lives. Prensky best describes Gen Z as 'Digital Natives', reflecting their early childhood immersion in technology [24]. As they enter university, they are in emerging adulthood, a stage marked by changes in developmental status, demographic diversity, instability, and identity exploration [25]. Therefore, university years are a particularly intense period for SNS use since university students use these platforms to connect, express themselves, and build their identity.

This finding was supported by qualitative evidence, suggesting that the transition from high school to college for Gen Z students involves concrete changes in their practical lives and is characterized by a new sense of independence, reflected in freedom from parental control, new social circles, exposure to different cultural and class influences, and an intense focus on visual self-reinvention [26, 27]. During this period, SM plays a central role as both a mediating environment and a performative space where young people explore autonomy, authenticity, and belonging. It provides them with a “clean slate” to try new versions of themselves, explore different identities, and present maturity and independence [26]. However, this freedom also brings pressure and intensified visibility, making the transition both a period of empowerment and a source of anxiety, as youth construct and project new identities in digital spaces while navigating shifting social expectations [26].

Given the central role of SM in identity exploration during emerging adulthood and the variability in how students experience and engage with these platforms, understanding social media use (SMU) among university students requires theoretical frameworks capable of explaining why individuals use SM for more than basic communication purposes and why its effects differ across users. Over several decades, media researchers have proposed multiple explanatory models, later synthesized into three overarching paradigms by Patti M. Valkenburg [28]. The first paradigm is the selectivity paradigm, which suggests that SM users, including university students, can process only a limited portion of available content, and that this selection is influenced by their individual orientations, needs, and aspirations [28]. Consequently, only the media content with which users actively engage is likely to influence them [28]. The second paradigm, the transactionality paradigm, emphasizes how users’ dispositions, developmental stages, and social contexts jointly shape both their patterns of SMU and its effects [28, 29]. Within this perspective, the focus shifts from media exposure alone to the dynamic interaction between the user and the media, implying that media effects depend not only on the content consumed but also on individual characteristics and situational factors [28]. The third paradigm, the conditionality paradigm, builds on these principles by proposing that SM effects are not uniform across individuals and may be strengthened or attenuated by personal traits, developmental status, and social context [28, 29].

Together, these explanatory frameworks provide a conceptual basis for understanding variability in SM effects; however, empirical evidence further confirms that intensive and problematic use can have measurable consequences for students’ psychological well-being. Over the past few decades, a large body of research has explored these effects across college students. Psychologically, PSMU has been linked to increased levels of stress, anxiety, and depression symptoms, as well as loneliness, emotional dysregulation, and psychological distress [30–34]. Behaviorally, it has been linked to lower conscientiousness traits and greater motor impulsivity, among other negative outcomes [32]. These findings fall under the broader umbrella of PSMU. However, within this context, especially among university students, one important area has received less attention: academic performance (AP). University students today use SNSs in both their personal and educational lives, which may influence their academic achievement either positively or negatively.

The use of SM in higher education has influenced the way in which students learn, interact, and communicate. SNS has several benefits, such as making learning more engaging and collaborative, encouraging the development of cognitive skills and openness, supporting learning management systems by improving accessibility and communication, and strengthening institutional connections and community spirit [35]. However, their integration remains debated due to challenges such as distraction, limited digital skills, and institutional resistance [36]. This raises an important question: what happens when SMU shifts from a healthy, purposeful activity to addictive behavior? When it becomes problematic, how does it affect the AP of university students?

This question has drawn the attention of researchers. For example, a global meta-analysis that analyzed the findings of 16 studies conducted up to 2022 revealed a pooled negative correlation of r = −0.172 (95% CI: −0.320—(−0.016)) between social networking addiction (SNA) and AP [37]. These results clearly indicate that PSMU and AP are indeed linked, as observed across diverse contexts, highlighting the importance of examining this problem across different regions, including MENA. In this region, the prevalence of PSMU is notably high, often exceeding 50%. For example, 57% of Moroccan university students and 55.2% of Saudi medical students were found to be addicted, whereas the rate was comparatively lower among Iranian students, reaching 44% [38–40].

In light of these findings, a closer examination of the relationship between PSMU and AP in the MENA region is warranted. To our knowledge, no previous systematic review has specifically synthesized evidence on this relationship within this region. Given the high prevalence of PSMU, the rapid digitalization of societies, and potential cultural and educational specificities, a region-focused synthesis is needed. Therefore, the main objective of the present review is to systematically examine the relationship between PSMU and AP among university students in the MENA region, complemented by an exploratory meta-analysis providing a preliminary quantitative synthesis and examining variability in effect sizes across the available studies.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [41]. To capture newly published studies and guarantee that no potentially eligible articles were missed, we fully repeated the systematic search and screening process in June 2025 after an initial search was conducted in August 2024 across four databases: Web of Science, PubMed, ScienceDirect, and Scopus. Email alerts were also activated in all four databases, and notifications of newly published studies matching the search were automatically sent to the principal author. The study protocol was registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO reference number CRD42024575886).

Search strategy

In the present systematic review, first, we aimed to investigate how PSMU affects MENA university students. However, the term "impact" alone is too general, since the PSMU can affect various aspects of students' lives. Therefore, we needed to determine which particular factor should be considered. To do so, we used the Boolean search strategy to investigate different determinants and select the most relevant one. This approach relies on connecting keywords based on the power of AND, OR, and NOT operators. The study adopted the following research equation: ("Social media* addiction*" OR "Social media* dependenc*" OR "Social media* disorder" OR "Social media* use*" OR "Problematic social media* use*" OR "Compulsive social media* use*" OR "Excessive social media* use*" OR "YouTube addiction*" OR "YouTube use*" OR "Problematic YouTube use*" OR "Facebook addiction*" OR "Facebook use*" OR "Problematic Facebook use*" OR "YouTube addiction*" OR "YouTube use*" OR "Problematic Youtube use*" OR "Instagram addiction*" OR "Instagram use*" OR "Problematic Instagram use*" OR "Tiktok addiction*" OR "Tiktok use*" OR "Problematic Tiktok use*") AND ("University students" OR "Colleg* students" OR "Undergraduate students" OR "Postsecondary students" OR "Higher education students" OR "Tertiary students" OR "Bachelor's students" OR "Master's students" OR "PhD students" OR "Doctoral students") AND ("MENA" OR "Middle East and North Africa" OR "Algeria*" OR "Bahrain*" OR "Egypt*" OR "Iran*" OR "Iraq*" OR "Israel*" OR "Jordan*" OR "Kuwait*" OR "Lebanon*" OR "Lebanese*" OR "Libya*" OR "Morocco*" OR "Oman*" OR "Qatar*" OR "Saudi Arabia*" OR "Palestine*" OR "Syria*" OR "Tunisia*" OR "United Arab Emirate*" OR "Yemen*"). After the literature was explored on the basis of our initial research equation, AP was chosen as a key factor. As a final step, to ensure that we did not miss any studies that must be included, we conducted an additional search, adding the term "AP" and its related keywords to our research equation.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies that addressed the relationship between PSMU and AP, either directly or indirectly, were included in the review. For those that assessed this relationship indirectly, the condition of inclusion was the presence of statistical evidence supporting the link, not just separate analyses (e.g., PSMU → stress; stress → AP). Additionally, we selected publications based on the following criteria: only quantitative observational studies (cross-sectional, case‒control, and cohort studies) conducted solely in the MENA region and focused on university students. Full-text articles written in either English or French were considered. Additionally, we excluded qualitative and experimental research. Studies carried out outside the MENA region, even if they included MENA students (e.g., Moroccan students at Oxford University), were excluded from the review. Furthermore, we did not include studies that relied solely on time spent or usage frequency as indicators of SMU and did not use a validated scale to evaluate problematic use itself. We also excluded research that addressed its role in education, such as those focused on collaborative learning, interaction with teachers, and students’ engagement. Articles that explored students’ perceptions or opinions rather than objectively measured behaviors or outcomes were also excluded. In terms of AP, studies were eligible for inclusion if operationalized through objective metrics (e.g., Grade Point Average, standardized test scores) or validated scales assessing perceived academic functioning and decline. Finally, there were no restrictions regarding age or sex, and there was no time limit for this review.

Data extraction

Data extraction for eligible studies was carried out and independently verified by two authors (DZ and KK) via a standardized form in Microsoft Excel. First, study characteristics and participant demographics, including authors, year of the study, objectives, study design, country, university/college, sample size, gender, age, and students' major, were extracted. Second, SMU details, such as the PSMU measure, the most and least frequently used SM platforms, and the time spent on SM, were extracted. Third, details about AP were gathered. Finally, the results of the relationship between PSMU and AP were examined. This approach allowed for an independent comparison of SMU among university students in the MENA region, AP separately, and, finally, the association between the two. This strategic approach enables a comprehensive comparison across all three aspects. Any discrepancies in the extracted data were resolved through discussions with another independent author (BZ).

Quality assessment

To assess the quality of the studies included in this review, we applied the National Institute of Health Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-sectional Studies [40]. According to this tool, we first appraised fundamental key elements: the quality of the research objective, the specificity and definition of the study population, participation rates, and uniformity in the recruitment of participants from the same or similar populations. We then delved into the appraisal of methodological metrics: justification of sample size and assessment of different levels of exposure (e.g., level of addiction to SM). We also looked at whether exposures and outcomes were well-defined, valid, reliable, and executed. Subsequently, we ascertained if studies identified and statistically adjusted for key confounding variables that may impact the association between the exposure of interest, PSMU, and the outcome of interest, AP. Finally, each of these studies was rated as having good, fair, or poor quality based on the overall risk of bias, the strength of the methodological approach, and the study's ability to accurately assess associations between exposure and outcome. No study was excluded based on the quality assessment (Table 1).

Data analysis and publication bias

While this study is primarily a systematic review, we supplemented the narrative synthesis with an exploratory meta-analysis. The objective was to quantitatively synthesize the available evidence to examine the overall direction and magnitude of the association while assessing the extent and potential sources of between-study heterogeneity. This approach enables the generation of targeted hypotheses regarding the complex relationship between PSMU and AP. For the quantitative synthesis, only studies that used correlation-based statistics were included. The reported r values were first converted into Fisher’s Z scores to stabilize variance across studies. For each study, we calculated the Fisher’s Z-transformed effect size, its standard error, and the 95% confidence interval. A random-effects model was applied to account for differences between studies. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q and the I2 index, where 25%, 50%, and 75% indicate low, moderate, and high variability. Publication bias was examined using a funnel plot and further tested with Egger’s regression test and Begg’s rank correlation test to detect small-study effects. All analyses were performed using JASP software (version 0.19.3.0).

Results

Study selection

Following the research strategy previously detailed, we initially identified a total of 3077 articles from four databases (Fig. 1). We then started our review process by screening titles and excluding studies based on type, such as systematic reviews, meta-analyses, theses, and conference papers. Next, we removed studies that did not align with the scope of our review. Therefore, we proceeded with abstract screening, carefully reviewing and double-checking each study. All studies that did not directly explore the relationship between PSMU and AP from our intended perspective were excluded at this stage, resulting in a final selection of 58 articles. A meticulous review of the full texts was then carried out to ensure that each study clearly and reliably addressed the relationship between PSMU and AP. To finalize our selection, we applied a final quality check based on the PEO framework. Studies were not included if the population (P) of interest was not university students (P), if the exposure (E) was general SMU or lacked a measure of problematic use, or if the outcome (O) did not assess AP. During this stage, qualitative and experimental studies were also excluded. After applying all the filters, 11 studies met all the inclusion criteria and were selected for inclusion in this systematic review.

Sample characteristics

Eleven articles from the MENA region met our inclusion criteria and were included in our systematic review. A substantial portion of these articles were carried out in Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries, specifically Saudi Arabia (n = 4), the United Arab Emirates (UAE) (n = 1), and Qatar (n = 1) [39, 43, 44, 46, 50, 51]. Outside the GCC countries, four studies were conducted in Egypt, Iran, Jordan, and Lebanon [42, 44, 47–49]. In addition, one study followed a unique strategy and included participants from various countries, including Jordan, the United Arab Emirates, and Saudi Arabia, bringing the total number of study contributions to five for Saudi Arabia and two each for the UAE and Jordan [39, 43–46, 49, 51]. In summary, seven out of 19 MENA countries were covered (Fig. 2).

Geographical distribution of studies on the relationship between PSMU and AP in the MENA region (5 inclusions from Saudi Arabia, 2 from Jordan, 2 from the UAE, and 1 each from Egypt, Iran, Lebanon, and Qatar)

Among the eleven included studies, eight explicitly reported the year of data collection and were conducted between 2015 and 2020 [43–45, 47–51]. Three studies did not report the year of data collection; however, they were published in 2019, 2023, and 2025, respectively [39, 42, 46]. It is important to note that the publication year does not necessarily reflect the timing of data collection, and therefore the exact study periods for these articles remain uncertain. Overall, the included studies span pre-pandemic and pandemic periods, with no studies explicitly conducted in the post-pandemic era.

The sample sizes of the eleven studies ranged from 273 to 997 students, and the participants’ ages ranged from 17 to 44 years. However, the majority of the studies targeted students aged 18 to 25 years, with a focus on younger university students. This was also confirmed by the mean age, which ranged from 21.0 to 25.4 years. All studies included both males and females, with a high proportion of females in most studies. In terms of academic majors, nearly all the studies focused on students in medical and health-related fields (e.g., medicine, pharmacy, nursing, dentistry). On the other hand, other studies included various disciplines, such as engineering and IT, education, humanities, economics, literature, social sciences, law, and business (Table 2).

PSMU measurement tools and their psychometric properties

Across the 11 studies included in the review, four different scales were used to evaluate PSMU among university students. Among these instruments, the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (BSMAS) was the most frequently used, appearing in four studies, followed by the Social Media Addiction Scale (SMAS), adapted from the Internet Addiction Test (IAT), which appeared in three studies. Additionally, the Bergen Facebook Addiction Scale (BFAS) and the Chen Internet Addiction Scale-Social Networking Site (CIAS-SNS), an adapted version of the Chen Internet Addiction Scale (CIAS), appeared less frequently, each in two studies.

For the psychometric properties, all 4 measures showed acceptable to excellent internal consistency, as reflected by the overall Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.74 to 0.94, indicating the strong reliability of the instruments used across the studies, except for the SMAS scale, which reported only subscale alpha values ranging from 0.61 to 0.75, a range that is still considered acceptable. Moving to validity, which was evaluated using Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), as in the case of the BFAS and BSMAS, good results were obtained in model testing (e.g., CFI = 0.96–0.99, TLI = 0.93–0.94, RMSEA = 0.046–0.066), confirming their theoretical one-factor structures. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was also used for the BFAS (Egyptian version), SMAS, and CIAS-SNS. The results supported one-, three-, and four-factor solutions. These factors match key aspects of behavioral addiction, such as tolerance, compulsion and withdrawal, time management, and interpersonal problems. In addition to the CFI and EFA analysis, the CIAS also showed diagnostic validity via Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis, indicating strong discriminatory power (AUC = 0.896). Together, these psychometric results support the accuracy of the four scales used across our included studies (Table 3).

SMU patterns

Across the eleven studies, several patterns were explored. First, we chose to focus on the most and least frequently used SM apps among the university students included in our review. WhatsApp was the leading platform in terms of frequency of use, with a usage rate of up to 95.4%, followed by Snapchat (up to 89.3%), Instagram (up to 88%), and YouTube (up to 85.9%) [45, 46, 50]. Twitter ranked as the fifth most used app, followed by Facebook and TikTok, although with lower usage rates ranging from 75.8% to 58.1% [39, 44, 45]. However, in some studies, the rankings changed, with Facebook and Twitter appearing among the least used platforms, alongside Telegram and Skype [39, 44–46]. This variation highlights the diversity and variability in SM preferences across studies.

After identifying the most and least commonly used SM platforms, it is also important to examine how much time students actually spend on these platforms, with a particular focus on their daily usage. Data on this aspect are limited to a few studies. Among those, more than 40% of the participants reported spending more than four to five hours per day on SM, with one study indicating that up to 62.5% exceeded this cutoff [44, 45, 47, 50]. Nevertheless, less than 5% of students reported spending less than one hour per day on SM [44, 45, 50]. Some studies have provided platform-specific data, showing that between 14.6% and 23.3% of students spend more than three hours per day on Snapchat, WhatsApp, and Twitter specifically [46]. These findings suggest a high level of SMU among university students in the MENA region on the basis of the included studies (Table 4).

PSMU prevalence, mean scores and distribution

The results related to PSMU were presented through prevalence rates, mean scores, and categorical classifications. The prevalence was reported in only two studies [39, 42]. The first study revealed that approximately half of the students (55.2%) were addicted to SM, whereas the second study focused only on Facebook addiction (FA) and reported a prevalence of 16.6%. In addition to reporting the prevalence, some studies reported the mean scores found based on the scales they used. Regarding the BSMAS, studies that specifically adopted the 30-point version of this tool reported similar results, as the mean score ranged from 16.6 ± 5.06 to 16.89 ± 5.95, indicating a moderate level of addiction [39, 44]. Similarly, a study that applied the 90-point version of the BSMAS reported an average score of 50.83 ± 13.00 [48]. Another scale, the SNS-adapted Chen Internet Addiction Scale (SNS-CIAS), was used in two studies. The first study reported a mean total score of 61.85 ± 13.28 out of 104, placing students in the “engaged” range. In the second study, mean dimension scores ranged from 2.60 to 3.09 on a 5-point Likert scale indicating relatively low levels of PSMU, although the withdrawal dimension slightly exceeded the proposed problematic threshold (> 3) [45, 50]. When addiction was represented using different categories, moderate addiction remained the most prevalent category. This was supported by two studies in which over two-thirds of participants (68.93% and 70.6%, respectively) were classified as moderately addicted [43, 48]. Severe addiction was less prevalent, with rates ranging from 7.83% to 11.7% [43, 48] (Table 4).

Academic performance

Across the 11 studies, AP was predominantly assessed using the GPA. However, two studies relied on alternative objective indicators, with one reporting end-of-semester grades and another reporting students’ academic scores from the previous academic year [42, 43]. One study represented an exception, as it did not rely on GPA-based indicators and instead assessed AP using a more holistic measure, namely the Academic Performance Decrement Scale (APDS). This instrument, used by Homaid (2022), was adapted from a study by Kubey et al. (2001) and applied by Malik et al. (2020) [45, 62, 63]. In this study, the APDS demonstrated high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.897 [45].

Most studies represented GPA both as continuous data (mean ± SD) and categorical distributions. The results of the reported GPAs indicate an intermediate performance. The mean GPA ranged from 2.87 ± 0.70 to 3.27 ± 0.45, reflecting good to very good performance [50, 51]. Similar results were found when GPA was categorized; the most common AP level was "Good" to "Very Good" in many studies reporting that more than 60% of participants belonged to these categories [39, 44, 49]. On the other hand, excellent performance was less common, with percentages ranging from 13.7% to 45.3% [42, 49]. An interesting finding was synthesized from a study that reported GPA scores based on FA, revealing decreased AP among university students with addiction compared with their nonaddicted peers [42]. In the study by Homaid (2022), the results differed, as the APDS does not measure absolute AP (such as GPA or test scores), but rather assesses whether AP has declined. The mean APDS score was 2.84 ± 1.16 out of 5, indicating a moderate decline in AP [45] (Table 4).

Statistical methods used

Different statistical analysis methods have been used to explore the relationship between PSMU and AP. Among the methods used, correlation analysis was the most common. In many studies, Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlations were used to assess the strength and direction of the associations evaluated [43, 48–51]. In other studies, group comparisons were conducted using ANOVA, t-tests, and Chi-square tests to compare SMA across AP levels, or vice versa, depending on the study design [42, 44, 50]. To account for confounding variables, many studies have used logistic regression models, including both univariable and multivariable analyses, whereas others have applied multiple linear regression [39, 44, 46, 47]. Additionally, Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM), which is a more advanced analysis, was used by two studies to explore both direct and indirect relationships among variables [45, 49] (Table 5).

The PSMU and AP

A negative relationship between PSMU and AP was revealed in the majority of the included studies (n = 9), and none of them reported a positive relationship. Furthermore, two studies conducted by Malak et al. (2021) and A. M. Alkhalaf et al. (2018) reported no significant associations [49, 51]. Diving into the details of the negative associations revealed, a recent study conducted by Alhrahsheh and Al Majali (2023) reported an r = −0.189 (p ≤ 0.01), indicating a significant negative relationship [43]. Similarly, Al-Yafi et al. (2018) found a negative but slightly stronger significant correlation (r = −0.23, p = 0.01). Studies that adjusted for confounding variables presented stronger associations [50]. For example, Alhusban et al. (2022) reported an OR = 0.92 (95% CI: 0.86–0.98, p = 0.009), indicating that higher levels of PSMU decreased the odds of achieving high AP after adjusting for age, gender, university major, SM app usage, and BSMAS scores [44].

Consistent findings were also observed when AP was operationalized as academic performance decrement (APD) rather than GPAs. Homaid (2022) reported a strong positive direct effect of PSMU on APD (β = 0.685, p < 0.001), indicating that higher levels of PSMU were associated with greater declines in AP [45]. Even when focusing on the problematic use of specific platforms, the relationship remains negative, as confirmed by a study conducted by Abu-Sneineh et al. (2019) that focused on Instagram and WhatsApp [46]. The results of this study show an OR = 1.776 (95% CI: 1.028–3.070, p = 0.040) for Instagram, indicating that students moderately addicted to this platform were more likely to have lower AP [46]. For WhatsApp, the study revealed an OR = 0.525 (95% CI: 0.330–0.834, p = 0.006), suggesting that students with higher AP were less likely to demonstrate addictive use of the app [46] (Table 5).

Factors associated with PSMU

Five studies examined gender differences in PSMU. Three of them reported a significant association, with males showing higher problematic SM scores than their female counterparts did. Alfaya et al. (2023) reported that they scored 4.52 points higher on the BSMAS, Alhrahsheh and Al Majali (2023) reported a mean difference of 2.10 points in their favor, and Azizi et al. (2019) observed significantly higher scores across all BSMAS subscales [39, 43, 48]. Concerning age, a study conducted by Alfaya et al. (2023) was the only one that reported a significant association out of four studies that examined this factor. Higher scores were observed among older students aged 21–23 years in comparison to those aged 18–20 years (17.99 vs 14.80, p = 0.006) [39]. Interestingly, PSMU was strongly associated with mental health problems, particularly stress, anxiety, and depression. Mohamed et al. (2025) reported that 16.6% of medical students with FA exhibited higher levels of depression (50% extremely severe), anxiety (67.3% extremely severe), and stress (28.8% extremely severe) [42]. Similarly, Alfaya et al. (2023) reported that students who were identified as having symptoms of depression (20.90 vs 14.56) and anxiety (21.42 vs 14.71) had higher levels of addiction to SM compared to non-addicted ones [39]. These findings were also supported by the multiple linear regression analysis conducted in the same study, where depression (β = + 1.85, 95% CI: 1.10–3.02, p = 0.005) and anxiety (β = + 2.79, 95% CI: 0.95–4.62, p = 0.003) were still significant predictors of higher addiction scores after adjusting for sociodemographic, academic, behavioral, and physical activity variables [39].

Other factors, including sleep-related issues, were also associated with SMA. Sleep quantity was proven to be negatively associated with FA, whereas students addicted to WhatsApp experienced more sleep disturbances, with 41.3% sleeping more than 8 h per day compared with 26.8% of nonaddicted students [42, 51]. In addition to the previously mentioned factors, another relevant factor linked to addiction itself was explored by Mohamed et al. (2025): uncontrollable internet use [42]. Their study found that 96.2% of Facebook-addicted students experienced uncontrollable internet use, compared to 80.1% of their non-addicted peers (χ2 = 24.859, p < 0.001) [42].

Mediating factors in the relationship between PSMU and AP

Among the included studies, two studies examined psychological mechanisms underlying the relationship between PSMU and AP using a Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) approach. Malak et al. (2021) focused on stress, anxiety, and depression, initially reporting that SMA was significantly associated with anxiety, depression, and stress, but not with AP. The study began by analyzing correlations among five variables, including anxiety, AP, depression, SMA, and stress. The results of this first investigation revealed that SMA was associated with anxiety, depression, and stress but not with AP. Following this, and in order to test the indirect relationships between the variables, a Structural Equation Modeling analysis using Partial Least Squares (SEM-PLS) was subsequently conducted. The results indicated that SMA may influence students’ stress levels, which in turn could lead to anxiety and eventually cause depression [SMA → Stress → Anxiety → Depression (β = 0.096, t = 5.915, p = 0.00)]. However, the study did not find any direct, nor indirect relationship between SMA and AP [49]. Homaid (2022), using a stressor–strain–outcome (SSO) framework, examined technostress and exhaustion as mediating factors and demonstrated that PSMU significantly increased both constructs, which in turn contributed to APD [45]. Specifically, technostress (β = 0.355, p < 0.001) and exhaustion (β = 0.188, p < 0.001) significantly mediated the relationship between PSMU and APD [45] (Table 5).

Factors associated with AP

Factors related to AP were explored in four studies. Among the variables evaluated, sociodemographic factors, specifically sex and age, were the first to be associated with AP. The study conducted by Abu-Sneineh et al. (2019) reported that sex was significantly associated with AP based on both adjusted and unadjusted logistic regression analyses [46]. First, the univariate analysis revealed that female students had nearly triple the odds of achieving an excellent GPA (≥ 4.5) compared with male students (OR = 2.953; 95% CI: 1.943–4.488; p < 0.001) [46]. This association remained significant after adjusting for age, marital status, housing situation, smoking status, sleep quality, and Instagram/WhatsApp addiction [46]. Compared with male students, female students were 2.2 times more likely to report excellent AP (AOR = 2.226; 95% CI: 1.333–3.716; p = 0.002) [46]. Similar results were reported by Alhusban et al. (2022), who reported a significant difference in AP between male and female students when adjusting for age, gender, university major, SM app usage, and BSMAS scores (OR = 0.482; 95% CI: 0.19–1.00; p = 0.05) [44].

As previously mentioned, another sociodemographic factor that has been proven to be associated with AP is age. Younger students were more likely to achieve better GPA scores (OR = 0.879; 95% CI: 0.794–0.972; p = 0.012) [46]. This result was strongly supported by Hamam et al. (2022), whose multiple regression analysis revealed that as age increased, not only did GPA decrease (β = − 0.12; 95% CI: − 0.078–0.025), but other AP-related indicators were also negatively affected; specifically, the passing rate decreased (β = − 0.15; 95% CI: − 0.025 to − 0.010), and the absence rate increased (β = + 0.15; 95% CI: 0.485–1.183) [47]. These findings are based on a multivariate model adjusted for gender, university area, daily hours spent on SM, and three categories of SMA (social consequences, compulsive feelings, and time displacement). Marital status, place of residence, and living situation were also explored as potential factors related to AP. The results revealed that being single, living with family, and living in rural areas could impact AP. More precisely, Abu-Sneineh et al. (2019) reported in their univariate analysis that single students had higher odds of academic success (OR = 0.314; 95% CI: 0.141–0.699; p = 0.005), similar to students who live with their families (OR = 0.323; 95% CI: 0.186–0.563; p = 0.001) [46]. In contrast, living in rural areas was negatively associated with AP (β = − 0.10; 95% CI: − 0.320 to − 0.067) in the Hamam et al. (2022) multivariate model [47].

Out of sociodemographic factors, AP was related to several aspects of SMU. Alhusban et al. (2022) reported that students with lower AP were significantly more likely to report that SMU interfered with exam preparation (14.3% vs. 4.1%; χ2 = 18.3; p = 0.02) and reported more disruption due to nighttime usage, making it harder to wake up and attend morning lectures (16.1% vs. 2.7%; χ2 = 16.4; p = 0.04) [44]. Additionally, Homaid (2022) found that time spent on SM had a significant effect on the APD [45].

Heterogeneity measures

An exploratory random-effects meta-analysis of correlation coefficients (r) was conducted to provide a preliminary quantitative assessment of the relationship between PSMU and AP and to examine variability in effect sizes, including four studies (n = 1526). These were the only studies that reported Pearson’s or an equivalent correlation measure; thus, the remaining studies could not be included because of the incomparability of the statistical analyses. The pooled effect size was small and negative (r = −0.114, 95% CI: −0.283 to 0.054), indicating a weak inverse association, although it was not statistically significant (p = 0.120) (Fig. 3). High heterogeneity was observed among the four included studies, as confirmed by the I2 statistic, which was 76.86% (95% CI: 29.65% to 98.24%), suggesting that a large portion of the observed variance was due to real differences rather than sampling error. The Q test was also significant (Q = 13.97, df = 3, p = 0.003), confirming this heterogeneity.

Forest plot of the correlation between PSMU and AP

Contributing factors to heterogeneity

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I2 = 76.87%), indicating considerable variability in effect sizes. In line with the exploratory nature of the meta-analysis, this variability was examined in relation to study-level characteristics rather than assuming a common underlying effect. Several factors may contribute to this heterogeneity. First, the number of studies included in the meta-analysis was limited (n = 4), which may affect the stability of the pooled estimate. Second, the included studies differed in the measurement of PSMU, reflecting the use of multiple assessment tools. Additionally, AP was operationalized using different indicators, including GPA, semester grades, and the APD scale, indicating variability in its measurement across studies. Third, the meta-analysis was based on correlation coefficients, representing unadjusted associations across studies. Differences in study characteristics, including sample composition (e.g., field of study) and methodological approaches, were also observed. Finally, variations in study settings, including differences in countries, institutions, and educational contexts, were present across the included studies and may further contribute to these differences.

Publication bias

According to the funnel plot, the studies appeared fairly symmetrical around the mean effect size, although two studies fell outside the funnel boundaries. This slight asymmetry does not clearly suggest publication bias. Egger’s test (z = –438, p = 0.661) and Begg’s test (τ = –0.333, p = 0.750) also revealed no significant asymmetry. However, with only four studies, these results should be interpreted with caution (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Prevalence of PSMU

Within the set of studies included in this review, the prevalence of PSMU was reported by only two, highlighting the limited availability of prevalence data in the included literature [39, 42]. It should be noted, however, that other studies in the MENA region have examined the prevalence more extensively as this was their primary objective rather than the examination of the relationship between PSMU and AP [38, 64]. Among the two included studies that reported the prevalence, the first was conducted by Alfaya et al. (2023) in Saudi Arabia and showed that more than half of the students (55.2%) were addicted to SM [39]. At the regional level, this prevalence falls within the range observed in non-included MENA countries, such as Morocco (57%) and Tunisia (31.3%) [38, 42, 64]. At the international level, this prevalence is three times higher than the global pooled prevalence, which was estimated at 18.4%, as reported by a recent systematic review and meta-analysis [15]. This may be related to several factors. First, the sample size of the study was small (n = 326). However, this hypothesis does not align with the findings of the global systematic review, which examined the effect of sample size on prevalence and reported that larger sample sizes were associated with higher PSMU prevalence [15]. Second, the characteristics of the study population, as the study focused exclusively on medical students, a specific group with particular characteristics. This point is important, since the prevalence rates may vary across fields of study, as students from different disciplines do not share the same patterns of use. For example, in the present review, the included studies involved both medical and non-medical students. Medical students often use SNSs differently than non-medical students do, which may in turn influence the overall use of SNSs. In fact, a multicentric study demonstrated this difference and showed that medical students use SNSs more frequently for educational and professional purposes, whereas their non-medical peers predominantly use these platforms for social connection [65]. These findings suggest that sociocultural, environmental, and contextual factors may also influence PSMU and highlight the need for more studies in the MENA region to establish a pooled regional prevalence and identify the main determinants, neither of which has been comprehensively examined in the MENA region.

The second study that reported the prevalence in the present review was conducted by Mohamed et al. (2025) in Egypt [42]. It focused on Facebook only and reported an estimate of 16.6% [39]. While this value is lower than the global pooled prevalence, it is higher than the general estimate of Africa (9.6%), the region to which Egypt belongs [15]. Africa, in turn, has the lowest prevalence globally, whereas Asia has the highest prevalence (22.8%), followed by America (15.8%) and Europe (12.4%) [15]. This non-comparability may be explained by the fact that the Egyptian study concentrated on Facebook, as mentioned earlier. Therefore, compared with international studies that focused on FA, the rate was lower. For example, a study conducted in Bangladesh reported an estimate that reached 36.9%, and an Ethiopian study reported even more alarming results, with 67.2% of students addicted to Facebook [66, 67]. Studies on FA in the MENA region are rare, and for good comparability, more studies should be conducted on this problem since Facebook is one of the most commonly used SNSs globally in 2025 [68]. Beyond prevalence estimates, all of the remaining included articles reported a moderate level of PSMU based on the scores of the scales they employed [44, 50]. These results are broadly consistent with the global prevalence.

Nevertheless, the interpretation of the prevalence estimates remains constrained by methodological limitations. Mohamed et al. (2025) and Alfaya et al. (2023) did not specify the period of data collection. Reporting this information would have enabled examination of the influence of data collection timing on PSMU prevalence, which is an important contextual factor, particularly during exceptional periods such as the COVID-19 pandemic. Conversely, the studies that reported the period of data collection did not provide prevalence estimates. As a result, although the review included studies conducted before and during the COVID-19 era, a clear temporal comparison between these periods was not possible. This limitation may affect the timeliness and generalizability of the findings. Future studies exploring the relationship between PSMU and AP should explicitly report the year or period of data collection, as this is a crucial methodological detail that reflects study quality and enhances the interpretability of findings by accounting for contextual influences.

Several included studies examined PSMU-related factors, revealing links with sociodemographic characteristics, principally sex and age, and highlighted the effects of mental health problems such as depression, stress, and anxiety [37, 40, 41, 46, 47, 49]. These correlates have also been widely examined, whereas the association with AP has received comparatively less attention. In summary, the moderate prevalence of PSMU among university students underscores the importance of extending research beyond commonly studied related factors, with AP standing out as particularly important yet underexplored.

Association between PSMU and AP

The main aim of this systematic review was to examine the relationship between PSMU and AP among university students in the MENA region. Overall, the narrative synthesis indicated a consistent negative association between PSMU and AP. However, the exploratory meta-analysis yielded a small and non-significant pooled effect, highlighting variability across studies.

Narrative synthesis

The included studies largely indicate a negative relationship between PSMU and AP among MENA-based university students. These findings are consistent with and supported by evidence from international literature. While our systematic review represents the most recent effort to evaluate this relationship, a global meta-analysis limited to studies published up to 2022 similarly reported a negative association, with a heterogeneity of 97.4% and a pooled correlation of –0.172 (95% CI [–0.320, –0.016]), thereby reinforcing the evidence of a negative impact. Similar negative associations were reported in several studies conducted primarily in non-MENA regions, with a concentration in Asian countries such as Turkey, China, India, Malaysia, Bangladesh, and Pakistan, as well as African countries such as Ethiopia [67, 69–77]. Among these studies, some have examined PSMU without focusing on a specific SNS [70, 74], whereas others have concentrated on particular platforms such as Facebook, Instagram, and WeChat [67, 71–73, 75–77].

Furthermore, several studies included in the present review reinforced this negative relationship, as they explored several additional factors. While they examined the effects of some PSMU-related factors, such as the use of multiple SNSs and the time spent on them, they also shed light on academic-related factors, such as the effect of PSMU on study hours. As an example of PSMU-related factors, Alhusban et al. (2022) examined three variables: the use of multiple SNSs, the time spent on them, and their interference with exams. All three factors were significantly associated with lower AP, confirming that the intensity and extent of SMU can influence students’ academic outcomes [44]. As for academic-related factors, Mohamed et al. (2025) found that FA reduces study hours, suggesting that addiction to SNSs may exacerbate AP due to time displacement [42].

However, a minority of studies included in this review reported non-significant associations between PSMU and AP [49, 51]. Notably, Alkhalaf et al. (2018) found no significant relationship among medical students [51]. To better interpret this finding, results from international studies reporting similar non-significant associations were considered. One potential explanation relates to differences in the measurement scales used across studies reporting non-significant results. While the studies included in our review that reported non-significant associations employed relevant and validated instruments, some studies conducted outside the MENA region relied on less commonly used and less extensively validated scales [49, 51, 78, 79]. For instance, the study of Fauzi et al. (2021) reported a non-significant association using the Social Media Addiction Scale–Student Form (SMAS-SF), a tool that is not as frequently employed or as empirically strong as the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (BSMAS), which is widely used and recognized for its strong psychometric properties [55, 79]. This variability in scale choice may therefore contribute to differences in observed associations between PSMU and AP.

A second interpretative consideration relates to sample characteristics. Among the included studies reporting non-significant findings, Alkhalaf et al. (2018) focused on medical students, while Fauzi et al. (2021) examined nursing students [51, 79]. At first glance, this may suggest that academic specialty influences the relationship between PSMU and AP. However, as detailed in the introduction, the paradigms of selectivity, transactionality, and conditionality emphasize that media effects depend on users’ priorities, goals, and contextual demands. Accordingly, students from different academic specialties may selectively engage with SM content that aligns with their academic needs, making the type of content consumed more relevant than disciplinary affiliation itself. Consistent with this interpretation, both the present review, which included a substantial proportion of health-related student population, and comparative studies conducted outside the MENA region reported predominantly negative associations across different academic specialties [67, 71–77]. This suggests that, despite differences in disciplinary focus, shared academic pressures and goal-oriented media use may lead to broadly similar patterns of association between PSMU and AP. However, to further confirm or refute this observed non-relationship, there is a need for comparative longitudinal studies that follow students from different academic backgrounds over time, tracking both their PSMU and academic outcomes.

Finally, platform-specific effects were also considered. Some studies have focused on specific problematic types of SNS use. For example, Alkhalaf et al. (2018), included in the present review, examined WhatsApp addiction (WA) and reported no significant association with AP, a finding that is consistent with results reported by Pekpazar et al. (2021), who focused on Instagram addiction (IA) in a different context [51, 78]. This result suggests that different platforms might have different impacts on the AP, as each platform is characterized by its own features and metrics. However, this idea has not been thoroughly explored since most studies do not directly compare multiple problematic types of SNS use within the same research design. As a result, no consistent evidence has been provided to confirm or deny this association. Hill et al. (2024) partially addressed this issue, and found that university students preferred platforms such as TikTok, Snapchat, and Instagram [80]. These preferences impacted how they perceived the effect of SM on their grades, but in fact, there were no significant GPA differences between users. However, it is important to note that this study did not measure PSMU but only students’ perceptions of its use. Building on this, further studies are needed to understand how addiction to specific SM platforms may affect AP differently from one platform to another.

In contrast to the previously discussed findings that revealed positive or non-significant associations, an interesting study from Nigeria reported a positive correlation between PSMU and AP [81]. This result may be due to the characteristics of the students from Nnamdi Azikiwe University and their behavioral habits, meaning that even if they were addicted, they tend to use SNSs more for academic purposes rather than for entertainment and leisure.

Overall, our findings were largely consistent with international evidence, supporting the negative association between PSMU and AP. However, the interpretation of these findings should take into account the characteristics of the included studies. All of them were cross-sectional, and most were conducted among students in medical or health-related fields, with samples that were predominantly female. While these features provide valuable insight into specific student populations, they may also influence the extent to which the findings reflect the broader university population in the MENA region. In particular, the cross-sectional design does not allow for causal interpretation, and the overrepresentation of specific student groups may further limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, methodological differences across studies should be considered when interpreting the findings, particularly in terms of both outcome operationalization and measurement approaches. In the present review, AP is conceptualized as an overarching construct encompassing both absolute academic achievement (e.g., GPA) and relative academic decline (e.g., APD). This inclusive approach is justified as these measures capture complementary dimensions of academic functioning. While GPA reflects students’ overall level of objective academic achievement, APD captures functional impairments and behavioral disruptions associated with PSMU, such as sleep deprivation, fatigue, and difficulties completing academic tasks [45, 82]. Notably, the study using APD also found that higher levels of PSMU were associated with greater APD, indicating poorer AP [45]. This consistency across different operationalizations strengthens the overall evidence supporting a negative relationship between PSMU and AP, regardless of how AP is defined. By integrating both dimensions, this review provides a more comprehensive understanding of how PSMU affects not only final AP outcomes but also students’ day-to-day academic functioning. Although both are considered under the broader AP umbrella, they remain conceptually distinct, as GPA represents a formal institutional measure, whereas APD reflects a self-reported decline in AP. Although APD has been less frequently used, its inclusion contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of AP by capturing aspects not reflected in GPA-based measures.

However, greater variability was observed in the measurement of PSMU, with four different scales used across the eleven included studies. Each of these instruments is grounded in a distinct theoretical framework. This heterogeneity in measurement tools may have influenced the magnitude and direction of the reported associations, as effect sizes can vary depending on the conceptualization of PSMU adopted by each scale. Therefore, a comparison of the theoretical foundations underlying these scales is necessary to better understand potential sources of variability in the reported findings.

Beginning with the BFAS, this instrument was developed by Cecilie Schou Andreassen in 2012 [52]. It was constructed based on six core components of addiction originally proposed by Brown (1993) and later refined by Griffiths (1996, 2005) [11, 82, 83]. These components include: salience (preoccupation with Facebook use), mood modification (use of Facebook to regulate emotional states), tolerance (increasing amounts of use required to achieve the same effect), withdrawal (negative emotions when access is restricted), conflict (interpersonal, academic, or occupational problems resulting from use), and relapse (repeated unsuccessful attempts to reduce use) [11, 83]. The scale was specifically designed to capture addictive patterns of Facebook use rather than general or frequent use, thereby conceptualizing FA as a behavioral addiction within a biopsychosocial framework. BSMAS, in turn, was developed by the same author in 2016 as a conceptual extension of the BFAS [56]. The primary modification introduced in the BSMAS consists of replacing the platform-specific reference to Facebook with the more general term SM, thereby broadening the scope of assessment to encompass addictive behaviors across multiple SNS platforms [56, 84]. Apart from this terminological adaptation, the underlying addiction component remains unchanged, allowing the BSMAS to retain the same conceptualization of problematic use as a behavioral addiction while enhancing its applicability in more diverse and evolving digital contexts.

Turning our attention to the SMAS, a scale that was theoretically grounded in the Internet Addiction Test (IAT) framework developed by Young (1998), which conceptualizes problematic Internet use (PIU) using pathological gambling, as defined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV), as its reference model [58].Within this framework, PIU is defined as a non-substance impulse-control disorder and is operationalized through diagnostic criteria reflecting behavioral indicators such as preoccupation with online activity, increasing time spent online, unsuccessful attempts to control use, negative emotional states when reducing use, and continued engagement despite academic, social, or occupational impairment [58]. The SMAS builds on the IAT framework, substituting the term “Internet” with “social media” [59]. In the Arabic validation of the SMAS, Al-Menayes et al. (2015) identified a three-factor structure consisting of social consequences of SMU, time displacement, and compulsive tendencies [59]. These factors capture central behavioral and psychosocial dimensions of problematic use. However, when evaluated against Griffith’s six-component model, the SMAS demonstrates incomplete coverage. While the SMAS effectively measures salience through compulsive tendencies and conflict through social consequences, and even includes signs of withdrawal such as irritability when interrupted, other key components are either weakly represented or missing. Tolerance is only indirectly suggested by one item on increased use, and relapse, or the inability to reduce use, is not assessed. The scale also lacks items that capture mood modification, or the use of SM to manage emotions. Therefore, while the SMAS highlights important aspects of behavioral overuse and functional difficulties, its focus is narrower than the full behavioral addiction model, emphasizing external impacts and compulsive use rather than the emotional and motivational processes involved in addiction.

The fourth scale used in the included studies is the CIAS, which was developed by Chen et al. (2003) within a traditional addiction framework, drawing on DSM-IV diagnostic criteria for addictive disorders and clinical observations of psychological distress among heavy Internet users [60]. The scale adopts a symptom-based conceptualization of internet addiction, distinguishing between core symptoms and addiction-related functional problems [60]. Specifically, the CIAS conceptualizes PIU through core symptoms of tolerance, compulsive use, and withdrawal, as well as related problems of Internet use, including interpersonal and health-related problems and time-management difficulties [60]. Building on the CIAS, Ko et al. (2005) expanded Chen’s work by developing diagnostic criteria for Internet use in adolescents, drawing on diagnostic criteria from previous studies, including Young’s criteria and Griffiths’ behavioral addiction model, alongside clinician interviews and statistical analyses to define a symptom-based cutoff and improve the clinical usefulness of the CIAS [61].

Although the four scales demonstrate acceptable psychometric properties, each is grounded in a specific theoretical framework, which makes direct comparison of their results difficult. This issue was also highlighted in a scoping review that examined the conceptual and operational descriptions of negative SNS use in adolescents across 37 measurement instruments [85]. Although focused on adolescents, the review concluded that the dimensions assessed by these instruments were vaguely defined and often overlapped with one another, and that no standardized theoretical framework currently exists to assess negative SNS use in adolescents [85].

Additionally, even within the same scale, differences in score interpretation further contribute to measurement heterogeneity. For instance, the BFAS, which is the most widely used scale for assessing PIU, the original developer did not provide a cutoff point, indicating only that higher scores reflect greater levels of FA [52]. However, several subsequent studies have suggested cutoff values. For instance, Luo et al. (2021) proposed a cutoff score of 24, which was identified as the optimal threshold based on a clinical diagnostic standard [86]. Among the two studies included in the present systematic review that used this scale, Mohamed et al. (2025) applied this cutoff and explicitly reported a prevalence estimate of 16.6%, whereas Abu-Snieneh et al. (2019) classified students as “addicted” if they scored 3 (“sometimes”) or higher on at least four of the six items, or scored 3 or higher on all six items [42, 46].

For BSMAS, the SM version of the BFAS, was used by four studies included in this review, with substantial variability in the classification criteria. Alfaya et al. (2023) applied a strict polythetic classification scheme, whereby individuals scoring ≥ 4 on at least two-thirds of the items (corresponding to a cutoff score of 16) were considered indicative of SMA [39]. In contrast, Alhusban et al. (2022) used a single cutoff point of 18 to identify SMA [44]. Using a multi-level severity classification, Alhrahsheh and Al Majali (2023) categorized participants into multiple severity levels, defining scores of 18–19 as normal use, 20–47 as mild addiction, 48–69 as moderate addiction, and 70–90 as severe addiction [43]. In another study, Azizi et al. (2019) classified participants into normal use (0–19), mild addiction (20–35), moderate addiction (40–69), and severe addiction (70–90) based on total scores [48].

Regarding the SMAS, which was employed in three of the included studies, the scale was applied using different analytical approaches. Alkhalaf et al. (2018) adapted the scale to assess WhatsApp use and conducted factor analysis, which resulted in a three-factor structure including items related to deterioration of school performance, use of WhatsApp while driving, reduced social interactions, and preoccupation with WhatsApp [51]. In contrast, Hamam et al. (2022) applied the SMAS within a broader SM context [47]. His analysis focused on the scale’s original three dimensions, namely the consequences of SMU, time displacement, and compulsive tendencies [47]. In both studies, SMAS scores were analyzed primarily through their factor structures and examined in relation to AP indicators [47, 51]. On the other hand, Malak et al. (2021) reported the use of cut-off points to classify levels of SMA; however, the authors did not provide detailed justification or explicit numerical criteria for these thresholds [49].

In the case of CIAS-SNS, the two included studies employed this scale with different analytical purposes. In the study by Al-Yafi et al. (2018), they explicitly applied the clinical cutoff proposed by Ko et al. (2005) (CIAS score ≥ 64) to classify students into addicted and non-addicted groups [50, 61]. This cutoff was then used to examine differences in AP across usage profiles and to further derive additional categories (passive, engaged, and addicted users) based on score distributions [50]. In contrast, Homaid (2022) employed the CIAS-SNS as a continuous multidimensional measure of PSMU, focusing on its five symptom dimensions within a SSO framework [45].

The interpretation of findings across studies is complicated by substantial methodological heterogeneity related to both the choice and application of PSMU measurement instruments. First, the scales employed in the included studies are grounded in different theoretical frameworks, meaning that PSMU is not conceptualized uniformly. Consequently, these instruments may capture different dimensions of problematic use, which can influence the observed associations with AP. Second, heterogeneity is also evident within studies using the same scale, as authors applied different interpretative strategies, classification rules, and cutoff points, operationalizing PSMU either as a continuous construct or as a categorical status. Such variability limits direct comparability across studies, even when identical instruments are used.

In light of the substantial heterogeneity observed in both the theoretical foundations and operational use of PSMU instruments, future research would benefit from greater standardization in measurement approaches. The absence of a unified theoretical framework to assess negative SNS use makes it difficult to compare results across studies and to accurately determine the true extent of the problem. This issue is particularly salient in the MENA region, where variations in scale selection, cutoff application, and analytical strategies further limit cross-study comparability. Accordingly, future studies should prioritize the adoption of standardized, theoretically grounded PSMU assessment frameworks and the use of validated instruments appropriate to the regional context. Establishing such unified assessment standards is critical for improving comparability across studies and for accumulating robust, high-quality evidence on the relationship between PSMU and AP.

Exploratory meta-analysis

As an exploratory, hypothesis-generating approach, the meta-analysis yielded a small and non-significant pooled effect (r = −0.114, 95% CI: −0.283 to 0.054), indicating a weak negative association between PSMU and AP. In this context, the objective of the meta-analysis was not to derive a single common effect, but rather to explain why effect sizes vary across studies. As described in the literature, exploratory meta-analysis focuses on investigating the causes of variation in effect sizes and identifying study-level characteristics that may account for these differences [87].

The observed divergence between the narrative synthesis and the meta-analytic estimate reflects this underlying variability and may be explained by several factors. First, the small number of included studies (n = 4) in the meta-analysis reduces statistical power and increases uncertainty around the pooled estimate. Second, substantial methodological heterogeneity across studies, particularly in the measurement and conceptualization of PSMU, may have attenuated the overall effect size, as combining conceptually diverse constructs can dilute underlying associations. Variability in the measurement of AP was also substantial, as studies relied on different indicators, including GPA, semester grades, and performance-based scales, reflecting heterogeneity in its operationalization across studies. Third, the meta-analysis was based on correlation coefficients, which reflect unadjusted associations. As such, potential confounding variables were not consistently accounted for across studies, which may have contributed to variability in effect sizes and reduced the consistency of the pooled estimate. In addition, variations in sample characteristics (e.g., field of study) may have introduced additional between-study variability, limiting comparability.

From a hypothesis-generating perspective, these findings suggest that the negative association between PSMU and AP may be context-dependent and sensitive to how PSMU is conceptualized and measured, as well as to differences in the operationalization of AP. They also indicate that more consistent associations may emerge in studies using standardized, theory-driven instruments and appropriately controlled analytical models. Importantly, the exploratory meta-analysis reinforces the variability and methodological limitations identified in the narrative synthesis, including inconsistencies in PSMU measurement and variability in the operationalization of AP, reliance on unadjusted analyses, and limited comparability across studies. By identifying these limitations, the present synthesis contributes to refining future research designs and reducing the recurrence of similar methodological shortcomings. Accordingly, this exploratory meta-analysis should be interpreted as an initial quantitative exploration aimed at informing future research and improving methodological rigor, rather than drawing definitive conclusions.

Mediating factors in the relationship between PSMU and AP

Beyond methodological limitations, an additional gap in the current literature concerns the limited investigation of mediating mechanisms underlying the relationship between PSMU and AP. Evidence on mediating mechanisms linking PSMU to AP remains limited in MENA region. Among the included studies, only two investigated mediating factors, with a primary focus on psychological and psychosocial dimensions [45, 49].

Malak et al. (2022) explored the mediating role of psychological factors, particularly stress, anxiety, and depression, within a mediation framework conceptually grounded in emotion theory, which emphasizes the interdependence between emotional and psychological reactions and their cognitive regulation [49, 88]. The results did not identify any statistically supported direct or indirect association between SMA and AP [49]. Nevertheless, the study reported that SMA was associated with increased stress levels, which in turn lead to anxiety and eventually depression [49]. In addition, AP was negatively influenced by anxiety through stress, independent of direct or mediated effects from the SMA [49]. Nevertheless, the coexistence of the significant indirect relationships SMA → Stress → Anxiety → Depression and Stress → Anxiety → AP observed in the model suggests a possible chain linking PSMU and AP through psychological distress, a pathway that still needs to be explored in further MENA studies [49]. Using a different conceptual approach, Homaid (2022) examined the mechanisms through which PSMU affects AP by focusing on psychosocial mediating factors, using the Stressor–Strain–Outcome (SSO) model [45, 89]. Within this framework, PSMU was conceptualized as a stressor, technostress and exhaustion as strain variables, and APD as the outcome [45]. The findings revealed that PSMU was significantly associated with higher levels of technostress and exhaustion, both of which were, in turn, significantly linked to poorer AP [45]. Moreover, technostress and exhaustion were identified as significant mediators in the relationship between PSMU and APD, indicating that the negative academic impact of PSMU operates largely through these psychological strain mechanisms [45].

Taken together, the present systematic review confirms the mediating role of technostress and exhaustion in the relationship between PSMU and AP in the MENA region, particularly among Saudi Arabian students [45]. Nevertheless, evidence on mediating mechanisms linking PSMU and AP remains limited within the MENA region, highlighting the need for further research. In this context, comparison with international studies examining mediating factors may provide deeper explanatory insight and help inform the development of a more coherent conceptual model to guide future research in the MENA region and beyond.

Following an extensive literature search, nine studies were identified that investigated factors mediating the relationship between PSMU and AP. In addition to psychological and psychosocial factors, these studies examined physiological, behavioral, and academic-related factors, several of which demonstrated significant mediating effects. Regarding psychological factors, in contrast to the findings of Malak et al. (2022) included in the present systematic review, evidence from international studies has confirmed the mediating roles of depression and stress [49]. Landa-Blanco et al. (2024) reported that depression, among other factors, mediates the negative relationship between PSMU and AP [90]. Specifically, SMA increases symptoms of depression, which in turn decreases students’ academic engagement scores, whereas anxiety did not exhibit a significant mediating effect [90]. Based on the same study, self-esteem also emerged as an intermediary mechanism mediating the negative relationship between PSMU and AP [90]. Stress was also identified as a mediator, as Zhao (2023) found that higher levels of SMA were associated with increased stress, which in turn negatively affected the AP of college students [91].

Alongside depression, self-esteem, and stress, self-control was also identified as a mediator by Ning and Inan (2024) [92]. Their findings showed that self-control functions as an indirect pathway, whereby SNS addiction reduces students’ self-control, ultimately leading to poorer academic outcomes [92]. Additionally, Singh et al. (2023), emphasized the role of social media fatigue, confirming its indirect effect on the relationship between PSMU and APD, whereby higher levels of compulsive SMU increased social media fatigue, which in turn exacerbated APD [93].

Beyond psychological factors, some physiological factors were shown to play a mediating role in the relationship between PSMU and AP. Findings from studies by Zhuang et al. (2023) and Khalaf et al. (2025) indicated that SMA is associated with poorer sleep quality, which in turn negatively affects students’ AP or engagement [94, 95]. Both studies also examined the role of fatigue independently. While Khalaf et al. (2025) did not identify a significant independent mediating effect of fatigue, Zhuang et al. (2023) reported that fatigue not only independently mediated the link between PSMU and academic engagement but also, together with sleep quality, formed a serial mediation pathway (PSMU → poorer sleep quality → increased fatigue → reduced academic engagement) [94, 95]. Taken together, these findings suggest that sleep quality and fatigue constitute important biological mechanisms through which PSMU may indirectly affect academic engagement and performance.

In addition to psychological and physiological factors, procrastination appears as a behavioral factor in this relationship, which prevents students from showing their true abilities. Pekpazar et al. (2021) showed that procrastination fully mediates the relationship between PSMU and AP [78]. In other words, students who excessively engage in SM may postpone academic tasks and spend more time online at the expense of study activities, which negatively affects their AP.

Academic-related factors were also implicated as important indirect mechanisms, particularly academic self-discipline and learning engagement, both of which were associated with PSMU and adverse academic outcomes. Tekin (2024) reported that academic self-discipline serves as a significant partial mediator in the relationship between SMA and academic achievement; specifically, higher levels of SMA were associated with lower academic self-discipline, and higher academic self-discipline was in turn associated with better academic achievement [96]. Additionally, Li et al. (2019) demonstrated that learning engagement partially mediates the association between SNS addiction and academic achievement, showing that excessive SNS use undermines vigor, dedication, and absorption in study tasks, which in turn leads to poorer academic outcomes [97].

Beyond the mediating factors identified in the existing literature examining the relationship between PSMU and AP, another emerging concept that warrants attention is ‘brain rot’, which has been discussed in relation to procrastination and short-form content consumption [98].

Brain rot refers to “the supposed deterioration of a person’s mental or intellectual state, especially viewed as the result of overconsumption of material (now particularly online content) considered to be trivial or unchallenging. Also: something characterized as likely to lead to such deterioration” [99]. In recent years, growing concern has emerged that continuous exposure to superficial or rapidly changing content, including short-form videos such as “reels” and “shorts”, may be harmful to our ability to maintain long-lasting attention and think deeply. This has been supported by quantitative and qualitative evidence. Research has indicated that prolonged exposure to fast and fragmented digital, brain rotting content can weaken key cognitive functions such as attention, memory, and problem-solving abilities [100]. Interestingly, it has also been confirmed that brain rot is associated with some specific negative digital behaviors, including doomscrolling, zombie scrolling, and SMA, all of which were linked to psychological distress, anxiety, and depression [100]. These results suggest that brain rot may indirectly affect AP through the impairment of cognitive functions and the reinforcement of maladaptive behaviors.

In summary, the present systematic review indicates a negative relationship between PSMU and AP among university students in the MENA region. However, the findings of the meta-analysis, while exploratory in nature, underline the need for more methodologically consistent studies, particularly using standardized measurement approaches and appropriate control of confounding variables, to support more robust and reliable future meta-analytic synthesis. In parallel, the limited number of studies identified in the systematic review highlights the need for further research in this context. Future research should prioritize longitudinal and interventional study designs to better clarify the directionality of this relationship and to identify the underlying mechanisms involved. Moreover, the mediating mechanisms examined in MENA-based studies remain relatively limited, highlighting the need to investigate additional mediating factors. In particular, greater attention should be given to psychological distress and self-regulation mechanisms, including depression, anxiety, stress, self-esteem, self-control, and social media fatigue. Physiological mediators, such as sleep quality and fatigue, also merit deeper examination. Behavioral pathways, especially procrastination, alongside academic-related mediators such as academic self-discipline and learning engagement, remain underexplored. The integrative conceptual model proposed in this review offers a structured framework to guide future research on these mediating pathways between PSMU and AP in the MENA region and may also be applicable to international research contexts.

Limitations and recommendations

While our systematic review and exploratory meta-analysis provide valuable findings and address an important question regarding the relationship between PSMU and AP among university students, particularly in the MENA region, it also presents some limitations. First, during the selection of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, we chose to focus solely on cross-sectional studies as an initial step to explore this relationship and describe the current situation. However, cross-sectional designs provide only a snapshot in time and rely mostly on self-reported questionnaires. Both aspects represent limitations, because they do not allow temporal or causal interpretations and they may introduce biases such as memory errors and social desirability. In addition, although one study explicitly conducted during the COVID-19 period was included in the review, several included studies did not explicitly report their data collection periods, which restricted our ability to compare studies across different timeframes and examine potential time-related effects. Second, although we aimed to include studies from all MENA countries, the final set of included studies reflects differences in data availability across the region. Some countries, especially in North Africa, were underrepresented. Third, most included studies were conducted among students in medical or health-related fields and involved predominantly female samples, which may limit the representativeness of the findings across the broader university student population in the MENA region. Fourth, substantial heterogeneity existed in the measurement of PSMU across included studies, as differences in theoretical foundations, constructs assessed, and cutoff criteria across instruments limited comparability across studies. In addition, heterogeneity was also observed in the operationalization of AP, as studies relied on different indicators. Fifth, the exploration of mediating mechanisms linking PSMU to AP remains limited in the MENA region. Only a small number of included studies examined mediating factors, which constrained region-specific conclusions regarding these mechanisms. Finally, the available studies did not provide sufficiently comparable and consistently reported data to support a fully analytic meta-analysis. Differences in statistical indicators and measurement approaches limited the ability to synthesize results using a unified effect size. Consequently, the meta-analysis was restricted to an exploratory, hypothesis-generating approach, focused on examining variability in effect sizes rather than estimating a definitive pooled association.

To address these limitations, we propose several recommendations. First, we encourage researchers in the MENA region and internationally to further investigate PSMU and AP, particularly in light of the rapid development of digital technologies and artificial intelligence, which may intensify patterns of use and associated consequences. Future studies should clearly report data collection periods to allow temporal comparisons and better assessment of time-related effects. Researchers are also encouraged to diversify study designs by conducting longitudinal and interventional studies, rather than relying solely on cross-sectional designs, in order to strengthen causal inference and provide new perspectives. In addition, greater efforts are needed to include more diverse and representative university populations across different academic fields. Conducting large-scale, nationally representative cross-sectional studies without restriction to specific specialties would help improve the generalizability of findings in the MENA region. Furthermore, the development and adoption of standardized PSMU assessment tools grounded in unified theoretical models would enhance comparability across studies. In parallel, greater consistency in the measurement of AP is needed, through the use of clearly defined and comparable measures. In the MENA context, particular attention should also be given to examining mediating mechanisms based on the integrative conceptual model proposed in this review, such as psychological mediators (e.g., stress, depression, self-esteem, self-control, and social media fatigue), physiological mediators (e.g., sleep quality and fatigue), behavioral mediators (e.g., procrastination), and academic-related mediators (e.g., academic self-discipline and learning engagement), to better clarify the pathways through which PSMU may be associated with AP. Finally, as more studies become available, future research should consider conducting analytic meta-analyses based on larger and more methodologically homogeneous datasets, which would allow for more robust and reliable quantitative estimates of the relationship between PSMU and AP.

As a way to mitigate the negative relationship identified in our review, awareness and prevention efforts are essential. Universities, public schools, and health institutions should organize prevention programs to inform parents, adults in general, and university students in particular about the effects of PSMU. Teachers, parents, and researchers should collaborate to develop training and educational programs that promote essential life skills, along with real-life activities such as clubs and community projects that help students remain engaged and less dependent on SM. The media can also contribute by broadcasting awareness campaigns and educational content that encourage balanced and responsible SMU. In summary, addressing PSMU requires coordinated action from all stakeholders. Policymakers, educators, parents, and healthcare professionals, in particular, can contribute to developing strategies that promote responsible SMU and protect students’ AP and overall well-being.
