Physiological phenotypes of obstructive sleep apnea identified by cluster analysis: Association with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Saeko Osawa, Mitsutaka Taniguchi, Naohiko Ito, Hiromitsu Tabata, Motoharu Ohi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims/IntroductionObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a multifaceted condition that may variably influence metabolic risk. This study aimed to identify physiological phenotypes of OSA using polysomnographic (PSG) data and examine their association with type 2 diabetes (T2DM) in a Japanese cohort.Materials and MethodsAmong 3,683 individuals who underwent attended PSG between April 2019 and March 2024, 602 adults with OSA were included. Phenotypes were identified by k‐means clustering. The association between phenotype and T2DM was assessed using logistic regression adjusted for covariates.ResultsFive phenotypes were identified: Severe desaturation, REM‐predominant, PLMS‐dominant, Sleep‐fragmented, and Mild. Severe desaturation and REM‐predominant types showed higher T2DM prevalence (54.5% and 45.7%). In multivariable analysis, both were independently associated with T2DM compared with the Mild type (adjusted odds ratio [OR] 5.00, 95% CI 1.52–16.4; and OR 3.12, 95% CI 1.32–7.38). To further explore the underlying mechanisms, logistic regression including all participants showed that CT90 was independently associated with T2DM, even after adjustment for arousal threshold and AHI.ConclusionsData‐driven clustering identified distinct physiological phenotypes of OSA. In addition to the Severe desaturation type, the REM‐predominant type was also strongly associated with T2DM, underscoring the metabolic heterogeneity of OSA. Prospective studies are needed to determine causal relationships and evaluate phenotype‐targeted interventions.Keywords: Cluster analysis, Obstructive sleep apnea, Type 2 diabetes mellitus

INTRODUCTION

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common sleep‐related breathing disorder characterized by recurrent upper‐airway obstruction during sleep, leading to intermittent hypoxia and sleep fragmentation. OSA affects nearly one billion adults worldwide. In Asian populations, craniofacial anatomical characteristics—such as smaller upper airways and retrognathia—may confer greater susceptibility to OSA even at lower levels of obesity 1 , 2 .

Beyond its impact on subjective symptoms, particularly excessive daytime sleepiness, OSA has also emerged as an important metabolic disorder that is strongly associated with insulin resistance and diabetes. Beyond its impact on subjective symptoms, particularly excessive daytime sleepiness, OSA has also emerged as an important metabolic disorder that is strongly associated with insulin resistance and diabetes. The bidirectional relationship between OSA and diabetes has been increasingly recognized 3 . Recurrent episodes of hypoxemia and sleep fragmentation are known to induce sympathetic activation, oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, and hormonal dysregulation, which in turn lead to β‐cell dysfunction and insulin resistance 4 , 5 . By contrast, autonomic dysfunction associated with diabetic neuropathy may compromise ventilatory regulation and reduce activity in upper‐airway dilator muscles, thereby worsening OSA 6 .

Although the close relationship between OSA and diabetes has been established, continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy—the standard treatment for OSA—has shown limited effects on metabolic outcomes. One possible explanation for this variability is the heterogeneous nature of OSA pathophysiology 7 . Traditional methods of classifying OSA severity based exclusively on the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) do not fully capture the complexity of the condition, including nocturnal hypoxemic burden, frequency of arousals, and respiratory events specific to rapid eye movement (REM) sleep 8 .

Recently, advanced data‐driven approaches such as cluster analysis have been applied to identify distinct clinical phenotypes and pathophysiological endotypes of OSA 9 . Yet, little is known about how these physiological OSA phenotypes relate specifically to diabetes, particularly in Asian populations where OSA may manifest with less obesity but heightened metabolic vulnerability.

Therefore, this study aimed to identify data‐driven physiological phenotypes of OSA using an unsupervised machine‐learning approach and to examine their association with T2DM in a Japanese cohort. By applying cluster analysis to multiple PSG‐derived parameters, we aimed to better characterize the physiological heterogeneity of OSA and explore its relationship with T2DM.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

A total of 3,683 adults who underwent in‐lab attended polysomnography (PSG) at our Sleep Center between April 2019 and March 2024 were initially enrolled. These individuals visited the Sleep Center for evaluation of sleep‐related symptoms, apnea observed by a bed partner, or abnormal nocturnal oxygen desaturation detected during routine health examinations.

Among the 3,683 individuals, those aged ≥18 years with a confirmed diagnosis of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), defined as an apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) ≥5 events per hour, were considered eligible.

Patients were excluded if they exhibited predominant central sleep apnea (central AHI comprising at least 50% of total events), had <30 min of REM sleep, a documented history of upper‐airway surgery, a prior history of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) treatment, underwent CPAP titration during PSG, or lacked essential data for key variables. Additionally, individuals under 18 years of age were excluded. After applying these criteria, 602 participants were included in the final analysis.

The protocol for this research project has been approved by a suitably constituted Ethics Committee of the institution, and it conforms to the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki. Committee of Osaka Kaisei Hospital, Approval No. 22–12; 2022. All informed consent was obtained from the subjects.

Polysomnography and extracted variables

All participants underwent in‐lab attended PSG using a standardized system (Alice 6; Philips Respironics, Murrysville, PA, USA or Embla M Drive N7000; Chest M.I., Inc. Tokyo, Japan). Sleep stages and respiratory events were manually scored according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) Scoring Manual, Version 2.5 or later, by registered sleep technologists 10 . Apnea and hypopnea were scored according to AASM criteria.

The following PSG‐derived variables were extracted: total sleep time, sleep efficiency, proportions of rapid eye movement (REM) and non‐REM sleep, AHI, REM‐AHI, NREM‐AHI, minimum oxygen saturation (SpO2 nadir), mean SpO2, percentage of total sleep time with SpO2 <90% (CT90), arousal index, periodic limb movement index (PLMI), and body position parameters. REM OSA was defined as REM‐AHI/NREM‐AHI >2 with NREM‐AHI <15, as previously described 11 . The estimated arousal threshold (ArTH) was calculated using a previously established regression model 12 , 13 .

Statistical analyses

Unsupervised cluster analysis was performed to identify physiological phenotypes of OSA.

To identify representative PSG variables for clustering, we initially performed principal component analysis (PCA) on 17 polysomnographic parameters, including respiratory indices (AHI, NREM‐AHI, REM‐AHI, apnea index, hypopnea index), hypoxemia metrics (CT90, minimum SpO2, mean SpO2, CT80), sleep architecture (sleep efficiency, N1%, N2%, N3%, REM%), arousal index, PLMI, and body position (supine time). All variables were standardized to z‐scores prior to PCA (correlation matrix method). Components with eigenvalues ≥1 were retained, which accounted for approximately 74% of the total variance (6 principal components). Based on the PCA loading patterns and clinical interpretability, six variables were selected to represent distinct physiological domains: hypoxemia burden (CT90, minimum SpO2), sleep quality (sleep efficiency, N3%), REM‐specific pathology (REM‐AHI), and periodic limb movements (PLMI). These variables were then entered into the k‐means clustering algorithm. The optimal number of clusters was evaluated using multiple complementary criteria: the gap statistic (500 bootstrap iterations), silhouette coefficient, and within‐cluster sum of squares (elbow method). The silhouette coefficient showed a local peak at K = 5 (0.25), and the gap statistic demonstrated a plateau from K = 5 to K = 7, indicating that multiple solutions were statistically supported. Sensitivity analyses with K = 4 and K = 6 confirmed the robustness of our primary findings. K = 5 was selected as it allowed meaningful distinction between phenotypes with different pathophysiological mechanisms while maintaining clinical interpretability. Cluster stability was assessed using two approaches. First, k‐means clustering with 10 different random seeds (nstart = 25) was performed, and reproducibility was quantified using the adjusted Rand index (ARI = 1 indicates perfect agreement). Second, bootstrap stability was evaluated using 1,000 resamples, calculating Jaccard similarity coefficients for each cluster (≥0.75: highly stable; ≥0.60: moderately stable). K‐means was implemented with k‐means++ initialization, nstart = 25, and maximum 100 iterations, with a convergence criterion of relative change in within‐cluster sum of squares <1 × 10−6. For reproducibility assessment, 10 different random seeds (100, 200, …, 1,000) were used, each initialized with nstart = 25. Differences in clinical and biochemical characteristics among clusters were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the chi‐square test for categorical variables, with post hoc analyses performed when appropriate.

The association between cluster membership and T2DM was initially evaluated using logistic regression analysis adjusted for age, sex, and BMI.

Subsequently, a multivariable logistic regression model was constructed in the overall study population to identify variables independently associated with T2DM. Model fit was evaluated using the likelihood ratio test and the Akaike information criterion (AIC). Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF <5 indicating acceptable collinearity). Sensitivity analyses using log transformation and restricted cubic splines assessed CT90 non‐linearity (Supplementary Materials S1).

All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Japan), a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) 14 .

Clinical and biochemical data

Demographic data, anthropometric measurements, and comorbidities were obtained from electronic medical records. Hypertension, dyslipidemia, and type 2 diabetes were defined according to current Japanese clinical guidelines or medication use. Laboratory data were extracted from electronic medical records using results obtained within 3 months of PSG. Demographic information, including age, sex, and BMI, as well as the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) score, was also collected.

K‐means clustering showed perfect reproducibility (adjusted Rand index = 1.00). Bootstrap stability revealed Jaccard coefficients of 0.55–0.77, with the highest stability in the Severe desaturation cluster (0.77).

K = 5 was selected based on statistical validation and clinical interpretability (Methods). Sensitivity analyses (K = 4, K = 6) confirmed robust associations between hypoxia‐predominant phenotypes and T2DM.

Severe desaturation type (n = 22): This cluster showed marked hypoxemia with the highest AHI (median 71.8 events/h), prolonged oxygen desaturation (median CT90 43.5%), and severely disturbed sleep architecture.

The estimated arousal threshold (ArTH) was high, suggesting that cortical arousal was less likely to occur during respiratory events, which may have resulted in more profound and sustained oxygen desaturation. Patients were generally obese (median BMI 29.1 kg/m2), and the prevalence of T2DM was the highest among all phenotypes (54.5%).

REM‐predominant type (n = 151):This cluster was characterized by a disproportionately elevated AHI during REM sleep compared with NREM sleep (median REM‐AHI 49.6 vs. NREM‐AHI 42.0 events/h). The degree of hypoxemia was the second most severe among the five clusters. ArTH was lower than in the severe desaturation type, suggesting different underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. Patients had a relatively high BMI (median 27.7 kg/m2), and the prevalence of T2DM was also high (45.7%).

PLMS‐dominant type (n = 61): This phenotype showed modest OSA severity (median AHI 17.5 events/h) but markedly elevated PLMS index (median 64.7/h). This cluster included the oldest patients among the five phenotypes (median 70.0 years, range 18–89).

Sleep‐fragmented type (n = 259): This was the largest cluster, characterized by predominantly moderate OSA (median AHI 16.0 events/h). According to the conventional AHI‐based classification, more than 80% of these patients would be categorized as having mild or moderate OSA. ArTH was low, comparable to that in the REM‐predominant type, suggesting greater sleep fragmentation.

Although indices of respiratory events were similar to those in the mild type, this cluster exhibited poorer sleep efficiency, reduced N3 sleep, and an increased proportion of N1 sleep, further indicating disrupted sleep architecture.

Mild type (n = 109): This cluster had an AHI profile similar to the sleep‐fragmented type (median AHI 14.9 vs. 16.0 events/h), but sleep architecture was better preserved, with a higher proportion of N3 sleep (median 13.1%). The prevalence of T2DM was the lowest (7.5%).

Figure 1 shows that T2DM prevalence differed significantly across clusters (P < 0.001): Severe desaturation type (54.5%) and REM‐predominant type (45.7%) were most common, while mild type was least frequent (7.5%). Pairwise comparisons revealed no significant difference between the Severe desaturation and REM‐predominant groups (Bonferroni‐adjusted P = 1.00), but both had a higher proportion than the mild, sleep‐fragmented, and PLMS‐dominant clusters.

In multivariable logistic regression (Figure 2) adjusted for age, BMI, and sex, severe desaturation type (adjusted OR 5.00, 95% CI 1.52–16.4) and REM‐predominant type (adjusted OR 3.12, 95% CI 1.32–7.38) were independently associated with T2DM versus mild type. PLMS‐dominant type and sleep‐fragmented type were not associated with T2DM. By contrast, when severity was assessed using the AHI metric, severe OSA demonstrated an association with T2DM (adjusted odds ratio: 1.83; 95% confidence interval: 1.04–3.21).

Intermittent hypoxemia and sleep fragmentation have been considered the principal physiological pathways linking OSA to glucose dysregulation 3 , 5 . In our cluster analysis, the severe desaturation type was characterized by pronounced hypoxemia, whereas the REM‐predominant type also exhibited a relatively high hypoxic burden together with a lower estimated arousal threshold (ArTH), suggesting distinct underlying mechanisms of sleep fragmentation and ventilatory control. To further clarify which of these physiological disturbances was more strongly associated with T2DM, logistic regression analyses were performed in the overall study population, using CT90 as an indicator of hypoxic burden and ArTH as an indicator of sleep fragmentation (Table 3). In Model 1, which included age, sex, BMI, CT90, and ArTH as explanatory variables, CT90 remained independently associated with T2DM, whereas ArTH did not. In Model 2, following the inclusion of AHI—the conventional index used to assess OSA severity—CT90 remained independently associated with T2DM, while neither AHI nor ArTH showed a significant independent association (see Table 3). Log‐transformed CT90 associations and non‐linearity analyses are in Supplementary Materials S1.

DISCUSSION

Our study identified five distinct phenotypes of OSA using an unsupervised machine‐learning approach based on multiple PSG‐derived physiological parameters, which provides a broader understanding of OSA pathophysiology beyond conventional AHI. In particular, both the severe desaturation and REM‐predominant types showed a significant association with T2DM, independent of age, sex, and BMI.

Accumulated evidence indicates close relationships between OSA and T2DM. In our study, the prevalence of T2DM among patients with OSA (26%) was markedly higher than that in the general Japanese adult population (8.1%) 15 . This finding reinforces the substantial association between OSA and metabolic comorbidities. Furthermore, previous studies have indicated that patients with both OSA and T2DM may have an additive risk of cardiovascular disease compared to those with OSA alone 6 , 14 . Understanding the pathophysiology of coexisting OSA and DM is clinically essential.

As previously mentioned, although there is a notable connection between OSA and DM, the improvements in glucose metabolism resulting from CPAP therapy have generally been modest. For example, a recent meta‐analysis indicated that CPAP therapy led to a reduction in HbA1c by −0.24% 16 . While this finding reached statistical significance, the effect is considered to have limited clinical relevance when compared to the approximately 1% decrease generally observed with glucose‐lowering medications. Suboptimal CPAP adherence and insufficient usage time have been suggested as possible reasons for the limited metabolic benefits of therapy 17 . However, this limited metabolic response may also be influenced by the heterogeneous nature of OSA, which involves diverse pathophysiological mechanisms and clinical features 7 , 8 , 18 . For instance, the conventional approach to severity classification using only AHI does not fully capture the complex nature of the condition, including nocturnal hypoxia duration and intensity, respiratory events during REM sleep, arousal frequency, and periodic limb movements (PLMS) 6 , 7 . It has been suggested that the absence of consistent cardiovascular benefit from CPAP therapy in large clinical trials such as SAVE and RICCADSA may partly reflect this pathophysiological heterogeneity 9 . Therefore, multidimensional phenotyping is important to identify subgroups with distinct risks and treatment responsiveness.

Several studies have used PSG‐based clustering to delineate OSA subtypes. Zinchuk et al. identified seven polysomnographic phenotypes in a U.S. veteran cohort, showing differential cardiovascular risk 7 . Similarly, a Korean cohort study identified an elevated risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular mortality among individuals in hypoxia clusters 19 . Regarding metabolic outcomes, Ding et al. conducted an analysis based on Zinchuk's clusters and discovered that both the Hypopnea & Hypoxia cluster and the PLMS cluster were independently linked to the development of T2DM 20 . In a Japanese population, a hierarchical cluster analysis identified five phenotypes, but the prevalence of diabetes did not significantly differ among clusters. The analysis incorporated both clinical characteristics (e.g., age, BMI, self‐reported comorbidities including diabetes) and a small number of PSG parameters (AHI and ODI) 21 . Our study is the first to examine how PSG‐defined phenotypes relate to T2DM within a Japanese population, offering new perspectives on the relationship between specific OSA profiles and T2DM.

In our study, the prevalence of T2DM was significantly higher not only in severe desaturation type, which is similar to conventional severe OSA, but also in REM‐predominant type. Regarding REM‐related respiratory events, a previous study on T2DM has demonstrated that REM‐AHI, but not NREM‐AHI, was significantly associated with HbA1c levels 17 . In another study combining PSG and continuous glucose monitoring, it was found that under normal conditions, subcutaneous glucose levels declined during REM sleep but paradoxically increased in the presence of SDB, whereas NREM events had little impact on glucose variability 4 . These findings suggest that respiratory events during REM sleep are more closely associated with glucose metabolism than those during NREM sleep.

REM sleep is characterized by reduced activity of upper‐airway dilator muscles and decreased ventilatory drive, making the upper airway more prone to collapsible than during NREM sleep. As a result, respiratory events in REM sleep are generally longer and accompanied by more profound oxygen desaturation 18 , 22 . In addition, REM sleep features abrupt and unpredictable surges and drops in blood pressure and heart rate, along with exaggerated post‐event sympathetic activation 22 .

Separately, in relation to respiratory events during REM sleep, the concept of REM sleep‐related OSA (REM OSA) has been previously described 11 . REM OSA is typically a mild to moderate subtype of OSA, occurring more frequently in women and often associated with poor adherence to continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy. Although its definition varies across studies, it is generally characterized by a REM‐AHI at least twice the NREM‐AHI (REM‐AHI/NREM‐AHI >2) and an NREM‐AHI < 15. As a physiological endotype, REM OSA has been associated with a low arousal threshold (ArTH). In patients with low ArTH, respiratory events tend to terminate early due to their higher propensity for arousal, thereby preventing severe hypoxemia. However, frequent cortical arousals following each event lead to significant sleep fragmentation. This repetitive arousal not only disrupts sleep architecture but also triggers exaggerated ventilatory responses, which may enhance dynamic ventilatory instability and predispose to subsequent respiratory events—further contributing to sleep fragmentation.

In our present study, this conventional REM OSA pattern was most common in the mild type (30%), whereas only 22% of patients classified as the REM‐predominant type met these criteria (Table S2). Accordingly, the REM‐predominant type identified in our analysis exhibited characteristics consistent with the traditional definition of REM OSA, while also constituting a distinct phenotype. Notably, this group displayed the second‐highest CT90 and the second‐lowest minimum SpO2 among the five clusters, following the severe desaturation category, indicating a marked degree of nocturnal hypoxemia. In addition, its ArTH was as low as that of the sleep‐fragmented type, suggesting a strong component of sleep fragmentation.

OSA is characterized by two major pathophysiological components—intermittent hypoxemia and sleep fragmentation. To determine which of these components is more strongly associated with T2DM, we subsequently performed a logistic regression analysis using T2DM as the dependent variable. CT90 was used as an indicator of hypoxemia, and ArTH was used as a surrogate marker of sleep fragmentation. In this analysis, only CT90 remained an independent predictor of T2DM, whereas ArTH did not, suggesting that nocturnal hypoxemia, rather than sleep fragmentation, appears to be the predominant factor associated with T2DM in patients with OSA. Moreover, CT90 remained an independent predictor even after including AHI in the model, indicating that the burden of nocturnal hypoxemia was more strongly related to T2DM than the conventional AHI metric.

Previous cluster‐based studies have similarly shown that phenotypes characterized by greater nocturnal hypoxia are more likely to exhibit glucose intolerance and a higher prevalence of cardiovascular disease 7 , 19 , 20 . Although our findings were generally consistent, some differences were observed compared with previous cluster analyses using polysomnographic indices. In a U.S. veteran cohort, the Hypopnea & Hypoxia and PLMS phenotypes were associated with a higher risk of new‐onset T2DM, whereas the REM & Hypoxia phenotype was not significant 20 . In another cohort from Korea, the Hypopnea cluster—comparable to our REM‐predominant type—showed a diabetes prevalence similar to the Hypoxemia cluster. However, after adjustment, only the Hypoxemia cluster remained a significant predictor of cardiovascular outcomes 19 . Notably, even in these high‐risk clusters, the prevalence of diabetes was relatively low (12–13%) compared with our cohort.

These discrepancies likely reflect variations in study design, analytical methods, and cohort characteristics, rather than a single underlying factor.

This study has several limitations. First, because of its cross‐sectional design, causal relationships between OSA phenotypes and type 2 diabetes cannot be inferred. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether specific phenotypes predict incident T2DM, diabetic complications, and cardiovascular outcomes, and whether phenotype‐targeted interventions can improve metabolic control.

Second, the study was conducted in a single Japanese sleep center; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to other ethnicities or clinical settings. In addition, diabetes status and treatment information were not fully standardized, which may have led to potential misclassification.

Third, cluster analysis is sometimes criticized for limited reproducibility. Although the gap statistic was used to determine the optimal number of clusters, cluster analysis is inherently sensitive to variable selection and initial centroids. To enhance reproducibility, principal component analysis (PCA) was first applied to reduce data dimensionality before clustering. Moreover, the k‐means algorithm—used in this study—has been shown to demonstrate relatively high stability and robustness compared with other clustering methods 23 . Bootstrap stability revealed moderate cluster stability (Jaccard coefficients 0.55–0.77), reflecting OSA's continuous pathophysiology. However, algorithmic reproducibility was excellent (ARI = 1.00), and primary findings were robust across different clustering solutions.

Fourth, we could not adjust for smoking, alcohol consumption, and hypnotic medication use due to incomplete data. Although we adjusted for age, sex, and BMI, residual confounding from these unmeasured lifestyle factors may have influenced the observed associations.

In summary, this study identified distinct polysomnographic phenotypes of OSA using an unsupervised machine‐learning approach and demonstrated that the severe desaturation and REM‐predominant types were independently associated with diabetes. In a complementary analysis, CT90—an index of nocturnal hypoxic burden—remained independently associated with T2DM even after adjustment for AHI and arousal threshold, suggesting that nocturnal hypoxemia, rather than sleep fragmentation or overall AHI severity, plays a predominant role in glucose dysregulation. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of physiological phenotyping in elucidating the metabolic heterogeneity of OSA and may contribute to a more individualized approach to risk assessment and management.

Future longitudinal and interventional studies are warranted to determine whether targeting specific phenotypes can improve glycemic control and reduce cardiometabolic complications, as well as to explore whether treatment responses differ across these phenotypes.
