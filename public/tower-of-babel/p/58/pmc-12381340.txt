Yemen’s Malaria Crisis and its Implications for the GCC (Gulf Cooperation Council) Countries
Authors: Shahul H. Ebrahim, Muna Al-Maslamani, Farida Al-Hosani, Saif Al-Abri, Manaf Alqahtani, Barrak Alahmad, Simon Bland, Ziad Memish
Published: 2025

The six member (Gulf Cooperation Council) GCC countries– Kingdom of |Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar & UAE—have made unprecedented progress in the health of their populations, reflected in the life expectancy rates (2023; range: females 76.5 to 83.1, males 72.2 to 79.5) [1]. The momentum in the health sector is reflected in the different GCC visions such as UAE Vision 2021, Qatar Vision 2030, Saudi Arabia Vision 2030, Kuwait Vision 2035, Bahrain Vision 2030 and Oman vision 2040. However, being in the region characterized by intricate conflicts, diverse climatic conditions, and varying development indices among its nations, GCC countries face significant challenges in ensuring health security. A pertinent example is the issue of malaria in Yemen.

Since 2000, during the first two decades of the global Roll Back Malaria partnership (now known as RBM Partnership to End Malaria), worldwide malaria-related deaths halved through a combination of scaling up access to insecticidal mosquito nets or indoor residual spraying and access to effective rapid diagnostic testing and drug therapies [2]. However, from 2019 on, in parallel with a dramatic escalation in conflicts and internally displaced people, the progress has stalled for a number of reasons including resistance to both insecticide and antimalarials, changes in parasite biology, and the introduction of An. stephensi. An additional challenge emerging in 2025 is the decline in global aid to malaria endemic resource poor countries.

In Yemen, about 2/3rd of the population is at risk for Malaria, the third leading reason for health consultations [3–6]. Less than half of the suspected Malaria cases are subjected to microscopy (2023, 29%), and the positivity rate is around 10%, with the fatal species Plasmodium falciparum, accounting for nearly all the confirmed cases (2023, 99.5%). [King Salman Humanitarian Aid & Relief Center (KSrelief) Program data].

In Yemen, conflict-related destruction of urban areas and population displacement internally to urban settings, destruction of public and private infrastructure limiting access to basic services, compounded by COVID-19’s impact, and undocumented migration from the Horn of Africa – are all inextricably linked to the persistence of Malaria and expanding list of vectors [3–6]. An important point in the spread of this highly invasive vector to Yemen lies in the context of the movement of the immunologically naïve Yemeni population from areas of low to high risk and exposure to vectors largely imported by migrants from the Horn of Africa. Of particular concern in Yemen is the establishment of An. stephensi in southern Yemen within an urban setting hosting internally displaced people, alongside recent identification in the Horn of Africa [4, 5]. World Health Organization (WHO) Threat Map between October 2021 and March 2022, registered the vector in Aden City, Al Hodayda (situated to the far east on the Red Sea), and Hadramout (northeast) [7]. An. stephensi appears to be well-established within the country and likely exists in many more locations. Other vectors recorded include An. arabiensis, An. culicifacies s.l., and An. rhodeniesis. A national vector survey, desirable to map the vector’s spread across Yemen, would not be feasible due to human resource capacity and conflict security conditions [6–9].

In the GCC, indigenous malaria cases are absent in Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar and UAE and have progressively declined in Oman and Saudi Arabia [8, 9]. In the GCC, only imported malaria cases continue to occur in countries that have large expatriate populations originating from malaria-endemic countries [3]. Furthermore, several bio-geo-ecological and economic factors could change this scenario if Yemen remains a perpetual reservoir for the sporadic introduction of malaria to the GCC.

First is the emergence of the vector An. stephensi in the GCC. An. stephensi has been reported in Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Qatar [8]. Though there are no reports of An stephensi as a carrier of the plasmodium pathogen in the GCC, unlike most Anopheles species, An. stephensi is considered an urban vector well adapted to breeding sites created by urbanization and thrives in such settings. Second, the continuous flow of Yemeni migrants and pilgrims to Saudi Arabia offers opportunities for malaria expansion, and the open borders among the GCC countries can further facilitate the spread. Third, the presence of heterogenous population groups across the GCC, some of whom are potentially immune-native. Fourth, changing climatic conditions, including excess rainfall observed in the GCC, combined with aggressive urban greening efforts, can facilitate temperature-dependent expansion of the range of mosquito species and can further complicate the situation. Finally, though prior screening of newly arrived expatriate workers in their native countries to detect malaria infection before their departure to GCC and stringent border controls during COVID-19 in the GCC may have helped reduce the incidence of imported malaria in recent years, the post-COVID-19 boom in tourism, economic activities, and religious pilgrimage can reverse the situation.

The implications of malaria outbreaks in the GCC include health care costs, impact on tourism due to increased screening and health checks and public anxiety, interruptions in trade and commerce leading to supply chain disruptions, and stigmatization of migrant workers.

Disease control programs in GCC countries are robust and well-equipped to respond effectively to outbreaks of known conditions. Also, air conditioning is universal in the GCC which can help minimize the spread of mosquitoes in dwellings. However, as GCC hosts a substantial number of migrant workers, whose healthcare-seeking behaviors may not be sufficient to enable the detection of disease clusters. An additional consideration for the GCC countries is that the threat of An. stephensi cannot be considered in isolation from malaria. Given its similarities in urban ecology to Aedes mosquitoes which transmit four major disease pathogens (dengue virus, yellow fever virus, chikungunya virus, and Zika virus), An stephensi presence in the region should be considered an impetus for GCC to increase alertness for these and other vector-borne diseases, and potentially other uncommon conditions.

Therefore, Yemen’s malaria crisis calls for vigilance and proactiveness in the GCC (Box 1). Indeed, a permanent solution to ending the crisis in Yemen and achieving the development and stability of that country can help reduce the health security challenges of the GCC countries. In the interim, assuring uninterrupted fidelity and scale of vector control and monitoring activities combined with detection, treatment, and resistance monitoring of imported cases can help GCC remain malaria-free.
