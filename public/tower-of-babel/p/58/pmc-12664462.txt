PROTACs therapeutically target the polyglutamine androgen receptor in spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy models
Authors: Agamjot Sangotra, Satya L. Reddy, Curtis J. Kuo, Weiguo Xiang, Diane E. Merry, Christopher Grunseich, Shaomeng Wang, Andrew P. Lieberman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy (SBMA) is a CAG/polyglutamine (polyQ) repeat expansion disorder in which the mutant androgen receptor (AR) protein triggers progressive degeneration of the neuromuscular system in men. As the misfolded polyQ AR is the proximal mediator of toxicity, therapeutic efforts have focused on targeting the mutant protein, but these prior efforts have met with limited success in SBMA patients. Here, we examine the efficacy of small molecule AR proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) degraders that rapidly and potently promote AR ubiquitination and degradation by the proteasome. We show that the AR PROTAC degrader ARD-1676 clears polyQ AR in an over-expression system, in patient iPSC-derived induced motor neurons and skeletal muscle cells, and in a gene targeted mouse model of disease. Furthermore, we demonstrate that 24-h treatment with ARD-1676 rescues transcriptional dysregulation in SBMA induced skeletal muscle cells. These data provide evidence of therapeutic efficacy and in vivo target engagement, establishing AR PROTAC degraders as potential therapeutic agents for the treatment of SBMA.

Introducution

Spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy (SBMA) is a slowly progressive, X-linked and sex-limited degenerative disorder that affects lower motor neurons and skeletal muscle [1]. It is caused by a CAG/polyglutamine (polyQ) tract expansion in the gene encoding the androgen receptor (AR), which is located on the X chromosome [2]. Affected men develop age-dependent proximal limb and bulbar muscle weakness along with signs of partial androgen insensitivity [1]. Female carriers of a polyQ expanded AR allele are relatively asymptomatic, as male levels of circulating androgens are required to trigger nuclear translocation and misfolding of the polyQ AR protein, features which drive disease pathogenesis [3]. There are currently no FDA-approved therapies for SBMA, highlighting the significant unmet medical need for this patient population.

SBMA is one of ten neurodegenerative disorders caused by a CAG/polyQ tract expansion in a gene unique to the disorder [4]. Although these genes are otherwise unrelated, the disorders share several phenotypic and mechanistic similarities. Notably, misfolding of the disease-causing proteins underlies a toxic gain-of-function that significantly contributes to pathogenesis [5]. Based on this notion, therapeutic efforts have focused on strategies to modulate the toxicity of the mutated proteins [6]. In SBMA, these efforts have included prior clinical trials targeting the ligand-dependent protein misfolding of the polyQ AR protein, but have been met with limited success and therefore alternative approaches are sought [7].

Here, we explore one such alternative therapeutic strategy by using AR-targeted proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs), a class of bifunctional small molecules that were initially developed to promote degradation of the AR protein in individuals with prostate cancer. PROTACs are comprised of three domains: an E3 ubiquitin ligase-binding ligand, a target protein-binding ligand, and a linker that connects these two regions. Once ubiquitinated by the recruited E3 ligase, the target protein is degraded by the proteasome, and the PROTAC is recycled to be reused within the cell [8]. PROTACs that bind to and degrade AR, or AR degraders (ARDs), are orally bioavailable and have advanced to clinical trials for treatment of prostate cancer [[9], [10], [11]]. This study tests the therapeutic potential of ARDs to target the polyQ AR in SBMA. We demonstrate that ARDs potently promote proteasome-mediated degradation of the polyQ AR in cell models of SBMA, including human iPSC-derived induced motor neurons and skeletal muscle. Moreover, we establish in vivo target engagement by demonstrating the ability of ARDs to degrade the polyQ AR in a gene targeted mouse model of SBMA. Finally, we show that the clearance of the polyQ AR by ARDs in human induced skeletal muscle cells has therapeutic benefits on transcriptional dysregulation in these cells. Taken together, these studies provide compelling support for pursuing this novel therapeutic approach in SBMA.

Materials and methods

Antibodies

Primary antibodies

The following primary antibodies (antigen, dilution, vendor) were used: AR, 1:1000 (western) 1:100 (ICC), 06–680 Millipore; GAPDH, 1:10000, NB600-502 Novus Biologicals; Actin, 1:2000, MA1-91399 Thermo Fisher; Lamin B1, 1:1000, ab16048 Abcam; Tuj1, 1:1000, G712A Promega; Isl1, 1:250, ab109517 Abcam; HB9, 1:200, clone 81.5C10 Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank; Desmin, 1:200, ab15200 Abcam.

Secondary antibodies

The following secondary antibodies (antigen, dilution, vendor) were used: Goat anti-mouse IgG (H ​+ ​L)-HRP conjugate, 1:2,000, Bio-Rad 170-6516; Goat anti-rabbit IgG (H ​+ ​L)-HRP conjugate, 1:2,000, Bio-Rad 170-6515; Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H ​+ ​L), 1:1000, Invitrogen A11012; Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-mouse IgG (H ​+ ​L), 1:1000, Invitrogen A-11032; and Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H ​+ ​L), 1:1000, A11008.

Mice

AR113Q knock-in mice were generated using exon 1 specific targeting as previously described [12,13]. Mice were housed in a 12-h light/dark cycle facility. Ear samples were collected to confirm genotype using the following primers: AR forward: 5′-CCAGAATCTGTTCCAGAGCGTG-3′ (MilliporeSigma, 6-FAM labeled); AR reverse: 5′-TGTTCCCCTGGACTCAGATG-3′ (Invitrogen). Samples were sent to Laragen to determine CAG repeat length, and male mice with glutamine tracts greater than 97Q were chosen for studies. For oral gavage studies, 8–10-week-old AR113Q males were randomized to treatment or vehicle control groups. ARD-1676 was dissolved in PEG-200 and administered (30 ​mg/kg body weight) once. Animals were euthanized after 6 ​h for tissue collection. All procedures involving mice were approved by the University of Michigan Committee on Use and Care of Animals (PRO00010017) and conducted in accordance with institutional and federal guidelines.

Cell lines

PC12 cells

PC12 ​cells expressing AR112Q controlled by the Tet-on system were described previously [14]. Briefly, PC12 ​cells were grown and passaged on poly-d-lysine coated plates in high-glucose phenol-red–free DMEM (Invitrogen 21063029) containing 10 ​% charcoal-stripped horse serum (Invitrogen), 5 ​% charcoal-stripped FBS (Atlantic Biologicals), 100 units/ml penicillin/streptomycin (Invitrogen 10378016), 200 ​μg/mL hygromycin B (Invitrogen 10687010), and 100 ​μg/mL G418 (Invitrogen 10131035) at 37 ​°C in 10 ​% CO2.

iPSCs

Patient-derived iPSCs stably expressing the hNIL cassette were described previously [15,16]. Briefly, iPSCs were cultured in StemFlex Basal Medium (Gibco A33493-01) with StemFlex Supplement on plates coated for 1 ​h with Geltrex (Thermo Fisher Scientific A1413302) diluted in DMEM/F12 (Gibco 11330-032). Media changes occurred every two days, or as needed. Once iPSCs reached confluency, they were split by rinsing with PBS, no calcium, no magnesium (PBS−/−; Gibco 10010-23), then incubated with Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent (STEMCELL Technologies 100–0485) for approximately 2 ​min, and plated on new Geltrex-coated plates.

iMNs

iPSCs were disassociated into a single-cell suspension after a 5 ​min incubation with Accutase (STEMCELL Technologies 07922), spun at a low speed, and seeded on Geltrex-coated plates in StemFlex media in the presence of 10 ​μM Y-27632. On days 0 and 1, neuronal differentiation was induced with Neural Induction Medium: DMEM/F12 (Invitrogen 11330032), B27 (1 ​× ​; Gibco 17504044), NEAA (1 ​× ​; Invitrogen 11140050), GlutaMax (1 ​× ​; Invitrogen 35050061), compound E (0.1 ​μg/mL; Calbiochem 565790), Y-27632 (10 ​μM; Tocris 1254), doxycycline (1 ​μg/mL; MilliporeSigma D9891). On day 2, cells were incubated with Accutase for 5 ​min, and were then replated on Geltrex-coated plates with Neural Differentiation Medium (NDM): DMEM/F12 (Gibco 11330-032), BrainPhys neuronal medium (StemCell 05790), B27 (1 ​× ​; Gibco 17504044), NEAA (1 ​× ​; Invitrogen 11140050), GlutaMax (1 ​× ​; Invitrogen 35050061), compound E (0.1 ​μg/mL; Calbiochem 565790), Y-27632 (10 ​μM; Tocris 1254), doxycycline (1 ​μg/mL; MilliporeSigma D9891), BDNF (10 ​ng/mL; Peprotech 450-02), GDNF (10 ​ng/mL; Peprotech 450-10), NT3 (10 ​ng/mL; Peprotech 450-03). Cells received full media changes daily until day 4 when they were treated as described in figure legends.

iSKMs

iPSCs were incubated with Accutase for 5 ​min to allow for disassociation to single cells, spun at a low speed, then seeded onto Geltrex-coated plates in Skeletal Muscle Induction Media (amsbio SKM01). Media changes were carried out every 2–3 days until day 7–10. Then, the cells were incubated in Accutase for 5 ​min, and plated into Skeletal Muscle Myoblast Media (amsbio SKM02) with half media changes every other day. At day 18, or when cells reached confluency, Skeletal Muscle Myoblast Media was replaced with Skeletal Muscle Myotube Media (amsbio SKM03). From this point on, cells received half media changes every other day until day 26, at which time cells were treated as described in figure legends.

Drug treatment

Cells were treated as indicated with R1881 (R0908 Sigma-Aldrich) or indicated PROTACS that were synthesized as described previously [[17], [18], [19]].

Western blot

Cell collection

PC12 ​cells, iMNs, or iSKMs were washed with PBS, pelleted, and resuspended in RIPA (Teknova R3792) with cOmplete Mini protease inhibitor (Roche 46264500). Cells were then sonicated and spun at 15000 rcf for 15 ​min at 4 ​°C. The supernatant was separated, and protein concentration in the soluble fraction was quantified using DC-Assay (BioRad). For cytoplasmic and nuclear extractions, the NE-PER reagent kit (Pierce 7833) was used following manufacturer's protocol.

Tissue collection

Muscle and lumbar spinal cord were collected post-mortem and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. Tissues were resuspended in lysate buffer as above and homogenized by a tissue homogenizer and sonicater. Tissues were spun at 15000 rcf for 15 ​min at 4 ​°C. Supernatants were separated and protein concentrations quantified as above.

Gels were run for 1.5 ​h at 130 ​V in 1 ​× ​NuPAGE MOPS SDS running buffer (Invitrogen NP0001). Gels were transferred to Immobilon-P 0.45 ​μm PVDF (Merck Millipore IPVH00010) membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5 ​% nonfat dry milk for 30 ​min at room temperature, then incubated in primary antibody overnight at 4 ​°C. Membranes were incubated in secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 ​h. Immunoreactivity was detected with SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific 34577) or Thermo Scientific™ Pierce™ ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific PI32106) via an iBright FL1500 imaging system (Invitrogen A44241). Quantification of bands within the linear exposure range was performed using ImageStudio Lite (Licor), and band intensity was normalized to the indicated loading control.

Results

PROTACs targeting the androgen receptor degrade polyQ AR in PC12 ​cells

To identify potential therapeutics for SBMA, we tested three previously characterized, potent ARDs for the ability to promote clearance of the polyQ AR. The chemical structure of these ARDs (ARD-2128, ARD-2585, and ARD-1676) is shown in Fig. 1a [[17], [18], [19]]. All three ARDs bind cereblon, as previous research identified it as one of the more optimal E3 ubiquitin ligases to target for generating potent PROTACs with oral bioavailability [19]. ARD-2128 and ARD-2585 share structural similarities in their AR ligand and their cereblon ligand, thalidomide, but differ in their linkers [17]. The AR ligand domain of ARD-2128 and ARD-2585 uses aryloxy tetramethylcyclobutane as a template to derive its structure [29]. ARD-1676 makes improvements to both the AR and cereblon ligands [17]. Its AR ligand utilizes spiro ring systems, as the resulting reduced polar surface and conformational flexibility increases the compound's oral bioavailability, while thalidomide has been modified to form a tricyclic structure [17]. To examine the potential of these ARDs to degrade polyQ AR, we first tested their effects in Tet-on PC12 ​cells that overexpress polyQ AR with 112 CAG/glutamine repeats (AR112Q) [14]. The three ARDs showed a similar concentration response following treatment for 24 ​h, with significant effects occurring as low as 10 ​nM (Fig. 1b–d, f-g). We sought to determine whether ARD-1676 promoted degradation of the polyQ AR in the nucleus or cytoplasm. To accomplish this, PC12 ​cells expressing polyQ AR were treated with the synthetic androgen R1881 for 72 ​h to ensure nuclear translocation and protein misfolding. Given that both R1881 and ARD-1676, like the other ARDs tested here, bind to the AR's ligand binding domain, we followed this treatment with 48 ​h of media changes with neither doxycycline nor R1881 to wash both out of the system. After washout, cells were treated with 100 ​nM ARD-1676 for 6 ​h. Analysis of nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions revealed significant decreases in both compartments (Fig. 2d).

As a validation of the specificity of the mechanism that ARD-1676 utilizes to degrade the polyQ AR, cells were treated with an inhibitor of ARD-1676 (ARI), which consists of the AR ligand of ARD-1676 without the cereblon ligand. Pretreatment with ARI or with the proteasome inhibitor epoxomicin blocked the effect of the ARD, demonstrating that ARD-1676 directly bound to and promoted proteasomal degradation of the polyQ AR in PC12 ​cells (Fig. 2e). Analysis of ARD-2128 yielded similar findings (Supplemental Fig. 1b).

ARD-1676 degrades polyQ AR in iMNs

After establishing the efficacy of ARD-1676 in cells that overexpress the polyQ AR, we sought to test this compound in a system that expressed polyQ AR with a clinically relevant polyQ tract length, at endogenous levels, and in primary cells that are disease targets. To accomplish this, we used a previously established panel of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC) that contain a doxycycline-inducible NGN2-ISL1-LHX3 (hNIL) gene cassette that promotes differentiation into induced motor neurons (iMNs) [28]. These iPSCs were established from individuals with SBMA or healthy controls and express either polyQ AR (Q-tract length range from 51 to 53) or WT AR [16]. Following 4 days of doxycycline treatment, both control (HT179) and SBMA (SB3) iMNs expressed Tuj1, a neuronal marker, ISL1 and HB9, motor neuron markers, and AR (Fig. 3a). Treatment with ARD-1676 for 24 ​h significantly decreased AR protein but not AR mRNA levels in both control and SBMA iMNs (Fig. 3b–d) without causing toxicity (Fig. 3e). Similar results were seen with iMNs derived from an additional control (HT180) and SBMA (SB17) iPSC line (Supplemental Fig. 2a–d) and following treatment with ARD-2128 (Supplemental Fig. 2e–h). ARD-1676 degrades polyQ AR in iSKMs

As SBMA is characterized by degeneration of the neuromuscular system, we sought to determine the extent to which ARDs promote clearance of the polyQ AR in skeletal muscle. To begin to address this question, we used an established growth-factor based protocol to differentiate the same iPSC lines studied in Fig. 3 into induced skeletal muscle myotubes (iSKMs) (Fig. 4a) [30]. We confirmed that differentiation of both control (HT179) and SBMA (SB3, SB17) iPSCs to iSKM was accompanied by decreased expression of the pluripotency marker NANOG and increased expression of the muscle markers MYOD and DESMIN (Fig. 4b–d, Supplemental Fig. 3a–c). Both control and SBMA iSKM expressed desmin and AR proteins as visualized by immunofluorescence (Fig. 4e and f). Treatment with ARD-1676 for 24 ​h significantly decreased AR protein in both control and SBMA iSKM (Fig. 4g–h, j-k, Supplemental Fig. 3d and e). These effects were accompanied by a slight decrease in AR mRNA levels (Fig. 4i, l, Supplemental Fig. 3f). ARD-1676 demonstrates in vivo target engagement and therapeutic efficacy

To determine whether ARD-1676 promotes polyQ AR protein degradation in vivo, we studied AR113Q mice, a mouse model of SBMA in which gene targeting was used to humanize the mouse AR gene while inserting an expanded CAG repeat [12,13]. AR113Q male mice received a single administration of ARD-1676 or vehicle control by oral gavage. Analysis of muscle tissue collected 6 ​h after treatment demonstrated significantly lower levels of AR protein, but no change in Ar mRNA expression (Fig. 5a–c). No significant change in AR protein levels was seen in lumbar spinal cord (Supplemental Fig. 4). Treatment did not alter serum biomarkers of liver or kidney function in WT or AR113Q males (Supplemental Fig. 5), consistent with a lack of acute toxicity following drug administration. To examine whether diminished levels of the polyQ AR rescued disease phenotypes in vitro, we utilized control and SBMA iSKM. Cells were treated with or without the synthetic androgen R1881 for 24 ​h and then treated with ARD-1676 for 24 ​h. Western blot demonstrated the expected increase of AR protein levels after R1881 treatment due to the ligand-dependent increase in AR protein half-life. Treatment with ARD-1676 in presence of R1881 resulted in significantly diminished levels of WT and polyQ AR (Fig. 5d–f). RNA from these experiments was collected from two different WT lines (HT179 and HT180) and two different SBMA lines (SB3 and SB17) and submitted for bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Analysis of the significantly differentially expressed (DE) genes in SBMA vs control iSKM (adjusted p value ​< ​0.05 and |log2(fold-change)| ​> ​1) revealed that of the 23,756 genes identified through sequencing, 763 were up-regulated and 457 were down-regulated (Fig. 5g–Supplemental Table 1). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of these DE genes revealed that the top up-regulated molecular functions related to ion channels while the top down-regulated molecular functions related to transcription (Fig. 5h). Notably, a subset of these DE genes was significantly rescued by treatment with ARD-1676 (Fig. 5i–Supplemental Table 2). Of this set of rescued genes in SBMA iSKM, the expression of only four genes (PCDH1, ENSG00000273312, LINC01886, LINC01894) was significantly altered in control iSKMs following treatment with ARD-1676, reflecting that most of these genes were not androgen-responsive. These data indicated that the rescue of their expression in SBMA iSKM reflected loss of the toxic gain-of-function conferred by the polyQ expansion.

Discussion

The neuromuscular degeneration that characterizes SBMA is mediated by toxicity from the misfolded polyQ AR protein within the nucleus, resulting in disruption of downstream homeostatic mechanisms. Efforts to mitigate this toxicity underlie several emerging therapeutic strategies [6]. Here, we test the efficacy of AR-targeted PROTACs, bifunctional small molecules that promote the ubiquitination and degradation of the AR protein. Our findings demonstrate the potent and rapid clearance of the polyQ AR protein by PROTACs in an immortalized cell line, in patient iPSC-derived iMNs and iSKMs, and in gene targeted mice, establishing in vivo target engagement. This treatment facilitated proteasomal degradation of the polyQ AR in both the cytoplasm and nucleus in the absence of cytotoxicity. Moreover, RNA-seq analysis following treatment with the PROTAC ARD-1676 demonstrated partial rescue of the transcriptional dysregulation that occurs in SBMA iSKMs. These results provide compelling support for pursuing this novel therapeutic approach in SBMA and justify future long-term studies.

Although PROTACs do not cross the blood brain barrier to gain access to brainstem and spinal cord lower motor neurons, SBMA is characterized by degeneration of the neuromuscular system that, in mouse models, is rescued by targeting polyQ AR expression only in the peripheral tissues including muscle [31,32]. These observations and others in transgenic mice [33] and in patient-derived biospecimens [34,35], support a conceptual model of disease pathogenesis in which manifestations in skeletal muscle precede lower motor neuron degeneration and can be targeted to rescue pathology [36]. As such, degrading the polyQ AR in SBMA muscle is a therapeutically attractive approach. It is noted that prior studies demonstrated beneficial effects from peripherally administered AR-targeted antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) in SBMA knock-in mice [31]. Like PROTAC treatment (Fig. 5a and b), ASO administration causes partial loss of AR expression in mouse muscle after treatment [31]. It is possible that combined administration would synergistically enable more complete protein knockdown at lower drug concentrations.

A consideration for using PROTACs in SBMA is the inherent competition between circulating androgens and the ARD, as both bind to the ligand binding domain (LBD) of AR. As explored in this paper, an inhibitor that binds to the AR LBD blocks the effect of ARD-1676 in cell culture (Fig. 2e). Nonetheless, we observed a significant decrease in polyQ AR levels in iSKMs that were co-treated with ARD-1676 and the synthetic androgen R1881 (Fig. 5d). Furthermore, target engagement was observed in skeletal muscle of AR113Q mice that have endogenous levels of circulating testosterone (Fig. 5a and b). Together, these findings indicate that ARD-1676 has the ability to overcome this natural competition and significantly clear polyQ AR levels.

PROTACs such as ARD-1676 are characterized by promising pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, high oral bioavailability, and initial successes in the clinic for the treatment of prostate cancer. These attributes complement the fact that targeting the polyQ AR in peripheral tissues may provide additional benefits beyond the neuromuscular system by rescuing metabolic and cardiac abnormalities that occur in some individuals [5]. We conclude that PROTACs warrant further exploration as a treatment modality in SBMA.
