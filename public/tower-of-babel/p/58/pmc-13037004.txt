4-phenylbutyric acid attenuates hepatic ER stress, pyroptosis, and NAFLD in adult rat offspring exposed to a high-fat, high-fructose diet during perinatal and adulthood
Authors: Sara Ojaghi, Farzaneh Eskandari, Fariba Azarkish, Haniyeh Soltani, Fateme Binayi, Behnam Saeidi, Mehdi Hedayati, Afsaneh Goudarzi, Abdollah Amini, Fariba khodagholi, Homeira Zardooz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPerinatal exposure to high-fat and/or high-fructose diets affects offspring metabolic programming, increasing the risk of metabolic disorders such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). In addition, re-exposure to these diets during adulthood exacerbates metabolic disturbances, indicating an interaction between early- and later-life diets in disease development. Therefore, the present study was designed to assess the effects of high-fat, high-fructose (HFF) diet exposure during the perinatal period and adulthood on hepatic endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, pyroptosis, and NAFLD development, as well as to evaluate the potential therapeutic effects of 4-phenylbutyric acid (4-PBA).MethodsMale rat offspring were randomly assigned to six groups based on perinatal and adulthood diets: normal diet during both periods (N-N), normal perinatal diet with adulthood HFF diet (N-HFF), perinatal HFF diet with normal adulthood diet (HFF-N), perinatal and adulthood HFF diet (HFF-HFF), perinatal and adulthood HFF diet with DMSO treatment (HFF-HFF-DMSO), and perinatal and adulthood HFF diet with 4-PBA treatment (HFF-HFF-4PBA). Metabolic parameters, inflammatory markers, oxidative and ER stress indicators, a pyroptosis marker, and hepatic histological parameters were evaluated.ResultsExposure to the HFF diet during the perinatal period and adulthood induced insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, hepatic oxidative and ER stress, inflammation, pyroptosis, lipid droplet accumulation, and NAFLD development. Treatment with 4-PBA improved all adverse effects caused by HFF diet exposure during both the perinatal and adult periods in offspring.ConclusionThese findings highlight the importance of dietary interventions during critical developmental windows and suggest ER stress inhibition as a promising therapeutic approach for NAFLD.

Background

Unhealthy maternal nutrition may elevate the likelihood of metabolic diseases in offspring, including insulin resistance (IR) and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [1]. The perinatal period is a crucial window during which maternal nutrition can exert long-lasting effects on offspring metabolic programming by influencing organ development and inducing epigenetic changes [2, 3]. The resulting metabolic impairments may remain silent when a standard diet is followed after weaning; however, they can re-emerge upon re-exposure to a high-fat diet, potentially leading to more severe metabolic disturbances [4]. NAFLD comprises a range of hepatic disorders, from simple steatosis— characterized by triglyceride accumulation exceeding 5% of hepatocytes—to the more severe non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) without substantial alcohol intake [5]. While hepatic steatosis is often reversible, the persistent accumulation of lipotoxic intermediates, including ceramides and free fatty acids (FFAs), can trigger cellular dysfunction, leading to inflammation and disrupted insulin signaling [1]. Hepatic fat accumulation promotes IR through various mechanisms. Increased lipid β-oxidation in the liver leads to elevated ROS production and oxidative stress, resulting in the secretion of inflammatory mediators that impair insulin function [6, 7]. The typical Western diet, characterized by high amounts of refined sugars—especially fructose—and saturated fats, is strongly associated with metabolic disorders, including NAFLD. Rising fructose consumption worldwide is driven by processed foods containing high amounts of fructose [8]. Fructose is primarily metabolized in the liver, where it bypasses key regulatory steps of glycolysis, causing de novo lipogenesis, oxidative stress, and excessive production of uric acid (UA) [9, 10]. Oxidative and ER stress arising from a high-fat diet downregulate the expression and enzymatic activity of insulin-degrading enzyme (IDE) [11, 12]. IDE is a key enzyme involved in hepatic insulin clearance, and reduced IDE levels and activity have been linked to compromised insulin degradation, IR, disrupted insulin secretion from beta cells, and greater susceptibility to type 2 diabetes [13, 14]. IR in turn can result in endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, which activates several cellular stress pathways [15]. Programmed cell death, such as pyroptosis, can also be triggered by ER stress [16]. Pyroptosis in hepatic cells triggers cell membrane rupture, releasing proinflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF‑α) and interleukin-1β (IL‑1β), which accelerate liver inflammation and fibrosis. The role of pyroptosis and its components is therefore crucial in NAFLD pathogenesis [17]. Given the extensive involvement of ER stress in NAFLD progression and its interplay with various mechanisms, including autophagy, apoptosis, and pyroptosis, targeting ER stress presents a promising therapeutic approach. 4-PBA, a chemical chaperone with a short-chain fatty acid structure, has shown potential to enhance insulin sensitivity in vivo. It also exerts protective effects against lipid accumulation and lipotoxicity through autophagy activation [18, 19]. However, despite the well-established role of maternal overnutrition and Western diets in the developmental programming of NAFLD, few studies have examined whether perinatal exposure to a high-fat, high-fructose (HFF) diet predisposes offspring to NAFLD and metabolic disturbances that re-emerge upon re-exposure to the same diet in adulthood, independent of overt obesity. Moreover, the specific contribution of hepatic ER stress and pyroptosis to this programmed susceptibility, as well as their interaction with IDE–mediated insulin clearance, remains poorly defined in this context. Although 4‑PBA has been employed to inhibit ER stress in other models of liver injury and fructose-induced steatosis, its potential to ameliorate perinatally programmed, diet-induced NAFLD and to modulate hepatic pyroptosis and IDE in adult offspring has not been evaluated. Therefore, the current study was designed to assess how HFF diet exposure during the perinatal and adult periods contributes to hepatic oxidative and ER stress, inflammation, pyroptosis, and NAFLD development in adult male offspring, and to assess the therapeutic effects of 4-PBA. In this regard, the study assessed anthropometric parameters (body weight, abdominal fat tissue weight, and food intake, which significantly influences these anthropometric parameters); metabolic factors (glucose, insulin, and lipid profile); inflammatory markers (IL-1β and TNF-α); indicators of oxidative stress (superoxide dismutase (SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione (GSH)) and ER stress (Binding immunoglobulin protein (BIP), and C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP)); a pyroptosis marker (Gasdermin D (GSDMD)); hepatic enzymes (aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT)); hepatic IDE content; and hepatic histological alterations using Oil Red O. Additionally, the effects of 4-PBA on these parameters were examined. By identifying the molecular mechanisms underlying diet-induced NAFLD, this study offers a new understanding of possible preventive and therapeutic approaches for this common disorder.

Materials and methods

Animal studies

All experimental procedures involving animals were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 80–23, revised 1996) and were approved by the Research and Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran (IR.SBMU.AEC.1402.097). Clinical trial registration: not applicable. Female and male Wistar rats (200 ± 10 g and 250 ± 20 g, in the corresponding order) were obtained from SBMU Neuroscience Research Center (NRC) (Tehran, Iran) and kept under standardized conditions, with a 12 h light/dark cycle at 22 ± 2 °C. Following a 1-week acclimatization period, female animals (mothers) were categorized into two groups: one receiving the HFF diet and the other maintained on a standard diet. After 2 weeks of dietary intervention, the rats were paired for mating overnight (one male and two female rats per cage); detection of a vaginal plug confirmed successful mating. The dams maintained their assigned diets during both gestation and lactation. Including the 2 weeks of dietary intervention prior to pregnancy, the total duration of the dietary intervention for the dams was 8 weeks. Following the lactation period, male offspring from both groups were selected and fed a standard diet for 4 weeks (female offspring were used for another study). This normal-diet interval (postnatal day (PND) 21–49) provided a more realistic model of human dietary pattern. In humans, excessive intake of HFF diets commonly begins or becomes more pronounced during adolescence or adulthood rather than continuously from early life. Introducing a period of normal diet before re-exposure in adulthood mimicked this human-like pattern and helped to evaluate how prior perinatal exposure influences susceptibility to a later HFF diet. In addition, by allocating this normal diet interval, it was feasible to assess the metabolic consequences of re-exposure to the same dietary challenge. Using this approach helped to determine whether offspring exposed to the HFF diet during gestation and lactation exhibit different metabolic outcomes when re-exposed in adulthood compared with those exposed only perinatally or only in adulthood. After this initial phase, the offspring in each group were randomly categorized into two subgroups: one that received the HFF diet (31% fat, 5% fructose, 20% sucrose) and another that continued on the standard chow diet. These dietary regimens were maintained for an additional 8 weeks [5]. However, at the end of the 4th week, the offspring that received the HFF diet during both the perinatal and adult periods (the HFF-HFF group) were subdivided into three experimental subgroups: one receiving only the HFF diet, one receiving the HFF diet plus 4-PBA, and one receiving the HFF diet plus dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), the solvent for 4-PBA.

Body weight

Offspring body weight (n = 6/group) was measured weekly from birth until PND 105 using a digital scale (Amput, China, resolution 0.01 g).

Monitoring of food intake

Weekly measurements of food intake (n = 6/group) were taken over 24 h intervals (from the post-weaning period until the study completion, PND 28–105). Average food intake per animal was calculated as (food given – food remaining) / the number of animals per cage.

Methodology for Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT) and Insulin Tolerance Test (ITT)

One week prior to the study completion, an OGTT was performed after an 8 h fasting period (7 a.m. − 3 p.m.). Animals were exposed to isoflurane (Abbott, India) in a desiccator, and anesthesia was confirmed by observing a 50% decrease in respiratory rate, loss of righting reflex, and unresponsiveness to toe pinch. Fasting blood samples (200 uL) were collected via retro-orbital plexus puncture into heparinized microtubes (10 uL heparin [5000 IU/mL] per 1 mL blood). Plasma was separated by centrifugation (664 × g, 10 min, and 4 °C) to measure fasting insulin and glucose levels. Subsequently, an oral glucose solution (2 g/kg of a 45% solution in distilled water) was administered. Blood specimens were obtained at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min (100 uL at each time point), and plasma separation was performed as mentioned above. Plasma glucose levels were quantified using the glucose oxidase assay (Cat. No.: 10–538, ZistChem Co., Tehran, Iran). Plasma insulin levels were measured with a rat-specific ELISA assay Kit (Cat. No.: ZB-10707 C-R9648, Zellbio, GmbH, Lonsee, Germany). The Retro-orbital puncture method was chosen for blood sampling [22, 23] because it allows rapid sampling, minimizing the risk of hemolysis or clot formation, which could distort measured parameters such as glucose and insulin levels. Moreover, since this method provides rapid blood sampling, it allows blood samples to be obtained at precise time points in studies such as the OGTT. The ITT was performed 48 h later [24, 25] following a 6–8 h fast. Human insulin (0.2 IU/kg) was administered intraperitoneally, and plasma glucose concentrations were measured at the same intervals as in the OGTT.

Terminal blood collection and intra-abdominal fat removal

At the conclusion of the study, after a 14–16 h fasting period [12] and isoflurane anesthesia with toe-pinch confirmation (as described above), animals were decapitated using a guillotine. Subsequently, trunk blood was collected in heparinized microtubes (10 uL heparin [5000 IU/mL] per 1 mL blood), and centrifuged (664 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), then the resulting plasma samples were kept at − 80 °C. Following abdominal dissection, the intra-abdominal fat pads (mesenteric, perirenal, and epididymal) were carefully excised and weighed using a digital scale (Amput, China, resolution 0.01 g). Subsequently, adipose tissue mass relative to total body weight was calculated and expressed as a percentage.

Plasma biomarkers assessment

Colorimetric assays were used to analyze uric acid (Cat. No.: 10–581, ZistChem Co., Tehran, Iran) and the lipid profile, including LDL (Cat. No.: 10560, ZistChem Co., Tehran, Iran), HDL (Cat. No.: 10–547, ZistChem Co., Tehran, Iran), cholesterol (CHOL) (Cat. No.: 10–521, ZistChem Co., Tehran, Iran), and FFAs (Cat. No.: ZB-FFA-96 A, ZistChem Co., Tehran, Iran). Moreover, the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry (IFCC)-based biochemical kits were used to measure the AST (Cat. No.: 10–541, ZistChem Co., Tehran, Iran) and ALT (Cat. No.: 10–542, ZistChem Co., Tehran, Iran) activities.

Hepatic tissue preparation and measurements

Following decapitation and blood collection at the conclusion of the experimental period, half of the right liver lobes were collected and homogenized in lysis buffer, while the other half was used for Oil Red O staining. For preparation 10 mL of lysis buffer 500 uL of 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), 0.025 g sodium deoxycholate (0.25%) (Merck, Germany), 0.08 g NaCl (Merck, Germany), 0.01 g SDS (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), 0.003 g EDTA (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), 10 uL of 0.1% Triton X-100 (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and one tablet of protease inhibitor cocktail [26] (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) were added to a falcon tube and dissolved by vortexing. The total volume was then adjusted to 10 mL using distilled water. The frozen liver tissue samples, stored at − 80 °C, were finely minced. Approximately 200 mg of tissue was weighed in specialized homogenizer microtubes, and lysis buffer was added at a ratio of 3 uL per mg of tissue. The samples were homogenized until a uniform mixture with no visible fragments was obtained. The homogenate was then centrifuged, and the obtained supernatant was kept at − 80 °C for further analysis. Total protein in the supernatant was quantified via the Bradford method [27]. IL-1β and TNF-α levels were determined by rat-specific ELISA kits (Cat. No.: PTC-10119-R9648, Padgin Teb Co., Iran, and Cat. No.: 438204, BioLegend, USA, in the corresponding order). GSH (Cat. No.: ZB-GSH-96 A, Zellbio, GmbH, Lonsee, Germany), SOD (Cat. No.: ZB-SOD-96 A, Zellbio, GmbH, Lonsee, Germany), and MDA (Cat. No.: ZB-MDA-96 A, Zellbio, GmbH, Lonsee, Germany) were measured using commercial assay kits. The level of IDE was also assessed using a rat-specific ELISA kit (Cat. No.: ZB-11387 C-R9648, Zellbio, GmbH, Lonsee, Germany).

Evaluation of the IR index

Insulin resistance was evaluated via the Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) and calculated as follows: [fasting insulin (uU/mL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)] / 405 [22].

Western blot analysis

Protein concentrations in the supernatant (n = 3/group) were quantified using the Bradford method [27]. Electrophoretic separation was then performed on 12% SDS–PAGE gels at 120 V for 60 min. The amount of protein loaded per lane was 60 ug for PIP and CHOP, and 40 ug for GSDMD using a Mini-PROTEAN II system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The separated proteins were blotted onto PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) at 100 V for 85 min and the membranes were blocked with 5% BSA (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) in 0.1% Tween-20 for 90 min at ambient temperature (24 °C) and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GSDMD (Cat. No.: 39754 S Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), BIP (Cat. No.: 3183 S, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), CHOP (Cat. No.: 5554 S, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) and β-actin (for BIP and CHOP, 1:4000, Cat. No.: 4970, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; for GSDMD, 1:2500, Cat. No: ab8227, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). After primary antibody treatment, membranes were rinsed three times with TBST buffer. Then, following 1 h of incubation with secondary antibody (for BIP and CHOP, HRP conjugated, 1:5000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; for GSDMD, goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (HRP), 1:10000, Cat. No: ab6721; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), at ambient temperature (24 °C), three further washes with TBST were performed. Enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) (Cat. No.: 18121164, Amersham (Cytiva), Uppsala, Sweden) was used to visualize protein bands. BIP and CHOP were visualized via a Cytation Cell Imaging Multi-Mode Reader (BioTek, VT, USA), whereas GSDMD was visualized on X-ray film under optimized exposure conditions. ImageJ was employed to analyze band density, which was standardized relative to β-actin, serving as the loading control [28].

Oil red O staining

To prepare a 0.5% Oil Red O stock solution (n = 6/group), 0.5 g of the powder was dissolved in 100 mL of isopropanol and incubated at 60 °C. This stock solution was diluted with double-distilled water in a 3:2 ratio and filtered, then left at ambient temperature for at least 10 min. Samples from the right hepatic lobe were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound and frozen at − 20 °C for 30 min prior to sectioning. Serial slices with a thickness of 8 μm and 48 μm distance between sections were prepared using a freezing microtome, mounted on slides, and equilibrated at ambient temperature for 30 min. The mounted sections were fixed in formaldehyde for 5 min at 4 °C by placing the slides in a staining tub containing a paper towel moistened with 10% formalin. After fixation, slides were dipped twice in 60% isopropanol and incubated with Oil Red O for 15 min at ambient temperature, protected from light. Slides were rinsed twice in 60% isopropanol and subsequently in distilled water after Oil Red O staining. After counterstaining with hematoxylin for 1 min, the slides were immersed in tap water for 5–10 min and then briefly rinsed with distilled water. After that, stained sections were coverslipped. In order to visualize and identify lipid droplets, imaging and microscopic analysis were carried out at 40 × magnification [29].

Estimation of oil red O-positive cell density

To calculate the numerical Oil Red O-positive cells density, 10 sections of each liver were sampled systematically. From each section, four representative fields of view were selected, distributed evenly across the four quadrants. For unbiased quantification, images of these fields were analyzed using a counting frame with defined inclusion and exclusion boundaries. The Oil Red O-positive cells were counted according to the optical dissector principle: Only cells that came into focus within the predetermined optical disector height and did not touch the forbidden (left and lower) borders of the counting frame were counted. The numerical density (Nv) of ORO+ cells was subsequently calculated using the following formula:

Nv = (ΣQ⁻ / (h × a/f × ΣP)) × (t / BA), where, ΣQ⁻ is the total count of Oil Red O-positive cells across all sampled fields, h is the height of the optical dissector, a/f represents the area of the counting frame, ΣP denotes the total number of counting frames analyzed, t is the measured average section thickness after processing (8 μm), and BA corresponds to the microtome setting (block advance) of 26 μm [30, 31].

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM and analyzed with GraphPad Prism 10; P < 0.05 was considered significant. One-way ANOVA with diet or drug as independent factors was used to analyze the AUC of OGTT, AUC of ITT, AUC of food intake, AUC of body weight, plasma HDL, LDL, FFA, UA, AST, ALT, IDE, GSH, MDA, SOD, IL-1β, TNF-α, BIP, CHOP, and Oil Red O staining. OGTT, ITT, food intake, and body weight over time were evaluated by two-way mixed ANOVA (diet/drug × time), and Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied as needed.

Result

Perinatal and/or adulthood exposure to the HFF diet altered body weight, food intake, abdominal fat accumulation, and the liver-to-body weight ratio; 4-PBA treatment showed therapeutic effects

Offspring exposed to a perinatal HFF diet (the HFF-N and HFF-HFF groups) had lower initial body weights at birth than those fed a perinatal normal diet (HFF-N vs. N-N, P < 0.001; HFF-HFF vs. N-N, P < 0.001; HFF-HFF-DMSO vs. N-N, P < 0.0001; HFF-HFF-4PBA vs. N-N, P < 0.001; N-HFF vs. HFF-N, P < 0.0001; N-HFF vs. HFF-HFF, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2a). Moreover, throughout the 105-day experimental period, significant differences were observed at specific time points between the groups that received the HFF diet during the perinatal period and those that received the normal diet during the same timeframe. Similarly, Fig. 2a inset shows that the body weight AUC of offspring raised on a perinatal HFF diet (the HFF-N, HFF-HFF, and HFF-HFF-DMSO groups) was significantly lower than that of offspring exposed to a perinatal normal diet (the N-N, and N-HFF groups), with the following comparisons: HFF-N vs. N-N, P < 0.001; HFF-HFF vs. N-N, P < 0.001; HFF-HFF-DMSO vs. N-N, P < 0.0001; HFF-HFF-4PBA vs. N-N, P < 0.0001; HFF-N vs. N-HFF, P < 0.01; HFF-HFF vs. N-HFF, P < 0.01. Administration of 4-PBA in the HFF-HFF-4PBA group did not significantly alter body weight compared with the HFF-HFF-DMSO and HFF-HFF groups and remained markedly lower than that of the N-N group (P < 0.0001).

Figure 2b illustrates food intake patterns in each experimental group from PND 28 to 105. Perinatal and/or adulthood exposure to the HFF diet impaired glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity; 4-PBA treatment improved metabolic homeostasis

In the OGTT analysis, each experimental group had a different baseline glucose concentration at 0 min. The groups that received the HFF diet during adulthood exhibited higher fasting glucose levels. Accordingly, baseline plasma glucose concentrations in the HFF-HFF (143.55 ± 4.70 mg/dL) and HFF-HFF-DMSO (137.81 ± 8.43 mg/dL) groups were higher than the N-N (93.84 ± 6.614 mg/dL) group (P < 0.001 and P < 0.05, in the corresponding order) (Fig. 3a). On the other hand, the HFF-N group, despite being exposed to the HFF diet during the perinatal period, showed a relatively normal fasting glucose concentration at 0 min (90.27 ± 8.20 mg/dL). At 15 min post-glucose administration, glucose levels remained significantly elevated in the HFF-HFF (198.40 ± 3.72 mg/dL) and HFF-HFF-DMSO (201.82 ± 12.10 mg/dL) groups relative to the N-N (127.66 ± 6.91 mg/dL) group (P < 0.001 and P < 0.01, in the corresponding order) (Fig. 3a). The N-HFF group that was exposed to the HFF diet only during adulthood also showed higher plasma glucose levels (183.21 ± 7.12 mg/dL) compared with the N-N group (P < 0.01), at 15 min time point. All the study groups showed a continuous decline at 120 min post-glucose challenge. However, at this time point, the plasma glucose concentration was higher in the N-HFF (120.03 ± 4.37 mg/dL), HFF-HFF (118.07 ± 4.05 mg/dL), and HFF-HFF-DMSO (127.49 ± 3.64 mg/dL) groups than the N-N (84.24 ± 4.44 mg/dL) group (P < 0.001, P < 0.01, and P < 0.001, in the corresponding order) (Fig. 3a). The HFF-HFF-4PBA group exhibited improved glucose clearance relative to the DMSO control group, and its plasma glucose levels were comparable to the N-N group at every measured time point. Also, as shown in Fig. 3a inset, the groups exposed to the HFF diet during adulthood (the N-HFF, HFF-HFF, and HFF-HFF-DMSO groups) exhibited higher glucose AUC values relative to the N-N group (P < 0.05, P < 0.001, and P < 0.0001, in the corresponding order). 4-PBA administration reduced the glucose AUC in the HFF-HFF-4PBA group in comparison to the HFF-HFF and HFF-HFF-DMSO groups (P < 0.001), nearly reaching the value observed in the N-N group.

Analysis of ITT revealed different insulin sensitivity patterns in experimental groups with different dietary exposures (Fig. 3b). The N-N group demonstrated the lowest fasting glucose concentration, whereas the N-HFF, HFF-N, and HFF-HFF-4PBA groups exhibited intermediate values. The HFF-HFF (140.50 ± 8.76 mg/dL) and HFF-HFF-DMSO (136.44 ± 7.53 mg/dL) groups exhibited elevated fasting glycemia at the 0 min time point compared with the N-N (97.60 ± 3.72 mg/dL) group (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01, in the corresponding order). Furthermore, at 15 min, glucose levels remained significantly elevated in the HFF-HFF (65.79 ± 5.84 mg/dL) and HFF-HFF-DMSO (76.56 ± 5.04 mg/dL) groups compared with the N-N (38.97 ± 3.64 mg/dL) group (P < 0.05 and P < 0.001, in the corresponding order). At the 120 min time point, the HFF-HFF (105.49 ± 3.26 mg/dL) and HFF-HFF-DMSO (96.91 ± 3.35 mg/dL) groups maintained elevated glucose concentrations compared with the N-N (64.64 ± 4.26 mg/dL) group (P < 0.001). In contrast, the N-HFF (71.44 ± 4.08 mg/dL), HFF-N (70.70 ± 7.28 mg/dL), and HFF-HFF-4PBA (66.36 ± 2.52 mg/dL) groups did not exhibit a notable difference relative to the N‑N group. The kinetics of insulin-mediated glucose disposal varied among experimental groups; however, all groups displayed maximal glucose suppression at 15 min after insulin administration. The N-N and HFF-N groups showed the strongest hypoglycemic response, indicating maintained insulin sensitivity, despite different dietary exposures. The reduction in plasma glucose levels in response to the injected insulin did not exhibit any notable change in the N-HFF group compared to the N-N group; however, the hypoglycemic response was profoundly attenuated in the HFF-HFF and HFF-HFF-DMSO groups. The HFF-HFF-4PBA group displayed enhanced insulin sensitivity relative to the HFF-HFF-DMSO group (P < 0.05 at 0, P < 0.01 at 15 min, and P < 0.001 at 120 min), and its insulin sensitivity was comparable to that of the N-N group. The analysis of the ITT AUC data showed notable variations between the experimental groups (Fig. 3b, inset). The HFF-HFF and HFF-HFF-DMSO groups had the highest glucose AUC values compared with the N-N group (P < 0.001 and P < 0.0001, in the corresponding order). The N-HFF and HFF-N groups displayed no significant difference versus the N-N group. Remarkably, 4-PBA administration significantly reduced the glucose AUC in the HFF-HFF-4PBA group in comparison with the HFF-HFF-DMSO group and nearly reached that of the N-N group.

Figure 3c illustrates plasma glucose concentration dynamics as a percentage of baseline (time 0) in ITT. The N-N and HFF-N groups demonstrated the most efficient initial glucose clearance, dropping to approximately 35–40% at 15 min with a steeper slope, followed by subsequent recovery. The N-HFF group followed similar trajectories. The HFF-HFF-DMSO group exhibited a slightly attenuated initial decline (reaching ~ 55% at 15 min) with a less steep slope and maintained comparatively higher glucose values over the subsequent time points. Compared with the N-N group, the HFF-HFF group showed a moderate early decrease, followed by a more pronounced upward trajectory during the recovery phase, resulting in the highest glucose value at 120 min (~ 75%, vs. ~60%). In the HFF-HFF-4PBA group, 4-PBA administration partially restored the glucose response, suggesting some remedial effect of the 4-PBA treatment.

According to the data shown in Fig. 3d and e, neither the different dietary interventions, at various life stages, nor the 4-PBA treatment produced statistically significant changes in insulin levels and HOMA-IR values compared with their respective controls.

Plasma HDL concentrations were somewhat lower in the N-HFF (36.90 ± 5.95 mg/dL) and HFF-N (34.07 ± 1.98 mg/dL) groups compared with the N-N group. The HFF-HFF (22.71 ± 2.18 mg/dL) group demonstrated a significant reduction in plasma HDL level compared with the N-N (P < 0.01). The HFF-HFF-DMSO (27.86 ± 2.73 mg/dL) group exhibited HDL levels similar to the HFF-HFF group but lower than those in the N-N group (P < 0.01). Importantly, the HFF-HFF-4PBA group exhibited markedly elevated plasma HDL levels (70.00 ± 1.97 mg/dL), which were substantially higher than in both the N-N and the HFF-HFF-DMSO groups (P < 0.0001).

Discussion

The results showed that, even in the absence of obesity, exposure to the HFF diet during infancy and/or adulthood caused metabolic abnormalities in offspring that later manifested as NAFLD. The present findings also unequivocally demonstrated that maternal diet is a substantial component in determining the offspring’s metabolic health and increasing the risk of metabolic problems in adulthood. Continued exposure to the same HFF diet in adulthood exacerbated these metabolic programming effects. This dual exposure resulted in significant disruptions in glucose homeostasis and liver pathology in the HFF-HFF and HFF-HFF-DMSO groups compared with the N-N group. 4-PBA treatment demonstrated a significant therapeutic effect by reducing diet-induced metabolic dysfunction. Additionally, the offspring exposed to a perinatal HFF diet (the HFF-N, HFF-HFF, HFF-HFF-DMSO, and HFF-HFF-4PBA groups) exhibited lower birth weights than those exposed to a perinatal normal diet (the N-N and N-HFF groups), which aligns with previous reports [32–35] and can be attributed to intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) associated with the maternal high-fat diet through various metabolic and molecular signaling pathways [36, 37]. Studies have indicated that fructose-rich diets are linked to placental mitochondrial dysfunction and reduced expression of nutrient transporters such as GLUT1 (intra-placental glucose transporter), which may restrict fetal growth and lead to lower birth weights [38, 39]. The developmental origins of health and disease (DOHaD) hypothesis proposes that reduced birth weight is commonly associated with a higher susceptibility to metabolic disorders later in life [40]. Consistently, maternal dietary restriction during pregnancy causes postnatal hyperphagia by disrupting the central neural circuits that control appetite, primarily due to impaired hypothalamic leptin signaling, decreased STAT3 phosphorylation, and altered neuropeptide expression (increased orexigenic and decreased anorexigenic neurons) [41]. Hyperphagia is also influenced by exposure to the HFF diet only in adulthood. Exposure to high-fat diets in adulthood activates prepronociceptin (PNOC) neurons in the arcuate nucleus (ARC), which inhibit anorexigenic neurons, disrupting leptin and insulin receptor-mediated satiety signaling [42]. These findings were supported by the present study, which showed that in offspring exposed to the HFF diet during both the perinatal period and adulthood (the HFF-HFF, HFF-HFF-DMSO, and HFF-HFF-4PBA groups) or just in adulthood (the N-HFF group) displayed hyperphagia. Notably, the HFF-HFF group showed significantly greater hyperphagia than the N-HFF group (p < 0.05), indicating a stronger impact of combined perinatal and adulthood exposure. Interestingly, offspring in the HFF-N group, which were exposed to the HFF diet only during the perinatal period, did not develop hyperphagia, suggesting that post-weaning exposure to an HFF diet is a stronger driver of increased energy intake than perinatal programming alone [43, 44]. However, despite this elevated energy consumption, these offspring did not gain weight, potentially due to decreased feed efficiency, altered substrate utilization, or increased energy expenditure on basal metabolic processes [28, 45]. Some studies suggest that animals exposed to high-calorie diets may exhibit increased energy expenditure due to metabolic adaptations or higher activity levels, which can counterbalance the effects of hyperphagia and prevent significant weight gain [45–48]. These findings support the “thrifty phenotype” hypothesis and earlier intrauterine growth restriction models, which postulate that nutritional insults during early life cause long-lasting adaptations that decouple calorie intake from weight gain [45, 49]. The findings reveal that even without obesity, metabolic adaptations raise metabolic vulnerability by limiting overall fat but increasing abdominal fat in offspring. This pattern in HFF fed offspring, partially resembles the “metabolically obese, normal weight” (MONW) phenotype [50, 51]; ), which is correlated with elevated visceral adiposity and metabolic abnormalities, including insulin resistance and inflammation [52, 53]. In line with these findings, offspring exposed to the HFF diet during both the perinatal period and adulthood (HFF-HFF, HFF-HFF-DMSO, and HFF-HFF-4PBA groups) displayed a significantly higher liver weight to body weight% compared with controls, despite the absence of overt obesity. This relative hepatomegaly has been reported as a sensitive marker of diet-induced hepatic lipid accumulation and inflammation in developmental NAFLD models [54, 55]. Furthermore, the results of the current study demonstrated a pronounced depletion of hepatic antioxidant defenses, specifically SOD and GSH, accompanied by an increase in MDA, a marker of lipid peroxidation. These changes highlight a redox imbalance in the liver of HFF-exposed offspring. This is consistent with increased hepatic β-oxidation and ROS overload, since excessive fatty acid metabolism, due to the HFF diet, exacerbates oxidative stress in hepatic tissue [56]. As shown in the results of previous studies, prolonged exposure to a HFF diet and oxidative stress can lead to ER stress and activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR) [57]. The unfolded protein accumulation triggers the UPR via the dissociation of BIP from ER membrane sensors, including protein kinase R-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK), inositol-requiring enzyme 1 α (IRE1α), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6). PERK phosphorylates eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 α (eIF2α), which increases activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) and CHOP expression. In this regard, in offspring exposed to the HFF diet, the elevated protein levels of BIP and CHOP indicate activation of the UPR and the occurrence of ER stress [58]. Furthermore, the presence of palmitic acid and other saturated fatty acids in the HFF diet appears to upregulate IRE1α [59]. This promotes thioredoxin interacting protein (TXNIP) expression and nucleotide-binding domain, leucine-rich–containing family, pyrin domain–containing-3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation, leading to liver inflammation and pyroptosis mediated by the cleavage of GSDMD [60–62]. During pyroptotic hepatocyte lysis, inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-1β are released, which accelerate hepatic inflammation and fibrosis [63]. The present study data revealed a rise in the pyroptosis executor protein (GSDMD) expression in the HFF diet-exposed offspring. These data indicate that pyroptosis is a crucial process linking ER stress to inflammation and cell death in NAFLD development. Regarding hepatic inflammation and pyroptosis, the present study data showed a significant increase in the proinflammatory cytokine IL-1β but not TNF-α in the livers of offspring exposed to the HFF diet. This selective increase in IL-1β indicates activation of the pyroptosis pathway rather than classical apoptosis, which in hepatocytes is more frequently associated with TNF-α [64]. Currently, it is understood that IL-1β is the principal cytokine in pyroptosis that is released through the action of caspase-1 [65]. This supports a model where the HFF diet promotes hepatic pyroptosis rather than classical apoptosis or necroinflammation. Some literature suggests that high-fat feeding can induce TNF-α mediated inflammation, whereas fructose consumption does not elevate TNF-α as much as HFD consumption [66, 67]. The current study findings demonstrated that exposure to an HFF diet during pregnancy and adulthood creates a liver environment characterized by oxidative stress, ER stress, and pyroptosis, which synergistically exacerbate liver damage and drive NAFLD progression. Additionally, offspring exposed to the HFF diet exhibited a pattern of atherogenic dyslipidemia, characterized by increased LDL-C, total CHOL, and FFA, and decreased HDL-C and HDL-C/LDL-C ratio. Increased FFA availability (from both enhanced dietary flux and adipose lipolysis) amplifies hepatic lipotoxicity and IR [9]. Fructose metabolism generates excess acetyl-CoA and glycerol-3-phosphate, promoting lipogenesis and triglyceride accumulation [68, 69]. Moreover, fructose impairs mitochondrial beta-oxidation of fatty acids, limiting fat breakdown and preventing the liver from exporting triglycerides, thus exacerbating hepatic lipid retention [9]. In addition, fructose phosphorylation depletes ATP, leading to AMP accumulation and increased uric acid (UA) production via AMP deaminase [10]. Because fructose bypasses key glycolytic regulation, it enhances pentose phosphate pathway activity and ribose-5-phosphate availability, promoting de novo purine synthesis and further increasing UA levels [70, 71]. Elevated UA, in turn, induces oxidative stress and promotes hepatic triglyceride accumulation by upregulating lipogenic enzymes [72]. Persistent accumulation of hepatic lipids, together with the lipotoxic intermediates such as ceramides and FFA, disrupts insulin signaling, particularly at the insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS-1) – phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) – Akt pathway, thereby promoting IR and compensatory hyperinsulinemia [73]. In this study, offspring exposed to the HFF diet during the perinatal and adulthood periods (the HFF-HFF and HFF-HFF-DMSO groups) demonstrated significantly reduced insulin sensitivity in the ITT and impaired glucose tolerance in the OGTT. Recent research supports the use of OGTT and ITT as dynamic and sensitive tools to detect insulin resistance [74]. Compared to controls, offspring that were exposed to the HFF diet during both perinatal and adulthood periods (the HFF-HFF and HFF-HFF-DMSO groups) or only during adulthood (the N-HFF group) showed higher plasma glucose levels following a glucose challenge, indicating reduced glucose tolerance. The decrease in glucose tolerance may result from β-cell dysfunction, insulin resistance or both [75]. In this regard, during the ITT, insulin administration resulted in a less pronounced decrease in blood glucose levels, indicating reduced insulin sensitivity. Notably, the significant reduction in hepatic IDE content observed in HFF diet exposed offspring, potentially due to oxidative stress [12, 76] and elevated FFAs, including non-esterified fatty acids (NEFAs) [77], might impair β-cell insulin secretion, ultimately contributing to glucose intolerance [14] and/or insulin resistance [77]. Alongside these physiological impairments, fasting insulin concentrations at baseline (time 0) did not differ significantly across dietary groups. This can be explained by the dynamic nature of insulin metabolism and the limitations of measuring insulin only at fasting. Fasting insulin levels and HOMA-IR alone often do not fully reflect insulin resistance or hyperinsulinemia and primarily reflect hepatic insulin resistance [78, 79]. This phenomenon has been documented in individuals or models with insulin resistance, where fasting insulin remains normal but postprandial insulin is elevated, or glucose clearance is delayed [80, 81]. Notably, confident assessment of insulin status requires direct measurement of insulin levels during glucose or insulin tolerance tests. In the current study, due to the absence of direct insulin measurements during these tests, definitive conclusions about insulin secretion or β-cell function cannot be made. However, the observed trend in glucose concentration during both OGTT and ITT supports the likelihood of impairments in insulin signaling and the development of insulin resistance. Moreover, chronic HFF diet exposure, both during the perinatal period and adulthood, accentuates hepatic lipid accumulation, as evidenced histologically by enhanced Oil Red O staining and increased hepatic neutral lipid quantification (numerical density measures), consistent with the biochemical findings. The histopathological changes directly reflected the biochemical disturbances, including elevated plasma glucose, IR, dyslipidemia (higher LDL-C and free fatty acids), hepatic oxidative stress (higher MDA, lower SOD, and GSH), and ER stress, as indicated by increased BIP and CHOP protein levels. These metabolic alterations are consistent with and likely drive the observed lipid accumulation. The study demonstrated that administration of 4-PBA could improve hepatosteatosis, inflammation, oxidative stress, ER stress, insulin resistance, and inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis in lean NAFLD caused by the HFF diet exposure during the perinatal period and adulthood. It is likely that 4-PBA alleviated ER stress and UPR hyperactivation by promoting proper protein folding [82].

Study strengths and limitations

The current research, has been highlighted the unique integration of high-fat/high-fructose exposure during both the perinatal period and adulthood, separated by a post-weaning normal-diet phase. This design closely reflects real-world human dietary patterns and offers a biologically relevant framework for investigating early-life programming and later-life dietary re-exposure in the pathogenesis of NAFLD. Additionally, the study utilized animals from separate litters and the group size (n = 6 per group) was consistent with established experimental standards and with validated models, previously conducted in this lab [12, 21]. This approach minimizes litter-related bias and ensures sufficient statistical power to detect biologically meaningful differences. Furthermore, the research employed well-established biochemical assays, validated ELISA kits, Western blot analyses, and quantitative histopathological scoring. The inclusion of multiple complementary endpoints strengthens internal validity and reduces the likelihood of methodological bias. Finally, the model successfully replicated key features of lean NAFLD, a clinically important yet underexplored phenotype. Moreover, the combined assessment of metabolic dysfunction, oxidative stress, inflammation, ER stress, and pyroptosis, along with pharmacological modulation using 4-PBA provided mechanistic insights with clear translational implications. Therefore, this research has substantially expanded the discussion of the clinical and scientific relevance of the findings.

However, a notable limitation of this study is the lack of plasma insulin measurements during the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Multiple time point measurements of insulin would provide crucial information about the insulin secretory dynamics and pancreatic β-cell function, improving the ability to distinguish between secretory defects and insulin resistance as well as to evaluate early- and late-phase insulin responses. Moreover, although β-actin was employed as the loading control in the present work and appeared stable in the high-fat/high-fructose diet–induced fatty liver model, its potential variability in other liver disease settings is acknowledged, and future experiments should incorporate additional or alternative normalization strategies [83]. Additionally, placental analysis—which could provide insights into HFF diet–induced alterations, including impaired nutrient transport, inflammation, and oxidative and ER stress—was not performed in this study. These alterations may influence fetal development and metabolic programming. Furthermore, although IDE levels were quantified, IDE activity, which can clarify insulin metabolism more effectively, was not evaluated. Also, energy expenditure was not directly measured (e.g., via indirect calorimetry), limiting the ability to fully account for the observed lower body weight despite hyperphagia; future studies should incorporate comprehensive metabolic cage assessments to evaluate resting metabolic rate, physical activity, and thermogenesis. Finally, circulating leptin levels and hypothalamic anorexigenic (e.g., POMC, CART) and orexigenic (e.g., AgRP, NPY) neuronal activity—that critically regulate energy expenditure—were not assessed, which could help explain discrepancies in body-weight regulation.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that exposure to an HFF diet during both the perinatal and adult periods leads to a cascade of metabolic disturbances—including IR, dyslipidemia, hepatic oxidative stress, increased ER stress markers (BIP and CHOP), elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, and GSDMD–mediated pyroptosis—which collectively contribute to the initiation and progression of NAFLD, even in the absence of obesity. Crucially, 4-PBA administration improved insulin sensitivity, reduced ER stress marker protein expression, ameliorated inflammatory and pyroptotic responses, restored hepatic antioxidant capacity (increasing SOD activity and GSH levels while decreasing MDA), and reduced hepatic lipid accumulation. While 4-PBA increased GSH levels and reduced hepatic lipid content, these parameters did not fully return to those observed in the N-N control group. Similarly, although 4-PBA decreased BIP expression relative to the HFF-fed groups, its expression remained elevated compared with the control group. These findings indicate that 4-PBA confers meaningful—but not complete—restoration of metabolic homeostasis, emphasizing the pivotal role of ER stress in the pathogenesis of lean NAFLD, independent of adiposity. According to these results, targeting the ER stress pathway is a potentially effective therapeutic approach for lean NAFLD patients who may not respond to conventional obesity-–based therapies. This treatment approach is also consistent with recent research demonstrating that ER stress is a key contributor to NAFLD pathogenesis independent of obesity.
