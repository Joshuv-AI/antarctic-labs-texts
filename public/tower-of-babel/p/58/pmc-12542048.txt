DNA methylation: a potential mediator between air pollution exposures and asthma control
Authors: My-Nhi Nguyen, Katelyn Queen, Frank D Gilliland, Sung Chun, Benjamin Raby, Joshua Millstein
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExposure to air pollution is known to contribute to the development and exacerbation of asthma symptoms, impacting respiratory health through complex, not yet fully understood biological mechanisms. It is hypothesized that DNA methylation (DNAm) changes triggered by air pollutants may mediate these effects on asthma symptom control.ObjectivesWe aimed to investigate the effects of air pollution on DNAm in peripheral blood and asthma control. We also explored whether DNAm could potentially mediate the relationship between air pollution exposure and asthma control.MethodsThis study included 651 participants with asthma exposed to ambient air pollutants, NO2, PM2.5, PM10, O3, elemental carbon (EC), and NOx. Self-reported Asthma Control Test (ACT) scores for the preceding six-month period were used to assess asthma symptom control. DNAm was quantified using the Infinium HumanMethylation450 BeadChip array in whole blood and CD4 + naive T cells. An Epigenome-Wide Association Study (EWAS) was conducted to identify CpGs associated with air pollutants, with sample sizes ranging from 169 to 329. The Causal Inference Test (CIT) was employed to examine whether DNAm mediates the effects of air pollution exposure on asthma control.ResultsExposure to short-term PM10 was associated with poorer asthma control. We identified DNA methylation changes linked to air pollution in both whole blood and CD4 + cells, with associations observed for PM10, EC and NOx in whole blood, and for PM10, NO2 and PM2.5 in CD4 + cells. Three CpG sites in whole blood—cg04605532 (p = 0.0004), cg05492904 (p = 0.0008), and cg23622322 (p = 0.001)– and one CpG in CD4 + cells –cg10022248 (p = 0.0084)—were associated with air pollution exposure and linked to asthma control. Additionally, CIT-based mediation analysis identified two CpG sites, cg02324789 (p = 0.05) and cg10758278 (p = 0.06), as potential mediators in the relationship between short-term exposure to PM10 and asthma control.DiscussionThese findings suggest that air pollution-associated changes in DNA methylation may affect asthma control for some loci, thus help elucidate underlying mechanisms in asthmatic patients. These epigenetic alterations highlight potential targets for understanding the pathophysiology of asthma due to air pollution. While our findings suggest possible links between air pollution, DNA methylation, and asthma control, more evidence is needed to establish causal relationships and unravel the complexities of these connections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-025-01979-w.Keywords: Air pollution, DNA methylation, Asthma, Causal mediation

Introduction

Asthma is a common respiratory disorder that affects millions of people worldwide. It is characterized by the presence of chronic inflammation in the lower airways, resulting in recurrent episodes of coughing, wheezing, breathlessness, and chest tightness [1, 2]. The development of asthma is known to be influenced by complex interactions between genetic factors and environmental exposures [3].

Air pollutants are widely recognized as risk factors for childhood asthma and exacerbation of respiratory symptoms [4–7], lung function impairment [8–10], chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [11], and hospitalization [12, 13]. Various studies have demonstrated that being exposed to local and nearby roadway air pollutants, such as nitrogen dioxide (NO2), particulate matter (PM), nitrogen oxides (NOx, including NO and NO2) and ozone (O3), can contribute to the increased risk of developing and aggravating asthma symptoms [10, 14–16]. The impact of air pollution on asthma pathophysiology varies with the duration of exposure, with short-term exposure activating acute immune responses and triggering asthma exacerbation, while long-term exposure may induce oxidative stress, ultimately leading to adverse asthma outcomes [3, 17].

Although the association between air pollution and asthma is well established, the exact underlying mechanisms explaining this relationship are still under investigation. One proposed mechanism is through epigenetic modifications where exposure to air pollution may contribute to the development and persistence of asthma [18, 19]. Studies have indicated that traffic-related air pollution (TRAP) components, such as PM, black carbon (BC), O3, NOx, and polyaromatic hydrocarbons, are associated with changes in DNA methylation (DNAm), typically resulting in decreased methylation levels after exposure [20]. DNAm patterns associated with air pollution exposure are often correlated with adverse health outcomes. In children, exposure to air pollution has been linked to methylation of immunoregulatory genes, altered immune cell profiles, and increased blood pressure [21]. Other findings suggest that modifications to blood DNAm, particularly in response to NO2 exposure, may mediate changes in lung function [22]. A study conducted on volunteers in Boston revealed that 28-day average exposures to BC, CO, PM2.5, or NO2 caused significantly decreased lung function, with this association potentially influenced by epigenetic mechanisms related to inflammation and immunity [23]. Therefore, changes in DNAm due to air pollution exposure may contribute to the development and exacerbation of various diseases.

This study examines the effects of air pollution exposure on DNAm in peripheral blood and its impact on asthma symptom control. Also considered is the potential mediating role of DNAm in the relationship between air pollution and asthma control. Data were collected from 651 asthmatic participants enrolled in the Asthma BRIDGE study [24, 25], with findings based on epigenome-wide association analysis.

Materials and methods

Asthma BRIDGE

The Asthma BRIDGE dataset, which has been described previously [24, 25], includes 651 participants who have been diagnosed with asthma by a physician. Using clinical phenotype data collected from the asthma control test (ACT) questionnaire [26], a composite chronic score was developed to summarize self-reported asthma control in the preceding 6 months. The score ranged from 4 to 20 and was derived from four components: patient waking due to cough or wheeze, cough or wheeze with and without exercise, and Albuterol use for asthma. Each component score ranged from 1 to 5 with higher scores indicating worse asthma control. Participants were classified into two groups based on their asthma control scores: optimal and suboptimal. Optimal asthma control was defined as a score equal to or below the median score, while suboptimal asthma control was defined as a score above the median score, following the approach used by Croteau-Chonka et al. [25] and Liao et al. [27]. In addition, socio-demographic information was collected, including gender, study site, age in years, race, income and education levels. Data on personal exposure to environmental smoke, asthma medication use in the past week, and the season of data collection were also recorded. Ambient air pollution assessment

In this study, a set of assignments related to local traffic and regional air pollution exposures [10, 28, 29], which were shown to be associated with asthma and health outcomes, was acquired for BRIDGE participants. These assignments covered a range of pollutants, including O3, NO2, particulate matter with diameter of 10 µm or less (PM10), particulate matter with diameter of 2.5 µm or less (PM2.5), NOx and Elemental Carbon (EC). Data were collected for the 12 months preceding their genetic test dates.

Hourly concentration of O3 (measured in parts per billion (ppb)) and NO2 (ppb), as well as both hourly and daily concentration of PM10 (µg/m3) and PM2.5 (µg/m3) exposures, were estimated using information from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s (EPA) Air Quality System (AQS) database, Canadian Environment’s National Air Pollution surveillance (NAPS) network and Mexican Sistema de Monitoreo Atmosferico (SIMAT) databases. The hourly concentration data were aggregated into daily exposures for NO2, PM10 and PM2.5, and daily 8-h maximum (8hrMx) O3. Daily air quality exposure values were spatially interpolated (mapped) using inverse distance-squared weighting from up to four air quality monitoring stations within a 50 km radius to the relevant home and work geocoded addresses for each subject during the corresponding time periods.

Air pollution exposure assignments of NOx and EC, measured in µg/m3, were estimated from the California line source dispersion model (CALINE4) [30] for local traffic sources. This included on-road vehicle emissions on freeways (FCC1), highways (FCC2), arterials (FCC3), and small roads (FCC4) within 5 km of each subject’s location. CALINE4 considers factors such as traffic volume, vehicle emission rates, proximity to local roadways, and meteorological parameters to determine air pollution levels. The daily mean NOx and EC concentration for all four local traffic sources during the specified period and location, were obtained.

Monthly averages were computed from the daily air pollution exposures for 12 months prior to the genetic test date. A 75% data completeness criterion was applied to ensure the validity of daily and monthly average exposures. Furthermore, monthly air pollution exposure was averaged for two different time periods before the genetic test date: 1 month prior and 2–12 months prior, representing short and mid-term exposure to air pollution, respectively.

DNA methylation

DNAm was quantified for whole blood and CD4 + naive T cells using the Infinium HumanMethylation450 BeadChip array (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA). We used R package minfi [31] to perform preprocessing and quality control. DNAm level as beta-values, ranging from 0 to 1, were calculated as β = M/(M + U + 100), where M and U denote the methylated and unmethylated signals, respectively. The HG19 genomic annotations were integrated via the Bioconductor package IlluminaHuman-

Methylation450kanno.ilmn12.hg19 [32]. Quality control steps included the following: (i) To assess potential cross-sample contamination, the getSnpBeta function in the minfi package was used to extract built-in SNP control probes from the 450K array, with histograms expected to display three distinct peaks corresponding to AA, AB, and BB genotypes. (ii) Samples with poor intensity (mean log median of methylated and unmethylated intensities < 10.5), density plots deviating from the expected bimodal distribution of beta values, or more than 5% of probes with a detection p-value > 0.05, were excluded. Additionally, samples with no ID mapping or mismatched sex between epigenetically predicted and reported sex, were also removed. (iii) Probes were excluded if they had a detection p-value exceeding 0.05 in more than 5% of samples, contained a SNP at the CpG interrogation or at the single nucleotide extension with a minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.01 (as identified and calculated by the getSnpInfo function in minfi), or were classified as non-specific or cross-reactive [33]. Probes targeting non-CpG regions (Probe ID prefix = “ch”) and probes on sex chromosomes were also removed. Normalization was performed on raw beta values using the within-array combination of Noob [34] and BMIQ [35] to enhance signal sensitivity, as recommended by Liu and Siegmund (2016) [36]. Known batch effects were corrected using ComBat [37]. The sva R package [38] was used to assess potential unknown batch effects, and any estimated surrogate variables were included in downstream analyses where applicable. For duplicate samples from the same subject, the sample with the highest mean log median of methylated and unmethylated intensities was retained. Samples from non-asthmatic participants were filtered out from further analysis. The final dataset comprised 397,436 CpGs in 340 whole blood samples and 425,704 CpGs in 239 CD4 + samples (refer to the flowchart in Fig. 2). Only eight participants had both whole blood and CD4 + samples, and 397,348 CpGs overlapped between the two datasets. M-values were used in all analyses to meet the assumptions of the statistical approaches [39].

Flowchart of preprocessing and quality control steps, detailing the sample and CpG selection process for whole blood (WB) and CD4 + DNA methylation data

To mitigate potential confounding bias arising from varying whole blood cell type composition in downstream analyses that involve DNAm, the relative proportion of pure cell types was inferred by the minfi package using the algorithm developed by Houseman et al. [40]. The distribution of five different cell types, including CD4 + and CD8+ T-cells, natural killer cells, monocytes, granulocytes, and B-cells, was estimated for each sample.

To account for the effect of genetic ancestry on DNAm, we included the top ten principal components (PCs) derived from genome-wide genotype data as covariates. Genotyping methods have been detailed in prior studies [41, 42]. Briefly, genotyping was profiled using various platforms based on the study site, and imputation was performed using the Michigan Imputation Server [43]. Genotypes with an imputation score (R2) below 0.80 or a minor allele frequency (MAF) less than 0.10 were excluded. Genotype data were available for 300 participants with whole blood DNAm and 200 participants with CD4 + DNAm. For participants without genotype data, we used the R package missForest [44] to impute missing PCs based on gender, age, race, and collection site.

Statistical analysis

Associations between air pollution and asthma control

Logistic regression analysis was conducted for each ACT score type (Sect. "Asthma BRIDGE"), encompassing both composite and component scores, followed by Wald tests to evaluate the effect of each air pollutant on asthma control status. Adjustment covariates included gender, age, race, income, education, collection site, whether the respondent is frequently exposed to environmental smoke, total dose of inhaled corticosteroid use in past week, and the season when data were collected. The air pollutants targeted for study comprised NO2, O3, PM2.5, PM10, EC and NOx. To maximize statistical power, the largest available sample size was used for each pollutant. Significantly associated pairs of air pollutants and asthma control status were identified at Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (BH FDR) less than or equal to 0.05. Odds ratios (ORs) of suboptimal asthma control in comparison to optimal control, scaled to one standard deviation change in air pollution levels, were reported.

Epigenome-wide association study (EWAS): assessing DNA methylation variations in response to air pollution

We conducted EWAS for both whole blood and CD4 + DNAm data. Linear regression analyses were performed to assess associations between DNAm variations and changes in air pollution levels at individual CpG sites. For each air pollutant, linear regression models were fitted for methylation as a function of air pollution levels using the limma R package [45], with adjustment covariates including cell-type proportion, the first 10 PCs of genotype, as well as gender, age, race, income, education, collection site, exposure to environmental smoke, and total dose of inhaled corticosteroid use in past week. To account for hidden batch effects and other unmeasured confounders in the DNAm data, the first 10 PCs of the DNAm data were additionally included as covariates. If Quantile–Quantile (Q-Q) plots indicated genomic inflation, the R package bacon [46] was applied to correct for inflation and bias. Differentially methylated positions (DMPs) were identified at the 0.05 BH FDR.

Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the PANTHER pathway database [47] for genes annotated to the DMPs to identify pathways associated with air pollution exposure. An FDR threshold of 0.05 from the overrepresentation test was used to determine significant pathways.

Additionally, for DMPs significantly associated with air pollutants, we examined associations between methylation and asthma control status using logistic regression (Asthma control ∼ DNAm + covariates), incorporating the same covariates and applying a FDR threshold of 0.05.

Epigenetic mediation analysis

The Causal Inference Test (CIT) [48, 49] was employed to evaluate the potential mediation effect of DNAm in the relationship between air pollution as a causal factor and asthma control as an outcome. Briefly, given an exposure X, an outcome Y, and a potential mediator M, CIT is a hypothesis testing method based on four conditions:

X and Y are associated; X is associated with M|Y; M is associated with Y|X; and X is independent of Y|M. A p-value is computed for each condition using a general linear modeling framework, and the maximum p-value is interpreted as an omnibus hypothesis test for the intersection of the component rejection regions. A suggestive FDR threshold of 0.1 was used to identify significant mediation relationships.

A series of filtering steps were implemented to identify air pollutant, CpG, and asthma control candidate trios for the mediation analysis. Adjustment covariates included cell-type proportions, the first 10 PCs of genotype, the first 10 PCs of DNAm, gender, age, race, income, education, collection site, environmental smoke exposure, inhaled corticosteroid used over past week, and the data collection season. Figure 3, Panel B, illustrates the filtering procedure based on the independent model, which is shown in Panel A as part of the composite null hypothesis. The process consists of two steps: (1) identify air pollutants associated with asthma control at a suggestive significance level p ≤ 0.05, and (2) identify CpG sites associated with the air pollutant(s) from Step 1 at FDR ≤ 0.05. CIT mediation analysis was then conducted for all selected air pollutant-CpG-asthma control trios.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the impact of potential outliers in environmental exposure data, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by identifying and removing extreme values beyond the 97th percentile for the air pollution data. Outliers were detected using Tukey’s method (values beyond 1.5 times the interquartile range) and visualized through boxplots to ensure consistency in distribution. After excluding these outliers, we re-ran the analyses to evaluate the robustness of our findings.

Analysis software

All analysis was performed in R 4.4 and Bioconductor 3.15.

Results

Establishing associations between air pollution exposures and asthma control

A significant association was found between asthma control status for cough/wheeze unrelated to exercise and exposure to PM10 in month 1 (BH FDR = 0.0232). The odds of having sub-optimal asthma control, compared to optimal asthma control, increased by 97% (OR: 1.97, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.37–2.9, p = 0.0004) per one standard deviation increase in PM10, while controlling for covariates. Several suggestive associations had p-values < 0.05 but did not meet the FDR threshold. These included PM10 in month 1 with composite asthma control (p = 0.0405) and waking due to cough/wheeze (p = 0.0466); PM2.5 in month 1 with cough/wheeze without exercise (p = 0.0250), albuterol use for asthma (p = 0.0380), and waking due to cough/wheeze (p = 0.0410); and O3-8hrMx in month 1 with cough/wheeze without exercise (p = 0.0446) (see Table 2).

In CD4 + cells, multiple DMPs were significantly associated with NO2, PM10, and PM2.5 exposures, whereas in whole blood, associations were primarily observed for PM10, EC and NOx. Specifically, in CD4 + samples, cg25289569 and cg23902105 were associated with NO2 in month 1; cg14660446, cg02324789, and cg10758278 with PM10 in month 1; and cg10022248 with PM2.5 in month 2–12. By contrast, no significant DMPs for NO2 or PM2.5 were detected in whole blood, where cg10777851 was associated with PM10 in month 1, and additional DMPs were identified for EC and NOx across both short- and longer-term exposures. These findings point to cell type–specific epigenetic responses to air pollutants, with CD4 + cells more sensitive to NO2 and PM2.5, while whole blood responded more strongly to EC and NOx.

Sensitivity analysis

After removing the outliers from the air pollution data and re-running the analyses, the overall association patterns between air pollution and asthma control remained broadly consistent, with minor variations in effect sizes and significance levels. PM10 in month 1 continued to show a suggestive association with cough/wheeze without exercise (OR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.27–3.15, p = 0.0031, FDR = 0.1867). Additional suggestive associations emerged, including PM10 with composite asthma control (OR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.17–2.82, p = 0.0088) and albuterol use (OR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.16–3.43, p = 0.0147). Associations with O3-8hrMx and PM2.5 in month 1 were consistent with the main analysis (see Supplementary Table S1).

For the EWAS results, removal of outliers reduced the findings to a single DMP.

Discussion

The findings that short-term exposure to PM10 was associated with increased odds of having cough/wheeze unrelated to exercise, along with the suggestive associations between short-term PM2.5 and O3 exposure and asthma control status for various symptoms, are consistent with previous studies. These studies have shown that expo-sure to air pollutants such as PM10, PM2.5 and NO2 increased chronic lower respiratory symptoms, phlegm, production, bronchitis, wheeze and medication use [10]. Increases in short-term PM10 and O3 exposures were associated with an increased risk of more severe asthma symptoms (eg. wheeze), medication use, asthma-related ED visits or hospitalisations [50–53]. From a mechanistic perspective, Guarnieri and Balmes [3] suggested that specific pollutants could cause airway inflammation and oxidative stress (eg, O3, NO2, and PM2.5), and airway hyper-responsiveness (O3 and NO2).

We identified 94 CpG sites in whole blood and 6 in CD4 + cells showing differential methylation associated with air pollution. The discrepancy observed in EWAS results between whole blood and CD4 + T cells likely reflects biological and methodological differences between these sample types. Whole blood represents a heterogeneous mixture of immune and non-immune cells, each with distinct methylation profiles. This cellular diversity can both increase the likelihood of detecting pollutant-associated signals (due to contributions from multiple cell types) and, at the same time, dilute cell-type–specific effects. In contrast, analyses restricted to purified CD4 + T cells provide a more homogeneous and targeted view, capturing epigenetic changes specific to this immune subset. Thus, while whole blood may reveal broader systemic responses to air pollution, CD4 + cells highlight more focused, cell-specific epigenetic alterations that could be directly relevant to immune-mediated pathways in asthma.

Three CpG sites in whole blood-cg05492904 (located in the 5’UTR of CYP19A1), cg04605532 (intergenic), and cg23622322 (intergenic)-and one CpG site in CD4 + cells (cg10022248, intergenic) were associated with air pollutant exposure and linked to asthma control. At the gene level, CYP19A1, which encodes aromatase, has been implicated in asthma, particularly in women, through its role in sex hormone synthesis and metabolism [54]. Variants in CYP19A1 have also been associated with lung cancer risk [55–57] and severe COVID-19 outcomes through hormone regulation [58].

The mediation analysis using the CIT identified two CpG sites-cg02324789 in NIN and cg10758278 in SPCS2-that may mediate the relationship between short-term PM10 exposure and asthma control at suggestive levels of statistical significance. While these findings require replication in independent cohorts, they highlight potential mechanistic pathways linking air pollution to asthma outcomes. NIN has been associated to exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [59], suggesting a role in maintaining airway structural integrity. SPCS2 encodes a subunit of the signal peptidase complex (SPC), which is critical for protein processing within the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [60, 61]. While not yet directly associated with asthma, SPCS2 may influence respiratory health through modulation of ER stress, a process increasingly recognized as contributing to severe asthma [62, 63].

The sensitivity analysis showed that, even after removing outliers, the overall associations between air pollution exposure, asthma control, and DNA methylation remained broadly consistent. While many CpG’s from the EWAS results and CpG-asthma control associations in whole blood were no longer statistically significant, the original signals may still reflect biologically relevant pathways. Although some findings were partly driven by extreme values, the underlying biological mechanisms identified in the original analysis could still reflect genuine epigenetic responses to air pollution exposure.

The study has several limitations. First, the lack of replication of our findings in an external cohort may limit the generalizability of the results. Second, the Illumina 450K array provides only partial genomic coverage, potentially overlooking relevant methylation sites. Third, the assessment of DNAm in peripheral blood rather than in respiratory tissues that are more directly exposed to air pollutants, which may mask tissue-specific effects. Fourth, the ABRIDGE spans a wide age range-including both children and adults-introducing variability in sensitivity to air pollution and asthma severity as age-related physiological differences could influence our findings. Finally, the reference panel used for cell type composition did not include eosinophil counts, a crucial cell type in asthma pathology whose varying proportions among asthma subtypes and healthy individuals could influence the observed DNAm patterns.

To the best of our knowledge, this is among the first epigenome-wide studies using causal mediation analysis to link to air pollution exposures, epigenetics, and asthma control. Specific air pollutant-associated changes in DNAm were revealed that may explain variations in asthma control and the underlying mechanisms. However, further research is needed to unravel the complexities of these connections.
