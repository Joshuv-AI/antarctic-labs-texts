Application of artificial intelligence chatbots in interpreting magnetic resonance imaging reports: a comparative study
Authors: Xuexue Bai, Ming Feng, Wenbin Ma, Yonghong Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots have emerged as promising tools for enhancing medical communication, yet their efficacy in interpreting complex radiological reports remains underexplored. This study evaluates the performance of AI chatbots in translating magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) reports into patient-friendly language and providing clinical recommendations. A cross-sectional analysis was conducted on 6174 MRI reports from tumor patients across three hospitals. Two AI chatbots, GPT o1-preview (Chatbot 1) and Deepseek-R1 (Chatbot 2), were tasked with interpreting reports, classifying tumor characteristics, assessing surgical necessity, and suggesting treatments. Readability was measured using Flesch-Kincaid and Gunning Fog metrics, while accuracy was evaluated by medical reviewers. Statistical analyses included Friedman and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Both chatbots significantly improved readability, with Chatbot 2 achieving higher Flesch–Kincaid Reading Ease scores (median: 58.70 vs. 46.00, p < 0.001) and lower text complexity. Chatbot 2 outperformed Chatbot 1 in diagnostic accuracy (92.05% vs. 89.03% for tumor classification; 95.12% vs. 84.73% for surgical necessity, p < 0.001). Treatment recommendations from Chatbot 2 were more clinically relevant (98.10% acceptable vs. 75.41%), though both demonstrated high empathy (92.82–96.11%). Errors included misinterpretations of medical terminology and occasional hallucinations. AI chatbots, particularly Deepseek-R1, effectively enhance the readability and accuracy of MRI report interpretations for patients. However, physician oversight remains critical to mitigate errors. Introduction

The widespread adoption of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in clinical oncology underscores its pivotal role in tumor diagnosis1–3. Upon completion of an MRI scan, radiologists prepare a detailed report based on the images. This report outlines both normal anatomical structures and abnormal lesion signals, providing a preliminary diagnosis4. Such reports are crucial for diagnosing tumors and guiding treatment strategies, as they detail the tumor’s location, size, margins, vascular distribution, and relationship with adjacent structures5. However, the medical jargon in these reports can pose challenges for non-medical professionals to understand. Despite electronic imaging reports being accessible, their complex content remains a significant barrier for patients trying to understand their own health status6. As a result, most patients require additional consultations with physicians to interpret their MRI reports, which can lead to prolonged waiting times for consultations and potentially delay diagnosis and treatment. This situation not only increases the workload for outpatient physicians but also adds to the overall healthcare burden7.

Recent breakthroughs in artificial intelligence (AI) have led to the development of large language models (LLMs), sophisticated natural language processing systems developed using vast amounts of data8. These chatbots, fueled by LLMs, embody a generative AI application exemplified by ChatGPT, capable of producing responses in a conversational manner based on user inputs9. Within two months of its release in November 2022, ChatGPT surpassed 100 million users10. The ability to actively interact with models like ChatGPT makes LLMs attractive tools in various fields, including medicine11. LLMs could potentially assist in various areas of medicine, given their capability to process complex concepts and respond to diverse requests and questions (prompts)12,13. An initial conjecture proposed the potential application of this technology within clinical decision support systems. However, previous research has posited that LLMs are currently immature, prone to errors, and exhibit instability in their outputs, which significantly limits their utilization in clinical workflows14,15. Nonetheless, several studies have demonstrated the commendable performance of AI chatbots in interactive dialogues. It has been observed that these chatbots exhibit a remarkable capability for clinical decision support based on input content16,17. Notably, some research has revealed that chatbots can accurately respond to clinical queries posed on social media platforms, often exhibiting greater empathy than physician responses18,19. Nevertheless, the potential of chatbots in clinical settings remains largely untapped. In this study, we explore the ability of chatbots to interpret MRI reports for patients without a medical background and identify the key elements relevant to disease management.

Methods

Data collection

We collected MRI reports from 6,174 tumor patients who underwent scans between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2024, at three locations. These reports, varying in length and complexity, were authored by radiologists specializing in multiple anatomical systems, including but not limited to the nervous, digestive, and urinary systems. Each report meticulously detailed the normal anatomical structures of the scanned regions, the abnormal lesion signals observed, and provided a concise preliminary diagnosis. Two independent reviewers analyzed the ​original MRI reports alongside corresponding scans​ to classify findings into benign, atypical, or malignant categories (Table 1). In cases of disagreement, a third oncologist made the final determination. To preserve the authenticity of the data, no alterations were made to the content of the original reports. Additionally, all identifiable information, such as patient details, examination dates, registration numbers, and physician names, was anonymized to protect patient confidentiality.

Study design

This study utilized two chatbots: GPT o1-preview (developed by OpenAI), referred to hereafter as Chatbot 1, and Deepseek-R1 (developed by DeepSeek), designated as Chatbot 2. The study period spanned from February 1 to March 31, 2025. During this period, the models were engaged with specific queries derived from the original reports. To standardize readability comparisons, all original MRI reports and submitted queries were exclusively in English. The chatbots were tasked with sequentially answering four questions: first, to interpret the reports in a manner understandable to patients without a medical background; second, to classify the lesions as benign, atypical, or malignant; third, to assess the necessity of surgical intervention; and fourth, to recommend a treatment plan based on the report’s content (Table 2). To minimize bias, a new chat session was initiated for each report analyzed. The responses from the chatbots to each prompt were documented.

In this study, readability assessments for both the original MRI reports and the explanatory reports generated by the chatbots were conducted using an online tool available at https://www.webfx.com/tools/read-able/. Three widely recognized readability indices were primarily calculated: the Flesch-Kincaid Reading Ease (FRE) score, the Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL), and the Gunning Fog Score (GFS). The readability assessments were completed during the study period, which spanned from February 1 to March 31, 2025. Subsequently, the responses provided by the chatbots underwent medical review. Each response thread generated by the chatbots was independently evaluated by two medical reviewers. In cases of disagreement, a third oncologist was consulted to adjudicate the discrepancy.

The medical review of the explanatory reports, i.e., responses to the first question, categorizes findings into four distinct levels: correct, partially correct, partially incorrect, and incorrect. “Correct” indicates that all content from the original report is accurately included without errors. “Partially correct” refers to omissions of details that do not affect patient management, such as failure to describe normal variations in sulci and gyri. “Partially incorrect” includes errors that slightly affect patient management, such as minor inaccuracies in describing tumor size or shape that are not significant enough to alter the diagnosis or treatment recommendations. “Incorrect” signifies errors that significantly impact patient management, such as misdescribing the tumor’s location.

The second and third questions aim to classify the nature of the tumor and determine the necessity of surgical intervention, respectively. For these classification tasks, the outcomes are assessed as either correct or incorrect. Additionally, reviewers employed the Likert scale to rate the quality of the treatment suggestions and the empathy demonstrated by the chatbots during the response process, on a scale from 1 (very poor) to 5 (excellent).

Ethical considerations

The Academic Ethics Review Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, provided an exemption from ethical review for this cross-sectional study (Exemption No. SZ-3192). All data utilized in this study were de-identified to ensure the privacy and confidentiality of human subjects. The Institutional Review Board of Peking Union Medical College Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences waived the requirement for the original informed consent and permitted secondary analysis without additional consent. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the guidelines of the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE).

Statistical analysis

The Friedman test was employed to compare the readability differences between the original report and two chatbot-generated explanatory reports. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare the readability, quality of therapeutic recommendations, and empathy among the two chatbot-generated reports. Additionally, the Chi-square test was applied to evaluate the differences in medical review performance between the two chatbots. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM), and a two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Readability analysis

Substantial readability improvements were evident between the original reports and those generated by Chatbot 1 and Chatbot 2, with significant differences identified using the Friedman test (p < 0.001). Chatbot-generated reports showed enhanced FRE scores and reduced FKGL and GFS scores, indicating enhanced readability and simpler text complexity. Detailed metrics, including complex word percentage and average word count, are outlined in Table 3. Further comparison between the two chatbots using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test revealed statistically significant differences in readability metrics (p < 0.001), confirming that Chatbot 2 outperformed Chatbot 1 in simplifying text.

Medical review

A comprehensive analysis was conducted on the explanatory reports generated by Chatbot1 and Chatbot2. In medical review tasks, Chatbot2 outperformed Chatbot 1, particularly in its accuracy and a significant reduction in errors, including incorrect classifications and hallucinations, where it showed only 26 (0.42%) incorrect and 11 (0.18%) hallucinatory instances, compared to 134 (2.17%) incorrect and 107 (1.73%) hallucinations for Chatbot1. Further, Chatbot2 adhered more closely to single-word response requirements in medical question classification, successfully meeting this criterion in 6029 reports (97.65%), markedly outperforming Chatbot1, which complied in only 4724 reports (76.51%).

Errors and hallucinations

Common errors primarily included misinterpreting medical terminology and incorrectly interpreting non-standard symbols. For example, both chatbots occasionally misdiagnosed tumors as malignant when processing terms such as “heterogeneous enhancement” or “invasion,” thereby increasing the risk of interpretative errors. Additionally, Chatbot1 sometimes overlooked tumor size when evaluating the necessity of surgery, leading to inaccurate assessments. Hallucinations typically manifested as erroneous introductions of causal relationships where none existed and involved over-interpreting unfamiliar terms or improperly combining unrelated information, resulting in misunderstandings. These issues highlight the limitations of chatbots in managing complex medical texts.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first cross-sectional study to explore the use of generative AI chatbots in interpreting MRI reports, aiming to enhance patient understanding of radiological findings, streamline the consultation process, and reduce outpatient workload. A pioneering study by Ayers et al. highlighted the potential of chatbots in healthcare, documenting their use in responding to informal patient inquiries on online forums18. Notably, these chatbot responses were found to be of acceptable quality and displayed greater empathy compared to some physician responses. Additionally, other studies have examined the capabilities of chatbots in addressing physician queries and supporting clinical decision-making, with mixed results20.

The deployment of chatbots to produce understandable reports from raw MRI reports has immense potential to deliver quick and accurate medical support to millions of patients21. Our research findings show that chatbots can transform raw MRI reports, often laden with medical terminology, into comprehensible and highly accurate documents. Unlike online medical educational materials that typically require at least an 11th-grade education level to understand, chatbot-generated reports simplify the understanding of health conditions for individuals22–24. Traditionally, physicians communicate MRI outcomes to patients, empowering them with knowledge necessary for medical decision-making25. The integration of AI chatbots into clinical practice could bridge the medical knowledge gap between physicians and patients, fostering proactive engagement and potentially improving disease outcomes26. Furthermore, chatbots can alleviate physicians’ workloads by communicating report results to patients, reducing the anxiety associated with waiting for follow-up appointments, and often providing free or low-cost medical support to socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, who tend to have poorer health outcomes27,28. These explanatory reports can also serve as educational aids for medical students and junior doctors, enhancing their learning experiences and facilitating a more comprehensive understanding of medical imaging findings.

Chatbots exhibit remarkable precision in medical classification tasks, especially in distinguishing the characteristics of tumors. Their responses to open-ended questions not only maintain accuracy but also demonstrate a higher degree of empathy, especially when compared to the responses from some clinical practitioners. Furthermore, this technology provides junior clinicians with additional medical advice, thereby enhancing their decision-making capabilities. It also boosts the efficiency of clinical workflows by effectively identifying tumor types and prioritizing urgent cases29.

While chatbots demonstrate a high degree of accuracy in classifying the nature of tumors and initially assessing the need for surgery, their responses can be verbose and may diverge from the core question when providing specific treatment recommendations. Although these detailed answers may demonstrate empathy towards patients, they may also lead to reduced satisfaction among medical professionals. Chatbots typically include non-medical disclaimers and always advise patients to seek professional medical advice. This underscores their role as auxiliary tools in healthcare and highlighting their inability to replace doctors’ clinical decision-making.

A potential linguistic bias warrants consideration. Although all inputs were standardized in English, DeepSeek-R1’s training corpus incorporates a richer foundation in Chinese medical lexicons due to its developmental origin. This multilingual architecture may partially explain its superior performance in semantic parsing of complex terms (e.g., ‘heterogeneous enhancement’) and contextual interpretation of anatomical relationships. Future studies should explicitly test language-pairing effects through parallel translation experiments to disentangle the impact of linguistic alignment on performance.

In clinical settings, inaccuracies in generative chatbots’ responses can have severe consequences for patients’ diagnosis and treatment. The primary challenges in deploying these chatbots in medicine relate to accuracy, reliability, and the occurrence of hallucinations30. Although some remedial strategies have been proposed, they have yet to achieve satisfactory outcomes. Fine-tuning involves additional training of the model on a dataset containing correct answers specific to certain questions or tasks, which has been shown to improve responses to customized queries31. Further research suggests that more extensive fine-tuning could significantly enhance the accuracy of these chatbots and reduce the incidence of hallucinations32. Therefore, future studies should focus on refining the ability of chatbots to interpret tumor MRI reports through targeted fine-tuning.

When deploying chatbots in clinical settings, it is crucial to pay attention to patients’ privacy, ethics, and legal considerations. It is essential to avoid entering identifiable information during interactions with chatbots, as patients could potentially be identified from the data used in training and input queries. Regarding responsibility, it has been suggested that clinicians or researchers utilizing these tools should be held accountable33. Additionally, since patients without a medical background cannot judge the accuracy of chatbots’ responses, these responses must be medically reviewed by clinicians to ensure their accuracy.

This study inevitably faces certain limitations. First, the MRI reports were exclusively sourced from tumor patients, who inherently possess more distinct features. This specificity may limit the applicability of the chatbot’s responses to conditions other than tumors, potentially reducing the generalizability of our findings. Secondly, the chatbot operates on a probabilistic basis, meaning that the formulation and sequence of questions can influence its replies. Even when identical questions are asked in the same order, subtle variations in the responses can still occur.

Conclusions

This study confirms the potential of AI chatbots to interpret MRI reports of tumor patients, identify key management features, and suggest treatments. However, integrating them into clinical practice requires rigorous validation of their responses and careful consideration of privacy and ethical standards.
