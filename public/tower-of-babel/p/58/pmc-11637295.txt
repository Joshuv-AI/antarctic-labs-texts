The impact of institutional measures on optimal use of intravenous immunoglobulin
Authors: Jean‐Nicolas Champagne, Antoine Desilets, Guillaume Roy, Océane Landon‐Cardinal, Hugo Chapdelaine, Geneviève Matte, Claudia Bouchard, Benjamin Rioux‐Massé, Anne‐Sophie Lemay
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) shortage represents an emerging issue in transfusion medicine. Limited data are available to determine effective strategies for optimal use. The objective of this retrospective observational study was to determine the impact of institutional measures on IVIG use at a large academic center.MethodsIVIG infusions from November 26, 2018 to September 25, 2022 were categorized according to their appropriateness (Recommended, Option of treatment, or Unrecommended), based on provincial guidelines, and separated into three phases: Reference, Transition, and Post‐Implementation phases, the latter following the adoption of restrictive measures, including mandatory standardized order forms, a blood bank gatekeeping strategy, and the creation of a stewardship committee.ResultsA total of 5431 IVIG infusions were administered to 544 patients, accounting for 295,033 g. The most common indication categories were neurology (30.4%), immunology (29.0%), and hematology (17.4%). From Reference to Post‐Implementation phase, IVIG infusions decreased from 2275 to 2000 with unrecommended indications dropping from 9.5% to 7.4% (p = 0.01), and a global reduction of 23.0% (from 131,163 g to 100,936 g of IVIG). Decrease in chronic immunomodulation accounted for 48.3% of total reduction (14,610 g of 30,227 g), whereas single‐use immunomodulation, 40.5% (12,237 g of 30,227 g). Moreover, an absolute reduction of 16.9% was observed in orders exceeding the recommended doses (20.8% to 3.9%; p < 0.0001). Together, the unrecommended and excessive IVIG doses decreased from 19,975 g (15.2%) to 6670 g (6.6%).ConclusionsA global reduction in IVIG use and a preferential decrease in the unrecommended orders were observed, most likely attributable to the bundle of restrictive strategies implemented.Keywords: institutional measures, IVIG, patient blood management, transfusion medicine

BACKGROUND

The province of Quebec is one of the largest users of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) in Canada, with up to 291.8 g per 1000 people in 2019–2020. 1 IVIG is a fractionated blood product derived from pooled human plasma from several donors, marketed by various companies. Hema‐Quebec is the sole blood supplier in the province, responsible for the management and distribution of blood products in every hospital. Although Quebec's IVIG self‐sufficiency rate has improved from 21.4% in 2019–2020 2 to 31.0% in 2022–2023, 3 the province remains heavily reliant on imports from the United States and Europe. 4 Despite the product's scarcity, IVIG consumption continues to rise as indications broaden and off‐label uses account for a significant proportion of its use. 5 Concerns about potential shortages due to limited manufacturing capacities existed even before the COVID‐19 pandemic. The pandemic further exacerbated worldwide IVIG supply issues, leading to threats of shortages and drastic price inflation. 6 These recent events have spurred interest in regulatory strategies to optimize IVIG use.

Various authorities sought strategies to promote evidence‐based IVIG use. In Quebec, from May 2017 to October 2022, several guidelines were published on IVIG use in neurology, 7 hematology, 8 immunology, 9 dermatology, 10 rheumatology, 11 infectious disease, 12 transplant, 13 and other fields. 14 These guidelines categorized indications as Recommended, Option of treatment, Insufficient data, or Unrecommended, based on the available literature. However, no provincial management plans were in place to enforce appropriate IVIG utilization nor local gatekeeping strategies existed in most healthcare facilities.

In addition to the financial burden associated with inappropriate IVIG use, adverse events occur in 5–15% of IVIG infusions, 15 including immediate systemic reactions, such as headaches, fever, chills, or anaphylactoid reactions, as well as hemolytic reactions 16 and thrombotic complications. 15 Less common side effects like rash 17 and acute kidney injury 18 may also occur, though their correlation with IVIG infusion is not always recognized by prescribing physicians. For some indications, in the absence of demonstrated benefits, these risks should be considered prohibitive.

Retrospective studies indicate that up to 28.5% of IVIG orders were considered inappropriate. 5 Similarly, in Quebec, it was estimated that 30% of orders from 2018 to 2019 were placed for unclear or invalid indications. 19 Although some interventions, such as educational sessions or the implementation of standardized order forms, 20 have shown encouraging results in reducing inappropriate use, a meta‐analysis failed to demonstrate their efficacy due to insufficient data. 21 This meta‐analysis included only three trials, 5 , 20 , 22 leading the authors to conclude that more research is needed to better document the impact of institutional measures on IVIG orders. However, in other areas of patient‐blood management, such as red blood cell (RBC) transfusions, institutional interventions have been shown to reduce inappropriate transfusions by 9% to 77%. 23 Therefore, we conducted the present study to assess and compare the IVIG use before and after implementing regulatory strategies to optimize its use.

METHODS

Study design

This retrospective observational single‐center study was conducted at the Centre Hospitalier de l'Université de Montréal (CHUM), an academic tertiary care hospital with approximately 1200 beds, and a reference center for solid‐organ transplants and adult immunology services. All IVIG infusions, from November 26, 2018 to September 25, 2022, were analyzed. IVIG doses administered in both inpatient and outpatient settings were included. A chart review was conducted to extract the indication for IVIG, which was categorized according to its level of appropriateness (Recommended, Option of Treatment, or Unrecommended), largely based on Quebec provincial guidelines. Indications not defined in these guidelines were discussed with expert members of the stewardship committee during data collection and analysis to reach a consensus on the appropriateness of each order. Detailed information on clinical indications and categories is available in Table. IVIG was prescribed either as single‐use immunomodulation, chronic immunomodulation, or replacement therapy depending on the underlying indication. According to current guidelines, recommended immunomodulatory doses are between 1–2 g/kg over 2 to 5 days, and replacement therapy doses are between 0.4 to 0.6 g/kg/month, tailored to the patient's response (clinical efficacy for immunomodulation, or pre‐infusion IgG level of 6–9 g/L and absence of infection for replacement therapy). For each order, the recommended IVIG dose was assessed based on the patient's ideal body weight using Devine's formula. 24 For men, the ideal body weight is 50.0 kg + 2.3 kg per inch over 5 feet; for women, 45.5 kg + 2.3 kg per inch over 5 feet. Excess doses (ED), calculated as the difference between the prescribed dose and the recommended dose based on adjusted body weight, were compiled. IVIG infusions administered on multiple consecutive days were compiled as a single IVIG infusion, and every infusion was considered separately for patients receiving IVIG on a chronic basis (e.g., every 2 weeks). Data concerning IVIG treatments and doses were extracted from our blood transfusion software, eTraceline (MAK‐system).

The study period was divided into three phases: Reference phase (560 days), Transition phase (280 days), and Post‐Implementation phase (560 days), as illustrated in Figure 1.

The Transition phase was defined by a series of nonrestrictive measures. Shortly after the start of the COVID‐19 pandemic, letters from the Ministry of Health (MOH) and the Comité consultatif national de médecine transfusionnelle (CCNMT, equivalent to the National Advisory Committee on Blood and Blood Products (NAC) in Quebec) were sent to hospital administrators regarding the threat of an IVIG shortage and a key lime phase (when stocks are still sufficient or slightly reduced but signals indicate a potential shortage) was announced in April 2020, commanding for a reduction of 10% of IVIG usage. 25

In June 2020, an internal communication was sent to all physicians in our institution setting a specific goal of 10% reduction in overall use, marking the start of the Transition phase. A further 20% reduction in IVIG utilization was requested by the MOH in fall 2020, leading to a second internal communication to our prescribers in December 2020, aiming for a 20% reduction in IVIG use. Internal communications summarized good IVIG prescribing practices, including using ideal body weight and published provincial guidelines for recognized indications, as well as rounding up the dose to the lowest 5 g if possible. Although the provincial guidelines on neurology indications were available since 2017, 7 others appeared throughout 8 , 9 , 10 and even after 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 the Transition phase. During this time, provincial education sessions on appropriate IVIG use were held by neurology and hematology‐oncology associations.

Mandatory use of standardized IVIG order forms, for neurological and non‐neurological indications and the implementation of a blood bank gatekeeping system inaugurated the Post‐Implementation phase. The order forms included a list of recommended indications/diagnoses for IVIG (specifying the indication was mandatory; if the indication was not listed, it had to be specified in a blank space on the form), the desired dose, the administration interval, and the patient's height and weight. A link to a dose calculator based on ideal body weight derived from Devine formula, 24 rounded to the nearest 5 g, was provided. All forms were reviewed by blood bank technologists for conformity, and all required the approval of the blood bank director during opening hours (Monday to Friday, 8 a.m. to 5 p.m.). Out‐of‐hours orders were validated by the blood bank medical director on the next working day, even if IVIG had already been delivered. Prescribing physicians were contacted for out‐of‐guidelines indications or dosing. In case of disagreement, it was escalated to the stewardship committee (see below). New orders for chronic treatments were valid for 6 months, whereas renewal orders were valid for 12 months. During this phase, an IVIG stewardship subcommittee was created, composed of blood bank directors and representatives from different specialties, including immunology, hematology, neurology, rheumatology, and internal medicine. Statutory meetings occurred every 4 months to discuss conflicting IVIG indications. Committee members provided expertise, offered input on diagnoses warranting IVIG consideration, and proposed alternatives before resorting to IVIG. Additionally, they acted as ambassadors in their own divisions, raising awareness among colleagues about the scarcity of the resource. Toward the end of this phase, it was decided that all IVIG recipients receiving chronic immunomodulatory treatment would be reviewed biennially.

Exemption from Ethics Committee

The Hospital Research Ethics Board (REB) waived the requirement for a formal review by the Ethics Committee and approved the study protocol.

Statistics

For IVIG doses administration, Chi‐square tests were performed for comparing all three phases, and used for dichotomous comparisons. Statistical tests were interpreted with an α error of 5%. The null hypothesis was rejected if p < α. Only descriptive statistics were used when comparing IVIG quantities (in gram). Comparisons were mainly between the Reference and Post‐Implementation phases, as they had the same duration and allowed for a full assessment of the measures' impact. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 and R Studio version 1.4.1564 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Between November 26, 2018 and September 25, 2022, a total of 5431 IVIG infusions were administered to 544 patients, totaling 295,033 g. The main indications were neurology (30.4%), immunology (29.0%), hematology (17.4%—including immunomodulation and secondary immunodeficiency), and rheumatology (15.3%). A complete list of indications can be found in Table S2. IVIG was used for replacement therapy in 3045 infusions (56.1%) corresponding to 109,975 g (37.3%) of the total quantity of IVIG used. In contrast, 443 infusions (8.1%) were for single‐use immunomodulation therapy (47,635 g or 16.1%) and 1943 infusions (35.7%) were for chronic immunomodulation therapy (137,420 g or 46.6%).

During the Reference phase, a total of 131,160 g of IVIG were administered, compared to 100,935 g during the Post‐Implementation phase, resulting in a 23.0% global reduction in IVIG use (Figure 2). IVIG administered within the recommended dose category increased from 54,440 g to 56,555 g. However, reduction was observed in the categories of “Option of Treatment” (56,745 g to 37,710 g), “Unrecommended” (9685 g to 5615 g), and ED (10,290 g to 1055 g), leading to an overall reduction of 30,225 g (23.0%). The number of infusions decreased from 2275 to 2000 in the same time span, representing a 12.0% reduction.

In the Reference phase, 9.5% of IVIG administrations were considered as “Unrecommended” per guidelines, compared to 7.4% in the Post‐Implementation phase (an absolute reduction of 2.1%; p = .01). The impact varied by indication category, ranging from −27.3% (transplant) to +11.0% (infectious disease) (Table S3). A relative increase of 28.6% in IVIG infusions was observed in dermatology, which was attributed to an increase of only eight infusions. Stability or a decrease in the total number of infusions was observed in other categories, varying from 0% to −68.2% (Table S3). Similarly, the quantity of IVIG (g) changed by −6.4% to −77.9% from the Reference to the Post‐Implementation phase, except for immunology, which saw a slight increase of 0.7% (Figure 3, Table S3).

The proportion of orders with dosages above recommendations was lower in the Post‐Implementation phase (3.9%) compared to the Reference phase (20.8%) (p < 0.0001). The total excessive quantity of IVIG (ED) in the Reference phase was of 10,290 g (distributed over 473 infusions), decreasing to 1055 g (divided over 77 infusions) in the Post‐Implementation phase. When combining “Unrecommended” IVIG doses and ED, the absolute reduction was 13,305 g, decreasing from 19,975 g (15.2%) in the Reference phase to 6670 g (6.6%) in the Post‐Implementation phase, with a downward trend evident in the monthly IVIG use (Figure 4).

Median monthly dosing remained unchanged for the chronic IVIG replacement therapy group (0.60 vs. 0.61 g/kg/month, p = .13). However, a significant decrease was noted in the chronic immunomodulation therapy group (median dose 1.23 vs. 0.95 g/kg/month, p < .0001). A decrease in the median dose per infusion was observed in patients receiving single‐use immunomodulation IVIG treatments (1.95 g/kg vs. 1.13 g/kg, p < .0001) (Figure 5). There was a 32.4% reduction in the number of IVIG infusions for single‐use immunomodulation indications, accounting for 12,235 g (40.5% of total IVIG reduction) (Table S4 and Figure 6). Despite no change in the number of patients receiving chronic immunomodulation therapy, the decreased frequency and dose per infusion led to a 24.0% reduction in IVIG use in this group, accounting for nearly half (48.3%) of the total reduction observed in the study. Similarly, for replacement therapy, only one additional patient received IVIG in the Post‐Implementation phase, but the total IVIG quantity decreased by 7.6%, representing 11.2% of the overall reduction.

Finally, as shown in Table S5, monthly IVIG use decreased from 6558 to 6294 g (−4.0%) from the Reference to the Transition phase, reflecting the modest impact of nonrestrictive measures. A further 19.8% reduction was observed from the Transition to the Post‐Implementation phase, with an average monthly use of 5047 g. In the Reference phase, “Unrecommended” indications and ED represented 15.5% of the total IVIG use, decreasing to 6.6% in the Post‐Implementation phase. This global reduction, as well as the relative decrease in “Unrecommended” and ED use, persisted throughout the implementation period, demonstrating the sustainability of the restrictive measures put in place (Figure 4).

DISCUSSION

Our study demonstrates a significant reduction in both the total amount of IVIG administered and the number of IVIG infusions, following the implementation of restrictive measures. Notably, this reduction occurred without a significant impact on the recommended dose received. As illustrated in Figure 2, there was an appreciable reduction of IVIG as an Option of treatment. This may be due to decreased dose per infusion but may also be due to prescribers seeking alternative therapies for which IVIG could be indicated, for example thrombopoietin analogs for immune thrombocytopenia, which became more readily available during the same period. Additionally, reductions in both unrecommended indications and doses exceeding the recommendations were observed.

Evaluating the impact of individual measures is challenging in this retrospective study due to the bundle of interventions implemented. Nonrestrictive measures, such as practice guidelines and informative letters underlining good practice standards, led to only a modest initial 4.0% decrease in IVIG consumption during the Transition period. However, the introduction of restrictive measures resulted in a further 19.8% reduction. This suggests that while educational documents and awareness letters alone may have some effectiveness, restrictive measures seemed more impactful for optimizing IVIG use. We must acknowledge, however, that the shorter “transition” phase may not have allowed enough time for the nonrestrictive measures to have their full effect, as patients on chronic therapy may have prescriptions renewed only every 6 to 12 months. In addition, it would have been impossible to isolate the “carry‐over” effect from the educational interventions that preceded the “restrictive phase” in the Post‐Implementation phase.

The most substantial IVIG reductions were observed in neurology and rheumatology, with hematology also showing notable decreases. Chronic immunomodulation therapy was a major contributor to IVIG use in the first two subcategories. During the study period, we observed a significant reassessment of IVIG dosing, as evidenced by reductions in frequency, dose per infusion, or both, leading to a significant decrease in IVIG use for chronic immunomodulation therapy. It was previously recognized that a reduction in “single‐use” recipients may have only little effect on the quantity of IVIG consumed. 26 In this study, the reduction in IVIG administered to patients receiving single‐use immunomodulation treatments accounted for 40.5% of the total IVIG reduction.

The mandatory use of standardized order forms can be effectively implemented, with rapid potential gains. These forms outline the most common indications for IVIG and may include a dose calculator, reinforcing the use of ideal body weight for dosage. By only displaying recommended and option‐of‐treatment categories, the forms compelled prescribers to justify any deviations. This approach effectively reduced the number of unrecommended IVIG orders and excessive dosages; the impact of standardized order forms having already been published in the literature. 5 , 20 Each IVIG order was reviewed by blood bank technologists and required approval from the blood bank director with a time commitment of about 2–3 h per week. Doses were recalculated, ensuring adequate dosing was prescribed. While this system worked well, it had limitations, including the absence of a blood bank medical director on call during off hours, leading to retrospective review of some orders. Additionally, the blood bank director's expertise did not cover every condition warranting IVIG, making the inclusion of specialists from various subspecialties in the IVIG stewardship committee essential. Concerns on colleagues overlooking prescribed orders were raised, but similarly to what is seen in antimicrobial stewardship programs, confrontations with noncompliant prescribers were rare. 27 The stewardship committee played a crucial role by providing expertise across specialties, especially regarding IVIG adjudication for off‐label indications when scientific evidence is scare and expert opinion sometimes prevail, advocating for appropriate IVIG use among colleagues, and regularly reviewing chronic IVIG orders. Finally, the blood bank gatekeeping strategy, together with the periodic follow‐up and review of chronic IVIG orders by the stewardship committee, led physicians to regularly and proactively reassess IVIG treatment (dose and frequency of infusions), leading to dose reduction and discontinuation of therapies no longer required. It is conceivable that in certain situations, the fact of being under constant surveillance may have encouraged physicians to reflect on their ordering habits, potentially contributing to the sustained effects observed beyond the study period.

While our study did not focus on individual IVIG indications, we acknowledge the growing use of IVIG for various conditions. Many IVIG indications in the current study, although being considered off‐label by current guidelines, where adjudicated as reasonable, or justified, based on expert bodies or some literature. Furthermore, practices change, and guidelines published before the study and even during our study period may not accurately reflect current practices anymore. Additionally, emerging therapies in hematological malignancies, such as CAR‐T and bispecific antibodies for example, are associated with significant hypogammaglobulinemia 28 and associated infections which may increase IVIG demand. Despite the paucity of data, consensus recommendations remain in favor of IVIG replacement therapy for bispecific antibody therapy in lymphoma, 29 and myeloma, 28 or CAR‐T. 30 In the myeloma setting, retrospective study demonstrated a tenfold reduction in serious infections while being on a bispecific therapy 31 ; however, the question has been raised about using alternative solutions such as prophylactic antibiotics which may be more effective, 32 but also cheaper. 33 Ongoing trials are exploring the role of IVIG in these settings, but until more data are available, implementing strategies like those used in our institution remains crucial.

The retrospective nature of this study limits our ability to isolate the impact of individual interventions. Additionally, the influence of the COVID‐19 pandemic on IVIG use—whether leading to undertreatment or overtreatment—remains uncertain. Our study was also not designed to fully capture alternative treatments in cases of IVIG treatment discontinuation, such as plasma exchange, other drug therapies, or even SCIG, or even the potential clinical impact of underdosing. Very few studies have looked into optimal IVIG dosing for immunomodulation. However, a recent trial in CIDP demonstrated no significant difference in response rate between 1.0 g/kg versus 2.0 g/kg, 34 leading us to believe that the effect of underdosing may not be clinically significant. Despite these limitations, no adverse outcomes were reported to the IVIG stewardship committee, although a recent study suggested otherwise. 35 Our institution, a large academic reference center with expertise in neurology, solid organ transplant, and immune deficiencies, may have different IVIG usage patterns compared to smaller centers. We also acknowledge that the implementation of a gatekeeping strategy and the creation of a stewardship committee may have been facilitated by the presence of three transfusion medicine specialists on site, which allowed for continuous IVIG surveillance coverage on weekdays, and by the easy access to experts from different specialties. Nevertheless, more passive measures such as standardized order forms could easily be implemented in smaller centers to ensure that IVIG is prescribed for recognized indications, according to current guidelines and facilitate review of the indications that fall outside these guidelines.

With an estimated cost of $100 CAD per gram of IVIG at the time of our study, 14 the implementation of these measures likely resulted in substantial savings, potentially over $800,000 CAD annually in IVIG costs related to unrecommended use and excess dosing, and an additional $1,200,000 CAD from titrated dose reductions, targeting the lowest effective dose, and alternative treatments. These savings exclude additional costs such as nursing time and potential treatment‐related adverse events but do not account for the cost of alternative treatments.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, a global reduction in IVIG use and a preferential decrease in the unrecommended orders of IVIG were observed in this retrospective study. While nonrestrictive measures initially led to a modest reduction, the implementation of restrictive measures resulted in a more substantial and sustained decrease in IVIG use. These changes were likely due to the bundle of strategies employed, including mandatory use of IVIG order forms, blood bank gatekeeping, and the IVIG stewardship subcommittee. The study confirms the effectiveness of these methods, though the magnitude of the impact depends on the initial level of ordering appropriateness.
